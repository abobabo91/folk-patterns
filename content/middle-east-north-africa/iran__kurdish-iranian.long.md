## Overview

Kurds in Iran number roughly 9–10 million and occupy the mountainous western rim of the country — Kurdistan, Kermanshah, West Azerbaijan, Ilam and parts of Lorestan provinces — in the Zagros ranges and the high valleys that drain toward Mesopotamia. A separate, long-displaced community of Shi'ite Khorasani Kurds lives in North Khorasan, in Iran's northeast, resettled there by the Safavids as a frontier guard. The territory, called *Rojhilatê Kurdistanê* (Eastern Kurdistan), takes in the towns of Sanandaj, Mahabad, Kermanshah, Marivan, Baneh, Piranshahr and Sardasht, and the Hewraman (Avroman) highlands on the Iraqi border. Most Iranian Kurds are bilingual in Kurdish and Persian. In folk-culture terms this is one of the densest weaving regions of western Asia: the Senneh and Bijar carpet traditions, the felt and flatweave repertoire of the tribal confederations, and a continuous costume tradition that survived the Pahlavi dress reforms largely intact.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Senneh carpet** — the pile carpet of Sanandaj (Senneh, the old name of the town), woven on a single-warp-depressed foundation with the finest knot count in Kurdistan and a famously thin, stiff handle. The *Senneh* knot (asymmetric, open left) is named for the town though it is by no means confined to it; the characteristic design is a diapered field of tiny *boteh* or *herati* repeating over the whole ground, in madder red, indigo and ivory, quite unlike the bold large-scale drawing of surrounding Kurdish villages.

**Bijar carpet** — from Bijar (Bicar) in Garrus, the heaviest rug of the Iranian repertoire. The weft is beaten down with a metal comb over a wet, dampened warp, producing a rigid, plank-like fabric that will not fold; collectors call them the "iron carpets of Persia." Designs run to a large central medallion with *herati* field, or the all-over *mina khani* lattice, in deep madder and camel ground.

**Kurdish kilim / *gelim*** — slit-tapestry flatweaves woven by women on horizontal ground looms, used as floor cover, bedding wrap and dowry goods. Kurdish kilim drawing favours large stepped lozenges, hooked medallions and banded field divisions, with wefts of hand-spun wool dyed with madder, indigo, walnut husk and pomegranate rind.

**Jaff rug** — the signature weaving of the Jaff confederation of the Kermanshah–Sulaymaniyah border zone: a field packed edge to edge with concentric hooked diamonds (*chelleh* lozenges), each latch-hooked and set on a diagonal grid, in hot madder, apricot, indigo and undyed brown. Jaff bags — the small square *khorjin* faces — are the most collected Kurdish weaving outside the carpet trade.

**Kolyayi rug** and **Sauj Bulagh rug** — village and tribal pile weavings named for the Kolyayi district southeast of Kermanshah and for Sauj Bulagh, the old name of Mahabad. Sauj Bulagh weaving is the archaic end of the Kurdish repertoire: long-piled, loosely knotted, dark-toned rugs with archaic medallion and *harshang* (crab palmette) drawing, largely pre-1900.

**Jajim** — warp-faced striped weaving in narrow loom widths sewn selvedge to selvedge into a large cloth, used as bedding cover, wrap and horse blanket. Kurdish *jajim* is finer and more polychrome than the Shahsevan equivalent, with narrow stripes carrying small geometric filler motifs.

**Khorjin, *mafrash*, cradle cover** — the woven container repertoire of a transhumant household: the double saddlebag (*khorjin*), the large rectangular bedding-transport bag (*mafrash*, built of five soumak or kilim panels), the salt bag (*namakdan*), and the small flat cradle cover woven for a specific child.

**Kurdish felt (*nemed*)** — pressed wool floor felts and shepherds' capes (*kapanak*), made by rolling scoured wool with hot water over a reed mat; coloured wool is laid into the surface before rolling to produce inlaid mosaic patterning rather than sewn appliqué.

**Klash** (also *giveh*) — the white cotton-topped shoe of Hewraman, its upper hand-knitted or needle-woven in fine cotton, its sole stitched from layered cotton cloth and, in the Hewrami version, pressed and hardened with a wooden last. Now the emblematic Iranian Kurdish craft object; *giveh* is the wider Iranian term for the same footwear family.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Boteh* (the almond/paisley, in Senneh a miniature repeated diaper); *herati* (rosette in a lozenge flanked by four curved "fish" leaves); *mina khani* (all-over lattice of four-petalled flowers); *harshang* (crab palmette); *gol-e farang* ("foreign flower," the European-derived rose of nineteenth-century Kurdish and Bijar weaving); hooked lozenge or *chelleh* diamond (Jaff); *muhr* / medallion; *shakh-e gavazn* (stag's-horn latch hook); running-dog and reciprocal-trefoil borders; comb and running-water border bands.

### Clothing & dress

Men's dress is the *shal u shapik*: a matched suit of wide, gathered trousers (*shal*) and a short jacket (*shapik*) cut from local wool or, in the Hewraman, from hand-woven goat-hair cloth, worn over a shirt with long triangular cuff-tails (*lachak*) that are knotted at the wrist. Round the waist goes the *pshtwen*, a wide sash of many metres wound repeatedly and used as a pocket, with a dagger or pistol tucked in on festive occasions. The head is wrapped in the *jamaneh* or *kolaw u desmal* — a stiff cap with a long fringed scarf, usually black-and-white or silk, wound turban-fashion. On the feet, *klash*.

Women's dress is built on a long, full-skirted dress (*kras*) of printed or shot silk and synthetic brocade in strong colours, worn over gathered under-trousers, with a fitted short waistcoat (*salta*, *kawa*) and an open-fronted gown (*kolejeh*). A gold- or coin-hung headscarf arrangement (*klaw* with *desmal*), often stacked over a fez-like cap, carries the family's silver. Sanandaj and Kermanshah urban women's dress historically used heavier silks and *termeh* (Persian patterned silk) for the waistcoat.

### Architecture

The Zagros village is a stacked terrace settlement: flat-roofed, dry-stone or stone-and-mud houses built into the slope so that each house's roof is the street and courtyard of the house above. **Hewraman (Uraman Takht)** is the canonical example, a village of stepped stone terraces on an almost vertical mountain face, built without mortar and roofed with poplar beams, brushwood and beaten earth; it is inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as the Cultural Landscape of Uramanat. Roofs are rolled after rain with a stone cylinder (*bangor*) kept on the roof for the purpose, and double as threshing floor, sleeping place in summer and drying ground for fruit and *keshk*.

House plans are single-storey around a small walled yard, with a stable and fodder store on the lower level and living rooms above; a deep veranda (*eywan*) faces the valley. Interiors are largely unfurnished, the floor covered with felt and kilim and the walls lined with stacked bedding in a *mafrash*. Tented and semi-nomadic groups used the black goat-hair tent (*kon*, *reshmal*) on summer pasture. In the towns, Sanandaj and Kermanshah have Qajar-period courtyard houses with mirrored *orosi* sash windows and carved stucco — the Khan Palace (Asef Vaziri) in Sanandaj is the surviving urban example.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Sanandaj was a centre of inlaid and engraved metalwork under the Ardalan governors, producing engraved brass and tinned-copper trays, ewers, bowls and samovars, and *qalamzani* (chased) work on copper. Everyday cooking is in tinned copper (*mes*), retinned periodically by an itinerant *safar-kar*. The wooden repertoire is domestic and pastoral: turned poplar bowls and butter churns, the *kolawreza* spindle, carved wooden cradles, spoons, and the *tambur* and *daf* frames themselves. Sanandaj is also the historic Iranian centre for *oud* and *tar* making. Household kilims and felts substitute almost entirely for furniture. Village pottery in Kurdistan is unglazed red earthenware for water storage and yoghurt, not a decorated tradition.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Kurdish silver is heavy, granulated and coin-hung: the bride's headdress carries ranks of pierced coins and small silver bells over the *desmal*; the neck carries broad hinged collars and *tumar*-type cylindrical amulet cases holding a written prayer. Cast and filigreed silver bracelets (*bazin*), wide belts with a worked buckle (*pshtwen* fittings for women, in silver rather than cloth), and hollow ankle rings complete the set. Turquoise, carnelian and cheap coloured glass are the standard settings; blue beads and the pierced coin serve against the evil eye. Gold, where affordable, replaces silver in urban Sanandaj and Kermanshah. Henna (*hena*) is applied to the bride's hands and feet on the night before the wedding, the *shev-i hena*. Facial tattooing (*deq*), chin and temple dots in indigo, was common among older rural and tribal women into the mid-twentieth century and has now effectively ended.

## Music & performance

The central Iranian Kurdish instruments are the *daf* — a large frame drum hung with rings on the inside of the hoop, whose modern concert revival across Iran came directly out of Kurdish Sufi practice — and the long-necked lute *tanbur* (*tembûr*), a three-stringed, fretted instrument with a carved mulberry body that is the sacred instrument of the Yarsan of Kermanshah and Guran. Also played: the double-reed *zurna* (*sorna*) with the *dohol* barrel drum as the outdoor wedding pair, the end-blown *shemshal* flute of the shepherds, the *bilur* reed flute, and the *kamancheh* and *ney* in urban settings.

Genres are sharply distinguished. *Siya chamana* is the Hewrami modal vocal form, sung antiphonally by two singers in the mountains. *Beyt* and *heyran* are long narrative and lyric song forms; *lawk* and *lawuk* are strophic epic-narrative songs, often about battles and tribal feuds; *mirishan* and *payizoke* are seasonal and mourning laments. The *maqam-e tanbur* repertoire of the Guran district is the Yarsani liturgical cycle, a fixed body of instrumental and sung pieces of considerable antiquity, transmitted among *kalam*-reciting families. Qadiriyya *tekyes* around Sanandaj and Marivan perform *zikr* with massed *daf* ensembles. *Horeh* is the unaccompanied solo lament-song of the Ilam and Lorestan Kurds. Sanandaj has also been an art-music centre, producing many of Iran's leading classical musicians.

## Dance & theatre

The core form is the **govend** (*halparke*, *rash balak*), an open line dance in which dancers link by interlocked little fingers or shoulders and move in a slow counter-clockwise chain, the leader (*sarchopi*) at the head holding a handkerchief and setting the figure. Dozens of named regional variants exist — *garyan*, *shalakhe*, *sepe*, *chapi*, *fatakeh*, *khan amiri* — distinguished by step count, tempo and shoulder movement, and danced by men and women together, which distinguishes Kurdish dance sharply from its neighbours. The wedding *govend* is accompanied by *zurna* and *dohol*, danced outdoors in the courtyard or on the threshing floor and lasting the length of a day.

Yarsani ritual gathering (*jam*) is ceremony rather than theatre: seated, with *tanbur* and the recitation of *kalam*, ending in ecstatic states. Qadiriyya *zikr* likewise involves rhythmic collective movement, not dance. Iranian Kurdistan has no indigenous puppet or masked-theatre tradition; the Shi'ite *ta'ziyeh* passion play is performed in Kermanshah and among the Feyli and Khorasani Kurds as part of the surrounding Iranian Shi'ite practice, not as a specifically Kurdish form.

## Festivals & rituals

**Newroz** (21 March) is the pivot of the year and the most heavily invested Kurdish festival: bonfires on the hillsides on the eve, new clothes, the *govend* danced in public squares, and — in its modern political register — a mass assertion of Kurdish identity, which has made Newroz gatherings in Mahabad, Sanandaj and Marivan a recurring point of friction with the state. **Chelle** (the winter solstice night, *Shev-e Chelle* / Yalda) is marked with watermelon, pomegranate and poetry. **Pir Shalyar** in Uraman Takht is the distinctive local festival: held in February (and again in May), it commemorates the marriage of the saint Pir Shalyar to the deaf daughter of the ruler of Bukhara, with massed *daf* playing, walnut distribution, sacrifice of animals and a communal stew, drawing visitors from across Kurdistan.

The Islamic calendar overlays this: Ramadan and the two Eids for Sunni Shafi'i Kurds; Muharram and Ashura observance among the Feyli and Khorasani Shi'a. The Yarsan keep their own three-day fast (*Marnow*) in winter and their own festival days.

Life-cycle rites: birth is marked by the *shash-e* sixth-night gathering and an amulet pinned to the cradle. Weddings run three days — the *shev-i hena* henna night for the bride, the transfer of the bride with a mounted or motorised procession and gunfire, and the *govend* in the courtyard; the *shirbaha* (milk price) and dowry of kilims, *jajim* and copper are negotiated between the families beforehand. Funerals involve three days of receiving condolences in a *dewe* tent, with men and women separated, and the singing of *mirishan* lament; the *pirozbayi* visit is repeated at forty days.

## Foodways

Wheat is the staple, eaten as thin bread baked on a domed iron plate (*sac*) or against the wall of a clay oven (*tenur*): *nan-e tiri*, *nan-e saji*, and the thick Kurdish *kulera*. Rice is festival and town food. **Dolma** (*yaprakh*) — vine, cabbage and chard leaves plus hollowed onion, aubergine and pepper stuffed with rice, split peas and lamb, packed upright in one pot — is the dish most claimed as Kurdish. **Kufteh** in the Kurdish form is a very large rice-and-meat ball stuffed with dried fruit, walnut and barberry, poached in broth; the Sanandaj version is a regional signature. Other staples: *ash* (thick herb-and-legume soup), *dou-ineh* / *dogh*ba (yoghurt soups thickened with *keshk*, the dried sour whey balls that are the Zagros preserved protein), *sheli* stew with pulses, *kelaneh* (flatbread stuffed with wild leek, *tareh*, and fried in ghee — a Kermanshah and Sanandaj dish), and *serku shorbaw*.

Dairy is central to the pastoral economy: sheep's-milk yoghurt, *mast*, strained *chakeh*, clarified butter (*roon-e heywani*, the Kermanshah animal ghee being nationally prized), and skin-churned buttermilk. Sumac, dried mint, wild thyme, fenugreek and the mountain herbs gathered in spring (*tareh*, *reeves* rhubarb, *kangar* gundelia) flavour everything. Black tea drunk from a small waisted glass (*estekan*) with a sugar lump held in the teeth is the constant of hospitality. Pork is excluded; Sunni Shafi'i and Shi'a households follow halal slaughter. The *qorbani* sacrifice at Eid al-Adha and at shrine visits distributes meat to neighbours.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kurdish narrative singing preserves a body of verse romances performed by professional and semi-professional singers: **Mem û Zîn**, the tragic romance of Mem and Zin, written down as a masnavi by Ahmad Khani in 1692 and functioning as the Kurdish national epic; **Khaj û Siyamend**, **Zembilfrosh** (the basket-seller who resists a queen's advances), and **Las û Khazal**. Battle *lawk* commemorate tribal engagements and named heroes; the *beyt* is the long sung narrative form in which they are carried, performed by a *beytbêj*.

Iranian Kurdistan produced a distinct written tradition in the Gorani literary language under the Ardalan principality of Sanandaj, with the seventeenth–nineteenth-century poets Mele Perîşan, Khana Qubadi and Mestûre Ardalan — the last a woman poet and the author of a history of the Ardalan house. The Hewrami/Gorani *kalam* corpus of the Yarsan, attributed to Sultan Sahak and his companions, is a sacred oral literature transmitted with the *tanbur*. Sorani Kurdish literary revival in the twentieth century centred on Mahabad and produced Hemin Mukriyani and Hejar; the latter's Kurdish–Persian dictionary and his translation of the Qur'an are standard works.

## Language & religion

Kurdish is a Northwestern Iranian language of the Indo-European family. Iran holds three of its branches: **Sorani** (Central Kurdish) in Kurdistan and West Azerbaijan provinces, written in a modified Perso-Arabic alphabet; **Southern Kurdish** (Kalhuri, Feyli, Laki) in Kermanshah, Ilam and Lorestan; and **Kurmanji** in the northwest and among the Khorasani Kurds. **Gorani/Hewrami**, spoken in Uraman and Guran, is a distinct Zaza–Gorani language rather than a Kurdish dialect, though its speakers identify as Kurds and it served as the Ardalan court literary language.

Religiously the population is split. Sunni Islam of the Shafi'i school predominates in Kurdistan and West Azerbaijan, with active Qadiriyya and Naqshbandi Sufi orders (the Qadiriyya *tekyes* around Marivan and Sanandaj, whose places of worship have been targets of state destruction). Twelver Shi'ism is the religion of the Feyli Kurds of Kermanshah and Ilam and of the Khorasani Kurds; researchers disagree over which denomination holds the national majority, Frye and van Bruinessen arguing for Sunni Shafi'i and Leinonen for Twelver Shi'a. **Yarsanism** (*Ahl-e Haqq*, *Yaresan*) is a distinct syncretic religion of the Guran and Kermanshah region with its own *kalam* scripture, *tanbur* liturgy and jam ceremony, and is likewise subject to state pressure. Small Baháʼí and formerly Jewish Kurdish communities existed, the latter now almost entirely emigrated to Israel.
