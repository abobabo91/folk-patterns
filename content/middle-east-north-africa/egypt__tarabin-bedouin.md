## Overview

The Tarabin Bedouin, also called Tirabin or Al-Tirabin, are an Arab tribe historically associated with the Sinai Peninsula and the Negev. Today they live in the Sinai Peninsula and in Cairo, Ismailia, Giza, Al Sharqia, Suez, Israel’s Negev, Jordan, Saudi Arabia and the Gaza Strip. The source describes them as the largest Bedouin tribe in the Negev and Sinai Peninsula and in Egypt, with over 500,000 people. Their history includes semi-nomadic pastoral and agricultural life, sedentarization, tribal governance, pilgrimage-route protection, trade, traditional medicine and disputes over land. The sources identify Arabic names and Arabic script.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources mention several textile and clothing practices connected with the Tarabin and the Aydi family:

**Embroidered female costumes** — In 1898, Ilya Repin received fifteen female costumes from the “Heritage from the Fragrance of the Land” Museum for use in the Imperial Academy of Arts’ paintings of Palestinian historical scenes. The dresses were embroidered and are described as being held at the Museum of the Academy of Arts in Saint Petersburg.

**Rugs** — Rugs were produced within the Aydi family’s museum and craft tradition. They were among the objects sold to Egypt, Jerusalem and Mecca through the tribal codes of the Aydi family.

**Embroidered ornaments** — Embroidered ornaments were made by members of the community and were also sold to Egypt, Jerusalem and Mecca.

**Jewelry** — Jewelry is listed with rugs and embroidered ornaments among the objects produced for everyday use and trade.

### Clothing & dress

The sources document women’s embroidered dresses. Fifteen female costumes from the “Heritage from the Fragrance of the Land” Museum were given to Ilya Repin in 1898 for the Imperial Academy of Arts’ paintings of Palestinian historical scenes. The sources also state that Dr. Hissan Aydi Tarabin used the museum collection at the end of the 19th century to teach Bedouins to replicate objects and produce items for everyday life. They do not distinguish everyday clothing from ceremonial clothing or provide comparable descriptions of men’s dress.

### Architecture

The sources say that Bedouin settlement changed through sedentarization and urbanization. Before the establishment of Israel, the Negev Bedouins were a semi-nomadic society; by 1931, roughly 17,000 lived in British Mandate Palestine, and after 1948 about 11,000 remained in the Negev. Those who remained were relocated to an area east and southeast of Beersheba called *Siyag*, described in the source as “fence” in Hebrew. Between 1969 and 1989, seven Bedouin townships with developed infrastructure were established. The sources state that these townships were completely urban, while Bedouins preferred rural-type settlements.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Aydi family’s museum collection included amulets, talismans, stones, seeds, plants and manuscripts associated with Avicenna and Abu Al Qasim Al Zahrawi. The collection was used to preserve ancestral knowledge and to teach Bedouins to reproduce objects needed for everyday life.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Jewelry appears in the source as one of the objects produced for use and trade by the Aydi family’s museum tradition.

## Music & performance

They mention tourism-related work such as tour guiding and campground or cafe management. No wedding, funeral, court, pilgrimage or other musical tradition is described.

## Dance & theatre

No ceremonial or entertainment dance context is recorded.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document several religiously and socially significant practices. The Aydi tribe was entrusted with safeguarding the pilgrimage route from Egypt to Mecca in 1263, and this responsibility continued until the opening of the Suez Canal in 1896. Aydi sheikhs also acted as guarantors for contracts concerning Saint Catherine’s Monastery in Sinai. The monastery preserved records from 1592 to 1851 mentioning this role, which the source says was implemented at least since 1540.

The sources further describe healing practices associated with the Aydi branch from the Negev. Negev Bedouins used amulets, talismans, stones, seeds and plants in traditional medicine. They visited maqams associated with physicians, including *maqam Abu Ayada* and *Maqam Al Hajja Hakimah*. The source states that people believed Dr. Hakmah Aydi’s soul helped women carry healthy babies if they prayed by her maqam or performed particular amulet processes.

## Foodways

They mention food supplies in connection with Gaza conflicts and accusations that food convoys or supplies were diverted.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe genealogical traditions rather than named epics or literary genres. Tarabin ancestry is traced to *'Atiya*, who is said to have belonged to the Quraysh tribe and to have migrated to Sinai in the 14th Century. Five sons are associated with different Tarabin sections: Musa'id with the Qusar, Hasbal with the Hasabila, Nab'a with the Naba'at and Sari with the Sarayi'a; the source states that these four sections lived in Sinai. In the Negev, Nijm’s descendants are associated with the Nijmat and Ghawali branches.

The sources also describe oral and family-based transmission of medical, craft and genealogical knowledge through the “Heritage from the Fragrance of the Land” Museum.

## Language & religion

The sources identify the Tarabin as an Arab tribe and provide Arabic forms of several names, including الترابين for Tarabin. The origin account connects *'Atiya* with the Quraysh tribe, to which Mohammed the prophet of Islam belonged. The sources also refer to Islam, pilgrimage to Mecca, Saint Catherine’s Monastery and prayers at maqams.
