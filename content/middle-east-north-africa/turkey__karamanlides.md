## Overview

The Karamanlides (Greek: Καραμανλήδες, romanized: Karamanlídes; Turkish: Karamanlılar), also known as Karamanli Greeks or Karamanlis, are a traditionally Turkish-speaking and Greek Orthodox people native to the region of Karaman in Anatolia. Their exact ethnic origin is disputed. Explanations include descent from Byzantine Greeks who were linguistically Turkified, descent from Christian Turkic soldiers who settled in the region after the Turkic conquests, or a mixture of Anatolian Greeks and Christian Turks. Many were forced to leave Anatolia during the 1923 population exchange between Greece and Turkey. Today, a majority live in Greece and have integrated into Greek society.

## Music & performance

They do state that Karamanlides chanted hymns in Karamanlidika and that their religious practice included books and printed religious texts in Turkish. The Wikipedia article also records that the four Gospels and prayers were printed in Turkish in the region and that public worship among Greeks in a large part of Anatolia was performed in the Turkish tongue. ## Festivals & rituals

They do describe religious and historical circumstances affecting the community. Karamanlides were Greek Orthodox, and their worship could be conducted in Turkish while liturgical reading remained in Greek. During the 1923 population exchange between Greece and Turkey, religion was made the criterion of exchange, and many Karamanlides were forced to leave their homes. Only Papa Eftim I, born Pávlos Karahisarídis, was allowed to remain in Anatolia; he was described as a Turkish nationalist and the creator of the Autocephalous Turkish Orthodox Patriarchate.

## Oral tradition & literature

Karamanlides culture included a substantial written literary production. The language’s literary corpus included books, religious literature, and publications dating from the 18th and 19th centuries. Karamanlides authors were productive in philosophy, religious writings, novels, and historical texts, while lyrical poetry in the late 19th century described confusion associated with being Turkish-speaking and Greek Orthodox. They printed books, particularly the Bible, in Turkish and chanted hymns in Karamanlidika. A fragment of a manuscript written in Karamanli was found in the Cairo Geniza. The sources name writers including Mauromates (1656–1740), Iosepos Moesiodax, and Papa Eftim I. No contemporary literary revival or preservation project is described.

## Language & religion

The Karamanlides used Karamanli Turkish, also called Karamanlídika or Karamanlı Türkçesi. It originally belonged to the family of Anatolian Turkish dialects and followed Turkish grammar, lexicon, and phonetics, while sometimes incorporating regional influences. Its distinctive written form used the Greek alphabet supplemented with Turkish phonetic symbols, unlike official Ottoman Turkish, which used Arabic script. The language is endangered, with very few speakers left in Greece. The Karamanlides were Greek Orthodox. Their culture combined Orthodox Christianity with a Turkish-Anatolian culture, and their religious texts, hymns, prayers, and sometimes public worship used Turkish. The origins of the community remain disputed: explanations include Byzantine Greeks who became linguistically Turkified, Christian Turkic soldiers who adopted Christianity, and a mixture of Anatolian Greeks and Christian Turks. The sources state that there is not enough evidence to prove how the early Karamanlides identified themselves.
