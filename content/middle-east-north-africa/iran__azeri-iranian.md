## Overview

The Azeris are Iran's largest ethnic minority, centered in the volcanic northwest around Lake Urmia and Tabriz, a historic Silk Road city. They speak Azerbaijani, a Turkic language, and sit culturally between the Turkic, Persian, and Caucasian worlds. They are known for two of West Asia's densest craft traditions: the Tabriz carpet workshops and the Shahsavan tribal flatweaves. Their culture blends Shi'a Islam with strong folk traditions in music, poetry, textiles, and seasonal festivals.

## Material culture

Azeri material culture centers on textiles and metalwork, from the knotted carpets of urban Tabriz to the flatweaves of pastoral Shahsavan tribes, alongside distinctive embroidered dress and engraved metalwork.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Carpets and flatweaves dominate, from the fine Tabriz workshop rugs to the figural Shahsavan verni, all patterned with stylized animals, geometry, and motifs carrying symbolic meaning.

- **Tabriz carpet** (*—*) — The archetypal Persian workshop rug knotted on cotton or silk with the Turkish knot, graded by knots-per-7cm, using a hooked *qollab* tool and ranging from medallion-and-corner to pictorial designs.
- **Heriz carpet** (*—*) — Village rugs from east of Tabriz with a large angular geometric medallion, woven on thick mineral-rich wool and among the most durable Iranian carpets.
- **Verni** — A weft-wrapped Shahsavan flatweave with rows of stylized animals in dark madder and indigo against an undyed field, a signature of Qaradağ and Moghan.
- **Kelaghayi** — A square silk headscarf patterned by wax resist and carved wooden blocks, dyed in madder, indigo, saffron and walnut, with colors carrying social meaning for weddings, mourning, and Nowruz.
- **Jajim** — A warp-faced narrow-strip weave sewn edge-to-edge into covers and bedding wraps, patterned in fine stripes and small reciprocal motifs, lighter and more finely spun than Kurdish equivalents.

### Clothing & dress

Village dress centers on layered skirts and fitted velvet jackets for women, silver coin belts, and headscarves; men wear long coats with cartridge-tube decoration and distinctive felt hats marking adult status.

- **Arkhaliq** — A short, fitted velvet jacket worn by women over a tiered, gathered skirt, part of the core village dress of Qaradağ and Moghan.
- **Tuman** — A gathered, tiered skirt worn in multiple layers, sometimes five or more for festive occasions, as part of women's village dress.
- **Chukha** — A knee-length men's coat with cartridge-tube decoration inherited from Caucasian circassian tradition, worn over a shirt and wide trousers.
- **Papaq** — A felt or sheepskin hat; the black astrakhan *papaq* marks adult male status in Ardabil and Tabriz.
- **Kemer** — A silver or coin belt with a large chased buckle, cinching the waist as part of women's dress.

### Architecture

Urban tradition centers on courtyard houses with garden pools, colored-glass windows, and vaulted cellars; the Tabriz Bazaar is the largest covered market of its kind, while villages use thick mud-brick or stone with flat earth roofs.

- **Tabriz Bazaar** (*—*) — A continuous brick-vaulted market complex with domed caravanserai halls, specialist lanes including a carpet *timcheh*, and a UNESCO World Heritage site.
- **Kandovan** (*—*) — A troglodytic village carved into volcanic tuff cones in the Sahand foothills, the region's most distinctive vernacular form.
- **Alachiq** — A domed trellis-and-felt tent lived in by Shahsavan pastoralists, structurally closer to the Central Asian yurt than to Zagros goat-hair tents.
- **Hayat** — The garden pool at the center of urban courtyard houses in Tabriz and Ardabil.
- **Zirzamin** — A vaulted brick cellar in urban houses providing refuge from summer heat.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Tabriz metalwork in repoussé and engraving dominates, alongside khatam micro-mosaic marquetry and carved wooden objects, while village pottery remains simple and utilitarian.

- **Tabriz metalwork** (*—*) — Repoussé and engraved brass and copper trays, ewers, bowls and vessels with *buta* and arabesque fields, with copper-smithing lanes still active in the bazaar.
- **Khatam** — Micro-mosaic marquetry assembling bone, brass wire and colored woods into star-and-polygon lattices, applied to boxes, mirror frames and inlaid doors.
- **Aftabeh** — A metal ewer, often engraved and enameled, used for water service in Azeri households.
- **Korsi** — A low wooden table set over a brazier and draped with quilts for winter warmth, a ubiquitous household item.
- **Ardabil knives** (*—*) — Renowned knives and inlaid metalwork from Ardabil, a signature craft of the region.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silversmithing produces belt plaques, filigree brooches, amulet cases and coin ornaments; bridal sets accumulate gold bracelets and turquoise inlay, while henna and evil-eye beads protect against harm.

- **Tumar** — A tubular or triangular amulet case holding a written prayer, worn at the neck or shoulder against the evil eye.
- **Qolbaq** — Gold bracelets worn as part of bridal jewelry sets, accumulating through a woman's life and passing as dowry.
- **Göz muncuğu** — The evil-eye bead, pinned to infants and animals for protection.
- **Hana yakhdi** — The henna-applying ceremony on the night before a wedding, when henna is applied to a bride's hands and feet.
- **Temple pendants** (*—*) — Filigree or granulated silver ornaments worn hanging from the headdress, part of a woman's daily or festive dress.

## Music & performance

Mugham modal art music and the ashiq bardic tradition dominate, with narrative epics sung to lute accompaniment in teahouses and at weddings, supported by folk instruments like zurna and balaban.

- **Mugham** (*muğam*) — A modal art music of suites built on named modes, sung with instrumental interludes and improvised sections, using texts from classical poets.
- **Ashiq** (*aşıq*) — An itinerant singer-poet performing narrative *dastan* romances to the saz lute in teahouses, weddings and competitive verse duels (*deyishme*).
- **Tar** — The Azerbaijani long-necked, waisted, skin-faced lute played chest-high as part of the mugham ensemble.
- **Zurna** — A double-reed woodwind paired with the naghara drum for outdoor wedding processions and celebratory music.
- **Balaban** — A soft cylindrical double-reed of apricot wood used for lament and interior playing, also called *duduk*.

## Dance & theatre

Social dances performed at weddings and Nowruz emphasize circle or line formations with characteristic hand and foot articulation; ta'ziyeh Shi'a passion plays dominate dramatic tradition.

- **Yallı** — A chain dance of the Aras valley and Nakhchivan–Qaradağ borderlands, led by a dancer with a kerchief and combining walking figures with sharp accelerations.
- **Uzundara** — A slow, lyrical women's dance originally associated with a bride's departure from her home.
- **Vağzalı** — The processional tune and dance that accompanies the bride out of the house at weddings.
- **Ta'ziyeh** — A Shi'a passion play of Karbala performed in Muharram in purpose-built *tekyeh* and village squares, with roles sung in mugham modes.
- **Kosa-kosa** — Comic strolling players who are masked seasonal figures appearing at pre-Nowruz festivities.

## Festivals & rituals

Nowruz vernal equinox celebrations with fire-leaping and the *haft-sin* table dominate the year, followed by Muharram ta'ziyeh and Ramadan, with life-cycle rites marking naming, weddings, and death.

- **Nowruz** (*Novruz*) — The vernal equinox new year on 20–21 March, preceded by four Tuesdays (*Charshanba*) and climaxing in *Chaharshanbe Suri*, the fire-leaping night before the last Wednesday.
- **Haft-sin** — A table set with seven items starting with 'S' for Nowruz, including sprouted wheat or lentil *semeni* and festival pastries like *shekerbura* and *pakhlava*.
- **Chilla** — The forty-day midwinter period marked by *Shab-e Chelleh* on the longest night, when families eat watermelon, pomegranate and nuts.
- **Muharram** — The Shi'a month bringing *ta'ziyeh* plays, nightly *rowzeh* recitations, chest-beating processions and votive food distribution on Ashura.
- **Hana yakhdi** — The henna night before a wedding, followed by the *Vağzalı* bridal procession, preceded by formal match-making visits and an engagement ceremony (*nishan*).

## Foodways

Wheat bread is the staple, with rice for festive occasions; sour flavors dominate in slow-cooked stews, stuffed vegetables, and herb soups, while tea from the samovar accompanies teahouse ashiq performances.

- **Köfte Tabrizi** (*—*) — A signature Tabriz meatball of ground lamb, rice and split peas stuffed with egg, walnuts, barberries and prunes, simmered in saffron-tomato broth.
- **Dolma** (*dolması*) — Stuffed vegetables appearing in many forms: *yarpaq dolması* in grape leaves, *badımcan dolması* in aubergine, peppers and tomato.
- **Abgusht** (*dizi*) — A mutton, chickpea and potato stew pounded at the table, the everyday hot meal of Azeri households.
- **Lavash** — Thin wheat bread, the primary staple alongside thick *barbari* and *sangak* baked on hot pebbles.
- **Doogh** — A salted yoghurt drink served cold at breakfast, accompanying fresh cheese and clotted cream.

## Oral tradition & literature

Bardic *dastan* epics sung by ashiqs form the backbone, especially *Koroghlu* and the *Asli və Kərəm* love-romances, while written literature from medieval Nasimi and Fuzuli to twentieth-century Shahriar continues to shape the language.

- **Koroghlu** — The epic of the bandit-hero and his horse Qırat, sung in cycles by ashiqs across Iranian Azerbaijan and the Caucasus.
- **Asli və Kərəm** — A love-romance of a Muslim boy and Christian girl, part of the ashiq *dastan* repertoire performed at weddings and teahouses.
- **Abbas və Gülgəz** — Another major love-romance in the *dastan* bardic tradition, sung by ashiqs across the region.
- **Bayatı** — A four-line quatrain used for lament, riddle and love song, a short lyric form in ashiq performance.
- **Heydar Babaya Salam** (*—*) — A long Azerbaijani poem by Mohammad-Hossein Shahriar of Tabriz (1954), the most widely recited text in Iranian Azerbaijani and trigger for a vernacular literary revival.

## Language & religion

Azerbaijani is a Turkic language of the Oghuz branch written in Perso-Arabic script, heavily layered with Persian and Arabic; the overwhelming majority are Twelver Shi'a Muslims with rich folk-religious practices centered on shrine visits and votive meals.

- **Azerbaijani** (*—*) — A Turkic language of the Oghuz branch, close to Turkish and Turkmen but with Persian and Arabic lexical layering, written in Perso-Arabic script in Iran.
- **Imamzadeh** — Local tombs and shrine sites visited as part of folk religious practice, centers of devotion and vows.
- **Nazri** — Votive meals distributed at religious occasions and shrine visits, a central folk-religious practice.
- **Esfand** — Rue smoke burned for evil-eye protection, a widespread folk-religious protective practice.
- **Sheikh Safi al-Din shrine** (*—*) — The Safavid shrine complex in Ardabil, a pilgrimage site and legacy of the dynasty that imposed Shi'ism on Iran.
