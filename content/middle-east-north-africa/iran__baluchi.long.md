## Overview

The Baluchi (Baloch) of Iran are a Northwest Iranian-speaking people concentrated in Sistan and Baluchestan Province in the country's arid southeast — the Sarhad highlands around Khash, Zahedan and the Taftan volcano; the Bampur and Sarbaz river valleys; the Makran coast at Chabahar, Konarak and Zarabad; and the Bashakerd mountains to the west. Substantial Baluchi communities also live far to the north in Khorasan, around Torbat-e Jam, Torbat-e Heydarieh and Ferdows, where much of Iran's "Baluch" carpet production has long been based. They number roughly 1.5–2 million in Iran, part of a wider Baloch population of some eight to ten million spread across Pakistan, Afghanistan, Oman and the Gulf. In folk-culture terms they matter disproportionately: a Sunni, largely tribal and until recently pastoral-nomadic population inside a Shi'a, Persian-speaking state, they have kept a distinct weaving corpus, a sung epic tradition, hand-built pottery made without the wheel, and one of the most elaborate embroidery systems in the Iranian world.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Baluch pile rug** (*qali-ye Baluch*) — Small-format wool-on-wool carpets knotted with the asymmetric knot on a woollen (sometimes goat-hair) foundation, with dark goat-hair selvedges and long kilim ends carrying weft-float bands. The palette is the group's signature: aubergine, oxblood and madder red, deep indigo and blue-black, lifted by undyed ivory and by expanses of natural **camel hair**. Unlike the brighter Turkmen and Qashqai work of the same trade routes, Baluch rugs read almost monochrome until seen in raking light.

**Baluch prayer rug** (*jonamaz*) — The most codified Baluch format: a stepped or turreted *mihrab*, frequently over a camel-ground field, with a pair of flanking *panjeh* ("hands") where the worshipper places the palms, and a tree-of-life filling the niche. Many were woven in the Khorasan villages of Torbat-e Jam and Torbat-e Heydarieh and among the Timuri and allied tribes.

**Khorjin and balisht** — *Khorjin* are double saddlebags joined by a flatwoven bridge, closed with loops and slits; *balisht* are single pile-faced cushion or bedding covers with a plainwoven back. Both are the tribal weaver's showpieces, and the pile face is usually finer-knotted than the rugs.

**Sofreh** — The eating and bread cloth, flatwoven in an ivory or undyed ground with narrow weft-substitution and sumak bands framing an open centre. It is functional furniture in a tent: the meal is laid on it, the dough kneaded on it, and it is rolled away afterwards.

**Sistan kilim and flatweave** — In the Hamun basin and the lower Helmand delta, slit-tapestry and weft-float flatweaves are woven in bolder, more open geometry than the pile rugs, alongside grain sacks, salt bags (*namakdan*) and tent bands for the black tent.

**Baluchi doch** (*doch*, embroidery) — Counted-thread silk-floss embroidery worked by women on cotton, so dense that the ground disappears. It is applied in fixed panels to the woman's dress: the chest plastron (*zeeh*), sleeve bands (*astin*), the large pocket panel on the skirt front (*pandol*), and the cuffs of the trousers. A single dress front can take months. Small circular mirrors are couched in with buttonhole stitch (*ayna-doch*, mirror work), a technique shared with Sindh and Kachchh but used here in tighter, more rectilinear compositions.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Mihrab* (prayer niche, often stepped/turreted), *dirakht* (tree of life), *boteh* (the almond/paisley), *mina khani* (repeating floral lattice), *herati*/*mahi* ("fish-in-the-pond" rosette-and-leaf), *panjeh* (the hand), latch-hook diamond, octagonal *gul*, "running dog" reciprocal border, eight-pointed star and rosette, camel-ground reserve panels.

### Clothing & dress

Women wear the *pashk*, a long, loose shift reaching below the knee, over wide trousers (*shalwar*) gathered at the ankle, with a light headscarf (*sarig*) draped over the hair and shoulders. The dress is the canvas for *doch*: the embroidered chest panel, the deep front pocket and the sleeve bands are made separately and stitched on, so a worn-out dress is cut up and the embroidery transferred to a new one — heirloom panels can be two or three garments old. Ceremonial dresses for weddings add mirror work, silver-wrapped thread and sequins. Men wear a long shirt over baggy trousers, both traditionally white or ecru, with a turban (*pag*) wound high and left with a hanging tail, and a shoulder cloth. In the Sarhad and on the coast the turban is tied differently enough to place a man's district. Footwear is the *chappal*, a flat sandal of leather with a wide strap, or palm-fibre sandals in Makran. Elders wear a waistcoat over the shirt; tribal leaders historically added a sword belt and an embroidered cloak.

### Architecture

Two building traditions run in parallel. The nomadic and semi-nomadic Baloch of the Sarhad live in the *gedan*, a black tent woven from goat hair in narrow strips and stretched over poles, its walls raised or dropped with the season; Philip Salzman's fieldwork among the Yarahmadzai of the Sarhad documented the tent as the basic social unit. Settled villages build in mud brick (*khesht*) and pisé, with thick walls, small high openings, and flat roofs on palm-trunk beams; in Sistan, where timber is scarce and the "120-day wind" (*bad-e sad-o-bist ruz*) blows all summer, vaulted and domed mud roofs are the norm and houses are oriented with blank backs to the north wind. On the Makran coast the *kapar* is built entirely of date-palm material: a frame of palm trunks and ribs clad in plaited fronds, raised above the ground for air, cool and replaceable. Coastal towns such as Chabahar also use the *badgir* wind-catcher of the Persian Gulf littoral. Households keep camels, goats and sheep, and the region gives its name to the Baluchi horse.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**Kalporegan pottery**, made in a single village near Saravan, is the outstanding case: hand-built entirely without the wheel by women, coiled and pinched into jars, bowls, water containers and lamps, burnished, then painted freehand with a dark reddish-brown slip in fine geometric bands — dots, combs, zigzags, triangles — and open-fired. Men dig and prepare the clay; the forming and painting are women's work passed mother to daughter, and the craft was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List in 2022. Elsewhere, date-palm and reed basketry dominates domestic equipment on the coast: mats, food covers, winnowing trays and the coiled palm-leaf storage baskets of Makran. Wooden objects include carved spoons, cradles and the *hawan* mortar. Metalwork is mostly silver (below) plus tinned copper trays and cooking pots, and the iron and brass fittings of the *tanur*, camel gear and saddlebag hardware.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Baluchi silver is heavy, flat and chased rather than granulated. The core pieces are the *dorr*, large hoop or pendant earrings so heavy they are supported by a chain over the head; the *pullu*, a nose ornament worn in the left nostril; the *hamail*, a broad chest chain or pectoral; and the amulet case — *ta'wiz* or *tumar*, a tubular or rectangular silver box holding a Qur'anic text, hung on the chest or tied to an infant's arm against the evil eye. Bangles (*kangan*) are worn in stacks; anklets are worn by girls. Carnelian, turquoise and coloured glass are set in raised bezels, and coins are used both as pendants and as portable wealth. Henna (*hana*) is applied to hands and feet at weddings, at Eid and after childbirth; the bride's henna night is a women's event of its own.

## Music & performance

Baluchi music is one of the most studied regional traditions in Iran, largely through the work of Jean During and Sabir Badalkhan. Its central instrument is the *soroz* (also *sorud*), a bowed short-necked lute with sympathetic strings, joined by the *qeychak*/*ghaychak* spike fiddle, the long-necked plucked *tanburag*, the *donali* double reed-flute, the *nal* flute, and the *benju*, a keyed box zither adopted in the twentieth century that has become emblematic of Makran. Percussion is the *doholak* barrel drum and hand-clapping. The repertoire is organised around named genres rather than a maqam system: *zahirok*, the modal melodies of longing that underpin most solo singing; *liko*, short couplets improvised by camel drivers and travellers; *nazenk*, wedding songs; *sepat*, songs of praise; *motk*, women's funeral laments; and *lolo*, lullabies. Epic narrative is sung by the *pahlawan*, a professional bard who accompanies himself and performs the long *sheyr* cycles at gatherings. Music is also therapy: in the *guati* (*gwati*) ceremony of Makran and the Gulf coast, a sufferer possessed by a *gwat* spirit is played into and out of trance over successive nights by a specialist musician-healer, with fixed melodies for each spirit. The related *zar* cult of the Iranian coast, shared with Afro-Iranian communities, uses the *tanbura* lyre and the *dohol*.

## Dance & theatre

There is no scripted theatre tradition; performance is danced and sung. The main collective dance is the *chap*, a men's circle dance built on a step-and-clap pattern that accelerates, performed at weddings and circumcision feasts, with the line of dancers turning around drummers in the centre. *Dammal* (*damali*) is the ecstatic swaying and head-circling dance performed at Sufi shrines and in *guati* healing sessions, where it is a therapeutic act rather than entertainment. On the Makran coast, Afro-Baluchi communities perform *lewa*, a drum-driven dance of African descent shared across the Gulf ports, distinguished by its polyrhythmic drumming and call-and-response singing. Women's dancing is generally performed apart from men, inside the wedding house, to hand-clapping and *doholak*, with songs of the *nazenk* type. Puppetry and mask traditions are not part of the Baluchi repertoire.

## Festivals & rituals

As Sunni Muslims of the Hanafi school, Iranian Baluchis keep the Islamic calendar — *Eid-e Fetr* at the end of Ramadan and *Eid-e Qorban* with the sacrifice and distribution of meat are the year's two poles, and Mawlid al-Nabi is marked at mosques and shrines. Nowruz, at the spring equinox, is observed as it is across Iran, though with less of the Shi'a-inflected ceremony of the interior. The agricultural calendar of Makran turns on *hamin*, the hot season of the date harvest, when families move to the palm groves and the harvest becomes a working festival of shared labour, gifts of fresh dates and evening music. Pilgrimage (*ziyarat*) to local saints' shrines punctuates the year, often combined with a fair.

Life-cycle rites: at birth the call to prayer is whispered in the infant's ear, and an amulet (*ta'wiz*) is tied on for protection; boys are circumcised in a public feast with *chap* dancing. Marriage is negotiated between families with bride-wealth (*lab*), sealed by *nikah*, and celebrated over several days — the henna night for the bride, *nazenk* songs, men's dancing, and the display of the bride's embroidered dress and silver. Death brings immediate burial, women's *motk* laments, a condolence gathering (*pursa*) at which the Qur'an is recited, and memorial meals on the third and fortieth days.

## Foodways

The staples are wheat flatbread, rice and dates. Bread is baked on the wall of a clay *tanur* or as thin discs on a convex iron plate; *kaak* is a hard, long-keeping bread baked among hot stones, made for travel and for the herding camps. Rice is eaten plain as *bat*, or as *dampokhtak*, cooked with meat and fat in a sealed pot so that it steams in its own moisture. The signature meat dishes are **sajji**, a whole lamb or chicken salted, skewered on a green stake and roasted slowly beside an open fire, and pit-cooked lamb stuffed with rice, buried over embers. Preserved meats matter in a desert economy: *tabahag* is salted, sun-dried strips of mutton or goat, and whole carcasses are dried for winter use. On the Makran coast the diet is fish — kingfish, sardine, shark — grilled, fried with turmeric and chilli, curried with tamarind, or salted and dried, showing clear Indian Ocean influence in the spicing. Dates are eaten fresh at *hamin*, pressed into paste for storage, and served with bread and butter at breakfast. Meals are laid out on the woven *sofreh* and eaten by hand from shared dishes. Tea is constant: strong black tea taken very sweet, or green tea with cardamom on the coast, poured into small handleless glasses and drunk with dates or rock sugar. Halal rules are observed strictly; hospitality obliges the slaughter of an animal for an honoured guest.

## Oral tradition & literature

Balochi was an unwritten language until the twentieth century, and its literature is a sung, memorised corpus carried by *pahlawan* bards. The historical core is the cycle of the Rind–Lashari wars of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries — Mir Chakar Rind, Gwaharam Lashari and the thirty-year feud set off by Gohar's camels — which functions as the Baloch national epic and also as a genealogical charter for tribes. Alongside it stand the romances: *Hani and Sheh Mureed*, *Dosten and Shiren*, *Shahdad and Mahnaz*, and the ballads of *Balach* and his revenge. Shorter forms include riddles, proverbs, and the improvised *liko* couplet, which can be composed on the road and answered by another traveller. Sabir Badalkhan's recordings and studies of Makrani bards are the principal scholarly documentation. Written Balochi developed mainly across the border in Pakistan, where Sayad Zahoor Shah Hashmi compiled the first major Balochi dictionary and the Balochi Academy in Quetta has published since 1961; in Iran, Balochi has no official standing in education, and publishing is limited, though cassette and now digital circulation of *zahirok* singing and epic recitation keeps the repertoire alive.

## Language & religion

Balochi belongs to the Northwestern Iranian branch of Indo-Iranian, closer to Kurdish and Zazaki than to Persian. Iranian speakers use Rakhshani (Sarhaddi) dialects in the north around Khash and Zahedan, Sarawani in the centre, and Kechi/Makrani and Coastal dialects in the south; the Brahui, a Dravidian-speaking people long intermarried with the Baloch, are present on the fringes. Written Balochi uses the Perso-Arabic script, with orthographies still not fully standardised. Almost all Iranian Baluchis are Sunni Muslims of the Hanafi school, in contrast with the Shi'a majority of Iran, and Sunni *madrasa* networks are a central institution. Sufi affiliation, particularly Naqshbandi and Qadiri, underpins shrine pilgrimage, *zikr* gatherings and the *dammal* dance. Alongside orthodox practice sit the *guati* spirit-healing ceremony and, on the coast, the *zar* cult — pre-Islamic and Indian Ocean in origin, tolerated as therapy.
