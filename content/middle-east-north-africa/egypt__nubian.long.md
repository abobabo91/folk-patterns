## Overview

The Nubians are indigenous to the Nile valley between the First Cataract at Aswan and the Fourth Cataract near al-Dabbah, a stretch of narrow floodplain and desert escarpment straddling the Egypt–Sudan border. In Egypt they concentrate in Aswan Governorate — Kom Ombo, Nasr al-Nuba, the resettlement villages of "New Nubia" — and in Cairo and Alexandria; in Sudan, in Northern State from Wadi Halfa southward, and in Khartoum. They speak Nubian languages of the Eastern Sudanic branch of Nilo-Saharan, with Arabic as a second language; the main Nile groups from north to south are the *Kenzi* (Kenzi/Mattokki), *Faddicca* and *Halfawi* and *Mahas* (Nobiin), *Sukkot*, and *Danagla* (Andaandi). Their descent from the A-Group, C-Group, Kerma, Napatan and Meroitic cultures makes Nubian material culture one of the longest continuously documented in Africa — and the 1964 flooding of Lower Nubia behind the Aswan High Dam, which displaced roughly 100,000 people, turned that culture into an object of salvage documentation on an unprecedented scale.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Nubian house painting** — the most conspicuous Nubian surface-pattern tradition, and the one that carries the motif vocabulary other media borrow from. Mud-plaster and lime-washed façades, gate surrounds and courtyard walls are painted in brilliant blue, ochre, green and white with geometric registers, and set with pressed-in plates, saucers, mirror shards and pebbles. Because it is renewed at marriages and after pilgrimages, it functions as a household chronicle rather than a fixed decorative scheme.

**Nubian basketry and palm-frond weaving** — coiled and plaited work in date-palm leaf (*birsh* matting, lidded food covers, trays and grain containers), dyed in bands of red, black and green against natural straw. The flat circular food-cover tray, hung on a wall when not in use, is the emblematic Nubian form; its concentric banded rosettes are the closest textile analogue to the painted gate.

**Nubian beadwork** — glass seed-bead netting and strapwork applied to belts, hair ornaments, gourd covers, leather sheaths and bridal accessories, in dense red-white-black-blue counts. Unlike Beja beadwork to the east, Nubian work is usually mounted on tanned hide rather than worked as free-standing netted collars.

**Nubian textile** — Nubians are consumers and finishers rather than large-scale weavers: the defining garments are lengths of undyed or indigo-dyed cotton sheeting, finished at home with embroidery, appliqué, fringing and cowrie or bead attachment. Handloom weaving of coarse cotton and wool blankets and rugs persisted in the Dongola reach.

**Nubian leatherwork** — tanned goat and cow hide made into bridal aprons and belts, amulet cases, sandals, water-skins and quiver-work; the historic association is with the Nubian bow, for which "Land of the Bow" (*ta-seti*) was the Egyptian name for the region. Leather is the usual carrier for beadwork and cowrie attachment.

**Nubian wedding costume** — the assembled bridal ensemble rather than a single garment: a coloured overdress, an embroidered and bead-hung belt or apron, layered silver and amber ornament, henna on hands and feet, and a face-framing veil. Regional signatures distinguish *Kenzi*, *Faddicca* and *Mahas* assemblages.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Nahas* — the evil eye, countered by an outward-facing eye or a palm print; geometric chevron and zigzag registers read as Nile water; date-palm and palm-frond trees flanking a gate; the crescent and star; inset ceramic plate rosettes; stepped triangle and diamond lattice; the hand (*khamsa*) in henna and on plaster; caravan and steamer boats, and later aeroplanes, marking a journey made; Qur'anic inscription bands over the doorway; the lion and the cobra, inherited from Napatan and Meroitic iconography.

### Clothing & dress

Men wear the ankle-length *jallabiya* — in Nubia typically wide-sleeved and worn very white — over loose *sirwal* trousers, with a long wound white turban (*imma*) whose tail is left hanging, a waistcoat or the Sudanese-style overshirt for formal occasions, an embroidered skullcap (*taqiya*) under the turban, and leather sandals. Older men carried a walking staff and a leather amulet case on a neck cord.

Women wear a coloured or printed dress under the *jarjar* — a long, wide, often black, semi-transparent overgarment whose loose cut and trailing hem are distinctively Nubian and not shared with Upper Egyptian *fellahin* dress — with a head-and-body wrap (*tarha*), and a great deal of jewelry worn on the person rather than stored. Girls wore bead-hung plaited hair; married women covered the head fully. Bridal dress adds the embroidered belt or apron, silver anklets, and a shawl heavy with metal ornament. Cowrie shells, the *khamsa* hand, and Qur'anic silver cases are attached to children's clothing as protection.

### Architecture

The Nubian house is a walled compound: a high mud-brick perimeter enclosing a large open courtyard (*hosh*), with rooms opening onto it, a raised exterior bench (*mastaba*) beside the entrance, and a single monumental gate — ornately decorated, and preferably facing the Nile. The gate is the architectural centre of the tradition: stucco in bright colour, inset crockery and mirror, painted palms, boats, crescents and eyes, and often the household's name or a Qur'anic line. Roofs are flat, of palm trunks and matting over beaten mud, or vaulted.

Nubians invented the **Nubian vault** — a leaning catenary vault of unfired mud brick laid without centring, against an end wall. It allows a fired-brick- and timber-free roof span and is the single Nubian structural contribution with worldwide currency, popularized for modern earth building by the Egyptian architect Hassan Fathy. Resettlement after 1964 replaced these compounds with government-built rows at Kom Ombo and New Halfa; the painted gate has been the most deliberately reconstructed element, along with revivals on the Aswan-area tourist villages of Elephantine and Gharb Soheil.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**Nubian pottery** is handbuilt and open-fired: water jars (*zir*) set in wooden stands or wall niches to cool by evaporation, cooking vessels, incense burners and coffee pots, in burnished red-brown or black with incised and impressed banded decoration — a technique continuous with the Kerma and Meroitic wares excavated in the same reach of the river. Household equipment includes the *saqiya* waterwheel pot, palm-rib bedsteads (*angareeb*) strung with hide or rope, wooden grain mortars, carved bowls and ladles, and the *jabana*-style long-spouted coffee pot. Metalwork is dominated by silver ornament and amulet cases; brass and copper appear in trays, incense burners and coffee sets. Ostrich-feather fans, palm-leaf food covers and gourd containers complete the domestic inventory.

### Jewelry & body adornment

**Nubian silverwork** supplies the bulk of ornament: hoop and crescent earrings, nose rings, broad hinged bangles, heavy anklets, filigree pendants, and the cylindrical or rectangular **Nubian amulet** case (*hijab*) holding a written Qur'anic text, worn at the neck or upper arm against the evil eye. Gold — the metal of the Nubian mines the Egyptian pharaohs fought over — appears in wedding wealth; amber, carnelian, coral and glass beads, coral-and-silver necklaces, and cowrie shells are strung between metal elements. Henna patterns the hands and feet of brides, and the fingertips and hair of men before feasts. **Nubian scarification** was a group marker: Mahas men and women bore three vertical cuts on each cheek, the Danagla the same marks on the temples — a practice abandoned by younger generations. **Nubian tattoo** in indigo — lip, gum and chin marks, and dotted rows on hands and arms — belongs to the same abandoned repertoire.

## Music & performance

Nubian music is built on a pentatonic scale and dominated by the frame drum and the plucked lyre. The five-string bowl lyre — *kisir* in Nubia, cognate with the Sudanese *tanbūra* and the Egyptian *simsimiyya* — carries the melodic line; the large single-headed frame drum (*daff*, *tar*) and pairs of hand drums carry the cycle, reinforced by handclaps, finger cymbals and the women's *zaghrouta* trill. Songs are overwhelmingly antiphonal: a lead voice and a responding chorus over a clapped ostinato, performed by a standing, swaying line. Genres are tied to occasion — wedding songs sung through the night, Nile boat and waterwheel work songs, palm-harvest songs, lament, *dhikr* and *madīḥ* (praise of the Prophet) led by Sufi brotherhoods, and *aragid* dance songs.

Displacement turned this repertoire into an explicitly political music. The Aswan High Dam resettlements produced a body of songs of loss and of the drowned villages, and the twentieth-century Nubian revival carried them out of the village into recording: Hamza El Din, the Wadi Halfa–born *ʿūd* and lyre player, brought Nubian song to international audiences from the 1960s; Ali Hassan Kuban and Mohamed Mounir fused it with brass, electric bass and Egyptian pop in Cairo. The Cairo Nubian clubs (*al-andiya al-nubiya*), founded by migrant labourers by village of origin, remain the principal urban performance venue.

## Dance & theatre

Nubian dance is communal, lined and largely non-narrative: men and women form facing or parallel rows, advance and retreat with a shoulder-shimmying, small-stepped gait, and turn in place, with soloists breaking out to improvise before the line. The generic Nubian term *aragid* covers this line dance; at weddings it runs for hours and is the frame within which the bride and groom are displayed. Stick and sword flourishes appear in men's celebratory dancing, and women's dancing emphasizes hair-tossing and the trilling ululation rather than the torso articulation of Egyptian *raqs baladi*. The *zār* spirit-possession ceremony, shared with Upper Egypt and Sudan, uses drumming and the *tanbūra* lyre to bring on trance for healing. There is no indigenous masked or puppet theatre; dramatic performance in Nubian is a twentieth-century development, staged in the Cairo clubs and Aswan cultural associations as a vehicle for the language.

## Festivals & rituals

The calendar is Islamic: *Ramadan* with its nightly communal meals, *ʿId al-Fitr* and *ʿId al-Adha*, the Prophet's birthday (*Mawlid al-Nabi*), and local saints' *mawlids* at Sufi shrines, where *dhikr*, *madīḥ* singing and processions dominate. Nubians also keep the Nile's own rhythms and the Coptic-calendar spring festival of *Sham al-Nassim*, and the palm-date harvest is its own occasion for song and shared labour.

Life-cycle rites are the strongest surviving ritual complex. **Sebu**, the birth ceremony held on the seventh day, is the named Nubian rite: the infant is carried around the house, grain, salt and sweets are scattered, the name is given, and noise is made over the child's head. Weddings run for several days — henna night for the bride, processions to the Nile where the couple's hands are washed in the river, gate-painting and courtyard feasting, the long night of *aragid* dancing, and gift and ornament display; the bride's silver, amber and embroidered belt are the visible statement of the transfer. Circumcision is marked with processions and song. Funerals follow Islamic rites with three days of condolence gathering in the courtyard, followed by the fortieth-day commemoration. Protective ritual runs alongside all of it: Qur'anic amulets, the *khamsa* hand, indigo and palm placed against the evil eye, and the *zār* ceremony for affliction that ordinary medicine has not answered.

## Foodways

The staples are sorghum, millet, wheat and dates, with the Nile supplying fish and the compound supplying goat, sheep and chicken. The defining bread is *kisra* — a thin, slightly sour fermented sorghum pancake poured onto a hot griddle — eaten with stews; wheat flatbreads and the fermented sourdough *ʿaysh shamsi* ("sun bread") of Upper Egypt also appear. Stews are built on dried okra powder (*weika* or *bamia*), dried meat (*sharmout*), tomato and onion, and the sesame paste and yoghurt sauces of the Sudanese *mullah* family; *ful mudammas* (stewed fava beans), lentils and the Egyptian greens soup *mulukhiyya* are everyday. Nile fish is grilled, salted or dried. Dates are eaten fresh, dried, pressed into cakes, and fed to livestock; palm dates and their by-products are the region's cash crop. Drinks mark hospitality: spiced tea, hibiscus infusion (*karkade*), and above all *jabana* — coffee roasted, pounded with ginger, cardamom or cloves in front of the guest, brewed in a long-necked clay pot and poured through a palm-fibre filter into small cups, in successive rounds that structure the whole visit. Wedding and *ʿId* food centres on slaughtered sheep, rice dishes, and sweets of date, sesame and clarified butter. Alcohol is prohibited, though millet beer survived in the pre-Islamic and syncretic margins.

## Oral tradition & literature

Nubian oral tradition is carried in the *ahaji* — evening folktales told in Nobiin and Andaandi by older women in the courtyard, peopled by river spirits, crocodiles, the *dogir* ogress, clever animals and the Nile itself as an agent. Narrative songs recount migrations, floods, feuds and the deeds of local strongmen, and a dense body of proverbs and riddles regulates speech at weddings and in dispute. There is no single monumental epic; the corpus is short-form, occasional and sung as often as spoken.

Nubian is also one of the oldest written languages of Africa outside Afroasiatic: **Old Nubian**, attested from the eighth century AD, was written in a Coptic-derived alphabet with three letters taken from Meroitic, and survives in biblical translations, liturgy, letters and legal documents from Qasr Ibrim and elsewhere. That script is the model for the modern revival, in which Nobiin is again written in the Old Nubian alphabet by language activists in Aswan, Khartoum and the diaspora. Displacement produced a literature of its own: the Egyptian Nubian novelists Idris Ali (*Dongola: A Novel of Nubia*) and Haggag Hassan Oddoul (*Nights of Musk*) write in Arabic about the drowned villages, and poetry, song and memoir collection in the Nubian clubs continues as explicit language preservation.

## Language & religion

Nubian languages belong to the Eastern Sudanic branch of Nilo-Saharan: **Nobiin** (Faddicca, Halfawi, Mahas), **Kenzi/Mattokki** and **Andaandi** (Dongolawi) along the Nile, with the related Midob of North Darfur, Birgid of Central Africa Republic-bordering Darfur, and the Hill Nubian languages of Kordofan. Nobiin and Kenzi-Dongolawi are not mutually intelligible. Old Nubian, written in a Coptic-based alphabet, is attested from the eighth century; Arabic is the language of school, state and increasingly of the home, and transmission of Nubian is now the central concern of Nubian associations. Nubians are Sunni Muslims, converted gradually from Christianity between the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries largely through Sufi preaching; Sufi brotherhood practice — *dhikr*, shrine *mawlids*, *madīḥ* singing, written amulets — remains the devotional idiom, coexisting with the *zār* and evil-eye complex. Six centuries of Nubian Christianity under Nobatia, Makuria and Alodia, and before that 2,500 years of Amun worship at Jebel Barkal, are visible archaeologically rather than in living practice.
