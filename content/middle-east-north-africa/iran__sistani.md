## Overview

The Sistani Persians, also known as the Sistanis, Sajestani, and historically Sagzi, are a branch of Persians who mainly live in Iranian Sistan in southeastern Iran. They inhabit the northern parts of Sistan and Baluchistan province, while many have migrated in recent decades to other parts of Iran, including Tehran and Golestan. Sistanis speak Sistani, a dialect continuum of the Persian language. Sistani belongs to the Southwestern Iranian branch of the Indo-Iranian group of Indo-European languages. The sources describe Sistani culture through its language, clothing, handicrafts, cooking, religious history, and literary associations with Sistan, Zabulistan, and the mythological hero Rostam.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Black embroidery* is a type of needlework used on the collars and cuffs of Sistani women's everyday clothing. The source describes it as a characteristic feature of this clothing, which consists of a shirt and loose pants.

*Sistani clothing* includes men's and women's garments shaped by the culture and geography of Sistan and by long coexistence with nature.

### Clothing & dress

Men's clothing mainly includes a girdle, hood, shirt, and pants. The local-dialect name for the turban or head covering is *Lengote*, and it is mostly white. Men's shirts are long and knee-length, occurring in pleated, saree, and torn models. Trousers may be plain or pleated; in the local dialect they are called *Tamo* or *Tumon*. Shirts are called *Penr*, while the cracked type is called *Chel Treez*. This shirt has a sleeve-down loop consisting of at least 34 cracks. Men also wear a vest over the shirt called *Jalezqa*. Silk embroidery is associated with the clothing of wealthy people.

Women's clothing is described as long, loose, and simple, with both everyday garments and clothing for holidays and celebrations. Everyday dress includes a shirt and loose pants, with the shirt extending below the knee and pleated around the waist. Women wear a rectangular headscarf. Festive clothing includes pleated pants, a knee-length shirt with two side slits, and a pleated skirt called *temu*. The skirt is knee-length and its width reaches 9 meters. The sources also name two-necked shirts, Tajik shirts, lace, and chador as elements of Sistani women's local clothing.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources mention Sistan handicrafts as traditional handicrafts of Iran with a history in the region dating back centuries. Artisans have historically produced handwoven and decorative items using techniques passed down through generations. No specific ceramic forms, metalwork, wooden objects, tools, or household goods are identified.

## Music & performance

The sources identify a religious hymn in the Sistani dialect that is attributed to the end of the Sassanid period. The song *Karkuye Bonfire* is described as a religious hymn of the Zoroastrians of Sistan and as something sung loudly while lighting bonfires.

Sistan also has a strong historical connection with Zoroastrianism. During Sassanid times, Lake Hamun was one of two pilgrimage sites for followers of that religion. In Zoroastrian tradition, the lake is the keeper of Zoroaster's seed; before the final renovation of the world, three maidens enter the lake and each gives birth to a *saoshyan*, described in the source as a saviour of mankind.

## Festivals & rituals

They do, however, describe religious practices and traditions connected with Zoroastrianism. During Sassanid times, Lake Hamun was one of two pilgrimage sites for followers of that religion. Zoroastrian tradition presents the lake as the keeper of Zoroaster's seed and associates it with the final renovation of the world, when three maidens enter the lake and give birth to the *saoshyans*, the saviours of mankind.

The clothing source distinguishes ordinary dress from clothing prepared for holidays and celebrations. Festive women's clothing includes pleated pants, a knee-length shirt with two slits, and a pleated skirt called *temu*.

## Foodways

Sistani cooking is described as a style and method of cooking among the Sistani people. Although it has been influenced over the centuries by the cuisines of various cultures, the source characterizes it as unique and diverse. The cuisines of neighboring cultures have also been influenced by Sistan's cuisine.

The history of the Sistani cooking method is described as old, like the history of the Sistan people. Its preparation and taste are said to resemble Eastern cooking, including its use of spices, while remaining distinct in its historical roots and character.

## Oral tradition & literature

In the *Shahnameh*, Sistan is also called Zabulistan, after the region in the eastern part of present-day Afghanistan. Ferdowsi's epic describes Zabulistan as the homeland of the mythological hero Rostam. These are the only named literary and heroic traditions in the sources.

The sources also state that Abureyhan al-Biruni, in *Al-Saidna*, gave some words from the old Sistani language. Many Sistani words are said not to have changed much since a thousand years ago.

## Language & religion

Sistani is a dialect continuum of Persian spoken by Sistani people in Iranian Sistan. It belongs to the Southwestern Iranian branch of the Indo-Iranian group of Indo-European languages. The Sistani dialect is described as having more lexical and grammatical kinship with existing and past Khorasani than with the dead dialects of Mawara Al Nahri and current Tajik. Lexicographers have described it as one of the four abandoned Persian dialects. The original language, probably Segzi, is described as extinct, with only its dialect remaining.

The sources associate Sistan historically with Zoroastrianism. Its people were Zoroastrian, and Lake Hamun was a pilgrimage site during Sassanid times. The sources also mention Morteza Motahhari, a Twelver Shia Muslim cleric who was a Sistani from Fariman.
