## Overview

The Laz, or Lazi, are a Kartvelian ethnic group native to the South Caucasus, principally living in the Black Sea coastal regions of Turkey and Georgia. Their cultural region is known as *Laziǩa*, *Lazistan*, *Lazeti* or *Lazona*, extending through parts of northeastern Turkey and southwestern Georgia. Lazistan consists of narrow, rugged valleys running northward from the Pontic Alps, or *Anadolu Dağları*, toward the southern Black Sea. Parts of Adjara, Artvin, Rize and Trabzon are usually included. Turkey has 103,900 ethnic Laz, although only around 20,000 speak Laz. Laz belongs to the South Caucasian language family. Its oral literature, distinctive dance, instruments, festival traditions and clothing are central to the folk culture described in the available sources.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The traditional Laz men’s costume includes a peculiar bandanalike kerchief covering the head above the eyes, knotted at the side and hanging toward the shoulder and upper back. Men wore a snug-fitting jacket of coarse brown homespun with loose sleeves, baggy dark brown woolen trousers, and slim, knee-high leather boots. Laz men were also described as carrying numerous weapons and objects, including a rifle, pistol, powder horn, cartridge belts, a dagger and a coil of rope.

The women’s costume was similar to the wide-skirted princess gown found throughout Georgia. It included a kerchief similar to that worn by men and a rich scarf tied around the hips. The sources do not distinguish separate everyday and ceremonial forms of Laz dress.

### Architecture

They do mention ruined churches in present-day Rize and Artvin districts, including *Jibistasi* in Ardeşen, *Makriali* or *Noghedi* in Hopa, and *Pironity* in Arhavi.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that historical Laz industries included smelting, described as an ancient activity, and cutting timber for shipbuilding. Laz men were also described as making excellent homemade rifles.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The available clothing description mentions only the kerchiefs worn by men and women and the women’s rich scarf tied around the hips.

## Music & performance

The Laz national instruments listed in the sources are *guda*, a bagpipe; *kemenche*, a spike fiddle; *zurna*, an oboe; and *doli*, a drum. These instruments form part of the documented Laz musical tradition.

Kâzım Koyuncu became the first Laz musician to gain mainstream success in 1998. In the 1990s and 2000s, he attained significant popularity in Turkey and toured Georgia. He was a folk-rock musician, an activist for the Laz people, and a cultural hero. He died of cancer in 2005.

The sources also describe recent cultural activity intended to revitalize Laz language, education and tradition. The Laz Cultural Institute was founded in 1993, and the Laz Culture Association was founded in 2008. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription or museum catalogue record supplied with this atlas concerns Laz music.

## Dance & theatre

Laz folk dances are called the *Horon* dance of the Black Sea. The sources describe Horon as originally connected with pagan worship before becoming a sacred ritual dance. There are many different types in different regions. Horon is related to dances performed by the Ajarians, known as *Khorumi*.

Some forms are solemn and precise, performed by lines of men with carefully executed footwork. Others are extremely vigorous: men dance erect with linked hands, make short rapid movements with their feet, and punctuate the movement by dropping into a crouch. Women’s dances are described as graceful but swifter than dances encountered in Georgia. In Greece, such dances remain associated with Pontic Greeks who emigrated from the region after 1922.

During the celebration of *Kolkhoba*, theater performances are followed by a variety of activities.

## Festivals & rituals

*Kolkhoba* is described as an ancient Laz festival held at the end of August or the beginning of September in Sarpi village, Khelvachauri District. It revives the former lifestyle of Lazeti residents and forms of human relationship associated with the times of ancient Greece and Colchis. The festival is connected in the sources with the Argonauts’ journey to Colchis. Theater performances and other activities take place during the celebration, which is considered one of the main public festivals.

The sources also record religious commemorations. Georgian Orthodox believers commemorate the Three Hundred Laz Martyrs, who are credited with sacrificing their lives rather than giving up their identity under Ottoman persecution. Laz people were converted to Christianity in the 5th century, while many later converted to Sunni Islam during the Ottoman period.

## Foodways

Historically, Lazistan was known for producing hazelnuts. The traditional Laz economy included agriculture in steep mountain regions, the breeding of sheep, goats and cattle, orchard tending, beekeeping and hunting. The sources state that the Laz practised agriculture involving rice, maize, tobacco and fruit-trees.

## Oral tradition & literature

Laz folk literature has been transmitted orally and has not been systematically recorded. The Laz language does not have a written history, and Turkish and Georgian have served as the main literary languages for the Laz people.

The ancient region of Colchis is associated with the Greek myth of the Golden Fleece. In this tale, Jason and the Argonauts stole the Golden Fleece from King Aeetes with the help of his daughter Medea. The sources present Colchis as extending from West Georgia to northeast Turkey and connect the tale with the historical image of the region.

The first attempts to establish a distinct Laz cultural identity and create a literary language using the Arabic alphabet were made by Faik Efendisi in the 1870s. He was imprisoned by Ottoman authorities, and most of his works were destroyed. Written Laz literature based on the Laz script emerged in Soviet Georgia during the 1930s, but an official standard form was never established. The poet Mustafa Baniṣi spearheaded that short-lived movement. Later native poets in Turkey included Raşid Hilmi Pehlivanoğlu, a figure associated with Rize district.

## Language & religion

Laz, also called Lazuri, is a Kartvelian or South Caucasian language spoken on the southeastern shore of the Black Sea. It is closely related to Mingrelian and forms the Zan branch with it. The language has no official status in Turkey or Georgia and has no written standard. It is used mainly for familiar and casual interaction; Laz speakers use Turkish or Georgian for literary, business and other purposes.

Laz is written in Mkhedruli script and in an extension of the Turkish alphabet. In Turkey, a script based on the Turkish alphabet was created in 1984. The textbook *Nana-nena* or “Mother tongue” was published in 1991 using both Latin and Georgian alphabets, and the first Laz–Turkish dictionary was published in 1999. Speaking Laz was forbidden in Turkey between 1980 and 1991.

Andrew the Apostle is said to have built a church in Lazica in the first century AD. Christianity was declared the state religion of Lazica by Gubazes I of Lazica, and the Lazes were converted to Christianity in the 5th century. Most Laz subsequently converted to Sunni Islam. A few Christian Laz in Adjara have reconverted to Christianity.
