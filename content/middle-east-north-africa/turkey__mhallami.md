## Overview

The Mhallami, also known as Mardelli or Mahallami, are an Arab ethnic group traditionally associated with Mardin and the surrounding region of southeastern Turkey, particularly the district of Midyat and the Tur Abdin region. Their wider history is connected with Mesopotamia and with migration to Lebanon, Germany, the Netherlands, Denmark, Sweden, and other European countries. Estimates of their present number vary considerably, from 150,000 to around 1 million. They speak Mhallami, a distinct dialect of North Mesopotamian Arabic, also described as Qeltu, with heavy Turkish, Kurdish, and Aramaic influence. Their history includes competing accounts of Arab, Assyrian, and Kurdish origins, as well as migration, Islamization, clan organization, and the preservation of community identity across several countries.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The Mhallami are associated with settlements in and around Mardin, Midyat, and Tur Abdin. Some Mhallami continued to inhabit Assyrian villages in southeastern Turkey, including Gercüş and İçören. A community of Mardelli was reported in the Tur Abdin region in the late 90s, and some members were associated with maintenance work at Mor Gabriel Monastery.

## Music & performance

The sources identify the dance of *Kathfothe* (Syriac: ܪܩܕܐ ܕܐ ܟܬܦܬܐ) as a cultural element shared by some Mhallami with Assyrians in Turkey. It is mentioned in connection with Mhallami who identify with Assyrian roots and with the broader traditions of the region.

## Dance & theatre

The named Mhallami-associated dance in the sources is *Kathfothe*, written in Syriac as ܪܩܕܐ ܕܐ ܟܬܦܬܐ. The dance is presented as a tradition shared with Assyrians in Turkey and as one of the cultural elements connecting Mhallami communities with Assyrian traditions. No Mhallami theatrical, dramatic, shadow-puppet, mask, or storytelling performance tradition is documented.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe historical processes affecting community life. Mhallami migration to Lebanon began in the 1920s, and the tightening of clan structures was linked to the community’s lack of citizenship and need for social survival. The Lebanese Civil War produced further displacement, with refugees moving to Germany and other European countries from 1976 onward. The 1st International Mhallami Symposium was held in 2008 to discuss Mhallami origins and bring the community together. In 2015, Mehmet Ali Aslan became the first Mhallami elected to the Turkish Parliament.

## Oral tradition & literature

The principal traditional sources mentioned in the article are genealogical records known as *ansab*, or pedigrees. In accounts of Mhallami lineage, these genealogical traditions connect the group with Rabia ibn Nizar. Nizar is described as the ancestor of the tribal branches Rabia and Mudar, each of which included important tribes. The article also records traditions linking the name Mhallami with Muhallem, described in Arab genealogical traditions as a descendant of Nizar.

The sources describe competing origin traditions rather than a single epic or literary corpus. Some Mhallami traditions connect the group with Banu Hilal, while historical research presents Rabi'ah tribes and possibly Banu Shayban as more probable Arab connections. Other theories connect the group with Assyrian history, Tur Abdin, Beth Ahlam, and the Ahlamu-Aramean tribe.

## Language & religion

Mhallami is a distinct dialect of North Mesopotamian Arabic, also called Qeltu in the source, with heavy Turkish, Kurdish, and Aramaic influence. Many Mhallami retain Arabic names and use them for themselves, their families, or their clans. The article also notes that many Mhallami speak a unique dialect that differs from other dialects.

Most Mhallami are Sunni Muslims, while a small Syriac Christian minority is also present. Accounts of the group’s history include theories of Assyrian roots and descriptions of Syriac Christians who converted to Islam under pressure and later called themselves Mhalmoye. The sources identify persecution, divisions within Syriac-rite churches, and conflict before the Treaty of Nasuh Pasha among explanations associated with Islamization. They also describe continuing cultural connections with Assyrians in Turkey.
