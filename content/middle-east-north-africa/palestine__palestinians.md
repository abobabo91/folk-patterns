## Overview

Palestinians are an Arab national group native to the region of Palestine, sharing close linguistic and cultural ties with other Levantine Arabs. Half the world's Palestinian population lives in former Mandatory Palestine, with major diaspora communities in Jordan, Syria, Lebanon, and Saudi Arabia. The documented material culture focuses overwhelmingly on village women's embroidered dress and household furnishings. Palestinian identity as a self-conscious national category emerged in writing from 1898 and crystallized through newspapers and print after 1908.

## Material culture

Palestinian village material culture is dominated by embroidered textiles, particularly women's layered dress with named embroidered panels and machine-worked variations.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Embroidered cotton and linen textiles organize pattern into named panels rather than continuous fields, with cross-stitch in magenta and pink on white.

- **Thob** — The village woman's dress in white or indigo cotton, organized by named panels (chest, yoke, sleeves, cuffs, furrows, side panels) each worked in cross-stitch with deep magenta and pink thread.
- **Machine-worked thob** — Later examples seamed and embroidered on a sewing machine with wave or snail-like motifs in red thread along the same traditional panels.
- **Jellaya** — The festive dress of Beit Dajan in indigo-blue or black linen embroidered over its entire surface, worn at weddings with embroidered black linen trousers and the most costly item in a bride's trousseau.
- **Linen underdress** — Cream handwoven linen worn beneath the thob with red and blue selvedge stripes, a lightly embroidered chest panel, and a collar of red-and-white Syrian silk ikat.
- **Embroidered cushion covers** — Household furnishings in cotton embroidered with silk thread, finer examples in navy silk cross-stitched in multiple colours with ordered central panel motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress is layered and colour-coded by garment, with embroidery fixed in named positions; men's documented outerwear is a striped silk cape.

- **Sirwal** — Black linen trousers embroidered down the legs, worn with the jellaya at weddings and celebrations.
- **Abayeh** (*abayeh (also abay)*) — A square cape in green-and-white striped silk twill with seams trimmed in braided cotton wrapped in metal foil.
- **Qabbeh chest panel** (*qabbeh*) — The embroidered chest panel of the thob, worked with the densest and most vividly coloured cotton thread.

### Architecture

The documented architecture is monumental and religious, centred on a single structure and its theological significance.

- **Al-Aqsa Mosque** (*al-Masjid al-ʾAqṣā*) — The Farthest Mosque on the Temple Mount in Jerusalem's Old City, where Islamic tradition holds Muhammad was transported during the Night Journey and led prayers before being directed towards the Kaaba.
- **Haram esh-Sharif** (*Haram esh-Sharif (al-Aqsa Compound)*) — The sacred compound on the Temple Mount, known to Muslims as the site of Muhammad's Night Journey.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources do not document Palestinian ceramics, metalwork or wooden household objects; only decorative metal trim appears in the record.

- **Metal-foil-wrapped braid** — Braided cotton wrapped in metal foil used to trim the seams of the man's abayeh.

## Festivals & rituals

Marriage is the only life-cycle rite documented, known through bridal dress; secular commemorations mark the 1948 establishment of Israel and Palestinian displacements.

- **Bridal trousseau** — In Beit Dajan, a bride's trousseau comprised many dresses, with the embroidered jellaya being the most costly and worn at the moment the bride first appeared in public.
- **International Day of Solidarity with the Palestinian People** — Observed annually by the United Nations since 1978.

## Oral tradition & literature

Palestinian identity emerged through print literature from 1898, articulated by intellectuals and newspapers that defined a shared Palestinian nation and society.

- **Khalil Beidas** — Introduced the term Filasṭīnī in an 1898 Arabic translation of a Russian work on the Holy Land.
- **Palestinian newspapers (1908–1914)** — After the Young Turk Revolution eased Ottoman press censorship, dozens of newspapers including Al-Quds, Al-Munadi, Falastin, Al-Karmil and Al-Nafir used the word Filastini more than 170 times and wrote of Palestinian society, nation, and people.
- **Falastin newspaper** (*Falastin*) — A Christian-owned newspaper founded in 1911 that addressed its readers as Palestinians from the outset.

## Language & religion

Palestinians are Arabic-speaking and religiously mixed — Muslim, Christian, Samaritan, and Druze — unified by Arab national identity defined by language and culture.

- **Levantine Arabic** — Palestinians speak Arabic and share close linguistic ties with other Levantine Arabs; dialects are not described in the sources.
- **Palestinian National Charter definition** — Defines Palestinians as Arab nationals resident in Palestine until 1947, taking in Arabic-speaking Muslims, Christians, Samaritans, and Druze.

## Glossary

- *thob* — Village woman's dress, cut with long narrow or large pointed sleeves, embroidered in cross-stitch
- *jellaya* — Festive dress of Beit Dajan in indigo or black linen, fully embroidered, worn at weddings
- *sirwal* — Black linen trousers embroidered down the legs, worn with the jellaya
- *qabbeh* — The chest panel of the thob, worked with the densest and most vividly coloured embroidery
- *ikat* — Resist-dyed fabric, used for Syrian silk collars on Palestinian underdresses
- *al-Masjid al-ʾAqṣā* — Al-Aqsa Mosque, the Farthest Mosque on the Temple Mount in Jerusalem
- *Haram esh-Sharif* — The sacred compound on the Temple Mount, also called the al-Aqsa Compound
- *Filasṭīnī* — Palestinian; the term introduced in written Arabic in 1898
