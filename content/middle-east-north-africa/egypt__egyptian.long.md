## Overview

Egyptians (*Maṣriyyīn*, مَصريين) are the native people of the Nile Valley — the narrow cultivable strip running from the First Cataract at Aswan to the Mediterranean delta, hemmed by the Eastern and Western deserts. They number roughly 105 million, some 99.6% of Egypt's population, with density along the river in riverine governorates exceeding 1,900 persons per km². Their daily language is a continuum of Egyptian Arabic (*Masri*) in the north and Sa'idi Arabic in Upper Egypt, both Semitic within Afroasiatic; Coptic, the last stage of the ancient Egyptian language, survives as the liturgical tongue of the Coptic Orthodox Church. In folk-culture terms Egypt is unusual for the depth of its documented continuity: the *fellahin* still reckon agricultural seasons by months bearing their pharaonic names, and craft lineages in Cairo, Akhmim, Fustat and Siwa carry techniques traceable through Coptic, Fatimid and Mamluk workshops.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Coptic textile** — Wool-and-linen tapestry-woven roundels (*orbiculi*), squares (*tabulae*) and clavus bands set into undyed linen tunics, produced in Egyptian workshops from the 3rd century onward and preserved in the thousands by desert burial. Motifs move from Graeco-Roman vine, Nereid and hunter scenes to flattened, frontal Christian imagery — the only large surviving corpus of late-antique cloth anywhere.

**Coptic tunic** — The garment itself: a single-piece linen *tunica* woven to shape on the loom with sleeves, decorated in situ rather than appliquéd, distinguishing Egyptian practice from the cut-and-sewn Roman norm.

**Khayamiya / tentmaker appliqué** — Hand-stitched cotton appliqué panels made in the *Sharia al-Khayamiya* (Street of the Tentmakers) south of Bab Zuwayla in Cairo, originally for the great ceremonial tents (*suradiq*) erected for weddings, funerals and Ramadan. Layered cut-cloth arabesque, calligraphic cartouches and lotus palmettes are sewn down with a needle held under the work — an appliqué idiom distinct from the pieced patchwork of the Levant.

**Assiut / tulle bi telli** — Net ground (*tulle*) darned with flattened silver or nickel strip, worked in Asyut and Upper Egypt from the late 19th century into the 1920s and worn abroad as evening shawls. The technique produces chunky geometric silhouettes — camels, palms, water-carriers, dancing figures — rendered in solid metal against near-invisible mesh.

**Siwa embroidery** — Dense silk polychrome on black or indigo cotton from the Siwa Oasis, concentrated in the *asherah* wedding dress: a great sunburst of orange, yellow and red radiating from the neck slit, with mother-of-pearl buttons stitched in rows down the chest. Berber-speaking Siwa's needlework has no counterpart in Nile Valley dress.

**Bedouin embroidery of Sinai** — Cross-stitch and pattern-darning in red, orange and magenta on black *thob* panels among the Muzeina, Tarabin and Awlad Sa'id, with coin-and-bead headdresses (*burqu'*); tribal affiliation and marital status are legible in the density and colour of the chest panel.

**Kilim egyptien / Cairo Mamluk carpet** — Flatwoven *kilim* and the pile **Mamluk carpets** of 15th–16th-century Cairo, the latter built on kaleidoscopic octagon-in-square medallions in a restrained lac-red, blue and green palette, S-spun wool — technically unlike Anatolian or Persian weaving of the same date.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Lotus* (rebirth, from pharaonic ornament and still in khayamiya), *palmette*, *arabesque (tawriq)* — interlaced vegetal scroll, *girih* — geometric strapwork, *khamsa* — the hand of Fatima against the evil eye, *ʿayn* — the eye itself, *shajarat al-hayah* — tree of life, *mihrab* niche arch, *Nile boat (felucca)*, *ankh* and cross (Coptic conflation), *sunburst* of the Siwan *asherah*.

### Clothing & dress

The near-universal male garment is the *galabeya* (*jallabiyya*), an ankle-length unfitted cotton or wool shift with a straight or T-cut neck slit; the *fellah* version is plain, the Sa'idi version fuller and worn with a white skullcap (*taqiya*) and wound turban (*ʿimma*), sometimes over a striped undershirt (*ʿaraqi*). Urban men added the *quftan* and, under Ottoman and khedival rule, the *tarbush* (fez). Village women wear a black overdress (*tob sebleh* in the south) over a printed cotton dress, with a head-veil (*tarha*) and, formerly, the face-veil (*burqu'*) hung with coins. Sinai Bedouin and Siwan women retain the most ornamented dress: the Siwan bride wears a stack of seven embroidered *asherah* dresses over the course of the wedding week, with a silver *aghraw* disc at the throat. Footwear is the slipper (*balgha*) or bare feet in the fields. Coptic clergy retain black *jibba* and the embroidered liturgical *badrashin*-style vestments descended from late-antique tunic forms.

### Architecture

The Nile Valley house is mudbrick (*tub labin*) — Nile silt puddled with chopped straw, sun-dried, laid up around a courtyard (*hosh*) that holds the oven (*furn*), the grain bins and the animals. Roofs are palm-trunk beams carrying palm-rib matting and a mud screed, used as a drying and sleeping platform. Upper Egyptian and Nubian builders raise mudbrick vaults and domes without centring — the technique Hassan Fathy documented at Gourna and revived as *architecture for the poor*. Nubian houses are further distinguished by whitewashed façades painted with palm, camel, boat and plate motifs, and by mudbrick relief around the doorway, with ceramic plates and pebbles set into the plaster. The Cairene courtyard house or *bayt* culminates in the *mashrabiya*: a projecting oriel screen of thousands of hand-turned wooden spindles, assembled without glue so it breathes, cools stored water jars and screens women from the street. Associated features are the *maqʿad* (raised loggia), *qaʿa* (double-height reception hall with a central *durqaʿa* fountain) and *malqaf* (north-facing wind-catcher).

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**Fustat pottery** — the sherd mountains of Old Cairo yield the sequence of Egyptian glazed ware: Fatimid lustre, splash-and-sgraffito, and the white-ground blue-painted vessels that carried Chinese influence west. **Egyptian faience** — the pharaonic non-clay quartz body, self-glazing to turquoise, made for amulets, beads and the *shabti* figures — is revived in modern Qena and Fustat workshops. Village potters still turn the unglazed porous water jar (*zir*) and the ubiquitous drinking *qulla*, kept in the shade to cool by evaporation. **Cairene woodwork** and **mother-of-pearl inlay** produce folding Qur'an stands (*kursi*), *muqarnas*-panelled doors and the geometric *khatam* marquetry of bone, ebony and shell. Copper and brass work gives the engraved tray-table (*sini*), the coffee pot (*kanaka*), and pierced mosque lamps. The *shadouf* — counterweighted lift-pole for raising Nile water — and the ox-driven *saqiya* wheel remained standard field equipment into the 20th century.

### Jewelry & body adornment

**Egyptian silver** covers a repertoire of amulet pendants: the *khamsa* hand, the Qur'anic cylinder case (*hirz*), the crescent, and the openwork filigree of Asyut and Akhmim. **Nubian jewelry** is heavier and more architectural — large hollow silver hoop earrings, the nose ring (*zummam*), coin-hung headbands and the bridal *hilaq*. Sinai Bedouin women wear coin-strung face-veils and forehead chains, wealth kept mobile and personal. Coptic women and men wear a small cross tattooed on the inner wrist, applied in childhood and functioning as an unremovable declaration of faith — the most distinctive Egyptian body marking. Rural women's blue-black tattooing (*daqq*) on chin, lip and hands is now largely a practice of older generations. *Henna* (*hinna*) is applied to a bride's hands and feet at the *laylat al-hinna* the night before the wedding, and used for hair and for children at feast days. Kohl (*kuhl*) from galena or lampblack is applied to infants' eyes as prophylaxis and protection.

## Music & performance

Egyptian instruments divide between the urban *takht* ensemble and the village. The *takht* — the classical chamber grouping behind Sayyid Darwish, Umm Kulthum and Abdel Wahab — is built on the *ʿud* (short-necked lute), *qanun* (trapezoidal plucked zither), *nay* (end-blown reed flute), *kamanja* (violin) and *riqq* (small tambourine), with the goblet drum *tabla* (darabukka) and frame drum *duff*. Village and Sa'idi music runs on the *rababa*, a two- or four-string spike fiddle that accompanies epic recitation; the double-reed *mizmar*, played in threes for Upper Egyptian celebration; the *arghul*, a single-reed clarinet with an extended drone pipe unique to Egypt; the *salamiya* flute; and in the Delta the *simsimiyya*, a box lyre of Suez, Port Said and the Red Sea coast around which a whole port-city song repertoire (*bambutiyya*) grew. Nubian music is separate again, driven by the frame drum (*tar*) and the five-tone *kisir* lyre, with call-and-response *aragid* wedding songs. Sufi orders supply the religious layer: the *dhikr* with *inshad dini* hymnody, and the *madh* praise-singing of the *munshid*. Genres include *mawwal* (melismatic unmetered lament on a colloquial quatrain), *taqtuqa* (light strophic song), *layali* vocal improvisation, and *shaʿbi* — the brash urban working-class style from which *mahraganat* electronic street music descends.

## Dance & theatre

The best-known form is *raqs sharqi*, the solo torso-and-hip dance of the urban stage, descended from the repertoire of the professional *ghawazi* dancing women of Upper Egypt and the *ʿawalim* singer-entertainers of Cairo. Its village counterpart is *raqs baladi*, danced socially by women at weddings. Men's dances are weapon-based: the Sa'idi *tahtib*, a stick duel of cane staves fought to *mizmar* and drum, now inscribed in Egypt's own heritage lists and performed as *raqs al-assaya* in stylized form. The *tanoura* is the spinning dance of the Egyptian Mevlevi and Sufi orders, the dancer whirling weighted concentric skirts while manipulating frame drums and discs — a devotional practice reframed for audiences at Cairo's Wikala al-Ghouri. Nubian *aragid* line dances move in shoulder-to-shoulder rows with handclap cross-rhythms. Theatre traditions include *Aragoz*, the hand-puppet glove figure whose voice is produced with a reed swazzle and whose sketches satirize authority, and *khayal al-zill*, shadow puppetry attested in Egypt from the Fatimid and Mamluk periods with the *Bab al-Timsah* (Crocodile) play among its surviving scripts.

## Festivals & rituals

*Sham al-Nessim* ("smelling of the breeze") falls on the Monday after Coptic Easter and is kept by Muslims and Christians alike — families eat salted *fesikh*, spring onions and coloured eggs in the open air, a spring festival continuous with the pharaonic *Shemu* harvest season. *Mulid* celebrations — saints' birthdays — are the great popular festivals: the *Mulid al-Nabi* for the Prophet, with its sugar dolls (*ʿarusa al-mulid*) and horse figures; the *Mulid* of Sayyid Ahmad al-Badawi at Tanta, drawing enormous crowds in October; the *Mulid* of Sayyida Zaynab and Imam al-Husayn in Cairo; and Coptic *mulids* for the Virgin at Durunka and Saint George. Ramadan brings the *fanus* lantern, the street *iftar* tables (*maidat al-rahman*) and the *mesaharati* drummer waking sleepers for *suhur*; it closes with *Eid al-Fitr* and *kahk* biscuits. The Coptic year runs on the ancient calendar dated from Diocletian's accession, with *Nayrouz* (1 Thout, 11 September) as new year and the *Feast of the Entry of the Lord into Egypt* on 1 June commemorating the Holy Family's flight. Life-cycle rites centre on the *sebou*, the seventh-day naming ceremony for a newborn — the baby is carried in a sieve, salt is scattered against the evil eye, and brass mortars are struck to accustom the child to noise. Weddings run from *laylat al-hinna* through the *zaffa* procession behind drums and *mizmar*.

## Oral tradition & literature

The great Egyptian epic is the *Sirat Bani Hilal*, the cycle of the Banu Hilal tribe's migration west and the fate of the hero Abu Zayd al-Hilali — sung in rhymed colloquial verse over many nights by a *shaʿir* (poet-singer) accompanying himself on the *rababa*, still performed in Sharqiyya and Upper Egypt and the last living Arabic oral epic tradition. Alongside it circulate the *Sirat al-Zahir Baybars*, centred on the Mamluk sultan, and the Egyptian recensions of the *Alf Layla wa-Layla* (Thousand and One Nights), whose Cairene layer supplies much of the collection's urban texture. Shorter forms include the *mawwal* quatrain, a compact narrative or lament built on punning rhyme; *nukta*, the political joke, a recognized Egyptian genre in its own right; and a vast proverb literature collected by Ahmad Taymur. Egyptian folklorists Ahmed Morsi and Hasan El-Shamy have documented the tale corpus systematically, El-Shamy's *Folktales of Egypt* (1980) and *Types of the Folktale in the Arab World* (2004) being the standard indexes.

## Language & religion

Egyptian Arabic (*Masri*) and Sa'idi Arabic are Semitic languages of the Afroasiatic family; Masri is the most widely understood Arabic vernacular in the Arab world because of Cairo's film and music industries. Coptic — ancient Egyptian written in the Greek alphabet plus six Demotic letters — was the majority spoken language until at least the 10th century and remains in liturgical use in Bohairic form. Siwi (Berber) and Nobiin and Kenzi (Nubian) are spoken at the edges. Between 84–90% of Egyptians are Muslim, overwhelmingly Sunni with a small Shia minority and a substantial constituency attached to Sufi orders (*turuq*) — Shadhiliyya, Rifaʿiyya, Burhaniyya — whose *dhikr*, *mulid* and shrine visitation (*ziyara*) constitute the living body of folk religion. 10–15% are Christian, nearly all Coptic Orthodox, with pilgrimage to Holy Family sites, the Coptic fasting calendar covering over 200 days a year, and the wrist cross tattoo. Amulets, the *khamsa*, kohl, salt and Qur'anic *hirz* cases guard against *ʿayn* (the evil eye) across both communities.
