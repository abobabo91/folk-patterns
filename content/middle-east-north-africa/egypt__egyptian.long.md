## Overview

The Egyptians (*al-Miṣriyyūn*) are a Nile Valley people whose modern identity fuses a deep pharaonic and Coptic Christian substrate with more than a millennium of Arab-Islamic acculturation. They speak Egyptian Arabic (*al-lugha al-miṣriyya*), a Semitic vernacular of the Arabic dialect continuum whose vocabulary, syntax and phonology preserve numerous Coptic and, more remotely, Ancient Egyptian survivals; the liturgical Coptic language, itself the last stage of Ancient Egyptian written in a Greek-derived alphabet, persists in the Coptic Orthodox Church. With a population of roughly 105–110 million within Egypt and several million more in diaspora across the Gulf, Europe and North America, they are the demographic anchor of the Arab world. Settlement is overwhelmingly along the Nile — from Aswan and Nubia in the south through the Ṣaʿīd (Upper Egypt), the historic urban core of Cairo (*al-Qāhira*) and Fustat, into the Delta towns of Rashid (Rosetta), Damietta and Tanta — with distinctive peripheral communities of Bedouin (*ʿArab al-Badū*) in the Sinai and Eastern Desert, Nubians (*al-Nūba*) at the southern frontier, and Siwan Berbers in the western oases. In folk-culture terms the Egyptians matter as inheritors of an unbroken craft continuum: the Fayum funerary portraitists and Coptic tunic weavers of late antiquity, the faience workshops of pharaonic and Fatimid Fustat, the Mamluk woodcarvers and mother-of-pearl inlayers of Cairo, and the *khayamiya* tentmakers of the Street of the Tentmakers all sit within their tradition.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Coptic textile** — the late-antique (3rd–8th century) tapestry-woven linen and wool tunic panels excavated from cemeteries at Akhmim, Antinoë and Panopolis, worked with looped-pile *bouclé* grounds and inset *clavi* medallions in purple, ochre and indigo. Their vocabulary of hares, vine scrolls, dancing Erotes and haloed saints marks the transition from Hellenistic to early Christian imagery, and the surviving *Coptic tunic* is the most complete corpus of late-antique dress anywhere.

**Khayamiya** (*khayāmiyya*) — the appliqué panels of the Cairene *Shāriʿ al-Khayyāmiyya* ("Street of the Tentmakers"), in which brightly dyed cotton shapes are hand-stitched onto a heavy canvas ground to produce arabesque *arabesk*, *islimi* vine, calligraphic and pictorial compositions. Historically the interior linings of the ceremonial *sirādiq* pavilions raised at weddings, funerals and Ramadan gatherings; the surviving *tentmaker applique* workshops are one of the last hand-sewn textile crafts of the Arab city.

**Assiut** (*asyūṭ*, also *tulle bi telli*) — a shawl fabric from Upper Egypt made by hammering flat strips of silver or silver-gilt metal through a black or ecru cotton net (*tulle*), producing geometric pharaonic-revival motifs that became fashionable in Europe during the 1920s Tutankhamun craze.

**Egyptian embroidery** — a broad category encompassing the drawn-thread and cross-stitch *ṭarz* of the Delta villages, and, more distinctively, the regional schools of the periphery. *Siwa embroidery* on the black or indigo bridal dress (*asherah*) of the Siwan Berber oasis massed silk-floss chest panels in orange, red, green and yellow around a central sun disc, with mother-of-pearl buttons at the neck. *Bedouin embroidery* of the Sinai (as practised by Jabaliya, Tarabin and Muzeina women) works dense cross-stitch on the black *thob*, in palettes that commonly signal marital status — reds and oranges for married women, blues for widows.

**Kilim égyptien** — the flat-woven wool rugs and cushion covers of the Nile Delta and the Fayum, typically slit-tapestry in madder red, indigo and undyed camel, patterned with lozenge medallions and stylised *ḥayāt* (life-tree) motifs; often confused with North African kilims in the market, but distinguishable by their coarser wool and Coptic-inflected iconography.

**Motif vocabulary.** *ʿAyn* (eye, apotropaic), *khamsa* (hand of Fatima), *nakhla* (date palm), *hilāl* (crescent), *najma* (star), *shajarat al-ḥayāh* (tree of life), *ṭayr* (bird), *arabesk* scrolling vine, *kūfī* and *thuluth* calligraphic bands, and — in Coptic and Nubian contexts — the equal-armed cross.

### Clothing & dress

The signature male garment is the *galabeya* (*jallābiyya*), an ankle-length wide-sleeved cotton or wool robe, worn in undyed beige or striped *dimyāṭī* cotton by *fellāḥīn* (peasants) and in fine white broadcloth by urban notables. Over it a man may wear the *kaftān* and a heavy woollen wrap (*ʿabāya*); on the head the small brimless *ṭāqiyya* under a red felt *ṭarbūsh* (fez, urban until the 1952 revolution) or, in the countryside, a wound turban (*ʿimāma*) whose colour and manner of tying encode village, tariqa and clerical rank. Al-Azhar shaykhs retain the white *ʿimāma* over a red *ṭarbūsh*. Rural women wear a long dark dress over trousers, with a head-and-shoulder wrap (*milāya laff*) — the black wool *milāya* wound and tucked at the hip is the classic Alexandrian and Delta form. Bedouin women of the Sinai and Western Desert wear the embroidered black *thob* with a coin-hung face veil (*burquʿ*); Siwan brides layer up to seven silk dresses under a silver-heavy headpiece. Nubian women's dress is the flowing *jarjar*, a light black overdress with wide gathered sleeves. Coptic clergy retain distinctive black robes, embroidered stoles, and the turban-like *shamla*.

### Architecture

The traditional Egyptian urban house is a courtyard structure of mud-brick, fired brick and timber, presenting a nearly blank street façade broken only by projecting oriel windows screened with turned-wood latticework — the *mashrabiya*, whose thousands of small lathe-cut spindles admit air and filtered light while shielding the women's quarters (*ḥarīm*) from view. Interiors are organised around a covered reception hall (*qāʿa*) with a sunken central marble fountain (*fasqiyya*), an *īwān* recess on each side, and a small octagonal lantern (*shukhshaykha*) admitting light from above. Walls are lined with polychrome marble revetment (*abaq*), and the upper zone carries carved and painted wooden ceilings and *muqarnas* cornices. *Cairene woodwork* is the great signature: geometric strapwork panels assembled from thousands of interlocking turned and inlaid pieces, and doors and *minbar* panels of *Cairo mamluk* production inlaid in *mother-of-pearl inlay Egypt* and ivory (*taʿshīr*), a technique that continued into Ottoman workshops in Damietta. Monumental architecture — the Mamluk complexes of Sultan Ḥasan and Qalāwūn, the Fatimid mosques of al-Ḥākim and al-Azhar, the Ottoman Muḥammad ʿAlī mosque — is faced in banded *ablaq* stone with carved stone *muqarnas* portals and pencil minarets. In the Delta town of Rashid (Rosetta) the surviving merchants' houses of the 17th–18th centuries are famous for their *Rashid tiles* — locally produced polychrome underglaze tiles set into brick façades in floral and calligraphic panels. In Upper Egypt and the Western Desert, mud-brick vaulted and domed rural architecture — canonised in the twentieth century by the architect Ḥasan Fatḥī at New Gourna — descends directly from pharaonic technique, alongside the ancient water-lifting device the *shadouf* (*shādūf*), a counter-weighted swing-beam still visible on the riverbank. Nubian houses of the pre-inundation villages were whitewashed mud-brick with vaulted roofs and vivid painted façades of ships, palms and geometric bands.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**Egyptian faience** — the ancient alkaline-glazed non-clay ceramic of pharaonic Egypt, a self-glazing quartz frit fired to the characteristic turquoise-blue of shabtis and amulets, is the ancestral ceramic. Its medieval descendant is **Fustat pottery**, the lustreware, sgraffito and *Fayyūmī* underglaze wares excavated from the Fatimid capital, whose polychrome fritware is a direct precursor of later Iznik. Contemporary village pottery in the Nile Delta, the Fayum and the Ṣaʿīd continues to produce unglazed water jars (*zīr*, *qulla*), whose porous walls cool the water by evaporation, and dark cooking wares. **Naqsh hajar** ("stone carving") is a broad term for the incised limestone and alabaster work of Upper Egyptian workshops around Luxor. Coppersmiths of the Cairene *Naḥḥāsīn* quarter produce hand-hammered and engraved trays (*ṣīniyya*), long-spouted coffee pots (*dallāh* / *kanaka*), *ṭāsa* basins and pierced lanterns (*fānūs*) — the latter the ubiquitous Ramadan object. The *fānūs Ramaḍān* of tin and coloured glass, historically made in the workshops of Bāb Zuwayla, is one of the most recognisable folk objects of the Arab world.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Egyptian jewellery divides into an urban goldsmiths' tradition and a rural silver one. **Egyptian silver** is the material of the countryside and the desert peripheries: heavy hollow-cast anklets (*khulkhāl*), coin necklaces, filigree pendants and — especially — apotropaic amulets in the form of the *khamsa* hand, the crescent (*hilāl*), and the eye (*ʿayn*). Bedouin women of the Sinai wear the coin-hung *burquʿ* face veil, whose density of Ottoman and Maria Theresa thalers is a portable dowry. **Nubian jewelry** favours large hollow-worked gold or silver-gilt nose rings, wide filigree bracelets, and the *shanaf* nose stud. The urban Cairene goldsmiths of the *Ṣāgha* quarter produce fine granulated and filigree gold bridal sets — necklace, earrings, bracelets and belt (*shabka*) — presented at the engagement. Bridal henna (*ḥinnā*) is applied at a dedicated evening gathering (*laylat al-ḥinnā*) shared across Muslim, Coptic and Jewish Egyptian tradition. The **Fayum portrait** — the 1st–3rd century encaustic panel portraits fixed to mummy wrappings at Hawara and er-Rubayat — is not jewellery but is the earliest surviving evidence of Egyptian adornment as worn, showing gold wreaths, pearl earrings and gem-set necklaces on individuals whose faces still confront the viewer directly.

## Music & performance

The classical core of Egyptian art music is the *waṣla* suite of the 19th–20th century Cairene *takht* ensemble — *ʿūd*, *qānūn*, *nāy*, *kamanja* / violin, and *riqq* — built on the modal system of *maqām* and the rhythmic cycles (*īqāʿāt*) shared with the wider Arab world, but stamped by the Egyptian preference for expressive vocal ornament (*tarab*). The 20th century canon of Umm Kulthūm, Muḥammad ʿAbd al-Wahhāb, Farīd al-Aṭrash and ʿAbd al-Ḥalīm Ḥāfiẓ made Cairo the recording and cinematic capital of the Arab world. Older folk layers include the *madīḥ nabawī* devotional song of the Sufi orders, the *inshād dīnī* of the mawlid processions, the epic *sīrat Banī Hilāl* — sung by professional poet-narrators (*shāʿir al-rabāba*) to the two-string spike fiddle *rabāba*, and inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity — and the ecstatic *zār* possession-healing repertoire of women's ritual. Upper Egyptian *ṣaʿīdī* music centres on the double-reed *mizmār* and the large frame drum *ṭabla baladī*, while Nubian music, sung in Nobiin and Kenzi rather than Arabic, uses the lyre *kisir* / *ṭambūra* and a distinctive clapped duple metre. Coptic Orthodox liturgical chant is a separate and archaic tradition, transmitted orally by cantors (*muʿallimīn*) and preserving melodies commonly held to descend from pharaonic temple practice, though the evidence for that continuity is debated.

## Dance & theatre

The signature Egyptian dance form is *raqṣ sharqī* ("Eastern dance") — the solo torso-and-hip vocabulary codified in the mid-20th century Cairo cabaret and cinema by dancers such as Tahiya Carioca and Samia Gamal, ultimately drawn from the older *ghawāzī* professional dancing women of Upper Egypt and the domestic women's *raqṣ baladī*. Male folk forms include the *taḥṭīb* stick-dance-and-duel of Upper Egypt, inscribed by UNESCO in 2016, and the whirling *tanoura* of the Mevlevi-derived Egyptian Sufi orders, now also a stage genre. Traditional theatre took the form of the shadow-puppet play (*khayāl al-ẓill*) — attested in medieval Cairo in the plays of Ibn Dāniyāl — and the improvised comic street theatre *fasl muḍḥik*, ancestor of the modern Cairene stage.

## Festivals & rituals

The Islamic ritual year — Ramadan with its evening *iftār*, communal *tarāwīḥ* prayers, and the paper-and-tin *fānūs* lanterns; the two ʿīds; the Prophet's birthday (*mawlid al-nabī*), celebrated with the sugar *ʿarūsat al-mūlid* doll — is layered over an older cycle of saint's-day festivals (*mawālid*, sing. *mawlid*) at Sufi shrines, of which the *mawlid* of Sayyid al-Badawī at Ṭanṭā and of al-Ḥusayn in Cairo draw millions. The Coptic year runs on the ancient Alexandrian calendar and preserves *Sham al-Nasīm*, a spring-Monday picnic festival of salted fish (*fasīkh*), spring onions and coloured eggs that descends demonstrably from pharaonic *Shemu* and is shared across confessional lines as a national festival. Coptic Christmas (7 January) and Easter (*ʿĪd al-Qiyāma*), the Feast of the Cross, and the annual *mūlids* of the Virgin at Dayr al-Muḥarraq punctuate the Christian calendar. Life-cycle rituals include the seventh-day *subūʿ* naming ceremony for a new baby, with clay pots, incense and a chanted lullaby telling the child to obey its parents; weddings preceded by the *laylat al-ḥinnā*; and forty-day mourning periods.

## Foodways

Egyptian foodways are anchored in the Nile grain economy. The national dish is *kushari*, a Cairene layered plate of rice, lentils, macaroni, chickpeas, fried onions and a spiced tomato-vinegar sauce. *Fūl mudammas* — slow-stewed fava beans dressed with oil, lemon and cumin — is the breakfast of the country, paired with *ṭaʿmiyya*, the Egyptian fava-bean fritter that is the local form of falafel. Flatbreads are the coarse whole-wheat *ʿaysh baladī*, baked in domed clay ovens and puffed in the fire. *Molokhiyya*, a mucilaginous green soup of jute mallow leaves, is the peasant summer dish par excellence. Feast dishes include *fatta* — layered bread, rice, garlic-vinegar broth and meat, associated with ʿīd al-aḍḥā and with a mother's first meal after childbirth — and stuffed pigeon (*ḥamām maḥshī*). Sweet traditions include *kunāfa* and *qaṭāyif* for Ramadan, *basbūsa* semolina cake, and *umm ʿalī*. Coptic fasting seasons — roughly two-thirds of the year — have produced a rich vegan repertoire.

## Oral tradition & literature

The great oral epic is the *Sīrat Banī Hilāl*, the sung prose-and-verse cycle of the migration of the Banū Hilāl Bedouin from the Arabian Peninsula through Egypt into the Maghrib in the 11th century, still performed in Upper Egypt by hereditary *shāʿir* poets accompanying themselves on the *rabāba*, and inscribed on the UNESCO list in 2008. Folk narrative includes the trickster cycles of *Juḥā* and *Goha*, the *ḥadduta* fairy-tale repertoire told to children by grandmothers, and a large corpus of proverbs (*amthāl*) whose density in daily Egyptian Arabic is proverbial in itself. Written literature has an unbroken record from the pharaonic *Story of Sinuhe* through the Coptic monastic apophthegmata and the Fatimid and Mamluk chronicles to the modern Cairene novel of Naguib Mahfouz — the first Arabic-language Nobel laureate — and the vernacular colloquial poetry (*zajal*, *shiʿr ʿāmmī*) of Bayram al-Tūnisī and Aḥmad Fuʾād Nigm.

## Language & religion

Modern Egyptians speak Egyptian Arabic (*al-lugha al-miṣriyya*), the most widely understood Arabic dialect in the Arab world by virtue of Cairo's twentieth-century dominance in cinema and broadcasting; classical Arabic (*al-fuṣḥā*) is the register of Qurʾān, press and formal address. Coptic, the last stage of Ancient Egyptian, is retained as the liturgical language of the Coptic Orthodox Church, whose faithful form roughly 10% of the population, alongside smaller Catholic and Protestant communities. Sunni Islam of the Shāfiʿī and Ḥanafī schools, structured around al-Azhar and a dense network of Sufi turuq (Aḥmadiyya, Burhāniyya, Shādhiliyya, Rifāʿiyya), is the majority tradition. Peripheral languages include Nobiin and Kenzi (Nilo-Saharan) in Nubia, Siwi Berber in the Siwa oasis, and Domari among Egyptian Roma.
