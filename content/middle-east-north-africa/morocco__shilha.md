## Overview

The Schleuh, also known as the Shilha, Chleuh, Ichelhiyen, Shluh, or Schlöh, are a Berber ethnic group indigenous to Morocco’s Anti-Atlas, High Atlas, and Sous valley. They are the southernmost residing Berber population and mainly live in Morocco’s Atlas Mountains and Sous Valley. Traditionally, they farm and keep herds; some communities are semi-nomadic, growing crops when water is available and moving with their herds during the dry season. Their language, Tashelhit, belongs to the Berber branch of the Afro-Asiatic family. The sources state that around 5.2 million Shilha speakers existed as of 2024, constituting 14.2% of Morocco’s population. Their folk-cultural profile includes seasonal pastoral cooperation, distinctive village and tribal dress, silver adornment, and a substantial literary and musical tradition.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Asmal* or *Achayt Caftan* — A long red shirt worn by Shilha women, embroidered with distinctive patterns made from wool threads colored red, yellow, and green.

*Ssayah* — A long skirt in white or black, embroidered with patterns and worn over the Asmal caftan.

*Addal* — A long white robe reaching to the feet, similar to the Haik, used to cover the body and head.

### Clothing & dress

Shilha women and girls wear clothing associated with their tribe and village, allowing observers in the region to recognize tribal affiliation through dress. The Asmal or Achayt Caftan is a long red shirt embroidered with patterns in red, yellow, and green wool threads. It is worn with the ssayah, a white or black embroidered skirt, and colorful shoes called *Iddukan*. Women cover their hair with the *Qatib*, a popular red silk scarf that can also appear in different colors. The Qatib may be replaced by the *Addal*, a long white robe similar to the Haik that covers the body and head. The source distinguishes everyday and occasional wear but gives no detailed account of men’s clothing, belts, or separate ceremonial garments.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver adornment is described as the most important feature of women’s traditional costume, and its form differs between tribes. The *Tawnza* is a crown made of silver and coral and can distinguish one tribe from another. Women also wear *Taẓeṛẓit*, silver Amazigh fibulae that adorn the chest and abdomen, and *Tanbalt*, a pair of silver hinged bracelets.

## Music & performance

The sources mention traditional Shilha music and identify Oudaden as a popular musical group that plays it. The band was formed in 1978 in Ben Sergao, near Agadir, in the Sous region. Ammouri Mbarek is described as a Shilha musician widely known for changing the traditional music scene in Morocco. The sources also identify Hassan Arsmouk and Hindi Zahra as popular singers, and Loreen as a singer and songwriter of Shilha descent.

## Festivals & rituals

Shilha communities in the southwestern mountains of Morocco traditionally cooperated by providing reciprocal grazing rights as seasons changed and during periods of war. These alliances were reaffirmed through annual festive gatherings, at which one Shilha community invited nearby and distant Shilha communities.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources identify Mohammed Awzal as the most prolific and most important author in the literary tradition of the Tachelhit language, producing hundreds of works during his lifetime. Mririda n Ayt Atiq is described as a Tashelhit poet whose poetry addressed topics considered taboo at the time, including divorce, love between women, household problems, and unrequited love. Her poetry openly voiced her inner thoughts as a form of rebellion and resistance against a patriarchal culture.

Mohammed Khaïr-Eddine is identified as a Moroccan poet and writer who was among the most famous Moroccan Amazigh literary figures and left a lasting mark on Indigenous Moroccan literature.

## Language & religion

The Ishelhien speak Tashelhit, a Berber language belonging to the Berber branch of the Afro-Asiatic family. The language is also called Sous-Berber and differs considerably from some other Berber languages, including those spoken by the Tuareg. The name Taclḥit is a feminine noun derived from masculine Aclḥiy, meaning a male speaker of Shilha; in most usages, Aclḥiy means a speaker of Shilha. Tashelhit is written in the Tifinagh script in the form ⵜⴰⵛⵍⵃⵉⵜ. The sources describe historical Islamic dynasties, Islamic theologians, religious scholars, saints, and spiritual leaders connected with Shilha history.
