## Overview

Ottoman Greeks, also called *Graikoi* or *Romioi*, were ethnic Greeks who lived in the Ottoman Empire from 1299 to 1922. Their communities extended across the Ottoman Empire, including modern Turkey, Greece, Cyprus, the Balkans and the Eastern Mediterranean, as well as Ottoman Armenia, Ottoman Syria and the Ottoman Caucasus. The Rum community was especially important in Istanbul, where Greek Orthodox Christians had lived since before 330 AD, when the city was called Byzantium. An 1883 census registered 152,741 Greeks in Istanbul, while an estimate for 1897 gave 236,000. Ottoman Greeks retained Greek culture, customs, language and educational traditions within the wider Orthodox Christian community.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do record that Christians in the Ottoman Empire were forbidden to carry weapons or ride atop horses, and that religious practices were legally required to defer to those of Muslims.

### Architecture

They identify the Phanar quarter of Istanbul as the origin of the Phanariots and state that the Orthodox Patriarchate was located there. Phanar was near the docks of the Golden Horn and a short boat ride from Topkapi Palace, the seat of the sultanate.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

One judicial record states that Maryule daughter of Nikola possessed goods worth 135,600 akçes at her death in 1788.

## Music & performance

The sources also state that the Phanariots contributed to a Greek cultural revival and that Rum authors such as Nektaria Anastasiadou demonstrate the resilience and continuing culture of the Rum community.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe the religious and institutional importance of the Ecumenical Patriarchate of Constantinople. After the fall of Constantinople in 1453, the Sultan recognized the Ecumenical Patriarch as the religious and national leader of the Orthodox population. The Patriarchate became a central institution for Greeks inside the Ottoman state and was associated with projects of Greek renaissance centered on the resurrection and revitalization of the Byzantine Empire.

The sources also describe family and religious life among Rum women. In the 19th century, the matriarch of a Rum family commanded her household and was treated with respect and obedience by younger relatives. Rum women were largely restricted to the private sphere, while girls’ schools supported women’s education. No birth, coming-of-age, wedding or funeral rites are described.

## Foodways

They state that the Phanariots served in food provision for the capital and that Phanar was close to the docks of the Golden Horn, where necessary provisions were brought to Istanbul.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do identify several literary and intellectual figures. Adamantios Korais is described as a Greek humanist scholar, Evangelinos Misailidis as an Ottoman writer and journalist, Elias Venezis as a writer from Ayvalık, and Giorgos Seferis as a Greek poet who was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature. The sources also mention Nektaria Anastasiadou as a Rum author whose work demonstrates the community’s resilience and retention of a vibrant culture.

## Language & religion

Ottoman Greeks retained Greek culture, customs, language and traditions of education. They identified themselves as *Graikoi*, meaning “Greeks,” and as *Romaioi* or *Romioi*, meaning “Romans.” The Ottoman state’s Muslim dhimmi system treated former Byzantine Greeks who remained Christians as members of the Rum millet and guaranteed them limited freedoms, including the right to worship, while treating them as second-class citizens. Christians and Jews were not considered equal to Muslims in law, and their testimony against Muslims was inadmissible in courts. The Ecumenical Patriarchate of Constantinople represented the Orthodox population, which the Ottoman state legally regarded as a single entity rather than distinguishing nationality from religion.
