## Overview

The Lurs, Lors or Luris (Persian: لر) are an Iranian people living mainly in western and southern Iran. Their principal subgroups are the Bakhtiari, Mamasani, Kohgiluyeh and Lur proper, linked especially by the Luri language. Lur communities are found in Lorestan, Fars, Chaharmahal and Bakhtiari, Kohgiluyeh and Boyer-Ahmad, Khuzestan, Hamadan, Isfahan, Tehran, southern Ilam province and Genaveh county in Bushehr province. Luri is a Western Iranian language continuum spoken by about four million people, comprising Bakhtiari, Luristani and Southern Luri. The Lurs are significant in folk culture through their textiles and weaving, music, clothing and folk dances, as well as through the continuing influence of tribal elders among nomadic populations.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Luri textiles* are highly esteemed for their workmanship and beauty. The sources identify Luri textiles and weaving skills as important cultural characteristics.

### Clothing & dress

Luri clothing is described as one of the distinctive ethno-cultural characteristics of the Lurs. The sources state that Lur women have greater freedom than women in other groups within the region to wear diverse types of female clothing and to participate in social activities, singing and dancing at ceremonies. They also state that Lur women have greater freedom than women in other groups within the region.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They identify Luri textiles and weaving skills, but provide no catalogue of other material objects.

## Music & performance

Luri music is identified as one of the most distinctive ethno-cultural characteristics of the Lurs. The sources also state that a few Lurs are traveling musicians and describe Shahmirza Moradi as an Iranian Lur especially versed in playing *sorna*. No further account is provided of the sorna’s construction, repertoire or performance setting.

Music is also connected with the cultural freedom attributed to Lur women. The sources state that women have greater freedom to sing and dance in different ceremonies. They likewise do not describe musical institutions, professional ensembles, instruments besides the sorna, or distinctions between nomadic and settled performance practice.

The sources connect music with Lur identity more generally. Luri historically served as the principal link between the Lur branches and helped consolidate Lur ethnic identity, while Luri music, clothing and folk dances are presented as distinctive features of the group.

## Dance & theatre

Luri folk dances are identified as one of the distinctive ethno-cultural characteristics of the Lurs. The sources state that Lur women have freedom to dance in different ceremonies.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources state only that Lur women sing and dance in different ceremonies and that pre-Islamic beliefs continued among Lurs alongside Islam.

Religious history is described in broad periods. The Lurs converted to Islam after the Muslim conquest of Persia and adopted the Shia sect during the Safavid conversion of Iran to Shia Islam. Pre-Islamic beliefs continued among Lurs, and they were not fully Islamized until after the 1979 Islamic revolution. Historically, a significant part of the Lurs followed Yarsanism, especially those with closer affinities to Kurds. Luristan was considered the heart of Yarsanism before Islamization. A small Sunni Muslim community and a small minority of Christian Lurs are also described.

## Foodways

They state only that, according to the Encyclopaedia of Islam, the Lurs revere bread and fire like the Zoroastrians.

## Oral tradition & literature

The name of the Luri tribes is associated in the sources with *Vis and Rāmin*, described as a famous Persian story. A line in that work mentions Kurds, Lurs, bandits and Ayyārs. The sources also discuss the early history of the term “Lur,” including its appearance in Islamic sources and its later use as an ethnic designation.

The Luri language was first mentioned in *Tarikh-i Guzida*, where Hamdullah Mustawfi described features that he claimed did not exist in Luri. The oldest surviving Luri manuscript is described as three pages and dated to the 11th Islamic century. An 8th-century book, *Nukhbat al-dahr*, used the Arabic term “ar-Riṭāna” to describe the language of a region named “Lūz,” identified in the source as a misspelling of “Lur.”

The source’s further reading includes *Tales from Luristan (Matalyâ Lurissu)* and *Folk tales from a Persian tribe: forty-five tales from Sisakht in Luri and English*.

## Language & religion

Luri is a Western Iranian language continuum spoken by about four million people. It consists of the Bakhtiari, Luristani and Southern Luri dialects, which linguist Anonby situates between Kurdish and Persian. The language was historically the principal link between the Lur branches and helped consolidate Lur ethnic identity. The sources also state that the distinctive characteristics of Lur dialects imply that the Lurs were Iranized by Persis rather than Media.

The Lurs are predominantly Shia Muslims. They converted to Islam after the Muslim conquest of Persia and adopted Shia Islam during the Safavid conversion of Iran. Religious practice also incorporated native beliefs, and the sources describe the Lurs as historically unorthodox. Yarsanism was historically significant, particularly among Lurs with closer affinities to Kurds. A small Sunni Muslim community and a small Christian minority are also present. The sources describe Christianity among Lurs as developing a unique Christian Lur tradition during the reign of Constantine the Great.
