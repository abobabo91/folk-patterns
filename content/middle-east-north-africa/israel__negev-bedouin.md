## Overview

The Negev Bedouin are traditionally pastoral nomadic Arab tribes who historically moved between Hijaz and the Sinai Peninsula. Today most live in the Negev region of Israel, while a minority expelled during the 1948 war live in Palestine. Their population in the Negev numbers 200,000–210,000. Just over half live in seven government-built Bedouin-only towns, while 90,000 live in 46 villages, including villages that remain unrecognized by the state. The largest Bedouin locality in Israel is Rahat. The community is Muslim, traditional, and conservative, with a defined value system regulating behavior and interpersonal relations. Their folk-culture history is closely connected with pastoralism, agriculture, desert movement, tribal organization, land use, and the transition from nomadic and semi-nomadic life to sedentarized townships.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

One historical passage records that Moshe Dayan imagined an urban transformation in which Bedouin men would wear pants and no dagger, but this is a quotation describing a proposed social change rather than a catalogue of customary dress. The sources also describe Bedouin identity as closely tied to pastoral nomadic life, a way of life that many Negev Bedouin say is over.

### Architecture

The sources describe a small number of traditional built forms. *Baika* buildings served as seasonal dwellings during the rainy season, when Bedouin stopped to engage in farming. The sources also mention open-air mosques without roofs, dating from the early Islamic period, which remained in use. Historically, the Bedouin established very few permanent settlements, although they conducted farming on plots scattered throughout the Negev.

Sedentarization produced other forms of settlement. The Bedouin built approximately 60 new villages and dispersed settlements by the end of the British Mandate, and Israel later established seven urban townships between 1968 and 1989. These included Rahat, Tel as-Sabi, Shaqib al-Salam, Ar'arat an-Naqab, Kuseife, Lakiya, and Hura. The sources describe the townships as lacking an urban policy framework, business districts, and industrial zones. Bedouin who resisted urban life remained in rural villages, many of which were not connected to electrical grids, water mains, or trash collection.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do record pastoral and agricultural equipment indirectly through references to sheep, goats, camels, farming plots, water access, and the movement of encampments.

## Music & performance

They describe the community’s historical occupations as nomadic herding, agriculture, fishing in some cases, and transporting goods and people across the desert.

The sources do record political and military performances of tribal organization. During World War I, Sheikh Hamad Pasha al-Sufi, Sheikh of the Nijmat sub-tribe of the Tarabin, led a force of 1,500 men from the Al-Tarabin, Al-Tayaha, and Al-Azazma tribes in the Turkish offensive against the Suez Canal. During the 1948 Palestine war, Bedouin groups supported both Jewish and Arab sides, and fighting Bedouin societies participated in conflicts throughout the country, including the Galilee region. The first Bedouin tribe described as aligning with the Jews was Arab-al-Hib, which later became a tracker unit in the Israel Defence Forces. These passages concern military and tribal history rather than music.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that the Bedouin adhere to Islam and mention open-air mosques dating from the early Islamic period.

The sources do describe seasonal movement connected with agriculture. Traditional *baika* buildings were used as seasonal dwellings during the rainy season, when the Bedouin stopped to farm. Scarcity of water and permanent pastoral land required constant movement in earlier periods. The Bedouin also possessed grazing rights and systems of water access, and their historical way of life combined herding, cultivation, and transport across the desert. These seasonal and economic practices are the only ritual-adjacent or calendar-related material covered by the sources.

## Foodways

They state that the Bedouin adhere to Islam, but do not elaborate on food practices associated with that religion.

The sources do describe food-related economic activity. Bedouin historically transported goods and people across the desert and, after the rise of the Wahhabi sect, acquired a monopoly on guiding pilgrim caravans to Mecca and selling them provisions. They also practiced agriculture on scattered plots throughout the Negev. During the Ottoman period, some Bedouin land was cultivated by tenants brought to newly acquired estates. In the twentieth century, much of the Bedouin population was settled, semi-nomadic, and engaged in agriculture within systems of land ownership, grazing rights, and water access. These references concern provisioning and food production rather than named cuisine.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record expressions through which Bedouin identity and history are represented. Many Bedouin today call themselves “Negev Arabs” rather than “Bedouin,” explaining that the term “Bedouin” is closely tied to a pastoral nomadic way of life that they consider over.

The sources also preserve descriptions by outside authorities and scholars. A 1927 report described the Bedouin as the “untamed denizens of the Arabian deserts,” while later scholarship challenged the idea that Bedouin society was “fossilized” or “stagnant.” Emanuel Marx described a constantly dynamic reciprocal relationship between Bedouin and urban centers, and Michael Meeker stated that “the city was to be found in their midst.” These are scholarly descriptions of social life rather than indigenous literary works.

## Language & religion

The Negev Bedouin are Arab tribes, and the source identifies the Arabic name *Badwu an-Naqab*. They identify Hebrew as the Hebrew name for the group, *HaBedu'im BaNegev*.

The Bedouin adhere to Islam, and most are Israeli citizens forming part of the Israeli Bedouin. The sources mention open-air mosques without roofs dating from the early Islamic period, which were common and still in use. They also describe the conversion of Wallachian soldiers stationed at Saint Catherine’s Monastery to Islam and their adoption of an Arab Bedouin lifestyle. No further sectarian divisions, syncretic practices, saint cults, or folk-religious traditions are documented.
