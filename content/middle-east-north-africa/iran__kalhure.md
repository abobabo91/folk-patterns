## Overview

The Kalhor, here identified as Kalhure, are a Kurdish tribe native to the southern regions of Kurdistan. They traditionally lived on both sides of the Iran-Iraq border, between Kermanshah and Khanaqin. The tribe was historically native to Kermanshah province in Iran, where it was described as the most powerful tribe, and it also had communities in Ilam province and Khanaqin. The Kalhor speak Southern Kurdish, specifically the Kalhori dialect, which extends from southwestern Kermanshah province through northern Ilam province to eastern Iraq. They are mostly Shia Muslims, with Yarsani and Ali-Illahi minorities. Their historical importance includes local government, military service, alliances with Safavid and Zand rulers, and a prominent role in Kurdish political history.

## Material culture

### Architecture

They do mention the historic **Pul-i Kalhur**, or the Kalhur Bridge, located on the Kashkan river north of Mamulan in Lorestan province. The bridge stood next to inscriptions dedicated to the Kurdish dynasty of the Hasanwayhids.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record religious change within the tribe. The Kalhor were historically Yarsani, increasingly converted to Shia Islam after the 1830s, and were mostly Shia Muslim by the early 1900s. Some Kalhor remained Yarsani after most of the tribe became Shia Muslim. In Kermanshah province, local government cooperated with Shia clergy to promote Shia Islam, especially in Kermanshah city. A madrasa was established under the governorship of Emad al-Dowleh in 1868.

## Foodways

The etymological discussion records that Vladimir Minorsky speculated that the name Kalhor might derive from “kal-xwar,” meaning “buffalo-eaters,” but the source presents this only as a theory about the name and does not describe Kalhor foodways.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources identify several literary and historical traditions associated with Kalhor origins and political memory. The Kalhor leaders claimed descent from Gudarz, the father of Giv in the Shahnama. In the Sharafnama, Sharafkhan Bidlisi wrote that the Kurdish nation consisted of four subdivisions: the Goran, Kurmanj, Kalhor, and Lur. The sources also record theories that the Kalhor originated farther north and that they descended from Jews carried into captivity by Nebuchadnezzar. These accounts are presented as historical theories or claims rather than as a documented body of Kalhor oral performance.

## Language & religion

The Kalhor are a Kurdish tribe whose dialect, Kalhori, is a branch of Southern Kurdish. The dialect extends from southwestern Kermanshah province to northern Ilam province and eastern Iraq. The tribe traditionally pronounced its name as *Kełeř*, while “Kalhor” was the Persian pronunciation. The name was also sometimes rendered as “Kalkhur.” The Guran tribe referred to the Kalhor as *keł weř*. The sources discuss competing explanations of the name, including “mountain goat” and “the sun,” “buffalo-eaters,” and a term associated with conquering or capturing a mountain goat.

The Kalhor are mostly Shia Muslims, with Yarsani and Ali-Illahi minorities. The tribe was historically Yarsani and increasingly converted to Shia Islam after the 1830s. Some Kalhor remained Yarsani after the majority became Shia Muslim.
