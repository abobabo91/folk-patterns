## Overview

Rum Arabs, also called Rūm, are an ethnoreligious Eastern Christian group native to the Levant. Their historical homeland includes Syria, Lebanon, Israel, Palestine, Jordan, and the southern Turkish province of Hatay, including Antakya, ancient Antioch. In Syria, communities are concentrated in Wadi al-Nasara, known as the Valley of the Christians, and in the surrounding cities of Mhardeh, Hama, and Homs; historical communities also exist in Aleppo, Damascus, and Latakia. They primarily speak Levantine Arabic, while Maaloula near Damascus preserves a Western Aramaic dialect. Members belong mainly to the Greek Orthodox Church of Antioch or the Melkite Greek Catholic Church. The worldwide population is stated as more than 2 to 3 million Antiochian Greek Orthodox and Greek Catholic Christians.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources mention churches, monasteries, and religious sites. Named religious buildings and sites include the Monastery of Our Lady of Balamand, the churches and monasteries of Bab Tuma, and the Greek Orthodox churches under the Jerusalem Patriarchate.

## Music & performance

The sources do state that Greek was historically used in liturgy and that Arabic gradually replaced Greek as the primary liturgical and administrative language of the Greek Orthodox Church of Antioch during the early modern period, especially in the 17th–18th centuries.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document religious and historical events involving the community. Christianity spread through the region and dominated by the fourth century. In Jerusalem, the majority population during the Arab conquest was Christian, and the Umariyya Covenant, also called the Covenant of Omar, allowed religious freedom for Christians in exchange for jizya. The sources also describe the 1724 schism within the Patriarchate of Antioch, after which one part entered communion with the Roman Catholic Church and formed the Melkite Greek Catholic Church, while the remaining majority continued as the Greek Orthodox Church of Antioch.

The sources mention religious sites and communal refuge during violence, including the churches and monasteries of Bab Tuma, meaning “Saint Thomas’ Gate,” where Antiochians took refuge during the Damascus Massacre of 1860.

## Foodways

They do state that the homeland of the Antiochians, known as the Diocese of the East, was historically a major commercial and agricultural area of the Empire.

## Oral tradition & literature

They identify several writers associated with the community, including Abdallah Marrash, Francis Marrash, Maryana Marrash, Jurji Zaydan, and George Antonius. Francis Marrash is described as a Syrian scholar, publicist, writer, and poet; Maryana Marrash as a Syrian writer and poet; and Jurji Zaydan as a Lebanese novelist, journalist, editor, and teacher.

## Language & religion

Rum Arabs primarily speak Levantine Arabic. Maaloula near Damascus is identified as one of the few places where a Western Aramaic dialect is still spoken. During the Byzantine period, Byzantine or Medieval Greek, known as “Romaic,” stood between the Hellenistic, or Koine, and modern phases of Greek. “Romaic” referred to the vulgar Greek language, while “Hellenic” was its literary or doctrinal form. Greek also remained the administrative language in the new Muslim territories for more than 50 years after the Arab conquests.

The community is associated with the Greek Orthodox Church of Antioch and the Melkite Greek Catholic Church. In 1724, a portion of the hierarchy of the Patriarchate of Antioch entered communion with the Roman Catholic Church, forming the Melkite Greek Catholic Church. Arabic later became the primary liturgical and administrative language of the Greek Orthodox Church of Antioch. Most Antiochian Greek Christians identify as Arabs, although some reject the Arab label and identify as Levantine Greek.
