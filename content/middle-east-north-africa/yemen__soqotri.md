## Overview

Soqotrans, also called Socotrans, are a South Arabian ethnographic group native to the Yemeni island of Socotra. They primarily inhabit the Socotra Archipelago, including Socotra island and the Abd al Kuri, Darsah and Samhah districts of the Amanat Al Asimah governorate, Yemen. Ethnologue estimated 110,000 Soqotri speakers in 2020, while the 1990 Yemeni census of Socotra recorded around 57,000 Soqotrans. They speak Soqotri, a Modern South Arabian language in the Afroasiatic family. The sources present Soqotri poetry, song, oral literature and folklore preservation as important aspects of the group’s cultural life, while describing the language as severely endangered under pressure from Arabic, modernization and changing attitudes among younger people.

## Material culture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Soqotri language source mentions *tˤarb*, meaning “a piece of wood,” and *tob*, meaning “cloth,”.

## Music & performance

Poetry and song were described as a normal part of everyday life on Socotra and as a means of communicating with humans, animals, spirits of the dead, jinn sorcerers and the divine. Soqotri couplets are presented as the basic building blocks of Soqotri poetry and song. One sample is a humorous piece about a stingy fisherman; its translated version describes a man named Abdullah who conceals fish until bluebottles swarm around them. Another sample consists of greetings and criticism of someone who does not wipe his face after eating.

The sources state that Soqotri poetry has been overlooked and that the skill of the island’s poets has been ignored. They also describe Soqotri folklore as endangered, partly because the language has been neglected and partly because modernization is changing the cultural environment. An Arabic-based alphabet developed in 2014 by a Russian team led by Vitaly Naumkin helped narrators of oral lore collaborate with researchers in composing literature regarded as truthful to its origins.

No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription or museum catalogue record supplied with the sources documents Soqotri music or performance.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that Soqotrans were historically Nestorian Christians from the Christianization of the island between the 4th and 6th centuries until the 15th century, and that the island was occupied by the Mehri Sultanate in 1480; this occupation led to the slow Islamisation of the Soqotrans.

## Foodways

The Soqotri language source includes a poetic comparison involving goats feeding on *šeber* plants, part of the Euphorbia plant group. These plants survive long dry seasons and have a milky latex that goats feed on during shortage. The source states that the latex provides no nutritious benefit but helps relieve the goats’ thirst; when goats feed on latex from damaged plants, it stains their muzzles and causes sores around the mouth. This information appears as cultural knowledge needed to understand a poetic image, not as a description of human foodways.

## Oral tradition & literature

Soqotri oral literature includes poetry, song and folktales. The sources state that poetry and song formerly formed part of everyday communication and could address humans, animals, spirits of the dead, jinn sorcerers and the divine. They give examples of couplets and translations, including a humorous fisherman piece and a passage comparing a shameless man to goats feeding on *šeber* plants.

The sources also refer to a *Mehazelo* tale fragment identified as a Cinderella tale and to a Two Brothers tale fragment. A Russian team led by Vitaly Naumkin developed an Arabic-based alphabet for Soqotri in 2014 after five years of work. The orthography enabled narrators of oral lore to work with researchers in composing literature faithful to its origins. The *Corpus of Soqotri Oral Literature*, edited or authored by Vitaly Naumkin, Leonid Kogan, Dmitry Cherkashin, Maria Bulakh and Ekaterina Vizirova, is listed as a major documentation project.

## Language & religion

Soqotri is a South Semitic language spoken by the Soqotrans on the islands of the Socotra Archipelago in Yemen. It belongs to the group known as Modern South Arabian languages and is classified within the Afro-Asiatic and Semitic language families. The sources state that it is not Arabic, although Arabic is also spoken in dialectal form on Socotra. Soqotri has several dialects, including ’Abd Al-Kuri, Central Soqotri, Northern Soqotri, Southern Soqotri and Western Soqotri; another account identifies Eastern, Central and Western dialects. The language is written using the Naskh variant of the Arabic script and is also transcribed with the Latin script.

Soqotri has no official status. It is described as severely endangered because Arabic functions as the privileged lingua franca, the government has neglected Soqotri, and younger people may regard it as irrelevant to improving the island’s socio-economic status. Most Soqotrans are Sunni Muslim. The sources describe earlier Nestorian Christianity and the subsequent slow Islamisation of the island.
