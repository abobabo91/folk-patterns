## Overview

Egyptians are the native people of the Nile Valley, densely settled along the river with deep documented continuity in craft and agriculture. Their daily language is Egyptian Arabic or Sa'idi Arabic; Coptic survives in the Orthodox Church. The fellahin reckon agricultural seasons by pharaonic month names, and Cairo, Akhmim, Fustat and Siwa workshops carry techniques traceable through Coptic, Fatimid and Mamluk periods.

## Material culture

Egyptian material culture spans thousands of years of textiles, ceramics and architectural forms shaped by the Nile and preserved by desert climate.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Coptic tapestry-woven textiles from the 3rd century onward represent the only large surviving corpus of late-antique cloth, while later traditions include khayamiya appliqué, Assiut tulle and Siwa embroidery.

- **Coptic textile** (*orbiculi, tabulae*) — Wool-and-linen tapestry-woven roundels, squares and clavus bands set into undyed linen tunics, produced from the 3rd century onward and preserved in the thousands by desert burial.
- **Coptic tunic** (*tunica*) — A single-piece linen garment woven to shape on the loom with sleeves, decorated in situ with geometric and Christian imagery.
- **Khayamiya appliqué** (*Sharia al-Khayamiya*) — Hand-stitched cotton appliqué panels made in the Street of the Tentmakers in Cairo, originally for ceremonial tents, with layered cut-cloth arabesques and calligraphic cartouches sewn with a needle held under the work.
- **Assiut tulle** (*tulle bi telli*) — Net ground darned with flattened silver or nickel strip, worked in Asyut and Upper Egypt from the late 19th century, producing chunky geometric silhouettes like camels, palms and dancing figures in solid metal against near-invisible mesh.
- **Siwa embroidery** (*asherah*) — Dense silk polychrome on black or indigo cotton from the Siwa Oasis, concentrated in the wedding dress with a sunburst of orange, yellow and red radiating from the neck slit and mother-of-pearl buttons down the chest.

### Clothing & dress

The galabeya is the near-universal male garment, while village women wear a black overdress over printed cotton with a head-veil, and Sinai Bedouin and Siwan women retain the most ornamented dress traditions.

- **Galabeya** (*jallabiyya*) — An ankle-length unfitted cotton or wool shift with a straight or T-cut neck slit worn by men; the Sa'idi version is fuller and worn with a white skullcap and wound turban.
- **Undershirt** (*ʿaraqi*) — A striped undershirt worn under the Sa'idi galabeya.
- **Overdress** (*tob sebleh*) — A black overdress worn by village women in the south over a printed cotton dress.
- **Head-veil** (*tarha*) — A veil worn by village women, sometimes with the face-veil hung with coins in former practice.
- **Siwan bridal dress** (*asherah*) — A stack of seven embroidered dresses worn over the wedding week with a silver disc at the throat.

### Architecture

The Nile Valley house is built of sun-dried mudbrick around a courtyard, while Upper Egyptian builders raise mudbrick vaults and domes without centring, and the Cairene courtyard house features the mashrabiya oriel screen.

- **Mudbrick** (*tub labin*) — Nile silt puddled with chopped straw and sun-dried, the standard building material for valley houses laid up around a courtyard.
- **Courtyard** (*hosh*) — The central open court of the valley house that holds the oven, grain bins and animals.
- **Oven** (*furn*) — A communal cooking facility located in the courtyard of the mudbrick house.
- **Mashrabiya** — A projecting oriel screen of thousands of hand-turned wooden spindles assembled without glue, breathing and cooling stored water jars while screening women from the street.
- **Wind-catcher** (*malqaf*) — A north-facing architectural feature that channels air into the Cairene courtyard house.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Fatimid and later glazed ware from Fustat, pharaonic Egyptian faience, unglazed village potteryware, and Cairo's woodwork and metal-work traditions reflect continuous craft lineage.

- **Fustat pottery** (*Fustat*) — Glazed ware from Old Cairo including Fatimid lustre, splash-and-sgraffito, and white-ground blue-painted vessels that carried Chinese influence west.
- **Water jar** (*zir*) — An unglazed porous water jar made by village potters and kept in shade to cool by evaporation.
- **Drinking vessel** (*qulla*) — The ubiquitous drinking vessel made in unglazed pottery.
- **Qur'an stand** (*kursi*) — A folding stand for the Qur'an produced by Cairene woodwork craftsmen with mother-of-pearl inlay.
- **Water-lift** (*shadouf*) — A counterweighted lift-pole for raising Nile water, remaining standard field equipment into the 20th century.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Egyptian silver amulets include the khamsa hand and Qur'anic cylinder case, Nubian jewelry is heavier and more architectural, and the Coptic wrist cross tattoo is the most distinctive Egyptian body marking.

- **Khamsa hand** (*khamsa*) — A silver amulet pendant shaped as a hand against the evil eye, worn as jewelry and protection.
- **Qur'anic cylinder case** (*hirz*) — A silver amulet pendant holding Qur'anic text, functioning as protection.
- **Nose ring** (*zummam*) — A piece of Nubian jewelry worn by women.
- **Bridal ornament** (*hilaq*) — A Nubian bridal jewelry piece.
- **Coptic wrist cross** (*cross*) — A small cross tattooed on the inner wrist in childhood, functioning as an unremovable declaration of faith and the most distinctive Egyptian body marking.

## Music & performance

Urban takht ensemble music backed the great singers, while village and Sa'idi music runs on the rababa spike fiddle and double-reed mizmar, and Nubian music is driven by frame drum and five-tone kisir lyre.

- **ʿud** — A short-necked lute that forms the foundation of the urban takht ensemble.
- **Qanun** — A trapezoidal plucked zither used in the classical takht chamber ensemble.
- **Nay** — An end-blown reed flute played in the takht ensemble.
- **Rababa** — A two- or four-string spike fiddle that accompanies epic recitation in village and Sa'idi music.
- **Arghul** — A single-reed clarinet with an extended drone pipe unique to Egypt.

## Dance & theatre

Raqs sharqi is the solo torso-and-hip dance descended from professional dancing women, while men perform the Sa'idi stick duel and Sufi orders practise the spinning tanoura.

- **Raqs sharqi** — The solo torso-and-hip dance of the urban stage, descended from the repertoire of professional ghawazi dancing women and ʿawalim singer-entertainers.
- **Raqs baladi** — A village dance performed socially by women at weddings.
- **Tahtib** — A Sa'idi stick duel of cane staves fought to mizmar and drum, now performed in stylized form as raqs al-assaya.
- **Tanoura** — The spinning dance of the Egyptian Mevlevi and Sufi orders, whirling weighted concentric skirts while manipulating frame drums and discs.
- **Aragoz** — A hand-puppet glove figure whose voice is produced with a reed swazzle and whose sketches satirize authority.

## Festivals & rituals

Sham al-Nessim spring festival and mulid saints' birthdays are the great popular celebrations, while Ramadan brings the fanus lantern and street iftar tables, and life-cycle rites centre on the sebou naming ceremony.

- **Sham al-Nessim** — The Monday after Coptic Easter kept by Muslims and Christians alike, with families eating salted fesikh, spring onions and coloured eggs in the open air.
- **Mulid al-Nabi** — The Prophet's birthday celebration featuring sugar dolls and horse figures.
- **Mulid al-Sayyida Zaynab** (*Mulid*) — A saint's birthday celebration held in Cairo drawing large crowds.
- **Fanus lantern** (*fanus*) — A lantern that appears during Ramadan street celebrations.
- **Sebou** — The seventh-day naming ceremony for a newborn, where the baby is carried in a sieve and salt is scattered against the evil eye.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Sirat Bani Hilal, the cycle of the Banu Hilal tribe's migration, is still performed in rhymed colloquial verse by a shaʿir poet-singer accompanying himself on the rababa, the last living Arabic oral epic tradition.

- **Sirat Bani Hilal** — The great Egyptian epic cycle of the Banu Hilal tribe's migration west and the fate of hero Abu Zayd al-Hilali, sung in rhymed colloquial verse over many nights and still performed in Sharqiyya and Upper Egypt.
- **Sirat al-Zahir Baybars** — An Egyptian epic centred on the Mamluk sultan.
- **Alf Layla wa-Layla** — The Thousand and One Nights, Egyptian recensions of which supply much of the collection's urban texture.
- **Mawwal** — A compact narrative or lament quatrain built on punning rhyme.
- **Nukta** — The political joke, a recognized Egyptian genre in its own right.

## Language & religion

Egyptian Arabic and Sa'idi Arabic are Semitic Afroasiatic languages; Coptic survives liturgically; between 84–90% of Egyptians are Muslim with substantial Sufi constituencies, and 10–15% are Christian Coptic Orthodox.

- **Egyptian Arabic** (*Masri*) — A Semitic Afroasiatic language, the most widely understood Arabic vernacular in the Arab world because of Cairo's film and music industries.
- **Coptic** — Ancient Egyptian written in the Greek alphabet plus six Demotic letters, the majority spoken language until at least the 10th century and remaining in liturgical use in Bohairic form.
- **Sufi orders** (*turuq*) — Shadhiliyya, Rifaʿiyya and Burhaniyya orders constitute the living body of folk religion through dhikr, mulid and shrine visitation.
- **Dhikr** — A Sufi devotional practice of ritual recitation and remembrance.
- **Evil eye** (*ʿayn*) — A spiritual harm guarded against across both Muslim and Christian communities with amulets, khamsa, salt and Qur'anic hirz cases.
