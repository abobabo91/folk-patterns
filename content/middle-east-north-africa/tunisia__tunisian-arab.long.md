## Overview

Tunisian Arabs are the Arabic-speaking majority of Tunisia, roughly eleven million people occupying the Sahel coastal plain from Hammamet to Sfax, the Medjerda river valley, the holy inland city of Kairouan, the Cap Bon peninsula, and the date-palm oases of the Jerid (Tozeur, Nefta) at the Saharan margin. They speak *Derja*, a Maghrebi Arabic dialect of the Semitic branch of Afroasiatic, heavily stratified with Amazigh substrate, Andalusi, Turkish and Italian vocabulary. Their population is the product of successive layers — Phoenician-Punic Carthage, the Arab conquests that founded Kairouan in 670 CE, the Banu Hilal migrations of the 11th century, Andalusi Muslim refugees expelled from Spain after 1609, and Ottoman rule from 1574. In folk-culture terms Tunisia matters because these layers stayed visible rather than merging: a Kairouan knotted carpet, a Sejnane hand-built figurine, and a Testour Andalusi wedding song each belong to a different historical stratum, all still practiced.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Kairouan carpet** (*zerbia kairouaniya*) — the symmetrically knotted wool pile carpet of Kairouan, built around a central lozenge medallion (*el-mihrab* or *el-hurma*) with stepped corner spandrels and multiple concentric guard borders. Unlike Anatolian prayer rugs it descends from, the Kairouan field is dense with small filler motifs and uses madder red and indigo on a wool warp; it is the only major knotted-pile tradition in the Maghreb and was codified by an urban guild system under the beys.

**Mergoum** (also *margoum*) — a flatweave in weft-faced tapestry and supplementary-weft technique, woven in bands of geometry across the width rather than centred on a medallion. Gafsa, Oudref and the Jerid produce the boldest versions; the banded format distinguishes it from the medallion-centred pile carpet and links it to the older Amazigh loom repertoire.

**Klim / kilim tunisien** — the plain slit-tapestry flatweave, often combined with *mergoum* bands in a single piece, used as blanket, floor cover and tent divider.

**Fouta** — the striped cotton or cotton-silk bath and body wrap of the hammam, woven in plain weave with warp stripes; the Tunisian *fouta* is the ancestor of the modern global "Turkish towel" trade and remains woven in Ksar Hellal and Moknine.

**Sifsari** — the fine cream silk or silk-cotton outer wrap worn by urban Tunisois women, undecorated and nearly translucent, distinct from the heavier Algerian *haik* by its yellowish natural-silk tone.

**Tunisian brocade** (*ketfiya*, *fouta w blousa* silks) — Djerba and Mahdia silk weaving with metallic-thread supplementary weft, producing the gold-shot wedding textiles of the Sahel.

**Raf Raf embroidery** — dense silk and metal-thread floss embroidery from the Cap Bon village of Raf Raf, worked in solid blocks of colour on wedding tunics, with a heavy couched-gold register that separates it from the lighter Hammamet and Nabeul work.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Khomsa* (open hand of five, apotropaic), *el-mihrab* (the carpet's central niche-lozenge), *ain* (eye, against the evil eye), *msalleb* (cross/quatrefoil), *chebka* (net or lattice field), *hout* (fish, fertility), *nakhla* (date palm), *mchat* (comb), *aqrab* (scorpion), *nejma* (star, usually eight-pointed).

### Clothing & dress

Men's dress centres on the *jebba*, a sleeveless or short-sleeved tunic of wool, silk or cotton worn over a shirt and the baggy *sirwal* trousers, with a waistcoat (*farmla*) and wide sash (*hzem*) between them. Over it goes the *burnous*, the hooded sleeveless wool mantle of the Maghreb, white for ceremony and brown or undyed for work. The *chechia*, the felted crimson skullcap, is the national marker: it is knitted from wool, fulled, dyed and brushed in the Souk des Chéchias of Tunis, and its craftsmen (*chaouachis*) constitute one of the oldest continuous guilds in the country. Footwear is the *balgha*, a backless leather slipper.

Women wear the wrapped *melia* or *houli*, a rectangle of wool or cotton pinned at both shoulders with large fibulae (*khlel*) and belted, over a tunic; the *blousa* is the embroidered overdress of the Sahel. Outdoors urban women wore the *sifsari*, rural women the *haik*. Bridal costume is regionally specific — the Hammamet bride's gold-embroidered *keswa*, the Djerba silk ensemble, the Raf Raf couched-gold tunic — and is worn across a sequence of nights rather than once.

### Architecture

The Tunisian house is inward-facing: a *dar* built around a paved courtyard (*wast ed-dar*) with rooms opening onto it through horseshoe-arched doorways, the street façade blank except for a studded, arched door painted the blue of Sidi Bou Saïd. Urban interiors are lined to shoulder height with glazed tile panels (*qallaline* tiles from the Tunis workshops), with carved stucco (*naqsh hadida*) above and painted cedar ceilings. The medinas of Tunis, Sousse and Kairouan organise these houses around a congregational mosque, covered souks and *funduqs* (caravanserais).

Distinctive rural forms include the *ghorfa* — the barrel-vaulted, stacked storage cell of the Dahar, aggregated into fortified granary complexes (*ksour*) at Medenine and Ksar Ouled Soltane — and the troglodyte pit-house of Matmata, where a sunken courtyard is dug into the loess and living rooms tunnelled off it. Djerba's landscape is built of whitewashed, domed *menzel* farmstead compounds and squat fortress-mosques. Coastal defence is anchored by the *ribat* of Monastir and Sousse, 8th–9th century fortified frontier monasteries, and the Great Mosque of Kairouan, whose square minaret set the model for Maghrebi and Andalusi mosque towers.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**Nabeul pottery** is the polychrome, tin-glazed and painted wheelwork of the Cap Bon — plates, tiles and jars in cobalt, green and amber with fish, floral arabesque and *khomsa* designs, continuous with the Andalusi-Ottoman *qallaline* tile tradition of Tunis. **Guellala pottery** on Djerba is its opposite: unglazed or minimally glazed utilitarian earthenware from local clay, thrown by a male potter caste and fired in subterranean kilns, producing amphorae, couscous steamers (*keskes*) and water jars. **Sejnane pottery**, made in the Mogod hills of the north by women without a wheel, is coil-built, burnished, open-fired in straw, and painted after firing with red ochre and black geometric bands and stylized female figurines — inscribed by UNESCO in 2018 as *Pottery skills of the women of Sejnane*. Metalwork includes engraved and pierced brass trays and lanterns from the Souk el-Nhas in Tunis, cedar and olive-wood chests, and the *tabouna* clay bread oven found in nearly every rural courtyard.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver is rural and Amazigh-inflected, gold urban and Andalusi-Ottoman. The **Moknine** and Djerba workshops produced the classic repertoire: *khlel*, the large triangular or disc fibulae joined by a chain that pin the *melia*; *khomsa*, the hand-of-Fatima pendant, in filigree, enamel or plain sheet; *hedid* and *skhab* necklaces strung with amber, clove and coral; heavy ankle rings (*khalkhal*) and hinged bracelets (*mesaïs*). Urban bridal gold includes the *harraz*, a hinged amulet case holding a written charm, and the *kholkhal* in chased gold. Cloisonné enamel in green, blue and yellow on silver is a Moknine and Djerba speciality. Women's facial and hand tattooing (*wachma*) in blue-black indigo lines — chevrons on the chin, a vertical line between the brows — was widespread in the rural north and Sahel among older generations. Henna (*hanna*) is applied in geometric stains at weddings, circumcisions and returns from pilgrimage, with the bride's night of henna forming a distinct ceremony.

## Music & performance

Tunisian art music is the *malouf*, the local branch of the Andalusi *nūba* suite tradition carried west by refugees from Seville and Córdoba and reorganised under the Ottoman beys; it is structured into thirteen modal suites, each progressing through fixed rhythmic movements (*msaddar*, *btayhi*, *barwal*, *draj*, *khafif*, *khatm*). The Rashidiyya Institute, founded in Tunis in 1934, transcribed and standardised the repertoire and remains its institutional home. The ensemble uses the *oud arbi* (the four-course North African lute, distinct from the Middle Eastern *oud sharqi*), *rebab* (bowed spike fiddle), *naqarat* (small kettledrums), *tar* and *darbuka*.

Rural and popular music runs on the *mizwid*, a mouth-blown bagpipe with two chanters and a goatskin bag, paired with the *bendir* frame drum and the *darbuka*; *mizwid* song is the dominant urban working-class genre, sung in *Derja* with an insistent duple pulse. The *zukra* (shawm) and *tabl* (double-headed drum) announce weddings and processions. Sufi brotherhoods — the Aissawa, Sulamiyya and Tijaniyya — maintain their own sung *hadra* liturgy of drums, frame drums and responsorial chant, and the *stambeli* tradition of Tunis, brought by sub-Saharan enslaved populations, is a healing music built on the *gumbri* three-string bass lute and iron *shqashiq* clappers.

## Dance & theatre

Wedding and festival dance is largely women's dance — *raqs sharqi*-adjacent hip and torso articulation performed indoors among women — alongside the distinctive hair dance of the south and the Jerid, in which women whip loosened hair in circles to the *mizwid* and *bendir*. Men's line dance in the Sahel and Cap Bon moves shoulder-to-shoulder to the *zukra*. The *stambeli* possession ceremony is danced as ritual rather than entertainment, each spirit summoned by its own rhythm and colour. Sufi *hadra* produces its own rocking, standing dhikr movement. Tunisia's dramatic tradition includes *karakouz* (*karagöz*), the Ottoman shadow-puppet theatre of satirical leather figures played in coffee houses, which declined in the 20th century, and the storyteller (*fdawi*) who performed serial epic in cafés during Ramadan nights.

## Festivals & rituals

The year is set by the Islamic calendar: Ramadan, with its nightly *ftour* and café storytelling; *Eid al-Fitr* (*Aïd es-Seghir*); *Eid al-Adha* (*Aïd el-Kebir*), when a sheep is slaughtered in the courtyard; and *Mouled*, the Prophet's birthday, marked in Kairouan by the *assida zgougou* pudding. Cutting across it is the solar agricultural calendar and the local saint's festivals (*zarda* or *mawsim*) — pilgrimage-picnics at a marabout's tomb with animal sacrifice, Sufi processions and communal cooking. Sidi Bou Saïd, Sidi Ali Azouz and the Kairouan saints each draw their own. The **Festival International du Sahara** at Douz (December) stages camel racing, *mizwid* and *saluki* coursing; Sfax and Testour hold their own malouf festivals.

Life-cycle ritual is dense. Birth is followed by the seventh-day naming (*sboua*) with *assida* and sacrifice. Male circumcision (*tahara*) is a public celebration with the boy dressed in miniature *jebba* and gold. Weddings run over a week: the *outia* (trousseau display), *hammam* day, the *hanna* night of henna, the *outia* procession of the bride's furniture, and the *jelwa* unveiling at which the bride is presented in successive regional costumes. Death brings three days of receiving mourners, Quranic recitation, and the fortieth-day *arbaïn* commemoration.

## Foodways

Wheat is the base: hard durum becomes *couscous* (*kosksi*), hand-rolled and steamed in a *keskes* over a stew of lamb, fish or vegetables, and the national dish; barley couscous (*mhamsa*, *borghol*) belongs to the countryside. *Tabouna* bread is slapped onto the wall of a clay oven. The defining seasoning is *harissa*, a paste of dried red chilli, garlic, caraway and coriander, and the defining preserve is the salted-and-fermented lemon and the pickled vegetable *torshi*. Signature dishes include *brik*, a triangle of *malsouka* pastry folded around egg, tuna and parsley and deep-fried; *lablabi*, chickpea broth poured over torn bread with cumin, harissa and a raw egg; *ojja*, eggs poached in tomato, pepper and *merguez*; *mloukhia*, a slow-cooked jute-leaf stew eaten at New Year; *chorba frik* with green freekeh wheat; and *kamounia*, cumin-heavy offal stew. The Sahel and Kerkennah cook grilled and stewed fish with *charmoula* — here an onion-and-raisin sweet-sour relish eaten at Eid, not the Moroccan herb marinade. Sweets include *makroudh*, the date-filled semolina diamond of Kairouan, *zlebia* and *bambalouni*. Mint tea is served with pine nuts (*sanaouber*) or almonds; Tunisian coffee is often scented with rosewater or *zhar* (orange-blossom water) distilled in Nabeul each spring.

## Oral tradition & literature

The café storyteller (*fdawi*) narrated serialized versions of the *Sirat Bani Hilal*, the Arabic oral epic of the Banu Hilal tribes' westward migration into the Maghreb — the historical event that arabized rural Tunisia in the 11th century, and thus an origin story rather than a borrowed one. The *Antar* romance and the *Sirat al-Zahir Baybars* circulated in the same setting. Women's oral genres include the lullaby, the wedding *zaghrouta* (ululation) and sung laments. Proverb (*mathal*) and riddle (*hajjaya*) exchange is domestic and competitive, and folktales open with the formula *hajitek ma jitek* ("I'll tell you a tale..."). *Malouf* poetry retains Andalusi strophic forms — the *muwashshah* and the colloquial *zajal* — and the 20th-century poet Abou el Kacem Chebbi wrote in a register drawing on both classical and vernacular sources. The Centre des Musiques Arabes et Méditerranéennes at Ennejma Ezzahra in Sidi Bou Saïd archives the sung repertoire.

## Language & religion

Tunisian Arabic (*Derja*) is a Maghrebi Arabic variety within Central Semitic, written when written at all in Arabic script; Modern Standard Arabic is the language of administration and French remains a widespread second language of education and commerce. Amazigh (Chenini, Douiret, Djerba) survives in a handful of southern villages. The overwhelming majority are Sunni Muslims of the Maliki school — the distinctive Maghrebi legal tradition — with an Ibadi Muslim community on Djerba and a very old Jewish community centred on the El Ghriba synagogue there, whose annual Lag BaOmer pilgrimage continues. Folk religion runs through the cult of the *wali* (saint), with tomb visitation, vow-making and *zarda* sacrifice, and through Sufi orders (Aissawa, Qadiriyya, Tijaniyya, Rahmaniyya). Protective practice is pervasive: the *khomsa*, the eye motif, incense (*bkhour*), and *stambeli* healing for affliction attributed to spirits.
