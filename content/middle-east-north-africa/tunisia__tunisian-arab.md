## Overview

The Tunisian Arabs (*at-Tūnisiyyūn*, sing. *Tūnisī*) are the Arabic-speaking majority population of the Republic of Tunisia, numbering roughly 12 million within the country and forming sizeable diasporas in France, Italy, Germany and the Gulf. Their vernacular, Tunisian Arabic (*Tounsi* or *Darja*), belongs to the Maghrebi branch of Arabic and rests on a substrate of Berber (Amazigh), Punic, Latin and later Andalusi, Ottoman-Turkish, Italian and French borrowings, mirroring the successive layers of Carthaginian, Roman, Vandal, Byzantine, Arab, Hilalian, Hafsid, Ottoman and French colonial rule. Culturally they occupy the narrow, well-watered Sahel and Tell between the Mediterranean and the Saharan margins, with historic urban cores at Tunis, Kairouan (*al-Qayrawān*), Sfax, Sousse, Mahdia, Monastir and the *ksour* of the south. Their identity fused after the eleventh-century Banū Hilāl migrations, when incoming Arab tribes Arabised the older Ifriqiyan Berber population, and was further stratified by the arrival of Andalusi Moriscos in the seventeenth century — refugees who reshaped urban craft, agriculture and music in the Medjerda valley and the coastal cities. In folk-culture terms the Tunisian Arabs matter as heirs of a distinctly Mediterranean-Islamic synthesis: the knotted *zerbia* carpets of Kairouan, the flat-woven *mergoum* of the steppe, the crimson *chechia* felt cap, the Andalusi *maʾlūf* repertoire, the polychrome tilework of the Tunis medina, and the black-fired figurative pottery of Sejnane all sit within their tradition.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Zerbia / *tapis tunisien*** — the knotted pile carpet, whose most prestigious variant is the **Kairouan carpet** (*zarbiyya qayrawāniyya*), a symmetrically knotted wool rug organised around a central mihrab-like medallion (*ṭāra*) flanked by pendants and framed by multiple guard borders, said to have been codified in the early nineteenth century by the daughter of a local bey and now graded officially by knot density (*ʿalūsha*, *shaʿshūʿī*, *zarbiyya*).

**Mergoum** (*marqūm*, also *margoum*) — a warp-faced tapestry-weave rug with tightly packed geometric lozenges, hooked crosses and zigzag bands in madder red, indigo and undyed wool, produced across the central steppe (Gafsa, Oudref, El Djem) and worn as much as a wall hanging as a floor covering.

**Klim / kilim tunisien** — a lighter slit-tapestry flat-weave, often combined in a single piece with sections of *mergoum* and bands of pile knotting, characteristic of the Gafsa and Djerid oases where Berber and Arab motif vocabularies overlap.

**Tunisian brocade** (*brokār* or *ṭarz al-fīḍḍa*) — the silk-and-metal-thread cloth of the coastal cities, most famously the gold- and silver-brocaded wedding *keswa el-kbira* of Hammamet, Nabeul, Mahdia and the Jewish communities of Djerba, woven on the drawloom and cut into unfitted tunics and jackets.

**Fouta tunisienne** — the long striped cotton or cotton-linen wrap, historically the bathing and household cloth of the *ḥammām*, now equally a beach towel and a light coverlet; the Djerba and Ksibet el-Médiouni looms are the surviving centres.

**Tunisian embroidery** — a family of regional schools rather than a single style. **Raf Raf embroidery** on the Cap Bon peninsula is worked in dense polychrome silk on velvet or fine cotton, covering the bodice and cuffs of the bridal costume; the Nabeul, Moknine and Mahdia schools each keep distinct palettes and stitch inventories, and the *ṭarz al-ʿaqd* (knotted couching) of the capital is used for men's ceremonial *jebba*.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Khomsa* (hand of Fatima, apotropaic), *ʿayn* (eye), *samaka* (fish, fertility and evil-eye deflection), *ḥūt* and paired doves, *nakhla* (date palm), *tāʾūs* (peacock), *warda* (rose), *hilāl* (crescent), the eight-pointed Kairouan star, the *miḥrāb* niche, and dense *islimi* scrollwork inherited from the Andalusi repertoire.

### Clothing & dress

Men's traditional dress in the coastal cities centres on the *jebba* — an unfitted sleeveless or short-sleeved tunic of fine wool, silk or *lūbān* linen worn over a long shirt (*qamīja*) and *sirwāl* trousers, belted with a folded sash and, for ceremony, covered by the woollen *burnous*, the hooded Maghrebi cloak whose white winter and dark summer versions signal both region and status. The **chechia** (*shāshiyya*) — the soft crimson knitted-and-felted wool cap produced almost exclusively in the *Souk ech-Chaouachine* of Tunis by a guild of craftsmen who card, knit, felt, dye with cochineal or kermes, block and brush the caps in a sequence unchanged since the seventeenth century — is the emblematic Tunisian headgear, exported historically across the Ottoman world. Rural and southern men add the striped woollen *kadroun* or *farmla* jacket. Women's urban dress layers a fine chemise (*qamīja*), a fitted embroidered bodice (*farmla* or *bulīra*), wide *sirwāl* and, for going out, the **sifsari** — the ivory or cream silk-and-cotton wrap draped over the head and body, drawn across the lower face by one hand — that remained ubiquitous in Tunis until the mid-twentieth century. In the countryside women wear the **haik** or *milḥafa*, a rectangular length of undyed wool wrapped and pinned at the shoulders with silver fibulae (*khlāl*) over a long shift, cinched with a woven belt. The *keswa el-kbira*, the "grand costume" of brides in Hammamet and among Tunisian Jewish communities, is a stiff gold-brocaded ensemble of skirt, jacket and conical headdress laden with jewellery.

### Architecture

The vernacular urban house of the Tunis, Sfax and Kairouan medinas is a whitewashed lime-plastered courtyard dwelling (*dār*) with a central open patio (*wast ad-dār*) surrounded by T-shaped reception rooms (*bīt*) whose end alcoves hold the beds. Doorways are framed in carved sandstone with pointed or horseshoe arches and studded in black iron nails arranged in *khomsa*, fish and crescent patterns on doors painted in the celebrated Tunis blue (*azraq tūnisī*) against whitewash. Interiors are faced to shoulder height in polychrome tin-glazed tiles (*qallālīn*) — the Qallaline workshops of eighteenth-century Tunis produced the distinctive panels of vase-of-flowers, cypress and ship motifs — above which stucco (*jibs*) is carved in low-relief arabesques. Monumental architecture is anchored by the Great Mosque of Kairouan (*Jāmiʿ ʿUqba*), the oldest congregational mosque in the Maghreb and the archetype for the region's square-shafted minarets and hypostyle prayer halls, and by the Aghlabid basins, the Zaytuna mosque of Tunis, and the Hafsid and Ottoman madrasas and *zawāyā* of the medinas. In the arid south, Berber-influenced Arab villagers built the fortified granary *ksar* of stacked vaulted cells (*ghorfa*) at Ksar Ouled Soltane and Medenine, the troglodyte courtyard houses of Matmata carved down into the loess, and the coral-stone *ḥūsh* farmsteads of Djerba with their squat domed cisterns and small whitewashed mosques.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Tunisia sustains three living pottery traditions of quite different technical registers. **Nabeul pottery** on Cap Bon is the classic tin-glazed maiolica of the country, worked since the sixteenth-century arrival of Andalusi potters into polychrome plates, tiles and *jarra* storage jars painted in cobalt, manganese, iron-yellow and copper-green on a white ground; the Kharraz and Ben Sedrine workshops set the reference. **Guellala pottery** on Djerba is an unglazed and lead-glazed earthenware and stoneware tradition specialising in enormous olive-oil and water jars (*khābiya*, *qulla*), fired in half-buried updraught kilns and traded across the Mediterranean; the workshops sit atop the clay beds they exploit. **Sejnane pottery**, made by Berber-descended Arab women of the Mogods hills, is a handbuilt, coil-raised, open-fired earthenware inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2018 — figurines, whistles, dolls and domestic vessels painted with iron-oxide red and manganese black in bold zigzag, chevron and eye motifs directly continuous with pre-Roman North African design. Metalwork centres on the chased and pierced brass of the Souk en-Nhas in Tunis (trays, ewers, incense burners), the tin-plated copper cookware of the medinas, and the wrought-iron *mashrabiyya* grilles of urban windows. Carved and painted wooden objects — the *maqʿad* low bench, the *ṣunduq* dowry chest banded in iron, the octagonal *mīda* dining table, and the painted cradle — furnish most traditional households.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Tunisian jewellery divides between an urban Andalusi-Ottoman gold tradition of the coastal medinas and a heavier rural Berber-Arab silver tradition of the interior and the south. Urban brides receive the *ḥarz* gold pectoral, filigree *khātim* rings, granulated bracelets (*mʿaḍḍ*), and the *ʿaqd* choker of pearls and enamelled gold plaques. **Moknine jewelry** in the Sahel is a specialist urban school known for its filigree gold and enamelled work supplied to brides across the Sahel and the coastal towns. **Berber jewellery** of Tunisia — worn by Arabised Berber and rural Arab women alike in the south, at Matmata, Douz, Tataouine and on Djerba — is a heavy silver assemblage of triangular *khlāl* fibulae linked by chains across the chest, forehead diadems (*ṣfīfa*), coin necklaces (*jnāwī*) strung with Ottoman and Spanish coinage, cylindrical amulet cases (*ḥarz*, *ḥrūz*) holding Quranic verses, and dangling temple ornaments. The **khomsa** — the stylised open hand, in silver, gold, glass paste or painted — is the single most pervasive amuletic form, worn as pendant, hung over doors and cradles, and painted on house walls to deflect the evil eye (*ʿayn*). Henna (*ḥinnāʾ*) is applied to the bride's hands and feet during the dedicated *laylat al-ḥinnāʾ* the night before the wedding, in geometric patterns commonly denser at the fingertips.

## Music & performance

The classical core of Tunisian music is the *maʾlūf* — the Andalusi *nawba* suite tradition brought by the seventeenth-century Morisco refugees and codified in Tunis under the Hafsid and Husaynid patronage, organised into thirteen *nawbāt* each pairing metric instrumental preludes (*istiftāḥ*, *msaddar*, *btāyḥī*) with sung poetry in classical Arabic and Andalusi *muwashshaḥ* and *zajal* forms. The *Rashīdiyya* institute, founded in Tunis in 1934, standardised the repertoire and shaped its twentieth-century transmission. Alongside *maʾlūf* sit the urban *mizwid* — a bagpipe-led popular song of the working-class quarters — the Sufi *ḥaḍra* of the Isawiyya, Qadiriyya and Sulamiyya brotherhoods with its trance drumming, and the *stambeli* of the descendants of enslaved sub-Saharans, a healing ritual centred on the *gumbrī* three-string lute and iron *shqāshiq* clappers that is closely paralleled by Moroccan *gnawa*. Rural genres include the *ṣalḥī* and *fazzānī* sung poetry of the Bedouin steppe, the pearl-diving songs of Kerkennah, and the *nūba banjā* of Djerba. Instruments include the *ʿūd ʿarbī* (the four-course Tunisian lute distinct from the Mashriqi *ʿūd*), the *rabāb* spike fiddle, the *nāy* end-blown flute, the *qānūn* trapezoidal zither, the *darbūka* goblet drum, the frame drums *bendīr* and *ṭār*, and outdoors the shawm *zukra* and *mizwid* bagpipe with the *ṭabla* drum, which announce weddings and pilgrimages.

## Dance & theatre

Tunisian folk dance is regional and largely social. Urban women perform seated *raqṣ* and standing *fazzānī* at weddings, the shoulder-shimmy and hip-articulation vocabulary shared with the wider Maghreb. Men of the Sahel and the Cap Bon perform stick and sabre dances (*raqṣ al-ʿaṣā*) in lines. The Bedouin south preserves the *nagāza* and *ḥadra* group dances, and the black Tunisian *stambeli* is as much choreographed possession-trance as music. The tea-house shadow-puppet theatre *Karagöz* (*Qaraqūz*), inherited from Ottoman times and performed in Tunisian Arabic with a stock cast of comic types, survived into the twentieth century in Tunis; secular spoken theatre in Tunisian Arabic has been a strong local form since the pioneering troupes of the 1910s and remains internationally visible through the Journées Théâtrales de Carthage.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual year follows the Islamic lunar calendar — *Ramaḍān* with its nightly *ftūr* meals, communal *tarāwīḥ* prayer and the sweet *zlābiyya* fritters of the Tunis medina; *ʿĪd al-Fiṭr* and *ʿĪd al-Aḍḥā* with the ram sacrifice; and *Mūlid an-Nabī*, celebrated with particular intensity at Kairouan with processions, *ʿassīda zgougou* (a pine-nut pudding of Aleppo-pine pollen), and Sufi *ḥaḍra*. Local pilgrimages (*ziyārāt*) to the shrines of saints (*awliyāʾ*) — Sidi Bou Said above the bay of Tunis, Sidi Mahrez in the capital, Sidi Sahbi in Kairouan — punctuate the year, and the annual *Ghriba* pilgrimage of the Djerba Jewish community draws a broader participation. Weddings run for several days, structured around the *ḥammām* bath, the *ḥinnāʾ* night, the *jelwa* display of the bride in successive costumes including the *keswa el-kbira*, and the groom's procession. The solar Berber-Amazigh new year *Yennayer* is observed in some rural areas with a special couscous.

## Foodways

The Tunisian table is built on wheat — hard-wheat semolina rolled by hand into *couscous* (*kuskus*), the national dish served with fish on the coast, lamb and vegetables inland, and sweetened *masfūf* for feasts — and on olive oil, of which Tunisia is one of the world's largest producers. The country is distinguished within the Maghreb by the intensity of its chilli use through *harīsa*, the pounded paste of dried red chillies, garlic, caraway and coriander that flavours nearly every savoury dish and was itself inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2022. Signature dishes include *brik* (a paper-thin *malsūqa* pastry folded around egg, tuna, capers and parsley and deep-fried), *lablabi* (chickpea soup poured over stale bread with harissa, cumin, olive oil and a raw egg), *shakshūka*, *ʿujja* (herb omelette), *ṭājīn tūnisī* (a set egg-and-cheese casserole quite unlike the Moroccan stew of the same name), *mʿalej* and *makrūna* pasta dishes reflecting Sicilian influence, and grilled *mūrjān* and *loup de mer* on the coast. Preserved foods include *mʿallqa* salted meat, olives in every stage of cure, and the *ʿassīda* semolina puddings of feast days. Sweets are Andalusi and Ottoman in inheritance: *maqrūḍ* (semolina-and-date diamonds of Kairouan), *baqlāwa*, *kaʿak warqa*, *ghrayba*, *bāmbālūnī* (fried dough rings sold on the Sidi Bou Said corniche), and *būza* (a sorghum-and-hazelnut drink). Mint tea (*shāy bin-naʿnaʿ*) is drunk everywhere; strong Turkish-style coffee (*qahwa turkiyya*) flavoured with orange-blossom or rosewater in Tunis; and *būkha*, the fig eau-de-vie of Jewish origin, remains the local spirit.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tunisian oral literature holds the Bedouin *ḥilāliyya* — the sung epic cycle of the Banū Hilāl migration from Najd to Ifriqiya, whose central hero Abū Zayd al-Hilālī is invoked from Egypt to Morocco and whose recitation tradition is on the UNESCO Representative List — alongside a rich body of *malḥūn* strophic poetry in vernacular Arabic, folktales (*ḥkāyāt*) framed by the storytellers (*fdāwī*) of the Tunis and Kairouan cafés, and the collected proverbs (*amthāl*) of everyday speech that are one of the most-studied corpora in the Maghreb. Classical Arabic letters have deep local roots — the Kairouan school of Islamic jurisprudence and the writings of Ibn Rashiq — and the fourteenth-century historian Ibn Khaldūn, born in Tunis, is claimed as a founding figure. Modern Tunisian Arabic literature and the francophone writing of Albert Memmi, Abdelwahab Meddeb and Habib Selmi extend the tradition into the present.

## Language & religion

Tunisian Arabic (*Tounsi* or *ad-dārija at-tūnisiyya*) is the everyday vernacular, sharing a Maghrebi grammar and lexicon with Algerian and Libyan Arabic but marked by heavier Berber substrate, notable Andalusi retentions, and a dense Italian and French borrowing layer from the pre-independence period; French remains a widely used second language and Modern Standard Arabic is the official written language. Small Berber-speaking (*Shelḥa*) communities survive in the south around Chenini, Douiret, Matmata and on Djerba. The overwhelming majority of Tunisian Arabs are Sunni Muslims of the Mālikī school, with the Ibāḍī minority concentrated on Djerba and a very small but ancient Jewish community, most visible in the Ghriba synagogue on Djerba and formerly in the *ḥāra* of Tunis. Popular religion is inflected by the *awliyāʾ* cults and the Sufi orders — Qadiriyya, Shādhiliyya, Isawiyya, Sulamiyya, Tijaniyya, Raḥmāniyya — whose *zawāyā* remain nodes of neighbourhood life.
