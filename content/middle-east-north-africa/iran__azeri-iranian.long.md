## Overview

The Azeris — Azerbaijanis, *Azərbaycanlılar*, called *Torkhā* or Azeri Turks in Persian usage — are the largest ethnic minority in Iran, numbering roughly 15–18 million and concentrated in the northwest: the provinces of East Azerbaijan, West Azerbaijan, Ardabil and Zanjan, with large communities in Tehran, Karaj and Qazvin. Their heartland is the basin of Lake Urmia and the Aras river valley, ringed by the volcanic cone of Sahand and the Talysh and Qaradağ ranges, with Tabriz — a Silk Road entrepôt and repeated Safavid and Qajar capital — as its cultural centre. They speak Azerbaijani, a Turkic language of the Oghuz branch, written in Iran with the Perso-Arabic script; the name also attaches to Old Azeri, an extinct Iranian language of the same region. In folk-culture terms they sit at the hinge of the Turkic, Persian and Caucasian worlds, and produced two of the densest craft repertoires in West Asia: the Tabriz carpet workshop tradition and the flatweave corpus of the Shahsavan tribal confederacy.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Tabriz carpet** — the archetypal urban Persian workshop rug, knotted on cotton or silk foundations with the Turkish (symmetrical) knot, in qualities graded by *raj* (knots per 7 cm). Tabriz weavers use a hooked tool, the *qollab*, which distinguishes their bench technique from the bare-fingered knotting of Kashan or Kerman. Designs run from medallion-and-corner (*lachak-toranj*) to pictorial and *Mahi* (herati "fish") repeats.

**Heriz carpet** — village rugs from the Heriz/Bakhshayesh district east of Tabriz, with a large angular geometric medallion drawn from curvilinear city patterns and coarsened into bold rectilinear form. Woven on very thick wool from sheep grazing mineral-rich slopes near Sahand, they are among the most durable Iranian carpets.

**Verni** — a weft-wrapped Shahsavan flatweave, pileless and thin, with rows of stylized animals — S-shaped dragons, deer, peacocks, camels — in dark madder and indigo against an undyed field. The *verni* is a Qaradağ and Moghan signature; neighbouring Kurds produced nothing comparable in figural density.

**Sumakh / soumak** — the extra-weft wrapping technique that gives *verni* and the larger Shahsavan bedding bags their ribbed, embroidery-like surface, with loose weft tails left hanging on the reverse. Shahsavan *mafrash* (large bedding containers), *khorjin* (saddlebags), salt bags (*namakdan*) and cradle covers are the classic forms.

**Jajim** — a warp-faced narrow-strip weave, sewn edge to edge into covers and bedding wraps, patterned in fine stripes, lozenges and small reciprocal motifs. Iranian Azeri and Shahsavan *jajim* is lighter and more finely spun than Kurdish or Lori equivalents.

**Kelaghayi** — the square silk headscarf, woven in Azerbaijani silk and patterned by wax resist with carved wooden blocks (*qalib*), then dyed in madder, indigo, saffron and walnut. Colour carries meaning: bright grounds for weddings and Nowruz, black or dark blue for mourning. Its production and wearing is an emblem of Azerbaijani identity on both sides of the Aras.

**Azeri embroidery** — chain-stitch and couched metal-thread work on velvet and silk for *takyeh* covers, mirror cases, Qur'an wrappers and bridal trousseau textiles, produced above all in Tabriz and Ardabil workshops.

**Karabagh carpet** — the Caucasian highland design family, long woven by Azeri-speaking villagers, with brilliant reds and a repertoire of *khanlyk* (khan's) medallions, *chelaberd* "eagle" and *cloudband* motifs.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Buta* (the almond/paisley figure, ubiquitous on *kelaghayi* and carpets), *lachak-toranj* (corner-and-medallion), *Mahi dar ham* (interlaced "fish" / herati), *gol* (rosette), *S-ejder* (S-shaped dragon), *shakh-e-ghuch* (ram's horn, fertility and male strength), *pitrak* (burdock, protective), *sarv* (cypress, immortality), *chelipa* (cross-form quartering of the field), *najagi* (axe-head border).

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress in the Azeri villages of Qaradağ and Moghan centres on a short, fitted velvet jacket (*arkhaliq*) over a gathered, tiered skirt (*tuman*) worn in multiple layers — sometimes five or more for festive occasions — with a *köynek* chemise beneath. The head is covered by the *kelaghayi* silk square, knotted over a small cap, and in Shahsavan camps by a tall padded headdress wrapped with cloth. Silver or coin belts (*kemer*), often with a large chased buckle, cinch the waist. Men wore the *chukha*, a knee-length coat with cartridge-tube decoration inherited from the Caucasian circassian coat, over a shirt and wide trousers, with a felt or sheepskin hat (*papaq*) — the black astrakhan *papaq* being the mark of adult status in Ardabil and Tabriz alike. Footwear ranged from stitched leather *charukh* slippers to knee-high riding boots among Shahsavan herders. Bridal dress adds gold-thread embroidery, a full complement of coin jewellery, and a red veil.

### Architecture

Tabriz and Ardabil carry a dense urban tradition: courtyard houses with a *hayat* garden pool, an *orsi* window of coloured glass in wooden lattice, and vaulted brick *zirzamin* cellars for summer heat. The Tabriz Bazaar — a continuous brick-vaulted complex of *timcheh* (domed caravanserai halls), *saray* courtyards and specialist lanes including the carpet *timcheh* of Mozaffariyeh — is the largest covered market of its kind and a UNESCO World Heritage site. Village building in the Sahand foothills uses thick mud-brick or stone walls with flat earth roofs rolled smooth with a stone cylinder; the troglodytic village of **Kandovan**, carved into volcanic tuff cones, is the region's most distinctive vernacular form. Shahsavan pastoralists lived in the *alachiq*, a domed trellis-and-felt tent closer to the Central Asian yurt than to the black goat-hair tents of the Zagros nomads — a structural marker of their Turkic pedigree.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**Tabriz metalwork** covers repoussé and engraved brass and copper trays, ewers (*aftabeh*), bowls and samovar-adjacent vessels, often with *buta* and arabesque fields chased against a hatched ground; copper-smithing lanes still operate in the bazaar. Ardabil and Zanjan are known for knives and inlaid work. **Khatam** — micro-mosaic marquetry assembling bone, brass wire and coloured woods into star-and-polygon lattices, applied to boxes, mirror frames, Qur'an stands and inlaid doors — is a Persian court craft strongly worked in the northwest. Wooden objects include carved spoon sets, cradle frames, and the low *korsi* table set over a brazier and draped with quilts for winter warmth. Village pottery is unglazed or simply green- and turquoise-glazed water jars and yoghurt vessels rather than a named painted school.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Azeri silversmithing produces belt plaques, filigree and granulated brooches, temple pendants worn from the headdress, and above all the *tumar* — a tubular or triangular amulet case holding a written prayer, hung at the neck or shoulder against the evil eye. Coin necklaces and coin-sewn headbands accumulate through a woman's life and pass as dowry. Bridal sets combine gold bracelets (*qolbaq*), rings, and forehead ornaments. Niello and turquoise inlay appear on men's belt fittings and dagger mounts. Henna (*hana*) is applied to a bride's hands and feet at the *hana yakhdi* night before the wedding, and to grooms in some districts; the evil-eye bead (*göz muncuğu*) is pinned to infants and to animals.

## Music & performance

The central tradition is **mugham** (*muğam*) — a modal art music of suites built on named modes (*Rast*, *Shur*, *Segah*, *Chahargah*, *Bayati-Shiraz*), sung with instrumental interludes and improvised sections, texts drawn from Fuzuli, Nasimi and Hafez. The classic trio is *khananda* (singer, holding a frame drum) with *tar* (the Azerbaijani long-necked, waisted, skin-faced lute, played chest-high), *kamancha* (spike fiddle) and *daf* or *qaval* frame drum. Folk and ceremonial music uses the double-reed *zurna* paired with the *naghara* drum for outdoor wedding processions, and the *balaban* (also *duduk*), a soft cylindrical double-reed of apricot wood, for lament and interior playing. The **ashiq** (*aşıq*) tradition is the region's distinctive bardic art: an itinerant singer-poet accompanying himself on the *saz* (long-necked fretted lute), performing narrative *dastan* romances — *Koroghlu*, *Asli və Kərəm*, *Abbas və Gülgəz* — in teahouses, at weddings and in competitive verse duels (*deyishme*). Ardabil, Qaradağ and the Urmia basin sustain distinct *ashiq* schools, with the Tabriz and Khoy lineages among the most documented in Iran.

## Dance & theatre

Azeri social dance is performed in open circles or lines at weddings and Nowruz, to *zurna*-and-*naghara* or *ashiq* accompaniment. Named dances include **Yallı**, a chain dance of the Aras valley and Nakhchivan–Qaradağ borderlands, led by a dancer with a kerchief and combining walking figures with sharp accelerations; **Uzundara**, a slow, lyrical women's dance originally associated with a bride's departure from her home; and **Vağzalı**, the processional tune and dance that accompanies the bride out of the house. Men's dances emphasize upright carriage, toe-and-heel steps and rapid turns; women's emphasize wrist and hand articulation with a near-motionless torso. Dramatic tradition is dominated by **ta'ziyeh**, the Shi'a passion play of Karbala performed in the month of Muharram in purpose-built *tekyeh* and in village squares, with roles sung in mugham modes — in Azeri districts it is performed in Azerbaijani. The comic strolling players *kosa-kosa*, a masked seasonal figure, appear at the pre-Nowruz festivities.

## Festivals & rituals

**Nowruz** (*Novruz*), the vernal equinox new year on 20–21 March, is the largest festival, preceded by four Tuesday evenings (*Charshanba*), each dedicated to one element — water, fire, earth, wind — culminating in *Chaharshanbe Suri*, the fire-leaping night before the last Wednesday. Households set the *haft-sin* table, sprout wheat or lentil *semeni* in dishes, dye eggs, and bake the festival pastries *shekerbura*, *pakhlava* and *shorgoghal*. The twelfth day, *Sizdah Bedar*, is spent outdoors. Seasonal markers include **Chilla** — the forty-day midwinter — with *Shab-e Chelleh* / *Chilla gecəsi* on the longest night, when families eat watermelon, pomegranate and nuts. The Shi'a calendar brings **Muharram**, with *ta'ziyeh*, nightly *rowzeh* recitation, chest-beating processions (*sinezani*) and the distribution of *nazri* votive food on Ashura; Ramadan ends in *Eid al-Fitr*. Life-cycle rites: the naming and first cradle-placing of an infant; the *hana yakhdi* henna night and *Vağzalı* procession at weddings, preceded by formal *elchilik* match-making visits and the *nishan* engagement; and funerary observances at the third, seventh and fortieth days after death, with communal *halva* and *khash* meals.

## Foodways

Wheat bread — *lavash*, thick *barbari* and *sangak* baked on hot pebbles — is the staple, with rice reserved for festive and urban tables. The signature Tabriz dish is *köfte Tabrizi*, a fist-sized or larger meatball of ground lamb, rice and split peas stuffed with a whole egg, walnuts, barberries and prunes, simmered in a saffron-tomato broth. **Dolma** appears in many forms — *yarpaq dolması* in grape leaves, *badımcan dolması* in aubergine, pepper and tomato — and *abgusht* / *dizi*, the mutton, chickpea and potato stew pounded at the table, is the everyday hot meal. *Ash-e doogh*, a herb-and-yoghurt soup, and *qormeh sabzi* cross over from the wider Iranian repertoire. Sour flavourings are pronounced: *qara alu* (dried plum), unripe grape juice (*abghureh*), pomegranate molasses and barberry. Fresh cheese, *doogh* (salted yoghurt drink) and clotted cream accompany breakfast. Tea (*chay*) is drunk black and strong from the samovar in tulip-shaped *estekan* glasses, taken *qand pahlu* — with a hard sugar lump held in the cheek — and Tabriz teahouses double as *ashiq* venues. Sweets include Tabriz *qurabiya* butter biscuits, *nougat* (*gaz*-style) and Ardabil honey confections. Diet is halal; alcohol is absent from public commensality.

## Oral tradition & literature

The bardic *dastan* repertoire is the backbone: **Koroghlu**, the epic of the bandit-hero and his horse Qırat, sung in cycles by *ashiq*s across Iranian Azerbaijan and the Caucasus, and the love-romances **Asli və Kərəm** and **Əsli–Kərəm**-type tales of a Muslim boy and Christian girl, and **Abbas və Gülgəz**. Short lyric forms include *bayatı*, the four-line quatrain used for lament, riddle and love song, and *qoshma* stanzas in *ashiq* performance. Prose folktales (*nağıl*) feature the trickster **Molla Nasreddin** — whose name Jalil Mammadguluzadeh borrowed for the satirical Tiflis journal that shaped Azeri modernism. The written tradition runs from the fourteenth-century Nasimi and the sixteenth-century Fuzuli, whose *Leyli və Məcnun* is still sung in mugham, to Mohammad-Hossein Shahriar of Tabriz, whose long Azerbaijani poem *Heydar Babaya Salam* (1954) is the most widely recited text in Iranian Azerbaijani and triggered a broad vernacular literary revival. Azerbaijani-language publishing, teahouse *ashiq* nights and recorded *dastan* collections continue that work.

## Language & religion

Azerbaijani belongs to the Oghuz branch of the Turkic family, close to Turkish and Turkmen but with heavy Persian and Arabic lexical layering and, in phonology and syntax, contact features from Iranian neighbours. Iranian dialects include Tabrizi, Ardabili, Urmiya and Qaradağ varieties; the language is written in Iran with the Perso-Arabic script (unlike the Latin orthography used in the Republic of Azerbaijan), and Old Azeri — an extinct Iranian language of the same territory — underlies much local toponymy. The overwhelming majority are Twelver Shi'a Muslims, a legacy of the Safavid dynasty that rose from Ardabil and imposed Shi'ism on Iran; the Safavid shrine complex of Sheikh Safi al-Din there remains a pilgrimage site. Folk religion includes visits to local *imamzadeh* tombs, vows and votive meals (*nazri*), evil-eye protection with rue smoke (*esfand*), and the veneration of *ashiq* lineages as carriers of Sufi-tinged verse.
