## Overview

The Turks (*Türkler*) are the largest Turkic-speaking people, some 60–65 million in Turkey itself, with long-settled communities in Northern Cyprus, Bulgarian Dobruja and Western Thrace, North Macedonia, Kosovo, Iraq, Syria and the Akkar region of Lebanon, and a diaspora that forms the largest ethnic minority in Germany, Austria, Denmark and the Netherlands. Their heartland is the Anatolian plateau — the Konya basin, the Kızılırmak and Sakarya valleys, the Taurus highlands, the Aegean river plains around Uşak and Milas, and the Black Sea littoral. Anatolian Turkish culture is layered by deliberate design: Central Asian pastoralism carried west by the Seljuks after Manzikert (1071), Turco-Persian court forms absorbed through Iran, Byzantine-Greek and Armenian craft substrates, and four centuries of Ottoman imperial patronage that standardized carpet, tile and silk production into named workshop traditions. Folk culture here is unusual in having both a courtly canon and a still-living nomadic (*Yörük*) tradition.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Kilim** — flat-woven slit-tapestry rugs in wool, made for floor, cradle, saddlebag and prayer use; Anatolian kilims are distinguished from Persian flatweaves by their bold reciprocal-field geometry, the *elibelinde* (hands-on-hips) goddess figure, and a preference for madder red against undyed camel and ivory grounds.

**Turkish carpet** — pile carpets knotted with the symmetrical *Gördes* (Turkish) knot, which gives a firmer, less fluid pile than the Persian asymmetrical knot. Regional schools are sharply distinct: **Uşak** (large medallion and star fields, loose weave, yellow-gold and soft red), **Ladik** (columnar prayer niches with tulip panels), **Ghiordes** (Gördes, prayer rugs with squared niches and stylized column borders), **Milas** (narrow-waisted *mihrab*, dry yellow-ochre grounds), **Konya** (early Seljuk fragments from Alâeddin Mosque, coarse long pile, minimal palette), and **Hereke**, the nineteenth-century court manufactory founded for the Ottoman palace weaving silk carpets at extreme knot densities.

**Çatma velvet** — Bursa-made silk pile velvet with brocaded metal-thread ground, used for cushion covers (*yastık*), throne covers and kaftan panels; the pile is cut in reserved areas so that gold wefts read as background.

**Bursa silk** and **Ottoman brocade** — *kemha* (silk-and-metal lampas), *seraser* (predominantly metal-thread cloth) and *serenk*, woven in Bursa and Istanbul under palace specification, with large-scale single motifs sized to be legible on a moving body.

**Yastık** — the small pile or flatwoven cushion face, roughly 60 × 90 cm, the standard domestic weaving of Anatolian households and the format in which regional motif dialects survive most clearly.

**Ottoman embroidery** — domestic *işleme* on linen and cotton towels (*havlu*), sashes, quilt covers and headscarves, worked in silk and metal thread with double-sided *Türk işi* darning stitch, gold *dival* couching over card, and Aegean *oya* needle-lace edgings whose flower forms carried coded messages.

**Suzani** — Central Asian silk-embroidered dowry hangings, indexed here for the Turkic continuum they belong to; they are Uzbek and Tajik in origin rather than Anatolian, and the Ottoman parallel is the embroidered *bohça* wrapping cloth.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Elibelinde* (hands on hips, fertility); *koçboynuzu* (ram's horn, male strength); *çintemani* (three pearls over paired wavy bands, an imperial motif of power drawn from Buddhist and Timurid sources); *saz* style (feathery serrated leaves with composite rosettes, devised by the painter Şahkulu); *rumi* (split-palmette arabesque); *hatayi* (idealized lotus-palmette "Cathay" scroll); *lale* (tulip, the signature Ottoman flower); *karanfil* (carnation); *çarkıfelek* (wheel of fortune); *bereket* (fertility pairing); *su yolu* (waterway border); *yıldız* (star, often eight-pointed).

### Clothing & dress

Ottoman-era men wore the *entari* (long open robe) or *şalvar* (baggy trousers gathered at the ankle) with a sleeveless *yelek* waistcoat, a wound sash (*kuşak*) doubling as purse and dagger-holder, and the *cepken*, a short embroidered jacket with slit hanging sleeves worn by Aegean *zeybek* men and irregular cavalry. Headgear was the marker of status and origin: turban (*sarık*) wound over a quilted cap, the conical felt *külah* of dervishes, the *kavuk*, then after 1829 the crimson *fes*, abolished by the Hat Law of 1925. Women's dress combined *şalvar*, a long *üç etek* ("three-skirt") coat slit into panels over the *içlik* shirt, an embroidered apron (*önlük*), and a silver or gilt belt clasp. Head covering layered a small cap with coin and *oya*-edged scarves; the outdoor *ferace* and *yaşmak* covered the urban Ottoman woman. Footwear ranged from soft *yemeni* slippers and *çarık* rawhide shoes of villagers to wooden bath clogs (*nalın*) inlaid with mother-of-pearl. Bridal dress added the red veil (*duvak*) and heavy dowry silver.

### Architecture

The classic *Türk evi* is a two-storey house with a masonry or rubble ground floor for animals and storage and a timber-frame upper floor with mud-brick or lath infill (*hımış*), jettied out over the street on brackets. Its organizing space is the *sofa* hall, from which square rooms open; each room is self-sufficient, with built-in cupboards (*yüklük*), a bathing niche (*gusülhane*), a carved plaster hood over the hearth (*ocak*), a low continuous divan (*sedir*) under the windows, and a *tavan göbeği* — a carved wooden rosette at the ceiling centre. Upper windows are screened with turned-wood *kafes* lattices; the projecting *cumba* bay is the house's public face. Safranbolu's hillside ensemble and the Ottoman mansions (*konak*) of Kula, Birgi and Amasya preserve whole quarters of it. Regional variants include Black Sea log-and-infill *göz dolma*, Cappadocian rock-cut dwellings and pigeon houses, and the Central Anatolian flat-roofed earth house rolled with a stone cylinder after rain. Yörük nomads still pitch the black goat-hair tent (*kara çadır*). Monumental idiom — mosque, *medrese*, *han*, *hamam*, Seljuk portal — supplies the tile and stone-carving vocabulary the domestic crafts echo.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**İznik ware** is the defining Ottoman ceramic: white slip-covered body under a clear alkaline glaze, painted in cobalt, turquoise, sage green and the raised sealing-wax red (*Armenian bole*) perfected around 1560, used for mosque tile revetments and for dishes, mosque lamps and tankards. **Kütahya** continued the industry into the eighteenth century in a smaller, brighter, more provincial register, including Armenian-commissioned pilgrim flasks and coffee cups. **Cuerda seca** — the "dry cord" technique of oil-and-manganese outlines separating coloured glazes — produced the early Bursa and Edirne tile panels before İznik underglaze displaced it. Çanakkale produced lead-glazed earthenware with applied animal spouts. In metal: tinned copper is the workhorse — *sahan* lidded pans, *güğüm* water jugs, *tepsi* trays, *cezve* coffee pots, and hammered *hamam tası* bath bowls with repoussé rosettes, still made in the Gaziantep and Safranbolu coppersmith bazaars. Woodwork means *kündekari* interlocking geometric joinery on doors and pulpits, mother-of-pearl inlay, and turned *nalın* clogs; *ebru* marbled paper and *tuğra* (the sultan's calligraphic cypher) belong to the same workshop world, as does **Ottoman calligraphy** in *sülüs*, *nesih* and *divanî* hands.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver dominates village adornment, gold marks urban and bridal wealth. The core forms are the coin ornament — strings of Ottoman *altın* and *çeyrek* sewn to caps, plaited into hair, or hung as the bridal *hasır* collar — and the belt clasp (*kemer tokası*) in filigree or niello, often the single most valuable object a woman owned. Headdresses carry *tepelik* crown discs and dangling *zülüflük* temple plates. Amulets against the eye are near-universal: the blue glass *nazar boncuğu* of Görece and İzmir glassmakers, the *maşallah* inscribed plaque pinned to infants, and small Qur'anic *muska* triangles in silver cases. Filigree (*telkâri*) from Midyat and Trabzon's silver wire-knit *hasır* bracelets are named regional schools. Henna (*kına*) is applied to the bride's palms — with a coin pressed into the paste — at the *kına gecesi*, and to sacrificial animals; brides' hair is braided and covered, and the tradition of the *saç bağı* hair ornament persists in Anatolian dowry sets.

## Music & performance

Anatolian folk music (*türkü*) is built around the *saz* or *bağlama*, a long-necked fretted lute played in *bağlama düzeni* and other scordatura tunings and often struck with the bare fingers (*şelpe*). Its relatives run from the small *cura* to the deep *divan sazı*. Bowed music uses the three-stringed *kemençe* of the Black Sea, played for *horon* dancing, and the fiddle-like *kabak kemane*. Wind instruments include the end-blown *kaval*, the double-reed *zurna* — inseparable from the *davul* double-headed drum in the *davul-zurna* pair that announces weddings, circumcisions and wrestling matches — the *mey*, and the bagpipe *tulum* of the eastern Black Sea. The frame drum *def* and clay *darbuka* carry urban rhythm. Genres are sharply distinguished: free-rhythm *uzun hava* laments; metered *kırık hava* dance songs; the *bozlak* of the Central Anatolian Abdal and Avşar communities; the *hoyrat* of the Iraqi Turkmen; and the *deyiş* and *nefes* hymns of Alevi–Bektaşi worship, sung by *aşık* bards and *zakir* liturgical singers in the *cem* ceremony. Parallel to this runs Ottoman classical music (*fasıl*) in the *makam* modal system with *usûl* rhythmic cycles, played on *ney*, *tanbur*, *kanun*, *ud* and *kemençe*; the Mevlevi *âyin*, a suite for *ney* and kudüm, is its sacred branch. *Mehter* military bands with *zurna*, *boru*, *nakkare*, *zil* and *kös* drums are the ancestor of European Janissary music.

## Dance & theatre

Village dance is regional and named. The *halay* is the line-and-circle dance of the southeast, led by a handkerchief-holding *halaybaşı* to *davul-zurna*; the *horon* of Trabzon and Rize is a tight shoulder-linked circle with rapid trembling steps to the *kemençe*; the *zeybek* of the Aegean is a slow, wide-armed solo in nine beats, danced by *efe* men as an assertion of bearing; the *bar* of Erzurum and the *karşılama* face-to-face couple dances of Thrace complete the main families. *Kaşık oyunu* spoon dances belong to the Konya–Silifke belt. Ritual movement includes the Mevlevi *semâ*, the turning ceremony of the Whirling Dervishes of Konya, and the *semah* of Alevi *cem* gatherings, danced by men and women together. Theatre is shadow and comic: *Karagöz ve Hacivat*, the leather shadow-puppet play performed against a backlit muslin screen (*ayna*) by a single *hayalî* voicing all characters, traditionally in Ramadan coffee-houses; *orta oyunu*, improvised arena comedy with fixed types; and *meddah*, the solo storyteller-mimic with staff and handkerchief.

## Festivals & rituals

The Islamic calendar frames the year: *Ramazan* with its pre-dawn *sahur* announced by drummers, and *Şeker Bayramı* (Eid al-Fitr), when children kiss elders' hands and receive sweets; *Kurban Bayramı* (Eid al-Adha) with the household sacrifice and distribution of thirds; and *Aşure* day in Muharrem, when a grain-and-fruit pudding is cooked in quantity and shared with neighbours — a rite of particular weight for Alevis, who observe twelve days of mourning for Kerbela. Overlaid on this is the older solar calendar: *Nevruz* on 21 March, marked with bonfire-jumping and sprouting wheat, especially in the east; *Hıdırellez* on 5–6 May, the meeting of Hızır and İlyas, when wishes are drawn or tied to a rose bush overnight; and *Mesir Macunu*, the Manisa paste-scattering festival at the Sultan Mosque each spring. Transhumant Yörük and highland villages hold *yayla* upland-pasture festivals at the summer move. Wrestling festivals — oiled *yağlı güreş* at Kırkpınar in Edirne since the fourteenth century, and camel wrestling (*deve güreşi*) in the Aegean in winter — are civic occasions with their own music. Life-cycle rites: the *mevlit* recitation and *kırk* fortieth-day bath after birth; *sünnet*, the circumcision feast with the boy in plumed cap and sequinned cape; the wedding sequence of *söz*, *nişan*, dowry display, *kına gecesi* henna night with its deliberate weeping songs, the *gelin alma* procession, and the bride's red sash tied by her brother; and funerals with *helva* cooked for the mourners and Qur'an readings on the seventh and fortieth days.

## Foodways

Wheat is the base — leavened *ekmek*, flatbread *pide*, tandoor-baked *tandır ekmeği*, and above all cracked and parboiled *bulgur*, used for *kısır* salad and for the raw-kneaded *çiğ köfte* of Şanlıurfa and Adıyaman. *Yufka*, the paper-thin sheet rolled on a low board with an *oklava* pin, yields *börek*, *baklava* and *mantı*, the small lamb dumplings of Kayseri served under garlic yoghurt and chilli butter. Lamb and mutton dominate: *kebap* in dozens of named forms (*şiş*, *Adana*, *cağ*, *İskender*), pit-cooked *tandır*, and *kuyu kebabı*. Vegetables cooked in olive oil and served cool (*zeytinyağlılar*) form a distinct Aegean category, as do *dolma* and *sarma* — stuffed peppers, quince, vine and cabbage leaves. Dairy is central: strained yoghurt (*süzme*), the drink *ayran*, dried-yoghurt *tarhana* soup, and sheep's-milk cheeses including brined *beyaz peynir*, *tulum* cured in a goatskin, and Erzurum's *civil*. Preserves and *pekmez* grape molasses, *sucuk* sausage and air-dried *pastırma* carry households through winter. Mezze and *rakı*, the aniseed spirit clouded with water, make the *rakı sofrası* table. Coffee is *Türk kahvesi*, boiled in a *cezve* with the grounds and read afterwards for fortunes; tea is *çay*, grown at Rize and served in tulip-shaped glasses from a stacked *çaydanlık*. Sweets: *baklava*, *lokum*, *helva*, *aşure*, *kadayıf*, *sütlaç*. Pork is excluded under *halal* rules; Alevi practice adds the twelve-day Muharrem abstention.

## Oral tradition & literature

The great Anatolian narrative is the **Book of Dede Korkut** (*Kitab-ı Dedem Korkut*), twelve Oghuz heroic tales in prose and verse centred on the bard-sage Korkut Ata; the **Köroğlu** cycle, about an outlaw bard avenging his blinded father, is its most widely sung successor. Romance epics — *Kerem ile Aslı*, *Ferhat ile Şirin*, *Arzu ile Kamber*, *Tahir ile Zühre* — were performed by *aşık* minstrels, who also compete in *atışma*, rhymed improvised duels judged on metre and wit, still held in Kars and Erzurum. Sung verse traditions include the *koşma*, *semai* and *destan* forms and the *mâni*, a four-line quatrain used for courtship, teasing and riddling. The comic anecdote tradition belongs to **Nasreddin Hoca** of Akşehir, whose thousands of paradox-tales function as proverb and argument; *Keloğlan*, the bald trickster boy, leads the wonder-tale (*masal*) repertoire, which opens with the untranslatable nonsense-prologue *tekerleme*. Alevi–Bektaşi devotional poetry — Yunus Emre, Pir Sultan Abdal, Kaygusuz Abdal — is simultaneously literature, liturgy and folk song. Collection began with Ziya Gökalp and Pertev Naili Boratav and continues through the Turkish Folklore Research Institute.

## Language & religion

Turkish (*Türkçe*) is Oghuz Turkic, agglutinative with vowel harmony, mutually intelligible with Azerbaijani, Gagauz and Turkmen. Its dialect geography splits Rumelian (Balkan), Anatolian (with Aegean, Mediterranean, Central, Eastern and a distinctive Black Sea group in which vowel harmony weakens), and Cypriot. Written in the Arabic-derived Ottoman script until the 1928 alphabet reform introduced a Latin orthography; earlier Turkic writing survives in the Orkhon runiform inscriptions of the Göktürks. The overwhelming majority are Sunni Muslims of the Hanafi school, with a substantial Alevi minority whose *cem* ritual, *dede* lineage guides, *semah* dance and veneration of Ali and the Twelve Imams stand outside mosque Islam; the Bektaşi order is its organized counterpart. Sufi orders — Mevlevi, Halveti, Nakşibendi — shaped music, poetry and calligraphy. Pre-Islamic Inner Asian layers persist in *türbe* saint-tomb visiting with wish-rags tied to trees, the evil eye, lead-pouring divination (*kurşun dökme*), and the sky-god vocabulary that survives in the word *Tanrı*.
