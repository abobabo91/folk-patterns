## Overview

The Bali, or Balī (Arabic: بلي), are an Arab tribe present in northwestern Saudi Arabia and Jordan, historically also found in Egypt and Sudan. They form a major component of the Quda'a tribal grouping. Their original territory included the northern Hejaz, north of Wadi Idam, extending toward Shaghab, Bada and the Tayma oasis. The tribe was divided between settled and nomadic communities and developed historical ties with Jewish communities in Arabian oases. Bali groups participated in the Muslim conquest of Egypt, later migrated into the region constituting modern Sudan, and contributed to the Arabization and Islamization of indigenous Beja and Baggara communities.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe religious and political events. In the pre-Islamic period, Bali groups maintained agreements with Jewish communities in Wadi al-Qura, whereby the Jews paid for protection from other nomadic tribes. A clan called the Banu Hishna fled to Jewish settlers in Tayma and converted to Judaism, while other Bali refugees later went to Medina and embraced Islam. Three Bali clans were counted among the Jewish clans of Medina at that time. Bali groups subsequently allied with the Ansar tribes of Medina, and several tribesmen fought on the side of Muhammad during the conflict with the Quraysh of Mecca, including the Battle of Badr in 624. In 630/31, a large delegation of southern Bali chieftains embraced Islam during a meeting with Muhammad. During World War I, in 1918, the Bali ultimately sided with the Arab forces of the Hashemite Sharif Hussein against the Ottomans.

## Foodways

 The sources mention that Wadi al-Qura was settled and cultivated by Jews.

## Oral tradition & literature

 The available historical account identifies Bali ibn Amr ibn al-Hafi ibn Quda'a as the tribe’s eponymous progenitor.

## Language & religion

The Bali are identified as an Arab tribe, but the sources do not classify their language within a language family or describe dialects or scripts. The sources record Jewish and Islamic affiliations in different historical contexts. One Bali clan, the Banu Hishna, converted to Judaism after seeking protection from Jewish settlers in Tayma. Bali townspeople in Medina embraced Islam with the advent of Islam, and a large delegation of southern Bali chieftains did so in 630/31. Following their migration into the region constituting modern Sudan, the Bali significantly contributed to the Islamization of indigenous Beja and Baggara communities. As late as the early 20th century, the Beja called the Arabic language “Balawiyyat” (“language of the Bali”).
