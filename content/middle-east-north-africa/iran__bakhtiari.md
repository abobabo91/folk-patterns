## Overview

The Bakhtiari are a Lur tribe from the western half of Iran. They primarily inhabit Chaharmahal and Bakhtiari and eastern Khuzestan, Lorestan, Bushehr, and Isfahan provinces, with especially large concentrations in Masjed Soleyman, Izeh, Shahr-e Kord, Andika, and surrounding villages. Their traditional territory extends from the Dez river, Shushtar, and Ramhormoz westward to Daran and the outskirts of Shahr-e Kord, across the central and highest part of the Zagros system. Population estimates vary: some sources give 3 to 6 million Bakhtiari Lurs, while another estimates 4 million. They speak Bakhtiari, a variety or dialect of Luri in the Southwestern Iranian branch, closely related to Persian. Their importance in folk culture is especially connected with pastoral migration, textiles, music, language, and the social organization of the Haft Lang and Chahar Lang.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Carpets* — Bakhtiari women are described as weavers, and colorful and stylish designs on carpets made them popular among buyers.

*Temporary dwellings* — Rectangular tents and brush or wood shelters are used by nomadic Bakhtiari when moving their herds. These are mobile forms of material culture associated with seasonal pastoralism rather than pattern-bearing textile traditions.

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that Bakhtiari women traditionally enjoyed a high degree of freedom partly because of their importance as weavers. They also state that Bakhtiaris were deeply attached to traditional Bakhtiari costume. During Reza Shah’s policy of deculturation in 1936, beards were prohibited and people were obliged to wear Western-style suits and the Pahlavi hat instead of traditional Bakhtiari dress.

### Architecture

Bakhtiari temporary dwellings include rectangular tents and brush or wood shelters, used while herds are being moved. Recently, some Bakhtiari have urbanized and settled in large villages and cities. The Jihad of Construction later built roads, bridges, silos, schools, and houses in Bakhtiari territory, while also bringing electricity and telephone lines to villages.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention only the practical material setting of pastoral migration, including tents, brush or wood shelters, livestock, agricultural products, and trade or barter with nearby villages.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention only beards in connection with Reza Shah’s prohibition of beards during the policy of deculturation in 1936.

## Music & performance

The Bakhtiari are noted in Iran for their music, which inspired the Russian composer Alexander Borodin.

Bakhtiari language-related activity includes recorded poetry and music. A collection of Bakhtiari folk poetry published by Fereydun Vahman and Garnik Asatrian includes love poems, wedding songs, lullabies, and laments. The sources therefore connect music and oral poetry with domestic and life-cycle contexts.

The sources also mention a Bakhtiari folk-song recording by Shusha Guppy in the 1970s, “The Lor Youth,” and a BBC documentary describing Bakhtiari nomadism as part of *The Ascent of Man* by Dr Jacob Bronowski. These references document outside recordings and representations.

## Dance & theatre

They mention only that the resemblance between Bakhtiyari and Greek dance has been used by some historians as an argument in theories about Bakhtiari origins.

## Festivals & rituals

Seasonal pastoral migration is documented in detail. Bakhtiari nomads migrate twice a year with their herds for pasture: in spring they move to the mountains in their summer quarters, *sardsīr* or *yaylāq*, and in autumn they move to valleys and plains in their winter quarters, *garmsīr* or *qishlāq*. In the Bakhtiari-speaking area, the boundary between summer pastures, *yeylāq*, and winter pastures, *garmsir*, roughly follows the Bazoft River. The summer quarters lie in Chaharmahal and the winter quarters in Khuzestan.

The sources also describe social and political ceremonies rather than religious festivals. These include the tribal conference of September 1948, the establishment of a civilian High Council of the Tribes in 1953, and the Shah’s marriage to Soraya Esfandiary-Bakhtiary in February, 1951, which was interpreted by Bakhtiari leaders as a sign of favor.

## Foodways

Bakhtiari nomadic pastoralists mainly raise goats, sheep, horses, and cattle. Some Bakhtiari cultivate wheat and other cereal grains. Nomadic Bakhtiari trade and barter with nearby villages and populations to obtain products they do not have or cannot produce themselves, including agricultural goods.

## Oral tradition & literature

Bakhtiari origin traditions include a story that the Lurs descended from youngsters who survived and fled from the demon Zahhak, a figure mentioned in Zoroastrian mythology and the Shahnameh. According to this account, they took shelter in Zardkuh and Kuhrang and named themselves Lur, while the name Bakhtiyar was associated with being “fortunate” after escaping danger. Other origin traditions connect the Bakhtiyaris with a legendary herdsman named Bakhtyar, with Bakhtiyarwand, or with historical groups and rulers.

The Bakhtiari writer Mohsen Farsani described the dialect as close to Pahlavi in an introduction to collected poems of “Molla Zolfali Bakhtiari.” A Bakhtiari adaptation of the Shahnameh was produced by Jamal Khosravinia in 2001. The sources also identify Darab Afsar Bakhtiari as a well-known Bakhtiari poet. Fereydun Vahman and Garnik Asatrian published a collection of Bakhtiari folk poetry containing love poems, wedding songs, lullabies, and laments.

## Language & religion

Bakhtiari is a variety or dialect of Luri spoken mainly in Khuzestan, Chaharmahal and Bakhtiari, and eastern Lorestan. It belongs to the Southwestern Iranian branch and is closely related to Persian. Luri has three main varieties: Northern Luri, Bakhtiari, and Southern Luri. Bakhtiari is linguistically intermediate between the other two, while the Bakhtiari tribe is divided into the Haft Lang and Chahar Lang, whose speech varieties also differ.

The sources describe Bakhtiari as spoken in a continuum with other Luri varieties and with Persian. Bakhtiari is generally vigorous in Luri-speaking areas, but Persian is the language of administration, media, and universal schooling, while emigration has also encouraged bilingualism.

Most Bakhtiaris are Twelver Shia Muslim. The sources also mention a story about conversion to Christianity during the time of Constantine the Great and a 2023 claim concerning a tendency among Bakhtiaris to convert to Zoroastrianism. These are the only religious developments documented in the supplied sources.
