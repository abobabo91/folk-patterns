## Overview

Palestinians (Arabic: الفلسطينيون, *al-Filasṭīniyyūn*) are an Arab national group native to the region of Palestine, sharing close linguistic and cultural ties with other Levantine Arabs. Roughly half of the world's Palestinian population lives in the territory of former Mandatory Palestine — the land between the Mediterranean Sea and the Jordan River, now encompassing Israel and the occupied Palestinian territories — including over 1.4 million refugees or internally displaced people in the Gaza Strip, over 870,000 in the West Bank and around 250,000 in Israel proper, where Palestinians make up almost 21 percent of the population. Of the diaspora, 2.3 million are registered refugees in Jordan, over a million live between Syria and Lebanon, about 750,000 in Saudi Arabia, and around half a million in Chile. In folk-culture terms the material documented here is overwhelmingly that of the villages — the *fellahin* — and above all their embroidered dress.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Thob** — The village woman's dress, cut either with long narrow sleeves and a square neck opening or with large pointed sleeves, in white or indigo-dyed cotton. Its surface is organised by named panels rather than by continuous field pattern: the chest panel (*qabbeh*), yoke, upper sleeves (*sawa'id*), cuffs, the front and back furrows (*mawaris*), the side panels (*banayeq*) and the lower back of the skirt, each worked in cross-stitch — deep magenta and pink on white grounds — with the densest and most vividly coloured cotton thread reserved for the chest panel.

**Machine-worked thob** — Later examples are both seamed and embroidered on a sewing machine, which carries a wave- or snail-like motif in red cotton thread alongside the *mawaris* and along the hem: the same panel scheme executed by a new tool.

**Jellaya** — The festive dress of Beit Dajan, made of indigo-blue or black linen embroidered over its entire surface, worn at weddings and other celebrations together with black linen trousers (*sirwal*) embroidered down the legs. It was the most costly of the many dresses making up a bride's trousseau in Beit Dajan and its surroundings, and was the dress a bride wore when she first appeared in public.

**Linen underdress** — Worn beneath the *thob*, of cream handwoven linen with red and blue selvedge stripes, its chest panel lightly worked in cross-stitch and satin stitch in burgundy, red and green floss silk. The collar is of red-and-white Syrian silk *ikat*, and the neck opening is trimmed with braided yellow silk cord looped to take braided green buttons — an assembly that puts imported Syrian silk against locally woven linen.

**Decorative seam and repair stitching** — Construction itself is ornamental: side and sleeve seams joined with knotted zigzag joining stitch in floss silk and then re-embroidered in feather stitch, chest panels bordered with zigzag appliqué, and patched neck openings re-worked in feather and buttonhole stitch in contrasting cotton, so that mending extends the pattern rather than interrupting it.

**Embroidered cushion covers** — Household furnishing in the same technique: cotton embroidered with silk thread, and finer examples in navy silk cross-stitched in yellow, brown, green, pink and white, with a central panel of ordered motifs.

**Motif vocabulary.** *mafatih nujum* (stars-and-keys), appearing four to a central panel; flowers with stems; a wave or snail-like running motif worked by machine along hems and furrows.

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress is layered: the handwoven linen underdress with its *ikat* collar beneath the embroidered *thob*, and for weddings the fully embroidered *jellaya* with embroidered *sirwal* trousers. Colour and ground fabric vary by garment — white cotton, indigo-dyed cotton, black or indigo linen — while the embroidered zones stay in the same named positions (*qabbeh*, *sawa'id*, *mawaris*, *banayeq*). Men's documented outerwear is the square cape *abayeh* (also *abay*), here in green-and-white striped silk twill with the seams trimmed in braided cotton wrapped in metal foil. The sources used do not describe Palestinian head coverings, belts or footwear, nor regional dress styles beyond the village examples above and the Beit Dajan bridal trousseau.

### Architecture

The sources used do not describe Palestinian vernacular house forms, materials or ornament. The single architectural subject documented is monumental and religious: Al-Aqsa Mosque (*al-Masjid al-ʾAqṣā*, "the Farthest Mosque") in the Old City of Jerusalem, built on the Temple Mount, known in Islam as the al-Aqsa Compound or *Haram esh-Sharif*. Muslims hold that Muhammad was transported there from the Great Mosque of Mecca during the Night Journey, and Islamic tradition holds that he led prayers towards the site until the sixteenth or seventeenth month after his migration from Mecca to Medina, when he was directed to turn towards the Kaaba. The wider built record of Palestine named in the sources is a sequence of periods rather than described forms — Biblical, Roman, Byzantine, Umayyad, Abbasid, Fatimid, Crusader, Ayyubid, Mamluk and Ottoman — strata that Palestinians have come to understand as part of their own identity over the last century.

## Festivals & rituals

The annual festival calendar is not covered by the sources used. Of life-cycle rites, only marriage is documented, and only through dress. In Beit Dajan and its surrounding villages a bride's trousseau comprised many dresses, of which the embroidered *jellaya* was the most costly; it was worn with embroidered *sirwal* trousers at weddings and other celebrations, and specifically at the moment the bride first appeared in public. The sources say nothing of birth, coming-of-age or funerary observance, and nothing of the ritual calendars of the religious communities they name. Two secular commemorations are recorded: the United Nations has observed an annual International Day of Solidarity with the Palestinian People since 1978, and the sources note the 1948 establishment of Israel, the 1948 Palestinian expulsion and the 1967 Palestinian exodus as the events around which a shared Palestinian future came to be articulated.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used document print rather than oral literature. Palestinian self-description in writing begins with Khalil Beidas, who introduced the term *Filasṭīnī* in a 1898 Arabic translation of a Russian work on the Holy Land, followed by Salim Quba'in and Najib Nassar in 1902. After the 1908 Young Turk Revolution eased Ottoman press censorship, dozens of newspapers and periodicals were founded in Palestine — among them *Al-Quds*, *Al-Munadi*, *Falastin*, *Al-Karmil* and *Al-Nafir* — which used the word "Filastini" more than 170 times across 110 articles between 1908 and 1914, and wrote of a "Palestinian society" (*al-mujtama' al-filasṭīnī*), a "Palestinian nation", the "people of Palestine" (*ahl*/*ahālī Filasṭīn*) and the "sons of Palestine" (*abnā' Filasṭīn*). Contributors included Christian and Muslim Palestinians, emigrants and non-Palestinian Arabs; the Christian-owned *Falastin*, founded in 1911, addressed its readers as Palestinians from the outset. The sources name no epic, folktale cycle or proverb collection.

## Language & religion

Palestinians are Arabic-speaking, writing in the Arabic script, and share close linguistic ties with other Levantine Arabs; the sources used do not describe Palestinian dialects. Religiously the population is mixed: in 1919 Palestinian Muslims and Christians together constituted 90 percent of the population of Palestine, and the Palestinian National Charter's definition of "Palestinians" as Arab nationals resident until 1947 takes in Arabic-speaking Muslims, Arab Christians, and the Samaritan and Druze communities. Some Palestinian families, notably in the Hebron and Nablus regions, claim Jewish and Samaritan ancestry respectively and preserve customs and traditions associated with it. Islam's place in local devotion is documented through Jerusalem: Al-Aqsa Mosque and the *Haram esh-Sharif* compound, tied to the Night Journey. Folk religious practice beyond this is not covered.
