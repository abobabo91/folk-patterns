## Overview

The Baluchi are a distinct Sunni tribal people in Iran's southeast, historically pastoral-nomadic and concentrated in the Sarhad highlands and Makran coast. They maintain a separate weaving tradition, hand-built pottery, and an elaborate embroidery system that mark them apart from surrounding Persian and Shi'a populations. Their material culture and sung epic traditions reflect both local desert life and broader Indian Ocean connections.

## Material culture

Baluchi material culture centres on wool pile-weaving, dense silk embroidery, and hand-built pottery, with regional variations from the mountain interior to the coastal palm groves.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Baluch weavers produce small-format wool rugs and flatweaves in a signature dark palette of aubergine, oxblood red, deep indigo and camel hair.

- **Baluch pile rug** (*qali-ye Baluch*) — Small-format wool-on-wool carpets in aubergine, oxblood red, deep indigo and camel hair that read almost monochrome until seen in raking light.
- **Baluch prayer rug** (*jonamaz*) — A stepped or turreted mihrab over a camel-ground field with flanking panjeh hand shapes and a tree-of-life filling the niche.
- **Saddlebags and cushion covers** (*khorjin and balisht*) — Khorjin are double saddlebags joined by a flatwoven bridge; balisht are single pile-faced cushion covers that serve as the tribal weaver's showpieces.
- **Eating cloth** (*sofreh*) — An ivory or undyed flatwoven cloth with narrow weft-substitution bands used for laying out meals and kneading dough in the tent.
- **Baluchi embroidery** (*doch*) — Counted-thread silk-floss embroidery so dense the ground disappears, applied in fixed panels to women's dresses and enhanced with small circular mirrors in mirror work.

### Clothing & dress

Women wear an embroidered long shift over wide trousers with a headscarf, while men wear a long shirt, baggy trousers and a high-wound turban with a hanging tail.

- **Woman's dress** (*pashk*) — A long loose shift reaching below the knee, worn over wide trousers, with embroidered chest panel, deep front pocket and sleeve bands that can be transferred to new garments.
- **Trousers** (*shalwar*) — Wide trousers gathered at the ankle, worn by both men and women.
- **Man's turban** (*pag*) — A high-wound turban left with a hanging tail, tied differently in the Sarhad and coast to mark a man's district.
- **Sandal** (*chappal*) — A flat leather sandal with a wide strap, or palm-fibre sandals in Makran.
- **Embroidered chest panel** (*zeeh*) — The main decorative plastron on a woman's dress, worked in dense counted-thread embroidery and often transferred between garments.

### Architecture

Nomadic Baloch live in black goat-hair tents, while settled villages build in mud brick with thick walls and flat roofs, and coastal communities use palm-frame structures.

- **Black goat-hair tent** (*gedan*) — A tent woven from goat hair in narrow strips and stretched over poles, with walls raised or dropped with the season, serving as the basic social unit.
- **Mud-brick house** (*khesht*) — Settled village houses with thick mud-brick walls, small high openings and flat roofs on palm-trunk beams, oriented in Sistan with blank backs to the north wind.
- **Palm-frame coastal house** (*kapar*) — A coastal structure built entirely of date-palm material: a frame of palm trunks and ribs clad in plaited fronds, raised above the ground for air and replaceable.
- **Wind-catcher** (*badgir*) — A cooling device used in coastal towns such as Chabahar, adopted from the Persian Gulf littoral.
- **The 120-day wind** (*bad-e sad-o-bist ruz*) — The summer wind of Sistan for which vaulted and domed mud roofs are the norm.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Hand-built pottery without the wheel, date-palm and reed basketry, and silver metalwork with copper and iron fittings dominate domestic equipment.

- **Kalporegan pottery** — Hand-built jars, bowls and containers coiled and pinched by women, burnished and painted freehand with geometric bands in dark reddish-brown slip, then open-fired.
- **Clay oven** (*tanur*) — A clay oven on whose wall bread is baked, featuring iron and brass fittings.
- **Preserved meat** (*tabahag*) — Salted, sun-dried strips of mutton or goat that provide preserved meat for the desert economy.
- **Wooden mortar** (*hawan*) — A carved wooden mortar used in domestic food preparation.
- **Palm-leaf storage baskets** (*coiled palm-leaf storage baskets*) — Coiled baskets of Makran used for storing food and goods on the coast.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Baluchi silver jewelry is heavy and chased, featuring large earrings, nose ornaments, chest chains and amulet cases holding Qur'anic texts.

- **Large hoop earrings** (*dorr*) — Heavy hoop or pendant earrings so large they are supported by a chain over the head.
- **Nose ornament** (*pullu*) — A silver ornament worn in the left nostril.
- **Chest chain** (*hamail*) — A broad silver chain or pectoral worn on the chest.
- **Amulet case** (*ta'wiz or tumar*) — A tubular or rectangular silver box holding a Qur'anic text, hung on the chest or tied to an infant's arm against the evil eye.
- **Bangles** (*kangan*) — Silver bracelets worn stacked on the wrists.

## Music & performance

The central instrument is the bowed soroz lute with sympathetic strings, accompanied by spike fiddles, long-necked lutes, flutes and drums in a repertoire organized by named genres rather than maqam.

- **Bowed lute** (*soroz or sorud*) — A bowed short-necked lute with sympathetic strings, the central instrument of Baluchi music.
- **Spike fiddle** (*qeychak or ghaychak*) — A spike fiddle that joins the soroz in the Baluchi ensemble.
- **Professional bard** (*pahlawan*) — A professional bard who accompanies himself and performs the long sheyr epic cycles at gatherings.
- **Spirit-healing ceremony** (*guati or gwati*) — A Makran ceremony in which a sufferer possessed by a gwat spirit is played into and out of trance over successive nights by a specialist musician-healer.
- **Keyed box zither** (*benju*) — A keyed box zither adopted in the twentieth century that has become emblematic of Makran.

## Dance & theatre

The main collective dance is the men's circle chap built on step-and-clap patterns, while dammal is ecstatic swaying performed at shrines and healing sessions.

- **Men's circle dance** (*chap*) — A men's circle dance built on a step-and-clap pattern that accelerates, performed at weddings and circumcision feasts with the line turning around drummers.
- **Ecstatic shrine dance** (*dammal or damali*) — Ecstatic swaying and head-circling dance performed at Sufi shrines and in guati healing sessions as a therapeutic act.
- **Afro-Baluchi drum dance** (*lewa*) — A drum-driven dance of African descent performed on the Makran coast, distinguished by polyrhythmic drumming and call-and-response singing.

## Festivals & rituals

Islamic holidays and shrine pilgrimage punctuate the year, while life-cycle rites centre on birth amulets, circumcision feasts, multi-day marriages and women's funeral laments.

- **End of Ramadan festival** (*Eid-e Fetr*) — One of the year's two poles, marking the end of Ramadan.
- **Sacrifice festival** (*Eid-e Qorban*) — The year's second pole, marked by ritual sacrifice and distribution of meat.
- **Date harvest festival** (*hamin*) — The hot season of the Makran date harvest when families move to palm groves and the harvest becomes a working festival of shared labour and evening music.
- **Shrine pilgrimage** (*ziyarat*) — Pilgrimage to local saints' shrines throughout the year, often combined with a fair.
- **Bride's henna night** (*henna night*) — A women's event where henna is applied to the bride's hands and feet before marriage.

## Foodways

The staples are wheat flatbread, rice and dates, with signature slow-roasted and pit-cooked lamb dishes, preserved meats, and coastal fish curried with Indian Ocean spices.

- **Whole roasted lamb or chicken** (*sajji*) — A whole lamb or chicken salted, skewered on a green stake and roasted slowly beside an open fire.
- **Steamed rice** (*dampokhtak*) — Rice cooked with meat and fat in a sealed pot so that it steams in its own moisture.
- **Travel bread** (*kaak*) — A hard, long-keeping bread baked among hot stones, made for travel and herding camps.
- **Plain rice** (*bat*) — Rice eaten plain as a staple.
- **Date paste** (*pressed into paste*) — Dates pressed into paste for storage and served with bread and butter at breakfast.

## Oral tradition & literature

The historical core is the Rind–Lashari wars cycle of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, performed by professional bards in long sheyr epic recitations.

- **Epic cycle** (*sheyr*) — Long epic cycles performed by professional bards, the central form of Baluchi oral literature.
- **Rind–Lashari wars** — A cycle of fifteenth and sixteenth century feuds centred on Mir Chakar Rind and Gwaharam Lashari that functions as the Baloch national epic and genealogical charter.
- **Romance cycles** (*romances*) — Sung stories including Hani and Sheh Mureed, Dosten and Shiren, and Shahdad and Mahnaz.
- **Improvised couplet** (*liko*) — Short couplets improvised by camel drivers and travellers and answered by others on the road.
- **Modal melodies** (*zahirok*) — Modal melodies of longing that underpin most solo singing and are kept alive through cassette and digital circulation.

## Language & religion

Balochi is a Northwestern Iranian language closer to Kurdish than Persian, written in Perso-Arabic script, and almost all Iranian Baluchis are Sunni Muslims of the Hanafi school.

- **Northern dialect** (*Rakhshani or Sarhaddi*) — A Balochi dialect spoken in the north around Khash and Zahedan.
- **Central dialect** (*Sarawani*) — A Balochi dialect spoken in the centre.
- **Southern dialect** (*Kechi, Makrani and Coastal*) — Balochi dialects spoken in the south and along the coast.
- **Islamic school** (*Hanafi*) — The school of Sunni Islam followed by almost all Iranian Baluchis, maintained through madrasa networks.
- **Sufi orders** (*Naqshbandi and Qadiri*) — Sufi affiliations that underpin shrine pilgrimage, zikr gatherings and dammal dance among Baluchi communities.
