## Overview

Lebanese people are native to or originate from Lebanon, including communities historically associated with Mount Lebanon and the Anti-Lebanon Mountains. The total Lebanese population is estimated at 8 to 18 million, including a large diaspora; approximately 4.7 million citizens reside in Lebanon. Lebanese communities are also found throughout the Americas, Europe, Australia and Africa. Lebanese Arabic is a variety of Levantine Arabic, while Modern Standard Arabic is the official language. French is widely used, and English is also spoken. Lebanese folk culture is expressed through regional foodways, music, poetry, festivals, architecture, clothing and religious traditions shaped by the Eastern Mediterranean and Levant.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

In cities, many Christians and most Muslims wear modern clothing. In the countryside, women sometimes wear traditional colorful skirts, while men wear the traditional *sherwal*, described as baggy trousers. Dress was historically Ottoman and remains part of folk culture, although almost all Lebanese people today wear modern clothing.

### Architecture

Architecture in Lebanon includes legacies associated with the Romans, Phoenicians, Ottomans and French, as well as post-independence development. Italianate and specifically Tuscan influence became prominent after Fakhreddine returned from Tuscany, Italy, in 1618. Italian engineers helped construct mansions and civil buildings, especially in Beirut and Sidon. Deir el Qamar contains buildings reflecting this influence, while Rue Gouraud and Rue Sursock retain historic houses with Italianate features. The Nicolas Sursock mansion, now a major museum, is identified as evidence of this continuing architectural influence.

## Music & performance

Music is prominent in Lebanese society. Traditional folk music remains popular alongside classical Arabic, Armenian, pop, fusion, rock, house and electronic music. Lebanese music is characterized by string and percussion instruments. Named instruments include the *oud*, *mijwiz*, *tablah*, *daf*, *buzuq* and *qanun*.

Traditional Lebanese music shares influences with Syria and Egypt. Beirut music also reflects Bedouin traditions and folk music from rural regions. Lebanese folk music includes Aramaean, Byzantine and later Islamic styles found across the Levant. Its characteristic features include poetic and rhythmic tunes, usually performed with a unison melody sung solo or by a group.

*Zajal* poetry is described as one of the best-known forms of traditional music in Lebanon. Other named forms include *ma'anna*, associated with melismatic and syllabic music, and *qarrâdi*, known for a village character. These styles are often performed with *dabke*.

The sources also describe efforts to preserve older music. Nahda Music, dated 1885–1940 and translated as “Arab Renaissance,” is being revived through Tajdīd min al-Dakhil, or “Renewal from Within.” The Music Institute at Antonine University and the Arabic Music Archiving and Research Foundation supported the recovery of performances, styles and instruments, including records, reels and wax cylinders from across the Middle East.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name *dabke* as a dance associated with *zajal*, *ma'anna* and *qarrâdi*. Lebanese theatre originated in passion plays, and the musical plays of Maroun Naccache from the mid-1800s are described as the birth of modern Arab theatre. Some scholars divide theatre in Lebanon into translations of European plays, Arab nationalism and realism.

## Festivals & rituals

Lebanon celebrates national holidays and both Christian and Muslim holidays. Christian observances follow the Gregorian and Julian calendars. Greek Orthodox, Maronite and Melkite Catholics, and Protestant Christians follow the Gregorian Calendar and celebrate Christmas on 25 December. Armenian Orthodox Christians celebrate Christmas on 6 January, following the Julian Calendar.

Muslim holidays follow the Islamic lunar calendar. Named observances include Eid al-Fitr, described as the three-day feast at the end of Ramadan; Eid al-Adha, the Feast of the Sacrifice, associated with the annual pilgrimage to Mecca and Abraham’s willingness to sacrifice his son; Mawlid, the Birthday of Muhammad; and Ashura. National holidays include Workers Day, Independence day and Martyrs Day.

Music festivals are commonly held at historical sites. Named festivals include the Baalbeck International Festival, Byblos International Festival, Beiteddine International Festival, Broumana Festival, Batroun Festival, Dhour Chwer Festival and Tyr Festival.

## Foodways

Lebanese cuisine belongs to the culinary tradition of the Eastern Mediterranean and uses grains, fruits, vegetables, fresh fish, seafood, poultry, lamb, beef, garlic, olive oil, salt, lemon juice, chickpeas and parsley. Foods are commonly grilled, baked or lightly cooked in olive oil. Vegetables may be eaten raw, pickled or cooked, and seasonal availability strongly influences meals.

The *mezze* is an arrangement of small dishes served before guests. It may include raw or pickled vegetables, hummus, *baba ghanouj*, bread, grilled seafood, skewered meats, salads and desserts. Named dishes include *tabbouleh*, *fattoush*, *kibbeh*, *falafel*, *sfeeha*, *shawarma*, *wara' enab*, *kousa mahshi*, *mujaddara*, *mouneh*, *labneh*, *makdous*, *sambousek*, *sayadieh*, *shish barak*, *baklawa*, *kanafeh*, *ma'amoul*, *sfouf* and *booza*.

The national beverage is *arak*, an anise-flavored liquor made from fermented grape juice and usually served with water and ice. Other named drinks include Arabic coffee, Turkish coffee, *ayran*, *jallab*, Lebanese wine, Lebanese beer and *maté*. Lebanese Christians practice abstention from meat during the Lenten period in traditions described as *akl aateh*.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources identify oral storytelling as part of Lebanese cultural documentation. The cited further reading includes Jihad Darwiche’s *Le conte oriental: La tradition orale au Liban* and Nathalie Zoghaib’s studies of Lebanese tales and their transmission.

Khalil Gibran is particularly known for *The Prophet* (1923), which has been translated into more than twenty languages. The sources also name Ameen Rihani, associated with the *mahjar* literary movement; Mikhail Naimy; Elias Khoury; Amin Maalouf; Hanan al-Shaykh; and Georges Schéhadé or Georges Schehadé. Gibran Khalil Gibran is also credited with poems titled “Love” in 1920 and “On Friendship” in 1923.

## Language & religion

Lebanese Arabic is a variety of Levantine Arabic, an Arabic language variety with a significant Aramaic substratum and later influences from Ottoman Turkish, French and English. Lebanese Arabic is primarily spoken in Lebanon and is also used among the Lebanese diaspora. Regional varieties include Beiruti, northern, southern, Beqaa and Mount Lebanon varieties. Modern Standard Arabic is the official language, while French is recognized and used alongside it. Syriac Aramaic remains a first language in some Syriac Catholic, Syriac Orthodox and Assyrian Lebanese communities and is used in Maronite liturgy.

Lebanon has 17 recognized religious sects. The principal religious communities are Maronite, Greek Orthodox, Melkite, Protestant, Sunni Muslim, Shia Muslim and Druze. Other communities named in the sources include Armenians, Assyrians, Copts and Jews. No official census has been taken since 1932 because of the political sensitivity of confessional balance.
