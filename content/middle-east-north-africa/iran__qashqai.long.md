## Overview

The Qashqai (*Qašqāʾi*, also Kashkai, Ghashghai) are a Turkic-speaking tribal confederacy of southern Iran, concentrated in Fars province and spilling into Khuzestan, Kohgiluyeh and Boyer-Ahmad, Chaharmahal and Bakhtiari, Bushehr and southern Isfahan. Their historic territory is the migration corridor that runs some 480 km between summer pastures (*yaylaq*) on the flanks of Kuh-e Dinar in the Zagros north of Shiraz and winter quarters (*qishlaq*) in the warm lowlands around Firuzabad, Kazerun and Farashband toward the Persian Gulf — the largest seasonal migration of any Iranian tribe. Their language, which they call *Turki*, belongs to the Oghuz branch of Turkic; nearly all speakers are also fluent in Persian. Numbers are contested — a mid-century tribal estimate put the confederacy at 400,000, and present-day speaker counts are commonly given near a million. In folk-culture terms they matter above all as weavers: Qashqai pile rugs, *gabbeh* and saddlebags are among the most collected tribal textiles in the world.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Qashqai carpet** — Wool-on-wool pile rugs, hand-knotted on horizontal ground looms that can be struck and reloaded at each camp. Fars wool, praised since Hawley's 1913 survey for taking madder red and indigo blue with unusual depth, gives the palette its saturation. Compositions favour stacked or triple diamond medallions on a field crowded with small animals and rosettes, unlike the empty-ground formality of city workshop rugs.

**Qashqai kilim / *gelim*** — Slit-tapestry flatweaves in banded or lattice layouts. The **Qashqai Gelime** received a UNESCO seal of authenticity in 2013.

***Gabbeh*** — A coarse, long-pile sleeping and sitting rug of very low knot density, often in undyed cream, brown and black fleece or a single madder or indigo ground, with a handful of oversized figures. The Fars *gabbeh* is the opposite pole of Persian weaving from the fine urban carpet, and the 1996 Makhmalbaf film *Gabbeh* made it internationally legible.

***Jajim*** — Narrow warp-faced striped bands woven separately and stitched edge to edge into blankets and covers. The Kashkuli Bozorg tribe is specifically noted for its *jajim*, described by Oliver Garrod as "tartan woolen blankets."

***Khorjin*** — Double saddlebags with pile or *sumak*-brocaded faces and plainweave backs, joined by a woven bridge; Qashqai examples have been called superior to any others made. Related bag forms include the small personal *chanteh*.

***Namakdan*** — The keyhole-shaped salt bag, with a narrow woven neck that closes against grazing animals, usually worked in *sumak* on a plainweave ground.

**Horse trappings** — The Darreshuri, the confederacy's great horse-breeders, weave decorated *jol* horse covers, breast bands and tassels; Reza Shah's forced sedentarisation destroyed 80–90 percent of their herds before the tribe rebuilt after 1945.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Boteh* (almond/paisley, often in diagonal rows), *toranj* (central medallion), *mihrab* (prayer niche), *gol-e farang* ("foreign flower," the European rose adopted through Shiraz), *sarv* (cypress, emblem of life), *morgh* (bird, frequently peacock or cockerel), *shir* (lion, the Fars talismanic beast also carved on graves), *mahi* / *herati* (fish-in-pond lattice), *setareh* (eight-pointed star), and stacked latch-hook diamonds filled with miniature dogs, deer and camels.

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress is the most conspicuous in southern Iran: several superimposed gathered skirts (*tuman*) of printed or shot cloth in clashing saturated colours, worn over loose wide-legged trousers, with a short waist-length tunic (*arkhaloq*) with open front and a long sleeveless coat. The head is dressed with a stiff headband (*lachak*) over which a large gauzy square scarf (*charqad*) is draped and pinned so that it frames the face and falls to the waist; coins, bangles and sewn braid trim the edges. Qashqai women do not veil the face, and their dress survived the Pahlavi unveiling decrees better than urban costume.

Men wear a tunic and wide trousers under a long coat with a wool sash (*shal*) wound at the waist, and the confederacy's identity badge: the *kolah-e do-gushi*, a short brimless felt cap with two upward flaps over the ears, standardised as tribal uniform under Sowlat od-Dowleh in the early twentieth century and still worn at weddings and political gatherings. Footwear is the *giveh*, a cotton-uppered shoe with a hard-pressed sole, replaced on migration by rawhide.

### Architecture

The canonical Qashqai dwelling is the black tent (*siah chador*), woven by women from hand-spun goat hair in long panels (*lat*) that are sewn together and stretched over wooden uprights and ridge poles, guyed to iron pins. Goat hair swells when wet and seals the roof; in heat the side walls are rolled up entirely. Reed and brushwood screens stacked along the interior walls divide the sleeping and cooking halves and carry the household's stored bags and rolled bedding, the weavings themselves functioning as the furniture and the insulation. In the winter lowlands near Bushehr and Kazerun, families build *kapar* — light huts of palm rib, reed and brush on a stone footing. Since the state settlement programmes begun under Reza Shah and intensified from the 1960s, most Qashqai live in mud-brick or cement village houses with flat roofs and walled yards; many keep a tent pitched beside the house for summer use and for weaving, and government-maintained migration corridors still carry those who move.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Nomadic households buy rather than make their hard goods, and Qashqai material culture is accordingly a culture of skin, wood and tinned copper. Milk is churned to butter and *dugh* in a whole goatskin bag (*mashk*) swung from a wooden tripod; water is carried in smaller skins. Bread is baked on the *saj*, a convex sheet-iron plate set over a brush fire. Tinned copper trays, bowls and the ubiquitous brass or nickel samovar come from the Shiraz and Kazerun bazaars, as do the tribesmen's knives and the silver-mounted bridles, bells and studded saddlery that Darreshuri horsemen commission for their animals. The distinctively Qashqai objects are loom tools: the ground-loom stakes, the heavy iron weft-beating comb, the spindle (*duk*) carried and worked while walking with the flocks.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver dominates: coin-strung necklaces and headbands assembled from Qajar and Pahlavi *rial* pieces, hollow bangles, hoop and pendant earrings worn from childhood, and the amulet forms shared with the wider Iranian world — the tubular *bazuband* bound to the upper arm and the flat *ta'viz* case holding a folded Quranic text against illness and the eye. Turquoise, carnelian, coral and blue glass beads are set or strung, blue explicitly as a defence against *cheshm-e nazar*, the evil eye; beads and bells are hung on children and on valuable animals alike. Women wear the hair in long plaits emerging from under the *lachak*; henna is applied to hands and hair at the bride's *hana-bandan* night and before major festivals. Jewelry doubles as portable capital, the only form of wealth a migrating household can carry through a bad year.

## Music & performance

Qashqai celebration music is outdoor, loud and processional: the double-reed shawm (*sorna*, in its longer form *karna*) paired with the cylindrical *dohol* or the paired kettledrums *naqareh*, played for weddings, circumcisions, stick dances and horse displays by professional musician families who stand slightly outside tribal society. Indoor and pastoral music is the province of the end-blown shepherd's flute (*ney*), of the bowed spike fiddle (*kamancheh*), and of women's unaccompanied song — lullabies, work songs sung at the loom and over the churn, wedding verses exchanged between the bride's and groom's parties, and laments raised at deaths. The prestige form is the *ashiq*, the Oghuz Turkic bard who sings narrative verse to a long-necked plucked lute; the Qashqai share the Oghuz heroic repertoire, above all the **Köroğlu** cycle, with Azerbaijan and Anatolia, and sing the quatrain genre *bayati*. Since the twentieth century, Qashqai Turkic song has become a deliberate marker of ethnic identity against Persian-language broadcasting, and cassette and now digital circulation of Qashqai singers is one of the confederacy's strongest living institutions.

## Dance & theatre

Two dances define Qashqai public celebration. ***Chub-bazi*** ("stick play") is a men's mock combat performed to *sorna* and *dohol*: one man swings a long staff at the ankles of an opponent who parries with a short stick, the pair circling inside a ring of spectators until another challenger steps in — a display of nerve shared with the neighbouring Lur and Bakhtiari. The women's dance is the handkerchief dance (*dastmal-bazi*), an open circle or facing lines in which dancers turn with a coloured kerchief in each hand, the multiple skirts flaring with the step; it is the visual centrepiece of a Qashqai wedding and the context in which the full costume is meant to be seen. Horsemanship substitutes for theatre among the Darreshuri, whose weddings and festivals feature mounted display and racing. There is no indigenous Qashqai masked or puppet theatre.

## Festivals & rituals

The tribal year turns on the two migrations (*kuch*) rather than on a harvest. The spring departure for the highlands begins in April, immediately after **Nowruz** (21 March), so that the new year and the reopening of the road to summer pasture are a single celebration; the autumn descent to the *qishlaq* is the other pivot. The pre-Nowruz fire-leaping of *Chaharshanbe Suri* and the thirteenth-day picnic *Sizdah Bedar* are kept as elsewhere in Iran. As Twelver Shi'a, the Qashqai observe Ramadan and the feasts of *Eid-e Fetr* and *Eid-e Qorban*, and mark Muharram with mourning gatherings, though tribal observance is markedly lighter than urban.

Life-cycle rites concentrate on marriage. Betrothal (*nishan*) and bride-price negotiations precede a wedding of up to three days, with a henna night for the bride, the transfer of her dowry — which is, in a weaving family, her own accumulated bags, *jajim* and rugs — and days of *chub-bazi*, *dastmal-bazi* and feasting on slaughtered sheep. Boys' circumcision (*khatneh-suran*) is celebrated with the same shawm-and-drum music. At death the body is buried quickly and mourned for three, seven and forty days with *halva* and recitations; across Fars, carved stone lions (*shir-e sangi*) were set over the graves of men who died with a reputation for courage.

## Foodways

Bread and dairy are the base. Thin unleavened wheat bread is baked on the *saj* over brushwood and eaten with the products of the flock: yogurt (*mast*), buttermilk (*dugh*) churned in the skin bag, clarified butter (*roghan*), and above all *kashk*, whey dried into hard pellets that keep through a migration and are reconstituted into sauces and soups. Wild greens, mountain herbs and gathered bulbs collected along the migration route go into thick *ash* pottages. Rice (*polow*) is festival food rather than daily fare, as is meat: a sheep is killed for guests, weddings and funerals and grilled as *kabab* or stewed long in the sealed pot as *abgusht*. Surplus meat is preserved fried in its own fat. Tea rules hospitality — brewed dark from the samovar, poured into small waisted glasses and drunk through a held lump of rock sugar (*nabat*), offered immediately and repeatedly to any visitor to the tent. Neighbouring Shiraz supplies dates, citrus and the sour-fruit flavourings of Fars cooking, and the food is halal.

## Oral tradition & literature

Qashqai transmission is explicitly oral and explicitly nocturnal: as the Ilbeg Malek Mansur put it in 1953, nomads "do not accumulate documents and archives. But in the evenings, around fires that are burning low, the elders will relate striking events, deeds of valour in which the tribes pride themselves." That repertoire is genealogical and historical — descent from the Ak Koyunlu, the "Tribe of the White Sheep," the settlement in Fars under Shah Ismail, the twentieth-century wars with the state — alongside Oghuz romance and heroic narrative, animal tales, riddles and a dense proverb stock. Sohrab Dolatkhah's *Qashqay Folktales* (2015) is the principal published corpus, part of the same scholar's documentation of the language. The decisive modern institution is Mohammad Bahmanbeigi's tent-school movement, which from the 1950s brought literacy to migrating camps and produced the first generation of Qashqai writers; contemporary poets now publish in Qashqai Turkic in the Perso-Arabic script.

## Language & religion

Qashqai (*Turki*) is an Oghuz Turkic language closely related to Azerbaijani, with substantial Persian lexical and syntactic borrowing and no standardised orthography, though it is written in the Perso-Arabic script. The confederacy is historically polyglot — it absorbed Lur, Kurdish and Arab clans alongside Turkic Khalaj, Afshar, Bayat, Qajar, Shamlu and Igdir elements — and Qashqai settled in and around Shiraz are shifting to Persian. Religiously the Qashqai are Twelver Shi'a Muslims, but with the light clerical footprint typical of pastoral societies: religious practice runs through vows (*nazr*), pilgrimage to local *imamzadeh* shrines, amulets and the burning of wild rue (*esfand*) against the evil eye rather than through mosque attendance. Relations with the clerical state have been adversarial since 1979.
