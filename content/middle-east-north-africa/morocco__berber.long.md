## Overview

The Berbers — *Imazighen* (sing. *Amazigh*, "free/noble people") — are the indigenous population of North Africa, and Morocco holds their largest concentration: roughly a third to a half of the country's 37 million people speak or descend from speakers of a Berber language. Three major dialect blocks map onto three landscapes. *Tarifit* speakers (Rifians) occupy the Rif range along the Mediterranean, from Nador to Al Hoceima. *Tamazight* proper is spoken by the Middle Atlas confederations — Zemmour, Zaiane, Ait Bou Ichaouen, Beni Ouarain — on the cedar plateaux around Khenifra and Azrou. *Tashelhit* (Chleuh) covers the High Atlas, the Anti-Atlas and the Souss plain behind Agadir and Taroudant, with weaving centres at Taznakht and Tazenakht's outlying villages, and Azilal in the central High Atlas. Berber languages belong to the Afroasiatic family, which also contains Egyptian, Semitic and Cushitic. For folk culture, the Moroccan Imazighen matter because a non-urban, non-courtly design vocabulary — geometric, tattoo-derived, matrilineally transmitted — survived Roman, Arab, Ottoman-adjacent and French rule largely intact, and is legible today in pile carpets, silver fibulae, and mud-brick granaries.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Beni Ourain** — Thick, ivory-ground pile rugs woven by the Beni Ouarain confederation of the eastern Middle Atlas from undyed, high-lanolin mountain sheep wool. The design is a sparse dark lozenge lattice or irregular diamond grid on cream, with the knot count deliberately low and the pile long: these were sleeping rugs for cold cedar-forest winters, not floor decoration, which is why they lack the dense polychromy of Anti-Atlas work.

**Azilal** — High Atlas rugs from the Azilal province, distinguished from Beni Ourain by an off-white or pale wool ground carrying free-drawn, asymmetric figuration in dyed wool and sometimes recycled cotton thread: single figures, ladders, hooked lines, isolated diamonds placed without regard to symmetry. Each is effectively a one-off composition.

**Taznakht (Ouaouzguite)** — Flatweave and mixed-technique rugs from the Ouaouzguite groups around Taznakht in the Siroua massif between the High Atlas and Anti-Atlas, dyed with madder, henna and indigo into a dense red-dominant field of narrow banded registers, often combining kilim, *soumak*-type wrapping and knotted pile in one piece.

**Boucherouite** — Rag rugs (from *bu sherwit*, "made of scraps") knotted from cut clothing, nylon, lurex and plastic when wool was unaffordable or unavailable. Twentieth-century, aggressively polychrome, and now collected as an art form in its own right; it is the one Moroccan Berber textile whose palette is governed by what came out of the household rag pile.

**Handira** — The bride's cape of the Middle Atlas, a cream or striped wool flatweave densely hung with sequins (*mrayat*, small mirrors or metal discs) so that it rustles and glitters in movement. Worn over the shoulders at the wedding procession, then repurposed as a blanket; the sequin field is apotropaic as much as ornamental.

**Hanbel / kilim Berber** — The general flatwoven class: tapestry-woven and weft-faced bands, used as blankets, floor covers, tent dividers and donkey trappings. Rif and Middle Atlas *hanbels* run to narrow horizontal stripes with inserted lozenge and comb motifs.

**Haïk and tagelmust** — The *haïk* is the large undyed woollen or cotton body wrap of women (and, in some regions, men), draped without tailoring. The *tagelmust* is the long indigo-dyed veil-turban of the Saharan Tuareg to the south, indexed here because the indigo cloth trade ran up through Moroccan oasis markets.

**Motif vocabulary.** *tiyni* / diamond-lozenge (female fertility, the eye); the hooked or barbed lozenge (protection against the evil eye); the comb (*imshed*, the weaver's beater, also a tattoo sign); the "tent" or triangle; the eight-pointed star; the serpent or zigzag (water, snake); the *khamsa* or open hand (five, warding); the frog/spider figure; the ladder; the *tifinagh*-derived cross-bar and dotted characters used decoratively.

### Clothing & dress

Men's daily dress is the *djellaba*, a hooded, loose, ankle-length robe in wool for winter and cotton for summer, over a *qamis*; the hood (*qob*) is functional against sun and cold. Over it, a *selham* or *burnous* — a semicircular hooded cloak, typically white or dark brown, the shepherd's and the notable's garment alike. Footwear is the *belgha*, a backless pointed slipper in soft leather, yellow for men. In the Souss and Anti-Atlas, men wound a light turban (*rezza*).

Women's dress varies sharply by region. In the High Atlas and Souss a length of cloth is wrapped and pinned at both shoulders with a pair of *fibulae* (*tizerzai*, sing. *tazersit*) joined by a chain, over which a belt (*tasmert*) cinches the waist — a construction with no tailoring at all, closer to a classical peplos than to Arab-urban dress. Rifian women wear the striped red-and-white *fouta* wrapped over the skirt and the wide-brimmed straw hat (*rezza n tmurt*) with woollen pompoms, unique in Morocco. Headscarves are layered and knotted with silver and amber; a bride adds the sequinned *handira* and, formerly, a painted or tattooed face.

### Architecture

The signature form is the *tighremt* (Arabic *kasbah*), a rammed-earth (*pisé*, locally *tabout*) fortified house of two to four storeys with battered corner towers, flat terrace roof on poplar and oleander joists, and upper walls incised or brick-laid into relief lozenges, chevrons and step patterns identical to the carpet vocabulary. Ground floor for animals and storage, middle for living, roof for drying grain and figs. Whole valley villages of these — the *ksar* (pl. *ksour*) — line the Drâa, Dadès and Todgha valleys, entered through a single gate.

Distinctively Berber and rarer is the *agadir* or *igherm*, the collective fortified granary of the Anti-Atlas and Souss: a walled compound of hundreds of stacked stone cells, each the property of one family and holding grain, oil, documents and silver, administered by an elected assembly under written customary law. In the High Atlas, houses are dry-stone with juniper beams and flat earth roofs, stepped against the slope so that one house's roof is the next one's terrace. The Rif builds in stone and mud with, unusually for Morocco, pitched thatched or tiled roofs against Mediterranean rain.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Rural Berber pottery is hand-built without the wheel, by women, fired in open bonfires — the opposite of the male, wheel-thrown, kiln-fired urban tradition of Fez and Safi. Rif pottery from the Jebala–Rif margin is painted in iron-red and manganese geometric bands on a cream slip, with the same lozenge and comb signs as tattoos. Common forms: the *tajine* base and conical lid, storage jars, water jars, the *tagra* bowl, oil lamps, and the *gedra* cooking pot. Woodwork centres on carved cedar and walnut from the Middle Atlas: doors, chests, ceiling panels, granary locks with wooden pin-tumbler mechanisms, spoons, and *atlas* carved bowls. Thuya burl from Essaouira is turned and inlaid. Metalwork includes engraved brass trays, henna-applied *nqash* dagger scabbards, and the curved *koumiya* dagger of the Souss with a silver-sheathed hilt.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Moroccan Berber silver is heavy, low-purity, and worked by engraving, niello, enamel and stone-setting rather than filigree. The defining object is the *fibula* (*tazersit*, pl. *tizerzai*), a triangular or disc-headed pin worn in pairs and chained across the chest, closing the woman's wrap; its triangle is read as female. Enamelled cloisonné work in yellow, green and blue on silver, set with cabochon glass and amber, is the speciality of Tiznit and the Ida ou Nadif of the Anti-Atlas. Other standard forms: the *khamsa* hand pendant; the *taounza* forehead band; heavy hinged bracelets (*nbala*) with spikes; coin necklaces strung with Maria Theresa thalers, amber (*luban*), coral and Saharan carnelian; and the *tasbih* amulet box holding a written charm. Berber tattoo (*oucham*) — vertical chin lines, brow and ankle marks in indigo soot — was applied to women at puberty and marriage, its motifs identical to weaving signs; the practice was suppressed under Islamic reformist pressure through the twentieth century and survives mainly on elderly women, though henna (*henna*) painting of hands and feet at weddings remains universal.

## Music & performance

Moroccan Berber music is regionally distinct and largely village-based. In the High Atlas and Souss, the *rwais* are professional Tashelhit poet-musicians who travel with a repertoire of sung poetry, led by a *rais* playing the single-string bowed *rebab* (*ribab*) and accompanied by *lotar* (a small plucked lute), *naqus* (a struck metal plate or brake drum) and hand-clapping; a *rwais* performance moves through an instrumental *astara* prelude, sung verse, and a fast concluding *tabraouine*. Their poetry is composed, memorised and politically pointed, and the great twentieth-century *rwais* recorded commercially from the 1930s on 78s.

Collective village music centres on the *bendir*, a large frame drum with a snare of gut across the head, and the *tallunt* frame drum; the *ganga* double-headed drum; the *ghaita* (shawm) at weddings and *moussems*; and the *awada* or *zamr* double-reed clarinet with cowhorn bells. The Middle Atlas has *izlan*, short sung couplets exchanged between men's and women's lines, and *tamawayt*, a long, free-rhythm, highly melismatic solo lament that opens a performance.

The *Gnawa* — descendants of enslaved West Africans, and a distinct people rather than Imazighen — perform all-night *lila* possession ceremonies across Morocco with the *guembri* (*hajhuj*) three-string bass lute and iron *qraqeb* castanets; Berber villages of the south host them and the repertoires have interpenetrated.

## Dance & theatre

**Ahwash** is the emblematic Tashelhit village dance of the High Atlas and Souss: an evening event in which men form a line or ring of *bendir* players, tuning their drums at a fire, while women in facing lines answer with sung verse and a slow, dense collective step, the tempo accelerating over an hour. The song text is improvised poetry, so *ahwash* is as much a verbal contest as a dance.

**Ahidous** is its Middle Atlas Tamazight counterpart, danced by mixed lines of men and women shoulder to shoulder, swaying and stamping in a circle or crescent with *bendir* accompaniment and antiphonal *izlan* verses, faster and more tightly interlocked than *ahwash*.

**Taskiwin**, of the western High Atlas, is a martial men's dance named for the powder-horn (*tiskt*) each dancer wears on the left shoulder; the dancers shake the shoulder against the horn while stepping, in a display of coordinated shoulder tremor. It is inscribed on UNESCO's list of intangible heritage in need of urgent safeguarding.

**Guedra**, from the Saharan margin at Guelmim and among the Tuareg-adjacent groups, is danced on the knees by a veiled woman, expressed entirely through hands, fingers and torso, over the beat of the *guedra* cooking-pot drum. There is no indigenous scripted theatre; narrative performance is carried by the *rwais* and by marketplace storytellers (*hlaykia*) in the Souss halqa circles.

## Festivals & rituals

The Amazigh new year, *Yennayer* or *Id Yennayer*, falls on 13–14 January by the Julian-derived agrarian calendar and is marked by a special couscous with seven vegetables, dried fruit and nuts, and by a household hiding of a date stone or almond in the dish for luck. Since 2024 it is an official public holiday in Morocco. The agricultural calendar also carries *Boujloud* (*Bilmawn*), the masked goat festival following Eid al-Adha in the Souss and Anti-Atlas: a man wrapped in the fresh skins and horns of the sacrificed goats runs through the village striking bystanders with hooves to drive out misfortune, accompanied by cross-dressed and blackened attendants — a licensed carnival inversion that Islamic reformers have repeatedly tried to suppress.

*Moussems* — saint's-day pilgrimage fairs at the tomb (*marabout*, *sidi*) of a local holy figure — punctuate the summer and autumn: the Moussem of Moulay Idriss Zerhoun, the Moussem of Sidi Ahmed ou Moussa in the Souss (historically the gathering of the *rwais* and acrobats), and the *Imilchil* betrothal moussem in the Middle Atlas in September, attached to the shrine of Sidi Mohammed el Maghani, where marriages among the Ait Haddidou are contracted. The *taghenja* rain rite — a decorated ladle carried in procession by children or women, with sung invocations — is performed in drought.

Life-cycle rites: the *sbou*, the naming ceremony on the seventh day after birth, with a sacrificed sheep; circumcision in early boyhood; the multi-day wedding with a henna night, the bride's procession in the *handira*, and the gifting of the *fibulae* and silver by the groom's family as the bride's inalienable property; and burial before sunset the following day, with a third-, seventh- and fortieth-day commemoration.

## Foodways

Barley, not wheat, is the Berber staple grain of the mountains, eaten as *aghroum* — a flat, dense hearth bread baked on a griddle or in a domed clay oven — and as *tacheddirt* or *baddaz*, coarse barley couscous. *Seksu* (couscous) is steamed in a *keskes* over a broth of mutton, turnip, pumpkin, carrot and chickpea and is the Friday and feast dish. *Tajine* names both the conical earthenware vessel and the slow stew cooked in it; the southern and Atlas versions run to lamb with preserved lemon and olives, chicken with almond and onion (*tfaya*), or the vegetarian *tajine* of the poor months. *Tanjia*, a Marrakshi urban dish of meat sealed in an amphora and cooked overnight in the ashes of the hammam furnace, crosses Arab–Berber lines. *Rfissa* and *trid* are shredded-pastry dishes with lentils and fenugreek, eaten by women after childbirth. *Amlou* — roast almond, honey and argan oil beaten to a paste — is a Souss speciality dependent on the *argan* tree endemic to the Anti-Atlas, whose oil is cold-pressed by women's cooperatives. *Smen* is clarified butter buried and aged for months, sometimes years, as a prestige condiment. Mint tea (*atay*) — Chinese gunpowder green tea, spearmint and a great deal of sugar, poured from height into small glasses in three servings of decreasing strength — is the compulsory rite of hospitality; in the Atlas it is brewed instead with wild thyme, sage or *chiba* (wormwood) in winter. Food is halal; the sacrificed sheep of Eid al-Adha is the year's principal meat event.

## Oral tradition & literature

There is no single Berber epic. The literary weight sits in sung poetry — the *rwais* repertoire of composed Tashelhit verse, the *izlan* couplets and the *tamawayt* laments of the Middle Atlas — and in *tinfusin*, prose folktales told at night by women, whose stock characters include the ogress *Teryel* (cognate with the Kabyle *Tseriel*), the trickster jackal (*Ouchen*), and the clever poor boy. The trickster cycle of *Jha* (Si Djeha) is shared with the Arabic-speaking Maghreb. Proverb (*inzan*) and riddle (*tinfusin n imukan*) exchange is a competitive evening genre. Written Berber literature in Morocco is old but discontinuous: Tashelhit religious and didactic verse was written in Arabic script from at least the sixteenth century, notably the work of Muhammad Awzal (d. 1749), whose *al-Ḥawḍ* and *Baḥr al-Dumūʿ* circulated widely in Souss zawiyas. Since the 2001 creation of IRCAM (the Royal Institute of Amazigh Culture) and the 2011 constitutional recognition of Tamazight as an official language, Neo-Tifinagh has been standardised, taught in schools, and used for a growing published literature, and Moroccan television broadcasts in all three dialect blocks.

## Language & religion

Berber (Amazigh) languages form a branch of Afroasiatic, alongside Semitic, Egyptian, Cushitic, Chadic and Omotic. Morocco's three principal varieties — Tarifit (Rif), Tamazight (Middle Atlas), Tashelhit (High Atlas, Anti-Atlas, Souss) — are not mutually intelligible across the extremes. The ancient *Tifinagh* consonantal script, attested in Libyco-Berber inscriptions found in the Oran region and across the Maghreb and preserved continuously only by the Tuareg, was revived and standardised as Neo-Tifinagh and adopted as Morocco's official Amazigh alphabet in 2003. Moroccan Imazighen are overwhelmingly Sunni Muslim of the Maliki school, with a strong Sufi overlay: the *zawiya* lodges, the veneration of local saints and their *marabout* tombs, and the *moussem* pilgrimages are the living form of religion in the mountains, alongside pre-Islamic survivals — the *taghenja* rain rite, *Boujloud*, the evil eye, *baraka* transmitted by touch, and the protective triangle and open hand worked into silver and wool. Pre-Islamic Berbers were variously Christian (the Donatist movement correlated strongly with Berber identity), Jewish, and polytheist; Morocco's indigenous Jewish communities, many of them Berber-speaking, were a fixture of Atlas and Souss villages until the mid-twentieth century.
