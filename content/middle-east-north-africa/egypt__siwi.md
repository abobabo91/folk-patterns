## Overview

The Siwi, or Siwan people, are a Berber ethnic group native to Egypt’s Siwa and Qara oases. The sources describe them as numbering about 30,000 and as the easternmost concentration of Berbers. Their language, Siwi, is a Berber language spoken by about 15,000 to 20,000 people in the oases of Siwa and Gara, near the Libyan border. Siwi is used for daily communication, while nearly all Siwis also learn Egyptian Arabic from an early age. The Siwi are historically connected with the Banu Wasin tribe, part of the Berber-speaking Zenata tribal confederation. Their folk-culture significance in the sources lies chiefly in oral literature, song, poetry, tales, riddles, proverbs, and distinctive linguistic practices.

## Music & performance

Siwi oral performance includes sung poetry, songs, and religious poetry. Sung poetry, called *adyaz*, is associated mainly with bachelors’ gatherings and tends to concern love. Religious poetry, called *ləqṣidət*, is recited. Malim distinguishes songs led by one man from shorter poems recited antiphonally by groups at weddings. The sources state that both forms are accompanied by drums and horns.

The sources describe a historical association between song and Siwi social life. In previous centuries, songs appear to have held symbolic importance for Siwi young men. A civil war in the oasis in 1712 was reportedly ended by a treaty containing a rule that if a Western *zaggālah*, or bachelor farm worker, stopped singing in a garden, an Eastern *zaggālah* had to begin and finish the song.

The earliest published Siwi lyrics were gathered by Bricchetti-Robetti. Other songs and poems were published by Jawharī and Souag, while Abd Allah and Malim provided songs and poems in translation. Schiffer studied the songs from a musicological perspective.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention antiphonal poetry at weddings and musical performance with drums and horns.

## Festivals & rituals

Weddings are identified as a performance context for shorter poems recited antiphonally by groups. The sources also describe religious poetry, *ləqṣidət*, as a recited form.

## Oral tradition & literature

Siwi is described as the vehicle of a little-documented oral literature. Four genres are especially noted: song lyrics or poems, fairy tales, riddles, and proverbs. Siwi verse is written in rhyme and is usually associated with song.

A Siwi tale, *tanf̣ast*, uses a specific opening formula and a closing formula. The tales were typically told by old women to children in the evenings, both to entertain and perhaps to educate them. Since the arrival of television in the oasis, this practice has largely disappeared. Characters include humans, talking animals, and the ogre *amẓa* or ogress *tamẓa*. The first Siwi tales printed were four short fables gathered from men by Laoust: “The Jackal and the Ewe,” “The Jackal and the Hyena,” “The Hare, the Jackal, the Hyena, and the Lion,” and “The Magic Ring.” Malim published “The Green Cow” and “The King’s Daughter and the Three Beautiful Girls” in English translation. Schiattarella transcribed and translated fourteen tales gathered from women.

Malim describes riddles as once the preferred pastime of Siwi women, who met at night to exchange them. Television has largely superseded this practice. Malim records about twenty riddles, while Schiattarella records four. Malim also distinguishes “morals,” or timeworn advice in proverbial form, from proverbs proper.

## Language & religion

Siwi, also known as Siwan or Siwa Berber, has the endonym *Jlan n isiwan*. It is the easternmost Berber language and the only Berber language indigenous to Egypt. It is spoken in Siwa and Gara, near the Libyan border. The sources describe Siwi as heavily influenced by Egyptian and Bedouin Arabic, as well as by earlier stages of Arabic. Nearly all Siwis learn Egyptian Arabic as a second language from an early age.

Siwi is not taught in local schools, used in the media, or recognised by the Egyptian government, and its long-term survival is described as threatened. The language is not normally written; Siwi people generally write in Standard Arabic. Within Berber classification, it has been associated with Zenati and with an Eastern Berber group including Awjila and Sokna. Other classifications connect it with Sokna and the Nafusi dialect cluster, while excluding Awjila. The Endangered Languages Project classifies Siwa as vulnerable to extinction.
