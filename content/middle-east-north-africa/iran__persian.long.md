## Overview

Persians are an Iranian ethnic group indigenous to the Iranian plateau and the majority population of modern Iran — roughly 45–50 million people. Their historic core is *Persis* (Pārsa, modern Fars province) in the southern Zagros, with Shiraz as its capital and the Achaemenid sites of Pasargadae and Persepolis on its plain; the demographic centre today runs north through the oasis cities of the central plateau rim — Isfahan on the Zayandeh Rud, Kashan and Qom at the edge of the Dasht-e Kavir, Yazd deep in the desert, Kerman to the southeast, Mashhad in Khorasan. They speak Persian (Fārsi), a West Iranian language of the Indo-European family descended through Middle Persian from Old Persian. In folk-culture terms they matter because the Persianate city workshop — carpet loom, tilework kiln, inlay bench, lacquer atelier — supplied the design vocabulary that Ottoman, Mughal, Central Asian and Caucasian craft traditions all worked from and against.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Persian carpet** (*farsh*, *qāli*) — hand-knotted pile on cotton or silk warp, wool or silk pile, tied with the asymmetric Persian (*senneh*) knot, which allows finer curvilinear drawing than the symmetric Turkish knot used to the west. Regional signatures are sharply distinct: **Kashan carpet** and **Kerman carpet** built on formal medallion-and-spandrel cartoons; **Tabriz rug** in high knot counts with a hard, dense handle; **Qom silk carpet**, a twentieth-century silk specialism; **Nain rug**, ivory-and-blue with very fine wool-and-silk highlighting; **Isfahan** carpets on silk warp with pronounced *shāh abbāsi* palmettes.

**Gabbeh** — thick, long-piled, coarsely knotted tribal rug from Fars, woven by Qashqai and Lur women in blocks of undyed or madder-red ground with reduced, almost abstract figures; it is the deliberate opposite of the city cartoon.

**Kilim** (*gelim*) — flat-woven slit-tapestry blanket, rug and grain-sack fabric, geometric by structural necessity, made across nomadic and village Iran alongside the related *jajim* warp-faced striped weave.

**Termeh** — Yazd's figured twill of fine merino-type wool, woven in vertical bands of *boteh* and vine on saffron, red or dark ground, traditionally a cover for gift trays, Qur'an stands and the Nowruz table rather than dress cloth.

**Yazd textile** — the desert city's broader silk and *zari* (metal-thread brocade) industry, also the source of *qadak* and block-printed *qalamkār* cotton, the resist- and stamp-printed cloth associated equally with Isfahan.

**Suzani** — chain-stitch and couched silk embroidery on cotton ground, shared with Central Asian Persian-speakers and worked in Iran as hangings and covers.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Boteh* (the almond/cypress bent-tip figure that became "paisley"); *shāh abbāsi* (the Safavid composite palmette); *gol o morgh* ("flower and bird"); *herāti* or *māhi dar ham* ("fish-in-pond", a rosette flanked by lanceolate leaves); *toranj* (central medallion) and *lachak* (corner spandrel); *eslimi* (split-leaf arabesque scroll); *khatāi* (Chinese-derived lotus and cloud-band); *sarv* (cypress, emblem of endurance); *vāgireh* (the repeating design unit); *boteh jeqqe* (crowned boteh).

### Clothing & dress

Urban Persian men of the Qajar era wore the *arkhalogh* (fitted short coat) over a collarless shirt, the wide *shalvār*, a *shāl* sash wound at the waist for carrying a knife and purse, a long *qabā* robe, and the black brimless *kolāh-e namadi* of felt or the tall astrakhan lambskin cap; clerics kept the wound turban (*ammāmeh*) and the *abā* cloak. Women's indoor dress under the Qajars was the short pleated skirt (*shaliteh*) over leggings with a bolero jacket, replaced in the street by the *chādor*, the face-veil *rubandeh* and the *chāqchur* trousered outer wrap. Pahlavi-era dress reform pushed European clothing in the cities. Rural and tribal dress kept its own logic: in Fars villages, layered gathered skirts, a fringed headscarf (*charqad*) pinned under the chin, and a spangled tunic for weddings. Footwear is the *giveh* — a hand-stitched shoe with a cotton-yarn upper worked over a leather or compressed-rag sole, made in Kermanshah and the Zagros towns and cool enough for summer walking; leather *na'layn* slippers and, in tribal use, *malaki* rawhide sandals.

### Architecture

The plateau house is inward-facing: a walled *hayāt* courtyard with a pool (*houz*) and fruit trees, rooms ranged on the *zemestān-neshin* (winter, south-facing) and *tābestān-neshin* (summer, north-facing) sides, and a vaulted *ivān* porch opening onto the court. Walls are mud-brick (*khesht*) or baked brick with straw-lime plaster; roofs are barrel vaults and shallow domes laid without centring, since timber is scarce. Yazd contributes the *bādgir* wind-catcher, a shaft raised above the roof to drive desert air down over a cistern, and the *āb anbār* domed reservoir fed by *qanāt* underground channels; the *yakhchāl* is a conical mud-brick ice-house. Ornament is masonry-borne: *kāshi-kāri* glazed revetment (**Isfahan tile**, **Kashan tile**, **Safavid tile** in cobalt, turquoise and mustard), *moaraq* cut-tile mosaic assembled piece by piece, *haft rang* seven-colour overglaze panels, *muqarnas* stalactite vaulting, *āyneh-kāri* mirror mosaic, carved *gach* stucco, and *orosi* sash windows glazed with coloured glass in wooden tracery. Fars keeps the Achaemenid and Sasanian stone legacy — Persepolis, Naqsh-e Rostam, the Palace of Ardashir. The quadripartite *chahārbāgh* Persian garden, walled and rill-fed, is inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List (2011).

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Kashan was the medieval centre for lustreware and *mināi* overglaze-enamelled bowls; Iznik and later European blue-and-white both answer Persian precedents. Later kilns at Kerman, Mashhad and Na'in produced blue-and-white and celadon-imitating wares; Lalejin near Hamadan remains the working pottery town. In metal, **qalamzani** is chased and repoussé engraving on copper, brass, silver and tinned copper trays, ewers, bowls and *qalyān* water-pipe bodies; **minakari** is champlevé and painted vitreous enamel on copper, almost always cobalt ground with floral roundels, an Isfahan bazaar speciality. **Khatam** / **khāṭamkāri** is micro-mosaic marquetry: bundles of brass wire, camel bone, ebony and jujube wood glued into triangular rods, sliced and veneered onto boxes, mirror frames, Qur'an stands and doors. **Persian lacquerware** — papier-mâché pen boxes (*qalamdān*), mirror cases and book covers painted in *gol o morgh* and varnished — peaked under the Qajars. Households also keep copper *deg* cauldrons, brass mortars from Borujerd, and *ghalyan* glass bases.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Gold is the standard for urban wedding jewelry — the bride's *shishdāng* or *nim-set* of necklace, earrings, ring and bangles, given as *mehriyeh*. Turquoise (*firuzeh*) from the Nishapur mines is the signature Iranian stone, set in silver seal rings alongside carnelian (*'aqiq*) and agate engraved with Qur'anic phrases or the names of the Imams — men's rings as much as women's. Protective forms include the *ta'viz* or *bāzuband* amulet case worn on the upper arm holding a written prayer, the *chashm-e nazar* blue eye-bead, and coin-strung tribal headbands and chin-chains among Qashqai and Bakhtiari women. Henna (*hanā*) is applied to the bride's hands and to the groom's at the pre-wedding *hanā-bandān*, and used as a hair dye by older women; *sorme* (kohl) rims the eyes of both infants and brides.

## Music & performance

Persian art music is organised as the *radif*, a memorised repertoire of some three hundred *gusheh* (melodic models) grouped into twelve *dastgāh* and *āvāz* — Shur, Māhur, Homāyun, Segāh, Chahārgāh, Navā, Rāst-Panjgāh and their derivatives — realised in improvisation rather than fixed composition. Sasanian practice already divided the octave into seventeen tones, and the modern system keeps microtonal intervals notated with the *koron* and *sori* accidentals. Instruments: the long-necked *tār* (mulberry body, lambskin face, brass frets), the smaller *setār* plucked with the index fingernail, the bowed spike-fiddle *kamāncheh*, the hammered trapezoid *santur*, the *ney* end-blown reed flute, the goblet drum *tombak* (*zarb*), and the frame drum *daf* used in Sufi assemblies. The *sorna* shawm and *naqāreh* kettledrums supply the outdoor *naqqāreh-khāneh* ensemble sound. Vocal genres are led by *āvāz*, unmetered solo singing on classical ghazals, and *tasnif*, the composed metrical ballad. Ritual singing is central: *rowzeh-khāni* mourning recitation, *nowheh* and *sineh-zani* chest-beating chants in Muharram, and *monājāt* and *azān* from the minaret. Coffee-house (*qahveh-khāneh*) performance was the popular frame for narrative chant, and the *maddāh* remains a professional religious singer.

## Dance & theatre

Solo improvised *raqs-e Irāni* (also *bābā karam* and the flirtatious *raqs-e chāqu* knife dance) works through wrist rotation, finger snaps, shoulder shimmy and small hip articulation rather than travelling figures; women dance at *mehmāni* parties and weddings, men in the more percussive *bandari* style of the Gulf coast. Sufi practice contributes *samā'* with the *daf*. The commanding dramatic tradition is **ta'ziyeh**, the Shi'i passion play of the martyrdom of Husayn at Karbala, staged in Muharram in the round in a *tekyeh* or *husayniyyeh*, with roles divided between sympathetic characters who chant in *dastgāh* modes and antagonists who declaim in prose, and all parts taken by men. Alongside it run the comic traditions: *ruhowzi* or *siāh-bāzi*, improvised farce played on boards over a courtyard pool with a blackface trickster servant; *khayāl-bāzi* shadow play; *kheimeh shab-bāzi*, glove-and-string puppetry performed from a small tent to *tombak* accompaniment; and *naqqāli*, dramatic solo storytelling from the *Shāhnāmeh*, delivered standing with a stick and a painted cloth in the coffee house. *Zurkhāneh* — the "house of strength" — is a hybrid of athletics, drum-and-bell recitation and Shahnameh chant.

## Festivals & rituals

**Nowruz**, the Zoroastrian-rooted New Year at the vernal equinox (20–21 March), is the pivot of the year: the *haft-sin* table of seven symbolic *s*-items, spring cleaning (*khāneh-tekāni*), thirteen days of visiting, and **Sizdah Bedar** on the thirteenth, when families picnic outdoors and throw the sprouted *sabzeh* into running water. The preceding Tuesday night is **Chahārshanbeh Suri**, jumping bonfires with the couplet *zardi-ye man az to, sorkhi-ye to az man*. **Shab-e Yaldā** on the longest night (20–21 December) means watermelon, pomegranate, nuts and divination from the *Divān* of Hafez. Zoroastrian and rural calendars keep the mid-year **Tirgān** water festival and **Mehregān** in autumn, with **Sadeh** bonfires fifty days before Nowruz. The Shi'i calendar overlays this: Ramadan fasting and *iftār*; **Ashura** and Tasu'a in Muharram with *ta'ziyeh*, *nazri* food distribution and *'alam* standard-bearing processions; Arba'in pilgrimage; the mourning of Fatimah; and *Eid-e Ghadir*. Life-cycle rites: the *azān* whispered into a newborn's ear and a *panj* hand-print or amulet against the *āl* birth demon; boys' circumcision as a feast; marriage in stages — *khāstegāri* proposal, *baleh-borān*, *aqd* contract before the *sofreh-ye aqd* spread of mirror, candelabra, bread, honey and Qur'an with a silk canopy held overhead while sugar cones are ground, then the *arusi* banquet. Death brings washing, shrouding, burial within a day, the third, seventh and fortieth-day gatherings (*chelleh*) and the annual *sāl*.

## Oral tradition & literature

The **Shāhnāmeh** of Ferdowsi (completed 1010, written under Samanid-era patronage of Persian) is the national epic and the backbone of oral performance — Rostam and Sohrab, Siyavash, Zahhak and Kaveh the blacksmith are recited by *naqqāl* storytellers and painted on coffee-house canvases. Romance cycles circulate independently: *Khosrow and Shirin* and *Layla and Majnun* from Nizami Ganjavi's *Panj Ganj*, the prose *Samak-e 'Ayyār* and *Amir Arsalan*. The lyric and didactic canon — Rumi's *Masnavi*, Sa'di's *Gulistan* and *Bustan*, Hafez's *Divān*, Attar's *Conference of the Birds*, the *Rubaiyat* of Omar Khayyam — is quoted conversationally, and *fāl-e Hāfez* (bibliomancy from the Divān) is a domestic ritual. Folk genres include the quatrain *do-bayti*, *chistān* riddles, *zarb ol-masal* proverbs, and the tale collections gathered by Sadeq Hedayat in *Neyrangestān* and by Abol-Qasem Enjavi Shirazi's folklore archive. Modern literature continues the line through Hedayat's *Buf-e Kur*, Forough Farrokhzad, Ahmad Shamlou, Mehdi Akhavan-Sales, Parvin E'tesami and Simin Daneshvar.

## Language & religion

Persian (*Fārsi*) is West Iranian within the Iranian branch of Indo-European, continuing Middle Persian (Pahlavi) and Old Persian, attested from the Behistun inscription of Darius the Great. It is written in the Perso-Arabic script with four added letters, in *nasta'liq* for poetry and *shekasteh* for cursive; Old Persian used cuneiform and Middle Persian the Pahlavi scripts, with Avestan retained liturgically by Zoroastrians. Close relatives include Dari and Tajik, Tat in the Caucasus, Luri, Hazaragi and Aimaqi. Dialect variation runs Tehrani, Isfahani, Shirazi, Yazdi, Mashhadi. Most Persians are Twelver Shi'i Muslims, the state creed since the Safavids; there remain Zoroastrian communities in Yazd and Kerman with fire temples and *dakhma* towers, plus Jewish, Armenian and Bahá'í minorities. Folk religion runs through shrine visiting (*ziyārat*) at Mashhad, Qom and small *imāmzādeh* tombs, vow meals (*sofreh-ye Abbās*, *sofreh-ye Nazr*), Sufi orders (Nematollahi, Qadiri), and the evil-eye rite of burning *esfand* seed.
