## Overview

The Lak are a Kurdish tribe native to Western Iran. They inhabit much of Lorestan province, where they constitute over 65% of the population, most eastern regions of Kermanshah, parts of western Ilam, and areas around western Hamadan. Their principal territory includes the southern and western parts of Hamadan, eastern Kermanshah, western and northern Lorestan, and eastern Ilam; the wider region extends from Hamadan toward northern Khuzestan and Shahr-e Kord. The area east of Mount Kabir is known as Pishe-Kuh and the area west of it as Poshte-Kuh. The Laks speak Laki, which most linguists consider a Kurdish dialect. Their history combines Kurdish, Lur, Safavid, Zand, Qajar, Ottoman, and Persian contexts, while their self-identification remains divided among Kurdish, Lur, Lak, and identities related to all three.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources document Lak connections with Iranian and Kurdish historical writing. The Laks were first mentioned in the *Sharafnama* alongside the Zands among the less prominent Kurdish tribes subject to Persia. Sharafkhan Bidlisi divided the Kurds into the Kurmanj, Kalhur, Guran, and Lur groups and included the Laks under the Kalhur category. The sources also mention *Bustan al-Siyaha* of Zain al-Abidin Shirvani, which provided a more detailed early account and identified the tribes of Zand, Mafi, Bajalan, and Zandi-yi Kala among the Laks.

The rise of Karim Khan Zand is described as the most distinguished point in Lak history. The sources also identify Yar-Mohammad Khan Kermanshahi and Khalu Qorban Harsini as revolutionary leaders who emerged from among the Laks. Yar-Mohammad Khan Kermanshahi fought alongside the Constitutionalists in Tabriz and was killed in battle against Farmanfarma in Kermanshah in October 1912. Khalu Qorban Harsini commanded partisan units of Mirza Kuchik Khan in the Jangali movement before the rise of the Pahlavi dynasty in 1920.

## Language & religion

The Laks of Western Iran speak Laki, which is considered a Kurdish dialect by most linguists. Lak communities were traditionally divided into the Laks of Pish-e-Kuh in Lorestan and the Laks of Posht-e-Kuh in Kermanshah, Ilam, and adjacent parts of Iraq. These groups were mainly linked by Laki but were otherwise relatively independent. Some Lak communities continued to speak Laki, while others adopted neighboring Kurdish, Luri, and Gorani dialects. Many Lak tribes of Posht-e-Kuh traditionally spoke the local Southern Kurdish dialect rather than Laki.

The sources describe Lak identity as disputed and complex. Laks have identified as Kurds, Lurs, a distinct group, or a group related to both. Most Laks of Pish-e-Kuh identified firmly as Lurs, while those of Posht-e-Kuh showed strong ties to both Kurds and Lurs. Laki and Luri are described as belonging to different Iranian branches.

The majority of Laks follow Shia Islam. A considerable number remained Yarsani, especially around Sahneh and Nurabad. The sources also mention Lak communities in Iraq that adopted Sunni Islam, and state that religious affiliation historically influenced whether Lak communities were subject to Ottoman or Persian authority.

The separate Lak language of Dagestan is a Northeast Caucasian language spoken by the Lak people of the Russian autonomous republic of Dagestan. It is unrelated to the Laki discussed in the sources on the Lak people of Iran.
