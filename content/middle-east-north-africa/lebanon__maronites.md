## Overview

Maronites are a Syriac Christian ethnoreligious group native to the Eastern Mediterranean and the Levant, particularly Lebanon. Their largest traditional concentration was near Mount Lebanon, while communities also live in Syria, Cyprus, Israel, Palestine, and the Lebanese diaspora in the Americas, Europe, Australia, and Africa. In Lebanon, the Maronite Church reported approximately 1,062,000 Maronites in 1994, constituting up to 32% of the population. The group belongs to the Maronite Church, an Eastern Catholic church in full communion with the pope. Maronite cultural identity combines Lebanese, Syriac Christian, and Arabic elements, with Classical Syriac retained as the liturgical language alongside Arabic.

## Material culture

### Architecture

They do identify Mount Lebanon, the coastline, the Orontes Valley, Tripoli, Byblos, Beirut, Sidon, Batroun, and Bkerke as places associated with Maronite history, settlement, or religious institutions.

## Music & performance

They state that the Maronite Church belongs to the Syriac Christian tradition and the West Syriac Rite, and that Classical Syriac remains its liturgical language alongside Arabic. The sources also describe the Maronite Church as using the Antiochian Rite and identify Syriac-Antiochene liturgical practice in the Maronite community of Aleppo. These liturgical traditions are the only performance-related practices described in the supplied material.

## Festivals & rituals

They do, however, document religious and historical events connected with Maronite identity. Saint Maron, dated to 350–410 AD, is presented as the monk whose teachings became the basis of the Maronite tradition. In 402 AD, Abraham of Cyrrhus, called the Apostle of Lebanon, set out to convert non-Christians in Lebanon. In 451 AD, the Maronites followed the Council of Chalcedon. In 685 AD, Saint John Maron became the first Patriarch of the Maronite Church. The sources also identify the First Crusade in 1096 AD, the Siege of Jerusalem in 1187 AD, and the Lebanese Council of 1736 as important historical moments. No birth, coming-of-age, wedding, or funeral rites are described.

## Foodways

The supplied material mentions famine during World War I and states that over 4,000 people died from hunger and disease as a result of the 1860 Mount Lebanon conflict.

## Oral tradition & literature

They preserve historical and religious designations connected with Maronite identity, including Saint Maron, Abraham of Cyrrhus as the “Apostle of Lebanon,” and the name “Maronites.” The sources also mention literary and intellectual figures associated with Maronite cultural and political identity, including Charles Corm, Michel Chiha, Said Aql, Naguib Azoury, Ameen Rihani, and Kahlil Gibran. They describe Phoenicianism as an identity movement and state that the Maronite elite in Lebanon favored a primarily Lebanese identity during the 20th century.

## Language & religion

Maronites belong to the Maronite Syriac Church of Antioch, an Eastern Catholic Syriac church using the Antiochian Rite. The church belongs to the Syriac Christian tradition and the West Syriac Rite. Classical Syriac remains its liturgical language alongside Arabic. The early Maronites were Hellenized Semites who spoke Greek and Syriac, while Lebanese Aramaic remained a daily language as late as the 19th century. The sources also describe Cypriot Maronite Arabic, which the Cypriot government designates as a dialect, although Cypriot Maronites today speak Greek. The Maronite Church traces its foundation to Maron, an early 4th-century Syriac monk, and is in full communion with the Catholic Church.
