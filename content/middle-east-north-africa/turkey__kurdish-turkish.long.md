## Overview

The Kurds are Turkey's largest ethnic minority, composing between 15% and 25% of the population — estimates range from the 12.6 million of a 2000 National Security Council report to the 20–25 million claimed by Kurdish nationalists, with the CIA figure at roughly 14 million. They are concentrated in the east and southeast, in the region Kurds call Turkish Kurdistan: the volcanic uplands around Lake Van and Mount Ararat, the Hakkari massif on the Iraqi border, the Botan and Great Zab valleys, the Tigris plain at Diyarbakır, and the Dersim (Tunceli) highlands. Most speak Kurmanji, a Northwest Iranian language of the Indo-European family; a minority speak Zazaki. In folk-culture terms they matter because pastoral transhumance in high, difficult country preserved flatweave, felt and silverwork idioms into the twentieth century, and because a century of official denial — Kurds were classed as "Mountain Turks" until 1991 — pushed cultural transmission into song, textile and oral epic rather than print.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Van kilim** — Slitweave flatweaves from the Lake Van basin, woven on narrow horizontal ground looms in two panels later seamed, in madder red, indigo and undyed wool. Van work favours large concentric hooked medallions and heavy latch-hook borders, coarser in weave and bolder in scale than the fine Anatolian Turkish kilims of Konya or Sivas.

**Hakkari kilim** — From the highest Kurdish districts on the Iraqi and Iranian borders; dark-ground weaves with dense small-scale geometry, often with *cicim* brocading added over the plainweave ground. Hakkari pieces are among the most saturated in colour of all Anatolian flatweaves, with aubergine and deep green from local dyestuffs.

**Botan kilim** and **Serhat kilim** — Regional idioms named for the Botan river district around Siirt/Şırnak and for the Serhat plateau around Ağrı, Van and Kars. Botan weaves lean to narrow banded compositions; Serhat pieces are more open, with fewer, larger medallions suited to nomadic tent interiors.

**Cicim and sumak** — *Cicim* is supplementary-weft brocading worked over a plainweave ground so the motif stands proud of the surface; *sumak* (soumak) is wrapped weft, giving a dense embroidery-like face with a loose back. Both are used for saddlebags, cradle covers and grain sacks rather than floor rugs.

**Jajim and verneh** — *Jajim* is warp-faced striped weaving in narrow bands, seamed into bedding covers and bundle wrappers; *verneh* (*vernê*) is a brocaded flatweave with animal and bird figures, made in the Van–Muş belt as dowry cloth and quilt facing.

**Kurdish saddlebag / heybe** — *Heybe* are double-pouch bags for donkey or shoulder, woven in sumak or cicim with the two faces designed as a matched pair; the closure system of woven loops and slits is diagnostic of Kurdish rather than Turkmen work.

**Kurdish felt** — Beaten wool felt (*keçe*) for shepherds' capes, prayer mats and tent linings, coloured wool laid in before beating so the motif is integral rather than applied.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Elibelinde* (hands-on-hips, female fertility figure), *koçboynuzu* (ram's horn, male potency and flock wealth), *bereket* (fertility, paired diamonds), *pıtrak* (burr, averting the evil eye), *yılan* (serpent, guardian), *akrep* (scorpion, protective), *göz*/*çeşm* (eye, apotropaic), *tarak* (comb, marriage and birth), *su yolu* (running water), *bukağı* (fetter, binding lovers together).

### Clothing & dress

Men's dress centres on *şal û şapik* — baggy trousers gathered at the ankle and a short matching jacket, cut from handwoven wool cloth in fawn, grey or striped patterns, with a long sash (*şûtik*) wound many times round the waist to carry dagger, tobacco and money. The head is wrapped in a *kefî* or *cemedanî*, a fringed cotton or silk cloth twisted into a turban over a stiff felt cap, the fringe left to hang at the temple. Footwear is *klash*, hand-stitched shoes with cotton-thread uppers and a sole traditionally built from compressed cloth and hide, made for rock and scree.

Women wear a long gathered dress (*kiras*) over baggy trousers, layered with a sleeveless or open-fronted coat (*elek*, *xiftan*) in velvet or silk, and an apron-wrap. The head is covered with a *çarşev* or a stack of scarves over a skullcap hung with coins, sequins and crocheted floral edging. Ceremonial dress multiplies the layers, adds gold-thread couching at cuff and hem, and loads the headdress with silver and coin.

### Architecture

Highland Kurdish villages in Hakkari, Van and Şırnak build in dry-laid or mud-mortared fieldstone with flat earth roofs carried on poplar beams and brushwood, rolled each autumn with a stone cylinder; the roof doubles as threshing floor, sleeping terrace and drying ground. Houses are stepped into the slope so one house's roof is the next one's yard. Interiors combine a hearth room, a stable wing whose animal heat warms the dwelling through winter, and a *tandır* pit for bread. Summer pasture is occupied with black goat-hair tents (*kon*) and reed-screen *çît* windbreaks.

On the Tigris plain, Diyarbakır's urban tradition is entirely different: courtyard houses in alternating courses of black basalt and pale limestone, with an open summer *eyvan* facing north, a shaded arcaded *hayat* around the courtyard, a well and a pool. The same black basalt builds the city's Roman-founded walls and the Ulu Camii. Hoşap Castle, strengthened by the Mahmudi chief Sarı Süleyman Bey in 1643, is the surviving monument of Kurdish tribal lordship in the Van region. More than 3,000 villages of this vernacular were wiped from the map during the 1990s evacuations.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Copper is the defining medium: Diyarbakır and Siirt coppersmiths' rows produced tinned copper trays (*sini*), long-spouted water ewers, cauldrons (*qazan*) and the deep *tandır* bread tools, hammered and chased with rosettes and inscription bands. Woodwork is poplar and walnut — cradles, hand looms, butter churns, and the carved *destar* spindle. Wool-processing equipment (carders, drop spindles, weft beaters) is itself often decorated. Household gourds and goat-skin bags (*meşk*) serve for churning yoghurt. Utilitarian glazed earthenware is bought from Anatolian centres rather than made locally; the Kurdish contribution is in metal, wood and wool rather than clay.

### Jewelry & body adornment

**Kurdish silver** is the region's signature: granulated and filigree work, niello, and repoussé set with turquoise, carnelian and coloured glass. Standard forms include the hinged cuff bracelet (*bazin*), the multi-strand coin necklace, the domed openwork brooch, heavy anklets, and the *tumar*-type cylindrical amulet case worn on a cord to hold a written Qur'anic text. Headdress silver — coin chains, temple pendants, forehead bands — is dowry property, individually owned by the woman and convertible in hardship, which is why so much has left the region. Turquoise and blue glass beads and the burr motif both serve against the evil eye. Henna (*hene*) is applied to the bride's hands and feet, and to the groom's little finger, on the night before the wedding.

## Music & performance

The long-necked lute *tembûr* is the core instrument, played by **Şivan Perwer**, the composer-vocalist regarded as the founder of politically engaged Kurdish song. Beside it stand the *bilûr* (shepherd's end-blown flute), *zurna* (shawm) paired with *dehol* (double-headed drum) for outdoor dance music, *def* frame drum, and the *kemançe* bowed fiddle. The central vocal genre is the *dengbêj* tradition: unaccompanied, highly ornamented solo narrative singing of battles, feuds, elopements and laments, performed in the *dîwan* — the guest room of a notable's house — where a dengbêj could hold an audience for hours. Sub-genres include *lawik* (narrative ballad), *heyranok* (love song, often sung by women in dialogue), *stran* (general song), and *şeşbendî*. Women's repertoire carries the *lorî* lullaby and the *şîn* funeral lament, sung antiphonally by kinswomen.

Between 1982 and 1991 performing or recording songs in Kurdish was banned on Turkish television and radio, affecting singers including Şivan Perwer, Mahsun Kırmızıgül and İbrahim Tatlıses; a black market of pirate stations and underground cassettes carried the repertoire instead. Nizamettin Arıç (Feqiyê Teyra) was imprisoned for singing in Kurdish and fled to Syria and then Germany, later directing and starring in *Klamek ji bo Beko* (A Song for Beko), among the first Kurdish-language films.

## Dance & theatre

The dominant form is *govend* (Turkish *halay*), a linked line or open-circle dance in which dancers hold hands, little fingers or shoulders and move with a driving stamped step, led by a *sergovend* who signals changes with a handkerchief. Named regional variants include **Delîlo** of the Diyarbakır–Bitlis belt, **Şêxanî**, **Çepik**, **Sê Pê** ("three feet") and the fast **Geryan**; the *zurna–dehol* pair or an unaccompanied vocal sets the metre. Govend is mixed-gender, danced at weddings, circumcisions and Newroz, and lines can run to several dozen people. Distinct from it are the solo *dilan* improvisations and the Alevi *semah*, a slow turning devotional dance of the Dersim highlands performed to *saz* accompaniment at the *cem* gathering — ritual rather than entertainment. There is no indigenous Kurdish masked or puppet theatre tradition; dramatic narrative is carried by the dengbêj rather than staged.

## Festivals & rituals

*Newroz* (21 March) is the pivotal calendar event — new-year and spring equinox, marked by hilltop bonfires leapt by young men, new clothes, mass picnics and the largest annual gathering of Kurdish political and cultural identity in Turkey. Its political charge is explicit: Abdullah Öcalan's ceasefire letter was read in Turkish and Kurdish at the Diyarbakır Newroz of 21 March 2013. The pastoral year is punctuated by the spring move to summer pasture (*zozan*) and the autumn return, both marked by feasting, and by the *beran berdan*, the release of rams to the flock in autumn.

Islamic festivals — *Remezan* fasting, *Cejna Remezanê* (Eid al-Fitr) and *Cejna Qurbanê* (Eid al-Adha) — are observed with notably high rates of fasting and Friday attendance among Kurds in eastern Turkey. Alevi Kurds of Dersim instead keep the twelve-day *Xizir* fast in February and gather at *cem* rituals.

Life-cycle rites: birth is followed by seclusion and the salting-and-swaddling of the infant; the cradle (*dergûş*) is a dowry item. Circumcision (*sunet*) is a public feast with zurna and govend. Marriage runs through *xwazgînî* (formal asking), *nîşan* (engagement), the *şîranî* sweets exchange, a *hene* night of henna and women's song, and the bride's procession to her husband's house. Death brings three days of *şîn* — women's antiphonal lament, mirrors and bright cloth covered, and communal cooking by neighbours — followed by the third, seventh and fortieth-day commemorations.

## Foodways

Wheat is the base: *nan* flatbread baked against the wall of a *tandır* pit, and cracked wheat in every form. Signature dishes include *kutilk* (Kurdish *içli köfte*), bulgur shells stuffed with spiced mutton and onion and boiled or fried; *dowjik* / *dowa* ceresk, hot yoghurt soup thickened with rice or wheat and wild greens; *savar* bulgur pilaf with lamb fat; and *şêrîn* pastries. Diyarbakır contributes *kaburga dolması*, a lamb ribcage stuffed with rice and almonds, and *meftune* with sour plums. Sheep and goat dominate over beef; the dried-meat *qawirme* preserved in its own fat is winter provision.

Dairy is the pastoral core — *mast* yoghurt, *dew* salted buttermilk churned in a goat-skin *meşk*, clarified butter, and sheep cheeses including the herb-veined *tîr* type and cheeses buried in skins to ripen. Wild plants gathered in spring — *tolik* (wild rhubarb), sorrel, mallow, mountain garlic — enter soups and stuffings, and *sumak* berries, pomegranate molasses and *isot* (the sun-dried, oil-worked Urfa chilli of the southern districts) provide the sour-hot register.

Tea is drunk continuously, brewed dark in a stacked double pot and served in tulip glasses with sugar held in the cheek (*kıtlama*); coffee is a guest formality rather than daily habit. Food is halal, with alcohol largely absent from Sunni households; the great majority of Kurds follow the Shafi'i school, unlike the surrounding Hanafi Turkish and Arab populations.

## Oral tradition & literature

The national epic is *Mem û Zin*, composed by **Ahmad Khani** (Ehmedê Xanî) in 1692 — a tragic romance of two lovers of rival clans, read as an allegory of a divided Kurdish nation, and still recited and sung in fragments by dengbêj. Alongside it stand the *Siyabend û Xecê* mountain-hunt romance and the *Dimdim* fortress epic. **Ali Hariri** (1425–1495), from the Hakkari region, is held by some sources to be the first well-known poet writing in Kurdish; **Sharafkhan Bidlisi** wrote the *Sharafname*, the sixteenth-century history of the Kurdish principalities. Faqi Teyran's verse gave its name to the musician Nizamettin Arıç's stage name.

Transmission has been overwhelmingly oral, by necessity: the letters X, Q and W — present in the Kurdish alphabet but not Turkish — were prohibited until 2013, and Kurdish cannot be a language of instruction in Turkish schools. Kurdish became an elective school subject in 2012, with enrolment rising from 18,847 students to 77,931 by 2015–16 before stagnating near 60,000; university Kurdish studies began in 2011, with Mardin Artuklu University receiving 2,500 applications for its master's programme in 2012–13. In July 2020 Turkey's Council of Higher Education banned dissertations in Kurdish.

## Language & religion

Kurmanji (Northern Kurdish) is the mother tongue of the great majority — roughly 92% of those identifying as Kurdish in the eastern regions speak a Kurdish language, 6.4% Turkish, 1.4% Zazaki — within the Northwest Iranian branch of Indo-European. Kurmanji in Turkey is written in a Latin alphabet (the Hawar orthography); Kurds in Iraq and Iran use a Perso-Arabic script for Sorani. Language shift is rapid: of Kurdish parents of children aged 3–13, 80% claim proficiency but only 24% use Kurdish as the main home language, and only 18% of urban Kurdish youth can read and write it.

Most Kurds in Turkey are Sunni, predominantly of the **Shafi'i** school — a marker that distinguishes them from Hanafi Turks and Arabs — with Naqshbandi and Qadiri Sufi orders historically powerful; the Sheikh Said rebellion of 1925 was led by a Naqshbandi shaykh. **Alevism** accounts for a substantial minority, concentrated in Dersim/Tunceli among Zazaki speakers, with its own *cem* assembly, *dede* lineage clergy, *semah* dance and *Xizir* fast. Small Yezidi communities, whose Sheikh Adi–centred faith and textile traditions are indexed here, survive in Batman and Şanlıurfa after heavy emigration to Germany.
