## Overview

The Kurds (*Kurd*, plural *Kurdên*) of Turkey are an Iranian-speaking people indigenous to the mountainous zone where the eastern Taurus meets the northern reaches of the Zagros and the upper watersheds of the Tigris and Euphrates. Within the Republic of Turkey they are concentrated in the southeastern and eastern provinces — Diyarbakır (*Amed*), Mardin, Van, Hakkari, Şırnak, Siirt, Bitlis, Muş, Ağrı, Bingöl, Tunceli (*Dersim*), Urfa (*Riha*) and Kars — with very large diaspora populations in İstanbul, Ankara, İzmir and Adana following twentieth-century labour migration and the displacements of the 1980s–90s conflict. Estimates place the Kurdish population of Turkey at roughly 15–20 million, the largest single national community of the estimated 30–40 million Kurds worldwide, whose homeland straddles Turkey, Iraq, Iran and Syria. Most Turkish Kurds speak *Kurmancî* (Northern Kurdish), an Iranian language of the northwestern branch written in a Latin-based orthography developed by Celadet Bedirxan, while a substantial minority in Tunceli and adjoining districts speak *Zazakî* (*Dimilkî* / *Kirmanckî*), a related but distinct Iranian language. Their culture rests on a long stratigraphy — pre-Islamic Iranian religious substrates preserved among the Yezidis (*Êzidî*) and Alevis, tribal (*eşîr*) confederations recorded from the medieval period, and the emirate-scale principalities of Bitlis, Hakkari, Cizre-Botan and Baban that persisted into the Tanzimat era. In folk-culture terms the Turkish Kurds matter above all as the makers of the great flatweave belt of the eastern Anatolian highlands: the *kilim* of Van, Hakkari, Malatya, and Sarıkamış; the slit-tapestry, *cicim* and *sûmak* weaves of the Botan and Serhat regions; the felted shepherd cloaks of the *koçer* pastoralists; and the distinctive silver-and-coral bridal jewellery of the eastern *aşîret*.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Kilim** — the slit-tapestry flatweave is the signature Kurdish textile, woven on horizontal ground looms by women in wool from the family's own sheep. Regional schools are readily distinguished: **Van kilims** favour a deep madder-red ground with stepped hexagonal medallions (*gol*) and paired ram's-horn (*serê beran*) hooks; **Hakkari kilims**, from the high Zab valleys near the Iraqi border, are narrower, often woven in two panels seamed centre-back, with dense small-scale diamond fields; **Botan kilims** from the Cizre-Şırnak-Siirt triangle use bold vertical stripe compositions with *elîbelinde* (hands-on-hips) goddess figures and *muska* (triangular amulet) rows; **Serhat kilims** from the Ağrı-Kars-Erzurum plateau lean cooler, with indigo and undyed ivory grounds and reciprocal saw-tooth borders.

**Kurdish carpet** (*xalî*) — pile-knotted rugs, generally in the symmetrical (Turkish / Gördes) knot, produced in Van, Hakkari, Bitlis and among the Herki and Jaff-descended groups; typical designs include the multi-medallion Herki, the Jaff diamond-lattice bag face, and the "memling gul" derived from older Turkmen prototypes.

***Cicim*** — a supplementary-weft brocade in which small pattern floats are laid in over a plain-weave ground, producing an embroidered look while remaining fully woven; used for cradle covers, cushion faces and dowry cloths.

***Sûmak*** (*sumak*) — a weft-wrapping technique giving a herringbone surface on the front and long floats on the back, worked in the Botan and Van regions for heavy bag faces, floor spreads, and the large *sofreh* eating-cloths.

***Jajim*** (*cacim*) — a warp-faced narrow-loom weave produced in long strips subsequently sewn edge-to-edge into blankets and mattress covers, typical of the Serhat plateau and the Alevi Kurdish villages of Tunceli and Bingöl.

***Verneh*** — a weft-float weave, sometimes classed as a variety of *cicim*, associated with the Karakoyunlu-descended tribes of the Muş-Van basin, characterised by large stylised bird and quadruped fields on a dark ground.

***Heybe*** — the double saddlebag, woven as a single long panel in mixed technique (pile ends, flat centre) and folded; the Kurdish *heybe* is a canonical form of the eastern provinces, alongside smaller salt bags (*xurcik*) with characteristic tab closures.

**Kurdish felt** (*kulav*, *lebbade*) — hand-rolled sheep-wool felt worked without weaving, used for the heavy shepherd's cloak with exaggerated shoulders (*kepenek* in Turkish, *kulav* in Kurmancî), for prayer rugs, and for yurt covers among the *koçer* transhumant groups of the Serhat.

**Yezidi textile** — among the *Êzidî* communities historically centred on the Tur Abdin and the Sinjar borderland, weaving carried explicit religious symbolism: the peacock (*Tawûsî Melek*), the seven-pointed rosette, and the sun disc recur on *kilim* and on ceremonial cushion covers, and red-white-black is a preferred triadic palette linked to religious dress.

**Kurdish embroidery** — the eastern women's tradition includes fine chain-stitch (*zencîre*) and cross-stitch on the yoke, cuffs and hem of the *kiras* dress, silver metal-thread couching (*telkarî*) on velvet waistcoats and bridal *fistan*, and the beadwork edging of headscarves. Motifs are broadly shared with the wider weaving repertoire — *serê beran*, *muska*, *çiçek* (flower), *bergul* (rose) — worked in a palette dominated by madder red, indigo, walnut brown and undyed ivory.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Gol* (medallion), *serê beran* (ram's horns), *muska* (triangular amulet), *elîbelinde* (goddess figure, hands on hips), *tarax* (comb, protection in childbirth), *çengel* (hook, evil-eye repellent), *bergul* (rose), *dara jiyanê* (tree of life), *tawûs* (peacock, Yezidi), *stêrk* (star), *şerît* (band, running border).

### Clothing & dress

Men's traditional dress in the eastern provinces is the ***şal û şapik*** — matching wide-cut trousers and short jacket cut from a heavy handwoven mohair-and-wool twill (*şal*), historically produced around Şırnak and Siirt in undyed browns, greys and blacks, sometimes striped. The suit is worn over a collarless shirt and closed at the waist with the ***pêştemal*** (or *şûtik*), a long narrow sash of woven silk or cotton wound many times around the body and used to carry a dagger, tobacco and small tools. Headgear is the ***cemedanî*** (or *keffiye*), a fringed square scarf of black-and-white or red-and-white silk twisted into a turban over a felted skullcap (*kum*); the specific twist and drape identifies region and, in some communities, tribal affiliation. Footwear is the ***klaş*** (*reşik*), a hand-stitched shoe with a woven cotton upper and a sole traditionally built from compressed cloth or plaited rags, historically made across the Hakkari-Behdinan borderland. Women's dress centres on the ***kiras*** or ***fistan***, a long-sleeved full-length dress often layered — an under-*kiras* of light printed cotton and an over-*kiras* of velvet, silk, or bright synthetic — worn with a short embroidered waistcoat (*êlek*, *sadrî*), wide underdrawers (*derpê*) gathered at the ankle, and a broad sash. The head is covered with a *lêçek* or *pûşî* scarf, in older bridal dress topped by a domed *taj* hung with coin pendants and silver chains falling across the forehead and temples. Yezidi men are traditionally marked by a white shirt closed to the throat without a collar opening, and Yezidi *pîr* and *şêx* clerics by a red-and-white or all-white turban of specified wrap.

### Architecture

The Kurdish highland village is built of local materials in a manner tightly adapted to the climate. On the volcanic plateaux of Muş, Ağrı and Kars the characteristic form is the semi-subterranean flat-roofed house of dry-laid basalt with heavy earth-covered roofs supported on massive timber beams and a central smoke-hole; livestock are housed under the same roof in adjoining bays. In the basalt country around Diyarbakır (*Amed*) the urban house is built of alternating courses of black basalt and pale limestone, with a colonnaded *eywan* (iwan) opening onto an interior courtyard containing a fountain and pomegranate trees; the black-basalt city walls of Diyarbakır and the Hevsel gardens along the Tigris are jointly inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List. In the Tur Abdin, shared with Syriac Christians, Kurdish villagers use fine ashlar limestone with carved lintels. In the high Hakkari and Şemdinli valleys stone-and-timber houses cluster in terraces up the mountainside, roofs of one house serving as the courtyard of the next. The pastoral *koçer* groups of the Serhat and the Nemrut-Süphan foothills historically used the black goat-hair tent (*reş mal*, "black house") pitched on wooden poles with side walls of *cit* reed screens, a form shared with neighbouring Arab and Turkmen pastoralists but decorated in Kurdish practice with woven *çuval* storage bags stacked at the rear as a textile wall.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Ceramics are a relatively minor Kurdish craft — glazed earthenware traditions in the region (Kütahya, Diyarbakır's own tile revival) are more strongly associated with Armenian and Ottoman-Turkish workshops — and Kurdish villages historically bought their glazed ware at the town bazaars while producing their own unglazed storage jars, water pitchers, and yoghurt-churn covers. Metalwork, by contrast, is a signature Kurdish contribution. **Cizre** on the Tigris was famous from the Artuqid period for chased and inlaid brass and bronze; the twelfth-century Cizre door-knockers now in İstanbul and Copenhagen are the best-known survivals of that tradition, and later Cizre and Silvan smiths worked engraved copper trays (*sênî*), long-spouted ewers (*misîn*), and coffee mortars. **Silvan** and **Bitlis** produced chased silver belts and dagger fittings. The Kurdish long knife (*şûr*, *xencer*) with a curved single-edged blade and a horn or bone hilt bound in silver wire was a standard male accoutrement into the twentieth century. Everyday wooden objects include the carved cradle (*dergûş*), the low round eating table (*sifra*) laid over a floor cloth, the tall wooden butter churn (*meşk*) or the alternative goat-skin churn slung from a tripod, and inlaid dowry chests (*sindoq*).

### Jewelry & body adornment

Kurdish jewellery is silver-dominant, with heavy pieces built up from cast, chased and granulated components, and set with turquoise, carnelian, coral, and — in the bridal ensemble — rows of Ottoman gold coins. Characteristic forms include the ***guşvare*** and coin-hung temple pendants; the ***bazin*** or *bazinek* wide hinged bracelet; the *tûq* torque; the domed and coin-fringed bridal crown (*taj*) worn under the *lêçek*; the *muska* triangular amulet case containing a written prayer or verse, worn on a chain across the chest; and heavy silver belts (*kember*) with repoussé clasps. Filigree (*telkarî*) work — fine drawn silver wire soldered into openwork panels — is the specialty of **Midyat** in Mardin province, shared between Kurdish, Syriac and Arab craftsmen and still a living workshop tradition. Coral bead necklaces are almost universal, and unmarried girls' single long braid, bound with silver-tipped cords and small coin pendants, contrasted traditionally with the multiple braids of a married woman gathered under the *lêçek*. Henna (*hena*) is applied to the bride's hands and feet at a dedicated *şeva hene* ("henna night") on the eve of the wedding, an occasion for women's line-song and lament.

## Music & performance

Kurdish music is built around the sung word, and its central figure is the ***dengbêj*** — literally "voice-sayer" — the solo unaccompanied singer who performs long narrative *kilam* and *stran*, epic (*destan*), lament (*şîn*, *lawj*), and love complaint from a repertoire memorised over decades. The *dengbêj* tradition, historically centred on Van, Erciş, Muş and the *dîwan* of tribal leaders, is maintained today in the Dengbêj House (*Mala Dengbêjan*) in Diyarbakır. Vocal genres also include the *lawik* (short love-song), the *heyranok* antiphonal love-dialogue sung by women, the wedding-line song *dîlok* / *govend*, the mourning *şîn*, and the shepherd's *bilûr* melody sung to the open pasture. The core instrument of pastoral Kurdistan is the long end-blown flute ***bilûr*** (or *şimşal*); other characteristic instruments are the long-necked lute ***tembûr*** (in Alevi and Yaresan practice the *tembûr* is a sacred instrument), the plucked *saz* / *bağlama* shared with Anatolian Turkish practice, the bowed *kemençe*, the double-reed *duduk* / *mey*, and outdoors the double-reed shawm ***zurna*** paired with the large double-headed drum ***dahol*** (*davul*), the standard wedding and *govend* ensemble across all Kurdish regions. Among Alevi Kurds of Dersim the *saz* accompanies the sacred *deyiş* and *nefes* hymns of the *cem* ritual, and the *semah* turning-dance. Yezidi religious music, performed by the *qewwal* caste at Lalish and in diaspora shrines, uses the frame drum (*def*) and the shawm (*şibab*) to accompany sacred hymns (*qewl*) in an archaic Kurmancî.

## Dance & theatre

The signature Kurdish social form is the line dance ***govend*** (*halay* in Turkish, *dîlan* as the occasion) — a linked-hand or linked-little-finger open circle led by the *sergovend* who holds a coloured handkerchief and signals figure changes with wrist flicks. Regional repertoires are extensive and named by step-count or by home district: *delîlo*, *çepikî*, *sepê*, *garzane*, *meyroke*, *tewrê*, *şêxanî*, *berîvanê*, and the fast three-step *govenda Botan*. The dance is performed at weddings, at *Newroz*, at circumcision feasts and at any village gathering, driven by the *zurna* and *dahol* pair outdoors and by voice and *erbane* frame drum indoors. Solo virtuoso forms are rare; the aesthetic is collective, with the line as a single body. Formal theatre in the Western sense is a modern urban development (the Kurdish-language stage in Diyarbakır dates in institutional form to the post-2000 easing of language restrictions), but older performative genres include the *dengbêj*'s narrative recital, the improvised sung dispute (*berhevdan*), and the shadow-play (*Karagöz*) which circulated in mixed Kurdish-Turkish-Armenian towns.

## Festivals & rituals

The great annual festival is ***Newroz*** (*Nawrûz*), the Iranian New Year at the spring equinox on 21 March, celebrated by the lighting of bonfires on hilltops the evening before, by leaping the flames, by the wearing of national dress, and by mass outdoor gatherings that in the Turkish Kurdish context have taken on strong political weight since the 1990s. *Newroz* is inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity as a shared tradition of multiple Iranian and Turkic peoples. The Islamic calendar contributes *Remezan* (Ramadan) and the two *Eids* (*Cejna Remezanê*, *Cejna Qurbanê*), observed in the standard Sunni manner by the majority. Alevi Kurds observe the twelve-day *Muharrem* fast commemorating Kerbela, breaking it with the *aşure* pudding, and gather for the ritual *cem* under the leadership of a *dede*. Yezidi Kurds observe the autumn *Cejna Cemaiyye* pilgrimage to Lalish (across the border in Iraq) and the *Çarşema Sor* "Red Wednesday" of April marking the descent of *Tawûsî Melek* to earth. Life-cycle rituals — the naming on the seventh day, the *sunet* (circumcision) feast, the multi-day wedding with its *şeva hene* eve, the *hebrandin* forty-day post-partum seclusion of the mother, and the *taziye* mourning gathering with formal *şîn* lament sung by women — remain widely observed.

## Foodways

Kurdish cuisine is a highland-pastoral cuisine of wheat, dairy, lamb, and wild greens, sharing much of its vocabulary with neighbouring Turkish, Armenian, Assyrian and Arab regional cooking but with distinctive dishes and techniques. Bulgur (*savar*) is the staple grain, prepared as pilaf, as the outer shell of stuffed *kutilk* / *kibbeh* dumplings (*içli köfte* in Turkish), and pounded raw with lamb and pepper paste as *çi kutilk* (raw *kibbeh*) in the Botan region. Flatbread is baked daily on a domed griddle (*sêl*) as ***nan-i sêl*** or in a clay oven (*tenûr*) as ***nan-i tenûrê***. Dairy is central: fresh cheese (*penîr*), the salt-cured *herîs* / *keşk* dried whey balls used to sour winter stews, thick strained yoghurt, and the herbed cheese ***tulum*** cured in a goatskin, most famously as the *Van otlu peyniri* studded with wild alliums and *sirmo* garlic-mustard. Signature dishes include ***serê pez*** (slow-cooked sheep's head), the wheat-and-mutton porridge *herîse* / *keşkek*, the stuffed lamb intestine *bumbar*, dolma of grape and chard leaves, and the Diyarbakır liver kebab (*ciğer*) eaten at breakfast. Sweets include *pelûze* wheat-starch pudding, mulberry (*tûyê*) leather dried on rooftops, and the walnut-and-grape-molasses sausage *bastêq*. Tea (*çay*) is the ubiquitous drink, served in tulip glasses; *dew* / *ayran* salted yoghurt drink accompanies meals; among older men *mirra* — a very bitter unfiltered coffee of the Botan region — is a formal welcome drink.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Kurdish oral tradition is exceptionally rich and forms the deep matrix from which the written literature emerged. The core epic is ***Mem û Zîn***, the tragic love-story of Mem of the Alan and Zîn of the Botan, rendered into a classical *masnawî* by **Ehmedê Xanî** (1651–1707) at Bayezid but circulating for centuries before in *dengbêj* recitation. Other great narrative cycles include ***Siyabend û Xecê*** (a Romeo-and-Juliet on Mount Süphan), ***Dewrêşê Evdî*** (the Yezidi warrior of the Sinjar-Serhat borderland), ***Kela Dimdim*** (the seventeenth-century siege), and the *Rustemê Zal* material shared with the wider Iranian *Shahnameh* tradition. Lyric genres include the *heyranok* love-dialogue, the *lawik*, and the improvised *berhevok* dispute. Classical written Kurdish poetry begins with **Elî Herîrî** (11th c.), matures with **Melayê Cizîrî** (1570–1640) whose *Dîwan* is a masterpiece of Kurmancî mystical verse in the *ghazal* form, and continues with **Feqiyê Teyran** (c.1590–c.1660), the composer of narrative *stran* including *Şêxê Sen'an* and *Qewlê Hespê Reş*. Alevi and Yezidi religious poetry (*deyîş*, *qewl*) form parallel oral canons. Proverbs (*gotinên pêşiyan*), riddles (*mamik*), and the tightly rhymed lullabies (*lorî*) round out the domestic repertoire.

## Language & religion

Turkish Kurds speak two closely related but mutually not-fully-intelligible Iranian languages: **Kurmancî** (Northern Kurdish), the language of the great majority, and **Zazakî** (also *Dimilkî* / *Kirmanckî*), spoken in Tunceli, parts of Bingöl, Elazığ, Diyarbakır, Erzincan and Sivas and classified by most Iranists as a distinct northwestern Iranian language rather than a dialect of Kurdish proper. Both are written in the Latin-based **Bedirxan alphabet** developed in the 1930s; use of Kurdish in public life in Turkey was legally restricted from 1924 until reforms beginning in 1991 and expanding through the 2000s, and a state Kurdish-language television channel (TRT Kurdî) has broadcast since 2009. Religiously the community is majority **Sunni Muslim of the Shafi'i legal school** (contrasting with the surrounding Hanafi Turkish majority), with the Naqshbandi and Qadiri Sufi orders historically dominant and their tekkes serving as centres of both piety and Kurdish-language learning. Substantial minorities are ***Alevi***, concentrated in Dersim (Tunceli), Bingöl, Muş, Kars and Sivas, largely Zazakî- or Kurmancî-speaking, with their own *cem* ritual, *dede* clerical lineages, and *semah* sacred dance. The ***Êzidî*** (Yezidi) Kurds, historically present in the Tur Abdin, around Viranşehir and in villages of Batman and Mardin, follow a syncretic religion centred on *Tawûsî Melek* and the shrine complex at Lalish; most Turkish Yezidis emigrated to Germany in the late twentieth century and only a small remnant remains. Small numbers of Kurdish-speaking Christians historically existed in the mixed Syriac-Kurdish villages of the Tur Abdin.
