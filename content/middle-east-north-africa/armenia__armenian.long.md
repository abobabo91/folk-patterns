## Overview

Armenians are a people of the Armenian Highlands whose language has Eastern and Western literary forms and a distinct alphabet. The [UNESCO account of Armenian letter art](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/armenian-letter-art-and-its-cultural-expressions-01513) dates the alphabet's creation to 405 CE and documents its continuing use in decorated writing and other arts. Communities also live well beyond the present Republic of Armenia. The traditions below have regional and historical variation; they do not describe every Armenian household.

This gallery combines nineteen objects catalogued by the Victoria and Albert Museum as made in Armenia, two British Museum textiles, and seven Europeana documentary photographs. The V&A pieces range from a church mitre and staff to aprons, embroidery, silver adornments and a jug. They show what particular collections acquired, especially nineteenth-century textiles, rather than a complete history of Armenian culture. ?Made in Armenia? records a place of production; where a maker is unnamed, it cannot independently establish that person's ethnicity. The documentary photographs likewise represent specific people and occasions.

## Material culture

### Embroidery and dress

The V&A's [nineteenth-century embroidered wool apron](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O361404) has bands of geometric and floral ornament worked in several stitches and thread colours. A [second apron](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O361405) uses embroidery, cloth and glass beads. The selected [belt](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O360697) and a [pair of knitted wool socks from the 1960s](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O367802) show further forms of dress across different periods. These are individual garments; they should not be assembled into a single standard costume.

Household embroidery is especially well represented. The [bed cover](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O318206), [embroidered panel](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O360698) and several [nineteenth-century covers](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O360692) display varied materials, formats and arrangements. A [border dated to the eighteenth century](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O287052) extends the time range. Museum descriptions identify the materials and techniques of particular pieces; they do not by themselves tell us who worked on them or how widely a design circulated. The two British Museum scarves and the photographs add other views, but the current selection contains no firmly attributed Armenian carpet. Carpet weaving should therefore not be inferred from these images alone.

### Silverwork, church objects and ceramics

Three V&A adornments dated 1850?1870 include a [silver necklace](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O79189), a [silver-gilt head ornament](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O79188) and a [necklace or headband](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O80024) mounted on red cloth. The museum qualifies the last object's place of origin as probable. They show different ways of combining metalwork with fabric; the records do not identify an everyday wearer or a universal meaning for the forms.

The [silver-gilt mitre](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O105458), catalogued with the Armenian Church, and a [silver and silver-gilt staff](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O108696) document religious metalwork in this collection. The [nineteenth-century earthenware jug](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O72362) carries an Armenian inscription and painted and incised decoration. Its inscription is more specific evidence of an Armenian connection than the place field alone. These objects show church and household contexts that differ from the clothing and embroidery.

Stone carving is a living practice outside the object gallery. [UNESCO's description of khachkars](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/armenian-cross-stones-art-symbolism-and-craftsmanship-of-khachkars-00434) identifies them as carved cross-stones used for worship and remembrance. It describes geometric and vegetal ornament, variation from one stone to another, and skills transmitted within families and from masters to apprentices. No khachkar is among the selected museum objects.

## Music, dance and oral performance

[UNESCO describes the duduk](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/duduk-and-its-music-00092) as an Armenian double-reed instrument, often made from apricot wood and commonly played by two musicians. The museum gallery does not contain a duduk or a recording of its music. The [Smithsonian's *Songs and Dances of Armenia* catalogue](https://collections.si.edu/search/detail/edanmdm:siris_arc_315254) points to a sound recording, which offers a different kind of evidence from the objects.

[Kochari](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/kochari-traditional-group-dance-01295) is a group dance performed at holidays, family ceremonies and other social events, according to UNESCO's Armenian nomination. Its transmission includes family practice, dance groups and teaching. The [*Daredevils of Sassoun*](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/performance-of-the-armenian-epic-of-daredevils-of-sassoun-or-david-of-sassoun-00743) is a performed epic, narrated and partly sung in varying versions. These records describe living performance traditions, not scenes identifiable in the selected object photographs.

## Food, faith and shared occasions

UNESCO's [lavash entry](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/lavash-the-preparation-meaning-and-appearance-of-traditional-bread-as-an-expression-of-culture-in-armenia-00985) describes thin bread baked against the wall of a clay oven, with preparation that brings several people together and a role in wedding customs. It is one documented food tradition, not a complete Armenian cuisine.

The [pilgrimage to St. Thaddeus Apostle Monastery](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/pilgrimage-to-the-st-thaddeus-apostle-monastery-01571) in northwestern Iran is a shared practice of Armenian communities in Iran and Armenia and followers of the Armenian Apostolic Church. UNESCO describes liturgy, processions, prayer, food and time together over three days. The selected mitre and staff speak to church material culture, but their catalogue records do not connect them to this particular pilgrimage.

## Living craft and transmission

The [Gyumri blacksmithing entry](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/tradition-of-blacksmithing-in-gyumri-01967) describes an urban craft maintained through families, museums and training institutions. It names ironwork in buildings and daily life, including gates, window grilles and household fittings. This tradition is not represented by an iron object in the present gallery. Armenian letter art, khachkar carving, music, dance and bread-making likewise extend beyond what a collection of portable museum objects can show.
