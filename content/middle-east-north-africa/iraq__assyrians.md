## Overview

Assyrians are a distinct ethnic group native to Mesopotamia, with roots in ancient Assyria. Their homeland lies in northern Mesopotamia, including areas along the Zab rivers and parts of modern Turkey, Iran, Iraq, and Syria. Large-scale displacement over the past two centuries has produced communities across North America, Europe, Australia, the Middle East, and the Caucasus. Assyrians speak varieties of Neo-Aramaic, a branch of the Semitic language family, and largely adhere to Syriac Christianity. They have maintained a continuous cultural and linguistic presence spanning over three millennia. Their historical importance includes the development of Assyrian civilization, the Church of the East, the East Syriac Rite, and the transmission of Christianity across Asia.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Assyrian architecture is mentioned only through historical urban sites. Assur existed by perhaps the 25th century BC and later became the heart of the Assyrian Empire. Nineveh became the capital under Sennacherib and was extensively expanded, covering about 750 hectares. Nimrud, the ancient Kalhu, became a royal seat and military capital under Ashurnasirpal II, whose building programs expanded its Acropolis and outer town.

## Festivals & rituals

 They record religious and ecclesiastical developments, including the early Christianisation of Assyrians between the first and third centuries in Roman Syria and Roman Assyria. Assyrians were traditionally held to have been evangelised by St. Thomas the Apostle and by St. Addai and St. Mari. In 410, the Council of Seleucia-Ctesiphon organised Christians in the Sasanian Empire into what became known as the Church of the East; a council held in 424 declared its Catholicos independent of Western ecclesiastical authorities.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources mention Assyrian literary and scholarly activity. Akkadian served as a language of administration, literature, and diplomacy in the Ancient Near East, while Sumerian continued as a liturgical and classical language for religious, artistic, and scholarly purposes. Assyrians later contributed to Islamic civilizations by translating works of Greek philosophers into Syriac and then Arabic. Bar Hebraeus is identified as an Assyrian scholar and hierarch, and the Legend of Mar Qardagh is mentioned as a Syriac source work portraying an Assyrian royal descent and a conversion to Christianity.

## Language & religion

Assyrians speak varieties of Neo-Aramaic, a branch of the Semitic language family. Modern Assyrian derives from ancient Aramaic and includes the principal dialects Suret and Turoyo. Around 700 BC, Aramaic gradually replaced Akkadian in the Near East, although bilingualism was common before the fall of the Neo-Assyrian Empire. The Aramaic spoken by Assyrians contains Assyrian-Akkadian loanwords. Aramaic script gradually replaced cuneiform for administrative and diplomatic purposes, while cuneiform continued for royal and religious texts.

Assyrians largely adhere to Syriac Christianity. The principal ecclesiastical traditions include the Chaldean Catholic Church, Assyrian Church of the East, Ancient Church of the East, Syriac Catholic Church, and Syriac Orthodox Church. These belong to East Syriac and West Syriac rites and differ in liturgy, dialect, theology, and historical development.
