## Overview

The Achomi, also known as Khodmooni, Laris, or Larestanis, are a Persian and Iranic group primarily associated with southern Iran. Their historical region, Irahistan or Laristan, includes areas of southern Fars Province and northern Hormozgan Province, including Lar, Gerash, Evaz, Khonj, Bastak, and Lamerd. Communities also live in Shiraz, India, and Arab countries in the Persian Gulf, including the UAE, Kuwait, Bahrain, and Qatar. The Achomi language is a Southwestern Iranian language derived from Middle Persian or Parsig and has reported dialects including Lari, Evazi, Gerashi, Khonji, Bastaki, and Ashkanani. UNESCO is described as counting approximately 1,180,000 speakers and classifying Achomi as “definitely endangered.” The sources identify migration, multilingualism, religious diversity, and local dialects as central features of Achomi life.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do mention discriminatory badges and headgear that Persian Jews were required to wear under a royal edict during the reign of Shāh Abbās I, but this concerns Jewish communities in Persia rather than an Achomi clothing tradition.

### Architecture

They mention Lar, Gerash Castle, a hermitage in Khonj, a seminary in Gerash, dokhmeh, and the tomb of Muhammad Jafar Khan.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention ships, shipbuilding, spices, pearls, and commercial activity around Hormuz.

### Jewelry & body adornment

One historical account states that henna from Muhammad Jafar Khan’s second marriage ceremony was still visible on his hand when his body was exhumed.

## Music & performance

The Achomi people’s dance is said to indicate at least a cultural connection to Bakhtiaris, but no dance music, instrumental ensemble, or named performance tradition is described. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription concerns this ethnic group.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used mention that Achomi dance indicates at least a cultural connection to Bakhtiaris.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do mention religious conversion, Jewish communal history, Islamic practice, and the burial of Muhammad Jafar Khan in Gerash and later Karbala, but these accounts do not constitute a comprehensive ritual description.

## Foodways

Ibn Battuta’s account of entering Khonj in 733 AH describes a meal at a religious hermitage, where Mohammad, who was fasting, broke his fast with the visitors; the group is described as Shafi’i in belief. The sources also mention spices and pearls in the trade of Hormuz.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources mention local traditions, historical travelogues, religious writings, poetry, and literary collections. According to the account presented, some Abbasid Khodmoonis claim an ancestral link to Ibn Abbas. Ibn Battuta recorded his visit to Khonj in 733 AH and described its religious hermitage and worshippers. Marco Polo described Hormuz, the Minab River, trade with Indian merchants, shipbuilding, and seasonal winds known as Teshbada. In the period of Dehbashi rule, Haj Asadullah and Shaykh ‘Ali Rashti produced religious and mystical writings, while Rostam Khan Gerashi compiled a poetic collection titled Baghestan; Mohammad Jafar Khan later contributed his own poetry collection.

## Language & religion

Achomi is described as a Southwestern Iranian language derived from Middle Persian, Parsig, or Pahlavi, and associated with the Sassanid Persian language. It is spoken in southern Fars and western Hormozgan and by Ajam citizens in the UAE, Bahrain, Kuwait, and neighbouring countries. Reported dialects include Lari, Evazi, Gerashi, Khonji, Bastaki, and Ashkanani. Native speakers refer to the language as *ačomī*; it is also called Khodmooni and Lari. The language has no official-language status in Iran and is described as endangered, with dialectal differences gradually disappearing. Achomi people are predominantly Sunni Muslims, with a Shia minority; Lar is described as 60% Shia Muslims and 40% Sunnis, while Gerash is predominantly Shia. The sources also mention earlier Zoroastrian religion, Jewish communities, and secular and non-religious minorities.
