## Overview

The Samaritans, often preferring the name Israelite Samaritans, are an ethnoreligious group indigenous to Samaria, the historical region of ancient Israel and Judah. Their contemporary communities are concentrated mainly in Kiryat Luza on Mount Gerizim near Nablus, identified with ancient Shechem, and in Neve Pinchas in Holon near Tel Aviv. As of 2024, the community numbered around 900 people, including some 460 in Holon and some 380 in Kiryat Luza. Those in Kiryat Luza speak South Levantine Arabic, while those in Holon primarily speak Modern Hebrew; Samaritan Hebrew and Samaritan Aramaic are used liturgically. Samaritan culture is especially distinctive through its Mount Gerizim-centered religion, hereditary priesthood, scriptural traditions, festivals, and Passover sacrifice.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do record that Samaritan elders usually wear a red turban and cassock, changing into more striking clothing on Saturdays and other sacred days. No further details of these garments are provided.

### Architecture

Mount Gerizim is the central sacred landscape of Samaritan culture. Samaritan tradition identifies it as the original Holy Place of the Israelites and as the true sacred mountain where God resides. The Samaritan temple on Mount Gerizim existed from the 5th century BCE, while its sacred precinct underwent large-scale construction beginning around the 4th century BCE. The Hasmonean king John Hyrcanus destroyed the temple around 120 BCE.

Samaritan synagogues are oriented toward Gerizim and are usually located on the outskirts of settlements. Their decoration is described as exclusively mosaic, including menorahs, shofars, trumpets, heads of doves, sheep, and goats.

## Music & performance

They do describe liturgical performance within Samaritan worship. On the Sabbath, observed weekly from Friday to Saturday beginning and ending at sundown, the community conducts seven prayer services: two on the Sabbath eve, two in the morning, two in the afternoon, and one at the concluding eve. The services include reading the weekly Torah portion according to the Samaritan yearly Torah cycle.

During holidays, Samaritan liturgy is recited with an audible, fully preserved Samaritan Hebrew pronunciation of the Tetragrammaton. The sources also mention the Defter, a prayer book of psalms and hymns, and the Samaritan Haggadah. Shavuot is characterized by nearly day-long services of continuous prayer, especially over stones on Mount Gerizim traditionally attributed to Joshua.

## Festivals & rituals

Samaritan religious life follows a calendar that includes Pesach, Shavuot, and Sukkot. The dates of these festivals are determined differently from the Jewish method. Yom Teru’ah, the Biblical name for “Rosh Hashanah,” begins at the start of Tishrei but is not considered a New Year in Samaritan religion.

Passover is especially important and culminates in the sacrifice of up to 40 sheep. The community has preserved the practice of slaughtering and eating lambs on Passover eve. Shavuot includes nearly day-long prayer services and a distinctive observance during the week before the festival, celebrating the continued commitment maintained by Samaritanism since the time of Moses. Sukkot is marked by building the sukkah inside houses rather than outdoors. Its roof is decorated with citrus fruits and branches of palm, myrtle, and willow trees, following the Samaritan interpretation of the four species designated in the Torah.

The Sabbath is observed for 24 hours. Families gather for worship, meals, rest, sleep, and visits with other community members. Electricity, except for minimal lighting, is disconnected; work, cooking, and driving are not permitted.

## Foodways

The clearest documented Samaritan food practice is the Passover sacrifice, when up to 40 sheep may be slaughtered and eaten. The sources also state that lambs are slaughtered and eaten on Passover eve. This practice is connected to the community’s high priesthood and its observance of biblical law.

Sabbath observance includes shared family meals. During the Sabbath, cooking is not permitted.

## Oral tradition & literature

Samaritan historical and religious traditions are preserved in chronicles and other writings. The Kitab al-Ta’rikh was compiled by Abu’l-Fath in 1355 and presents a Samaritan account of the community’s origins. Its central narrative describes a civil conflict involving Eli, son of Yafni, and the priestly line of Phinehas, followed by Eli’s departure to Shiloh and the division of Israel into factions. The Samaritan New Chronicle, or Adler, is believed to have been composed in the 18th century using earlier chronicles.

The Samaritan Chronicle, the Chronicle of Joshua, the Chronicle Adler, and the Kitab al-Tarikh of Abu ’l-Fath are listed among Samaritan historical writings. Other textual traditions include Al-Asatir, Memar Markah, Pinkhas on the Taheb, and Molad Maseh. The sources also name the Abisha Scroll, a manuscript of the Pentateuch tradition attributed to Abishua, grandson of Aaron. No Samaritan epic, folktale cycle, proverb tradition, riddle tradition, or storytelling setting is described.

## Language & religion

Samaritanism is an Abrahamic, monotheistic ethnic religion. Samaritans recognize one God called YHWH and regard the Torah as the only true holy book given by God to Moses. Moses is considered the final prophet, while authority after him belongs to the successive Samaritan priesthood from the line of Aaron. The principal sanctuary is Mount Gerizim, not Jerusalem or the Temple Mount.

The Samaritan community uses South Levantine Arabic in Kiryat Luza and Modern Hebrew primarily in Holon. Samaritan Hebrew and Samaritan Aramaic are used for liturgical purposes and are written in the Samaritan script. In their own language, Samaritans use names including “Israel,” “B’nai Israel,” and “Shamerim”; in Arabic they use al-Sāmiriyyūn. Samaritan Hebrew, Samaritan Aramaic, and the Samaritan script are the principal linguistic markers documented by the sources.

The community preserves a hereditary high priesthood. According to Samaritan tradition, the office has continued without interruption for the last 3600 years, beginning with Aaron. Since 2013, the 133rd Samaritan High Priest has been Aabed-El ben Asher ben Matzliach. The High Priest is chosen from the priestly caste, which is understood to descend from Aaron.
