## Overview

Moroccan Arabs are the Arabic-speaking majority of Morocco, concentrated in the Atlantic plains and the old imperial cities — Fez, Meknes, Rabat, Salé, Marrakesh, Tetouan, Chefchaouen — and along the Sebou, Bouregreg and Oum er-Rbia river basins, with a distinct rural population of the Gharb, Chaouia, Doukkala and Tadla plains. They number roughly 20–23 million of Morocco's population, speaking *Darija* (Moroccan Arabic), a Maghrebi dialect of the Central Semitic branch of Afroasiatic heavily layered with Amazigh substrate vocabulary. Their arrival came in two waves: the 7th–8th-century conquests that carried Arabic and Islam into the Maghreb, and the 11th–13th-century Banu Hilal and Banu Maqil bedouin migrations that Arabized the plains. In folk-culture terms they matter because the imperial cities became the Maghreb's densest concentration of urban craft guilds — embroidery, *zellige* mosaic, brocade weaving, leatherwork, brass — refined by Andalusi refugees from Córdoba, Seville and Granada after 1492 and transmitted through a *maallem* (master) apprenticeship system still functioning today.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Fez embroidery** (*terz el-Fassi*, also *terz el-ghelb*) — Monochrome silk counted-thread work, almost always dark blue or garnet red on white linen or cotton, worked so that face and reverse are identical. Its strict reversibility and geometric symmetry distinguish it from every other North African embroidery; it covers *ttiyafer* (bed valances), cushion covers and the long mirror-cloths of the bridal chamber.

**Tetouan and Chefchaouen embroidery** — *Terz Tetouani* uses polychrome silk in filling stitch on velvet or wool, with large-scale vases, carnations and pomegranates read directly from Ottoman-Andalusi pattern books; Chefchaouen work is looser, in magenta and green on coarse cotton. Both are worked from the front only, unlike Fez.

**Rabat carpet** (*zarbiya Rbatiya*) — Urban knotted-pile carpet with a central medallion, cornerpieces and multiple narrow border bands, in madder red grounds with ivory and indigo. The medallion layout is Anatolian in ancestry, brought via Ottoman trade, and separates it sharply from the loose-field Amazigh *hanbel* of the Middle Atlas.

**Handira** (*tahandirt*) — Wedding cape of hand-spun wool, densely hung with flattened sequins and long cotton fringe, woven in the Middle Atlas; it enters Arab urban repertoires through trade and intermarriage, worn shining side out during the bride's procession and reversed to fleece-side for warmth afterwards.

**Meknes and Fez brocade** (*mensuj*, *briocade*) — Silk and metal-thread lampas woven on the drawloom for caftan bodies, sash ends and cushion faces; the *sqalli* (gold-thread) grounds and small repeating *harir* lozenges are guild-controlled patterns.

**Haik** (*ḥaik*) — A single uncut rectangle of fine white or cream wool, some five to six metres long, wrapped and pinned as the outdoor wrap of urban women. Its plainness is the point: the pattern is in the drape and in the *fibula* pins that hold it.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Khamsa* (open hand, against the evil eye), *ain* (eye), *shabka* (net or interlace), *tasfift* (banding, the stacked border logic of carpets), *rumman* (pomegranate, fertility), *qarnfoul* (carnation, Ottoman-derived), *zellij* eight-point star (*khatem Sulayman*, Solomon's seal), *sahm* (arrow or dart infill), *taj* (crown, in brocade selvedges), *hilal* (crescent).

### Clothing & dress

Men wear the *djellaba*, a hooded unisex outer robe of wool or cotton with a pointed *qob* hood, over a *qamis* shirt and wide *serwal* trousers; the *selham* or *burnous* is the sleeveless woollen cloak, white for ceremony. The *jabador* two-piece is Fez formal wear. Headgear ranges from the stiff crimson *tarbouch* (fez) with black tassel to the coiled *rezza* turban of rural notables and the plaited-straw hats of the Rif margins. Footwear is the backless *babouche* (*belgha*), pointed and typically saffron-yellow for men, embroidered and sequinned for women, made from Fez's pit-tanned goat and sheepskin.

Women's ceremonial dress centres on the *caftan*, a full-length brocade or velvet robe closed with hand-knotted *sfifa* braid and *aakad* silk buttons, worn under the sheer *dfina* overgown to form the *takchita*. A stiff *mdamma* belt of gold-embroidered velvet or worked silver cinches the waist. Outdoors, the *haik* wrapped over the head and body — sometimes with the *litham* face veil — was standard in Fez, Rabat and Tetouan into the mid-twentieth century. Brides in Fez are dressed in successive costumes across the wedding night, each with its own jewellery and headdress.

### Architecture

The Arab urban unit is the *dar* or, at grand scale, the *riad*: an inward-facing courtyard house, blank-walled to the street, entered through a bent-axis corridor that blocks sight of the interior. Two or three storeys of galleried rooms surround a paved court with a central fountain and, in the *riad* proper, a sunken quadripartite garden. Walls carry *zellige* dadoes of hand-cut glazed terracotta, above which run carved *gebs* (stucco) bands of interlace and Kufic inscription, and then a *sedari* ceiling of painted and coffered cedar. Floors and basins are finished in *tadelakt*, a lime plaster polished with river stone and sealed with black olive soap to make it waterproof — the standard hammam surface. Windows onto the street are screened by turned-wood *moucharabieh* lattices. The *medina* aggregates these houses along dead-end *derb* alleys around a *masjid*, a *madrasa*, a *fondouk* caravanserai, a communal oven and a *hammam*. Rural plains architecture is different in kind: rammed-earth *pisé* walls, flat roofs of reed and earth over palm beams, and *tighremt*-style corner-towered compounds toward the pre-Sahara.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**Fez pottery** is the blue-and-white benchmark — cobalt on tin-white glaze, filled with *shabka* net interlace, on *jobbana* lidded jars, footed *tass* basins and large *ghorraf* water jars. **Safi** ware, developed around Colline des Potiers, works in polychrome with strong turquoise, manganese and an unmistakable metallic-lustre *reflet* glaze. **Salé** produces heavier utilitarian green-glazed and slip-painted vessels. Cooking and serving forms are the conical *tagine*, the *gsaa* wide kneading basin, the *keskes* couscoussier, and unglazed porous *barrada* water flasks. Metalwork includes engraved and pierced brass trays and *sniya* tea tables, tinned-copper *bradh* teapots, pierced brass and iron lanterns (*fanous*), and cedar *sanduq* chests with iron strapwork. The *koumia*, a curved dagger with a silver-mounted, wire-inlaid sheath and squared pommel, is the emblematic edged object.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Urban Arab goldsmithing in Fez and Essaouira — long a Jewish craft monopoly — produced filigree and granulated gold: the *taj* diadem, wide *khelkhal* ankle bracelets, plaque-and-chain *lebba* necklaces set with emerald-green enamel and seed pearls, and hoop *khors* earrings. Rural and Amazigh-inflected silverwork favours enamelled *fibulae* (*tizerzai*), niello and cloisonné on triangular *khamsa* pendants, coral and amber beads, and coin-hung *tasbih* strands. Henna (*ḥenna*) is applied in geometric interlace on hands and feet at the bridal *laylat al-henna*, by a professional *neqqasha*; the plainer hand-drawn Fez style differs from the finely dotted southern work. *Kohl* lines the eyes, *aker fassi* (poppy-and-cochineal cake) reddens lips and cheeks, and *swak* walnut bark stains the gums. Facial tattooing was Amazigh rather than Arab and has effectively ended.

## Music & performance

The prestige tradition is *al-Āla*, Moroccan Andalusi classical music, organised into eleven surviving *nubat* (suite-forms), each a sequence of five rhythmic *mizan* movements from slow *basit* to fast *quddam*, performed by an ensemble of *oud*, *rebab* (bowed lute held upright), *kamanja* (violin on the knee), *ta'rija* and *darbouka* drums, and a chorus of singers. Fez, Tetouan and Rabat each maintain their own *nuba* repertoire and lineage of masters. Sung urban strophic poetry takes two forms: *malhun*, in colloquial Darija *qasida* form, performed by a *shikh* with *taarija*, *swisen* lute and *hajhouj*; and *gharnati*, the Granadan-descended tradition strongest in Oujda and Rabat.

Sufi devotional music is equally central: *samaa* and *madih* hymnody sung in *zawaya*, the *Aissawa* and *Hmadcha* brotherhoods with their loud *ghaita* shawms, *nfar* long trumpets and double-headed *tbel* drums, and the *Gnawa* *lila* ceremony, where the *maalem* plays the three-string bass *hajhouj* (*guembri*) with iron *qraqeb* castanets to call the *mluk* spirits through a named colour sequence. Weddings bring *naffara* processional bands; Ramadan nights bring *nfar* callers. *Chaabi*, the amplified popular idiom built on *malhun* metres with *bendir* frame drum, violin and *banjo*, is the wedding-hall default.

## Dance & theatre

Moroccan Arab performance is participatory more than staged. *Shikhat* dancers — professional women singers-dancers of the Atlantic plains and Marrakesh — perform *aita*, a vocally strident plains repertoire, with hip-driven, shoulder-shaking movement at weddings and *moussem* tents. Urban women dance *chaabi* in mixed line and solo forms with scarf and belt play. The *Aissawa* and *Hmadcha* brotherhoods perform circular trance processions with head-swinging and controlled collapse; the *Gnawa* *lila* includes named possession dances tied to each *mluk* colour, including the bowl-and-fire figures of the *sabre* and *chouwafa* sequences. In the *halqa*, the circle that forms in Marrakesh's Jemaa el-Fnaa and in every weekly *souq*, storytellers, snake-charmers with *ghaita*, *Oulad Sidi Ahmed ou Moussa* acrobats from the Souss, monkey handlers and mock-legal comic sketches share the ground. There is no indigenous mask-drama or puppet tradition; *halqa* improvisation is the theatre, and it is the direct ancestor of Tayeb Saddiki's twentieth-century Moroccan stage.

## Festivals & rituals

The Islamic lunar calendar frames the year: Ramadan, broken nightly with *harira* and dates; *Eid al-Fitr*; *Eid al-Adha* (*Eid el-Kebir*), with household sheep sacrifice and the drying of *khlii* and *qaddid* meat; *Ashura*, distinctively Moroccan in its children's gifts, *taarija* drumming and *Baba Aichour* bonfires; and *Mawlid* (*Eid al-Mawlid*), when candle processions run in Salé's *Dour al-Chamaa* parade of wax lanterns. Layered onto this is the *moussem*, the saint's-day pilgrimage fair: Moulay Idriss Zerhoun for the founder of Fez, Moulay Abdallah Amghar near El Jadida with its mass horsemanship, Moulay Brahim in the Atlas foothills. *Tbourida*, the synchronised cavalry charge in which a row of riders fires flintlock *moukahla* muskets simultaneously, is the moussem's centrepiece. The solar-agrarian year survives in the Julian-reckoned *Laylat al-Qadr* observances and in the Amazigh New Year *Hagouza/Ennayer* kept in the plains.

Life-cycle rites: the seventh-day *sboua* naming feast with sacrifice and a first shaving of the head; boys' circumcision (*tahara*), dressed in miniature caftan and paraded; the multi-night wedding, with the *hammam* purification, *laylat al-henna*, the bride's arrival on the *amariya* palanquin, and the display of successive costumes; and burial before sunset with *Yasin* recitation and forty days of condolence visiting.

## Foodways

Wheat is the base — semolina for *couscous*, steamed thrice over broth in the *keskes* and eaten at Friday lunch with seven vegetables; flour for *khobz* round hearth loaves baked at the communal *ferran*, flaky *msemen* and *rghaif*, and honeycomb *baghrir*. The slow-cooked *tagine* covers a family of dishes: lamb with prunes and almonds, chicken with preserved lemon (*hamd mraqad*) and violet olives, *kefta* with egg, *mrouzia* with raisins and *ras el-hanout*. Fez's celebration dishes are *pastilla* — layered *warqa* pastry with pigeon or chicken, almonds, cinnamon and sugar — and *tanjia* is Marrakesh's, sealed in a clay urn and cooked in the hammam's ash furnace. *Harira* lentil-and-tomato soup and *bissara* fava purée are staples of Ramadan and winter mornings. Preserves and condiments define the pantry: *smen* fermented butter, *khlii*, *charmoula* marinade, and *harissa* at the Algerian margin. Tea is *atay bi na'na'*, gunpowder green tea with spearmint and pressed sugar cones, poured from height from a *bradh* into small glasses, served in three rounds and offered to every guest; *qahwa* is spiced with cardamom or black pepper. Sweets are almond-and-honey: *kaab el-ghzal* (gazelle horns), *chebakia* fried in honey and sesame for Ramadan, *sellou* nut-and-flour paste for new mothers.

## Oral tradition & literature

The core poetic corpus is *malhun*, colloquial Arabic *qasida* composed from the fourteenth century onward by named artisan-poets — Abdelaziz al-Maghraoui, Sidi Qaddour al-Alami, Thami Mdaghri — in recognised sub-genres (*harraz*, the guarded-lover narrative; *saqi*, the cup-bearer; *chamaa*, the candle) and still sung rather than read. Alongside it runs *aita*, the sung plains poetry of the Chaouia, Doukkala and Abda, whose named modes (*aita hasbaouia*, *marsaouia*, *haouzia*) carry an oral history of tribal resistance to the *makhzen* and to French conquest. Narrative prose belongs to the *halqa* storyteller (*hlaiqi*), who works *Alf Layla wa-Layla* episodes, Antar and Bani Hilal cycles, *Juha* trickster tales and local saints' *karamat* miracle stories. Household genres include *hjayat* animal tales opened with a fixed formula and closed with a rhyme, *lughz* riddles, and dense proverb speech (*mtal*). Jemaa el-Fnaa's oral repertoire was among the first elements UNESCO recognised in this domain, and *malhun* documentation is now centred on the Académie du Royaume and the Fez and Casablanca conservatories.

## Language & religion

*Darija* is a Maghrebi Arabic dialect — Afroasiatic, Central Semitic — marked by heavy vowel reduction, a *ka-/ta-* imperfect prefix, and a large Amazigh, Andalusi Romance, Spanish and French lexical layer; northern (Jebli, Tetouani), Atlantic-urban (Fassi, Rbati) and Hilalian plains varieties are mutually distinguishable. Classical Arabic in Maghrebi script is the written and liturgical register, and Moroccan manuscript culture — the *Maghribi mabsut* hand, gold-and-lapis illumination, goatskin-bound Qur'ans of Fez — is a distinct tradition from the Mashriq's. Religiously, the population is overwhelmingly Sunni Muslim of the Maliki school, with Ash'ari theology and an institutionalised Sufism (*Tijaniyya*, *Qadiriyya-Boutchichiya*, *Nasiriyya*) that sustains *zawaya*, saint veneration and *baraka* transmission. Folk practice runs alongside: *jnun* belief, the *khamsa* and eye amulets, *chouwafa* diviners, and Gnawa spirit ritual of sub-Saharan origin. Morocco's Jewish communities of Fez, Meknes, Essaouira and Marrakesh, now small, supplied much of the goldsmithing and shared *hiloula* pilgrimage forms with Muslim *moussem*.
