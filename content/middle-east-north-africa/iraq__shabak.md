## Overview

The Shabaks are a group inhabiting the Nineveh Plains in Iraq, in villages east of Mosul, in a triangle bounded by the Tigris and the Greater Zab. Their population was estimated at around 10,000 in 1925, 15,000 in 35 villages in the 1960 Iraqi census, and between 100,000 and 500,000 at the beginning of the 21st century. Shabaki is a Northwestern Iranian language belonging to the Shabaki-Bajelani subgroup of the Gorani group. Shabak ethnic identity has been disputed: some sources and Kurdish authorities have regarded them as Kurds, while most Shabaks consider themselves a distinct ethnic group, neither Arab nor Kurdish.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe a traditional Shabak religion closely related to Anatolian Alevism. A Shabak invocation given by Al-Sarraf claimed Haji Bektash and the adepts of Ardabil, identified with the Safavids, as founders of the spiritual path. A basic tenet was the belief that Allah, Muhammad, and Ali constituted a trinity, with Ali as the dominant manifestation of the divine. The sacred book was known as Kitab al-Manaqib, also called the Buyruk, and pronounced by Shabaks as Burukh.

Shabaks and Yazidis historically celebrated holidays together and had good relations despite theological differences. The Sarliyya, a Yarsani community, also celebrated Laylat al-Kafsha like the Shabaks. In the 1890s, Omer Vehbi Pasha invited Yazidi and Shabak leaders to Mosul and forced them to publicly convert to the Hanafi school of Sunni Islam.

## Language & religion

Shabaki is a Northwestern Iranian language in the Iranian branch of the Indo-Iranian subdivision of the Indo-European languages. It is spoken by the Shabak people in the Mosul region of northern Iraq and has been influenced by Arabic, Turkish, and Persian. Glottolog classifies it within the Shabaki-Bajelani subgroup of the Gorani group. The language has two dialects, Chabak and Sarli, although some sources classify it as a subdialect of Gorani alongside Hewrami, Sarli, and Bajalani. The Shabaks called their dialect Shabaki or Macho.

Shabaki is endangered. Its speakers remain concentrated in localized clusters in the villages of Ali Rach, Khazna, Talara, and Yangijain in the Nineveh Governorate. The number of speakers was estimated between 10,000 and 20,000 in 1989 and approximately 250,000 subsequently. Many Shabaks also spoke Kurmanji, Sorani, and Arabic.

The traditional religion was closely related to Anatolian Alevism and was associated with Safavid-Qizilbash affiliation. By the 21st century, Shabaks were majority Shia; after the war on the Islamic State, they were estimated at around 70% Shias, with the rest Sunnis and a few Christians. Some Shabaks became Yarsani. Religious affiliation influenced whether Shabaks identified as Kurds or as a separate group.
