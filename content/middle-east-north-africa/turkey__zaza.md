## Overview

The Zazas are an Iranian people who speak Zaza, an Iranian language belonging to the Indo-European language family. They live primarily in the Eastern Anatolia and Southeastern Anatolia regions of Turkey, including provinces such as Bingöl, Elazığ, Erzincan, Erzurum, Malatya, Muş, Bitlis, Tunceli, Adıyaman, Diyarbakır and Şanlıurfa. Communities are also found in Northeastern Anatolia, Central Anatolia and Black Sea regions of Turkey, while a diaspora exists in Western Europe, particularly in Germany. Their population is estimated at approximately 2 to 3 million, although exact numbers are unknown. Zazas are predominantly Muslims, divided between Sunni Islam and Alevism. Their language, festivals, dances, music, foodways and oral traditions form a distinctive cultural field shaped by contact with neighboring communities.

## Music & performance

Traditional Zaza music revolves around longing, love and the human condition. The sources specifically state that Zaza music predominantly explores exile and homesickness, love, brotherhood, the duality of poverty and prosperity, and existential reflections. No named musical instruments, song genres, performance venues or musical forms are identified in the supplied sources.

The Zaza minstrel tradition goes back to the medieval period. Zaza-speaking bards composed works both in their mother tongue and in Turkish. The sources also describe the Zaza language as having a recent written literary history: the first written statements were compiled by Peter Lerch in 1850, while religious writings by Ehmedê Xasi and Osman Efendîyo Babij appeared in 1898. These literary and musical traditions are presented as part of a wider history in which Zaza cultural expression continued alongside Turkish and neighboring languages.

## Dance & theatre

Traditional Zaza dances include wedding dances such as **Zaza halay**, **Karaçor**, **Devzer**, **Çepki**, **Fadiki** and **Kılaçep**. The **Eagle Dance** is identified as common in Bingöl. The sources characterize the named dances as part of wedding-dance traditions.

The sources also do not distinguish between ceremonial and recreational performances beyond identifying the listed dances as wedding dances.

## Festivals & rituals

The Zazas share some celebrations and special days with neighboring peoples, including Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha, while also observing feasts and special days that distinguish them from neighboring communities. Named Zaza celebrations include **Gağand**, **Newe Marti**, **Kormışkan**, **Hewtemal**, **Qereçarseme/Karaçarşamba** and **Serra Newe**.

**Kormışkan** and **Hewtemal** are Zaza spring festivals celebrated in March. Kormışkan is celebrated by Sunni Zazas, while Hewtemal is its equivalent among Alevi Zazas. Hewtemal is divided into the **Pil**, **Qıc** and **Peyen** celebrations. During these festivals, people celebrate the arrival of spring by going into nature and having picnics.

**Gağand** and **Serra Newe** are Zaza New Year festivals celebrated in December/January throughout Zaza regions. Serra Newe is associated with Sunni Zazas. Gağand is generally celebrated among Alevi Zazas and some Sunni Zazas. During Gağand, children dressed in their finest clothes, accompanied by **Khalo Gağan**, described as the Old Man, visit neighbors’ doors, greet them and receive gifts.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Zaza minstrel tradition extends back to the medieval period, when Zaza-speaking bards composed works in both Zaza and Turkish. The sources connect Zaza cultural history with oral traditions.

The earliest surviving literary works in Zaza are two poems with identical titles, *Mawlūd*, dating from the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The first literary work in Zaza is identified as *Mewlîdu'n-Nebîyyî'l-Qureyşîyyî* by Ehmedê Xasi in 1899, followed by *Mawlûd* by Osman Efendîyo Babij in 1903. Both works were written in Arabic script.

Modern Zaza literature appeared in the journal *Tîrêj* in 1979. During the 1980s and 1990s, much Zaza literature was published in Germany, France and especially Sweden. After the ban on the Kurdish language was lifted in Turkey in 1991, newspapers and journals began publishing in Zaza again. In 1996, Zaza-speaking authors gathered in Stockholm and established a common alphabet and orthographic rules. The sources state that at least 332 books had been published in Zaza as of 2018.

## Language & religion

Zaza is an Iranic language or group of languages belonging to the Northwestern Iranian branch. It is spoken by the Zaza people in various regions of Turkey and is closely related to Tati, Talysh, Sangsari, Semnani, Mazandarani and Gilaki. Zaza is recognized as a macrolanguage, including Southern Zaza and Northern Zaza. The language has northern, southern and central varieties, with extensive sub-dialectal variation and no recognized standard.

The northern dialect is spoken by Alevi Zazas, the southern dialect by Hanafi Zazas and the central dialect by Shafi Zazas. The southern variety is spoken predominantly in Siverek, Çermik, Çüngüş, Gerger, Mutki, Aksaray, Ergani and Kulp. Central or Eastern Zaza is spoken predominantly in Palu, Bingöl, Hani, Lice, Silvan, Solhan and Muş. Northern Zaza is spoken predominantly in places including Erzincan, Kemah, Aşkale, Hozat, Hınıs, Kelkit, Nazimiye, Ovacık, Pülümür, Tekman, Tercan, Varto and Yayladere.

The first written statements in Zaza were compiled by Peter Lerch in 1850. Religious writings by Ehmedê Xasi and Osman Efendîyo Babij were written in Arabic script. Zazas are predominantly Muslims and are divided between Sunni Islam and Alevism. Sunni Zazas are divided between the Hanafi and Shafi'i schools, while the northern group predominantly adheres to the Alevi faith. Zaza Alevism is described as a heterodox and mystical belief system incorporating local and natural elements.
