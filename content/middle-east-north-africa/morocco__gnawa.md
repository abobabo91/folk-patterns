## Overview

The Gnawa, also called Gnaoua, Ghanawa, Ghanawi, or Gnawi, are an ethnic minority inhabiting southern Morocco. They descend from Sahelian slaves brought to Morocco from West Africa, with linguistic analysis pointing especially to the Soninke, Bambara, Fulani, and Hausa. The sources describe a population originating in a broad region extending from Senegal to Chad and from Mali to Southern Nigeria, but they provide no population number. Gnawa communities and musical centres are documented in Marrakesh, Tangier, Rabat, Casablanca, Fez, Essaouira, Khamlia, and other Moroccan localities. Gnawa culture is significant for its combination of Islamic Sufism, West African traditions, ritual poetry, music, healing practices, trance, and dance.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions



**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Gnawa ritual practice includes clothing and accessories determined by the *moqadma* or *shuwafa*, the clairvoyant responsible for the ritual’s necessary materials. During the seven suites of the *derdeba*, dancers are covered by veils in seven different colours, corresponding to the seven families of *mluk*.

### Architecture

The sources mention sanctuaries associated with Gnawa festivals, including the sanctuary of Moulay Brahim in the Atlas Mountains and the sanctuary of Moulay Abdullah bin Tsain in the village of Tamesloht, between Marrakesh and the town of Amizmiz. They do not describe Gnawa houses, building materials, roof types, workshops, courtyards, or architectural decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Gnawa ritual music uses large, heavy iron castanets called *qraqab* or *krakeb*. The sources also mention incense and ritual accessories, but do not document Gnawa ceramics, household vessels, wooden objects, tools, or other everyday material culture.

## Music & performance

Gnawa music is a body of Moroccan religious songs and rhythms that emerged through the cultural fusion of West Africans brought to Morocco, especially Hausa, Fulani, and Bambara peoples. It combines ritual poetry with traditional music and dancing and is performed at *lila*, communal nights of prayer and healing guided by a Gnawa *maalem*, or master musician, together with musicians and dancers.

The central instruments are the three-string lute called *hajhuj*, *guembri*, *gimbri*, or *sentir*, and the large iron castanets called *qraqab* or *krakeb*. Gnawa musicians also use large drums called *tbel*. The music is characterized by low-toned rhythmic melodies, call-and-response singing, hand-clapping, and the interplay of triple and duple meters. The *hajhuj* has historical and musical links to West African lutes such as the Hausa *halam*.

Gnawa ceremonies use music and dance to evoke ancestral saints and other supernatural entities associated with healing. A musical phrase or a few lines may be repeated continuously, while songs describe spirits called *mluk*. In modern contexts, Gnawa musicians have collaborated with jazz, blues, reggae, hip-hop, rock, and rai performers. The Gnaoua World Music Festival of Essaouira presents Gnawa music alongside musicians from around the world.

## Dance & theatre

The sources describe dance as an essential part of Gnawa ritual rather than as a separate theatrical tradition. In the *derdeba*, the *moqadma* or *shuwafa* leads the ecstatic dance called *jedba*, while the *maalem* directs the musicians. The ceremony begins with the *krakeb* players performing continuous rhythmic patterns and a swirling acrobatic dance called the *aâda*. Participants enter a trance state through dances associated with the *mluk*, whose musical patterns, colours, incense, and footsteps identify them.

## Festivals & rituals

Gnawa ritual life centres on the complex liturgy called *lila* or *derdeba*. Preceded by an animal sacrifice, the all-night ceremony generally lasts from late evening until sunrise. It begins with the *aâda*, during which musicians play the *krakeb* and perform a swirling acrobatic dance. The *maalem* plays the *guembri*, burns incense, and calls the saints and supernatural entities to appear and possess the followers, while the *moqadma* determines the ritual clothing and accessories.

The ceremony evokes seven main manifestations of divine activity, seven saints and *mluk*, seven musical patterns, seven suites, seven types of incense, and seven colours. The ritual sequence, called the *treq*, guides the ecstatic passage across the realms of the seven *mluk* until the first light of dawn. Gnawa songs recall the origins and experiences of enslaved ancestors and their eventual redemption.

Gnawa communities in Marrakesh hold annual festivals at the sanctuary of Moulay Brahim in the Atlas Mountains and around the sanctuary of Moulay Abdullah bin Tsain in Tamesloht. These festivals take place in connection with the birthday of Muhammad. The Gnawa of Khamlia hold an annual festival in August. The Gnaoua World Music Festival of Essaouira takes place every June for four days and has been held since 1998.

## Oral tradition & literature

Gnawa ritual poetry and song preserve memories of enslavement and displacement. Gnawa music mentions “abduction, suffering and privation,” while the *lila* recalls the origins of Gnawa communities, the experiences of enslaved ancestors, and redemption. Some West African words and phrases remain in Gnawa songs.

The sources describe songs as repeated phrases or short sequences of lines that can continue for several hours. These songs identify spirits called *mluk*, including Sidi Moussa, Sidi Hamou, and Sidi Mimoun.

## Language & religion

The Gnawa became part of the Sufi order in the Maghreb and identify as Moroccan and Muslim. Gnawa music combines classical Islamic Sufism with pre-Islamic African folk traditions. Its ritual practices include the *lila* or *derdeba*, possession dances called *jedba*, invocations of saints and *mluk*, incense, trance, and healing.

The name Gnawa is explained through indigenous languages of North Africa and the Sahara Desert. According to Berber grammatical principles, *agnaw* is the singular and *ignawen* the plural, with the meaning “black person.” The sources also associate the community’s linguistic history with West African peoples including the Soninke, Bambara, Fulani, and Hausa. They do not provide a complete account of present-day Gnawa dialects, scripts, or language-family classification.
