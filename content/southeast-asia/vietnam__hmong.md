## Overview

The Hmong are a highland people of northern Vietnam who migrated from Guizhou and Yunnan in the nineteenth century. They live in mountainous provinces and cultivate maize, rice, and opium on steep slopes. Five subgroups—White, Black, Flower, Green, and Red Hmong—are distinguished by dress and dialect. They are known for one of Southeast Asia's most demanding textile traditions, a mouth-organ music that speaks the dead home, and a shamanic worldview that has survived a century of upheaval.

## Material culture

Hmong material culture centers on hemp textiles decorated with batik, reverse appliqué, appliqué, and embroidery; silver jewelry marking wealth and status; and metalwork forged by village blacksmiths.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Hmong textile complex (paj ntaub, 'flower cloth') combines four techniques on hemp or cotton, often on the same garment.

- **Hmong batik** (*paj ntaub ciab*) — Wax-resist decoration drawn freehand with a brass or copper stylus dipped in hot beeswax, then indigo-dyed, leaving white patterns on indigo.
- **Reverse appliqué** (*paj ntaub tais*) — Layers of contrasting cotton are stacked, the top layer cut away in geometric shapes, and raw edges turned under with tiny stitches to reveal the color beneath.
- **Appliqué** (*paj ntaub txiab*) — Cut shapes stitched onto a ground cloth, used for large-scale motifs on baby carriers and funerary jackets.
- **Cross-stitch and satin-stitch embroidery** (*paj ntaub xaws*) — Densely worked in silk floss on cotton, forming borders on collars, cuffs, sashes, and apron panels.
- **Story cloths** (*paj ntaub dab neeg*) — A twentieth-century diaspora innovation from Thai refugee camps that narrates the flight from Laos in figurative appliqué and embroidery.

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress is the primary marker of Hmong subgroup identity, with each group distinguished by distinctive skirts, turbans, and collars.

- **Black Hmong dress** — Women wear a knee-length indigo hemp tunic and leggings, often waxed to a metallic sheen.
- **Flower Hmong dress** — Women wear a pleated batik skirt overlaid with brilliantly banded appliqué panels of magenta, orange, and green, topped with a checked cloth turban.
- **White Hmong dress** — Women wear a plain white pleated hemp skirt for ceremonies with a heavily reverse-appliquéd collar that hangs down the back.
- **Baby carrier** (*nyias*) — An embroidered carrier whose central square is thought to protect the infant's soul.
- **Men's ceremonial jacket** — Loose indigo trousers with a wide crotch gusset paired with a short front-buttoning jacket and embroidered cuffs and back panels for New Year and weddings.

### Architecture

The Hmong house is a ground-level rectangular structure with pounded-earth or wood-plank walls, pitched roof, and geomancy-oriented main door.

- **Pounded-earth walls** (*tsev av*) — Constructed in the drier Hà Giang karst regions.
- **Split pine-plank walls** (*tsev ntoo*) — Used in the wetter Lào Cai and Lai Châu regions.
- **Ancestor altar** (*xwm kab*) — Fixed on the wall opposite the main door for household spirit veneration.
- **Dry-stone compound walls** — Built from stacked limestone in Hà Giang's Đồng Văn karst plateau, a landscape signature of the region.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Hmong are not potters but are skilled metalworkers; village blacksmiths forge tools and the distinctive Hmong knife.

- **Hmong knife** — A distinctive blade with a slight downward curve and horn handle, forged by village blacksmiths.
- **Batik stylus** (*ntaus ciab*) — A triangular copper reservoir on a short bamboo handle used to apply wax patterns.
- **Rice-serving box** (*ntim*) — A lacquered wood-and-bamboo container for serving rice.
- **Blacksmith's bellows** — Made from a pair of wooden cylinders piston-pumped like a double organ pipe.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver is the prestige metal and visible bank of a Hmong family, worn as neck rings, earrings, chains, and belts.

- **Neck rings** (*xauv*) — Stacked flat silver rings, sometimes three or four at once, worn by women with pendant chains ending in bells or spirals.
- **Silver belt** — A broad belt of linked plaques or French Indochinese silver piastres worn at New Year and weddings.
- **Silver earrings** — Heavy dangling earrings worn by women as status markers.
- **Baby soul ornaments** — Small silver bracelets and a silver cap ornament worn to anchor the infant's soul against illness.

## Music & performance

The signature Hmong instrument is the qeej, a free-reed mouth organ played simultaneously as dance and as a speech-surrogate that guides souls to the ancestors.

- **Mouth organ** (*qeej*) — A six-pipe bamboo instrument with hammered brass reeds fitted into a curved wooden wind chest, played while leaping and spinning, and used to guide souls at funerals.
- **Jaw harp** (*ncas*) — A brass jaw harp used in courtship rituals.
- **Courtship song** (*kwv txhiaj*) — Sung-poetry exchanged antiphonally between young men and women at markets and New Year, often with improvised verses.
- **Funeral recitation** (*qhuab ke*) — A sung funeral text performed at funerals that parallels the qeej recitation.
- **Soul-guiding qeej** (*qeej tu siav*) — A funerary qeej performance that literally speaks the 'Showing the Way' text guiding a soul back to the ancestors.

## Dance & theatre

Hmong dance is inseparable from the qeej; the player dances while performing, and young women dance in the New Year ball-toss game.

- **Qeej dance** — The qeej player performs choreography of squats, back-arches, and one-legged spins while sustaining the instrument at the mouth.
- **Ball-toss game** (*pov pob*) — Young men and women toss a black cloth ball back and forth, singing courtship songs between throws, with a dropped ball paid in a song or silver token.

## Festivals & rituals

The central festival is Noj Peb Caug (Hmong New Year), held after harvest with ancestor altar renewal, soul-calling, animal sacrifice, and communal qeej dancing and ball-tossing.

- **Hmong New Year** (*Noj Peb Caug*) — Held after the maize and rice harvest in late November or December, lasting several days with ancestor veneration, soul-calling ritual, sacrifice, and communal festivities.
- **Spring hilltop festival** (*Gầu Tào (Ntxaiv Tsug)*) — Held in the first lunar month by families vowing a ritual for a son or healing, featuring a bamboo pole hung with red cloth, feasting, qeej, and horse racing.
- **Soul-calling ritual** (*hu plig*) — Performed three days after birth to name the child and tie a protective cord to the wrist.
- **Marriage negotiation** (*zij poj niam*) — A two-stage negotiation by a go-between involving symbolic 'bride capture' and transfer of silver bridewealth.
- **Funeral rite** — The most elaborate Hmong rite, lasting three to twelve days, with sung qhuab ke, qeej tu siav performance, animal sacrifice, and soul guidance text.

## Foodways

The Hmong staple is maize rather than rice, milled into a couscous-like crumble eaten with broths, supplemented by dry rice, buckwheat, and highland vegetables.

- **Maize crumble** (*mèn mén*) — A coarse yellow couscous-like crumble of milled maize eaten with a broth of pickled mustard greens.
- **Horse meat stew** (*thắng cố*) — A market-day cauldron stew of horse, buffalo, or beef meat and offal simmered with cardamom, star anise, wild ginger, and black cardamom.
- **Maize distillate** (*rượu ngô*) — Maize alcohol fermented with a local yeast leaf, with the Bản Phố village version from Bắc Hà being the most famous.
- **Fermented soybean condiment** (*đậu xị*) — A condiment made from fermented black soybeans.
- **Buckwheat cake** (*bánh tam giác mạch*) — Flat cakes made from buckwheat flour on the Đồng Văn plateau.

## Oral tradition & literature

Hmong oral literature centers on folk tales, proverbs, courtship poetry, and ritual funeral texts that function as a distributed migration epic.

- **Folk tales** (*dab neeg*) — Stories including the widely told tale of the orphan boy Nraug Nkaus and tiger-bride tales.
- **Proverbs** (*paj lug*) — Traditional sayings transmitted orally.
- **Courtship poetry** (*kwv txhiaj*) — Sung poetry exchanged between young men and women, often with vocables and improvised verses.
- **Soul-guiding text** (*taw kev*) — A several-hour epic funeral performance called 'Showing the Way' that recounts the migration route from the Yellow River south.
- **Romanized Popular Alphabet** (*RPA*) — A Latin-based script devised in Laos in the 1950s by missionary linguists Barney, Smalley, and Bertrais.

## Language & religion

Hmong belongs to the Hmongic branch of the Hmong–Mien family; traditional religion is a household-based ancestor cult combined with animism and shamanism.

- **Ancestor altar** (*xwm kab*) — Every lineage maintains this altar to the household spirit.
- **Shaman** (*txiv neeb*) — A 'father of spirits' who enters trance on a wooden bench that 'becomes' a flying horse to retrieve lost souls.
- **Soul loss and healing** (*hu plig*) — Illness diagnosed as soul loss and treated by soul-calling ritual and animal sacrifice.
- **White Hmong dialect** (*Hmoob Dawb*) — One of the two principal Vietnamese Hmong dialects, standardized in the RPA.
- **Flower/Green Hmong dialect** (*Hmoob Lees / Hmoob Ntsuab*) — The second principal dialect of Vietnamese Hmong, standardized in the RPA and mostly mutually intelligible with White Hmong.
