## Overview

The Hmong (*Hmoob*) are a highland people of mainland Southeast Asia and southwestern China, part of the Hmong–Mien language family. In Vietnam they concentrate in the northern mountainous provinces — Hà Giang, Lào Cai (notably around Sa Pa and Bắc Hà), Lai Châu, Sơn La, Điện Biên, and Yên Bái — where they cultivate maize, dry rice, and opium poppy on steep slopes above the Tai-speaking valley peoples. Vietnamese census categories distinguish subgroups by dress and dialect: **White Hmong** (*Hmoob Dawb*), **Black Hmong** (*Hmoob Dub*), **Flower Hmong** (*Hmoob Lees*), **Green/Blue Hmong** (*Hmoob Ntsuab*), and **Red Hmong**. Their nineteenth-century migration from Guizhou and Yunnan into Tonkin followed the collapse of the Miao rebellions under the Qing. They matter in folk-culture terms for one of the most technically demanding batik-and-appliqué textile traditions in Southeast Asia, for a mouth-organ music tradition (*qeej*) that doubles as a funerary speech surrogate, and for a shamanic cosmology that survived a century of upheaval.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Hmong textile complex — collectively called *paj ntaub* ("flower cloth") — combines four techniques on hemp or cotton ground cloth, often on the same garment.

**Hmong batik (*paj ntaub ciab*)** — Wax-resist decoration applied with a small brass or copper stylus (*ntaus ciab*) dipped in hot beeswax, then indigo-dyed. The Black and Flower Hmong of Sa Pa and Hà Giang are the primary practitioners; White Hmong generally do not batik. The wax is drawn freehand in geometric fields, then boiled off after dyeing, leaving a white pattern on indigo. The line is thinner and more angular than the Miao batik of Guizhou.

**Reverse appliqué (*paj ntaub tais*)** — Layers of contrasting cotton are stacked, and the top layer is cut away in small geometric shapes and the raw edges turned under with tiny stitches to reveal the color beneath. The signature technique of White Hmong collar panels and of Green Hmong skirt borders.

**Appliqué (*paj ntaub txiab*)** — Cut shapes stitched onto a ground cloth. Used for large-scale motifs on baby carriers (*nyias*) and for the panels stitched onto funerary jackets that guide the soul home.

**Cross-stitch and satin-stitch embroidery (*paj ntaub xaws*)** — Densely worked in silk floss on cotton, forming borders on collars, cuffs, sashes, and apron panels. The stitch counts on a Flower Hmong festival apron can exceed a hundred thousand.

**Story cloths (*paj ntaub dab neeg*)** — A twentieth-century diaspora innovation from the Thai refugee camps (Ban Vinai, Wat Tham Krabok, 1975 onward), narrating the flight from Laos in figurative appliqué and embroidery. Now sold globally as a signed refugee art form.

**Hemp cloth (*maj*)** — The ground fabric for most traditional Hmong dress: bast retted from *Cannabis sativa*, spliced, spun, and back-strap or foot-treadle woven. Cultivation and processing are exclusively women's work and mark a girl's readiness for marriage.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Kab ntsig* (snail / spiral), *plaub taig* (four-pointed star or "elephant's foot"), *qws* (cross), *xub* (arrowhead), *ntiv tes* (fingertip zigzag), *kaus mom* (mountain), *nplooj* (leaf), *nyuj rog* (buffalo horns), *tshim* (ram's horn scroll), *qab zib* (honeycomb / rhombus grid).

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress is the primary marker of subgroup identity. **Black Hmong** women wear a knee-length indigo hemp tunic and leggings, often waxed to a metallic sheen. **Flower Hmong** women wear a pleated batik skirt overlaid with brilliantly banded appliqué panels of magenta, orange, and green, topped with a turban wound from a checked cloth. **White Hmong** women wear a plain white pleated hemp skirt for ceremonies (and indigo trousers daily), with a heavily reverse-appliquéd collar (*dab tsho*) that hangs down the back. **Green Hmong** women wear a pleated indigo batik skirt with a red-and-white embroidered waistband. Men across subgroups wear loose indigo trousers with a wide crotch gusset, a short front-buttoning jacket, and a cloth sash; ceremonial men's jackets have embroidered cuffs and back panels. Silver neck rings and coin belts are added for New Year and weddings. Infants are carried in an embroidered *nyias* baby carrier whose central square is thought to protect the soul.

### Architecture

The Hmong house is a ground-level rectangular structure, not raised on stilts like Tai and Vietnamese lowland houses — a diagnostic difference visible on any northern Vietnamese ridge. Walls are pounded earth (*tsev av*) in the drier Hà Giang karst or split pine planks (*tsev ntoo*) in Lào Cai and Lai Châu; roofs are pitched, thatched with cogon grass or shingled with split pomu (*Fokienia*) wood held down by stones. There are no windows or only very small ones. The interior is divided by a longitudinal partition into a public front room with the hearth and a rear sleeping area; the main door faces downslope and is oriented by geomancy. Two ritual features are fixed: the ancestor altar (*xwm kab*) on the wall opposite the main door, and a second cooking hearth used for large family and ritual meals. Granaries are separate small structures, and pigsties and buffalo stalls are attached to or below the house. In Hà Giang the Hmong of the Đồng Văn karst plateau enclose their compounds with dry-stone walls of stacked limestone, a landscape signature of the region.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Hmong are not a ceramic tradition — cooking pots and water jars are traded in from Kinh (Vietnamese) and Tai potters. Metalwork, however, is a defining Hmong craft. Village blacksmiths (*hlau*) forge plough shares, hoes, sickles, and the distinctive Hmong knife with a slightly downcurved blade and horn handle, using bellows made from a pair of wooden cylinders and piston-pumped like a double organ pipe. Silversmithing produces the heavy neck rings, collars, and chains worn at New Year. The batik stylus (*ntaus ciab*) — a triangular copper reservoir on a short bamboo handle — is itself a small metalworked object. Household objects include the *ntim*, a lacquered wood-and-bamboo rice-serving box; carved wooden mortars for husking maize; and split-bamboo winnowing trays.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver is the prestige metal and the visible bank of a Hmong family. Women wear stacked flat neck rings (*xauv*), sometimes three or four at once, with pendant chains ending in small bells, spirals, or fish; heavy silver earrings; and, at New Year and weddings, a broad silver belt of linked plaques or a plastron of French Indochinese silver piastres. Men wear a single neck ring at ceremonies. Silver is worked by Hmong and Nùng smiths from melted trade coins. Hair is worn long by both sexes traditionally; women coil it around a wooden or horn comb and bind it into the turban. Tattooing and henna are not Hmong practices. Babies wear small silver bracelets and a silver cap ornament to anchor the soul against illness.

## Music & performance

The signature Hmong instrument is the ***qeej*** (Vietnamese *khèn Mông*), a free-reed mouth organ of six bamboo pipes fitted into a curved wooden wind chest, each pipe with a hammered brass or bronze free reed. The *qeej* is played simultaneously danced — the player leaps, spins, and drops while sustaining a continuous polyphonic drone — and, most importantly, it functions as a speech-surrogate instrument: its tones map onto the tones of spoken Hmong, and the funerary *qeej tu siav* ("*qeej* that cuts the breath") literally speaks the "Showing the Way" text that guides a soul back to the ancestors. Other instruments include the *ncas* (a brass jaw harp used in courtship), the *raj nplaim* (small bamboo flute), the *raj pum liv* (leaf blown as a reed for love songs), and a two-string bowed fiddle. Song genres include *kwv txhiaj*, sung-poetry courtship songs exchanged antiphonally between young men and women at markets and New Year, often with vocables and improvised verses; *nkauj tshoob*, wedding songs performed by the go-between (*mej koob*); and *qhuab ke*, the sung funeral text that parallels the *qeej* recitation. Performance contexts are almost entirely domestic and ritual rather than staged, though state-sponsored *khèn* troupes now perform at the annual Sa Pa and Bắc Hà tourism festivals.

## Dance & theatre

The Hmong have no theatrical tradition in the sense of scripted drama, masks, or puppetry. Dance is inseparable from the *qeej*: the *qeej* player is the dancer, and the choreography — squats, back-arches, one-legged spins with the instrument held to the mouth — is performed at funerals and at New Year courtship gatherings (*tsov cuag*). Young women dance a restrained side-step in a line during the New Year ball-toss game (*pov pob*), in which unmarried men and women toss a black cloth ball back and forth across a courtyard, singing *kwv txhiaj* between throws; a dropped ball is paid in a song or a small silver token. In Vietnam the state cultural apparatus has choreographed staged versions of *khèn* dance for the National Ethnic Culture Village in Hanoi and for provincial festivals.

## Festivals & rituals

The central Hmong festival is ***Noj Peb Caug*** ("Eat Thirty"), the Hmong New Year, held after the maize and rice harvest — traditionally at the end of the twelfth Hmong lunar month, in late November or December, and lasting several days. The household ancestor altar is renewed, a *ua neeb* soul-calling ritual is performed by the shaman, pigs and chickens are sacrificed, and the community gathers for *pov pob* ball-tossing, *qeej* dancing, bullfights, and horse racing. In Vietnam this coincides with the Bắc Hà and Mèo Vạc market fairs. Weekly highland markets — Bắc Hà (Sunday), Cán Cấu (Saturday), Đồng Văn, Mèo Vạc, Lũng Phìn — are themselves quasi-ritual gatherings for courtship and exchange. The **Gầu Tào** (*Hmoob*: *Ntxaiv Tsug*) is a spring hilltop festival, held in the first lunar month, at which a family that has vowed a ritual for a son or for healing erects a bamboo pole hung with a red cloth and hosts feasting, *qeej*, and horse racing. Life-cycle rites are anchored by the shaman (*txiv neeb*): three days after birth a *hu plig* soul-calling names the child and ties a protective cord to the wrist; marriage involves a two-stage negotiation by a go-between (*mej koob*), a symbolic "bride capture" (*zij poj niam*), and the transfer of silver bridewealth; the funeral is the most elaborate rite, lasting three to twelve days, during which the *qhuab ke* is sung, the *qeej tu siav* is played, a cow or buffalo is sacrificed, and the "Showing the Way" text guides the soul back through each village the ancestors passed on the migration from China.

## Foodways

The staple across northern Vietnamese Hmong communities is maize rather than rice, milled into flour and steamed into ***mèn mén*** — a coarse yellow couscous-like crumble eaten with a broth of pickled mustard greens. Dry upland rice, buckwheat, and Job's tears are secondary staples; buckwheat flour is made into flat cakes (*bánh tam giác mạch*) on the Đồng Văn plateau, where the pink buckwheat bloom is now itself a tourist festival. Signature Hmong dishes include ***thắng cố***, a market-day cauldron stew of horse (or buffalo, or beef) meat and offal simmered with cardamom, star anise, wild ginger, and *thảo quả* (black cardamom), sold by the bowl at Bắc Hà and Mèo Vạc; smoked pork and buffalo hung above the hearth for months; sausages stuffed with rice and herbs; and *đậu xị*, a fermented black soybean condiment. The prestige drink is ***rượu ngô***, maize distillate fermented with a local yeast leaf (*men lá*) — the Bản Phố village version from Bắc Hà is the most famous. Corn beer and a millet beer drunk communally through bamboo straws from a shared jar also appear at festivals. Sacrificial pig and chicken are the ritual foods; the chicken's tongue, skull, and feet are read by the shaman for divination.

## Oral tradition & literature

Hmong oral literature is transmitted through four principal genres: *dab neeg* (folk tales, including the widely told story of the orphan boy Nraug Nkaus and the tiger-bride tales), *paj lug* (proverbs), *kwv txhiaj* (sung courtship poetry), and the ritual sung texts *qhuab ke* and *taw kev* ("Showing the Way") performed at funerals, which constitute a several-hour epic geography of the migration route from the Yellow River south. There is no single canonical epic on the scale of the Kirghiz *Manas*, but the funeral texts function as a distributed migration epic. Hmong was historically an oral language; a Latin-based Romanized Popular Alphabet (RPA) was devised in Laos in the 1950s by the missionary linguists Barney, Smalley, and Bertrais, and the indigenous **Pahawh Hmong** script was invented in 1959 by Shong Lue Yang in the Nong Het region of Laos, whose followers considered him a prophet. Both scripts are used by literate Hmong in Vietnam today, alongside a Vietnamese-orthography-based system. The story-cloth (*paj ntaub dab neeg*) is a contemporary pictorial extension of the oral repertoire.

## Language & religion

Hmong belongs to the Hmongic branch of the Hmong–Mien family, unrelated to Vietnamese, Chinese, or Tai. The Vietnamese Hmong speak principally two dialects — *Hmoob Dawb* (White) and *Hmoob Lees* / *Hmoob Ntsuab* (Flower/Green) — which correspond to the two dialects standardized in the RPA and are mostly mutually intelligible with adjustment. Traditional Hmong religion is a household-based ancestor cult combined with animism and shamanism: every lineage maintains the *xwm kab* altar to the household spirit; the shaman (*txiv neeb*, "father of spirits") enters trance on a wooden bench that "becomes" a flying horse to retrieve lost souls and negotiate with the spirit world (*dab*); illness is diagnosed as soul loss and treated by *hu plig* soul-calling and animal sacrifice. A significant minority of Vietnamese Hmong converted to Protestant Christianity from the 1990s onward, particularly through short-wave radio broadcasts, a movement that has periodically drawn state suppression in Điện Biên and Lai Châu.
