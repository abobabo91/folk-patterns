## Overview

The Nias, also called *Ono Niha* or Niasans, are an Austronesian ethnic group native to Nias, an island off the west coast of North Sumatra, Indonesia. In the Nias language, the island is known as *Tanö Niha*, with *Tanö* meaning “land.” Most Nias people live on Nias Island, while a significant number live in Riau Province. The 2010 Indonesian census recorded 1,041,925 Nias people, or 0.44% of Indonesia’s population. Nias society continues to follow indigenous norms and practices regulated by *fondrakö*, customary law covering life from birth to death. The group is notable for its megalithic culture, organized villages, wooden ritual figures, stone monuments, named dances, distinctive weapons, household objects, cuisine, and oral proverbs.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

 They state that Nias people produce *Katidi*, weavings from bamboo.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources describe Nias household objects as carved with zoomorphic, floral, or geometric motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources mention several ornaments. *Fondruru* is a men’s earring made of precious metal. *Kalabubu* is also known as the headhunter’s necklace. *Nifatali-tali* is a necklace of precious metal, while *Nifato-fato* is a men’s necklace of precious metal. *Suahu* is a comb made of wood or precious metal.

The sources also describe traditional Nias attire on the anthropomorphic stone figure *osa-osa*. This figure wears a *kalabubu* necklace and pendant earrings called *fondulu* or *saro dalinga*.

### Architecture

Nias people live in highly organized villages. Nias villages often contain stone monuments and large houses standing on earthquake-resistant timber pylons. Many older houses have disappeared because of deterioration and the difficulty of maintaining ancient wooden structures against rot, insects, and wear. The over-harvesting of forests is also given as a reason why old houses were not rebuilt.

In the past, villages, especially in South Nias, were strategically built on a ridge or hill and surrounded by ramparts and security gates. Entry was through two gates reached by steep staircases. These gates led to a straight paved avenue through the village center, with traditional houses on either side. Near the main square stood the house of the village founders, the *omo sebua*. The space in front of each house belonged to its inhabitants and served as a “front courtyard,” including for drying harvests before storage. Central Nias villages were usually smaller, and their houses stood farther from the main avenue. *Omo Hada* is identified as a traditional house.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

*Bowoa Tanö* is a clay pot. Other household objects include *Bari gana’a*, a miniature jewellery box; *Doghi* in North Nias and *fogao* or *dröghija* in South Nias, wooden coconut graters; *Figa lae*, banana leaves used as plates; *Halu*, a paddy pounder; *Haru*, a wooden spoon whose handle base may be carved in forms such as a fist; *Lösu*, a mortar and pestle; *Niru*, a tool for separating rice from its husk; *Gala*, a tray-like wooden item; *Sole Mbanio*, a drinking container made from coconut shell; and *Tumba* or *lauru*, tankards used to weigh rice.

Nias people also produced weapons from leather, cord or woven fibres, precious metal, iron, and brass. The *gari* is a sword, while the *balato* or *tolögu* is a steel sword with a brass hilt and a protective amulet. The *baluse* is an oval-shaped shield in South Nias, and the *dange* is a hexagonal-shaped shield from North Nias.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources identify precious-metal earrings and necklaces as Nias ornaments. *Fondruru* is a men’s earring, *Nifatali-tali* is a necklace, and *Nifato-fato* is a men’s necklace. *Suahu* is a comb made from wood or precious metal. The *kalabubu* is described as a headhunter’s necklace and appears as part of the traditional Nias attire represented on the *osa-osa* stone figure.

## Music & performance

 They identify several performance-related practices. *Fangowai* is a welcoming-of-guest dance. *Maena* is a group dance, and *Tari Moyo* is an eagle dance. *Tari Mogaele* is listed without description. *Fatele*, also called *Faluya* or *Faluaya*, is a war dance.

The sources connect performance and public display with stone monuments and social rank. The *Owasa* festival is described as a feast for raising a person’s rank. Publicly dedicating stone monuments was one of the requirements for claiming a higher rank and receiving honorary titles. The *osa-osa*, an anthropomorphic form of the *behu* megalith, was paraded around the village before being displayed in front of a house. The host could be seated or even stand on top of it.

## Dance & theatre

Nias performance traditions named in the sources include *Fahombo*, leaping over the rock; *Fatele*, *Faluya*, or *Faluaya*, the war dance; *Maena*, the group dance; *Tari Moyo*, the eagle dance; *Tari Mogaele*; and *Fangowai*, the welcoming-of-guest dance.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe *Owasa* as a festival or feast intended to raise a person’s rank. Stone monuments were central to the event, and their public dedication demonstrated that the participant had fulfilled requirements for a higher rank and honorary titles. The *behu*, a vertical stone column, was erected in front of a host’s house to commemorate former great celebrations. The number and size of *behu* displayed indicated the host’s position in the village: noblemen displayed larger and more numerous examples than commoners.

Nias customary law is called *fondrakö* and regulates all aspects of life from birth to death. When an elderly person died, the family made a wooden ancestor statue called *adu zatua*, which was unveiled on the fourth day after the death. The deceased person’s spirit was believed to reside in the statue. Ancestor statues received prayers and sacrifices, especially at births, marriages, and deaths, and were believed to ensure fertility for the family, livestock, and agricultural land.

*Fame Ono Nihalö* is identified as weddings.

## Foodways

The sources list cassava, pork, fish, bananas, sago, glutinous rice, crab, coconut, and palm products among Nias foods. *Gowi Nihandro* or *Gowi Nitutu* is pounded cassava. *Harinake* is minced pork, and *Ni’owuru* is salted pork for longer storage. *Godo-godo* consists of shredded cassava shaped into balls for boiling and later combined with coconut flakes. *Köfö-köfö* is minced fish shaped into balls to be dried or smoked.

*Rakigae* and *Gae Nibogö* are fried and grilled bananas. *Tamböyö* is *ketupat*, while *löma* is *lemang*. *Kazimone* is made of sago, and *Wawayasö* is glutinous rice. *Gulo-Gulo Farö* is candy made from distilled coconut milk. *Bato* is compressed crab meat shaped into balls for longer storage, as found on the Hinako Islands. *Nami* is salted crab eggs kept for longer storage, sometimes for months depending on the quantity of salt.

*Tuo nifarö* is palm wine. *Tuo mbanua* is raw palm wine with added *laru*, described as roots of various plants that give it a certain amount of alcohol.

## Oral tradition & literature

Nias mythology tells of a tree of life called *Tora Sigaru’a*, located at *Tetehöli Ana’a*. According to the myth, the first human arrival on Nias began during the days of King *Sirao*. His nine sons were banished from *Tetehöli Ana’a* after fighting over the throne and were regarded as the first people to set foot on Nias.

The sources also record three traditional proverbs. *Hulö ni femanga mao, ihene zinga* means “Like a cat that eats, starting from the sides” and advises beginning with the easiest task. *Hulö la’ewa nidanö ba ifuli fahalö-halö* means “Just like chopping the water, it will still remain” and refers to something inseparable. *Abakha zokho safuria moroi ba zi oföna* means “The wound is more severe at the later stage than the beginning” and concerns the effects of an action being felt most toward the end.

## Language & religion

Nias is an Austronesian language spoken on Nias Island and the Batu Islands off the west coast of Sumatra. Native speakers call it *Li Niha*. It belongs to the Northwest Sumatra–Barrier Islands subgroup, which also includes Mentawai and the Batak languages. The language had about 770,000 speakers in 2000 and has three main dialects: northern, central, and southern. The dialects have about 80% cognate similarity. The sources state that Nias is an open-syllable language with no syllable-final consonants and that its grammar has ergative–absolutive alignment.

The Nias alphabet differs from the Indonesian alphabet and includes characters such as *Öö* and *Ŵŵ*. The predominant religion is Protestant Christianity. Six out of seven Niasans are Protestant, while the remainder are divided approximately evenly between Muslim and Catholic. Missionaries first visited Nias in 1865, and Christianity grew rapidly in the early 1900s. The sources also describe ancestor figures, sacrifices, prayers, magical practices, and ancestor worship in earlier Nias culture.
