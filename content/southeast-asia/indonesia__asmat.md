## Overview

The Asmat are a Papuan people occupying roughly 18,000 km² of mangrove, tidal swamp, freshwater swamp and lowland rainforest along the Arafura Sea in Indonesia; the name refers both to the people and to the region. It is an umbrella term for twelve sub-groups with shared linguistic and cultural affinities. Their woodcarving, made to honour ancestors — particularly accomplished warriors — has been collected by museums worldwide and is sought by collectors.

## Material culture

Asmat material culture is dominated by carved and painted wood, with pattern carried in openwork relief, plaited fibre and feathers rather than woven cloth.

### Textile & pattern traditions

There is no woven-cloth tradition; the fibre arts are plaiting, netting and knotting, used above all for the mask costume.

- **Mask costume** (*jipae*) — A full-body costume of rattan and plant fibre, netted and knotted, with a fringe, worn during the jipae ritual.
- **Plaited fibre bags** — Painted with geometric, curvilinear or shield-motif panels and trimmed with coix seed and feather tassels.
- **Seed-worked belts** — Fibre belts densely worked with seeds, with a fringe.

### Woodcarving

Wood is the main medium, carved in openwork and relief and painted in red, white and black.

- **Memorial pole** (*bisj, mbis*) — Stacked human figures representing deceased ancestors above a canoe-shaped base, crowned by an openwork projecting "wing" (*cemen*); carved by religious carvers (*wow-ipits*) after a member of the community was killed.
- **War shield** — Tall carved wooden shields with relief human figures and curvilinear motifs, painted with ochre, white and black.
- **Canoe prow ornament** — Openwork carved prow-boards with scrollwork.
- **Figures and staffs** — Carved ancestor figures and figurative staffs.

### Clothing & dress

Dress on ceremonial occasions was built from feather, fibre, shell and bone rather than cloth.

- **Cassowary-feather headdress** — Cassowary feathers knotted onto plant-fibre cords.
- **Nose ornaments** — Of carved bone or shell, worn by men in early documentary photographs.

### Architecture

Because much of the land floods daily, dwellings (*jew*) have typically been built two or more metres above the ground on wooden posts.

- **Raised dwelling** (*jew*) — Built on posts two or more metres high.
- **Tree house** — In some inland regions the Asmat have lived in tree houses sometimes as high as 25 metres from the ground.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Everyday objects are wood, bamboo, bone and fibre, and materials for canoes, dwellings and carvings are all gathered locally.

- **Dugout canoe** (*ci*) — Central to daily life in a swampy land; its form recurs as the base of the bisj pole.
- **Paddles** — Carved and painted wooden blades.
- **Cassowary-bone dagger** — Decorated with feather and seed tufts.
- **Spear** — Wooden spears with carved barbs, some tipped with cassowary claw.
- **Sago pounder** — Bamboo blade with a carved wooden handle depicting ancestors.
- **Bowls and combs** — Carved wooden bowls with openwork figures; bamboo combs with carved figurative handles.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment is shell, bone, dog teeth, coix seed and feather.

- **Nose ornaments** — Carved bone, and shell with spiral scrollwork.
- **Necklaces** — Dog teeth on plant-fibre cord; long strands of coix seed.
- **Armlets and head ornaments** — Including fur strips with seed beading.

## Music & performance

Museum records hold carved hourglass drums and trumpets.

- **Hourglass drum** — Carved from wood with a lizard-skin membrane bound by rattan, an integral handle and openwork carving.
- **Trumpet** — Bamboo, incised with spiral, scroll or linear designs, some infilled with white lime.

## Festivals & rituals

Ritual life centred on the dead: bisj poles, the jipae mask ritual and shield ceremonies.

- **Bisj ceremony** — Poles were raised to satisfy the spirits of those killed and to send them to the afterlife (*Safan*) across the sea; a headhunt against an enemy village avenged the dead.
- **Jipae mask ritual** — Wearers of the full jipae costume represent the recently deceased.
- **Shield and bisj ceremonies** — Incorporated into an adapted Catholic liturgy.
- **Annual woodcarving competition** — Held in Agats in early October since 1981, founded by Bishop Alphonse Sowada, who also founded the Asmat Museum of Culture and Progress there in 1973.

## Foodways

The Asmat mainly subsist on starch from the sago palm.

- **Sago palm** (*Metroxylon sagu*) — The staple starch.
- **Sago beetle grub** (*Rhynchophorus bilineatus*) — A supplement to sago.
- **Crustaceans, fish and forest game** — Gathered from the surrounding forests and waters.

## Language & religion

The Asmat languages form the Asmat family within the Trans–New Guinea phylum; Catholic missions arrived in 1953.

- **Asmat language family** — Five or more dialects: Casuarina Coast Asmat, Yaosakor Asmat, Central Asmat, North Asmat, Tamnim and Citak.
- **Citak speakers** (*Kau*) — The Citak (Yupmakcain and Brazza) call themselves Kau, consider themselves a separate people, and are matrilineal.
- **Ancestor veneration** — Great emphasis on ancestors, particularly accomplished warriors.
- **Headhunting** — An important element of the culture until missionaries suppressed it.

## Glossary

- *bisj, mbis* — Memorial pole of stacked ancestor figures on a canoe-shaped base
- *cemen* — The openwork projecting "wing" at the top of a bisj pole
- *wow-ipits* — Religious woodcarvers
- *jipae* — Full-body mask costume of rattan and fibre, and the ritual it is worn in
- *jew* — Asmat dwelling, raised on posts
- *ci* — Dugout canoe
- *Safan* — The afterlife across the sea; also the name of one of the twelve sub-groups
- *Kau* — Self-name of the Citak
- *Metroxylon sagu* — Sago palm, the staple starch
- *Rhynchophorus bilineatus* — Sago beetle, whose grubs supplement sago
