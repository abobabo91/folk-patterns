## Overview

The Sakai, also called Senoi, are an indigenous population of Peninsular Malaysia and Sumatra. Traditionally, Sakai communities lived in the interior, where some continued nomadic hunting and gathering, while others practised agriculture, fishing, trading, gathering jungle produce, and wage labour. In Malaysia, the Senoi are one of three administrative divisions of the Orang Asli, alongside the Semang and Proto-Malays, although these categories are external classifications rather than identities shared by all the communities placed within them. The Senoi group consists of the Semai, Temiar, Mah Meri, Jah Hut, Semaq Beri and Cheq Wong peoples, with a total population of about 60,000. The Sakai or Senoi languages belong to the Aslian language group. The source describes their history through changing relations with agriculture, Malay states, colonial administration, forced relocation, logging, plantations and urbanisation.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources mention clothing traditions only in connection with the historical treatment of Orang Asli by Malay people, who made their clothing traditions and physical characteristics objects of ridicule.

### Architecture

 They state that communities historically lived in villages, camps, remote interiors and settlements, and that state governments later constructed new villages for Indigenous populations relocated from the jungle.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do mention that Orang Asli exchanged jungle produce, gold and tin ore for fabrics, iron tools, necklaces and food, including rice.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 Necklaces are mentioned only as goods received by Indigenous people of the Malay Peninsula through exchange.

## Festivals & rituals

 They state that the Malaysian government pursued the conversion of Indigenous people to Islam and their integration into the settled population, while Orang Asli communities preferred to modernise without becoming Malays, including in contexts where they converted to Islam. No specific religious ceremony or festival is identified.

## Foodways

The sources mention rice as a food received through exchange and describe several subsistence practices: slash-and-burn agriculture, hunting, fishing, gathering, cultivation of commercial crops, trading, wage labour and the processing and sale of jungle produce. The Semaq Beri traditionally combined slash-and-burn agriculture, hunting and gathering; the Mah Meri combined agriculture and fishing; and the Semelai combined slash-and-burn agriculture with fishing in lakes and wage labour. They also state that the introduction of rice was associated with migrant farmers and the emergence of agriculture on the Malay Peninsula.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They do mention languages and names preserved in ethnographic classification, including Central Aslian, Northern Aslian and Southern Aslian languages, but provide no texts or descriptions of storytelling. The source states that the name “chewong” arose from a misunderstanding by a British huntsman of the name of a Malay employee in the Department of Hunting, Siwang bin Ahmat, before the Second World War period.

## Language & religion

The Sakai or Senoi speak Aslian languages. The source distinguishes Central Aslian languages associated especially with the Semai and Temiar, Northern Aslian languages associated with the Cheq Wong, and Southern Aslian languages spoken by the Semaq Beri, Semelai and Mah Meri. Jah Hut occupies a special place among the Aslian languages, while the source also discusses the loss or replacement of Indigenous languages by Malay. The Senoi people are also known as Sakai among local Malays, but *sakai* is described as a derogatory Malay term, whereas *mensakai* means “to work together” in the Senoi language. The Central Senois, particularly in Batang Padang District, prefer *Mai Darat*. The Malaysian government has pursued conversion to Islam and integration into the national population. The source also describes earlier Malay classifications of Orang Asli as pagans and kafirs, and records continuing political and social inequalities.
