## Overview

The Mah Meri are an ethnic group native to the western part of Peninsular Malaysia. They are one of the 18 Orang Asli groups named by the Malaysian government and belong to the Senoi subgroup. Most live along the coast of South Selangor, from Sungai Pelek to Pulau Carey, with at least one community on the other side of the Klang River. Pulau Carey has five separate Mah Meri villages, while other communities live in small villages, or kampungs, on the fringes of nearby cities. According to the Orang Asli Office of the Malaysian government, their population numbered around 2,200 in 2005. The Mah Meri are internationally known for wood carving, especially carvings made on Pulau Carey. Their language, also called Besisi, is an endangered Austroasiatic language.

## Material culture

### Architecture

They state that most Mah Meri live in small villages, or kampungs, and that Pulau Carey contains five separate Mah Meri villages.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do document wood carving as an important material practice. Many Mah Meri are skilled in carving statues made from wood, including representations of deities, humans, flora, and fauna. The handicraft production of the Mah Meri community in Sungai Bumbun, Kuala Langat, is described as having high artistic value and potential for international recognition.

## Music & performance

They do identify the Bomoh as a shaman who plays an important role in the kampung. The sources also describe Main puteri, meaning “Playing princess,” as a dying ritualistic form of treatment performed by the Mah Meri shaman. It is used to rejuvenate patients experiencing emotional depression, physical fatigue, or psychological problems caused by metaphysical forces. The sources attribute the decline of this practice to Islamisation.

No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription is supplied for the Mah Meri or for a Mah Meri musical tradition.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name three Mah Meri traditional dances: Sewang dance, Tarian Jo'oh, and Tarian Topeng. Tarian Jo'oh is glossed as “Jungle dance,” while Tarian Topeng is glossed as “Mask dance.”

The sources also describe wood carvings of deities, humans, flora, and fauna.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do document Main puteri, a ritualistic form of treatment performed by the Mah Meri shaman, or Bomoh. Its meaning is given as “Playing princess,” and its purpose is to rejuvenate patients with emotional depression, physical fatigue, or psychological problems attributed to metaphysical forces. The practice is described as dying because of Islamisation.

Mah Meri settlements are governed locally through institutions shared with other Orang Asli villages. Each kampung elects its own Batin, or Village Headman, and a council of “elders” representing people living in the kampung. The Batin receives an annual salary from the Malaysian government.

## Foodways

The sources mention that the Mah Meri are considered Orang Laut because they live in settlements near seasides and work as fishermen.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that Mah Meri oral histories suggest that the distribution of the community once reached as far south as Batu Pahat. These oral histories also relate a belief that the Mah Meri migrated from islands in southern Johor to the coastal shores of Selangor to escape enemies.

The sources further state that Mah Meri ancestors are believed to be associated with the arrival of Austro-Asiatic migrants, the emergence of fire-cutting agriculture on the Malay Peninsula, and the emergence of rice.

## Language & religion

Mah Meri, also known as Besisi, Cellate, Hmaʼ Btsisiʼ, Maʼ Betisek, and pejoratively as Orang Sabat, is an Austroasiatic language spoken in the Malay Peninsula. It belongs to the Southern Aslian branch of the Aslian languages, together with Semaq Beri, Semelai, and Temoq. It is the only remaining Aslian language spoken in a coastal area, on the coasts of Negeri Sembilan and Selangor. Its speaker population is given as 3,675 in a record at the Orang Asli Museum in Gombak; another source estimates that 3,000 people still speak the language.

Mah Meri has two voice registers. Its syntax permits post-head modifiers and demonstratives, while prepositions occur before the head. Transitive clauses generally follow Agent-Verb-Object order, although Verb-Agent-Object is more common in natural discourse. Intransitive clauses allow both Subject-Verb and Verb-Subject order. Morphology is exclusively through prefixation and infixation.

According to Ethnologue, Mah Meri is “6b: Threatened,” meaning that it is used for face-to-face communication within all generations but is losing users. A study of language use and attitudes in four Mah Meri villages on Carey Island reports a strong and positive community status, alongside the possibility of a future shift toward Malay because younger people use Mah Meri less often.

The sources describe the Bomoh as a shaman and associate Main puteri with metaphysical treatment. They also describe Islamisation as affecting the practice.
