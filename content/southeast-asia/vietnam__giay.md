## Overview

The Giáy are an ethnic group in Vietnam and Laos. In Vietnam, most live in the mountainous northern provinces of Lào Cai, Hà Giang, Lai Châu, and Cao Bằng; named locations include Tả Van village near Sa Pa, Mường Khương District, Yên Minh District, Đồng Văn District, Mường Tè District, Phong Thổ District, and Bảo Lạc District. Their population was 67,858 in 2019. In Laos they are known as the Nhang or Yang. The Giáy speak Bouyei, a member of the Tai language family, and are also associated with several northern Tai-speaking communities. Their documented folk culture includes rice cultivation, communal agricultural practices, distinctive clothing, ancestor worship, village sacred groves, named dishes, songs, and the Roóng Poọc festival.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Cotton clothing** — Giáy clothing is made from cotton. Women’s clothing includes a brightly colored five-panel blouse with fastening under the right arm, a contrasting collar and cuffs, and colorful plaid headscarves.

**Indigo trousers** — Both women and men wear loose indigo trousers.

**Men’s tunics and turbans** — Men traditionally wear simple tunics and turbans.

**Plaid headscarves** — Women wear colorful plaid headscarves.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Women usually wear brightly colored five-panel blouses that button under the right arm and have contrasting collars and cuffs. They also wear colorful plaid headscarves. Men traditionally wear simple tunics and turbans. Both men and women wear loose indigo trousers. Western dress is now common among many Giáy, especially men.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention only agricultural tools and animals in general terms: rice is cultivated using simple technology, and water buffalo are used as draft animals to plow the land.

## Music & performance

The sources say that Giáy sing *vương* and *phướn*. No information is provided about whether these songs are performed at weddings, funerals, festivals, agricultural events, or other occasions.

The sources also state that Bouyei sentences contain many different levels of phrasing and that Bouyei has six tones. These are linguistic characteristics rather than descriptions of Giáy musical performance. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription concerns this ethnic group in the supplied material.

## Festivals & rituals

On the snake day of the first lunar month, the Giáy celebrate *Roóng Poọc*, a festival marking the start of the rice season.

The Giáy traditionally worship their ancestors and animistic spirits that govern the heavens, the soil, the kitchen, and other domains. Each village has a sacred grove called a *đoong xía*. Offerings are made twice per year at the biggest tree in the grove to ensure the favor of the spirit governing the village.

## Foodways

The Giáy traditionally cultivate rice in paddy fields. They also grow corn and cassava, sometimes on land too steep for rice cultivation, as feed for livestock. Water buffalo are raised as draft animals, while chickens and pigs are raised for food.

The sources name two dishes: *lạp siềng* and *lò nùng*.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Bouyei language article describes literary and written traditions associated with the Bouyei people. Ancient Bouyei writing borrowed elements from Chinese characters or imitated their forms. Collected texts included shaman’s books, Nuo books, and literary works. The epic poem *Wang Yulian* is described as a literary work believed to retell a Chinese story in the Bouyei language, with manual copies popular in Zhexiang Township, Wangmo County, in the Buyei and Miao Autonomous Prefecture in Southwest Guizhou.

## Language & religion

The Giáy speak Bouyei, a member of the Tai language family. The language article classifies Bouyei within the Northern Tai group of the Tai language branch of the Tai–Kadai language family. The Giáy language article states that Bouyei is also spoken in northern Vietnam by the Bouyei people and the Giáy. Giáy literacy in their native language is low, while most Giáy can also speak and write Vietnamese, learned in primary school.

The sources describe Bouyei as generally monosyllabic, with word order and particles as major grammatical forms. A contemporary Bouyei script was designed from 1981 to 1985, using the Wangmo County dialect as its foundation.

Religiously, the Giáy traditionally worship ancestors and animistic spirits associated with the heavens, soil, kitchen, and village. Sacred *đoong xía* groves and twice-yearly offerings are part of this documented practice.
