## Overview

The Muna or Wuna are an indigenous group primarily inhabiting the islands of Muna and Buton in Indonesia. Muna speakers are distributed across Muna Island and the west coast of Buton Island, including Muna Regency, West Muna, Buton, North Buton, Central Buton, and Bau-Bau City. Their language belongs to the Muna–Buton subgroup of the Celebic subgroup within the Austronesian family. Muna is spoken principally on Muna Island and north-west Buton Island, off the southeast coast of Sulawesi. In 2010, it had around 270,000 speakers. The sources document Muna cultural practices including the Kaghati Kolope leaf kite, the Karia coming-of-age ritual, the Kasambu pregnancy tradition, and beliefs surrounding pregnancy.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Woven sarong motifs** — Woven sarong motifs found on East Nusa Tenggara and Muna Island share features including horizontal lines and the basic colours yellow, green, red, and black.

**Headwear** — The shape of Muna headwear is described as resembling headwear found in East Nusa Tenggara.

**Motif vocabulary.** Horizontal lines; yellow, green, red, and black as basic colours.

### Clothing & dress

The sources mention woven sarongs, headwear, dark brown skin, and curly or wavy hair.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They state that pregnant women keep garlic with them and wear protective pins against malevolent influences.

## Music & performance

They describe the *Kaghati Kolope* as a traditional kite used by farmers for entertainment while looking after their fields.

## Festivals & rituals

Muna communities have ceremonies corresponding to stages of life, beginning with birth and ending with death. The sources specifically describe the *Karia* and *Kasambu* rituals.

*Karia* is a coming-of-age ritual through which women pass from childhood to adulthood. Women perform it at the age of 15–16 by secluding themselves for 96 hours. With the assistance of a *Pomantoto*, they undertake twelve processions involving instruction on parenting and Muna virtues, including social unity, self-realization, and caretaking. The sources state that, in Muna society, a woman should not be married if she has not undergone Karia. If the seclusion is interrupted, the woman may face exclusion and ostracism within her community. Among the Butonese people, the same transition is referred to as *Pusuo*.

*Kasambu* is a transition ritual held in Southeast Sulawesi before the birth of a couple’s first child, most often during the 7th or 8th month. Before this stage of pregnancy, husband and wife bathe together. The procession begins with the spouses feeding each other; the food must be finished, and any remainder is given to children present. The rest of the family then continues the feeding. The ritual brings together the families of the future parents, introduces the unborn child to the family circle, and expresses wishes for the child and for the safety of mother and child during delivery. A priest or imam ends the procession with a prayer.

Pregnancy is considered a blessing and may be celebrated by family members and sometimes neighbours. Popular Muna belief holds that pregnant women are especially susceptible to malevolent influences attracted by the smell of pregnancy.

## Foodways

The sources mention food only in connection with the Kasambu ritual. During its procession, spouses feed each other, and the food must be finished; any remainder is given to children present before the rest of the family continues the act of feeding.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that the *Pomantoto* instructs women during Karia on parenting and Muna virtues, including social unity, self-realization, and caretaking.

## Language & religion

Muna is an Austronesian language belonging to the Muna–Buton subgroup, a branch of the Celebic subgroup. Within Muna–Buton, it is the largest member of the Munic subbranch, which also includes Pancana, Kioko, Liabuku, Kaimbulawa, and Busoa. Muna has a dialect web with other languages of Sulawesi and Buton.

The sources identify three dialect areas: Standard Muna, the Tiworo dialect, and southern Muna, which includes the Siompu subdialect and various Gumas dialects. Standard Muna is spoken in the northern and central parts of Muna Island and on the north-western coast of Buton Island; in 1989 it had around 150,000 speakers. Tiworo is spoken in the north-western Tikep district with about 10,000 speakers. Siompu has around 7,000 speakers, while the Gumas dialects have around 60,000 speakers.
