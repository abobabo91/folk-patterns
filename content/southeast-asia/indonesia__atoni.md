## Overview

The Atoni, also known as the Atoin Meto, Atoin Pah Meto, or Dawan, are an ethnic group on Timor, living in Indonesian West Timor and the East Timorese enclave of Oecussi-Ambeno. They number around 844,030 and speak Uab Meto, also called Dawan, an Austronesian language cluster. Their villages generally contain 50 to 60 people and are surrounded by stone fences or shrubs, with fields and cattle cages on the periphery. Atoni culture is documented here especially through its spatial symbolism: houses express a gender dichotomy and a social order in which paired principles such as sun and earth, light and dark, and inner and outer structure the built environment.

### Architecture

Atoni villages consist of 50 to 60 people, with each village surrounded by a stone fence or shrubs. Fields and cattle cages stand on the periphery, while houses usually form a circular cluster or follow the road after the introduction of a road.

The Atoni house is organized through a male-female spatial principle. The right side of the house when facing the door is male, while the left is female; the center and attic are male, while the periphery is female. The interior is female and the terrace male, while the house is female and the yard male. This arrangement presents the house as a microcosmos and expresses social order.

A more elaborate house is called *Ume Atoni*. The Atoni entertain guests in a communal house called *Lopo*, which is always placed in front of a house and oriented toward the road. The sources also state that cardinal directions and different parts of the house are associated with gender.

## Language & religion

Uab Meto, also called Dawan, is an Austronesian language cluster spoken by the Atoni in the Indonesian region of West Timor and in the East Timorese municipality of Oecussi-Ambeno. In East Timor and other Portuguese-speaking countries it is often called Baikenu, although the supplied source says that this term more narrowly refers to the variety spoken in East Timor, which has been more influenced by Portuguese. The sources mention dialectal variation, including a phonemic /r/ heard in place of /l/ among dialects.
