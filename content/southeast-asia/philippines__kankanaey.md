## Overview

The Kankanaey are an Indigenous people of northern Luzon in the Philippines and belong to the Igorot peoples of the Cordillera. They live in western Mountain Province, northern Benguet, northeastern La Union and southeastern Ilocos Sur; communities also occur in other parts of the Philippines, including Cebu, Iloilo, Negros, Sultan Kudarat and Lanao del Norte. The 2010 Philippines census counted 362,833 self-identifying Kankanaey and 67,763 self-identifying Applai. The Applai of western Mountain Province are also associated with Sagada and Besao. Kankanaey is a South-Central Cordilleran language of the Austronesian family. Northern and Southern Kankanaey communities maintain distinctive social systems, rituals, clothing, houses, agricultural practices, musical instruments and funerary customs.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources describe woven garments and textiles.

**Wanes** — The *wanes* is a rectangular woven cloth or loincloth worn by men around the waist and between the legs. Among the Kankanaeys of Besao and Sagada, its design may vary according to social status or municipality.

**Tapis** — The *tapis* is a native woven skirt worn by women from the waist to the knees. It is held with a belt called *bagket*; descriptions of Kankanaey women’s dress identify combinations of black, white and red, while other accounts describe white with occasional dark blue.

**Woven blanket** — Southern Kankanaey men traditionally wear a woven blanket as an upper garment, sometimes together with a headband.

### Clothing & dress

Men traditionally wear the *wanes*, a woven loincloth or rectangular cloth wrapped around the waist and between the legs. One description notes that it is tightened at the back, while both ends hang loose at the front and back to provide additional cover. Southern Kankanaey men may also wear a woven blanket as an upper garment and sometimes a headband, usually colored red like the *wanes*. Women wear the *tapis*, a skirt wrapped around the lower body from the waist to the knees. It is secured with a thick belt called *bagket* or tucked into its upper edges. Descriptions of soft-speaking Kankanaey women’s dress emphasize black, white and red in the upper garment and skirt. Hard-speaking Kankanaey women’s dress is described as mainly red and black with some white.

### Architecture

Northern Kankanaey houses include the two-story *innagamang*, *binang-iyan*, *tinokbob* and elevated *tinabla*. Other buildings are the *agamang* granary, the male clubhouse called *dap-ay* or *abong*, and the female dormitory called *ebgan*. The *dap-ay* or *abong* is also the center of political, religious and social activity, with each one enjoying a degree of autonomy.

Southern Kankanaey house types include *binang-iyan*, described as a box-like compartment on four posts five feet high; *apa* or *inalpa*, a temporary shelter; *inalteb*, with a gabled roof and shorter eaves that allow windows and other openings at the side; and *allao*, a temporary field shelter. The *at-ato* or *dap-ay* is a men’s clubhouse or dormitory with a long, low, gable-roofed structure and a single entrance and exit. The women’s equivalent is the *ebgang* or *olog*. The sources also state that Kankanaey houses resemble other Igorot houses and reflect social status.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Kankanaey households are described as sparsely furnished. Objects include the *bangkito* or *tokdowan*, the *po-ok* storage box for rice and wine, clay pots and the carved bowl called *sokong*. Baskets are made from woven rattan, bamboo or *anes* and occur in different shapes and sizes.

Named tools include the *sagad* harrow, the *alado* plow dragged by a carabao, *sinowan*, and the *sanggap* and *kagitgit* digging tools. The sources also mention Chinese jars called *gosi* and copper gongs called *gangsa*. In Mankayan, copper and gold mining involves crushing ore with a large flat stone called *gai-dan*, separating gold with a water trough called *sabak* and *dayasan*, and melting it into gold cakes.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Both Southern Kankanaey men and women wear bead leglets, copper or shell earrings and beads made from copper coin. Tattoos function as body ornaments and “garments.” Ancient tattoos are documented on mummified Cordilleran remains from cave and hanging-coffin burials in northern Luzon. The sources describe tattoos covering the arms of female adults and the whole bodies of adult males. A Kankanaey mummy nicknamed “Apo Anno” is described as having tattoos on the soles of the feet and fingertips. Tattoo patterns were also carved on coffins. Tattooing continued until the mid-20th century, when modernization and conversion to Christianity made the tradition extinct among the Kankanaey.

## Music & performance

Kankanaey musical instruments include the *solibao* tubular drum, *gangsa* brass or copper gongs, *piwpiw* Jew’s harp, *kalaleng* nose flute and *agaldang* bamboo-wood guitar. Northern Kankanaey courtship may involve the man singing *day-eng*, or serenading the woman with an *awiding* harp, *diw-as* panpipe or *kalelleng* nose flute.

Music also forms part of funerary practice. Northern Kankanaey death rituals include *baya-o*, the singing of a dirge by three men; *menbaya-o*, an elegy; and *dedeg*, the song of the dead. These performances occur alongside offerings, vigils and the carrying of the body to its resting place. Southern Kankanaey funerals involve chanting dirges and vigils over a period lasting up to ten days.

## Dance & theatre

The Kankanaey dances named in the sources are *tayaw*, *pattong*, *balangbang*, *sakkuting*, *pinanyuan* and *bogi-bogi*. *Tayaw* is a community dance usually performed at weddings; it is also danced by the Ibaloi people, though with a different style. *Pattong* is a community dance from Mountain Province, with a style that varies by municipality. *Balangbang* is described as the modern word for *pattong*. *Pinanyuan* is a wedding dance, while *bogi-bogi* is a courtship dance.

## Festivals & rituals

They document life-cycle rituals, especially courtship, marriage and funerals.

Among Northern Kankanaey communities, courtship may begin with *day-eng*. If the parents agree to the marriage, the man and woman exchange work through *dok-ong* and *ob-obbo*. They then perform the preliminary marriage ritual called *pasya* and exchange food. The marriage celebration, *dawak* or *bayas*, includes *segep*, *pakde*, *betbet*, *playog* or *kolay*, *tebyag*, *mensupot*, *sekat di tawid* and *buka* or *inga*. The sources explain these as entering, sacrifice, pig butchering for omens, the marriage ceremony proper, merrymaking, gift giving, giving of inheritance and the end of the celebration. Adultery is forbidden, and a married couple cannot separate once a child is born.

Southern Kankanaey courtship includes *sabangan*, when the couple announces their wish to marry to their families. Parents discuss marriage terms, including the bride price. On the wedding day, a pig is butchered and its bile inspected for omens. The celebration usually lasts until the next day, but the bride and groom remain separated until they move to their own home.

Funerary customs include *sangadil* pre-interment rituals, burial in limestone caves and hanging-coffin interment. Northern rituals include *sangbo*, *baya-o*, *menbaya-o*, *sedey* and *dedeg*. Southern funerals may last up to ten days, with a pig sacrificed for each day of the vigil.

## Foodways

Northern Kankanaey wet-rice agriculture includes two rice varieties called *topeng* and *ginolot*. *Topeng* is planted in June and July and harvested in November and December; *ginolot* is planted in November and December and harvested in June and July. Northern communities also cultivate camote or sweet potato. Camote foods include *makimpit*, dried camotes; *boko*, camote cut into thin pieces; *sinalopsop*, steamed camote; *inab-abos-sang*, camote cooked and sweetened with sugar; and *kineykey*, camote added to rice before cooking. Squash, cucumber and other climbing vines are also planted.

Northern Kankanaey people hunt and fish small fish and eel. Crabs are used to make *tengba*, a gravy of pounded rice mixed with crabs, salted and aged in jars. The sources describe this as a common household viand eaten during childbirth.

Southern Kankanaey foodways include wet-rice agriculture, hunting and foraging. Their rice varieties are *kintoman* and *saranay* or *bayag*. *Kintoman* is also known as red rice, while *saranay* is whitish and small-grained. Wild animal meat, including deer, boar, civet cats and lizards, is salted and dried. Wild roots, honey and fruits supplement the diet. Eel, small river fish, crabs and other crustaceans are caught. Pigs, chickens, dogs and cattle are domesticated. Dog meat is described as a delicacy, while pigs and chickens are used mainly for ceremonial activities. A blood sausage called *pinuneg* is eaten by the Kankanaey people.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do describe sung and spoken forms connected with courtship, marriage and funerals, including *day-eng*, *baya-o*, *menbaya-o* and *dedeg*. Southern Kankanaey native priests called *mansip-ok*, *manbunong* and *mankotom* remember genealogies, while village elders know custom law and lead decision-making.

## Language & religion

Kankanaey is a South-Central Cordilleran language in the Austronesian family. It is spoken primarily on Luzon in the Philippines and is widely used by Cordillerans alongside Ilocano. It has slight mutual intelligibility with Ilocano. Named dialects include Mankayan-Buguias, Kapangan, Bakun-Kibungan and Guinzadan; Northern Kankanaey is listed as a separate language. Kankanaey is spoken in northern Benguet, southwestern Mountain Province, southeastern Ilocos Sur, northeastern La Union, southwestern Ifugao and northwest Nueva Vizcaya.

Northern Kankanaey beliefs include gods, spirits, omens, the *anito* or soul of the dead, and nature spirits. Southern Kankanaey accounts name Adikaila of the Skyworld, Kabunyan, Lumawig and Kabigat, ancestral spirits called *ap-apo* or *kakkading*, and earth spirits called *anito*. Ritual specialists supervise ceremonies, read omens and heal the sick. Rituals include *pedit*, *pasang* and *pakde*. The sources also state that Christianity contributed to cultural change and the disappearance of tattooing.
