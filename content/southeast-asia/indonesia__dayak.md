## Overview

The Dayak are Austronesian peoples living in Borneo's interior across Indonesia, Malaysia, and Brunei. They comprise over 200 ethnic groups speaking roughly 170 languages. Their material culture centers on textiles, beadwork, metalwork, and the iconic longhouse. They practice Kaharingan animism, though many have converted to Christianity or Islam.

## Material culture

Dayak material culture blends woven textiles, beadwork, metalwork, and carved wood into a cohesive artistic system tied to cosmology and achievement.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Dayak are master weavers of ikat cloth, using backstrap looms and natural dyes to create spiritually charged ceremonial textiles.

- **Pua kumbu** — An Iban ceremonial warp-ikat cotton blanket woven on a backstrap loom, dyed with indigo and Morinda red, used to receive newborns and wrap heirlooms.
- **Iban ikat skirts and jackets** (*kain kebat*) — Broader Iban warp-ikat tradition with dense, curvilinear patterning, where the weaver's progression through motifs mirrors a male warrior's headhunting grades.
- **Supplementary-weft textiles** (*sungkit*) — Iban cloths with raised red or black patterning on plain ground, considered even more spiritually charged than pua kumbu and historically used to wrap trophy heads.
- **Dayak beadwork panels** (*pesaka*) — Glass-bead panels on baby carriers, sun-hats, and skirts using red, yellow, white, turquoise, and black beads; heirloom beads are inherited across generations.
- **Dayak barkcloth** (*kapuo / ku'ut*) — Beaten bark from Artocarpus and Antiaris trees, formerly everyday cloth for interior groups and still used ceremonially.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress is minimal in the hot forest, but ceremonial dress transforms the body with feathered headdresses, layered silver, and heavy woven skirts.

- **Men's loincloth** (*sirat / cawat*) — Everyday wear made of beaten bark or cotton; ceremonially paired with a hornbill-feather headdress and jacket.
- **Women's wrap-skirt** (*bidang / ta'a*) — Short everyday skirt; toplessness was the norm for women until the 20th century.
- **Iban man's ceremonial jacket** (*baju burung*) — Formal jacket worn with a hornbill-feather headdress and sword, part of warrior display dress.
- **Iban woman's ceremonial dress** (*ngepan*) — Layered silver ensemble including a crown, coin belts, and beaded corset, worn with a heavy woven skirt.
- **Elongated earlobes with brass rings** (*hisang*) — Standard female beauty ideal until the 1960s, with up to twenty rings per ear worn by married women.

### Architecture

The signature Dayak building is the longhouse—a raised, communal ironwood structure over 200 meters long, divided into family apartments and a shared gallery.

- **Iban longhouse** (*rumah panjai*) — A raised communal structure of ironwood posts and planks, roofed with palm or ironwood shingles, divided into a public gallery and private family apartments.
- **Ngaju longhouse** (*betang*) — The Ngaju version of the raised communal longhouse structure.
- **Kenyah/Kayan longhouse** (*lamin / uma dado'*) — Regional variations of the longhouse used by Kenyah, Benuaq, and Kayan peoples.
- **Ngaju bone-house** (*sandung*) — A freestanding structure on carved pillars used to house the bones of the secondary dead.
- **Anthropomorphic sacrificial post** (*sapundu*) — A carved freestanding post used in ritual sacrifice ceremonies.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Dayak imported Chinese stoneware jars as paramount heirlooms and forged distinctive swords, blowpipes, and gong sets.

- **Chinese stoneware jar** (*tajau / tempayan*) — Imported heirloom jars traded from Tang to Ming periods, used to store rice wine, ferment offerings, and hold bones of the secondary dead.
- **Dayak sword** (*mandau / parang ilang*) — A soft-iron blade with a hard-iron cutting strip forge-welded together, fitted with a deer-antler or hardwood hilt carved in dragon motifs.
- **Blowpipe** (*sumpit*) — A drilled ironwood tube up to two meters long, fitted with a spear-blade and shooting darts poisoned with Antiaris latex.
- **Bronze gong ensemble** (*tawak / bebendai / engkerumong*) — Imported hanging bossed gongs and small-gong racks that accompany feasts and warrior displays.
- **Woven rattan basket** (*ingen / ambin*) — Carrying baskets woven from rattan, part of the household inventory.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver and brass jewelry is worn in bulk on ceremonial occasions, but tattooing is the most elaborate body art, marking achievement and rank.

- **Silver corset** (*marik empang*) — A beaded and silver-coin corset worn by Iban women as part of ceremonial dress.
- **Silver crown** (*sugu tinggi*) — A layered silver headdress worn by Iban women in ceremonial dress.
- **Layered coin belts** (*rawai*) — Stacked coin discs worn by Iban women as part of ceremonial dress.
- **Shoulder tattoo rosette** (*bunga terung*) — An eggplant-flower pattern that marks the beginning of a man's tattoo cycle, earned through the bejalai journey.
- **Thigh and forearm tattoos** (*tato / tutang*) — Dense tattoos worn by Kayan and Kenyah women to mark rank, applied with soot-blackened thorns.

## Music & performance

The sape boat-lute is the signature Dayak instrument, played for healing ceremonies and festival welcomes alongside gongs, mouth-organs, and xylophones.

- **Boat-lute** (*sape / sapeh*) — A two- to four-stringed instrument carved from a single block of wood, originally strung with rattan or palm fiber, now the pan-Dayak choice for international audiences.
- **Mouth-organ** (*keledi / keluri*) — A gourd and bamboo pipe instrument played at ceremonies.
- **Wooden xylophone** (*jatung utang*) — A wooden xylophone hung between longhouse posts.
- **Bamboo tube-zither** (*pagang*) — A bamboo stringed instrument.
- **Ritual poetry chant** (*timang / pengap*) — Invocations chanted by a bard through an entire night of the gawai festival, narrating tales of gods and heroes.

## Dance & theatre

Dayak performance is danced rather than staged, ranging from slow hornbill imitations to warrior dances with swords and shields to masked spirit dances.

- **Hornbill-imitation dance** (*datun julud*) — A slow solo dance for Kenyah women, arms extended with fans of hornbill tail-feathers strapped to the fingers.
- **Warrior dance** (*ngajat*) — An Iban dance performed with sword and shield, culminating in a leap and stylized beheading motion; a female version is more contained.
- **Circle dance** (*manasai / giring-giring*) — Communal circle dances danced by Ngaju and Bakumpai peoples, shaken to bamboo stampers.
- **Masked spirit dance** (*hudoq*) — Ritual masked dance of the Bahau, Modang, and Busang with enormous carved-wood masks representing rice-pest spirits, danced through the fields at planting.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual calendar tracks the rice cycle, with the Iban Gawai Dayak harvest festival and the Ngaju Tiwah secondary mortuary festival as major events.

- **Harvest festival** (*Gawai Dayak*) — Held 1 June each year and an official Sarawak holiday since 1965, closing the rice harvest with wine offerings, rites, and all-night ritual chanting.
- **Secondary mortuary festival** (*Tiwah*) — An Ngaju and Ot Danum festival held years after burial, where bones are exhumed, washed, and installed in a carved bone-house with buffalo sacrifice.
- **Rice-blessing masked dance** (*hudoq*) — Kenyah, Kayan, and Bahau rites at rice-planting featuring masked dancers representing rice-pest spirits and ancestral protectors.
- **Male coming-of-age journey** (*bejalai*) — An obligatory journey away from the longhouse for a young man to gain experience, wealth, and tattoos before returning.
- **Headhunting cessation agreement** (*Tumbang Anoi Agreement*) — An 1894 inter-tribal assembly in Central Kalimantan that formally ended headhunting raids across the Dayak world.

## Foodways

Hill rice grown by swidden is the staple, often cooked in bamboo tubes; forest fish, game, and bitter jungle ferns supplement the diet.

- **Bamboo-cooked rice** (*lemang / nasi pansuh*) — Rice cooked inside a green bamboo tube lined with banana leaf and roasted directly on the fire, sometimes stuffed with chicken and lemongrass.
- **Forest starches** (*sago / cassava*) — Sago flour from Eugeissona palm, cassava, and tubers supplement hill rice as staples.
- **Fermented fish preserve** (*kasam*) — Freshwater fish from major rivers fermented with rice into a sour preserve.
- **Ceremonial rice wine** (*tuak*) — Rice wine fermented in heirloom jars and served from a communal cup at festivals and to welcome longhouse guests.
- **Betel-nut chewing** (*sirih pinang*) — Betel-nut preparation that accompanies negotiation and courtship.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Iban preserve one of Southeast Asia's most extensive ritual oral literatures, chanted by bards through festivals, with folktales, riddles, and proverbs for children.

- **Epic ritual invocation** (*timang gawai*) — Epic invocations chanted by the bard to summon gods to the longhouse festival.
- **Warrior hero narratives** (*pengap*) — Narratives of the hornbill war-god Sengalang Burong and his warrior sons-in-law.
- **Sung dialogue poetry** (*renong*) — Iban sung poetry dialogues performed at festivals.
- **Creation cosmology scripture** (*Panaturan*) — An Ngaju scripture codified in 1971 that gathers creation myths in which the Tree of Life connects Upper-world and Under-world.
- **Genealogical chant** (*takna'*) — Kayan and Kenyah chants that recite ancestry back to the founding of a longhouse.

## Language & religion

Dayak languages fall into several Malayo-Polynesian branches with roughly 170 tongues; the indigenous religion is Kaharingan animism, though Christian and Muslim conversion is widespread.

- **Animist cosmology** (*Kaharingan*) — An indigenous system focused on a stratified cosmos (Upper-world, middle earth, Under-world) mediated by shamans who undertake ecstatic soul-flight.
- **Ritual specialist** (*balian / manang*) — A shaman who undertakes ecstatic soul-flight to heal illness and escort the dead.
- **Kaharingan temple** (*balai basarah*) — A prayer hall standardized after 1980 in Christianized areas that now serves as the institutional temple for Kaharingan.
- **Dayak language groups** (*Land Dayak / Malayic / Sabahan / Barito*) — Four Malayo-Polynesian subgroups that comprise the roughly 170 Dayak languages on Borneo.
