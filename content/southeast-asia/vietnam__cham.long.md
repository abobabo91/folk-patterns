## Overview

The Cham (*Urang Campa*) are an Austronesian people indigenous to the coastal lowlands of central and southern Vietnam and to the Mekong basin of Cambodia. Once the ruling population of Champa — a maritime confederation of Hindu-Buddhist principalities that flourished from the 2nd century until its final annexation by Emperor Minh Mạng in 1832 — they now number roughly 400,000 across the region, with dense concentrations in Ninh Thuận and Bình Thuận provinces (the historic Panduranga), the Mekong Delta province of An Giang, and Kampong Cham in Cambodia. A small offshoot, the Utsuls, lives on Hainan. Their language belongs to the Chamic branch of Malayo-Polynesian, making them the only Austronesian group whose homeland lies on the Southeast Asian mainland. They matter in folk-culture terms because they preserve one of the few living matrilineal Hindu traditions in mainland Southeast Asia (the *Ahier*), alongside a distinctive Bani Islamic strand and a weaving tradition traceable to the Champa royal ateliers.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Cham brocade weaving of Mỹ Nghiệp** — The village of *Mỹ Nghiệp* (*Ca Klaing*) near Phan Rang is the principal surviving center of Cham supplementary-weft brocade. Weavers use a body-tensioned frame loom to produce heavy cotton and silk cloths in which coloured wefts (traditionally indigo, madder red, and turmeric yellow) float over a dark ground to form geometric registers.

**Bàu Trúc–adjacent loom cloth (*aban*)** — The *aban*, a tubular wrap-skirt cloth, is the everyday format of Cham weaving; the finest examples carry banded end-panels with dense pattern registers reserved for ritual and wedding use.

**Ceremonial sashes and shoulder cloths (*khan matơm*, *khan djram*)** — Long narrow sashes worn by priests of the Ahier and Bani clergy, woven with reserved motif bands at each end and a plain field in the middle.

**Ikat-patterned skirts of Panduranga** — A minor but documented weft-ikat tradition survives alongside brocade, used mostly for women's ceremonial *aban*.

**Cham temple carving as pattern source** — The sandstone reliefs of Mỹ Sơn, Po Klong Garai, and Po Nagar — with their *makara*, *kala*, dancing *apsara*, and lotus-scroll registers — supply the vocabulary that Cham weavers still reproduce in thread. Motif transfer between temple stucco and textile is a defining feature of the tradition.

**Turban and headcloth weaving** — Long white cotton *khan* for men and coloured headcloths for married women are woven separately from body cloth, often with a red or yellow stripe indicating priestly rank.

**Motif vocabulary.** *bingu kalik* (spiral/scroll), *bingu jôm mưh* (lotus rosette), *bingu on* (turtle back diamond), *bingu tho* (elephant), *bingu kachak* (dragon/*naga*), *bingu manuk* (bird), *bingu kayau* (tree of life), *makara* (aquatic monster), *apsara* (celestial dancer), *swastika* rosette (from temple lintels).

### Clothing & dress

The everyday Cham woman's dress is the *aw kamei*, an ankle-length tunic worn over the tubular *aban* skirt, secured with a woven belt (*taley ka-in*). Married women bind their hair under a headcloth (*khan*); Bani women in An Giang wear a longer white veil closer to a Malay *tudung*, reflecting centuries of Malacca-Kelantan contact. Men wear a long white or indigo tunic (*aw tah*) with loose trousers or a wrap-skirt, and a white turban (*khan matơm*) whose folding indicates whether the wearer is lay, *acar* (Bani cleric), or *paseh* (Ahier priest). Priestly dress is elaborate: the *Po Adhia*, the highest Ahier priest, wears a white robe embroidered at the hem with red and yellow *bingu* bands, a broad woven sash, and a tall white turban topped with a tassel. Wedding costume for both sexes uses the densest brocade a household can afford, with the bride's *aban* and shoulder cloth carrying full-register pattern along both ends.

### Architecture

The great monuments of Cham material culture are the brick *kalan* — the tower-sanctuaries of the Champa temple complexes such as **Mỹ Sơn** (Quảng Nam), **Po Nagar** (Nha Trang), **Po Klong Garai** and **Po Rome** (Ninh Thuận). Built of fine kiln-fired brick set with a vegetal-resin mortar whose exact composition is still debated, they carry a square cella, a corbelled false-vault interior, and an exterior articulated by pilasters, false doors, and register upon register of carved sandstone. Roofs step upward in three or four diminishing storeys, each with miniature repeats of the sanctuary below and finials in the form of *linga*, lotus buds, or *makara* heads. Vernacular domestic architecture is more modest: the traditional Panduranga Cham compound (*sang*) is a cluster of low timber houses on short piles, thatched with palm leaf, with the *sang mư yâu* (the eastern "mother house") reserved for the matrilineal ancestor altar. Bani mosques (*sang magik*) in Ninh Thuận are single-storey structures with tiled hip roofs, closer in form to a Cham house than to a domed West Asian mosque, while An Giang riverine Cham build wooden stilt houses along the Mekong and its arms.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Cham potting village of **Bàu Trúc** (*Paley Hamu Trok*), a few kilometers from Phan Rang, is one of the oldest continuously functioning ceramic villages in mainland Southeast Asia. Its potters — women, by matrilineal transmission — build vessels without a wheel, walking backwards around the pot as they paddle and smooth the clay, then open-fire the ware in bonfires of rice straw and cow dung. The output is unglazed earthenware: water jars, cooking pots, incense burners, and ritual figurines in a warm russet ground streaked black from reduction. Cham metalwork is less industrialised but includes bronze and silver ritual vessels for temple offerings, small silver amulets, and the *kris*-like ceremonial daggers of the priesthood.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Cham women wear silver and gold ear ornaments, plain hoop earrings for daily use and elongated pendant earrings (*ana taney*) for ceremony. Ahier priestesses (*muk pajau*) wear heavy silver hair pins and a beaded chest chain during rituals. Wedding jewelry — bracelets, neck rings, and a filigree hairpin — is inherited matrilineally along with the *aban* and the ancestor cloths. Tattooing is not a Cham practice; permanent body marking is largely absent. Bani women use henna sparingly at weddings, a practice absorbed from Malay Muslim contact rather than an old Cham element.

## Music & performance

Cham ceremonial music is inseparable from the ritual calendar. The core ensemble accompanying temple worship pairs two great goblet drums — the *ginang*, a double-headed barrel drum played in pairs and struck with one hand and one stick — with the *baranâng*, a shallow single-headed frame drum, and the *saranai*, a quadruple-reed shawm cognate with the Indian *shehnai* and the Khmer *sralai*. This *ginang*–*saranai* ensemble provides the sound of every Kate procession, Rija ceremony, and royal offering at the towers of Po Klong Garai and Po Rome. A separate ceremonial oboe, the *kanhi*, a two-stringed spike fiddle carved from a single block of jackfruit wood and often crowned with an *apsara* figure, is played solo by the *Ong Kadhar* — a hereditary singer who chants the invocation hymns to the god-kings during Ahier rites. Song genres include the priestly recitation *damnây* (invocatory hymn cycles addressed to individual deified kings and queens), *ariya* narrative songs in Cham verse, and lullabies (*dauh ru con*). Bani communities have a parallel repertoire of Qur'anic recitation and *doa* chant, in Cham-inflected liturgical Arabic. Court music proper died with the annexation of Panduranga in 1832, but its instruments and repertoire were partly absorbed into Vietnamese *nhã nhạc* and survive in temple use.

## Dance & theatre

The signature Cham dance is the *Apsara* solo, a slow lyrical form whose iconography — the bent-elbow *tribhanga* pose, the flared fingers, the raised heel — is drawn directly from Champa temple relief. The *Múa quạt* (fan dance) and the water-jar balancing dance *Múa đội lu* are performed by young women at Kate. The *Rija Nagar* (New Year Rija) and *Rija Praong* (Great Rija) ceremonies incorporate ecstatic possession dance by the *muk rija*, a female medium in whose body the ancestral kings speak; her costume alternates male and female garments as different spirits mount her. Masked and puppet theatre are not a Cham tradition — those forms belong to their Vietnamese and Khmer neighbours — but the Ong Kadhar's chanted recitation of royal genealogies functions as a sung dramatic form.

## Festivals & rituals

The **Kate festival** (*Katé*), held on the first day of the seventh month of the Cham lunar calendar (falling in September or October), is the largest annual gathering of the Ahier community. It opens with a procession bearing the sacred garments of the god-kings from the Raglai highland custodians down to the tower-sanctuaries of Po Klong Garai, Po Rome, and Po Nagar, where priests bathe the *mukhalinga*, dress it in the new robe, and offer *ginang*-led music through three days of celebration. **Cambur** in the ninth month is the parallel women's festival at the same towers. **Rija Nagar**, at the Cham new year in the first month, is a village-level renewal rite with possession-dance. Bani Cham observe **Ramawan** — a distinctive month-long observance in which only the *acar* clergy fast strictly, while lay Cham observe a modified fast and a visit to ancestral graves at the month's opening. Life-cycle rites include the *karoeh* girl's coming-of-age ceremony among Muslim Cham, a symbolic passage marking marriageability; symbolic circumcision performed with a wooden knife; the elaborate matrilineal wedding at the bride's mother's house; and secondary burial rites for Ahier dead, whose small forehead-bone fragments (*klaong*) are kept in a family reliquary (*kut*) as the true seat of the ancestor.

## Foodways

Cham cuisine sits between Vietnamese, Khmer, and Malay foodways. Rice is the staple, both plain and as glutinous cakes wrapped in banana leaf for offering. Signature dishes include *tung lò mò* (Cham fermented beef sausage, the flagship halal charcuterie of An Giang and now sold across Ho Chi Minh City), *cà púa* (a beef and coconut milk curry of the An Giang Cham), *cari cà ri* — a mild coconut curry showing the Malay-Indian route — and *ka-pua*, a fish-paste stew of Panduranga. Fish sauce is used, but An Giang Bani Cham keep a parallel halal kitchen and produce their own beef-based fermented sauces. Palm sugar and toddy from the sugar palm are important; the palm-sugar block of the Cham of Cambodia is a recognised regional product. Ceremonial food at Kate centres on chicken, sticky rice, banana, and betel offerings placed before the *mukhalinga*, along with three cups of rice wine. Dietary rules split sharply: the Ahier abstain from beef (a legacy of the Hindu heritage) while the Bani abstain from pork; the historical episode of Emperor Minh Mạng forcing lizard and pork on Muslim Cham and beef on Hindu Cham is remembered as the archetypal cultural violation.

## Oral tradition & literature

Cham literary culture is old: the **Đông Yên Châu inscription** (4th century, in Old Cham) is the earliest attested Austronesian text anywhere. The classical repertoire includes the *Akayet* — long verse romances such as *Akayet Dewa Mưno*, *Akayet Inra Patra*, and *Akayet Um Mưrup*, the last two adapted from Malay hikayat during the Champa–Malacca contact of the 15th–17th centuries. Shorter genres are the *ariya* (verse narrative), *damnây* (priestly hymn), *kabbon* (didactic verse), *pauh catwai* (proverb), and *panwơc pađit* (riddle). Genealogical chronicles of the kings (*sakarai*) survive in palm-leaf and paper manuscripts held in family and mosque libraries; the Kan Imam San mosques in Kampong Chhnang preserve the largest corpus in Western Cham *akhar srak* script. Contemporary revival is anchored by the Center for Cham Studies at Ho Chi Minh City's Institute of Social Sciences and by community publishing in Ninh Thuận.

## Language & religion

Cham is a Malayo-Polynesian language of the Chamic subgroup, with an Eastern dialect in Vietnam and a Western dialect in Cambodia and the Mekong Delta. It is written in three scripts: *akhar thrah*, the Brahmi-derived Eastern Cham script still taught in Ninh Thuận; *akhar srak*, the Western Cham script preserved by the Kan Imam San sect around Phnom Oudong; and Jawi Arabic script used by Muslim Cham of the Mekong. The religious landscape has three strands: the **Ahier** (a localised Hindu-Shaivite tradition centred on the *mukhalinga* and the deified god-kings), the **Bani** (a syncretic Cham Islam in which only the *acar* clergy carry the full ritual load and which retains matrilineal inheritance and pre-Islamic ancestor rites), and orthodox Sunni Islam (**Cham Bani Islam** proper, or *Islam Asli*), dominant in An Giang and Cambodia. Ahier and Bani communities in Panduranga historically intermarry and cooperate at agricultural rites, treating the two as gendered halves of a single cosmological system.
