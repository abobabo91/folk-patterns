## Overview

The Marind live in South Papua, Indonesia, around the Bian River. Their culture includes clans, myths, ancestor figures, headhunting traditions, ritual practices, and distinctive objects. Dema were mythological ancestors who introduced plants, animals, and cultural goods. Christian missions and schools brought major changes.

## Material culture

Marind material culture is known from arrows, drums, fibre head-dresses, mourner’s hoods, and bodily adornment.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources do not describe a full textile tradition, but they record plaited fibre objects and painted or incised designs.

- **Plaited fibre head-dress** — This head-dress has a curved band of plaited vegetable fibre, red and black pith pieces, and an upright cane rod.
- **Mourner’s hood** — This hood is made from plaited vegetable fibre with a long bark-fibre fringe and a dark brown, white, yellow, and red-brown design.
- **Fibre arrow bindings** — Vegetable fibre bindings join the foreshafts and cane shafts of arrows, sometimes with white or red pigment.

### Clothing & dress

The sources give only limited information about clothing, mainly ceremonial headwear and a photograph of a girl with anklets.

- **Ceremonial head-dress** — A plaited vegetable-fibre head-dress has red and black pith pieces and a cane rod in the middle.
- **Mourner’s hood** — A vegetable-fibre hood has a thick bark-fibre fringe and a multicoloured plaited design.
- **Age-group anklets** (*wahuku*) — A museum photograph shows a Marind girl with plaited anklets who was in the “wahuku”-age.

### Architecture

The sources do not describe Marind houses, buildings, settlements, or architectural decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources do not document ceramics or metalwork, but they describe arrows and hour-glass-shaped drums.

- **Carved arrow with bone point** — This arrow has a wooden foreshaft with lime-filled carving and a bone point marked with red ochre.
- **Triangular-pointed arrow** — This arrow has a wooden head ending in a triangular point and parallel lines at the butt.
- **Hour-glass drum** — Marind drums were cut from solid wood and had skin membranes, fibre attachments, and painted designs.
- **Handled drum** — One drum had a side handle, a skin membrane, vegetable fibre, feathers, and painted designs.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources offer limited evidence of bodily adornment, especially plaited anklets and fibre headwear.

- **Plaited anklets** — A museum photograph shows a Marind girl wearing plaited anklets.
- **Fibre head-dress** — The head-dress combines vegetable fibre, pith, cane, pigments, and painted or plaited designs.
- **Mourner’s hood** — The mourner’s hood combines vegetable fibre, bark fibres, pigments, and a long fringe.

## Music & performance

The sources show that drums were used in ceremonial imagery, but they do not name Marind song or drum traditions.

- **Hour-glass drum** — Marind drums were made from solid wood and had skin membranes, fibre attachments, and painted designs.
- **Ceremonial drumming** — A photograph record shows Marind-Anim men dressed for ceremony with drums.
- **Fish totem performance image** — A ceremonial image shows a man holding a carved wooden fish totem.

## Dance & theatre

The sources do not name Marind dances, theatre forms, mask performances, or choreographic traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

Marind ritual life included clan traditions, dema ancestor veneration, former headhunting, and ritual intercourse, but no annual festival calendar is given.

- **Clan organization** (*boan*) — Marind society had two moieties made up of patrilineal clans called boans, which were divided into subclans.
- **Ancestor heroes** (*dema*) — Dema were demon-like mythological ancestors who acted as culture heroes and introduced plants, animals, and cultural goods.
- **Headhunting** — Former headhunting was directed at other tribes, linked to naming newborns, and connected with the belief that a skull held a mana-like force.
- **Ritual intercourse** (*otiv-bombari*) — Ritual intercourse called otiv-bombari was associated with a girl’s wedding and with occasions after childbirth.

## Foodways

The sources mention sago or coconut palms but do not describe Marind cooking, dishes, or food rules.

- **Sago palms** — Sago palm trees were originally planted in Marind territory.
- **Coconut palms** — Coconut palm trees were originally planted in Marind territory.

## Oral tradition & literature

Marind oral tradition centers on dema myths and family traditions passed down through extended families and their big men.

- **Dema myths** (*dema*) — Myths describe dema as culture heroes who arranged the ancient world and introduced plants, animals, and cultural goods.
- **Family traditions** — Each extended family kept and transmitted its tradition, especially through the work of its big men.
- **Demon and headhunting stories** — Hans Nevermann’s book contains stories about demons and headhunting recorded in New Guinea.

## Language & religion

Marind is a Papuan language, and traditional beliefs focused on ancestors, dema, totems, and the power of captured skulls.

- **Marind language** — Marind is spoken in Malind District, Merauke Regency, Indonesia, and has several dialects.
- **Marind dialects** — The named dialects are Southeast Marind, Gawir, Holifoersch, and Tugeri.
- **Bian Marind** (*Boven-Mbian*) — Bian Marind, also called Boven-Mbian or Northwest Marind, is distinct enough to be mutually unintelligible with other Marind varieties.
- **Negative marker** (*mbya*) — The marker mbya means negative and also marks collectivity, or “all,” in noun phrases.
- **Skull force** — Traditional belief held that a captured skull contained a mana-like force.

## Glossary

- *wahuku* — an age group
- *boan* — a patrilineal clan
- *dema* — mythological ancestor and culture hero
- *otiv-bombari* — ritual intercourse
- *Boven-Mbian* — another name for Bian Marind
- *mbya* — negative and collective marker
