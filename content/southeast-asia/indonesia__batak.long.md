## Overview

The Batak are a cluster of closely related Austronesian-speaking peoples centered on the highlands around Lake Toba in North Sumatra, Indonesia, with populations extending into East Aceh Regency and Rokan Hulu in Riau. The collective name covers six principal subgroups — **Toba**, **Karo**, **Simalungun**, **Pakpak-Dairi**, **Angkola**, and **Mandailing** — each with its own dialect, adat (customary law), and ritual repertoire. Numbering roughly 8–9 million people (about 3.58% of Indonesia's population per the 2010 census), they speak languages within the Austronesian family split into a northern branch (Karo, Pakpak-Dairi, Alas-Kluet) and a southern branch (Toba, Angkola, Mandailing, with Simalungun as an early offshoot). In folk-culture terms, they matter for a distinctive script (*Surat Batak*), a highly developed weaving tradition centered on the ritual cloth *ulos*, monumental stone tombs, saddle-roofed timber houses, and one of Island Southeast Asia's most elaborate ancestor-and-soul cosmologies.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Batak weaving is dominated by the *ulos*, a warp-faced supplementary-weft cotton cloth produced on a backstrap loom and given ritual life through the *dalihan na tolu* kinship system: cloth passes from wife-givers (*hulahula*) to wife-takers (*boru*) at every major life-cycle event. Unlike neighboring Minangkabau *songket* (weft-supplementary with metallic thread) or Acehnese embroidery, Batak textiles are typically indigo, deep red-brown, and black, with narrow supplementary-weft bands and long twisted warp fringes.

**Ulos Ragidup** — the "cloth of life," the most sacred Toba *ulos*, distinguished by three separately woven panels joined so the central field carries a distinct pattern from the borders; given by the bride's father to the groom's mother.

**Ulos Ragi Hotang** — the "rattan-pattern" *ulos*, given to a newly married couple as *ulos hela* to bind them like tightly wound rattan; the ground is dark with fine warp stripes and a supplementary-weft central band.

**Ulos Sadum** — the most colorful Batak cloth, woven with polychrome supplementary weft on a red or white ground, associated with Angkola and Toba communities and given as a cloth of blessing (*ulos pansamot*).

**Ulos Mangiring** — a "following" cloth patterned with small paired motifs, presented to a first grandchild and later used to carry the infant, symbolising the wish that more children will follow.

**Ulos Sibolang** — a mourning and widow's cloth in indigo with tie-resist bands of white, used at funerals and to mark a woman's transition to widowhood.

**Ulos Bintang Maratur** — the "orderly stars" *ulos*, with rows of small star-like supplementary-weft motifs arranged in regular files; used at housewarmings and blessings.

**Karo uis** — the Karo counterpart to Toba *ulos*, including *uis nipes*, *uis gara* (a red-and-black cloth used as a shoulder cloth and head-cloth), and *uis julu*, generally narrower and with a more restrained palette of indigo, madder, and undyed cotton.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Ragi* (pattern/plaid), *hotang* (rattan), *bintang* (star), *pinar* (motif), *sitompi* (buffalo yoke), *iran-iran* (zigzag), *desa na ualu* (eight compass directions), *gorga* (carved/painted scroll motif transferred from architecture), *jugia* (a rare sacred ground pattern), and *mangiring* (following/echoing pairs).

### Clothing & dress

Everyday Batak dress in the highlands has largely converged with common Indonesian styles — sarong, shirt, and *kebaya* — but ceremonial dress remains sharply distinct. Men wear a wrapped lower cloth with an *ulos* draped over one shoulder or knotted across the chest, and a folded head-cloth: the Toba *tali-tali* or the Karo *bulang-bulang*, tied with peaked ends that identify the wearer's subgroup. Women wear an *ulos* as sarong (*ulos ni abit*), a second as breast-cloth or shoulder wrap (*hoba-hoba* / *hande-hande*), and a third folded on the head — the Karo *tudung*, a heavy folded red cloth pinned into a broad crown, is the most recognisable. At weddings the bride and groom are wrapped together in the *ulos hela*. Priests (*datu*) historically wore a distinctive shoulder cloth and carried a magic staff. Footwear was not traditional; sandals and shoes are modern additions.

### Architecture

The signature Toba house, *ruma* (also *jabu*), is a saddle-roofed timber pile-dwelling with a steeply sloping thatch or *ijuk* (sugar-palm fibre) roof whose ridge sweeps up at both ends into projecting gables. The rectangular body sits on massive hardwood posts, walls lean slightly outward, and the front gable is faced with carved and painted boards. This façade carries *gorga* — deeply incised curvilinear scrollwork painted in the ritual triad of red (from earth pigment), black (soot), and white (lime) — with named panels including *gorga singa-singa* (a lion-like guardian face), *gorga jenggar* (a large mask above the door), *ipon-ipon* (dentil-like tooth borders), and *desa na ualu* (an eight-directional cosmological rosette). Karo *rumah adat* differ: they are much larger multi-family longhouses, up to eight extended families under one roof, with a double-tiered roof crowned by projecting buffalo-horn finials. Villages (*huta*) were traditionally fenced, entered by a single gate, and paired the dwelling houses with a *sopo* (rice granary on posts, whose upper storey served as a guest and sleeping space) and, especially among the Toba, monumental stone sarcophagi (*sarkopagus*) carved with human and *singa* faces.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Batak are not known for a fine-ware ceramic tradition, but wood, bone, buffalo horn, and bamboo carving are central. The most iconic ritual object is the *pustaha*, a folded bark-book of tree bast with wooden covers, inscribed in *Surat Batak* by a *datu* and containing divinatory, medical, and magical knowledge. Divination calendars (*porhalaan*) were incised on bamboo. The *tunggal panaluan* is a tall carved magic staff, its shaft stacked with human and animal figures and topped with hair, feathers, and a stopper concealing *pupuk* (ritual substance). Medicine containers (*naga morsarang* / *guri-guri*) were made from buffalo horn carved into a *singa* or dragon and stoppered with a wooden rider figure. Everyday hardwood objects include rice mortars, weaving swords for the backstrap loom, and betel-nut containers; brass and low-grade silver were worked into small vessels and pipe fittings.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Batak jewellery is dominated by heavy neck and ear ornaments. The *padung-padung* is a massive spiral silver ear ornament worn historically by married Karo women, weighing over a kilogram and looped over the head-cloth for support. *Rante* neck chains and *sertali* forehead ornaments in gilded silver were part of Karo bridal dress. Beadwork collars and gold-plated diadems appear in Simalungun and Toba wedding regalia. Amulets are typically small horn or wooden pendants filled with *pupuk*. Filed and blackened teeth were practised historically as a mark of adulthood, and hair was worn in a knotted bun, often bound with an *ulos* strip or the Karo *tudung*.

## Music & performance

The core Batak ensemble is the *gondang*, which exists in two principal Toba forms: *gondang sabangunan*, the "great" outdoor ensemble built around a set of five tuned single-headed drums (*taganing*) played melodically by one drummer, a large bass drum (*gordang*), four bossed gongs (*ogung*), a small time-keeping gong (*hesek*), and the double-reed *sarune bolon*; and *gondang hasapi*, an indoor chamber ensemble featuring the two-stringed boat-lute *hasapi*, the *sulim* transverse bamboo flute, the *garantung* wooden xylophone, and a small *sarune*. The Karo counterpart *gendang lima sedalanen* uses two *gendang* drums, two *penganak* small gongs, one large *gung*, and the *sarune* reed. Song genres include *andung* (Toba ritual lament), *ende* (sung poetry), and the responsorial choral singing that fed the 20th-century Batak trio tradition — a distinctive close-harmony guitar-and-voice style associated with figures such as Nahum Situmorang whose repertoire (*O Tao Toba*, *Sinanggar Tullo*) is now standard across Indonesia. Church music, especially HKBP Lutheran hymnody sung in four-part harmony, is itself a living Batak folk genre.

## Dance & theatre

The paradigmatic Batak dance is the *tortor*, performed to *gondang* music with stylised, restrained upper-body and hand movements: the dancer's feet barely leave the ground while the palms turn outward (*manortor*) in gestures of respect toward *hulahula* wife-givers. *Tortor* is not entertainment but ritual address, performed at weddings, funerals, and house consecrations, and each named piece (*gondang mula-mula*, *gondang somba*, *gondang liat-liat*) must be requested from the drummers in a fixed sequence. Among the Karo, the *guro-guro aron* is a courtship dance of young unmarried people. The *sigale-gale* is a wooden funerary puppet nearly life-sized, articulated with strings and made to dance the *tortor* at the funeral of a man who died without male heirs — historically found in the Samosir area of Lake Toba. Masked dance is less developed than in Bali or Java, but ritual *huda-huda* (Simalungun) uses a horse-headed effigy at mortuary rites.

## Festivals & rituals

The Batak ceremonial calendar is organised less around fixed annual festivals than around life-cycle events governed by *adat* and the *dalihan na tolu* triangle of *hulahula* (wife-givers), *boru* (wife-takers), and *dongan tubu* (agnates of the same *marga*). Birth is marked by the burial of the placenta beneath the house (regarded as the child's *saudara* or spirit sibling) and the presentation of an *ulos parompa* to carry the baby. Weddings (*pesta unjuk*) are the most elaborate event, extending over days, with the exchange of *ulos* and bride-price (*sinamot*) and the sequential invitation of *gondang* pieces. Funerals for a person who has reached full elder status with married descendants (*saur matua*) are festive rather than sombre, again featuring *tortor* and *gondang*. The most striking mortuary practice is *mangongkal holi*: the exhumation of ancestral bones some years after death and their transfer to a monumental family stone tomb (*tugu* or *tambak*), often accompanied by the sacrifice of buffalo. The Karo Pakpak *pekualuh* secondary cremation and the general *horja bius* (village-level community feast to petition the high god *Mula Jadi Na Bolon* for agricultural success) round out the ritual calendar. Christian and Muslim liturgical years (Christmas, Easter, Idul Fitri) are now overlaid on this framework.

## Foodways

Batak cooking is emphatic, sour, and hot, and — outside Muslim Mandailing and Angkola areas — includes pork and dog as ceremonial meats, a rare feature in largely Muslim Sumatra. The signature seasoning is *andaliman* (*Zanthoxylum acanthopodium*), a Sumatran relative of Sichuan pepper that produces the characteristic tingling numbness of Toba cuisine. **Saksang** is minced pork (or buffalo) cooked with *andaliman*, coconut milk, turmeric, and, in the fullest form, pig blood. **Arsik** is *ikan mas* (carp) from Lake Toba braised with *andaliman*, torch ginger, and *asam cikala* until the bones soften, and is the ritual dish presented by *hulahula* to a newly wedded couple. **Naniura** is a raw carp "cooked" only in *asam jungga* citrus juice with *andaliman* and shallots — a Toba dish once reserved for kings. **Dali ni horbo** is a "buffalo-milk cheese" curdled with papaya sap. **Mie gomak** is a thick spaghetti-like noodle from Tapanuli tossed with *andaliman* sambal. Rice (*eme*) is the staple, cultivated as both *sawah* (wet) and *ladang* (dry). Palm wine (*tuak*) drunk at the *lapo tuak* pavilion is the everyday social drink among Christian Batak, alongside strong Sumatran coffee.

## Oral tradition & literature

Batak oral tradition is anchored in the *tarombo*, the recited genealogy tracing every man's descent from **Si Raja Batak**, the mythic ancestor said to have settled at Sianjur Mula-Mula on the western shore of Lake Toba below the volcano Pusuk Buhit. Cosmological narratives centre on **Mula Jadi Na Bolon** (the "beginning of becoming"), his three sons **Batara Guru**, **Mangalabulan** and **Soripada**, and his granddaughter **Sideak Parujar**, who spun herself down from the sky and shaped the earth on the head of the underworld dragon **Naga Padoha** — whose thrashing beneath his iron fetters causes earthquakes. These *turi-turian* were traditionally recited by the *datu* and are also inscribed in *pustaha* bark-books. Proverbs (*umpasa*), delivered in obligatory couplets at every ceremonial speech, are still an active oral form: no *hulahula* addresses *boru* without one. In the 20th century Batak writers were disproportionately important in shaping modern Bahasa Indonesia: Merari Siregar's *Azab dan Sengsara* (1920), Mochtar Lubis's *Senja di Jakarta*, Iwan Simatupang's *Ziarah*, and the poetry of Sitor Situmorang all carry Batak sensibility into the national canon.

## Language & religion

Batak languages form a coordinate branch of Austronesian, split into a northern group (Karo, Pakpak-Dairi, Alas-Kluet) and a southern group (Toba, Angkola, Mandailing, with Simalungun as an early offshoot); the historical script *Surat Batak*, likely derived via southern Sumatran Kawi-descendants, is written on bamboo, bone, and tree bark and is now largely ceremonial. Contemporary religious affiliation is majority Christian — the Lutheran **HKBP** (Huria Kristen Batak Protestan), founded on the 19th-century mission of Ludwig Ingwer Nommensen, is the largest Protestant church in Indonesia, and the **GBKP** (Gereja Batak Karo Protestan) serves the Karo — with a substantial Muslim majority among Mandailing and Angkola converted during the early-19th-century Padri period. A minority still practise *agama si dekah* ("the old religion"), also called *pemena* (Karo) or *parmalim* (a codified Toba revivalist form founded by Sisingamangaraja XII's contemporary Raja Mulia Naipospos). Across all confessions the *tondi*/*tendi* soul-concept and the etiquette of *dalihan na tolu* remain operative.
