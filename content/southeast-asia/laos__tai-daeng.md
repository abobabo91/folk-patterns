## Overview

The Tai Daeng, or Red Tai, are an ethnic group of Vietnam and Laos. They speak the Tai Daeng language. In Laos, they are associated with Houaphanh province; in Vietnam, they are found in Thanh Hóa Province. The Tai Daeng population is given as 25,000 in Laos in 1991 and 140,000 in Vietnam in 2002, while populations in Thailand and the United States are unknown. The group is patriarchal. Tai Daeng belongs to the Tai-Kadai language group and is classified within the Tai languages and Southwestern Tai languages subgroups. In Vietnam, the Tai Daeng are included among the Thái people, the third largest of the fifty-four ethnic groups recognized by the Vietnamese government.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources identify Tai folk religion, Theravada Buddhism, Animism, and Christianity among the Tai Daeng.

## Language & religion

Tai Daeng, also called Táy-Môc-Châu or Red Tai, is spoken by the Tai Daeng people of northwestern Vietnam and across the border into northeastern Laos. It belongs to the Tai language family and is closely connected with Black Tai and White Tai, while being more distantly related to the language spoken in modern Thailand. Tai Daeng is classified within the Tai-Kadai language group and the Tai languages and Southwestern Tai languages subgroups. Tai Meuay is closely related to Tai Daeng.

The language has Subject-Verb-Object word order. It frequently uses serial verb constructions in which two or more verbs occur in one clause. Particles commonly occur at the end of a sentence to emphasize, question, command, or indicate familiarity or respect. Tai Daeng has no writing system of its own, although speakers make occasional use of the Tai Viet script.

In Vietnam, Tai Daeng is classified within the Thái official ethnic community. In Laos, it is part of the Phu Tai composite group. Speakers in Vietnam tend to identify with Black Tai, or Tai Dam, and therefore deny that they are Red Tai. The religions listed for the Tai Daeng are Tai folk religion, Theravada Buddhism, Animism, and Christianity. The source gives Theravada Buddhism as 95.5% and Christianity as 4.5%.
