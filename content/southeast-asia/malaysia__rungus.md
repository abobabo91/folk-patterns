## Overview

The Rungus, also known as the Momogun Rungus, are an Austronesian ethnic group indigenous to Sabah, Malaysia. They primarily live in the northern Kudat Division, including the Kudat and Bengkoka peninsula, especially in Kudat, Kota Marudu and Pitas. Smaller communities are recorded in Beluran, Telupid, Kota Kinabalu District, Gaya Island and the Federal Territory of Labuan. The sources estimate around 74,000 Rungus people across Sabah outside their native ranges. Their language belongs to the Dusunic branch of the Austronesian languages, and their culture is distinguished by black clothing, pinakol beadwork, longhouse architecture, rice farming, traditional ceremonies, gong-making, dance, oral literature and the indigenous labus belief system. Most Rungus are now Protestant Christians, while others belong to different Christian denominations, Islam or traditional religion.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Pinakol** — *Pinakol* consists of a pair of flat, beaded bandoleer-type belts worn crossed over the chest and back. Its beadwork distinguishes the Rungus from other indigenous ethnic groups of Sabah.

**Sandang** — *Sandang* is a pair of long beaded strands, mostly made with matching beads, worn crossed over the chest like the pinakol.

**Sulau** — *Sulau* is a flat, beaded choker worn around the neck. It has two clamshell discs, one at the front and one at the back, with small bells attached at the front. In the present day, the discs are made from plastic.

**Sisingal** — *Sisingal* is a narrow beaded band worn around the head.

**Rampai** — *Rampai* combines cotton, flowers and beads worked into the hair.

**Orot** — *Orot* is made from a small brass ring and an antique bead looped through thin strands of stripped bark called *togung*. It becomes a wide, colourful hipband, coiled slowly and carefully around the hip.

**Llobokon** — *Llobokon* is a final string of beads hung loosely from the coil of the orot.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional Rungus dress is black and may include hundreds or thousands of dollars’ worth of antique beads. Women traditionally wore heavy brass coils around their arms, legs and necks, often together with white and coral shell bracelets. Brass rings could also be worn around the waist. The *sandang*, *sulau*, *sisingal*, *rampai*, *orot* and *Llobokon* form part of the documented clothing and adornment vocabulary.

Men wear the short choker called *tinggot*, which may be single-beaded or made with narrow beadwork. The *togkul* is a necklace approximately 26 inches (66 cm) long, with beads similar to those of the sandang but smaller. *Sad'ang* are earrings that sometimes have beads attached. Many beads used today are plastic and glass imitations of older heirloom beads. Plastic spoons can be heated over a flame and wound onto a metal rod to produce yellow beads.

### Architecture

Many Rungus formerly lived in longhouses, with each family occupying separate quarters opening onto a common hall. At the edge of the communal hall, a well-ventilated platform made from split bamboo and enclosed by outward-sloping walls provides space for socialising and communal work. The Rungus longhouse differs from Murut longhouses.

Rungus houses are not placed on high stilts, standing only three to five feet above the ground. Their roofs are low and their walls slope outward. Longhouses among the older Rungus generation commonly had more than 75 doors, while modern longhouses rarely exceed 10 doors. Some modern two-storey versions exist, and single-family houses may be built near a longhouse.

The Rungus traditional house at Bavanggazo in Matunggong uses locally sourced rumbia, or *metroxylon sagu*, leaves and bamboo stems. Built in 1992 with assistance from the Sabah Tourism Board, it became part of the Bavanggazo Cultural Village. Tinangol Village is associated with a Rungus traditional handicraft centre, while Sumangkap Village is known as a gong-making centre.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do describe brass ornaments, including coils worn on the arms, legs and neck, brass rings worn around the waist, and the brass ring incorporated into the orot hipband. Women make containers from vine or beadwork, and the community uses backstrap looms for weaving cloth. Plastic spoons heated over a flame are used in the production of yellow beads. The sources also record bamboo as a building material and split bamboo as a material for the communal platform of the longhouse.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Rungus body adornment centres on beads, brass and shell. Women traditionally wore heavy brass coils around the arms, legs and neck, together with white and coral shell bracelets. Brass rings could be worn around the waist. The *pinakol*, *sandang*, *sulau*, *tinggot*, *togkul*, *sisingal*, *rampai*, *orot*, *Llobokon* and *sad'ang* are documented forms of beaded or metal adornment.

The orot is made by Rungus men, since the sources state that the technique is known only to them. It uses a brass ring and an antique bead attached to stripped bark called togung.

## Music & performance

Rungus musical culture includes gong-making in Sumangkap Village and music commonly paired with traditional dances. The sources identify Sumangkap as a gong-making centre.

Music and performance occur in cultural settings including Bavanggazo longhouse and Tinangol Village. During the Magahau festival, the celebration includes *mangantag*, described as a traditional festive dance, *mabbaris*, an ethnic dance similar to Kadazan-Dusun sumazau, and *manaradan*, a sacred Rungus dance. *Tinonggilan*, a slightly sparkling alcoholic drink made from maize, is served during festive occasions and as a refreshment for guests during *Mongigol Sumundai*, a ritual dance.

The sources also describe traditional ceremonies, music and cultural performances as elements of Rungus cultural life. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription concerns the Rungus in the supplied material.

## Dance & theatre

The Rungus dance vocabulary includes *Mongigol Sumundai*, a ritual dance associated with the serving of tinonggilan, and several Magahau performances. *Mangantag* is a traditional festive dance, *mabbaris* is an ethnic dance similar to Kadazan-Dusun sumazau, and *manaradan* is described as a sacred Rungus dance.

The Magahau programme also includes *mogunum*, the arrival of the crowd; *mangatod*, land clearing; *mogontong*, marriage; *lumuvas*, a clearing-away ritual ceremony; and *mongolosod*, a traditional ritual.

## Festivals & rituals

*Magahau* is the main and largest festival of the Rungus ethnic group. It is associated with the celebration of the new year according to the traditional Rungus calendar. Rungus people also celebrate *Kaamatan*, which became part of the Magahau Rungus festival. The sources state that the festive occasion is celebrated on 31 May every year.

The original purposes of Magahau include *mamapak*, *mamasi palad* and *mangaraha palad*, ceremonies related to sustenance; *mintutun*, introducing oneself; *monudung*, finding a partner; *gimpuhut*, the most beautiful girl; *bunjal*, barter-system or selling activities; *gontira*, sports; *posikib dot konsapatan sid keluarga om kinoruhangan*, sharing sustenance with family and friends; *mongodim dot kovorisan*, inviting relatives; and *humigak*, partying.

The celebration includes *mogunum*, *mangantag*, *mangatod*, *mogontong*, *lumuvas*, *mongolosod*, *mabbaris* and *manaradan*. Many original ritual practices shifted after the arrival of Christian missionaries in the 19th century during the administration of the North Borneo Chartered Company. Practices that contradicted Christian or Islamic teachings were abandoned by some communities.

## Foodways

Rungus food is prepared mainly by braising, grilling and baking. As a community of fishermen and farmers, the Rungus staple foods usually consist of rice and cassava, supplemented with green vegetables and fish. Rice farming is central to Rungus culture, and traditional beliefs include the rice spirit called *Odu-odu*.

*Linopot* is rice wrapped in leaves and is common among the Rungus and across the Dusunic and Murutic communities. *Tinunuvan soguntung* is the Rungus term for grilled or roasted eggplant. The cooked eggplant is peeled and served with chillies, lime juice, toasted anchovies or salted fish, and sprigs of *lompodos*, a local variety of basil.

*Tinonggilan* is a slightly sparkling alcoholic drink made from maize. It is a Rungus speciality served during festive occasions or as a refreshment for guests during Mongigol Sumundai. Simple sweet foods are prepared for breakfast or daily snacks, including *tinopis runti*, flatbread made from sweetened grated cassava; *bintanok dalai*, mashed corn kuih; mashed corn wrapped and steamed in corn husks; *bintanok runti*, mashed cassava kuih; and *bintanok punti*, mashed banana kuih.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Rungus have oral literature and origin narratives connected with Rungsud, also known as Aki Rungsud, who is described in Rungus ethnic scholarship as the figure who opened the Bandau-Kudat area as a major barter-trading area. Oral sources also connect the term “Rungus” with *Rungsud*, a Rungus forefather who migrated from the Nunuk Ragang area towards the Kudat Peninsula, Marudu Bay, Pitas, Beluran and Telupid.

Rungus traditions describe a settlement near Nunuk Ragang, an area associated with white sand called *tangar-tangar* and two rivers called *pirungusan*. In Rungus stories, the Rungus were led by Rungsud, Longguvai and Turumpok. The sources also mention Aki Nunuk Ragang and the relationship between Rungus and Kadazan traditions. Preservation efforts include Rungus language education schools with cultural arts training centres, programmes associated with the United Rungus Association of Sabah, and the use of the Rungus language in product advertising and public services such as road signs.

## Language & religion

Rungus is an Austronesian language and a branch of the Dusunic languages. The sources name several dialects: *Gandahon* in Kudat Division, *Gonsomon* south of Kudat Harbour, *Nuluw* at the northern tip of the Kudat Peninsula, and *Pilapazan*, also called Central Rungus or Tanga Rungus. Gonsomon and Nuluw are described as among the most popular dialects spoken by Rungus people today. Traditional Rungus writing is called *surip* and is described as a form of hieroglyphic writing.

The traditional Rungus belief system is called *labus*. Its priests or shamans are called *rampahan*, and the highest female priestesses are called *bobolizan*. The bobolizan acts as an intermediary with spirit worlds. Traditional beliefs identify three classes of supernatural beings: *Osunduw*, the God; *Rogon*, the spirit; and *Odu-odu*, the rice spirit. Most Rungus are now Christians, especially Protestants belonging to the Protestant Church in Sabah, while smaller minorities follow other Christian denominations, Islam or traditional religion.
