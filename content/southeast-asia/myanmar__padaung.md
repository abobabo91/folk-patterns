## Overview

The Padaung are the Kayan Lahwi, a subgroup of the Kayan and of the Red Karen, also called Karenni people, an ethnic minority of Myanmar. “Padaung” is a Shan term for the Kayan Lahwi, the group in which women wear brass neck rings; Kayan residents in Mae Hong Son province in northern Thailand prefer to call themselves Kayan. Kayan communities are found in Shan State, Kayah State, Kayin State, and Mandalay Region, including around Demawso, Loikaw, and Thandaung, as well as in Pyinmana Township. According to a 2004 estimate, the Kayan population was approximately 180,000. Their folk-cultural distinctiveness is especially associated with women’s brass coils, traditional religion, oral history, and the Kayan languages.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Women of the Kayan Lahwi are identified by permanent neck jewellery consisting of brass coils placed around the neck. Girls begin wearing the rings at around five years old; over time, the coil is replaced by a longer one and additional turns are added. The brass pushes the collar bone down, compresses the rib cage, and can pull about four thoracic vertebrae into the neck. The rings can stretch the neck to about 15 inches, or 38 cm.

In 2006, some younger women in Mae Hong Son began removing their rings to continue their education or to protest against the exploitation of their culture and the restrictions associated with the practice. The Myanmar government also discouraged neck rings, while the practice gained popularity in Thailand because tourists brought revenue to the Kayan and local businesspeople.

### Jewelry & body adornment

**Brass neck coils** are the principal documented form of Kayan Lahwi body adornment. They are permanent jewellery placed around the neck, with the weight of the brass pressing the collar bone down and compressing the rib cage. Once worn, a coil is seldom removed because coiling and uncoiling is lengthy; it is usually removed only to be replaced by a new or longer coil.

The muscles covered by the coil become weakened, and the neck and collarbone may become bruised and discolored. Many Kayan women prefer to keep the rings after the clavicle has been lowered. The sources record several explanations for the practice: protection from enslavement, enhancement of sexual dimorphism, resemblance to a dragon in Kayan folklore, and possible symbolic protection from tiger bites.

## Dance & theatre

The principal documented Kayan dance context is the three-day Kay Htoe Boe festival. The festival commemorates the belief that the creator god gave form to the world by planting a small post in the ground. A Kay Htoe Boe pole is erected during the festival, held in late March or early April, and participants dance around it.

## Festivals & rituals

The Kayan traditional religion is called Kan Khwan. Its major festival is the three-day Kay Htoe Boe, held in late March or early April. During the festival, a Kay Htoe Boe pole is erected and participants dance around it. The festival commemorates the belief that the creator god gave form to the world by planting a small post in the ground.

The Kayan have a strong belief in augury, and the sources state that nothing is done without reference to some form of divination. This includes breaking thatch grass and, most importantly, consulting chicken bones.

## Foodways

The reference to consulting chicken bones concerns divination rather than foodways, and the sources provide no further information about it.

## Oral tradition & literature

According to Kayan oral history, the Kayan settled around Demawso in Kayah State in 739 AD. The traditional religion includes the belief that the Kayan people resulted from a union between a female dragon and a male human/angel hybrid. The sources also state that the dragon is an important figure in Kayan folklore and that the neck coils may give women the appearance of a dragon.

Pascal Khoo Thwe’s 2002 memoir, *From the Land of Green Ghosts: A Burmese Odyssey*, is mentioned as a work in which the author calls his people Padaung.

## Language & religion

Kayan, also known as Padaung of Burma, is spoken by the Kayan people. The Kayan languages belong to the Tibeto-Burman language family, and the dialects share more than 90% lexical similarity. Padaung is 71% to 76% lexically similar to Lahta. Kayan languages are spoken in Kayah State, southern Shan State, and northern Karen State.

The sources describe four branches: Kangan, meaning “lowland dwellers”; Kakhaung, meaning “highland dwellers”; Lawi, meaning “South”; and Latha, meaning “North.” The Kayan Lahwi dialect is spoken in Lahwi and northwest Dimawso, Kayah State, and southern Pekhon in southern Shan State. Nangki, sometimes called Langki, belongs to the Kakhaung subgroup and is spoken only in one village.

Traditional Kayan religion is Kan Khwan. Traditional Kayaws believe in tree spirits, while younger generations are showing acceptance toward Buddhism and Christianity.
