## Overview

The Lisu are a Tibeto-Burman ethnic group inhabiting mountainous regions of Myanmar, southwest China, Thailand, and Arunachal Pradesh in India. In Myanmar they live in Kachin State, Shan State, Sagaing Division, and Mandalay Division, including Putao, Myitkyina, Danai, Waingmaw, Bhamo, Momeik, Namhsan, Lashio, Hopang, Kokang, Mogok, and Pyin Oo Lwin. An estimated 600,000 Lisu live in Myanmar, while about 55,000 live in Thailand and about 5,000 in India. Lisu is a tonal Tibeto-Burman language related to Lahu, Akha, Burmese, Jingphaw, and Yi. Their folk culture includes clan organization, mountain agriculture, textile clothing, silver adornment, bamboo and basket objects, ancestor altars, sacred groves, ritual specialists, and a history traditionally transmitted through songs.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The museum catalogue records a woman’s dress made of synthetic and cotton textile with appliqué, a cap made of synthetic and wool textile with appliqué, a cotton headscarf, a cotton patchwork bag, and a cotton patchwork cloth. The Wikipedia source states that Thai Lisu traditional costume differs from traditional Lisu costume widely used in Nujiang Lisu Autonomous Prefecture, Yunnan, China, and Putao, Danai, and Myitkyina in northern Myanmar.

### Clothing & dress

The sources document Lisu clothing only in general terms. During the main Lisu festival, people wear large amounts of silver jewelry and their best clothes to display their success in the previous agricultural year. The museum catalogue records a woman’s appliquéd dress made of synthetic and cotton textile, an appliquéd cap made of synthetic and wool textile, a cotton headscarf, and a cotton patchwork bag.

### Architecture

Lisu villages are usually built close to water for washing and drinking. Houses are usually built on the ground, with dirt floors and bamboo walls, although an increasing number of more affluent Lisu build houses of wood or concrete. The museum catalogue identifies baskets and other objects as Lisu household goods or equipment.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum catalogue records several Lisu objects from Kambaiti, Burma. These include bamboo containers for betel nuts and tobacco, with fern leaves used as a lid for the tobacco container; a mortar with pestle also used as a nutcracker; a scoop used to draw rice liquor from a large earthenware jar; a basket for carrying chickens tied with bamboo; and other baskets. The catalogue also records the objects as household goods, utensils, equipment, and implements.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources state that people wear large amounts of silver jewelry during the main Lisu festival, weddings, and other celebrations associated with music, feasting, and drinking. The Metropolitan Museum of Art catalogue records a silver necklace dated to the 19th century. No named jewelry types, body-marking practices, hair practices, or ritual functions of adornment are described.

## Music & performance

Lisu history is traditionally passed down through generations in the form of songs. Some songs are so extensive that performing a single song can take an entire night. Music is mentioned in connection with the main Lisu festival, weddings, feasting, and drinking.

Missionaries used hymns in their work among the Lisu. Christianity was spread through teaching hymns, sending medicine and doctors, helping the needy, funding domestic missionaries and evangelists, and developing agriculture. The sources mention the China Inland Mission, Disciples of Christ, Church of Christ, Assembly of God, Pentecostal Churches, Tibetan Lisuland Mission, and Lisuland Churches of Christ.

## Festivals & rituals

The main Lisu festival corresponds to Chinese New Year. It is celebrated with music, feasting, and drinking. People wear large amounts of silver jewelry and their best clothes to display their success in the previous agricultural year. Weddings are also associated with music, feasting, and drinking.

Before Christianity was introduced, Lisu people were animists. Archibald Rose described this as a simple form of animism or nat-worship, with sacrifices offered to the spirits of the mountains. Most important rituals are performed by shamans. Every traditional village has a sacred grove at the top of the village, where the sky spirit or, in China, the Old Grandfather Spirit, is propitiated with offerings. Each house has an ancestor altar at the back.

## Foodways

Lisu subsistence was based on paddy fields, mountain rice, fruit, and vegetables. Because they often lived in ecologically fragile regions that did not easily support subsistence, they depended on trade and worked as porters and caravan guards. The Lisu practiced swidden, or slash-and-burn, agriculture. In conditions of low population density, where land could be fallowed for many years, swiddening was described as an environmentally sustainable form of horticulture.

The opium poppy was introduced as a cash crop in the early 19th century. Many Lisu populations achieved economic stability through it for more than 100 years, but production later almost disappeared in Thailand and China because of interdiction. Very few Lisu used opium or heroin except for medicinal use by elders to alleviate arthritis.

The museum catalogue records rice liquor, including a scoop used to draw it from a large earthenware jar. It also records a bamboo container for betel nuts, a bamboo container for tobacco, a mortar used as a nutcracker, and baskets for carrying chickens.

## Oral tradition & literature

Lisu history is traditionally transmitted through songs performed across generations. A single song can take an entire night to perform.

The Lisu alphabet was used for early published works including a catechism, portions of scripture, and a complete New Testament in 1936.

## Language & religion

Lisu is a tonal Tibeto-Burman language spoken in Yunnan, northern Myanmar, Thailand, and a small part of India. It belongs to the Yi or Nuosu branch of the Sino-Tibetan family and is closely related to Lahu and Akha, as well as Burmese, Jingphaw, and Yi. Three dialects are distinguished in the sources: northern, central, and southern. Lisu people in India are called Yobin.

Two principal writing systems are in use: the Fraser alphabet and a script developed by the Chinese government based on pinyin. The Fraser alphabet was developed by James O. Fraser and the ethnic Karen evangelist Ba Thaw; the Chinese government officially recognized it as the official script of the Lisu language in 1992. The Lisu syllabary was invented from Chinese characters, Dongba script, and Geba script by Ngua-ze-bo between 1924 and 1930 and has 1250 glyphs and 989 characters.

Before Christianity, Lisu people were animists, with mountain-spirit offerings, shamans, sacred groves, sky-spirit or Old Grandfather Spirit worship, and ancestor altars. Many Lisu in India, Burma, and Yunnan converted to Christianity in the early 20th century. The sources state that only the Lisu of Thailand remained unchanged by Christian influences.
