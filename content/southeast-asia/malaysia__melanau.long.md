## Overview

The Melanau — *Orang Melanau* in Malay, *Tenawan Melanau* in their own speech, and *a-likou*, "people of the river," in the Mukah dialect — are an indigenous people of Sarawak on the island of Borneo, with a further presence in West Kalimantan, Indonesia. They are among the earliest settlers of Sarawak, historically clustered along the coastal swamp flats and the tributaries of the Rajang (Rejang) River in the centre of the state, in districts such as Mukah, Dalat, Oya, Igan, Matu, Daro, Balingian and Bintulu. The 2010 Malaysian census recorded about 123,410 Melanau; state statistics for 2014 gave 132,600, the fifth-largest ethnic group in Sarawak. Their language belongs to the North Bornean branch of the Malayo-Polynesian languages. In folk-culture terms they matter for a sago-based economy, a distinctive corpus of carved spirit figures, and burial-pole traditions shared with neighbouring peoples of central Borneo.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here do not describe Melanau weaving or cloth-making. What they do document is pattern carried on plaited and carved objects rather than on loom-woven textiles, and the named ornament below comes from that material.

**Plaited hats (*terindak*)** — Conical hats plaited from nipah palm leaf, rattan and bamboo, one type recorded as *matu terindak*: a piece of bamboo is lashed around the rim for support, the inside lined with cotton, and the outside worked in three bands of dark fibre with a band of green fibre on either side. Between the vertical bands a flower is sewn in red, yellow and white glass beads, with a metallic-thread knob at the point. Other hats in the collection are circular, made of palm leaf and cane with painted decoration, or of wood, or built from strips of palm leaf over a wooden centre piece with a rattan rim and radiating plaited strips in brown and black.

**Plaited baskets and lids** — Octagonal baskets over a wooden framework, the plaited sides patterned in brown, red and black, with a leaf bottom and lining and a lid of the same construction. Separate lids survive in leaf and rattan, in bamboo and rattan, and in rattan with beads and cloth. Boxes and their lids are made of cane and palm spathe, and food covers of nipah palm leaves.

**Motif vocabulary.** *betek binawa'*, tiers of chevrons carved on a rounded surface; *siret* ("hair"), chevrons covering an outstretched human figure; *nyipen*, a triangle or "teeth" pattern carved on the lower body of a figure; *nyipen jin*, "jin teeth," parallel lines across a figure's chest.

### Clothing & dress

The sources document headgear far better than the rest of Melanau dress. The *terindak* is the named hat form: plaited from nipah palm leaf over rattan and bamboo, conical, and in the finer examples beaded with glass flowers and finished with a metallic-thread knob. Other recorded hats are circular, of palm leaf and cane with painted decoration; several are men's hats made of wood, or of wood and cane. A woman's charm of wood is also recorded, worn or kept as a personal object rather than as part of a costume.

One body practice is documented directly. An instrument for flattening babies' heads — wood, cotton and linen cloth, cords and metal, with a Sarawak coin incorporated — survives in the collection, and a glass negative shows a Melanau woman in Sarawak with a baby wearing the equipment designed to flatten the skull. Beyond this, the sources say nothing about everyday or ceremonial garments, belts or footwear.

### Architecture

The Melanau formerly lived in tall stilt houses and longhouses; today they live in Malaysian *kampung*-style houses, individual and separate. The most distinctive built form the sources name is funerary rather than domestic: the burial pole known as *kelidieng*, *jerunei* or *lejeng*, a hanging-coffin and burial-pole tradition the Melanau share with several Orang Ulu peoples — the Punan Bah, Kejaman and Sekapan among them — and which is taken as evidence of a common ancient origin. The tradition is found in Melanau districts such as Mukah and Dalat, in Punan areas including Tatau and Belaga, and as far away as East and South Kalimantan. An ancient Melanau burial site is known to archaeologists at Niah, Sekaloh. Melanau graves in the Rajang District were photographed on a glass negative now in the British Museum. Another glass negative records a mission school at Oya with students and teachers on its verandah. The sources do not describe roof types, house decoration or urban building traditions.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The household and working objects recorded are overwhelmingly of plant material. Nipah palm leaf food covers, a water ladle of coconut shell, a wooden cooking knife and a wooden stirrer for rice make up the kitchen equipment; cane and palm-spathe boxes and plaited baskets hold goods. For edged tools the key form is the *parang latok*, a chopping sword used by both Malay and Melanau people for harvesting the sago palm and for personal protection: an unusually heavy and thick example from coastal north-west Borneo has a blade that bends sharply at its middle and an ivory hilt with a floral bone pommel, and is gripped with both hands, one palm laid on the back of the blade. Darts with their sheaths and a bamboo quiver are also recorded, as is part of a flute. Pottery appears as a dish with a circle-and-dot ground and bunches of larger circles under a discoloured underglaze blue and a clear glaze, and as a vase catalogued as currency. A model of a plank-built sailing boat for use on the surf is carved from a single piece of wood but incised to imitate planking, the seams filled with bitumen to represent waterproofing, and fitted with crossbeams, decking, oars, a stone anchor and a mast.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources name no Melanau jewellery types. The one documented body practice is infant skull flattening, carried out with a wooden and cloth device described above. Glass beads in red, yellow and white appear as ornament sewn onto a *terindak*, and beads are used on at least one basket lid, but as decoration of objects rather than as personal adornment.

## Music & performance

The sources record a single musical object — part of a flute in the British Museum — and say nothing further about Melanau instruments, song genres or performance occasions.

## Dance & theatre

The sources do not describe Melanau dance or theatre. They do record one modern dramatic treatment of Melanau material: *Sumpahan Jerunei* ("Curse of the Jerunei"), a Malaysian horror film released in 2023, built around the burial ritual of Melanau nobility in the 13th century and filmed in Mukah, Siburan and Santubong among other Sarawak locations.

## Festivals & rituals

The Melanau keep their own calendar, which begins in March, and the New Year is marked by the **Kaul** festival — the annual rite that survives most strongly, celebrated even by Melanau who are now Muslim or Christian, and described as the reminder of the older Melanau religion.

The richest ritual material in the collections is the carved *belum*, an image of a *tew*, a malevolent spirit, cut from sago palm pith. The figures are made singly, in pairs and in sets mounted on a carved wooden stand with mortise slots and wooden spikes; some are accompanied by a model spear or paddle. Named figures identify spirits of the three elements. Earth spirits include **Durig Ateng**, a kneeling female figure with carved hair and a carved necklace, hands on her stomach; **Tujah Pemarau**, a crouching male in a pointed hat with triangles on the lower body; and **Belangan Tou**, a crouching male with a crossed head-dress, one hand at the ear, part of a compound *belum* called *Dayeh Lebu'*. Air spirits include **Dalong Langit**, seated cross-legged with a flame-like head-dress, scales on the torso and wings on the back, his raised right hand holding a spear; **Jin Pontianak**, crowned with five points, bearded, hands at the ears, a downward-headed bird carved on his back; and **Bilum Rabong Pengamou Langit**, a bird-figure in a boat, its human-like face showing the tongue between the teeth and a roofed cavity in its back covered by folded wings. Water is represented by **Bilum Salui**, a set of four identical seated males in round hats whose touching elbows and knees leave "the shape of a canoe" between arms and legs, and by **Dalong Bau Batang**, whose separate arms hold a model spear point downward. **Jin Pâk Pangai** belongs to both water and air.

## Foodways

Melanau food rests on sago and on the products of the river and the swamp. Named dishes are *umai*, jungle ferns, fresh fish products, and *siet*, the edible larva of the sago palm weevil. Sago itself is eaten in several forms — *linut*, sago balls, and *tebaloi*, the sago-and-coconut biscuit the Melanau are best known for. The people farmed padi as well as sago and fished; those of Paloh were described in the 19th century as salt producers. Sago was a commodity as well as a food: the blockage of sago supplies from the Melanau districts to factories in Kuching was one of the events that brought James Brooke to intervene in Mukah in 1857. The *parang latok* is the tool used to harvest the sago palm. The sources describe no tea or coffee tradition and no ceremonial menu.

## Oral tradition & literature

Melanau oral literature is dominated by the figure of **Raja Tugau**, a well-known king in Melanau tradition who also appears in the Bruneian epic poem *Syair Awang Semaun*, which tells of the founding of the Brunei kingdom by fourteen brothers whose expansion began by conquering the Melanau lands held by Tugau and his allies, from Sambas to the upper Brunei River. The *Syair* survives in at least six versions, believed to have been committed to manuscript in the 19th century. Tugau is thought to be the oppressive Datu Makatunao of the Philippine *Maragtas* Code, whose rule drove ten datus from Borneo to Panay — a migration still commemorated in the Ati-Atihan festival there. Preservation is a live problem: many Melanau children no longer speak the language, the state's *Majlis Adat Istiadat* department lacks qualified Melanau staff to document the culture and history, and little has been done to teach the dialects to the current generation.

## Language & religion

Melanau is an Austronesian language of the North Bornean branch of Malayo-Polynesian, spoken in the coastal area of the Rajang delta and in Brunei. Its dialects include Mukah-Oya, Balingian, Bruit, Dalat, Lawas, Igan, Sarikei, Segahan, Prehan, Segalang and Siteng; the five larger groupings are Central, Matu-Daro, Kanowit-Sungai Tanjong, Sibu and the extinct Seru. The Bintulu dialect, also called Vaie, is barely intelligible to other Melanau speakers. Despite heavy Malay influence the language has kept enough of its own character to remain clearly separate from Malay. Originally animists, most Melanau are now Muslim, with Christians particularly among the Mukah and Dalat groups; one family may hold all three, with Christian and Muslim children and parents who keep strong animist beliefs. The *belum* figures and the Kaul festival belong to that animist layer.
