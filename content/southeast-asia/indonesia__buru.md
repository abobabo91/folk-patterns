## Overview

The Buru people, also called *Orang Buru*, are an ethnic group living mainly on the Indonesian island of Buru, with communities on Ambon Island, other Maluku Islands, in Jakarta, and in the Netherlands. They also call themselves *Gebfuka* or *Gebemliar*, meaning “people of the world” or “people of the land.” About 33,000 of 35,000 Buru people live on Buru, where they form about a quarter of the island’s population and are its most numerous native ethnicity. They are divided into several groups, including Rana, Masarete, Wae Sama, and Fogi. Their language belongs to the Central Maluku branch of the Malayo-Polynesian languages. Folk-cultural life documented in the sources includes farming, hunting, fishing, house construction, clothing, weapons, naming practices, local beliefs, and language traditions.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Traditional Buru clothing is described as similar to that of other Indonesian peoples. Men wear a *sarong*, described as a kind of kilt, together with a long-skirted tunic. Women wear a sarong and a shorter jacket. They do state that colors and decorative items differ substantially among the Masarete, Wae Sama, and Rana groups.

### Architecture

Traditional Buru houses are made from bamboo and are often built on stilts. Their roofs are covered with palm leaves or reeds, although tiles have become progressively popular.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They identify the straight machete, or *parang*, and the short spear as traditional Buru weapons.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 They mention decorative items only in connection with differences among the clothing of the Masarete, Wae Sama, and Rana groups.

## Music & performance

 No named musical tradition is identified.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do describe religious divisions between Sunni Muslims, who mostly live in the north of Buru, and Christian-Protestants in the south. Traditional local beliefs persist in many areas. In the central parts of the island, many people openly profess the cult of the supreme deity *Opo Hebe Snulat* and his messenger *Nabiat*. The sources also record religious conflicts in December 1999, when 43 people were killed and at least 150 houses were burned in Wainibe village.

## Foodways

Buru subsistence includes farming rice, millet, sago, sweet potato, and various spices. The sources specifically name allspice, nutmeg, and the Eucalyptus tree, which is used for aromatic oil. In inland areas, people hunt the wild pig Buru babirusa, deer, and possum. Coastal communities take part in tuna fishing.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They state that explanations for language taboos are found in associated myths and legends. These taboos concern kinship terms, nature, harvest, hunting, and fishing. Words for taboo items are not omitted but replaced by alternatives.

## Language & religion

Buru, also called Buruese or *li fuk Buru*, is a Malayo-Polynesian language of the Central Maluku branch. In 1991 it was spoken by approximately 45,000 Buru people on Buru, and it is also preserved in Buru communities on Ambon, other Maluku Islands, Jakarta, and the Netherlands. Three dialects correspond to major ethnic groups: Rana, Masarete, and Wae Sama. The Rana group also uses the so-called “secret dialect” *Ligahan*. The Fogi dialect is now extinct. Lexical similarities are about 90% between Masarete and Wae Sama, 88% between Masarete and Rana, and 80% between Wae Sama and Rana.

The language has five vowels and 17 consonants and uses a functional writing system based on the Latin alphabet. Buru Christians worship with a Bible written in their native language; the first translations were made in 1904 by Dutch missionaries. Most Buru people, especially in coastal regions and towns, have command of Indonesian, while coastal communities also use Ambonese Malay. Traditional names are used alongside Muslim or Christian names. Common names include *Lesnussa*, *Latbual*, *Nurlatu*, *Lehalima*, *Wael*, and *Sigmarlatu*. Relatives generally address one another by kin names rather than proper names, although deceased relatives may be referred to by name.
