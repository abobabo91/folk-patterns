## Overview

The Marind, also called Marind-Anim, are an ethnic group of New Guinea who live in South Papua, Indonesia. Their territory lies on both sides of the Bian River, extending from about 20 miles east of Merauke to the mouth of the Moeli River, between Frederik Hendrik Island and the mainland, east of Yos Sudarso Island, and mainly west of the Maro River. Marind languages form a small family within the Trans–New Guinea language phylum. Marind culture is documented through its clan system, myths, ancestor figures, headhunting traditions, ritual practices, language, and material objects such as arrows, drums, head-dresses, hoods, and photographed forms of bodily adornment.

## Festivals & rituals

Marind traditional social organization was characterized by a clan system. The Marind were divided into two moieties, each consisting of several patrilineal clans called boans, which were further divided into subclans. People lived in several extended families, each tracing its origin to a mythological ancestor. The sources state that each extended family maintained and transmitted its tradition, with this work especially associated with the family’s big men, whose influence did not extend beyond their extended family.

Marind ancestor veneration involved mythological ancestors called dema in the Marind languages. These figures were demon-like beings who appeared in myths and acted as culture heroes. They arranged the ancient world into its more recent state and introduced plants, animals, and cultural goods. They often took the form of plants or animals. Totems could appear in artefacts and myths, although the sources state that this form of totemism was not accompanied by a regular food taboo concerning the relevant animal or plant.

The sources describe headhunting as a former Marind practice directed at other tribes. It was connected to the naming of newborns, and the skull was believed to contain a mana-like force. They also describe ritual intercourse called *otiv-bombari*, including a ritual associated with a girl’s wedding and other occasions such as after a woman had given birth. The Dutch colonial administration forbade headhunting, ritual homosexuality, and rites involving intercourse between many men and one woman. Christian missions and schools also brought major changes. The sources do not provide an annual festival calendar or festival dates.

## Oral tradition & literature

Marind extended families traced their origins to mythological ancestors. These ancestors, called dema, appeared in myths as demon-like figures and culture heroes. They were credited with arranging the ancient world, introducing plants, animals, and cultural goods, and often taking the form of plants or animals. Each extended family kept and transferred its tradition, especially through the work of its big men.

The sources also refer to myths involving dema and to literature about Marind headhunting and demons. Hans Nevermann’s *Söhne des tötenden Vaters* is described as containing stories about demons and headhunting recorded in New Guinea. ## Language & religion

Marind is a Papuan language spoken in Malind District, Merauke Regency, Indonesia, by over ten thousand people. Its dialects are Southeast Marind, Gawir, Holifoersch, and Tugeri. Bian Marind, also called Boven-Mbian or Northwest Marind, is divergent enough not to be mutually intelligible and has been assigned a separate ISO code. Marind separates the Trans-Fly–Bulaka River languages, which would otherwise occupy a nearly continuous stretch of southern New Guinea.

Coastal Marind has four noun classes: class I for male humans, class II for female humans and animals, and classes III and IV for inanimates. Its verbs have complex morphology, including markers for actors, dative and genitive arguments, tense, aspect, mood, illocution, valency-changing devices, pluractionality, and some adverbial categories. The negative marker is “mbya,” which also marks collectivity, “all,” within noun phrases.

Traditional religious ideas included ancestor veneration, dema culture heroes, totemic forms, and the belief that a captured skull contained a mana-like force. The sources do not describe a current religious affiliation in detail. Christian missions produced major cultural changes, and schools were introduced to assimilate the people to western culture.
