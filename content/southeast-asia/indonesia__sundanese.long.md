## Overview

The Sundanese (*Urang Sunda*) are an Austronesian people native to the western third of Java, occupying the provinces of West Java, Banten, and the western edge of Central Java — a region historically called *Tatar Sunda* or *Pasundan*. Numbering roughly 42 million, they form Indonesia's second-largest ethnic group after the Javanese and the fourth largest in Southeast Asia. Their heartland is the volcanic *Parahyangan* highlands around Bandung, together with the coastal plains of Banten and Cirebon and the fertile Priangan uplands where tea, coffee, and wet rice have shaped village life for centuries. In folk-culture terms the Sundanese matter because their traditions preserve an older, less courtly stratum of Javan civilization: dry-field rice ritual, bamboo music, animist reverence for the rice goddess *Nyai Pohaci Sanghyang Asri*, and a distinctive north-coast batik tradition that fuses Chinese, Islamic, and indigenous imagery.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Batik Cirebon** — Produced in the north-coast sultanate city of Cirebon at the meeting point of Sundanese and Javanese territory, this *pesisir* (coastal) batik is famed for its bright, unrestrained palette compared with the muted browns and indigos of central Javanese court batik. Cirebonese workshops in *Trusmi* village have supplied the Keraton Kasepuhan and Kanoman courts since the 16th century and absorbed strong Chinese influence via the Ming-dynasty consorts of the Cirebon sultans.

**Megamendung** — The signature Cirebonese motif: stylized layered clouds (*mega* = cloud, *mendung* = overcast) rendered in graduated bands of colour, most iconically deep indigo bleeding into cream, or red on cream. Its ancestry is traced to Chinese cloud-collar iconography brought by Ming traders and formalized under Sunan Gunung Jati's marriage to the Chinese princess Ong Tien; the seven graded shades are read as the seven layers of the heavens.

**Batik Sunda (Batik Priangan)** — The inland batik tradition of the Priangan highlands, centred on Tasikmalaya, Garut, Ciamis, and Sukapura. Compared with Cirebonese work it uses a softer palette of ochre, indigo, and rust and draws motifs from the mountain-forest environment rather than the coast.

**Batik Garutan** — The Garut sub-tradition, distinguished by a warm cream-yellow ground (*gumading*) and diagonal lattice fills, often carrying floral and *merak* (peacock) motifs.

**Kain Poleng** and everyday **kain kebat** — Handwoven checkered and striped cotton wraps used in village dress and in ritual bundling of ceremonial objects.

**Iket kepala** cloths — Square head-cloths, often batik, folded into the distinctive Sundanese men's headwrap; the folding style (*iket Parekos Nangka*, *iket Barambang Semplak*, *iket Julang Ngapak*) is itself a marker of regional identity.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Megamendung* (layered clouds), *wadasan* (rock outcrops, often paired with megamendung), *taman arum* (perfumed garden with peacocks and phoenixes), *paksi naga liman* (a composite bird-dragon-elephant creature from Cirebon palace iconography), *singa barong* (heraldic lion of the Cirebon court), *merak ngibing* (dancing peacock), *kembang kanigaran*, *patran keris* (kris-blade foliage), and *lereng* (diagonal stripe fields).

### Clothing & dress

Everyday village dress for men traditionally combines a *pangsi* — a loose black cotton jacket and matching wide trousers — with an *iket kepala* head-cloth and often a *sarung* worn slung over the shoulder. Women wear the *kebaya* blouse over a batik *sinjang* or *samping* wrap, secured with a *stagen* sash. Ceremonial and wedding dress is far more elaborate: brides wear an embroidered *kebaya* with a *siger* — the tall gilded crown associated with Sundanese bridal costume, distinct from Javanese and Sumatran variants — while grooms wear a *jas takwa* jacket, patterned *sinjang*, and a *bendo* headpiece with a *keris* tucked at the back. The *pencak silat* fighter's costume adopts the pangsi in black with a red or batik sash. The Baduy of inland Lebak enforce a stricter dress code: the *Baduy Dalam* (inner Baduy) wear only undyed handwoven white for men and indigo-and-white for women, while the *Baduy Luar* (outer Baduy) wear all black. Footwear was historically bare feet or wooden clogs (*bakiak*); modern sandals have largely replaced these outside ritual contexts.

### Architecture

Sundanese vernacular houses are timber-frame stilt structures raised half a metre or so above the ground on short posts, with woven bamboo (*bilik*) walls and thatched roofs of *ijuk* (black sugar-palm fibre), *kirai*, or palm leaves. The roof form is the diagnostic feature and gives each house type its name. **Julang Ngapak** ("soaring bird") has an overhanging gablet resembling outstretched wings and is common around Tasikmalaya and Kuningan. Other named types include **Badak Heuay** ("yawning rhinoceros"), **Tagog Anjing** ("sitting dog"), **Jubleg Nangkub** (inverted mortar), **Capit Gunting** (scissor-blades), **Buka Pongpok**, and **Perahu Kemureb** (overturned boat). Ornament is deliberately restrained — the aesthetic values simplicity, harmony with the surrounding rice terraces and forest, and the honest exposure of bamboo and thatch. Next to the house, every traditional agricultural compound has a *leuit*, a small stilted rice barn with a steep thatched roof, treated as a sacred structure and central to the *Seren Taun* harvest ritual. The Baduy village of *Kanekes* preserves the most archaic form of these house-and-leuit compounds, arranged in rows on ridge tops and built entirely without nails.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The signature Sundanese metal object is the *kujang*, a small asymmetric ritual blade with a curving spine and two or more perforations, historically associated with the Pajajaran court of King Siliwangi and now a heraldic symbol of West Java (it appears on Bogor's civic emblem). Everyday agricultural tools include the *arit* sickle and the *bedog* machete carried by farmers throughout the highlands. Bamboo is the pre-eminent working material — used for *bilik* wall panels, water carriers, *lodong* cooking tubes, fish traps (*bubu*), and the whole family of musical instruments. Wooden objects centre on kitchenware: the *jubleg* wooden mortar and *halu* pestle for hulling rice, and the *boboko* woven-bamboo rice basket paired with the conical *aseupan* steamer and the *hihid* fan used to cool rice for *nasi timbel*. Cirebon and its hinterland produce a distinctive glazed earthenware, and the coastal town of *Sitiwinangun* near Cirebon is a documented pottery centre.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Sundanese jewelry is less elaborate than the gold traditions of Sumatra or Bali and is concentrated in bridal regalia. The bridal *siger* crown is the dominant piece — a tall openwork gilded diadem framing the bride's forehead, worn with *sumping* ear ornaments, *kembang goyang* trembling hair-flowers, and multiple gold necklaces (*kalung*). Groom's regalia includes the *keris* worn at the small of the back. In daily life, gold bangles and small ear studs are common among women, and the Islamic *jilbab* has become widespread head covering since the 1980s. Tattooing is not a documented Sundanese practice; the Baduy in particular reject body modification as part of their broader rejection of outside influence.

## Music & performance

Sundanese music is defined by bamboo and by a distinctive pentatonic tuning. The *angklung* — a set of tuned bamboo rattles shaken to sound single pitches, requiring an ensemble in which each player holds one or two notes — was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2010 and is now a national emblem. **Gamelan degung** is the courtly Sundanese gamelan, smaller and more intimate than its Javanese counterpart, built around bonang kettle-gongs, saron metallophones, a suling flute, and a kendang drum, and historically played in the regent's *pendopo*. **Kacapi suling** pairs the *kacapi* — a boat-shaped plucked zither with wire strings — with the bamboo *suling* flute in a chamber-music idiom used to accompany *tembang Sunda* sung poetry, especially the refined Cianjuran style developed in the 19th-century regency of Cianjur. **Rampak kendang** features a battery of barrel drums played in unison. **Calung** is a bamboo xylophone struck with mallets. Vocal traditions include *pantun Sunda*, epic chanting accompanied by the *kacapi*, in which a *juru pantun* recites cycles such as *Lutung Kasarung* or the deeds of King Siliwangi through the night. In coastal Cirebon a separate *tarling* genre — guitar and suling — developed in the 20th century.

## Dance & theatre

The most internationally known Sundanese dance is **Jaipongan**, developed in Bandung in the 1970s by Gugum Gumbira from older *ketuk tilu* village entertainments; it features fast kendang drumming and vigorous hip and shoulder movements and is often misread abroad as erotic. **Tari Merak** ("peacock dance"), choreographed by Raden Tjetje Somantri in the 1950s, dramatizes courting peafowl in a shimmering trained skirt. **Sisingaan** is the Subang tradition in which a boy about to be circumcised is paraded on the shoulders of four men carrying a wooden lion effigy. Court-derived dances such as *Tari Ratu Graeni* and *Tari Dewi* show Mataram Javanese influence. The dominant theatrical form is **wayang golek**, three-dimensional carved and painted wooden rod-puppets performed by a solo *dalang* with gamelan accompaniment; the repertoire draws mainly on the Mahabharata and Ramayana but with a strong Sundanese overlay of Islamic tales and the beloved *punakawan* clowns Cepot (Astrajingga), Dawala, and Gareng, whose satirical improvisation is central to a Sundanese performance's appeal.

## Festivals & rituals

The agricultural calendar culminates in **Seren Taun**, the annual rice harvest thanksgiving still celebrated in the traditional villages of *Ciptagelar* (Cisolok, Sukabumi), *Sindang Barang* (Bogor), and *Cigugur* (Kuningan), during which sheaves of rice are ritually carried into the *leuit* barns with offerings to Nyai Pohaci Sanghyang Asri. **Munggahan** in the Priangan region and **Cucurak** in Bogor are communal feasts held with extended family in the days before Ramadan begins. The Islamic calendar structures **Idul Fitri** (Lebaran) at Ramadan's end and **Idul Adha**, when the *akekahan* animal sacrifice is customary at births. Life-cycle rites cluster around pregnancy, birth, circumcision, marriage, and death. *Nujuh Bulanan* is the seventh-month prenatal bathing ceremony; *akekahan* follows shortly after birth with the slaughter of one goat for a girl or two for a boy. Circumcision is celebrated with the Sisingaan lion parade. Weddings run through a fixed sequence: *naroskeun* and *neundeun omong* (proposal and agreement), *siraman* (ritual bathing of the bride), *seserahan* (gift presentation), *akad nikah* (Islamic vows), *saweran* (showering of coins, flower petals, and sweets), *huap lingkung* (mutual feeding with arms entwined), and *bakakak hayam* (the couple tearing a grilled chicken in half — the one taking the larger share is said to dominate the household). Funerary observances follow Islamic *tahlilan* recitation on the day of death, the seventh, fortieth, hundredth, and thousandth day after.

## Foodways

Sundanese cooking is defined by freshness and by the primacy of raw vegetables — no other Indonesian cuisine features *lalab* (a plate of raw cucumber, basil, eggplant, papaya leaf, and bitter melon eaten with *sambal terasi* shrimp-paste chili) so centrally. Rice is prepared as steamed *nasi putih* and in the packed banana-leaf portion *nasi timbel*, and the *nasi liwet Sunda* pot-rice cooked with coconut milk and lemongrass differs from its Solonese namesake. Signature dishes include **karedok**, a raw-vegetable salad in raw-peanut sauce; **lotek**, its cooked-vegetable cousin; **pepes** (Sundanese *pais*), spiced fillings of carp, anchovy, oncom, tofu, mushroom, or minced meat wrapped in banana leaf and steamed then grilled; **soto Bandung** with white radish and beef; **empal gepuk** pounded fried beef; **nasi tutug oncom** rice mashed with fermented *oncom*; and the ubiquitous *ayam goreng* and *ikan bakar* with sambal. *Oncom* itself — a peanut-press-cake fermented with the orange *Neurospora* mold — is a signature Sundanese ingredient without a real counterpart elsewhere in Java. Freshwater carp (*ikan mas*) and gourami from mountain ponds are preferred over sea fish inland. Sweet snacks include *colenak*, *surabi* rice-flour pancakes cooked in earthenware moulds, and *cendol* iced coconut-milk-and-palm-sugar drink. As Muslims, Sundanese observe halal restrictions.

## Oral tradition & literature

The core Sundanese oral form is *pantun Sunda* — long sung narrative poems chanted through the night by a *juru pantun* accompanying himself on the *kacapi* zither. The standard repertoire includes **Lutung Kasarung** (the enchanted black monkey who is a disguised prince), **Ciung Wanara**, **Mundinglaya Dikusumah**, the cycle of King **Siliwangi** and the fall of Pajajaran, and the origin legend **Sangkuriang** — in which a spurned son unwittingly courts his own mother and, when refused, kicks over the boat he was building, forming the upturned prow that is Mount Tangkuban Perahu north of Bandung. Popular short tales for children include **Si Leungli**, the golden fish. Written literature has an older stratum in the *Sanghyang siksakanda ng karesian* (1518), a didactic manuscript in Old Sundanese preserving Hindu-Buddhist and pre-Islamic moral instruction, and a modern stratum in the 20th-century novels and poetry of Ajip Rosidi and the *Manglé* magazine, which has published in Sundanese since 1957.

## Language & religion

Sundanese is an Austronesian language of the Malayo-Polynesian branch, spoken by about 36 million people and second in Indonesia only to Javanese among regional languages. Major dialects include Priangan (regarded as the most refined, particularly around Cianjur), Banten, Bogor, and Cirebon; the Baduy speak an archaic form that predates the borrowing of Javanese-style speech levels (*undak-usuk basa*) that mark rank. Historically written in the *Aksara Sunda Kuno* script between the 14th and 18th centuries, then in Pegon (Arabic-based) and Latin scripts, the old script is undergoing state-sponsored revival and now appears on street signs across Bandung and other West Javan cities. Religiously, the great majority are Sunni Muslims, converted from the 15th and 16th centuries onward through the Sultanates of Banten and Cirebon. Pre-Islamic *Sunda Wiwitan* — animist reverence for ancestral *karuhun* spirits, the rice goddess *Nyai Pohaci Sanghyang Asri*, and the supreme *Sang Hyang Kersa* — survives among the Baduy of Kanekes, in Kampung Naga in Tasikmalaya, and in Cigugur Kuningan.
