## Overview

The Ibaloi, also spelled Ibaloy, are an indigenous ethnic group of the southeastern part of Benguet Province in the northern Philippines. Their settlements occupy mountain valleys and areas including parts of Baguio; their territory contains the Bued River, Agno River, Amburayan River, and Mount Pulag. The Ibaloi are among the indigenous peoples collectively known as Igorot, a term glossed as “hill-dwellers,” who live in the Cordillera Central of Luzon. Their native language is Ibaloi, also called Inibaloi or Nabaloi, an Austronesian language belonging to the Malayo-Polynesian branch. Ibaloi culture is documented through distinctive houses, agricultural practices, tools, clothing, musical instruments, mining-related adornment, foodways, mummification, and traditional religious beliefs.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Men wear a g-string called *kuval*. Wealthy men include a dark blue blanket called *kulabaw* or *alashang*, while others use a white blanket called *kolebao dja oles*. Women wear a blouse called *kambal* and a skirt called *aten* or *divet*. Gold-plated teeth covers, copper leglets, copper bracelets, and ear pendants are described as forms of bodily adornment rather than garments. Older Ibaloi people may have tattooed arms as a sign of prestige.

### Architecture

The Ibaloi build houses called *balai* or *baeng* near their farms. These houses are usually raised on five-foot posts called *tokod* and contain one room with no windows. Pine trees are commonly used in construction, especially for wealthy families. Poorer houses use bark bamboo for floors and walls and cogon grass for roofs, called *atup*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Ibaloi household objects include copper cooking pots called *kambung*, wooden food compartments called *shuyu*, wooden utensils, baskets, and coconut-shell containers. A wooden box filled with soil serves as a cooking place called *Shapolan*, while three stones form the stove called *shakilan*. Rice-processing equipment includes mortars called *dohsung*, which may be round or rectangular, and pestles called *al-o* or *bayu*. Rice winnowers called *dega-o* or *kiyag* are made of bamboo or rattan. Traditional weapons include the spear *kayang*, shield *kalasai*, bow and arrow *bekang* and *pana*, and war club *papa*.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ibaloi adornment includes gold-plated teeth covers called *shikang*, copper leglets called *batding*, copper bracelets called *karing*, and ear pendants called *tabing*. The sources connect these objects to the benefits of gold and copper mining. Older Ibaloi people may have tattooed arms as a sign of prestige.

## Music & performance

Music is important among the Ibaloi. Instruments named in the sources include the Jew’s harp *kodeng*, nose flute *kulesheng*, native guitar *kalsheng* or *Kambitong*, bamboo striking instruments, drums called *solibao*, and gongs called *kalsa*. The sources describe these instruments as sacred and state that they must always be played for a reason, such as a *cañao* feast. No named song genres, dances, musical scales, performance specialists, or specific arrangements are documented in the supplied material.

The Ibaloi musical instrument inventory includes both melodic and percussive forms. The *kodeng* is identified as a Jew’s harp, the *kulesheng* as a nose flute, and the *kalsheng* or *Kambitong* as a native guitar. Drums, gongs, and bamboo striking instruments extend the ensemble beyond individually played instruments.

## Festivals & rituals

They identify the *cañao* as a feast and give it as an example of a reason for playing sacred musical instruments. No further description of the feast is supplied.

Ibaloi religious practice includes beliefs in two kinds of spirits, called *anitos*. Nature spirits are associated with calamities, while ancestral spirits, called *ka-apuan*, make their presence known in dreams or by making a family member sick. Mount Pulag is culturally important and is considered the place where spirits join their ancestors.

The Ibaloi are also documented through a mummification process. Saltwater is used to prevent organ decomposition. Pounded guava and *patani* leaves are applied to the corpse to prevent maggot or worm infestation while the body dries. The process takes anywhere from two months to even a year, until the body becomes hardened.

## Foodways

The Ibaloi are predominantly farmers because of the fertile soils and climate of Benguet. Two varieties of rice are named: *kintoman* and *talon*. *Kintoman* is a red, long-grained rice with several forms. *Balatin-naw* is soft and sticky when cooked, *shaya-ut* is also soft, and *putaw* is slightly rough on the palate. *Kintoman* is also used to make the native rice wine called *tafey*. *Talon* is a white lowland variety planted during the rainy season.

Other cultivated crops include *camote*, *gabi*, cassava, potatoes, cabbage, celery, and *pechay*. The Ibaloi also gather wild mushrooms and grow or consume avocados, bananas, and mangoes. Meat includes pigs, cows, goats, and chickens, as well as wild deer called *olsa*, wild pigs called *alimanok*, and big lizards called *tilay*. Fish are taken from the few rivers in the area. Smoked pork called *kinuday* is a prominent Ibaloi food.

Cooking equipment includes copper pots called *kambung*, a soil-filled wooden cooking place called *Shapolan*, and a three-stone stove called *shakilan*.

## Language & religion

Ibaloi, also known as Inibaloi or Nabaloi, is the native language of the Ibaloi people. The Ibaloi language belongs to the Malayo-Polynesian branch of the Austronesian language family and is closely related to Pangasinan. It is spoken primarily in central and southern Benguet, western Nueva Vizcaya, and eastern La Union. Its dialects include Bokod, Daklan, and Kabayan. Ibaloi speakers often also speak Ilocano and Tagalog as second languages.

The language has forms including *ësël ivadoy*, glossed as “Ibaloi language,” and *ivadoy*. The sources describe verbal affixes including *-en*, *i-*, *-an*, *i-...-an*, *man-*, *on-*, *meki-*, and *pe-*. Many Ibaloi are now Christians of various denominations, while many continue to practice traditional Ibaloi faith. Traditional beliefs include *anitos*, divided into nature spirits and ancestral spirits called *ka-apuan*.
