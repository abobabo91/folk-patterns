## Overview

The Mường are an ethnic group native to northern Vietnam and the country’s third largest of 53 minority groups. The 2019 census recorded 1,452,095 Mường, or 1.51% of Vietnam’s population. They mainly inhabit the mountainous provinces of Hòa Bình, Thanh Hóa, Phú Thọ, and Sơn La. In Hòa Bình, important population centers include Mường Vang, Mường Bi, Mường Thàng, and Mường Động. The Mường language belongs to the Austroasiatic family and is closely related to Vietnamese. Mường culture is documented through epic traditions, village life, wet-rice cultivation, forest-resource use, weaving, clothing, religious practices, and relationships with neighboring Tai-speaking peoples and the Vietnamese Kinh.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Weaving** — Weaving is identified as a typical craft of the Mường.

**Knitting** — Knitting is listed among the typical crafts of the Mường.

**Reeling** — Reeling is also recorded as a typical Mường craft.

**Silk production** — The Mường epic cycle relates the acquisition of silk to the origins of cultural practices.

### Clothing & dress

Different Mường groups wear different clothing styles. Some clothing is borrowed from the Thái, while other clothing resembles Vietnamese dress. Women’s clothing generally consists of a tunic or robe, a headscarf, and a skirt. Some women formerly wore neck rings, as did other minorities in northern Vietnam. Men generally wear simple tunics and pants.

### Architecture

The Mường epic cycle says that the first Mường people lived in a cave on the mountain Hang Hao before their descendants resettled in villages, or *mường*. The epic also describes Dịt Dàng, or the king Việt, descending to the lowlands to build a capital city with a palace and a large market. It names this place Kinh Kỳ-Kẻ Chợ, understood in the tales as the area of the capital city and marketplace.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Mường epic does mention the acquisition of fire, house building, casting bronze drums, and the cutting and movement of a huge tree called Chu, described as having a copper trunk and iron branches. These references occur within the epic account and are not catalogue descriptions of surviving objects.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention that some Mường women formerly wore neck rings.

## Music & performance

They mention music in the description of a video concerning the Mường people in Hòa Bình, but provide no musical details.

## Festivals & rituals

The main Mường holidays are New Year and agrarian holidays. During New Year celebrations, Mường people pray to their ancestors. Similar prayers are arranged on revolutionary holidays, after which the whole village shares pre-cooked dishes.

Mường religious practice includes worship of ancestral spirits and other supernatural deities. The sources describe the Mường as primarily animist, meaning that they believe non-living objects have spirits, and they also describe the deification of local heroes who have died. Harmful spirits are called *ma tai*, *ma em*, and others.

## Foodways

Mường residents primarily grow wet rice; some also grow corn and cassava. Their economic resources include forest products such as mushrooms, wood ear, wood, bamboo, and rattan.

The Mường epic describes the origins of cultural practices including the acquisition of fire and the production of silk. In related discussion of Vietnamese traditions, it mentions glutinous rice, rice cooked in bamboo tubes, and stuffed steamed cakes called *bánh chưng* and *bánh dầy*. The sources state that similar cakes are also found among Zhuang people in Guangxi province. During New Year and revolutionary holidays, the whole village treats itself to pre-cooked dishes, without further description.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Mường have valuable epics, including *Te tấc te đác*, translated as “Giving rise to the Earth and the Water.” The Mường epic cycle traces mythological ancestry to a legendary bird couple, Chim Ây, the male bird, and Cái Ứa, the female bird. It relates the origins of natural phenomena, the first people, fire, house building, silk production, bronze-drum casting, weaving, and embroidery to the uplands.

According to the epic, the first Mường people lived in Hang Hao before spreading into villages. Only one son of the first Mường parents, Dịt Dàng, or the king Việt, descended to the lowlands and built a capital with a palace and market. The epic calls this place Kinh Kỳ-Kẻ Chợ. The stories also describe cooperation between uplanders and lowlanders, including their joint cutting and movement of the tree Chu.

## Language & religion

Mường is a group of dialects spoken by the Mường people of Vietnam. It belongs to the Austroasiatic language family and is closely related to Vietnamese. Mường dialects are spoken mainly in the mountainous regions of Hòa Bình, Thanh Hóa, Phú Thọ, Sơn La, Ninh Bình, Yên Bái, and Đắk Lắk. All Mường dialects are tonal, and Mường has all six tones of Vietnamese, although the distribution of the *nặng* and *sắc* tones differs by province.

Mường had no written form until Western academics developed a provisional alphabet in the 20th century based on a modified Vietnamese alphabet. In September 2016, the People’s Committee of Hòa Bình Province adopted resolution 2295/QĐ-UBND, specifying a new Mường alphabet for instruction in the province. The Mường aristocracy had earlier been familiar with Chinese writing through study of the Confucian canon. The language is mainly used domestically, while most native speakers also speak Vietnamese.

Mường religious life includes Buddhism, possibly Theravada, Christianity, often with local animistic influences, and traditional worship of ancestral spirits and supernatural deities. The sources state that adherence to many folk beliefs has declined with the introduction of modern medicine.
