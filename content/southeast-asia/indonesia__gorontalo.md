## Overview

The Gorontalo people, also known as Gorontalese, are a native ethnic group concentrated in Gorontalo, North Sulawesi, and the northern part of Central Sulawesi. They are the most populous ethnicity in the northern part of Sulawesi and are predominantly Muslim. Their native language is Gorontalo, an Austronesian language spoken by around one million speakers according to the 2000 census. Gorontalo society is organized through the *pohala'a* family-kinship system inherited from earlier kingdoms, while mutual cooperation, called *huyula*, remains part of daily life. Agriculture has long been the main traditional occupation, alongside forestry, fishery, crafting, and livestock farming. Their folk culture includes oral poetry, riddles, wedding and pregnancy ceremonies, distinctive houses, traditional clothing, dance, cuisine, and a developed musical culture.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Gorontalo traditional attire is multicolored, with each color representing a symbolic aspect. The sources identify *Bili’u* as the traditional attire worn by both the bride and bridegroom during the *Momonto* and *Modutu* wedding customs. During the *Molontalo* or *Tontalo* seventh-month ceremony, both parents of the unborn child must wear traditional Gorontalo attire.

### Architecture

The principal Gorontalo settlement form is the village. The traditional house is called *Dulohupa*, a frame structure built on stilts from selected timber and roofed with straw. It is divided into several rooms and has two staircases by the entrance. In the past, *Dulohupa* was used by royal rulers for discussions; traditional examples can still be found in several sub-districts in Gorontalo.

Another traditional house is *Bandayo Poboide*, whose existence is described as almost extinct throughout the region. One of the very few remaining examples stands in front of the Gorontalo Regent’s office on Jenderal Sudirman Road in Limboto, Gorontalo.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state only that crafting was a secondary means of income and that the *Dulohupa* house was built from selected timber and roofed with straw.

## Music & performance

The Gorontalo people are described as having a developed musical culture. They do identify *tanggomo*, a form of oral literature in the Gorontalo language performed by figures such as Manuli. He was known for memorizing thousands of *tanggomo* verses and composing new verses spontaneously in response to recent events or occurrences he had witnessed. He performed these verses in traditional markets in Gorontalo while selling goods, attracting crowds and increasing his sales. Many of his verses were recorded and archived as digital records by Radio Republik Indonesia Gorontalo.

The sources also describe oral poetic forms including *Tuja’i*, *Leningo*, *Taleningo*, and *Tinilo*. *Tuja’i* contains praise, religious advice, and customary wisdom; *Leningo* consists of proverbs, wise words, or expressions offering life guidance; *Taleningo* provides advice about death, birth, and preparation for the afterlife; and *Tinilo* is Gorontalo *pantun* containing praise, entertainment, prayers, history, and calls for good deeds. No UNESCO inscription concerns this ethnic group, and no museum catalogue records are supplied.

## Dance & theatre

The principal named Gorontalo dance in the sources is the *Polopalo* dance. It is described as a traditional dance popular among the Gorontalo community and extending into the North Sulawesi region.

## Festivals & rituals

At the end of Ramadan, Gorontalo people conduct *Tombbilotohe*, a cultural celebration in which oil lamps are lit around mosques and settlements. The sources describe this as a religiously influenced community celebration.

Gorontalo wedding customs include *Momonto* and *Modutu*. The wedding ceremony follows regulations and procedures for both bride and bridegroom and is held alternately at the houses of the bride and bridegroom. It can last for more than two days. Relatives work collectively for several days before the wedding, and both partners wear *Bili’u*. The bridal bedroom is used during the wedding reception according to Gorontalo customs.

*Molontalo*, also called *Tontalo*, is a seventh-month ceremony expressing gratitude when pregnancy reaches the seventh month. Both parents of the unborn child wear traditional Gorontalo attire. A little girl is carried by the prospective father around the house and then into a room to meet his pregnant wife. Afterward, a coconut-leaf string previously tied around the pregnant wife is severed. Seven dishes are served on seven different trays and distributed among invited guests.

## Foodways

Gorontalo cuisine, also called *Hulontalo* cuisine among migrating Gorontalo people, is associated with the Gorontalo Peninsula, North Sulawesi island, and Indonesia. It is known for fresh seafood, hot local chilies, spices, herbs, basil, and pandan. The cuisine has been influenced by communities migrating to Gorontalo, including people from the Arab world, China, and Ternate-Tidore; pastries are also described as influenced by European culture brought by the Dutch.

Cooking methods have distinct Gorontalo names. Boiling fish, prawns, and meat is called *ilahe*, while boiling banana, corn, or taro is called *bilanggahe*. Grilling meat and fish is *ilalango*, while grilling banana, corn, or taro is *tilenehu*. Fried dishes are called *tilinanga*; examples include *lambi tilinanga*, a fried banana, and *tola tilinanga*, a fried fish. Fried flour-coated bananas are called *sanggala*. Grilling or steaming food wrapped in banana leaf is called *ilepa’o*.

Two named spice mixtures are *pilitode* and *iloni*. *Pilitode* is a Gorontalese *gulai* sauce, while *iloni* is a Gorontalese grilling sauce. Both use candlenut, ginger, turmeric, and coconut milk. They are used with fish, chicken, beef, seafood, banana, cassava, and pumpkin. The sources describe Gorontaloans as predominantly Muslim.

## Oral tradition & literature

*Lumadu* is native Gorontalo oral literature consisting of brain-exercising riddles, metaphors, and parables. Children often use riddling *Lumadu* in games, while adults use metaphoric *Lumadu* in conversation to show courtesy, broaden discussion, and bring value to its subject.

The sources list several other oral forms. *Tanggomo* is narrative poetry not bound by lines and containing information about real events or true folklore. *Tuja’i* is rhyming poetry of praise, religious advice, and customary wisdom. *Leningo* consists of proverbs, wise words, or expressions that can serve as life guidance. *Lumadu* can also consist of one or two sentences conveying principles of life and the character of the Gorontalo people. *Taleningo* gives advice about death, birth, and preparation for the afterlife, while *Tinilo* is *pantun* containing praise, entertainment, prayers, history, and calls for good deeds.

The sources state that these forms are increasingly endangered by the passage of time, limited numbers of practitioners, and the scarcity of written documentation. Manuli preserved *tanggomo* through performance and recordings, while Mansoer Pateda contributed dictionaries and participated in translating the Quran into Gorontalo.

## Language & religion

Gorontalo, also called *Hulontalo*, belongs to the Austronesian language family. More specifically, it belongs to the Gorontalic language family, part of the Gorontalo-Mongondow languages and the Malayo-Polynesian branch of the Austronesian languages. Related languages named in the sources include Suwawa, Bolango, Buol, Bintauna, Kaidipang, and Lolak. Gorontalo dialects or closely similar varieties include Suwawa, Atinggola, Limboto, Kwandang, Tilamuta, and Sumawata; five main dialects are identified as east Gorontalo, Limboto, Gorontolo City, west Gorontalo, and Tilamuta.

Gorontalo has been influenced by Malay, Arabic, Portuguese, Dutch, and North Halmahera languages. Manado Malay and Indonesian are also spoken, and Gorontalo Malay became the lingua franca in the province. Gorontalo is now more often written in the Latin alphabet, while Indonesian is more widely used in schools, media, and official documents.

Most Gorontaloans adhere to Islam, adopted widely by the early 16th century through Ternate influence. The expression *Adat Bersendi Sara* and *Sara Bersendi Kitabullah* describes customs based on rules and rules based on the Quran.
