## Overview

The Cirebon or Cirebonese are a Javanese sub-ethnic native to Cirebon in the northeastern region of West Java Province of Indonesia. Their population is approximately 2 million, and they are mainly adherents of Sunni Islam. Their native language is Cirebonese, which combines elements of Javanese and Sundanese, with a heavier influence from Javanese. Cirebonese identity developed in relation to both Javanese and Sundanese cultures but came to be expressed through a distinct language, coastal batik traditions, Islamic patterns, the Cirebon palace, wedding customs, performance traditions and cuisine. Their presence was recognized nationally as a separate tribe through the 2010 population census, which included a specific Cirebonese category.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Coastal batik** — Cirebonese coastal batik does not follow the standards of the Javanese palace style commonly known as interior batik. The source associates the development of Cirebonese identity with the emergence of traditional Islamic patterns following the construction of the Cirebon palace in the 15th century.

**Wadasan cloth** — *Wadasan* is a batik sarong of Cirebonese style worn by the bride during the Siraman procession. The source states that it is usually green, a color that symbolizes fertility.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no specific Cirebonese textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe ceremonial clothing connected with the Cirebonese Royal Wedding rather than everyday dress. During Siraman, the bride wears a Cirebonese-style batik sarong called *Wadasan*, usually green. The wedding procession also includes a cloth belonging to the bride’s mother, which covers the groom after the consent of the bride’s parents has been granted; this signifies that he has become a son-in-law. Afterward, the cloth is taken back to indicate that the bride is no longer under the protection of her parents and must carry her own responsibility.

### Architecture

 They state that the construction of the Cirebon palace in the 15th century was associated with the emergence of traditional Islamic patterns and that the palace became a symbol of Cirebonese identity. The sources also mention the Cirebonese palace, the Kasepuhan palace, the cungkup used for Siraman and the Astana Gunung Jati Tomb of the Cirebonese Kings Complex.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention the *pipisan*, a type of millstone or rectangular stone wrapped in white cloth, as an object used during the Temon or Salam Temon wedding ceremony. The wedding dowry may include valuables in the form of jewelry or cash, and the *mas picis* bearers carry jewelry and cash to the woman’s parents.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Jewelry and cash are included among the possible Cirebonese wedding dowries. During the Parasan ceremony, the prospective bride undergoes *ngerik*, the removal of fine hair by the makeup person, while her parents and relatives watch.

## Music & performance

The sources name several musical forms and instruments associated with Cirebonese culture. During the Siraman procession, the bride is accompanied to the *cungkup* by traditional musical instruments, including the *nablong*. The Parasan ceremony is accompanied by *moblong*, described as karawitan music whose meaning is associated with a fountain of water like a full moon. The sources also list Gamelan Sekaten and Kemanak among Cirebonese arts.

Music appears in both ritual and entertainment contexts. At the end of the Royal Wedding, after the bride and bridegroom have received their parents’ blessings, relatives congratulate them and entertainment such as dancing is usually held. The source names Cirebon mask dance, Cirebonese cultural dance and Tayub dance in this context. A love song with advice in the form of *macapat* is played after the parental-blessings ceremony, expressing the hope that the newly married couple will manage their household in agreement throughout life and death.

## Dance & theatre

Cirebon mask dance is named as a Cirebonese art and as entertainment that may be performed at the closing of a Royal Wedding. Cirebonese cultural dance and Tayub dance are also named as possible wedding entertainment.

The sources also mention *Pewayangan* from Cirebon. The Gegesik dialect is often used as the intermediary language in Pewayangan by the *Dalang*, or puppet master. The source states that Gegesik may be a finer dialect than the dialect of the “Wong Cirebon” itself. No further description of the puppet theatre, its stories, staging or puppets is provided.

## Festivals & rituals

 They provide an extensive account of the Cirebonese Royal Wedding, called *Pelakrama Ageng* in Cirebonese. The ceremony emphasizes Islam and local moral values, including simplicity and the avoidance of *ria*, the attitude of wanting to be praised. Dowries may consist of tubers, vegetables and valuables according to the groom’s means.

The wedding procession begins with the proposal, called *tetali* or *njegog*, when a messenger visits the woman’s parents. The woman gives her answer in the messenger’s presence, and the wedding date is then discussed. Other stages include delivery of the dowry, Siraman, Parasan, grave visitation, fetching the groom, solemnization, Temon or Salam Temon, Sawer, sprinkling of *pugpugan*, partaking of yellow sticky rice and parental blessings.

Siraman, also called Siram Tawandari, symbolizes purity. The bride and groom are bathed in a traditional procedure before solemnization, and the water is later sprinkled over young women and men in the ceremony called *Bendrong Sirat*. Grave visitation, or *ziarah*, is performed to offer prayers for ancestors. A bride descended from the Cirebonese Sultanate may visit the tomb of Sunan Gunung Jati and the ancestral tombs of the Cirebonese sultans at Astana Gunung Jati to receive blessings.

## Foodways

The sources name several Cirebonese dishes: Docang, Empal gentong, Mie koclok, Sega jamblang and Tahu gejrot.

Food has a defined role in the Royal Wedding. During the dowry procession, groups carry fruit, tubers and vegetables, while *mas picis* bearers carry jewelry and cash. During the ceremony called adep-adep sekul, the bride and bridegroom partake of *nasi ketan kuning*, or yellow sticky rice. The rice is arranged in a circle of 13 grains. The bride’s parents feed the couple 4 grains, the groom’s parents feed them another 4, and the bride and bridegroom feed each other 2 grains. The remaining 1 grain is contested and symbolizes abundant blessing, although it is not consumed but given to the spouse. The ceremony signifies harmony among the couple, their parents and their parents-in-law.

In Sawer, coins mixed with yellow rice and turmeric are scattered so that the couple may receive abundant fortune, mutual respect, harmony and suitability. The excited noises of people rushing for the money, rice and turmeric are called *Surak*.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They mention *macapat*, a love song with advice played after the parental-blessings ceremony, and Pewayangan from Cirebon, in which the Gegesik dialect may be used by the *Dalang*. No stories, literary works or preservation projects are described.

## Language & religion

Cirebonese combines elements of Javanese and Sundanese, with a heavier influence from Javanese. It was used in coastal trade in West Java from Cirebon, one of the major ports, particularly from the 15th century until the 17th century. The language has also been influenced by Chinese, Arab and European culture. The source gives examples including “Taocang,” a loanword from Hokkien; “Bakda,” from Arabic; and “Sonder,” absorbed from Dutch. It also preserves older Javanese forms such as “Ingsun” and “Sira.”

The language’s classification is debated. Linguistic studies describe it as a dialect, while the 5th Regional Regulation of West Java Provincial, 2003 recognizes it as a language. The sources name the Plered, Jaware, Dermayon, Gegesik and Jawareh dialects. Jawareh combines part Javanese and part Sundanese and is also called Sawareh, meaning “partial” or “half” Javanese. Cirebonese people are mainly Sunni Muslims, and Islamic principles are central to the Royal Wedding and its purification rites.
