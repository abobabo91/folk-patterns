## Overview

The Malay are a seafaring Austronesian people spread across Southeast Asia, unified by Islam, Bahasa Melayu, and a coherent material and performative culture that crystallized around the 15th-century Malacca Sultanate. They shaped the maritime world of the Strait of Malacca for centuries and spread their artistic traditions—textile arts, shadow theatre, martial forms, and courtly music—to sultanates throughout the region. Today their culture blends Islamic observance with older Hindu-Buddhist and animist practices, and their arts remain vigorous across Malaysia and adjacent lands.

## Material culture

Malay material culture is built on textiles, wood, and metal, with strong regional variations and a vocabulary of auspicious botanical motifs drawn from Islamic and courtly conventions.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Textiles are the heart of Malay material culture, with wax-resist batik, gold-thread brocade, and embroidery serving ceremonial and everyday needs.

- **Batik Malaysia** (*batik tulis, batik cap*) — Hand-drawn and block-printed wax-resist textiles with large open floral designs and bright dyes, avoiding living creatures due to Islamic convention on the east coast.
- **Songket** (*kain songket*) — Supplementary-weft brocade with gold or silver thread floated over silk or cotton, worn as sarongs and shoulder cloths at weddings and royal installations.
- **Tekat** — Couched gold-thread embroidery over padded card on velvet, historically a Perak royal-workshop tradition used for bridal pillows and prayer-mat borders.
- **Kelingkan** — Metal-thread embroidery with flat gold or silver strips stitched through fine fabric, most often used for wedding shawls.
- **Kain limar** — Weft-ikat silk with resist-dyed yarns, sometimes combined with songket borders, a prestige cloth of eastern Peninsular courts.

### Clothing & dress

Malay dress distinguishes formal court attire from everyday wear, with regional variations and ceremonial elaborations in textiles and metalwork.

- **Baju Melayu** — Men's loose long-sleeved shirt with mandarin collar paired with trousers and a short sarong of songket for formal occasions.
- **Baju kurung** — Women's knee-length tunic over an ankle-length sarong, the everyday female dress of the Malay world.
- **Kebaya** — Fitted open-front blouse of fine cotton fastened with brooches and worn with a batik sarong, a UNESCO-inscribed garment.
- **Keris dagger** (*keris*) — Wavy-bladed dagger with a stylised bird or demon hilt, tucked into the sarong at royal audiences and weddings.
- **Tengkolok headcloth** (*tengkolok*) — Folded head cloth for men, tied in named styles like dendam tak sudah, part of court dress.

### Architecture

Malay houses are raised timber structures with strong regional variation in roof form, ventilation, and interior division.

- **Rumah Melayu** — Raised timber post-and-beam house on hardwood stilts with vertical plank or woven bamboo walls and thatch or tile roof.
- **Rumah bumbung panjang** — Kelantan-Pattani house form with a long gabled roof, one of the strongest regional variations.
- **Rumah Melaka** — The Malacca house type, famous for its front staircase faced with polychrome ceramic tiles.
- **Rumah Minangkabau** — Negeri Sembilan house form borrowing upswept buffalo-horn gables from Sumatran kinfolk.
- **Kekisi carved panels** (*kekisi, pemeleh*) — Pierced timber ventilation panels with awan larat cloud fretwork carved into gable ends and walls.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Malay metalwork centres on brass and silver functional objects for betel service and ablution; ceramics and plaited mats serve domestic and ceremonial life.

- **Tepak sirih** — Betel set with a lidded lime container, areca-nut cutter, and spittoon, a marker of courtly hospitality.
- **Labu sayong** — Black-fired unglazed water gourd from Perak's Sayong village, the best-known Malay ceramic.
- **Keris blade** (*keris, pamor*) — Pre-eminent weapon-object with pattern-welded blade, often carved scabbard and hilt of kemuning wood.
- **Tikar mengkuang** (*tikar mengkuang, tikar pandan*) — Mat-plaiting in screwpine and pandanus producing sleeping mats, prayer mats, and woven food covers.
- **Talam brass tray** (*talam*) — Pedestalled brass tray used in gift-exchange rites and formal dining.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Bridal jewelry in gold uses filigree and granulation techniques and is concentrated in the Kelantan and Sumatran centers.

- **Kerongsang brooch set** (*kerongsang*) — Linked three-brooch set in gold that fastens the kebaya, a signature of formal dress.
- **Dokoh pendant** (*dokoh*) — Pendant necklace worn as bridal jewelry, often with filigree detail.
- **Cucuk sanggul hair pins** (*cucuk sanggul*) — Gold hair pins worn in the coiled bun of the bride as part of the bridal suite.
- **Gelang kaki anklets** (*gelang kaki*) — Heavy gold ankle bracelets worn at weddings and formal ceremonies.
- **Henna application** (*inai*) — Henna applied to the palms and fingertips of bride and groom on the malam berinai night before the wedding.

## Music & performance

Malay ensemble music is built on drums, gongs, and reeds, with distinct courtly, devotional, and social repertoires.

- **Gamelan Melayu** — Court ensemble of bronze saron, kenong, gambang, and gong, faster and more strongly pulsed than Javanese gamelan.
- **Nobat royal orchestra** (*nobat*) — Sacred court orchestra of Kedah, Perak, Selangor, and Terengganu sultans, played only for installations and royal deaths.
- **Dondang sayang** — Malaccan pantun-exchange love ballad in which two singers trade quatrains extemporaneously, accompanied by violin, rebana, and gong.
- **Dikir barat** — Kelantanese group-chant with a lead improviser, performed in competitive settings.
- **Kulintang** — Knobbed-gong row played in Sabah and Sulu-influenced Malay communities, UNESCO-inscribed.

## Dance & theatre

Malay theatre and dance range from classical court forms to social dances and martial performance, all deeply bound to music and seasonal rite.

- **Mak Yong** — Classical dance-drama of Kelantan and Patani with all-female cast enacting stories of the Dewa Muda cycle, UNESCO-inscribed.
- **Wayang Kulit Kelantan** (*wayang kulit*) — Shadow-puppet theatre drawing on the Hikayat Maharaja Wana, with intricately incised buffalo-hide puppets manipulated by the Tok Dalang.
- **Menora** (*menora, manora*) — Mask-and-headdress dance-drama of Patani-Kelantan Malays, sharing repertoire with Thai nora.
- **Silat** — Codified Malay martial art with regional streams like silat Gayong and silat Cekak, danced as silat pengantin at weddings, UNESCO-inscribed.
- **Joget couple dance** (*joget*) — Fast social dance for couples, performed to gamelan or pop music.

## Festivals & rituals

The Malay calendar is Islamic and lunar, marked by Hari Raya observances and life-cycle rites that blend Islamic form with older animist practice.

- **Hari Raya Aidilfitri** — Festival ending Ramadan with communal prayer, balik kampung visits, open-house hospitality, and ketupat rice-cake decorations.
- **Hari Raya Aidiladha** — Festival commemorating Abraham's sacrifice with the slaughter of cattle and goats.
- **Bersanding** — Wedding climax where bride and groom sit enthroned on the pelamin dais while guests sprinkle them with yellow rice and rosewater.
- **Malam berinai** — Henna night before the wedding when inai is applied to bride and groom.
- **Kenduri arwah** — Commemorative meal held on the third, seventh, fortieth, and hundredth days after a funeral.

## Foodways

The Malay kitchen is built on rice, coconut milk, and pounded aromatic pastes, with elaborate ceremonial dishes and iconic noodle soups.

- **Nasi lemak** — Rice cooked in coconut milk served with sambal, anchovies, peanuts, cucumber, and egg.
- **Rendang** — Slow-braised beef or chicken in coconut and rempah until dry, a signature ceremonial dish.
- **Laksa** — Family of noodle soups ranging from sour tamarind laksa Penang to rich coconut laksa lemak.
- **Satay** — Skewered grilled meat with peanut sauce, a street and ceremonial food.
- **Kuih sweets** (*kuih*) — Sweets combining glutinous rice flour, palm sugar, and pandan, including kuih lapis, seri muka, and dodol.

## Oral tradition & literature

Classical Malay literature preserved in Jawi script includes historical chronicles and heroic narratives; the pantun four-line poem dominates oral and everyday speech.

- **Sejarah Melayu** — Malay Annals (c. 1612) tracing the genealogy and court customs of Malacca.
- **Hikayat Hang Tuah** — Narrative of the loyal warrior Hang Tuah and his companion-turned-rival Hang Jebat.
- **Pantun** — Four-line poem in abab rhyme with a figurative couplet foreshadowing the meaning of the second, traded in courtship and everyday speech.
- **Syair** — Monorhymed quatrain form used in longer sung narratives and didactic verse.
- **Gurindam** — Didactic couplet form, often used for moral instruction and folk wisdom.

## Language & religion

Bahasa Melayu is Austronesian with strong dialectal variation; Malays are Sunni Muslims, and Islam defines Malay legal and cultural identity, though older Hindu-Buddhist and animist practices persist.

- **Jawi script** (*jawi*) — Arabic-derived script adapted for Malay phonemes, the historical written standard before Latin script adoption.
- **Kelantan-Pattani dialect** (*kelantan-pattani*) — One of the major Malay dialect blocs, spoken in the east coast sultanates.
- **Bomoh healer** (*bomoh*) — Traditional healer and shaman preserving pre-Islamic animist practice in healing seances and rice-cycle rites.
- **Semangat soul-force** (*semangat*) — Spirit or soul-force believed to reside in rice, iron, and the keris, a remnant of animist cosmology.
- **Adat customary law** (*adat*) — Malay customary law and practice, blending Islamic form with older ritual traditions.
