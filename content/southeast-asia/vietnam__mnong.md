## Overview

The Mnong, also known as Munong, are an ethnic group living mainly in Vietnam’s Central Highlands and Southeast regions, as well as in eastern Cambodia. They comprise Western Mnong and Eastern Mnong groups, including smaller communities such as Bhiet, Bu-Neur, Rehong, Kong-Khang, Ksèh, Nong, Prèh, Tí-Prì, Perong, Bu-Deung, Gar, Briet, Kil, Krieng, Kesiong, and Rlam. Around 47,000 Mnong people live in Cambodia’s northeastern boundary province of Mondulkiri, where they are also known as Bunong, Phnong, Punong, or Pnong. Their language belongs to the Austro-Asiatic family and, more specifically, the South Bahnaric group of the Mon–Khmer family. Their epic traditions, including *Ot N’rong*, occupy an important place in Mnong life.

## Music & performance

The principal performance tradition documented in the sources is the Mnong epic, called *Ot N’rong* in the Mnong language. The expression is explained as “telling by singing”: *Ot* means telling by singing, while *N’rong* means old story. These epics are important in Mnong people’s life, and several are described as very long.

The sources name *Con đỉa nuốt bon Tiăng*, also given as *Ghu sok bon Tiăng*, with the English title “The leech swallows Tiăng village.” They also name *Mùa rẫy bon Tiăng*, translated as “The farming season of Tiăng village.”

Điểu Kâu, Điểu Klứt, and Điểu Klung are identified as three brothers who collected, recorded, and spread M’nong epics. Điểu Klứt and Điểu Klung are described as epic tellers. Điểu Kâu was an ethnologist and was regarded by the M’nong people as the keeper of their cultural identity. He died of old age in August 2008, an event described as a great loss for the M’nong people.

## Festivals & rituals

They record that *Mùa rẫy bon Tiăng* concerns the farming season of Tiăng village.

The sources do document historical actions involving Mnong communities. N’Trang Lơng, also known as Pa-Trang-Loeng, was a tribal chief who led villagers against French colonizers in a 24 year uprising from 1912 to 1935. The sources identify the assassination of Henri Maitre as one of the best-known actions associated with N’Trang Lơng.

## Oral tradition & literature

Mnong epic tradition is called *Ot N’rong*. The name refers to the telling of old stories by singing, and the sources state that epics occupy an important part of Mnong life. The documented examples are *Con đỉa nuốt bon Tiăng*, also called *Ghu sok bon Tiăng*, and *Mùa rẫy bon Tiăng*. The first is translated as “The leech swallows Tiăng village,” while the second is translated as “The farming season of Tiăng village.” The sources emphasize that such epics can be quite long.

The preservation and circulation of M’nong epics are associated with the brothers Điểu Kâu, Điểu Klứt, and Điểu Klung, who collected, recorded, and spread them. Điểu Klứt and Điểu Klung are identified as epic tellers. Điểu Kâu’s death in August 2008 was described as a major loss because the M’nong people considered him the keeper of their cultural identity.

## Language & religion

Mnong, also known as Pnong or Bunong, belongs to the Austro-Asiatic language family. In the classification given by the sources, Mnong is also part of the South Bahnaric group of the Mon–Khmer family. It is spoken by different Mnong groups in Vietnam and by a Pnong group in Cambodia.

The sources identify four major dialects according to Ethnologue: Central, Eastern, and Southern Mnong, spoken in Vietnam, and Kraol, spoken in Cambodia. They state that members of different dialect groups do not understand other dialects. Mnong is spoken in Đăk Song, Đăk Mil, Đăk R'Lấp, Krông Nô, Gia Nghĩa, and other nearby locations in Đắk Nông Province. The sources also list varieties and subgroups including Mnông Gar, Mnông Nong, Mnông Kuênh, Mnông Pré, Mnông Prâng, Mnông Rlăm, Mnông Bu-đâng, Mnông Chỉl, Mnông Bu Nor, Mnông Dih Bri, Mnông Đíp, Mnông Biat, Mnông Bu Đêh, Mnông Si Tô, Mnông K’ah, and Mnông Phê Đâm.
