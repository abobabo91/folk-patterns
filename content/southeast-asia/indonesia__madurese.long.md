## Overview

The Madurese are an Austronesian ethnic group native to Madura Island and the northern part of the eastern salient of Java, off Java’s northeastern coast. Communities also live in eastern Java, Kalimantan, Jakarta, Bali, the Bangka Belitung Islands, and other parts of Indonesia. Around six million native Madurese live on Madura Island, while other sources give a nationwide population of approximately 10–13 million. They speak Madurese, a Malayo-Sumbawan language within the Malayo-Polynesian branch of the Austronesian family. Their cultural profile includes distinctive dialects and speech registers, Sunni Islam and Pesantren education, animal farming, salt extraction, fishing, trade, batik manufacture, bull racing, satay, Pencak silat, and traditional objects such as the *celurit* sickle and the kris.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Madurese batik** — Batik manufacturing is identified as an important occupation among Madurese craftsmen. The sources state that black, red, yellow, white, green, and blue are cultural symbols in traditional Madurese-style batik.

**Red-and-white striped cloth** — Men’s clothing includes a shirt with broad red and white stripes. The colors are described as deriving from the Majapahit naval flag, associated with a historical eastern Javanese empire that once controlled Madura Island.

The sources used do not document the construction, dyeing methods, named motifs, or specific textile forms associated with Madurese batik.

### Clothing & dress

Men traditionally wear a completely black long-skirted coat with a wide belt, usually fastened beneath a shirt with broad red and white stripes, together with a checkered sarong. The sources also describe the *celurit*, a traditional crescent knife, as both a common peasant weapon and, in some areas, an attribute of traditional male attire. Women wear a dark blue or mottled jacket over a sarong. The sources do not describe footwear, head coverings, ceremonial clothing, or distinctions between everyday and formal dress beyond these garments.

### Architecture

Traditional Madurese settlements are scattered and rarely linear, with their layout depending on the direction of roads. Villages commonly contain cattle paddocks. Houses are made of bamboo and often stand on low stilts. They have a frame structure, usually supplemented by a veranda. Roofs are covered with palm leaves or reeds, although roof tiles became increasingly common from the last third of the 20th century. The sources do not identify a named Madurese house type or provide further information about decoration or urban architecture.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery, tanning, blacksmithing, and boat building are among the occupations listed for Madurese craftsmen. The sources also identify cattle enclosures, bamboo houses, palm-leaf or reed roofing, and the *celurit* as elements of material life. Museum records show kris with sheaths and spears associated with Indonesian collections, including kris objects described as having Madurese connections, ivory, wood, steel, silver, brass, copper, hair, and pelet wood. One Museum of World Culture record describes a composite kris with an old ivory winged horse hilt from Madura, a Sumatran Minangkabau sheath, and a Madurese blade.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used do not document Madurese jewelry, tattooing, henna, hair practices, or ritual body adornment. Museum records mention ivory kris hilts, silver or decorated sheaths, gold inlay, polished quartz, brass, and a decorated dragon or snake, but these are features of weapon objects rather than documented personal adornment.

## Music & performance

Madurese culture shares forms of music with eastern Javanese culture, including gamelan. Gamelan performances accompany Karapan sapi bull races and are part of the folk festivities surrounding them. The sources do not name particular gamelan instruments or describe song genres, musical structures, performance settings beyond bull racing, or musicians’ roles. They also state that Madurese and eastern Javanese cultures share folklore, dance, and shadow theatre or *wayang*. The sources do not provide a separate catalogue of Madurese musical traditions, ceremonial repertories, or named instruments beyond gamelan.

## Dance & theatre

The Madurese share dance and shadow theatre or *wayang* with eastern Javanese culture. The sources do not name individual Madurese dances, mask traditions, dramatic genres, performers, or ceremonial contexts. Gamelan performances and folk festivities accompany Karapan sapi races, but the sources do not specify whether dance or theatre forms part of those performances.

## Festivals & rituals

Karapan sapi is a distinctive Madurese bull-racing tradition. Locally bred bulls are harnessed to special light carts and led by a charioteer, usually a young man or teenager. Competitions are typical of Madura and serve as a major tourist attraction. Races are held annually in August and October in different localities; the winners compete in a final round traditionally held in Pamekasan. Races are usually accompanied by gamelan performances and folk festivities. By the end of the 1980s, the winner received a prize on behalf of the president of Indonesia, and race scenes appeared on the reverse of 100 Indonesian rupiah coins produced from 1991 to 1998.

Family and Islam structure important parts of social life. Marriage proposals are usually made by the groom’s parents, preferably to a first or second cousin. If accepted, the bride’s parents receive a “bride price,” usually cattle, and the groom’s parents set the wedding date. Newly married couples often live with the bride’s family. The sources do not describe birth, coming-of-age, funeral, or annual religious festivals.

## Foodways

Madurese cuisine is associated with Madura Island, eastern Java, and the southern coast of Kalimantan. It is often saltier than eastern Javanese cuisine, reflecting Madura’s importance as a salt-production center, and it also shows significant Javanese influences. Dishes use *petis ikan*, which the source explains is made from shrimp despite the word *ikan*, meaning fish. Madurese-style satay is described as one of the most popular satay variants in Indonesia.

Named dishes include *sate madura*, usually made with mutton or chicken and characterized by a black sauce of sweet soy sauce, palm sugar, garlic, fried shallots, peanut paste, shrimp paste, candlenut, and salt; *sate ayam*; *sate kambing*; *soto madura*; goat soup; *sup kambing*; *bebek bumbu hitam*; *bebek guring*; *Bebek Sinjay*; *campor*; *kaldu kokot*; *nasek bebek*; *nasek campur*; *nasek cumi hitam*; *nasek jagung*; *nasek serundeng*; *rojak cengur*; and *serundeng*. Snacks and desserts include *apem*, *dawet*, and *lopis*. The sources state that the majority of Madurese practice Sunni Islam but do not separately describe dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Madurese share forms of folklore with eastern Javanese culture, together with music, dance, and shadow theatre or *wayang*. The sources do not name specific folktales, epics, proverbs, riddles, storytelling occasions, literary works, or contemporary preservation projects. They identify the Sumenep dialect as the basis of literary Madurese, but provide no literary history beyond that statement.

## Language & religion

Madurese belongs to the Malayo-Sumbawan group of the Malayo-Polynesian language family within the Austronesian family. It is native to Madura Island and eastern Java and is also spoken by migrants in other parts of Indonesia. The language has several dialects, including Sumenep, Sampang, Pamekasan, Bangkalan, Bawean, Sapudi, Pendalungan, and Giliraja–Raas. The Sumenep dialect is the standard form and underlies literary Madurese; Bangkalan is the most common variety and often functions as a lingua franca. Madurese was traditionally written in the Javanese script and is now more commonly written in the Latin and Pegon scripts. Its speech levels are *Èngghi-Bhunten*, *Èngghi-Enten*, and *Enjâ'-Iyâ*.

The majority of Madurese practice Sunni Islam. Muslim theologians and Pesantren Muslim schools play important roles in spiritual and social life, and Islam is integral to social, political, and economic life.
