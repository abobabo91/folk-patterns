## Overview

The Khmu are an ethnic group of Southeast Asia and the indigenous inhabitants of northern Laos. The majority live in Laos, where they constitute the largest minority ethnic group and comprise eleven percent of the total population. There are more than 568,000 Khmu around the world, including populations in Laos, the Central Highlands of Vietnam, Thailand, China and the United States. In Laos, Khmu communities reside mainly across ten northern provinces, including Luang Prabang, Phongsaly, Oudomxay, Bokeo and Luangnamtha. The Khmu language belongs to the Austro-Asiatic family and forms part of the Khmuic branch. Khmu culture includes swidden agriculture, communal village work, animistic practices, ritualized houses and villages, storytelling, rice-related ceremonies and dances performed for a good harvest.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Khmu houses and villages are described as ritualized spaces associated with the spirits of the land. Villages are typically enclosed by fences with three or four gates, separating the Khmu from granaries and barns. Altars are placed outside the perimeter to ward off fires and storms.

An ancient Khmuic house was traditionally laid along the direction from east to west, and doors were placed in the east and toward the north or south; a door was not built on the west side. Houses with straight leans had a large outside balcony connected to the east door by a five- or seven-step stair, as well as a lean room with an east or north door. Houses with bent leans, described as having a half-moon shape, had a large outside balcony and an inside balcony connected to the east door. Roofs were covered with wooden tiles or thatch.

Khmuic people opened the east doors in the early morning to let the rising sun shine into the house. The sources give explanations connected with expelling devils, ghosts, bad spirits, bacteria and insects, and exposing the body to early morning sunlight. The sources also state that most Khmuic people nowadays ignore these beliefs and rules when building houses.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do describe elevated structures used to store rice outside villages, protecting it from fire, mice and rats. Bronze drums were used in rice-harvesting rituals; Khmuic people called them *yan* or *heurbang greh*, meaning “harvest gong.”

### Jewelry & body adornment

Some Khmu are heavily tattooed for decorative and religious reasons.

## Music & performance

The sources describe the sharing of silver pipes around evening fires during storytelling sessions. The pipes originally contained opium but are now predominantly associated with tobacco.

A bronze drum, called *yan* or *heurbang greh*, is used in rituals connected with the rice harvest. When rice ripens and becomes yellow or orange, Khmuic people hold small rituals to sacrifice rice souls or spirits on rice fields, especially upland rice fields or swidden fields. During these rituals they strike the bronze drum and ask the sun to shine and prevent rain. If it rains, they also strike the drum and recite sayings asking for the rain to stop or ease. In the *Moun Greh ceremony*, the *yan* is struck to thank the sun for shining during rice harvesting. The sources state that this practice is rare in the present day.

The Khmu language material identifies a repository and workspace for Austroasiatic intangible heritage, as well as a database of audio recordings in Khmu containing basic Catholic prayers.

## Dance & theatre

Dances to propitiate the rice goddess are common among the Khmu people and are performed to ensure a good harvest.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources state that Khmuic people formerly celebrated four festivals: rice planting, rice harvesting, new year, and wash or get rid of sin festivals. In ancient times, the new year festival was celebrated on the first waxing moon day of the first lunar month. In the present day, the new year festival is mostly not practiced or, when it is practiced, is integrated with the harvesting festival.

Rice-related rituals are connected with the weather and the protection of the crop. During the harvesting season, when abnormal rain could damage rice, Khmuic people held small rituals on rice fields to sacrifice rice souls or spirits. The fields were mostly upland rice fields or swidden fields, using the slash-and-burn technique called *hre hngo*. The bronze drum was struck to ask the sun to shine well and prevent rain. During unusually heavy rain, the drum was also struck while sayings were recited to ask for the rain to ease or stop for a time.

Khmu animistic practices include reverence for the house spirit *Hroi gang*. Villagers believe that a house, village and its surroundings are integrated with the spirits of the land. In the past, each Khmu family was believed to be protected by a totem such as a boar or an eagle, which had helped an ancestor and continued to protect the family.

Traditional Khmu cemeteries are divided into four sections: one for natural deaths, one for accidental deaths, one for children and one for people who died away from home. The Khmu do not generally believe in rebirth. Taboo is important in traditional Khmu animism, and villagers believe that violations result in vengeance by spirits. Forbidden activities include touching altars or amulets representing the house spirit, birth ceremonies for children born feet-first, and entering a house without permission.

## Foodways

Khmu society is agricultural, while gathering, hunting, trapping and fishing are also parts of the Khmu lifestyle. Crops include rice, especially white and black sticky rice, as well as corn, bananas, sugar cane, cucumbers, beans, sesame and a variety of vegetables.

Most agricultural work in Khmu villages is performed communally so that villagers can combine their strength and complete the work quickly. Harvesting rice from the swidden field is generally performed by village women. Rice is stored outside the village in elevated structures, both to protect it from fire and to protect it from mice and rats.

Rice also has a ritual dimension. When it ripens and becomes yellow or orange, Khmuic people hold rituals for rice souls or spirits. The ceremonies use the *yan*, or bronze drum, and the *heurbang greh*, or harvest gong, to ask for favorable weather. Dances to propitiate the rice goddess are performed to ensure a good harvest.

## Oral tradition & literature

Khmu culture is traditionally passed down through the recital of stories around evening fires. Storytelling sessions involve sharing silver pipes, originally containing opium and now predominantly containing tobacco.

The Khmu language material mentions “Poetic Parallelism in Kmhmu Verbal Arts: From Texts to Performances” among its further-reading references. The sources also identify the RWAAI Repository and Workspace for Austroasiatic Intangible Heritage and a Khmu digital archive.

## Language & religion

Khmu belongs to the Austro-Asiatic language family and forms part of the Khmuic branch. Closely related languages named in the sources include Puoc, O’du and Kniang. Khmu is spoken in northern Laos and in adjacent areas of Vietnam, Thailand and China. It has several dialects but no standard variety. Dialects differ in consonant inventory, register and the degree of influence from surrounding national languages. They are generally mutually intelligible, although communication can be difficult between speakers of geographically distant dialects.

The dialects are broadly divided into Western Khmu and Eastern Khmu. Western Khmu uses a contrast between “lax” breathy and “tense” modal registers; in Khmu Roek, this contrast has developed into two phonemic tones with six phonetic realizations. Eastern Khmu lacks register or tone distinctions and instead uses contrasts among voiced, voiceless and aspirated voiceless stops, and among voiced, voiceless and pre-glottalized nasals.

The sources describe traditional Khmu religion as animistic. Reverence is offered to the house spirit *Hroi gang*, and houses, villages and surrounding areas are understood as integrated with spirits of the land. The sources also mention magic, the casting of spells, the telling of fortunes, totems, taboos and rice spirits.
