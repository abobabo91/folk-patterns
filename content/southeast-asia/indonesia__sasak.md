## Overview

The Sasak people, also called Sasaknese, mainly live on the island of Lombok in West Nusa Tenggara Province, Indonesia. They number around 3.6 million, representing approximately 85% of Lombok’s population. The Sasak are related in language and ancestry to the Balinese and to ethnic groups on Sumbawa. The Bayan people are a distinct Sasak group and are described as the oldest group on Lombok. Sasak is an Austronesian language, closely related to Balinese and Sumbawa and associated with the Malayo-Sumbawan branch in one classification. Sasak folk culture includes weaving, lontar-leaf literature, ritual readings, puppet theatre, dance, and religious practices combining Islam with earlier local and Hindu-Buddhist influences.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

* weaving — Weaving is an important part of Sasak culture and is known as *sèsèk* in the Sasak language. The source describes the process as threading threads one by one, tightening them, and forming cloth by beating the threads on a loom. The sound of the loom is associated with “sak-sak,” a term also connected in one etymological explanation with the name Sasak.

## Music & performance

The Sasak people’s performance culture includes Gandrung dance and Gendang beleq dance, both named in the overview of Sasak art performances.

Sasak puppet theatre is documented as a use of Kawi, a literary language based on Old Javanese. Kawi is used in Sasak puppet theatre, poetry, and some texts written on lontar leaves, sometimes mixed with Sasak.

The sources also describe *pepaòsan*, performances involving readings of lontar texts. Such readings occur on occasions including funerals, weddings, and circumcision ceremonies. One *pepaòsan* described by Peter K. Austin was performed as part of a circumcision ceremony in 2002, using paper copies of lontar texts rather than palm leaves.

## Dance & theatre

Gandrung dance and Gendang beleq dance are named as Sasak art performances.

The Sasak language source records Sasak puppet theatre as a performance tradition in which Kawi is used. It also records *pepaòsan*, performances based on the reading of lontar texts. These readings are connected with funerals, weddings, and circumcision ceremonies.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe several religious and life-cycle contexts. Lontar readings known as *pepaòsan* are performed at funerals, weddings, and circumcision ceremonies. Rural Sasak read lontar texts as part of a ritual intended to ensure the fertility of farm animals.

Most Sasaks today follow the Lima Waktu version of Islam, whose name means “Five Times” and refers to the five daily prayers. Wetu Telu Islam, whose name means “Three Times,” is practiced by Sasaks who pray three times a day. Large numbers of Wetu Telu adherents can still be found throughout Lombok, especially in Bayan, where the religion originated. Communities are also recorded in Mataram, Pujung, Sengkol, Rambitan, Sade, Tetebatu, Bumbung, Sembalun, Senaru, Loyok, and Pasugulan.

The sources describe the conversion of Sasaks to Islam as occurring between the late sixteenth century and early seventeenth century. Islamic beliefs were frequently mixed with Animist beliefs to create Wetu Telu. Balinese influence also affected Wetu Telu, while Balinese Hinduism remains visible in ancient Hindu pura on Lombok.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Sasak have a tradition of writing on dried leaves of the lontar palm. The oldest surviving lontar texts date to the nineteenth century. Many were collected by the Dutch and kept in libraries in Leiden or Bali; the Mataram Museum in Lombok also has a collection, while individuals and families on Lombok preserve texts as heirlooms passed between generations.

Lombok’s lontar texts are written in Sasak, Kawi, or a combination of the two. Kawi is a literary language based on Old Javanese. The texts are used in *pepaòsan* readings and in Sasak puppet theatre, poetry, and some other lontar-based texts. The readings may take place at funerals, weddings, and circumcision ceremonies. Rural Sasak also read lontar texts in a ritual connected with the fertility of farm animals.

The texts are written in *hanacaraka*, a script nearly identical to Balinese script. Its basic letters consist of a consonant plus the vowel “a”; other vowels are represented with diacritics, and final consonants or consonant clusters may also be encoded. This writing system is also called Sasak script.

## Language & religion

Sasak, also called Base Sasaq or Sasaknese, is spoken by the Sasak people on Lombok. It belongs to the Austronesian language family. One classification places it in the Malayo-Sumbawan branch, while another places the Balinese-Sasak-Sumbawa languages in a proposed “Western Indonesian” subgroup. Sasak has no official status; Indonesian is the national and official language in areas where Sasak is spoken.

The language has regional varieties including Kutó-Kuté, Nggetó-Nggeté, Menó-Mené, Ngenó-Ngené, and Meriaq-Meriku. These names reflect pronunciations of “like that” and “like this.” Sasak also has speech levels associated with the social relationship between speaker and addressee. The levels include low, mid-, and high forms, as well as humble-honorific usage. Non-low forms are known as *alus*, meaning “smooth” or “polite,” and are used in formal contexts and with social superiors.

Sasak is predominantly Muslim. Lima Waktu Islam is distinguished from Wetu Telu Islam, which combines Islamic beliefs with Animist elements. Before the widespread adoption of Islam, Lombok was primarily Hindu, and Balinese Hindu influence remains visible in ancient pura such as Gunung Pengsong, Lingsar, Meru, and Suranadi.
