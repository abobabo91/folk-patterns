## Overview

The Tausug, also spelled Tausog and natively called *Tau Sūg*, are an Austronesian ethnic group native to the Sulu Archipelago and northeastern coastal areas of Borneo, across present-day Philippines, Malaysia and Indonesia. Large populations live in mainland Mindanao, especially Zamboanga City, Cotabato City and Davao City, and on Palawan. Smaller communities are found in Nunukan and Tarakan in North Kalimantan, Indonesia. The Tausug numbered 1,226,601 in the Philippines in 2010. Their language belongs to the Bisayan family of Austronesian languages and is closely related to Butuanon and Surigaonon. Historically, the Tausug established the Sultanate of Sulu, a thalassocratic state whose political and commercial connections shaped the Sulu Archipelago. Folk-cultural life in the sources is especially connected with Islam, local political communities, oral accounts of conflict, and the Tausug language.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

 The sources do mention *budjak* spears, *barung* and *kalis* in accounts of armed attacks.

### Architecture

Before the establishment of the sultanate, Tausug communities were called *banwa*. Each *banwa* was headed by a *panglima* and a shaman called a *mangungubat*, and each community was considered an independent state. After the sultanate was established, the *banwa* was divided into districts administered by a *panglima*. The sources also mention a mosque built by Karim ul-Makhdum at Tubig-Indangan on Simunul, whose pillars still stand, and identify it as the first Islamic temple constructed in the area and the first in the Philippines. They further mention Cottas as captured fortifications and the construction of a fort and settlement in Zamboanga in 1635. No house form, roofing system, domestic architecture, or ornamental building tradition is described.

The museum catalogue records supplied for this atlas contain no objects. The written sources mention trade between Sulu and China, as well as exchange with Arab, Persian, Malay and Indian Muslims. ## Music & performance

 They do mention that Professor Nur Misuari’s MNLF performed *Ramayana* during a ceasefire agreement in General Santos City. The sources therefore do not support a fuller account of Tausug music.

## Dance & theatre

 They mention *Ramayana* as a performance undertaken by Professor Nur Misuari’s MNLF in General Santos City during a ceasefire agreement, but provide no information about choreography, staging, performers, ritual function, or whether the performance was specifically Tausug. No shadow-puppet, mask-dance, court-dance, or other theatrical tradition is documented.

## Festivals & rituals

 They state that the Tausug were Islamized in the 14th century and that Islam has been a defining aspect of native Sulu culture. The sources describe Sunni Islam, the Shafi‘i section of Islam, Ash‘ari belief, Sufism, and the continued practice of some pre-Islamic customs within *adat*. They mention *pagkaja* and other *palipalihan* as forms of folk-Islam, and state that some such practices were allowed by Ulama. The sources also describe religious preparation for *magsasabil* and *parang sabil*, associated with beliefs that participants would receive divine protection and that Allah would determine their fate.

## Foodways

 They state that Tausug religious life is predominantly Sunni Muslim and that Islam is central to Sulu culture, but they do not explicitly connect this information to food preparation or eating practices.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources mention a Tausug saying: “Mayayao pa muti in bukug ayaw in tikud-tikud,” translated as “It is preferable to see the whiteness of your bone due to wounds than whiten your heel from running away.” They also mention *parang sabil* tales, including “The Story of War in Zambo,” identified as *Kissa sin Pagbunu ha Zambo*, concerning the 2013 attack in Zamboanga by MNLF commander Ustadz Habier Malik. These accounts connect oral tradition with warfare, religious struggle, courage and dishonor. The sources also state that some Tausug who went on *parang sabil* did so to redeem themselves in causes of dishonor, called *hiya*. No epic cycle, folktale collection, riddle tradition, contemporary literary revival, or preservation project is described.

## Language & religion

Tausūg, also called *Bahasa Sūg*, *Sinūg*, Wikang Bahasa Súg and Bahasa Suluk, is an Austronesian language classified within the Bisayan languages family. It is spoken in the Sulu Archipelago, the Zamboanga Peninsula, southern Palawan, eastern Sabah, Tarakan City and Nunukan Regency in North Kalimantan. It has lexical similarities with Surigaonon and Butuanon and contains Malay and Arabic vocabulary. Tausūg is primarily written in the Latin alphabet; historically, it was written with an Arabic-derived script based on Jawi, called *Sulat Sug*. The overwhelming majority of Tausug follow Islam, particularly Sunni Islam in the Shafi‘i tradition, with Ash‘ari belief and Sufism. The sources also describe retained pre-Islamic practices and a mixture of Islam and Animism in *adat*. A Christian minority exists.
