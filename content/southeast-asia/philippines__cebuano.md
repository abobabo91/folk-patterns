## Overview

The Cebuano people (*Sugboanon*) are the largest subgroup of the Visayans, a wider ethnolinguistic group. They originate from Central Visayas, including Cebu, Siquijor, Bohol, Negros Oriental, western and southern Leyte, western Samar, Masbate, and large parts of Mindanao. In a narrower sense, *Sugbuanon* refers to the native inhabitants of Cebu Island; more generally, Cebuano denotes native speakers of the Cebuano language in different regions of the Philippine archipelago. Cebuano is an Austronesian language and the most widely spoken of the Visayan or Bisayan languages. It is spoken by more than twenty million people in the Philippines, while a 2020 journal estimated 15.9 million speakers and 2020 Philippine Statistics Authority statistics recorded approximately 1.72 million households speaking Cebuano.

## Material culture

### Jewelry & body adornment

Tattooing is documented as an important historical form of Cebuano body adornment. Early Spanish colonists referred to Cebuanos and other Visayans as the *pintados*, meaning “the painted ones,” because of the widespread practice of tattooing to record battle exploits.

## Festivals & rituals

The most specifically named Cebuano festival in the sources is **Sinulog**. It is celebrated annually every third week of January and is described as a mixture of Christian and native cultural elements.

The religious landscape connected with Cebuano cultural life is predominantly Roman Catholic. Cebuanos are described as the first Filipinos to convert to Christianity, and many people in rural areas combine Catholicism with indigenous Bisayan folk religion. A minority of Cebuanos, specifically those in Mindanao, are described as Roman Catholic, while mixed Chinese-Cebuano families may combine Catholic beliefs with aspects of Buddhism or Taoism.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do document several stages in the history of Cebuano writing. The earliest record of the language was a list of words compiled by Antonio Pigafetta during Ferdinand Magellan’s 1521 expedition. Spaniards recorded a Visayan script called *kudlit-kabadlit* by the natives, although surviving documentation of Cebuano written in a non-Latin script is described as rare. A report from 1567 described how natives wrote the language, while later accounts differed over whether the writing tradition had been learned from Malays or Tagalogs.

Cebuano written literature is generally said to have begun with Vicente Yap Sotto, who wrote “Maming” in 1901. An earlier, more patriotic piece by Sotto was published a year later because of American censorship during the US occupation of the Philippines. The sources also identify a conduct book written in 1852 by Fray Antonio Ubeda de la Santísima Trinidad.

## Language & religion

Cebuano is an Austronesian language spoken by the Bisaya people and other ethnic groups as a secondary language. It is natively and informally called *Bisayâ* or *Binisayâ*, both translated into English as Visayan, and it is sometimes called Cebuan in English sources. Cebuano is spoken in Cebu, Bohol, Siquijor, the eastern half of Negros, the western half of Leyte, northern coastal areas of Northern Mindanao, and the eastern part of Zamboanga del Norte. It has also spread to the Davao Region, Cotabato, Camiguin, parts of the Dinagat Islands, and lowland Caraga.

The language is written with the Latin script, using a writing convention based on Filipino orthography. Earlier writing systems included *badlit*, though surviving accounts are scarce. Cebuano has numerous regional varieties, including Boholano, Leyteño or Kanâ, Mindanao Cebuano, Davaoeño, Negrense Cebuano, and Tagalog-influenced varieties in Luzon sometimes called *Bisalog*. Cebuano is the lingua franca of Central Visayas, western parts of Eastern Visayas, some western parts of Palawan, and most of Mindanao.

The majority of Cebuanos are Roman Catholic. Rural communities may synchronize Catholicism with indigenous Bisayan folk religion, while some mixed Chinese-Cebuano families incorporate Buddhism or Taoism.
