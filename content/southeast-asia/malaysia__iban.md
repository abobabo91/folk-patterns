## Overview

The Iban are a Dayak sub-ethnic group of Borneo, concentrated in the Malaysian state of Sarawak, where they constitute 28.8% of the population, with smaller communities in Brunei and West Kalimantan, Indonesia. Their ancestral homeland is the Kapuas basin — specifically the Ketungau tributary — from which they began migrating northward into Sarawak in the mid-16th century, settling along the Batang Lupar, Saribas, Skrang, Rejang, and Baleh river systems. The Iban language (*jaku Iban*) belongs to the Malayic branch of the Austronesian family. Historically called "Sea Dayaks" by James Brooke to distinguish them from the more settled Bidayuh "Land Dayaks," the Iban are internationally significant for their longhouse society, their headhunting (*kayau*) warrior tradition, and above all for *pua kumbu*, the ritual ikat textile that ranks among Southeast Asia's most iconographically dense weaving traditions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Iban weaving (*betenun*) is women's cultural counterpart to men's headhunting: the two are explicitly paired in oral tradition as parallel "warpaths," and mastery of the loom carries comparable prestige.

**Pua kumbu** — the great ceremonial ikat blanket, warp-tied and dyed with natural pigments (traditionally *engkudu* — Morinda citrifolia root — for red-brown), then woven on a body-tension backstrap loom. Used to wrap heirloom skulls, cradle newborns, curtain shamans during trance, and drape the *ranyai* ritual tree; the most powerful designs are considered so spiritually charged that only senior weavers may attempt them.

**Pua ikat** — the broader category of resist-dyed Iban blankets, distinguished from *pua kumbu* proper by scale, function, or dye technique; some are supplementary-weft rather than pure ikat.

**Kain kebat** — a woman's tubular skirt (*bidang*) worked in warp ikat, worn wrapped from waist to knee and often paired with silver-coin belts for ceremonial dress.

**Kain sungkit** — supplementary-weft weaving in which coloured threads (often silver or gold thread on trade cloth) are wrapped around ground warps with a needle, producing raised, embroidery-like motifs on shoulder cloths and skirts.

**Kain karap** — a densely patterned woven cloth used for prestige garments and ritual hangings.

**Kalambi (baju burong)** — the "bird jacket," a short sleeveless ceremonial vest for men, either woven in *sungkit* or *pilih* (supplementary-weft float) technique; worn with a loincloth (*sirat*) for the *ngajat* dance and warrior rites.

**Selampai** — long, narrow ceremonial shoulder or head cloths, often worked in *sungkit*, exchanged as gifts and worn by both sexes at *gawai* festivals.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Gajah Meram* (brooding elephant), *Tiang Sandong* (ritual pole), *Meligai* (shrine), *Tiang Ranyai* (central festival tree-pole), *engkeramba* (anthropomorphic ancestor/spirit figures), *nabau* (mythic serpent), *buah* (generic "fruit"/motif unit), *kelikai* (fern-frond scrolls), and hornbill (*kenyalang*) forms tied to the war god Sengalang Burung. A weaver who has completed the full training curriculum earns the title *tembu kayu* ("finished the wood").

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress is now largely modern Malaysian, but ceremonial regalia is elaborate and distinctly Iban. Men wear the *sirat* (long loincloth), a woven *kalambi* or *baju burong* vest, and, for warriors and dancers, the *ketapu sylong* — a rattan headdress crowned with hornbill feathers, sometimes with a full plume for men of distinction. A short parang (*ilang* or *nyabor*) is sheathed at the hip. Women's ceremonial dress centres on the *kain kebat* skirt, a *marik empang* beaded shoulder yoke, and the *sugu tinggi* — a tall repoussé silver crown-comb — together with heavy silver coin belts (*lampit* and *rawai*, spiralling brass or silver corset-rings) that clink audibly during the *ngajat indu* women's dance. Coin necklaces of Straits and Mexican silver dollars, and silver bangles stacked from wrist to elbow, complete the array. Elderly women historically wore hand-tapped tattoos on hands and forearms; men bore throat, shoulder, and thigh tattoos marking journeys (*bejalai*) and, in the past, successful head-taking.

### Architecture

The defining Iban structure is the *rumah panjai* (longhouse), a raised communal building running parallel to a riverbank on hardwood *belian* (ironwood) piles two to three metres off the ground, roofed with *sago* palm thatch or hardwood shingles and, today, corrugated zinc. Its internal logic is standardised: each family owns a private apartment (*bilik*) opening onto a covered common gallery (*ruai*) that runs the full length of the house, in front of which lies an open verandah (*tanju*) for drying rice and pepper. Above the *ruai* runs a loft (*sadau*) used for storage and unmarried women's sleeping quarters. A longhouse of 20–50 *bilik* is typical; some historic examples exceeded 100 doors. Entry is by a notched log ladder (*tangga*). The house is governed communally by a headman (*tuai rumah*) under adat law, and the whole structure is aligned to the river, which functions as the community's road. Skull trophies (*antu pala*), when still present, hang in a rattan bundle above the family hearth on the *ruai*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Iban are not a ceramic-producing culture; instead, imported Chinese stoneware jars (*tajau*) — martaban and dragon jars traded from the Ming and Qing periods — are the supreme heirlooms, valued as brideprice, funerary vessels, and vehicles of spiritual potency. Blacksmithing produces the *parang ilang* and *nyabor* swords and the agricultural *duku*. Rattan and bamboo work is prolific: *tikai lampit* (rattan mats), *raga* baskets, *lulun* bark quivers for blowpipe darts, and the *selabit* woman's back-basket with woven ideograms. Blowpipes (*sumpit*) of two-metre bored *belian* wood, paired with a socketed spearhead at the muzzle, remain a living hunting tool. Ritual carving includes *engkeramba* wooden guardian figures set at longhouse steps and *tuntun* omen-sticks used in augury.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver dominates women's regalia: the *sugu tinggi* crown-comb of stamped silver plate, *marik empang* beaded collars worked in tiny glass trade beads, brass *rawai* corset-coils, silver *lampit* belts, and stacked silver bangles (*tumpa*). Men wear brass earrings that elongate the lobes and, in older photographs, boar-tusk armlets. The Iban are one of Borneo's classic tattooing peoples: the *bunga terung* ("eggplant flower") shoulder rosette marks a young man's first *bejalai* journey, throat tattoos (*ukir rekong*) were once reserved for successful headhunters, and the *ketam lengan* wrist band and thigh scale-work (*pala tumpa*) accumulate through a lifetime. Designs are hand-tapped with a mallet and thorn needle using soot ink, following stencils (*kelingai*) cut from bark.

## Music & performance

Iban music is percussion-led. The core ensemble is the *taboh* or *gendang* — a suite of bossed bronze gongs comprising the *engkerumung* (a row of small kettle-gongs played melodically like a metallophone), the deep-voiced *tawak* (bass gong), the *bebendai* (mid-range snare-role gong), and the single-headed *ketebung* or *bedup* drum. Two named gong pieces dominate: *gendang rayah* ("swinging blow"), which accompanies the trance-like *rayah* invocation dance, and *gendang pampat* ("sweeping blow") for warrior contexts. Slower ceremonial tempos are called *alun lundai*. Solo lyrical music centres on the *sape* — a boat-shaped lute more strongly associated with the Kenyah and Kayan but also played by Iban — and the nose flute (*ensuling*). Vocal genres include the epic chant *pengap* or *timang*, sung by the ritual bard (*lemambang*) through the night at major *gawai* festivals; the courtship *pantun* quatrains; the mourning lament *sabak*, wailed by a female specialist; and the *renong* narrative songs. The *lemambang*'s chants explicitly follow the twenty-seven stages of the paddy cycle, mapping agriculture, headhunting, and cosmology onto a single ritual text.

## Dance & theatre

The principal Iban dance is the *ngajat*, a solo warrior dance performed by men to gong ensemble, with sharp bent-knee stances, slow rotations, and a *terabai* shield and *ilang* sword; the *ngajat lesong* is the virtuoso version in which the dancer lifts a heavy rice-pounding mortar with his teeth. Women perform *ngajat indu*, characterised by finely articulated fingers extended by long brass *sugu* nail-extensions and a stately gliding step. Ritual specialists perform the trance *rayah*, circling a central *ranyai* ritual tree. The Iban do not have a masked or puppet-theatre tradition comparable to Javanese *wayang*; dramatic content is instead carried by the *lemambang*'s sung epic and by the choreographed sequences of the *gawai* rites themselves.

## Festivals & rituals

The great annual celebration is **Gawai Dayak** on 1–2 June, marking the end of the rice harvest and now a Sarawak state holiday; longhouses open to guests, rice wine (*tuak*) is poured, and the *miring* offering ceremony presents seven plates to the gods. Major traditional *gawai* form a graded ritual hierarchy: **Gawai Batu** (whetstone festival, opening the farming year), **Gawai Benih** (seed festival), **Gawai Burong** (bird festival, invoking Sengalang Burung the war-god hornbill deity), **Gawai Kenyalang** (hornbill festival — the highest prestige rite, in which a carved hornbill effigy is paraded and consecrated), and **Gawai Antu** (festival of the dead), a triennial or generational mortuary rite in which a carved wooden mausoleum (*sungkup*) is dedicated to deceased kin and *garong* baskets of offerings are hung. Life-cycle rites include the naming ceremony (*melah pinang*), the *bejalai* rite-of-passage journey undertaken by young men, marriage negotiated through brideprice paid in *tajau* jars, and multi-stage secondary burial. Augury from the calls and flight of seven omen birds (notably the crested jay *ketupong* and the Brahminy kite *lang*) governs farming, travel, and war.

## Foodways

Hill rice (*padi bukit*) is the staple, grown by shifting cultivation in the twenty-seven-stage cycle documented by Derek Freeman, and eaten with river fish, wild boar, venison, and jungle ferns (*paku*). Signature preparations include *pansuh* (or *pansoh manok*) — chicken with lemongrass, tapioca leaves, and *bungkang* leaves stuffed into a bamboo tube and roasted over open fire; *tuak*, glutinous-rice wine fermented with rice yeast (*ragi*) and served from ceramic jars at every *gawai*; *ai buah* or *langkau*, a stronger distilled rice spirit; *tempoyak*, fermented durian relish; *kasam* (or *pekasam*), salt-fermented river fish or wild boar preserved in rice; *lulun*, glutinous rice cooked in bamboo; *midin*, a stir-fried wild jungle fern endemic to Sarawak; and *manok kacangma*, chicken with motherwort traditionally served to women in postpartum confinement. Rice, tuak, and a chicken sacrifice form the minimum triad of any *miring* offering.

## Oral tradition & literature

Iban oral literature is exceptionally elaborate and functionally central. History is inscribed on *papan turai*, incised wooden boards recording migration routes, genealogies (*tusut*), and adat law; these are read aloud by memory-specialists. Ritual chants divide into genres by function: *pengap* and *timang* (invocations sung to summon the gods to a *gawai*), *sabak* (funerary lament sung by a female *tukang sabak*), *renong* (narrative song), *ensera* (prose folktales), and *jaku silup* (metaphorical courting speech). Central mythic figures include Sengalang Burung, god of war and augury; his son-in-law Sera Gunting, the culture hero who received adat law and taught the reading of omen birds; and the war-hero *raja berani* cycles centred on Keling of Panggau Libau, the Iban warrior paradise. The 20th-century historian Benedict Sandin, himself Iban, produced foundational transcriptions and translations of migration epics (*The Sea Dayaks of Borneo Before White Rajah Rule*, 1967), and the *lemambang* Ngumbang and others were recorded by the Tun Jugah Foundation, which continues to publish critical editions.

## Language & religion

*Jaku Iban* is a Malayic Austronesian language mutually intelligible across regional dialects (Saribas, Skrang, Batang Ai, Rejang, Balau) and is taught as an elective in Sarawak schools; it was historically unwritten, with the *papan turai* pictoglyphs serving mnemonic rather than phonetic ends, and is now written in Latin script. Religiously, the majority of Iban today are Christian — mostly Anglican, Roman Catholic, and Methodist, following 19th- and 20th-century mission activity — while a smaller number in Brunei have converted to Islam through Malay intermarriage. The pre-Christian religion, sometimes glossed as *pagan* in Iban self-description, centres on a pantheon led by Sengalang Burung and the creator Petara, mediated by omen birds and by the ritual specialist *manang* (shaman) who conducts curing rites in trance. *Miring* offerings, *gawai* cycles, and augury persist widely alongside Christian practice, though certain churches now discourage *miring* and *Gawai Antu*.
