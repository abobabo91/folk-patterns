## Overview

The Iban migrated northward from the Kapuas basin into Sarawak beginning in the mid-16th century, settling along major river systems. They are famous for their elaborate ikat weaving, which rivals headhunting in cultural prestige, and for their longhouse villages governed by adat law. Their society centers on rice farming, warrior traditions, and ritual cycles marked by gawai festivals. Today most Iban are Christian, though traditional practices and beliefs persist.

## Material culture

Iban material culture emphasizes textiles, particularly ritual ikat blankets, alongside architectural, musical, and decorative arts that blend practical function with spiritual meaning.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Weaving is women's parallel to men's headhunting, requiring mastery of ikat, supplementary-weft, and resist-dye techniques on the backstrap loom.

- **Pua kumbu** — The great ceremonial ikat blanket, warp-tied and dyed with natural pigments, used to wrap skulls, cradle infants, and drape shamans; the most powerful designs are spiritually charged and restricted to senior weavers.
- **Kain kebat** — A woman's tubular warp-ikat skirt worn from waist to knee, often paired with silver-coin belts for ceremonial dress.
- **Kain sungkit** — Supplementary-weft weaving with coloured or metallic threads wrapped around ground warps, producing raised embroidery-like motifs on shoulder cloths and skirts.
- **Kalambi or bird jacket** (*kalambi (baju burong)*) — A short sleeveless ceremonial vest for men, woven in supplementary-weft technique, worn with a loincloth for the ngajat dance and warrior rites.
- **Selampai** — Long, narrow ceremonial shoulder or head cloths, often in sungkit technique, exchanged as gifts and worn by both sexes at gawai festivals.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress is now modern Malaysian, but ceremonial regalia is elaborate, with men wearing loincloths and woven vests and women displaying heavy silver jewelry and tall crown-combs.

- **Sirat** — A long woven loincloth worn by men as ceremonial dress.
- **Ketapu sylong** — A rattan headdress crowned with hornbill feathers, worn by warriors and dancers, sometimes with a full plume for distinguished men.
- **Sugu tinggi** — A tall repoussé silver crown-comb worn by women as a centerpiece of ceremonial dress.
- **Lampit and rawai** (*lampit, rawai*) — Silver and brass belts and corset-rings that spiral around the waist and clink audibly during women's dances.
- **Tattoo marks** (*bunga terung, ukir rekong, ketam lengan*) — Hand-tapped designs marking journeys, successful head-taking, and life stages, applied by mallet and thorn needle using soot ink.

### Architecture

The rumah panjai longhouse is the defining structure: a communal raised building running parallel to a river, with private family apartments and shared galleries governed by a headman under adat law.

- **Rumah panjai** — A raised communal longhouse on hardwood piles two to three meters high, roofed with sago thatch or corrugated zinc, containing 20–50 family apartments (bilik) opening onto a shared gallery (ruai).
- **Bilik** — A private family apartment within the longhouse, opening onto the shared gallery.
- **Ruai** — The covered common gallery running the full length of the longhouse where the family hearth and skull trophies hang.
- **Tanju** — An open verandah in front of the ruai used for drying rice and pepper.
- **Sadau** — A loft above the ruai used for storage and sleeping quarters for unmarried women.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Iban are not ceramic producers but prize imported Chinese stoneware jars as supreme heirlooms; blacksmithing and bamboo and rattan work are prolific.

- **Tajau** — Imported Chinese stoneware jars from Ming and Qing periods, valued as brideprice, funerary vessels, and vehicles of spiritual potency.
- **Parang ilang and nyabor** (*parang ilang, nyabor*) — Short swords forged by blacksmiths for warriors, sheathed at the hip.
- **Sumpit** — A two-metre blowpipe of bored belian wood paired with a socketed spearhead, used as a living hunting tool.
- **Raga and lulun** (*raga, lulun*) — Rattan baskets and bark quivers used for storage and carrying blowpipe darts.
- **Engkeramba** — Carved wooden guardian figures set at longhouse steps to ward off harm.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver dominates women's jewelry, featuring crown-combs, beaded collars, stacked bangles, and coin belts, while men wear brass earrings and historical boar-tusk armlets.

- **Sugu tinggi** — A tall stamped silver crown-comb worn as the centerpiece of women's ceremonial dress.
- **Marik empang** — Beaded shoulder yokes worked in tiny glass trade beads, worn by women.
- **Tumpa** — Stacked silver bangles worn by women from wrist to elbow.
- **Tattoo designs** (*bunga terung, ukir rekong, pala tumpa*) — Hand-tapped marks including shoulder rosettes marking first journeys, throat tattoos reserved for headhunters, and wrist bands and thigh scale-work accumulated over a lifetime.

## Music & performance

Iban music is percussion-led, centered on bronze gong ensembles accompanied by the sape lute, nose flute, and epic chanting by ritual bards.

- **Taboh or gendang** (*taboh, gendang*) — A suite of bossed bronze gongs including the engkerumung (melodic kettle-gongs), tawak (bass gong), bebendai (snare-role gong), and single-headed drum.
- **Gendang rayah** — A named gong piece played for the trance-like rayah invocation dance.
- **Sape** — A boat-shaped lute used for solo lyrical music.
- **Pengap or timang** (*pengap, timang*) — Epic chants sung by the lemambang ritual bard through the night at major gawai festivals, following the twenty-seven stages of the paddy cycle.
- **Pantun and sabak** (*pantun, sabak*) — Courtship quatrains and mourning laments wailed by female specialists during life-cycle rites.

## Dance & theatre

The ngajat is the principal warrior dance, performed solo to gongs with sharp stances and shield-and-sword; women perform ngajat indu with stately gliding steps and extended fingers.

- **Ngajat** — A solo warrior dance performed by men to gong ensemble, featuring sharp bent-knee stances, slow rotations, and terabai shield and ilang sword.
- **Ngajat lesong** — The virtuoso version of the ngajat dance in which the dancer lifts a heavy rice-pounding mortar with his teeth.
- **Ngajat indu** — Women's dance characterized by finely articulated fingers extended by long brass sugu nail-extensions and a stately gliding step.
- **Rayah** — A trance dance performed by ritual specialists, circling a central ranyai ritual tree.

## Festivals & rituals

Gawai Dayak on 1–2 June marks the rice harvest end and opens the longhouse to guests; other gawai form a hierarchy culminating in Gawai Kenyalang, the highest prestige hornbill festival.

- **Gawai Dayak** — The great annual celebration on 1–2 June marking the end of rice harvest, now a Sarawak state holiday, where longhouses open to guests and miring offerings are made to the gods.
- **Gawai Kenyalang** — The highest prestige festival in the gawai hierarchy, in which a carved hornbill effigy is paraded and consecrated to honor the war-god.
- **Gawai Antu** — A triennial or generational mortuary festival in which a wooden mausoleum (sungkup) is dedicated to deceased kin and offering baskets hang as memorials.
- **Bejalai** — A rite-of-passage journey undertaken by young men, marked by shoulder tattoos and recorded in adat law.
- **Miring** — An offering ceremony presenting seven plates to the gods, performed at every gawai festival using rice, tuak, and a chicken sacrifice as the minimum triad.

## Foodways

Hill rice grown in a twenty-seven-stage cycle is the staple, eaten with river fish, wild boar, venison, and jungle ferns; tuak rice wine features at every gawai.

- **Padi bukit** — Hill rice grown by shifting cultivation in a twenty-seven-stage cycle, the staple carbohydrate eaten at every meal.
- **Pansuh** (*pansuh (pansoh manok)*) — Chicken with lemongrass, tapioca leaves, and bungkang leaves stuffed into a bamboo tube and roasted over an open fire.
- **Tuak** — Glutinous-rice wine fermented with rice yeast (ragi) and served from ceramic jars at every gawai festival.
- **Tempoyak** — Fermented durian relish eaten as a condiment.
- **Kasam and lulun** (*kasam (pekasam), lulun*) — Salt-fermented river fish or boar preserved in rice, and glutinous rice cooked in bamboo tubes.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral literature is exceptionally elaborate and functionally central: genealogies and adat law are inscribed on wooden boards and recited by memory-specialists, while ritual chants summon the gods to festivals.

- **Papan turai** — Incised wooden boards recording migration routes, genealogies (tusut), and adat law, read aloud by memory-specialists.
- **Pengap and timang** (*pengap, timang*) — Epic invocations sung by the lemambang to summon the gods to a gawai festival, mapping agriculture, headhunting, and cosmology onto ritual texts.
- **Sabak** — Funerary lament sung by a female tukang sabak specialist during mortuary rites.
- **Sengalang Burung and Sera Gunting** (*Sengalang Burung, Sera Gunting*) — The god of war and augury and his son-in-law the culture hero, central figures in Iban mythology.
- **Raja berani cycles** (*raja berani*) — War-hero narrative cycles centered on Keling of Panggau Libau, the legendary Iban warrior paradise.

## Language & religion

Jaku Iban is a Malayic Austronesian language now written in Latin script; the majority of Iban today are Christian, while pre-Christian practices centered on omen birds and the shaman (manang) persist.

- **Jaku Iban** — A Malayic Austronesian language mutually intelligible across regional dialects, historically unwritten but now written in Latin script and taught in Sarawak schools.
- **Papan turai** — Incised wooden pictoglyphs serving mnemonic rather than phonetic ends, recording genealogies and adat law.
- **Petara** — The creator deity in the pre-Christian Iban pantheon, still invoked in traditional rites.
- **Manang** — A ritual specialist or shaman who conducts curing rites in trance and mediates between the human and spirit worlds.
- **Ketupong and lang** (*ketupong, lang*) — The crested jay and Brahminy kite, two of seven omen birds whose calls and flight govern farming, travel, and war decisions.

## Glossary

- *adat* — customary law and tradition governing longhouse community
- *bejalai* — rite-of-passage journey undertaken by young men
- *bilik* — private family apartment within a longhouse
- *bungkang* — leaves used in cooking pansuh
- *engkeramba* — carved wooden guardian figures or ancestor/spirit forms
- *gawai* — festival or ceremony
- *gendang* — bronze gong ensemble
- *ilang* — short sword or parang worn by warriors
- *jaku Iban* — the Iban language
- *kenyalang* — hornbill; motif and deity form
- *lemambang* — ritual bard or chant specialist
- *manang* — ritual specialist or shaman
- *miring* — offering ceremony to the gods using seven plates
- *ngajat* — warrior dance performed to gongs
- *padi bukit* — hill rice, the staple crop
- *papan turai* — incised wooden boards recording genealogies and law
- *pua kumbu* — ritual ikat blanket, most spiritually charged textile
- *ragi* — rice yeast used to ferment tuak wine
- *ranyai* — ritual tree-pole central to ceremonies
- *ruai* — covered gallery running the length of a longhouse
- *sape* — boat-shaped lute for solo lyrical music
- *tuak* — glutinous-rice wine fermented with ragi
- *tusut* — genealogy recorded on papan turai
