## Overview

The Malay (*Orang Melayu*) are an Austronesian ethnoreligious group native to the Malay Peninsula, eastern Sumatra, coastal Borneo, and the Riau Archipelago, today distributed across Malaysia, Indonesia, Singapore, Brunei, southern Thailand (Pattani, Yala, Narathiwat), and the southern tip of Myanmar. They number roughly 30 million across the region, with about 15 million in Peninsular Malaysia alone. Their language, Bahasa Melayu, belongs to the Malayic branch of the Austronesian family and served as the maritime lingua franca of Southeast Asia from the Srivijaya era onward. In folk-culture terms the Malays matter because the 15th-century Malacca Sultanate codified a template — Islam, courtly Malay, adat (customary law), textile arts, martial arts, and performing traditions — that later sultanates from Aceh to Brunei emulated, producing an unusually coherent yet regionally inflected material and performative culture along the Strait of Malacca and beyond.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Batik Malaysia** — hand-drawn (*batik tulis*) and block-printed (*batik cap*) wax-resist cotton and silk, distinguished from Javanese batik by larger, more open floral compositions, brighter aniline dye palettes, and a preference for botanical rather than figurative motifs, since Islamic convention on the east coast (Kelantan, Terengganu) discouraged depiction of living creatures.

**Songket** — supplementary-weft brocade woven on a floor loom, in which gold or silver-wrapped thread is floated over a silk or cotton ground. Terengganu, Kelantan, and the Sumatran Malay courts of Palembang and Siak are the great centres; ceremonial *kain songket* is worn as sarong and shoulder cloth at weddings and installations.

**Tekat** — couched gold-thread embroidery worked over a padded card template on a velvet ground, historically a Perak royal-workshop tradition. It ornaments bridal pillows (*bantal berperada*), fan cases, prayer-mat borders, and the *pelamin* (bridal dais).

**Kelingkan** — metal-thread embroidery in which flat strips of gold or silver are stitched through fine muslin or organza, most often used for the *selendang* (shawl) draped over the head at Malay weddings.

**Kain limar** — weft-ikat silk in which the weft yarns are resist-dyed before weaving, sometimes combined with supplementary *songket* borders. A prestige cloth of the eastern Peninsular sultanates.

**Tenun Pahang** — indigenous check and stripe silk weaving of Pahang, revived under royal patronage; distinct from *songket* in being a plain-weave patterned cloth rather than a brocade.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Bunga tanjung* (Cape jasmine), *pucuk rebung* (bamboo shoot, arranged as triangular border), *awan larat* (meandering cloud tendrils), *bunga cengkih* (clove flower), *bunga raya* (hibiscus), *itik pulang petang* (ducks returning at dusk, a rare zoomorphic motif), *bunga kunyit* (turmeric flower), *tampuk manggis* (mangosteen calyx), *siku keluang* (bat's elbow lozenge).

### Clothing & dress

Men wear the *baju Melayu*, a loose long-sleeved shirt with a mandarin collar (*cekak musang*) or open neckline (*teluk belanga*) paired with matching trousers, over which a short sarong (*sampin* or *samping*) of *songket* is folded at the waist for formal occasions. A soft brimless cap (*songkok*) or a folded head cloth (*tengkolok*, tied in named styles such as *dendam tak sudah*) completes court dress. The *keris*, a wavy-bladed dagger with a hilt carved as a stylised bird or *jawa demam* figure, is tucked into the *sampin* at royal audiences and weddings.

Women wear the *baju kurung*, a knee-length loose tunic over an ankle-length sarong, or the *kebaya* (UNESCO ICH RL/02090), a fitted open-front blouse of fine cotton or *voile* fastened with brooches (*kerongsang*) and worn with a batik sarong. The *selendang*, often of *kelingkan* work, is draped over the head or shoulder; the *tudung* headscarf has become general daily wear since the late 20th century. Footwear at court was traditionally embroidered velvet slippers (*capal* or *kasut manik*, the latter beaded).

### Architecture

The *rumah Melayu* is a raised timber post-and-beam house on hardwood stilts, walled with vertical planks or woven bamboo, and roofed in *attap* thatch or terracotta tile. Regional forms are strongly differentiated: the Kelantan-Pattani *rumah bumbung panjang* has a long gabled roof; the *rumah bumbung Perak* uses a hipped roof; the *rumah Melaka* is famous for its front staircase (*tangga Melaka*) faced with polychrome ceramic tiles; the Negeri Sembilan *rumah Minangkabau* borrows the upswept buffalo-horn gables of Sumatran kin. Carved timber panels (*kekisi*, *pemeleh*) with pierced floral *awan larat* fretwork ventilate walls and gable ends. Interiors are divided into the *serambi* (verandah for male guests), *rumah ibu* (main living hall), and *dapur* (kitchen wing). Palaces (*istana*) such as Istana Kenangan in Kuala Kangsar extend the same vocabulary in bamboo-lath walls and elaborate finials (*sulur bayung*), while the mosque form ranges from tiered pyramidal roofs (Masjid Kampung Laut, Kelantan) to Indo-Saracenic domes of the colonial period.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Malay metalwork centres on brass and silver: the *tepak sirih* (betel set) with its lidded lime container (*bekas kapur*), areca-nut cutter (*kacip*), and spittoon (*ketur*); the pedestalled brass tray (*talam*); and the ewer and basin (*kendi* and *bokor*) used for guest ablutions. Kelantan and Terengganu smiths are known for *tekat perak* (raised silverwork) and repoussé betel boxes. The *keris* is the pre-eminent weapon-object, its blade forged with pattern-welded *pamor* and its scabbard (*sarung*) and hilt (*hulu*) carved from *kemuning* wood. *Labu sayong*, a black-fired unglazed water gourd of Perak's Sayong village, is the best-known Malay ceramic. Domestic mat-plaiting (*tikar mengkuang*, *tikar pandan*) in screwpine and pandanus produces sleeping mats, prayer mats, and food covers (*tudung saji*) with plaited geometric bands.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Bridal jewelry sets in gold include the *kerongsang* (a linked three-brooch set fastening the *kebaya*), the *dokoh* pendant necklace, the *cucuk sanggul* hair pins worn in the coiled bun of the bride, and heavy *gelang kaki* anklets. Filigree (*tali air*) and granulation are the signature techniques, worked in Kelantan and in Sumatran Malay centres such as Kota Gede-influenced Deli. Henna (*inai*) is applied to the palms and fingertips of the bride and groom on the *malam berinai* night before the wedding. Face-whitening rice paste (*bedak sejuk*) and the chewing of *sirih* (which stained teeth and lips red) were older cosmetic practices, the latter now largely ceremonial.

## Music & performance

Malay ensemble music is built around drums, gongs, and reeds. The *gamelan Melayu* of the Pahang and Terengganu courts uses bronze *saron*, *kenong*, *gambang*, and *gong*, distinct from Javanese gamelan in its faster, more strongly pulsed repertoire that accompanies the *joget gamelan* court dance. Frame-drum ensembles include *kompang* (single-headed, played in interlocking rows at wedding processions), *rebana ubi* (giant Kelantanese log drums beaten in competition), and *hadrah*. The oboe-like *serunai* leads the *nobat* royal ensemble — the sacred court orchestra of the Kedah, Perak, Selangor, and Terengganu sultans, comprising *nafiri* trumpet, *gendang nobat* drums, and *nengkara* kettledrum, played only for installations and royal deaths.

Sung genres include *dondang sayang* (UNESCO ICH RL/01410), the Malaccan pantun-exchange love ballad accompanied by violin, rebana, and gong, in which two singers trade quatrains extemporaneously; *ghazal Melayu* of Johor, an Arabised salon song using harmonium and tabla; *asli*, *inang*, and *joget* dance-song forms; and *dikir barat*, the Kelantanese group-chant with a lead *tukang karut* improviser. The *kulintang* (UNESCO ICH RL/02131) knobbed-gong row is played in the Sabah and Sulu-influenced Malay periphery. In the *nasyid* devotional tradition, unaccompanied male vocal groups sing Islamic praise poetry.

## Dance & theatre

**Mak Yong** (UNESCO ICH RL/00167) is the classical dance-drama of Kelantan and Patani, once performed only at the royal court, in which an all-female cast (apart from the clowns) enacts stories of the *Dewa Muda* cycle to the accompaniment of *rebab*, *gendang*, and *tetawak* gongs. It was long suspected of pre-Islamic ritual origins and remained partially restricted in Kelantan.

**Wayang Kulit Kelantan** is Malay shadow-puppet theatre, drawing on the *Hikayat Maharaja Wana*, a Malay recension of the Ramayana, with intricately incised buffalo-hide puppets manipulated by the *Tok Dalang*.

**Menora** (or *Manora*) is the mask-and-headdress dance-drama of Patani-Kelantan Malays, sharing repertoire with Thai *nora*. **Silat** (UNESCO ICH RL/01504), the codified Malay martial art with regional streams such as *silat Gayong*, *silat Cekak*, and *silat Tua*, is danced as *silat pengantin* at weddings, two performers circling to the *gendang silat* drum and *serunai*. Social dances include the fast **joget** couple dance, the languid **zapin** of Arab origin, the **inang** courtly walking dance, and the **kuda kepang** hobby-horse trance dance of Malay-Javanese communities.

## Festivals & rituals

The Malay calendar is Islamic and lunar. **Hari Raya Aidilfitri** ends Ramadan with communal prayer, visits to parental homes (*balik kampung*), open house hospitality, and green *ketupat* rice cakes hung as door decorations; **Hari Raya Aidiladha** commemorates the sacrifice of Ibrahim with the slaughter of cattle and goats (*korban*). *Mawlid* (**Maulidur Rasul**) processions and *nasyid* singing mark the Prophet's birthday. **Awal Muharram** opens the Islamic new year.

Rice-cycle observances persist alongside these: the Kedah *puja pantai* (sea offering), the *menuai* harvest, and (in syncretic Kelantanese and Terengganu villages) *main puteri* healing seances led by a *bomoh* using *rebab* and trance. The Chinese-Malay Peranakan community observes the *Ong Chun* boat-burning rite (UNESCO ICH RL/01608) in Malacca to appease sea spirits.

Life-cycle rites include *cukur jambul* (the shaving of a newborn's hair on the seventh day, accompanied by *kompang*), *bersunat* (circumcision, once a courtly rite of passage), and the multi-stage Malay wedding: *merisik* (discreet enquiry), *meminang* (betrothal), *hantaran* (gift exchange arranged on decorated trays), *akad nikah* (Islamic marriage contract), *malam berinai* (henna night), and the culminating *bersanding*, when bride and groom sit enthroned on the *pelamin* dais in matching *songket* while guests sprinkle them with yellow rice and rosewater. Funerals follow Islamic rite with the *kenduri arwah* commemorative meal on the third, seventh, fortieth, and hundredth days.

## Foodways

The Malay kitchen is built on rice (*nasi*), coconut milk (*santan*), and the pounded aromatic paste (*rempah*) of shallot, garlic, ginger, galangal, lemongrass, turmeric, chilli, and dried shrimp paste (*belacan*). Signature dishes include *nasi lemak* (rice cooked in coconut milk with *sambal*, anchovies, peanuts, cucumber, and egg), *rendang* (slow-braised beef or chicken in coconut and *rempah* until dry), *satay* (skewered grilled meat with peanut sauce), *laksa* (a family of noodle soups, from the sour tamarind *laksa Penang* to the coconut *laksa lemak*), *nasi kerabu* (Kelantanese blue rice with herbs and grilled fish), *ayam percik*, *ikan bakar*, *gulai* curries, and *ulam* (raw-herb salads). Ceremonial rice preparations include *nasi minyak* and *nasi briyani* at weddings and *ketupat* (compressed rice in woven palm-leaf pouches) at Hari Raya. Sweets (*kuih*) — *kuih lapis*, *seri muka*, *dodol*, *wajik*, *bahulu* — combine glutinous rice flour, palm sugar (*gula Melaka*), and pandan. Sweet, condensed-milk *teh tarik* ("pulled tea") and *kopi* dominate the *kedai kopi* social scene. All food is *halal*: no pork, no alcohol.

## Oral tradition & literature

Classical Malay literature is preserved in Jawi-script manuscript form. The **Sejarah Melayu** (*Malay Annals*, c. 1612) traces the genealogy and court customs of Malacca, while the **Hikayat Hang Tuah** narrates the exploits of the loyal warrior-hero Hang Tuah and his companion-turned-rival Hang Jebat. The Ramayana survives as **Hikayat Seri Rama** and in the wayang repertoire as *Hikayat Maharaja Wana*; the *Panji* cycle of Javanese romance circulated widely as **Hikayat Panji Semirang**. Islamic didactic literature includes **Hikayat Muhammad Hanafiah** and the mystical writings of Hamzah Fansuri and Nuruddin ar-Raniri from 16th–17th-century Aceh. The oral repertoire is dominated by the **pantun**, a four-line poem in *abab* rhyme whose first couplet (*pembayang*) foreshadows the meaning of the second (*maksud*); pantun are traded extemporaneously in courtship, *dondang sayang* performance, and everyday speech. Longer sung narratives include the *syair* (a monorhymed quatrain form) and the *gurindam* (didactic couplet). Proverbs (*peribahasa*) and riddles (*teka-teki*) remain a vigorous everyday genre.

## Language & religion

Bahasa Melayu is Austronesian (Malayo-Polynesian, Malayic branch), with major dialect blocs including Kelantan-Pattani, Terengganu, Kedah, Perak, Johor-Riau (the basis of the modern national standards of Malaysia, Indonesia, Brunei, and Singapore), and the Sumatran Malay dialects of Palembang and Deli. It was historically written in *Jawi*, an Arabic-derived script adapted with additional letters for Malay phonemes, and earlier in the Indic *Rencong* and *Kawi* scripts; Rumi (Latin) script has been the everyday standard since the mid-20th century. Malays are overwhelmingly Sunni Muslim of the Shafi'i school, a defining marker of Malay identity since the Malacca era and legally constitutive in Malaysia (a Malay is defined in the Federal Constitution as one who professes Islam, habitually speaks Malay, and follows Malay custom). Pre-Islamic Hindu-Buddhist and animist substrata survive in *adat* ceremony, in the healing practices of the *bomoh* and *pawang*, in *semangat* (soul-force) beliefs attached to rice, iron, and the *keris*, and in the ritual openings of *wayang* and *mak yong* performance.
