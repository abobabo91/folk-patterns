## Overview

The Nuaulu live in two dialect groups on the north and south coasts of Central Seram, having been moved to the coast by the Dutch in the late 19th or early 20th century. Most still follow their ancestral religion, which shapes their daily life and ritual practices. Their material culture—barkcloth, textiles, and ritual objects—reflects a subsistence lifestyle based on sago farming and ocean resources.

## Material culture

Nuaulu material culture is built almost entirely from plant and natural materials: barkcloth, plaited fibers, bamboo, rattan, and shell.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Nuaulu weave cotton and beat bark into cloth, with older barkcloth traditions giving way to ikat-dyed textiles.

- **Ikat-dyed cotton cloth** (*kanomane*) — A woven cotton textile with two faces of different color—orange on one side, yellowish on the other—with fringes at both ends.
- **Beaten bark cloth** — An older Nuaulu fabric beaten from bark and used for men's ceremonial garments, sometimes with shell attachments.
- **Plaited fiber mat** — A mat of vegetable fiber used in male initiation ceremonies.
- **Openwork rattan basket** (*sokate*) — A plaited basket for storage and everyday use.
- **Betel-chewing box** — A container made of pandanus leaves for holding betel-chewing requisites.

### Clothing & dress

Men wear barkcloth and ritual bands; women wear a distinctive layered rain hat of pandanus leaves.

- **Man's barkcloth garment** — Barkcloth held up by a waistband of vegetable fiber, worn for ritual occasions.
- **Ritual leg bands** — Legbands made of rattan combined with shell or seeds, worn by men for ritual occasions.
- **Ritual arm bands** — Armbands made of cassowary quill bound with rattan and bark, or of coconut shell with vegetable fiber, for ritual wear.
- **Chief's head-dress** — A ceremonial headdress built of cockatoo feathers on bamboo with nautilus shell inlay.
- **Woman's rain hat** — A conical hat of pandanus leaves over a wooden frame, built in layers with serrated edge, dyed yellow and red with a red cloth strip to tie under the chin.

### Architecture

The Nuaulu build the Baileo, a structure for spiritual purposes, and organize villages by religion.

- **Baileo** — A building constructed for spiritual purposes; its exact form and materials are not described in available sources.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Nuaulu use almost no ceramics or metal; bamboo, rattan, palm and gourd provide household and hunting equipment.

- **Cast iron spear-head** (*atiene pina*) — A painted black metal spear-head combined with wood and rattan.
- **Wooden whisk** — A whisk made specifically for stirring sago porridge.
- **Rattan sieve** — A sieve for preparing coconut from harvested nuts.
- **Bamboo lime phial** — A cylindrical container for lime, incised with decoration and closed with a separable stopper of sago pith.
- **Wooden shield** — An oblong curved wooden shield inlaid with porcelain.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Nuaulu personal ornament is made from shell, quill, and turtleshell, mostly worn for ritual occasions rather than daily life.

- **Turtleshell bracelet** — A bracelet cut from turtleshell.
- **Cassowary quill armband** — An armband made of cassowary quill bound with rattan and bark.
- **Shell-trimmed legband** — A legband combining rattan with shell or seeds.

## Music & performance

Three instruments are documented: a wooden Jew's harp, a drum with deer-skin head, and a bamboo whistle.

- **Jew's harp** — A musical instrument made of wood and vegetable fiber.
- **Drum** — A drum with a body of wood and bamboo and a head of deer skin.
- **Bamboo whistle** — A whistle of bamboo with coconut-leaf fiber binding.

## Dance & theatre

No information is available on Nuaulu dance or theatre traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

Nuaulu religion centers on ancestor veneration; the most documented ritual is male initiation, which involves special clothing and carved charms.

- **Creator deity** (*Upu Kuanahatana*) — The original creator recognized in Nuaulu religion.
- **Baileo** — A building constructed for spiritual purposes, central to Nuaulu ritual life.
- **Male initiation ceremony** — A ritual complex using barkcloth garments with shell, plaited mats, and ritual arm and leg bands.
- **Protective charm** (*wate*) — A carved charm in the figure of a dog-like creature made from sago leafstalk with red cloth, a detachable tail, and white stones for eyes and anus.

## Foodways

Sago is the staple crop; the Nuaulu also grow coconuts, cloves and nutmeg, and hunt and fish.

- **Sago porridge** — The staple food, prepared with a wooden whisk and eaten as a main dish.
- **Shifting cultivation** — Subsistence farming method used to grow sago and other crops.
- **Betel chewing** — A practiced tradition, with dedicated containers for chewing requisites and lime.
- **Dried meat** — Hunted game hung and preserved using hooks of wood, coconut shell and fiber.

## Oral tradition & literature

Nuaulu oral traditions and storytelling are not documented, but linguistic studies record personal naming practices and language shift.

## Language & religion

Nuaulu has only sixteen phonemes, no adjectives, and two mutually unintelligible dialects; language use tracks religious practice.

- **Nuaulu language** (*Nuaulu*) — A Central-Eastern Malayo-Polynesian language with the fewest phonemes in its group, using (C)V(N) syllable structure.
- **Northern dialect** — One of two mutually unintelligible dialects spoken in northern villages.
- **Southern dialect** — One of two mutually unintelligible dialects with about 1,000 speakers in southern coastal villages.
- **Ancestral religion** — Traditional religion practiced by most Nuaulu, in which ancestors control daily life and improper ritual observance brings sickness and death.
- **Ambonese Malay** — A lingua franca adopted by converts to Islam and used for schooling, competing with Nuaulu in daily use.

## Glossary

- *kanomane* — Cotton textile, woven and ikat-dyed, with fringes at both ends and two different colored faces
- *sokate* — Openwork rattan basket for storage
- *atiene pina* — Cast iron spear-head painted black
- *wate* — Carved protective charm in the figure of a dog-like creature
- *Baileo* — Building constructed for spiritual purposes
- *Upu Kuanahatana* — Original creator recognized in Nuaulu religion
- *barkcloth* — Beaten cloth made from bark, used for ceremonial garments
- *Amahai* — District of Central Seram where the Nuaulu live
- *Seram* — Island in Maluku, Indonesia, ancestral home of the Nuaulu
- *Central-Eastern Malayo-Polynesian* — Language group to which Nuaulu belongs
- *shifting cultivation* — Subsistence farming method using rotation of land
- *sago* — Staple starch crop and main food source
- *betel chewing* — Social and cultural practice involving areca nut and lime
- *cassowary quill* — Feather material used in ritual armbands
- *turtleshell* — Material for bracelets and ornaments
- *pandanus leaves* — Plant material for hats, boxes and other containers
- *rattan* — Plant fiber used in baskets, bands and bindings
- *coconut shell* — Material for armbands, containers and tools
