## Overview

The Karo, also known as Karo Batak, are one of the sub-ethnic groups of the Batak people. They live mainly in Tanah Karo, or the Karo lands, in North Sumatra, Indonesia. The Karo lands include Karo Regency and neighboring areas in Southeast Aceh Regency, Langkat Regency, Dairi Regency, Simalungun Regency, and Deli Serdang Regency. Significant Karo populations also live in Binjai and Medan, especially in the Padang Bulan area of Medan, and in Sibolangit. The Karo language is a Northern Batak language, and Karo people also speak Indonesian. Their folk culture includes clan organization, longhouse architecture, marriage ritual, dance, distinctive foods, and continuing negotiations between traditional beliefs, Christianity, Islam, and Hinduism.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The Karo traditionally lived in shared longhouses called *rumah Siwaluh Jabu*. Very few of these buildings remain; the source identifies one in Desa Lingga, Karo district. New construction is described as exclusively modern in design.

Karo settlement history also includes villages established by a *Sibayak*, each with satellite villages inhabited by extended families from the main village. These satellite villages were created for villagers whose fields were relatively far from the main settlements. The sources name five founding villages: Suka, Lingga, Barusjahe, Sarinembah, and Kutabuluh. Their village leaders were called *urungs*.

## Music & performance

The sources provide limited information about Karo music. During the development of the Karo church, brass-band music had previously been promoted, while after Indonesian independence the church adopted more elements of traditional Karo culture, including music.

Marriage is associated with a large ritual called *Mbaba belo selambar*. The ceremony includes the chewing of betel nut, known as *sirih*, and traditional Karonese dancing focused on hand movements.

The external material mentioned in the source is a traditional Karo wedding dance called *Landek*. No further description of its choreography, musical accompaniment, or social setting is provided.

## Dance & theatre

Traditional Karonese dancing forms part of the marriage ritual *Mbaba belo selambar*. The source says that this dancing focuses on hand movements. A traditional Karo wedding dance is identified as *Landek*.

## Festivals & rituals

Karo marriage is described as a major social event, typically attended by 200 people from both marrying families. Its ritual name is *Mbaba belo selambar*. The ceremony includes the chewing of *sirih*, traditional Karonese dancing, and the payment of a nominal dowry to each member of the bride’s family, the *kalimbubu*. Food is prepared by the groom’s family, the *anakberu*, which spends hours cooking for the guests. This obligation is expected to be reciprocated, allowing Karo people to attend several weddings each month. A non-Karo person wishing to marry a Karonese would be adopted into a Karo *marga*.

Karo marriage rules prohibit marriage within the same *marga*. The five *merga* are Ginting, Karo-Karo, Perangin-Angin, Sembiring, and Tarigan. Traditionally, the relationship between *kalimbubu* and *anakberu* was reinforced by cross-cousin marriage, although the source says this tradition is no longer important in modern Karo society.

The Karo food *Cimpa* and *Cimpa Bohan* are traditionally consumed during the *Kerja Tahun*, or Yearly Party, festival.

Following the Indonesian killings of 1965–1966, the Balai Pustaka Adat Merga Si Lima was established in Berastagi. It proclaimed *agama Pemena*, or the religion of the founders. The organization conducted a ceremony at the Lau Debuk–Debuk hot spring that resembled a ceremony for investing in a new Karo village. The sources describe subsequent religious movements involving Pemena, the Association of Karo Hinduism, and Parisada Hindu Dharma Karo.

## Foodways

Karo foodways include pork, fish, meat, chicken, glutinous rice, coconut milk, rice flour, palm sugar, purple sweet potato, corn, and ginger. *Babi Panggang Karo*, also called BPK, is grilled pork with pig-blood sauce. *Gulai ikan* is a fish curry, while *Rendang cekala* is meat rendang cooked with cekala.

*Lemang-lemang* consists of glutinous rice, coconut milk, and salt cooked in a hollowed bamboo tube coated with banana leaves so that the rice does not stick to the bamboo. *Rires*, more commonly known as “Lemang,” is also made from glutinous rice and coconut milk. *Lomok-lomok* is a traditional spicy or savoury dish made with pork or dog. *Arsik*, or *tangas-tangas*, is a traditional goldfish or carp dish.

*Pagit-pagit*, or *Terites*, is a traditional Karo soup made from partially digested grass from a cow, mixed with other ingredients, herbs, and spices. *Tasak telu* is a Karo chicken dish cooked three times, using different ingredients and cooking methods each time. *Cipera* is a soup made with corn, chicken, and other spices, left to stew in water.

*Cimpa* consists of Karo cakes made from rice flour, coconut, and palm sugar. *Cimpa Bohan* consists of cakes made from purple sweet potato, coconut, and palm sugar. Both are associated with the *Kerja Tahun* festival. *Bandrek* is a traditional ginger tea with other spices.

The sources describe changing dietary practice among Karo Muslims, including a reduction in the prevalence of pig farming and cooking.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They mention Karo traditions concerning the origins of the *Merga Silima*. According to this tradition, the five *merga* originated from five villages, each established by a *Sibayak*: Suka by Sibayak Suka, Lingga by Sibayak Lingga, Barusjahe by Sibayak Barusjahe, Sarinembah by Sibayak Sarinembah, and Kutabuluh by Sibayak Kutabuluh.

The sources also describe the Aru Kingdom, also spelled Haru, which the Karo people established in the 1200s–1500s in the area of modern-day Medan City and Deli Serdang Regency.

## Language & religion

The Karo speak Karo, a Northern Batak language that is not mutually intelligible with Southern Batak languages, as well as Indonesian. Karo society is organized into *merga*, or clans, which are divided into sub-clans and families. The five principal *merga* are Karo-Karo, Ginting, Sembiring, Perangin-Angin, and Tarigan.

The Karo traditionally maintained animist beliefs involving ghosts and spirits, called *perbegu*, as well as traditional jungle medicine. The Aru Kingdom’s population followed native animism, Hinduism, and Islam. Today, most Karo people are Christian, while an increasing number living away from the Karo Highlands have converted to Islam. Some Muslims and Christians continue to retain traditional animist beliefs.

The Gereja Batak Karo Protestan, or Karo Batak Protestant Church, is the largest church among the largely Christian Karo people. It was established formally in 1941 as a Reformed-Calvinist church. The sources also describe Catholic and Pentecostal communities, and the later development of the *agama Pemena* and Parisada Hindu Dharma Karo movements.
