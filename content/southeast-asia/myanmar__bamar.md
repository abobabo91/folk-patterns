## Overview

The Bamar are Myanmar's largest ethnic group, numbering about 35 million and centered in the dry zone along the upper Irrawaddy River. Their culture emerged over a thousand years, from the Pagan Empire to the present, shaped by Buddhism and continuous statecraft. They are known as transmitters of Pali-derived literature, court music, lacquerware, silk weaving, and a syncretic spirit cult that coexists with orthodox Buddhism.

## Material culture

Bamar material culture unifies around shared patterns in textiles, lacquer, metalwork, and architecture that draw from a single decorative vocabulary and a thousand-year court tradition.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Silk and cotton textiles carry a shared vocabulary of wave, meander, and mythological motifs that also appears in lacquerware and metalwork.

- **Acheik silk tapestry** (*acheik (luntaya acheik)*) — Dense, multi-colored silk tapestry-weave from the Amarapura–Mandalay region, made with dozens of small shuttles to create wave and arabesque patterns, historically reserved for royal dress.
- **Longyi wrapped skirt** (*longyi (paso for men, htamein for women)*) — The ankle-length tube skirt worn by both sexes, with men's versions in checks and plaids and women's in finer figured weaves including acheik for formal occasions.
- **Kalaga appliquéd tapestry** (*kalaga*) — Mandalay-made hangings layering velvet, sequins, beads, and metallic thread over cotton, depicting scenes from religious tales, zodiac animals, and court processions.
- **Burmese lacquerware** (*yun*) — Layers of tree sap built over bamboo coils and incised with fine linework filled with red, green, and yellow pigments, carrying the same pattern vocabulary as acheik and kalaga.
- **Monastic robes** (*thingan*) — Ochre and maroon robes cut in a patchwork pattern mimicking a paddy field, with their offering and production a ritualized practice in Buddhist life.

### Clothing & dress

Both sexes wear the longyi, with men adding turbans and jackets for formal occasions and women wearing fitted blouses, while everyone applies thanakha cosmetic paste.

- **Gaung baung turban** (*gaung baung*) — A stiff cloth turban with a projecting flap worn by men with formal dress, historically color-coded by rank.
- **Taikpon jacket** (*taikpon*) — A fitted, collarless jacket worn over a white eingy shirt for formal occasions, comparable in cut to the Manchu magua.
- **Thanakha cosmetic paste** (*thanakha*) — A pale yellow paste ground from tree bark and worn in circular patches or leaf shapes on the cheeks as both sunscreen and adornment by women and children.
- **Gadiba phanat sandal** (*gadiba phanat*) — A Mandalay velvet thonged slipper worn as formal footwear by both sexes.

### Architecture

Bamar architecture ranges from timber stilt-houses in villages to monumental stupas and tiered temple roofs crowned with the distinctive odd-tiered pyatthat.

- **Pyatthat tiered roof** (*pyatthat*) — A steep, multi-tiered roof with an odd number of tiers (three, five, seven, or nine) that crowns monasteries, royal halls, and shrine pavilions.
- **Zedi stupa** (*zedi*) — A bell-shaped shrine structure in the Sinhalese style, often whitewashed or gilded, that dominates town skylines throughout Bamar regions.
- **Pahto hollow temple** (*pahto*) — A terraced temple with a central shrine, a canonical form established at Bagan between the 11th and 13th centuries.
- **Village monastery** (*kyaung*) — The largest wooden building in rural settlements, traditionally a multi-tiered hall on massive teak posts with elaborate pierced-wood eaves and gable boards.
- **Stilt house** (*yotaing (main pillar)*) — A timber post-and-beam structure raised on stilts with bamboo walls and pitched thatch roof, with space beneath for weaving and storage, oriented around a southwest guardian-spirit pillar.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Ceramic jars and bronze weights and bells carry functional and symbolic importance, while lacquered vessels and tables anchor daily domestic life.

- **Martaban storage jar** (*pegu jar*) — Large glazed earthenware jars from Kyaukmyaung village, once shipped oil and fish paste across the Bay of Bengal in the Indian Ocean trade.
- **Burmese opium weights** (*(no term)*) — Small cast bronze zoomorphic weights in the form of hintha birds, chinthe lions, or composite auspicious animals, used across mainland Southeast Asia from the 15th to 19th centuries.
- **Silver repoussé bowl** (*ngwe kwet*) — Deep-worked silver bowls from Sagaing and Mandalay bearing repoussé scenes from the Jātaka tales.
- **Lacquer offering vessel** (*hsun-ok*) — A tiered lacquer vessel used to bring rice to monks, a central object in Buddhist merit-making.
- **Betel box** (*kun-it*) — A lacquered box for storing betel leaf, areca nut, and lime, an everyday object of cultural and social importance.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Gold, rubies, and pearls dominate formal jewelry, while amuletic yantras and protective tattoos connect adornment to spiritual practice.

- **Multi-strand necklace** (*hsan zi*) — A signature gold jewelry form of multiple strands worn at formal occasions.
- **Protective yantra amulets** (*let-hpwe (sak yant-type designs)*) — Gold or silver capsules containing inscribed Pali in diagrams, and protective tattoos worn by esoteric practitioners to ward off harm.
- **Ear-boring ceremony** (*nahtwin mingala*) — A rite of passage for girls, one of the Twelve Auspicious Rites, marked by the gift of a first pair of gold earrings.

## Music & performance

Classical court music, the Mahāgīta, survives in two formats: the loud outdoor hsaing waing drum ensemble and the intimate indoor chamber ensemble centered on the ancient saung gauk harp.

- **Saung gauk arched harp** (*saung gauk*) — An ancient arched harp with 13–16 silk strings, one of the oldest continuously played harp forms in the world, documented in Pyu reliefs and central to chamber music.
- **Pat waing drum circle** (*pat waing*) — A circle of 21 tuned drums played by a seated musician at its center, the heart of the hsaing waing ensemble accompanying theatre and ceremonies.
- **Hsaing waing ensemble** (*hsaing waing*) — A loud outdoor ensemble combining the pat waing drums, gong circles, a shawm, and bells, used at theatre, spirit ceremonies, and pagoda festivals.
- **Than-gyat verse-shouting** (*than-gyat*) — A folk tradition of improvised verse-shouting performed during Thingyan water festival celebrations.

## Dance & theatre

Bamar dance culminates in all-night zat pwe theatre combining dance, drama, and comedy, with the oldest form being yoke thé marionette theatre of 28 canonical characters.

- **Yoke thé marionette theatre** (*yoke thé*) — String-puppet theatre with 28 canonical characters (kings, princes, ogres, animals, nats, and ministers) enacting Jātaka and Ramayana tales, whose jerky articulation style influenced live human dance.
- **Zat pwe all-night theatre** (*zat pwe*) — An all-night theatrical form combining dance, drama, orchestral music, and comic interludes, performed on festival occasions.
- **Anyeint variety show** (*anyeint*) — A more intimate variety show pairing solo female dancers with clown-narrators called lu shwin daw.
- **Yama Zatdaw dance-drama** (*Yama Zatdaw*) — A performance of the Burmese Ramayana adapted via Thai and Mon transmission, using distinctive stylized costumes derived from the Ayutthaya court.

## Festivals & rituals

The Bamar lunar calendar is studded with monthly festivals culminating in Thingyan water festival and ending with the Kathina robe-offering ceremony, while life passages are marked by the Twelve Auspicious Rites.

- **Thingyan water festival** (*Thingyan*) — The four-to-five-day New Year celebration in mid-April featuring water-throwing, than-gyat verse-shouting, and community gatherings.
- **Kathina robe-offering ceremony** (*Kathina*) — An October–November ceremony when new monastic robes are woven overnight in competitions at pagodas, particularly the Shwedagon, as a major merit-making act.
- **Thadingyut Festival of Lights** (*Thadingyut*) — A September–October festival marking the end of Buddhist Lent, celebrated with candles and paper lanterns commemorating the Buddha's descent from heaven.
- **Shinbyu novitiation ceremony** (*shinbyu*) — A boy's ordination as a Buddhist monk, the single most important family ceremony in Bamar life, often combined with a girl's ear-boring rite.
- **Taungbyone Nat Festival** (*nat pwe*) — An August gathering near Mandalay, the largest annual assembly of nat mediums honoring the Thirty-Seven Min spirit pantheon with possession dancing and ceremonies.

## Foodways

Rice and curries form the foundation of Bamar meals, anchored by fermented shrimp paste and elevated by regional noodle dishes and tea-leaf salads.

- **Mohinga fish noodle soup** (*mohinga*) — The national dish of rice vermicelli in a lemongrass-catfish broth thickened with chickpea flour and banana-pith, eaten for breakfast throughout the country.
- **Fermented tea-leaf salad** (*lahpet thoke*) — A ceremonial salad of pickled tea leaves mixed with fried garlic, peanuts, sesame, dried shrimp, and lime, historically used to seal legal agreements.
- **Fermented shrimp paste** (*ngapi*) — A pungent paste served raw as a dip or cooked into chilli-fish relish, anchoring every meal and defining Bamar flavor.
- **Burmese curry** (*hin*) — Slow-cooked pork, chicken, mutton, or fish in onion, garlic, ginger, and turmeric, cooked until the oil separates (si pyan, 'oil returns').
- **Ohn no khao swè noodles** (*ohn no khao swè*) — Chicken coconut noodles, one of the emblematic Bamar dishes served throughout the region.

## Oral tradition & literature

Burmese literature spans a thousand years from royal chronicles to vernacular fiction, with the Jātaka tales forming the plot bank for theatre, dance, and visual art.

- **Myazedi inscription** — A quadrilingual stone inscription from 1113 CE marking the beginning of Burmese literary tradition and the oldest secure attestation of the Burmese script.
- **Yazawin royal chronicles** (*yazawin*) — Pre-colonial historical chronicles including the Maha Yazawin, Yazawin Thit, and Hmannan Yazawin (Glass Palace Chronicle), recording royal lineages and campaigns.
- **Zatdaw Jātaka tales** (*Zatdaw (Zatgyi hse-bwe for the Ten Great)*) — Five hundred Buddhist birth tales, especially the Ten Great Jātakas, supplying the plots for puppetry, dance-drama, and mural painting.
- **Yama Zatdaw Ramayana** (*Yama Zatdaw*) — The Burmese adaptation of the Ramayana, transmitted through Thai and Mon traditions during the 18th century and performed as dance-drama.
- **Khit San literary movement** (*Khit San*) — A 20th-century vernacular literary revival at Rangoon University in the 1930s that reshaped Burmese prose into its modern form.

## Language & religion

Burmese, a Tibeto-Burman language with a literary and spoken register, is written in an Indic script, while religion merges Theravada Buddhism with nat spirit veneration and esoteric practice.

- **Burmese script** (*Burmese script (Myanmar script)*) — An Indic abugida derived via Mon from the South Indian Pallava script, first securely attested in the Myazedi inscription and later adopted by Shan, Ahom, Karen, and other languages.
- **Thirty-Seven Min spirits** (*Thirty-Seven Min (nats)*) — A pantheon of household and nature spirits venerated alongside Buddhism, with major festivals honoring them through medium possession and offerings.
- **Mahagiri household guardian** (*Mahagiri*) — The guardian spirit of the household, honored at the southwest pillar (yotaing) of every home with a coconut offering.
- **Weizza esoteric practice** (*weizza*) — An esoteric alchemical-tantric practice combining Buddhist, Hindu, and folk elements, practitioners often bearing protective yantras and tattoos.
