## Overview

The Banjar, also called the Banjarese, are an Austronesian ethnic group native to the Banjar regions of southeastern Kalimantan, Indonesia. Important areas include Banjarmasin, Banjarbaru, Banjar Regency, the Negara River valley, the Martapura and Barito rivers, and the Meratus mountain ranges. Communities also live in Central and East Kalimantan, Sumatra, Malaysia, Brunei, and Singapore. Banjarese belongs to the Malayic branch of the Austronesian language family and serves as a lingua franca for several indigenous communities. Banjar culture combines Dayak, Malay, and Javanese elements, with additional influences from Arab, Persian, Indian, and Chinese traders. Its folk-culture expressions include textiles, oral traditions, theatre, dance, music, foodways, and ritual customs.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Sasirangan** is a Banjar textile tradition whose name derives from *sirang* or *menyirang*, meaning to baste. The technique uses basting stitches and raffia string tied before the textile is dyed. Sasirangan is worn as a sarong or as a complementary scarf with the *Kubaya Panjang* and formal men's clothing.

**Bagajah Gamuling Baular Lulut** is a named form of Banjar wedding attire. The source identifies it as having heavy Hindu influence, but the supplied text does not provide further details about its materials or construction.

**Babaju Kun Galuh Pacinan**, **Baamar Galung Pancar Surya/Matahari**, and **Babaju Kubaya Panjang** are named prominent forms of Banjar traditional attire.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Gigi Haruan*, snakehead fish; *Hiris Gagatas*, diamond-shaped *kue*; *Kambang Sakaki*, a named Sasirangan motif.

### Clothing & dress

Banjar clothing reflects Dayak, Malay, Javanese, and Chinese cultural acculturation together with Islamic and Hindu influence. It is commonly worn at weddings and traditional ceremonies. During the Banjar sultanate era, the common male garment was the *taluk balanga* dress, while the neckless *baju kurung* was everyday clothing for women. The *Kubaya Panjang* is a long shirt worn with Sasirangan as a sarong or complementary scarf. Named ceremonial forms include *Bagajah Gamuling Baular Lulut*, *Babaju Kun Galuh Pacinan*, *Baamar Galung Pancar Surya/Matahari*, and *Babaju Kubaya Panjang*. Black velvet is used for a luxurious effect and to contrast with gold jewellery. Golden yellow symbolizes nobility, while green symbolizes coolness. Embroidered roses and jasmine symbolize the fragrance of virtue. Common accessories include the *laung*, a male headband or hat, the *sanggul*, a female bun, and gold jewellery.

### Architecture

The supplied sources mention river banks, islands, estuaries, hamlets, valleys, and water villages in connection with Banjar settlement.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Banjar attire uses gold jewellery, particularly in combination with black velvet and embroidered clothing. The sources also name the *laung* and *sanggul* as customary accessories: the former is a male headband or hat, and the latter is a female bun.

## Music & performance

One traditional Banjar music form is *musik panting*, which originated in the Tapin region of South Kalimantan. Its principal chordophone, the *panting*, derives from the Dayak *kecapi* zither and was later influenced by the Malay *gambus*, which resembles the Arabic *qanbūs* but is smaller. Formerly performed solo, *musik panting* is now commonly played with the *babun* drum, *agung* gong, and *piul* violin. The person who first gave the music its name was A.W. Syarbaini.

Another form is *musik kintung*, originating from Sungai Alat village in Astambul and Bincau village in Martapura in Banjar Regency. It was adopted from the Bukit ethnic group, also called Meratus Dayak, and is made from bamboo pieces. A set usually contains five pieces, while the instrument consists of seven bamboo segments with names including *hintalu randah*, *hintalu tinggi*, *tinti pajak*, *tinti gorak*, *pindua randah*, *pindua tinggi*, and *gorak tuha*. It was formerly used in rituals asking for rain and in competitions involving magical elements; today it is becoming increasingly rare and can accompany traditional Banjar songs.

Banjar shadow theatre uses gamelan music from Java.

## Dance & theatre

*Mamanda* is a traditional theatre art developed in South Kalimantan with Malay origin. It is usually performed in an arena, where actors interact with the audience and invite humorous comments. Its storyline generally centers on a royal court. Fixed characters include the *Raja*, *Perdana Menteri*, *Mangkubumi*, *Wazir*, *Panglima Perang*, *Harapan Pertama*, *Harapan Kedua*, *Khadam*, *Permaisuri*, and *Sandut*. Additional characters can include the *Raja dari Negeri Seberang*, *Perompak*, *Jin*, and *Kompeni*. The term is explained through *mama* or *mamarina*, meaning uncle in the Banjar language, and *nda*, meaning honourable.

*Wayang kulit Banjar* is Banjarese leather puppet shadow theatre. It is a syncretic Hindu-Buddhist-Animist musical theatre accompanied by Javanese gamelan, combining Javanese structural influence with local Banjar stories, characters, and language. It is performed for entertainment and moral guidance, including at weddings and traditional ceremonies and to fulfil vows. Its puppets are generally smaller than Javanese puppets and are made from cow's or goat's hide. Preservation initiatives are conducted through the South Kalimantan Cultural Park.

The Banjarese perform *Japin Sigam*, a Zapin dance from Kotabaru Regency. It uses eight dancers, four men and four women, and combines *Japin Anak Delapan* with the Sigam variety. *Baksa kembang* is a palace-derived dance associated with hospitality and refined manners. *Radap Rahayu* is a ritual dance especially associated with Gambut District in Banjar Regency and is used at weddings, bathing rituals, and the erection of new buildings.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do describe several life-cycle and ceremonial practices. The *baayun anak* tradition was a child-blessing ritual among Kaharingan followers involving the recitation of *balian* mantras. In present-day Banjar society, it uses Quranic verses and *salawat* for the Islamic Prophet Muhammad instead. *Bapukung* is a practice of putting infants to sleep in an upright sling made from tied fabric.

Banjar dances are also connected with ritual occasions. *Radap Rahayu* is performed at weddings, bathing rituals, and the erection of new buildings. *Japin Sigam* is a social dance among young people in coastal areas and is also performed as an offering to the royal family; in the present day it is more commonly used to honour guests on special occasions. *Baksa kembang* can be performed at public events such as weddings.

Banjar cultural customs are rooted in Kaharingan religious rituals that were modified after mass conversion to Sunni Islam following the establishment of the Sultanate of Banjar. The custom of accepting offered food or drink is described as a sign of respect for the giver and is associated with *kapuhunan* or *kepuhunan*, meaning luck or fate.

## Foodways

Banjar cuisine is the cooking tradition of the Banjar people of South Kalimantan and is also found in Brunei, Malaysia, and Singapore. It has been influenced by Malay, Javanese, Chinese, and Indian cuisine. Soupy foods include *soto banjar*, made with chicken, onion, white onion, cumin, fennel, cinnamon, and lime juice, and *sop ayam banjar*, served with steamed rice rather than the ketupat used with *soto banjar*. Its broth includes clove, star anise, nutmeg, and ginger and may be served with rice vermicelli, fried shallots, spring onion, and lime juice.

*Ketupat Kandangan Banjar* is associated with South Kalimantan and the Muslim holy day of Eid al-Fitr. It originated from Kandangan in South Hulu Sungai Regency and is commonly found during Ramadan. It uses tuna or baked cork fish cooked in yellow coconut milk sauce with cinnamon, cloves, and cardamom. *Bingka* is a very sweet, fat, and soft cake made from flour, eggs, coconut milk, sugar, and salt. It is used in the Banjar tradition of serving 41 types of cakes on special occasions such as weddings and is especially common during Ramadan.

Other listed dishes include *Apam Barabai*, *Amparan tatak*, *Babungku*, *Gangan humbut*, *Ikan haruan bakar*, *Lempeng*, *Lontong banjar*, *Lontong orari*, *Mi bancir*, *Nasi kuning*, *Pekasam*, *Pais*, *Petah*, *Petah Arab*, *Pupudak baras*, *Pundut Nasi*, *Sari pangantin*, and additional forms of *bingka*.

## Oral tradition & literature

Banjar oral traditions are influenced by Malay, Arabic, and Chinese cultures. They developed into art forms around the 18th century, especially *madihin* and *lamut*. *Madihin* is an oral form of expression in the Banjar language whose content can be compared with written literature and which serves as entertainment. Its name derives from the Arabic word *madah*, meaning praise. The source connects its development with the return of Sheikh Muhammad Arsyad al-Banjari, a Sunni Shafi'i cleric and royal member of the Banjar Sultanate, after studying in Mecca.

*Lamut* is a storytelling tradition conveying messages and values related to Banjar religion, society, and culture. It originated in China and was initially performed in the Chinese language before being adapted into Banjar when Chinese traders introduced it to the Banjarese. The *Hikayat Banjar*, or Tale of the Banjar, records the story of the early Banjarese kingdom and begins from the 16th century. The *Hikayat Lambung Mangkurat*, also identified with the *Hikayat Banjar*, records *Radap Rahayu*.

## Language & religion

Banjarese, also called *Basa Banjar* or *Jaku Banjar*, is an Austronesian language of the Malayic branch. It is predominantly spoken by the Banjarese and functions as a de facto lingua franca among indigenous communities, especially in South Kalimantan, as well as in parts of Central and East Kalimantan. The language has two principal dialect divisions: Banjar Hulu and Banjar Kuala. Banjar Hulu is predominantly spoken in South Hulu Sungai Regency and North Hulu Sungai Regency. The language uses the Jawi script, and the sources also provide a Banjar version of Article 1 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights.

The sources describe historical Animist-Buddhist-Hindu kingdoms, the Sultanate of Banjar, Kaharingan ritual traditions, and later mass conversion to Sunni Islam. Banjar customs were modified to align with Islam, including the use of Quranic verses and *salawat* in *baayun anak*. Banjar culture also preserves practices associated with earlier Kaharingan beliefs, including *kapuhunan* or *kepuhunan* and the former use of *balian* mantras.
