## Overview

The Enggano people are an isolated but contacted tribe inhabiting Enggano Island, a small island adjacent to the southwest coast of Sumatra in Indonesia. Population estimates are uncertain: a source for 2000 gives 1,500 people, while a 1999 source gives approximately 1,000; both indicate that the population is likely to continue decreasing. The people are divided into five tribes: Kauno, Kaitora, Kaarubi, Kaharuba, and Kaahoao, each led by a tribal chief. Their society retains distinct matrilineal features, including maternal clans, inherited customary titles, and property passing through the maternal line. Their language is generally classified within the Austronesian family, although its precise classification has been controversial.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Weaving** — The sources identify weaving among Enggano handicrafts.

**Mat weaving** — Enggano people weave mats.

**Bead ornaments** — Ornaments from beads are listed among Enggano handicrafts.

No named motifs are provided by the sources.

### Clothing & dress

Enggano men wear *kain* loincloths of various lengths. The length of the *kain* is described as directly proportional to the wearer’s economic status. Enggano women always wear long *kain*. Men’s outer garment is a long-sleeved shirt with a blind collar, while women wear a shirt without a collar.

### Architecture

Enggano settlements are structurally cumulus. Their houses have stilt frames and are stacked and rectangular in shape, although houses were rounded in the past. Walls and roofs are strengthened by rigid leaves. The Enggano language source also records that beehive houses were typical of Enggano Island and the Nicobar Islands when the Enggano people were first contacted by Europeans.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources mention pottery and woodcarving among Enggano handicrafts. They also describe masks, necklaces, woven mats, and bead ornaments.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Enggano handicrafts include necklaces and ornaments made from beads.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources mention Enggano myths and rituals in the title of a documentation project. The sources do state that ancestral worship, animism, and totemism continue to be preserved in some places.

## Foodways

Enggano food is mainly vegetable, while rice is usually bought. The sources list maize, yam, peanuts, taro, and coconut palm among the products of manual farming. They also mention hunting for turtles, breeding chickens, and fishing. No named dishes, cooking methods, ceremonial foods, beverages, dietary rules, or food-related festivals are described. Because currency circulation on the island is limited, items are usually obtained through bartering rather than buying and selling.

## Language & religion

Enggano, or Engganese, is spoken on Enggano Island off the southwestern coast of Sumatra, Indonesia. It is generally classified within the Austronesian language family, specifically in or near the Northwest Sumatra–Barrier Islands subgroup, although its classification has been controversial. Proposals have ranged from treating it as a language isolate to placing it in the Malayo-Polynesian branch. Edwards’s analysis derives pronouns, numerals, and many affixes from Proto-Malayo-Polynesian, while the language retains a low lexical retention rate: 21%, or 46 out of 217 words, compared with 59%, or 132.5 out of 223 words, for Malay. The language has unusual sound changes, nasal harmony, seven oral and seven nasal vowels, and a historically recorded vigesimal counting system. Most people now use Malay numerals, especially for higher numbers.

The majority of native Enggano people profess Christianity, mainly Protestantism, and Islam, mainly Sunni. In some places animism, totemism, and ancestral worship are preserved.
