## Overview

The Lao are a Tai-speaking people native to the middle and lower Mekong River valley, forming the majority population of the Lao People's Democratic Republic and the dominant population of Thailand's Isan region on the Khorat Plateau. Roughly 4 million ethnic Lao live in Laos itself, concentrated in the Mekong lowlands from Luang Prabang down through Vientiane to Champasak, while approximately 20 million Lao Isan live across northeastern Thailand. Together with Phuan, Phu Thai, and other closely related lowland Tai groups, they are classified in Laos as *Lao Loum* ("lowland Lao"). Their language belongs to the Kra–Dai (Tai–Kadai) family. In folk-culture terms, the Lao matter for a distinctive silk-and-cotton weaving complex, a Theravada Buddhist ritual calendar overlaid on animist *phi* worship, and the *khene* — the free-reed mouth organ that anchors nearly every genre of Lao music.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Lao weaving is one of the most technically layered textile traditions in mainland Southeast Asia, combining supplementary-weft brocade, warp ikat, and weft ikat, often within a single cloth.

**Sinh** (*sinh*) — the tube skirt worn by nearly all Lao women, constructed in three horizontally joined sections: the *hua sinh* (waistband), the *phuen sinh* (main body), and the *tin sinh* (hem border). The *tin sinh* carries the densest patterning and is the primary marker of a weaver's region, skill, and status.

**Lao silk** — a fine, lustrous filament silk historically raised in village households, with Xam Neua (Houaphanh Province) and Luang Prabang recognized as the highest-status production centers. Natural dyes from lac insects, indigo, ebony fruit, and stick lac produce the deep reds, blues, and blacks characteristic of ceremonial cloth.

**Mudmee Lao** (*mat mi*) — the Lao weft-ikat technique in which weft threads are tied off and dyed in patterns before weaving, creating slightly blurred, feathered figures. Lao *mat mi* is distinguished from Thai central *mudmee* by darker palettes, tighter zoomorphic patterning, and the frequent combination of ikat weft with supplementary-weft brocade in a single panel.

**Tai Lue weaving** — the tradition of the closely related Tai Lue of northern Laos (Luang Namtha, Bokeo) and Sipsongpanna, distinguished by red-ground supplementary-weft cloths with stepped diamond fields and rows of small stylized figures, often woven on body-tension looms.

**Pha biang** (*pha biang*) — a long ceremonial shawl worn diagonally across the chest by women (and by men at temple), typically silk with supplementary-weft or brocaded ends.

**Pha sabai** — an older court-adjacent form of the shoulder cloth, often in gold or silver-wrapped thread, worn for weddings and Buddhist merit-making.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Naga* (serpent-water spirit, often as *nak* or paired *nak khut*), *hong* (mythical swan/goose), diamond-lattice *dokkeo*, *sri* (stylized tree of life), elephant, *nyak* (guardian ogre), *dok chan* (sandalwood flower), zigzag river motifs, hook-and-key meanders, and small human ancestor figures woven into supplementary-weft bands.

### Clothing & dress

The everyday women's outfit pairs a plain or striped *sinh* with a fitted blouse; ceremonial dress replaces this with a heavily brocaded *sinh* whose *tin sinh* may take a weaver months, a silk *pha biang* draped from the left shoulder across the chest, and a bun secured with a gold or silver hairpin. Men historically wore the *salong* or *pha hang*, a length of cloth wrapped between the legs to form loose trousers, together with a collarless shirt; today Western dress is standard for men in daily life, but the *salong* returns for ordination and New Year rites. Novice and monk robes are dyed in the ochre-saffron range using jackfruit heartwood. At weddings, women wear a *sinh* in gold thread with matching *pha biang*, and the groom often wears silk trousers and a sash. Silver belts with repoussé plaques and thick silver bracelets accompany full ceremonial dress. Feet are typically bare or in simple sandals; embroidered slippers appear at royal-heritage events in Luang Prabang.

### Architecture

The vernacular Lao house is a rectangular pile-built dwelling raised two to three meters on hardwood posts, walled with woven bamboo or sawn planks and roofed originally in thatch or wooden shingles, now more often in corrugated metal or clay tile. The elevated floor keeps the living space above monsoon flooding and grazing water buffalo, and the under-house void serves as a loom shed, storage area, and shaded workspace where women weave. The open front veranda (*chan*) is the social heart of the house. Interior space is loosely divided into a sleeping area and a hearth room, with the household spirit shelf oriented to an auspicious direction.

Religious architecture centers on the *wat*, a walled monastic compound whose principal ordination hall (*sim*) has a rectangular plan, tiered roofs sweeping close to the ground, and gable ends terminating in curled *cho fa* finials representing stylized nagas or hong birds. The Luang Prabang style is distinguished by very low, multi-tiered roofs that almost brush the eaves of the surrounding walls; the Vientiane style produces taller, steeper sim with more vertical facades. Stupas (*that*) — most famously **That Luang** in Vientiane — are gilded, lotus-bud towers on stepped square bases. Carved wooden pediments and lacquered-and-gilded doors are the primary vehicles for religious ornament.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Lao material culture is heavier on textile and metal than on high-fired ceramic. Village pottery is low-fired earthenware for water jars, cooking pots, and the ubiquitous conical **sticky-rice steamer** (*huat*), paired with the woven bamboo **rice basket** (*tip khao*) that keeps steamed glutinous rice warm at the table. Silver-working, historically concentrated in Luang Prabang and Vientiane, produces betel boxes (*khan mak*), footed offering bowls, belts, and the tall pedestalled *khan* trays used to present offerings at Buddhist rites. Bronze casting supplies temple bells, monastery gongs, and Buddha images. The lacquered and gilded wooden *ho tai* (scripture chest) houses palm-leaf sutras in monastery libraries. In village households, hand-forged iron knives and short-hafted machetes, bamboo fish traps of many named types, and split-rattan basketry round out the everyday inventory.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Lao ceremonial jewelry is dominated by silver, with gold reserved for the wealthy and for royal-heritage contexts. Standard forms include heavy hollow-linked silver belts, filigree bracelets, hooped and disc-drop earrings, and the *pin* hairpin worn through a coiled bun. Amuletic pendants — small Buddha images (*phra*) or Pali-inscribed cylindrical *takrut* — are strung on braided cords and worn at the neck. The *baci* string, cotton thread knotted around the wrist during the *sou khwan* soul-calling rite, functions as adornment and blessing at once; recipients typically leave them on until they fall off. Sak yant sacred tattoos, applied by monks or lay masters with a long metal or bamboo needle, occur among Lao men but are more prominent in Thai-Lao Isan practice than in Laos proper.

## Music & performance

The defining Lao instrument is the *khene* (*khaen*), a free-reed bamboo mouth organ of paired vertical pipes fitted with small metal reeds, inscribed by UNESCO in 2017 as **khene music of the Lao people**. Its buzzing polyphonic drone accompanies nearly every folk-song genre. The core vocal tradition is *lam* — extemporized sung poetry in which a singer (*mor lam*) improvises verses over khene accompaniment, in regional styles such as *lam saravane* (southern Laos), *lam siphandone*, *lam khon savan*, and the Isan variant **mor lam**, now a mass-market popular idiom in northeastern Thailand. Instrumental ensembles include the *seep noi* and *seep gnai* small and large ensembles, drawing on the *khong wong* gong circle, *ranat* xylophone, *so u* and *so i* bowed spike fiddles, *pin* plucked lute, and the *khong* barrel drum. Court music (*piphat*) at Luang Prabang followed the broader mainland Southeast Asian gong-chime tradition, closely related to Thai *piphat*, and accompanied ritual, ordination, and royal dance. Buddhist chant of Pali suttas and merit-making sermons forms a parallel devotional soundscape heard from village *wat* at dawn and on lunar observance days.

## Dance & theatre

Classical Lao dance descends from the royal court of Luang Prabang and is closely related to Thai and Khmer court traditions. The most prominent form is *lakhon phra lak phra lam*, the Lao dance-drama version of the Ramayana in which stylized gestures, gilded headdresses, and painted masks depict Phra Lam (Rama), Phra Lak (Lakshmana), the demon king Hapkhanasouane (Ravana), and Hanuman. Masked players perform the demon and monkey roles while unmasked dancers take the human and celestial roles. Folk dance is dominated by the *lam vong*, a slow circular couple dance performed at weddings, New Year, and any village celebration, in which pairs rotate counterclockwise while making stylized hand movements in front of the chest. Shadow puppetry (*nang talung*), while more strongly associated with southern Thailand, is performed in southern Lao villages. Ritual masked performance survives most vividly in the **Bun Bang Fai** (rocket festival) and in the *phi ta khon*–adjacent masked processions of the Lao borderlands.

## Festivals & rituals

The Lao ritual calendar follows the lunar year and is called *Hit Sip Song* — the "twelve customs," one for each lunar month. Key annual festivals include **Bun Pi Mai** (Lao New Year, mid-April), a three-day water-pouring festival in which Buddha images are bathed with scented water and sand stupas are built in temple courtyards; **Bun Bang Fai** (Rocket Festival, sixth lunar month), in which villages launch enormous homemade bamboo rockets to petition the sky god Phaya Thaen for monsoon rain; **Bun Khao Phansa** (start of Buddhist Lent, eighth lunar month) and **Bun Ok Phansa** (end of Lent, eleventh lunar month), the latter marked by illuminated boat processions on the Mekong (*Lai Heua Fai*); **Bun That Luang** in Vientiane (twelfth lunar month), the great stupa festival at That Luang; and **Boun Souang Heua**, the Mekong boat races. Life-cycle rites center on the *baci* / *sou khwan* ceremony, in which elders knot cotton strings around the wrists of the honoree to call back the thirty-two *khwan* (soul components) at births, ordinations, weddings, homecomings, and departures. Male ordination as a novice monk (*pha noen*) is expected of most Lao men at least briefly. Funerals combine cremation with Buddhist merit-transfer and household spirit propitiation.

## Foodways

The Lao staple is **sticky rice** (*khao niaow*), steamed in a conical bamboo basket over a clay pot and eaten by hand from the shared *tip khao*, distinguishing Lao and Isan meal practice from the plain-rice traditions of central Thailand and Vietnam. Signature dishes include *laap* (*larb*) — minced meat or fish tossed with toasted rice powder, lime, fish sauce, chilies, mint, and shallots, considered the national dish; *tam mak hoong*, the Lao green papaya salad, pounded in a clay mortar and typically dressed with fermented fish sauce (*padaek*) and crab; *or lam*, a Luang Prabang stew of buffalo or beef with sakhan (a peppery wood), eggplant, and long beans; *mok pa*, fish steamed in banana leaf with lemongrass, dill, and chilies; and *khao poon*, rice vermicelli in a spiced coconut-fish broth served at weddings and merit-making. *Padaek*, a coarse fermented fish sauce with visible rice bran and fish pieces, is the foundational seasoning, distinct from the thinner Thai and Vietnamese *nam pla*/*nuoc mam*. *Beerlao* accompanies most meals; *lao-lao*, a distilled rice spirit, appears at rituals. Buddhist alms-giving at dawn (*tak bat*) sees laypeople offer sticky rice and dishes to monks lining village streets.

## Oral tradition & literature

The core Lao literary text is the ***Phra Lak Phra Lam***, the Lao Ramayana, transmitted both in palm-leaf manuscript and in oral recitation and dance-drama, in which the Rama story is localized to the Mekong landscape. The *Sang Sinxay* epic, composed in the seventeenth century by the court poet Pang Kham, follows the hero Sinxay's rescue of his aunt from a winged demon and remains widely quoted. Buddhist *Jataka* tales — especially the **Vessantara Jataka** (*Phra Vet*), recited in full at the *Bun Phra Vet* festival — form the backbone of religious storytelling. Village oral tradition includes *nithan* (folktales), etiological legends about the *nak* (naga) of the Mekong, and the recited genealogies of local *phi* (spirits). Sung poetry (*lam*) blurs the boundary between literature and music, with skilled *mor lam* singers extemporizing rhymed verses in real-time exchanges. Palm-leaf manuscripts (*bailan*) in the old *Tham* script preserve religious and literary texts in monastery libraries.

## Language & religion

The Lao language is a tonal, analytic Kra–Dai language written in the Lao script, an Indic-derived abugida related to Thai. Major dialect regions run north-to-south along the Mekong: Luang Prabang Lao, Vientiane Lao, Savannakhet, and Champasak, with Isan Lao across the Thai border. Ecclesiastical texts use the older *Tham* (Dharma) script. The dominant religion is Theravada Buddhism of the same lineage as Thai and Cambodian practice, layered onto an older animist substrate of *phi* (spirits) — territorial, ancestral, household, and pathological. Every village maintains a shrine to the *phi ban* (guardian spirit), and the *sou khwan* soul-calling rite predates Buddhism but is now performed by lay elders in a syncretic Buddhist idiom. Monastic ordination, alms-giving, and merit-making structure the male life course and the yearly calendar.
