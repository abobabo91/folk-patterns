## Overview

The Nuaulu — also called Naulu or Nunuhai — are an ethnic group of Seram, in Maluku, Indonesia, and belong to the Alifuru peoples native to the island's interior. Their name means 'head[waters] of the Nua River', the ancestral homeland from which they were moved down to the coast by the Dutch, for purposes of pacification, in the late 19th or early 20th century. They number about 2,500 and live in the Amahai district of Central Seram, split into a northern group in two villages on the north coast and a southern group in five villages on the south coast and inland. Their language belongs to the Central-Eastern Malayo-Polynesian group; the two dialects are not mutually intelligible. Most Nuaulu still follow the ancestral religion, and the objects museums hold from them — barkcloth, initiation mats, ritual arm and leg bands — come largely out of that religious life.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Kanomane** — a cotton textile, woven and ikat-dyed, with fringes at both ends and two faces of different colour, orange on one side and a yellowish tone on the other. The Ambonese Malay name for the same cloth is *Kain Timor*; *kanomane* is the Nuaulu word for it.

**Barkcloth** — beaten cloth is the older Nuaulu fabric. The beaters collected from them are stone. One barkcloth in the British Museum is a man's garment used in an initiation ceremony and carries shell attachments; a separate waistband of vegetable fibre exists specifically to support barkcloth on the body.

**Plaited fibre work** — plaiting rather than weaving carries much of the technical skill recorded. A plaited mat of vegetable fibre was used in the male initiation ceremony; *sokate* is the local name of an openwork rattan basket; boxes for betel-chewing requisites are made of pandanus leaves.

### Clothing & dress

Dress in the collected record is mostly men's and mostly ceremonial. Men wore barkcloth held up by a waistband of vegetable fibre, and for ritual occasions added bands at the limbs: legbands of rattan with shell, legbands of rattan with seeds, armbands of cassowary quill bound with rattan and bark, and armbands of coconut shell and vegetable fibre. A chief's head-dress is built of cockatoo feathers on bamboo with nautilus shell. Men also carried a comb of bamboo and vegetable fibre. The one woman's garment recorded is a rain hat of pandanus leaves over a wooden frame: conical, built of several layers with the inner surface plaited, cut with a serrated edge and reinforced inside and out by a woody band, stitched in concentric rings, and originally dyed in two colours, yellow and red, with a twisted red cloth strip to hold it under the chin. The Nuaulu are often mistaken for the Manusela, who likewise wear a red cloth on the head.

### Architecture

The sources name one Nuaulu building type: the *Baileo*, which the Nuaulu build for spiritual purposes. No description of its form, materials or ornament appears in the sources used here. One further detail of settlement layout is recorded, and it is social rather than architectural: villages are segregated by religion, with practitioners of the ancestral religion living in a secluded section apart from the main village — an arrangement that also shapes which language is spoken where.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

No Nuaulu ceramic tradition appears in the sources; porcelain enters instead as inlay, set into an oblong curved wooden shield. Metal appears in weaponry: a cast iron spear-head, painted black, is named *atiene pina*, and spears combine metal with wood and rattan. Most household equipment is vegetal. Bamboo, rattan, palm and gourd do the work elsewhere done by pottery and metal: a rattan sieve for preparing coconut; a wooden whisk for making sago porridge; a hook of wood, coconut shell, vegetable fibre and rattan for hanging dried meat; a gourd lime container; and cylindrical lime phials of bamboo, one incised with decoration and closed with a separable stopper of sago pith. Hunting and fishing gear is of the same materials — a bow of wood, rattan and vegetable-fibre string, bamboo arrows for small game, and a fish-spear of bamboo and palm-frond fibre.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Personal ornament is shell, quill and turtleshell rather than metal. A bracelet is cut from turtleshell; armbands are made of coconut shell with vegetable fibre, or of cassowary quill with rattan and bark; legbands combine rattan with shell or with seeds. Several of these are explicitly marked in the catalogue as men's ornaments for ritual occasions, not daily wear, and shell also appears sewn onto the initiation barkcloth. The sources record no tattooing, hair practice or cosmetic tradition.

## Music & performance

Three instruments are documented, all of them collected objects rather than described performances: a Jew's harp of wood and vegetable fibre, a drum with a body of wood and bamboo and a head of deer skin, and a whistle of bamboo with coconut-leaf fibre. The sources say nothing about song genres, repertoire, ensemble practice or the occasions on which any of these are played.

## Festivals & rituals

Nuaulu religion holds that the ancestors control everyday life, and that if the traditions they handed down are not followed correctly the living are punished with sickness, death and lack of prosperity. It names an original creator, *Upu Kuanahatana*, and recognises powerful magic that people can use for good or for bad purposes. The *Baileo* is built for spiritual purposes. The clearest ritual complex in the material record is male initiation: a barkcloth garment with shell attachments and a plaited mat of vegetable fibre are both catalogued as used in the initiation ceremony, and the men's armbands and legbands are recorded as for ritual occasions. A carved charm called *wate*, in the figure of a dog-like creature, is made from a sago leafstalk with red cloth attachments, a detachable tail, a small white stone for one eye and a second white stone set in the anus. The Nuaulu retained a custom of headhunting until the 1940s; the last incident was in 2005, when seven Nuaulu were involved in the killing of two residents of Rutah village near the Rutah River, and the perpetrators were sentenced to death by the court.

## Foodways

Sago is the staple. The Nuaulu are subsistence farmers using shifting cultivation, and also grow coconuts, cloves and nutmeg as cash crops. The kitchen equipment collected from them tracks that diet closely: a wooden whisk made specifically to stir sago porridge, a rattan sieve for preparing coconut, and a hook of wood, coconut shell, fibre and rattan for hanging dried meat. Betel chewing is attested by its own set of containers — a box of pandanus leaves for the chewing requisites, a lime container made from a gourd, and two bamboo lime phials, one incised and stoppered with sago pith, the other with a lid. Hunting contributes game: bamboo arrows are specified as for small game, and the drum is headed with deer skin. Fishing is done with a bamboo fish-spear. The sources describe no named dishes, ceremonial meals or drinking traditions beyond these.

## Oral tradition & literature

What the sources do record is a linguistic practice adjacent to it: Florey and Bolton's study of personal names, lexical replacement and language shift in eastern Indonesia draws on Nuaulu material.

## Language & religion

Nuaulu belongs to the Central-Eastern Malayo-Polynesian group, within which it has the fewest phonemes — sixteen in total, plus a glottal stop that Bolton treats as non-phonemic. Syllables follow a (C)V(N) shape. The language has no adjectives; nouns and verbs carry that function, and reduplication intensifies verbs, nouns and adverbs. Nouns fall into five classes, four marked by the suffixes *-e*, *-ne*, *-te* and *-ke*, and possession is split between alienable and inalienable marking. The northern and southern dialects are separated by a communication barrier; a 1991 survey of coastal villages in southern Seram found about 1,000 speakers of the southern dialect. Language use tracks religion: those who keep the ancestral religion use Nuaulu more, while converts use Ambonese Malay, which is needed for schooling.
