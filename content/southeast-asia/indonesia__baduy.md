## Overview

The Baduy, also known as Badui or Kanékés, are an indigenous Sundanese ethnic group native to the southeastern part of Banten, specifically Lebak Regency, Indonesia. Their homeland lies around the drainage basin of the Ci Ujung and Cikanekes rivers, near the foothills of Kendeng volcano, within the settlement of Kanekes in Leuwidamar district. The region consists of hilly forest at an elevation of 300–500 meters above sea level. Based on 2017 data, the population consisted of 11,699 individuals, or 3,413 families; by 2023 it had declined to 9,558 individuals. The Baduy language is conventionally considered a dialect of Sundanese, although it is also considered a separate language. The community is notable for its voluntary isolation, customary regulations, ancestral worship, oral traditions, and continuing rejection of many forms of modern technology.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Hand-woven black or white fabrics** — Inner Baduy people wear hand-woven black or white fabrics as part of their clothing.

**Blue-black homespun clothes** — The Baduy Luar traditionally wore blue-black homespun clothes, although conventional clothing has increasingly replaced them.

### Clothing & dress

Inner Baduy clothing is described as white and dark blue, together with a white headband. The Kanekes Dalam people follow customary regulations that prohibit modern clothing and permit only hand-woven black or white fabrics. No footwear is allowed in the Inner Baduy community. The Baduy Luar have traded their traditional blue-black homespun clothes for conventional clothing, and Kanekes Luar people may wear modern clothing such as T-shirts and jeans. The sources state that the use of flowers or perfumes is forbidden and that cutting the hair is prohibited.

### Architecture

The sources describe Kanekes houses as being built without changing the contour of the soil surface. Consequently, their poles are often not of the same length. Among the Inner Baduy, the door of the house should face north or south, except in the house of the pu'un, or customary chairman. Construction in Kanekes Luar permits modern tools such as saws, hammers, and nails. Modern household furnishings, including mattresses, pillows, and plastic or glass plates and cups, are allowed there.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention a mortar container at the Arca Domas complex, where rainwater is stored, and sharpened bamboo used instead of plows in agriculture. Modern tools are prohibited in Inner Baduy life, while Kanekes Luar construction may use saws, hammers, and nails. Gold and silver are not accepted under the Baduy taboo system, and touching money is forbidden in the stricter customary rules.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 They state that Inner Baduy people wear a white headband and that the Baduy are forbidden to wear flowers or perfumes and to cut their hair.

## Music & performance

 They state that Inner Baduy customary religious beliefs and ancestral folk tales are preserved through oral tradition because Inner Baduy people are mostly illiterate. The sources also mention specific chants used to ward off malevolent spirits associated with the name Pajajaran. No information is supplied about musical performance at weddings, funerals, festivals, courts, or other gatherings.

## Festivals & rituals

The Baduy livelihood of rice farming is organized into four phases over the course of a year: harvesting, post-harvest rituals, land clearing, and planting and field maintenance. During harvesting, outsiders are not allowed in the villages for around three months. The Kanekes community performs the **seba** ceremony each year, delivering crops such as rice and fruit to the Governor of Banten, formerly to the Governor of West Java, through the regent of Lebak.

The most important religious object for the Kanekes people is the Arca Domas, whose location is kept secret and considered sacred. The Kanekes people visit it once a year in the month of Kalima. In 2003, this coincided with July. Only the pu'un and several elected community members follow the entourage to worship. Rainwater kept in a mortar container is examined during the rite: clear water is understood as a sign of plentiful rain and a bountiful harvest, while a dry or turbid container signifies crop failure. They state that many mystical taboos regulate daily life, including prohibitions on killing, stealing, lying, adultery, drunkenness, night-time eating, conveyances, and the use of certain agricultural practices.

## Foodways

Rice farming is the main livelihood of the Baduy. Their agricultural rules prohibit growing sawah, or wet rice, and require fields to be worked without plows or terraces, using hoe-farming methods with sharpened bamboo. The Baduy also gather fruits in the jungle, including durian and tamarind-plum, and collect wild honey. These products provide additional income when sold.

The Kanekes people sell fruits, honey, and sugar palm through middlemen and purchase necessities that they do not produce themselves from markets. They state that some Outer Baduy villages eat meat caught with trained hunting dogs, while raising livestock remains banned. Eating food at night is among the listed taboos.

## Oral tradition & literature

Baduy oral tradition includes a creation story in which the Earth began as a viscous, transparent substance called *ngenclong*. It hardened and expanded into the Sasaka Domas, a megalithic structure regarded as the core and center of the Earth and the starting point for all life. The story describes Buana Nyungcung as the sky, Buana Larang as hell, and Buana Tengah as the home of humans, trees, and animals. Buana Suci Alam Padang, the dwelling place of the rice goddesses, consists of 18 layers. Its highest layer is Bumi Suci Alam Kahiyangan, or Mandala Hiyang, where Nyi Pohaci Sanghiyang Asri and Sunan Ambu live.

The Baduy regard themselves as descendants of Batara Cikal, one of seven deities sent to Earth. Ancestral folk tales and customary religious beliefs are preserved through oral tradition. The sources also describe the tradition of **seba puun**, in which the village leader, or puun, reports the condition of the village to the ruler or regent. No named epic, written literary tradition, proverb collection, riddle tradition, or contemporary literary revival is documented.

## Language & religion

Baduy, also called Kanekes, is spoken predominantly by the Baduy people and is conventionally considered a dialect of Sundanese. It is also regarded as a separate language because of diverging vocabulary and cultural differences. Baduy is placed within the Sundanese-Baduy languages, whose classification remains debated between Malayo-Sumbawan and Greater North Borneo, both branches of Malayo-Polynesian within the Austronesian languages. It has subject–verb–object word order and is agglutinative. The Baduy language was estimated to have 11,620 speakers as of 2015.

The Baduy religion is known as Agama Sunda Wiwitan and is rooted in ancestral worship and the honoring of spirits of natural forces. Its central customary principle is *pikukuh*, or absolute customary provisions. The Baduy preserve mountains, forests, rivers, gardens, springs, and other ecosystems as part of maintaining the balance of the universe. Their religious system includes elements of Hinduism and, to some extent, Islam. Islamic influence is especially present among the Baduy Luar of Cicakal Girang, which is inhabited primarily by Muslims.
