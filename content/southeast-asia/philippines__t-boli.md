## Overview

The T'boli are highland farmers and master weavers displaced to the mountains after World War II by settlers from the north. They are recognized as an indigenous people under Philippine law and grouped with other non-Muslim, non-Christian Mindanao groups as Lumad. They are most celebrated for t'nalak, one of Southeast Asia's finest ikat traditions, and for a distinctive material culture of brass work, beadwork, and carved wood. Their life centers on the longhouse, the loom, horse-fighting, and ritual performances tied to the agricultural year and life cycle.

## Material culture

T'boli material culture centers on woven abaca cloth, cast brass, and dense beadwork, with a vocabulary of named motifs, tools, and ornaments tied to weaving, warfare, and status.

### Textile & pattern traditions

T'nalak is a warp-ikat cloth of abaca fiber in cream, red, and black, woven on a backstrap loom with designs revealed in dreams to master weavers.

- **T'nalak** — A three-color warp-ikat woven from abaca fiber, dyed in cream, red (from loko root), and black (from k'nalum leaves), with designs said to come in dreams from Fu Dalu, the abaca spirit.
- **Dream weavers** — Master weavers who receive t'nalak designs in dreams, most famously Lang Dulay of Lake Sebu, a National Living Treasure.
- **T'boli brass casting** (*cire perdue*) — Lost-wax casting using clay-and-wax molds and hand-pumped bellows to make ornaments, betel-nut boxes, and bells in T'boli-specific forms.
- **T'boli beadwork** (*swat*) — Dense polychrome beaded collars, sashes, and headbands using imported glass seed beads with shell discs, coix seeds, and horsehair on a deep-red ground.
- **Kegal binsiwit** — A blouse of tie-dyed abaca or cotton worn over the t'nalak tube skirt, often edged with mother-of-pearl sequins and tiny bells.

### Clothing & dress

Women wear beaded blouses and t'nalak tube skirts with multiple brass ornaments and a coiled hairstyle fastened with a beaded comb; men wear collarless jackets and woven sashes.

- **K'gal binsiwit** — A long-sleeved blouse of dyed abaca decorated with beads, sequins, and small brass bells, worn as the upper garment of women's daily dress.
- **T'nalak skirt** (*k'gal*) — A tubular skirt woven from t'nalak cloth, wrapped and belted at the waist as the lower garment of women's dress.
- **Suwat** — An ornamental comb of carabao horn or wood inlaid with beads and horsehair, worn as both hair fastener and status marker.
- **Singkil leglets and anklets** (*singkil*) — Brass leglets and anklets with pellet bells worn by women so that walking produces a continuous jingle marking status.
- **S'laong kinibang domed hat** (*s'laong kinibang*) — A great domed hat of plaited rattan and horsehair worn by both sexes as a sunshade and emblematic T'boli object.

### Architecture

The T'boli longhouse is a raised timber-and-bamboo structure with a steeply pitched thatch roof, a single multi-family room, and a platform for the datu at one end.

- **Gono bong** — The traditional T'boli longhouse, a raised timber-and-bamboo structure on hardwood posts about two meters above ground with a steeply pitched thatch roof of cogon grass.
- **Desyung** — A raised platform at one end of the longhouse where the datu receives guests and where the weaving loom is set up.
- **Kumu rice granary** (*kumu*) — A separate rice storage structure standing on posts fitted with wooden disc rat-guards to prevent rodent access.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Cast brass is the signature medium, producing swords, daggers, betel-nut containers, and small pellet bells sewn into ceremonial dress.

- **Kafilan sword** (*kafilan*) — A long single-edged sword with brass-inlaid hilt and carved horn scabbard made by T'boli smiths.
- **Tok dagger** (*tok*) — A dagger with brass-inlaid hilt and carved horn scabbard, a prestige object made by T'boli smiths.
- **Betel-nut containers** (*kafilan*) — Ornate brass boxes with hinged lids for carrying areca nut, cast using the lost-wax method.
- **Singkil pellet bells** (*singkil*) — Small clustered pellet bells cast in brass and sewn into ceremonial dress to produce jingling sounds.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Women layer glass and shell bead necklaces, wear brass rings and ornamental combs, and traditionally tattoo geometric patterns on forearms and calves.

- **Lieg bead necklaces** (*lieg*) — Strands of glass and shell beads worn in multiple layers at the neck as a standard adornment for women.
- **Blonso brass belt** (*blonso*) — A broad brass belt of linked plates hung with pellet bells worn at weddings and healing rites.
- **Bak earplugs** (*bak*) — Heavy earplugs made of brass or shell worn as a mark of status.
- **Bemble tattoos** (*bemble*) — Geometric bands tattooed on forearms and calves, echoing t'nalak motifs; the practice has largely lapsed but is being revived.

## Music & performance

T'boli music is built on suspended bronze gongs without a melodic row, with melody carried by a two-string lute and accompanied by jew's harps, flutes, and tube zithers.

- **Agung gongs** (*agung*) — Suspended, knobbed bronze gongs played as a drone without a melodic row, forming the core of the T'boli ensemble.
- **Hegelung lute** (*hegelung*) — A two-string boat-shaped lute with beeswax frets, plucked to accompany courtship songs and epic recitation.
- **Kubing bamboo jaw's harp** (*kubing*) — A bamboo jew's harp played as a melodic instrument in the T'boli ensemble.
- **Sloli lip-valley flute** (*sloli*) — A lip-valley flute used in T'boli music performances.
- **Tud-om chanted narrative** (*tud-om*) — A long chanted narrative sung by an elder at wakes and weddings, ranging from cosmogonic accounts to genealogical recitations.

## Dance & theatre

T'boli dance is mimetic, tied to animals and the movement of the weaver, performed at weddings, horse-fights, and healing rites without masked or puppet-theatre traditions.

- **Madal tahaw bird dance** (*madal tahaw*) — A dance imitating the tailorbird with arms spread and slow crossing steps, performed by young women in full beaded dress.
- **Madal be lawan warrior dance** (*madal be lawan*) — A warrior dance for men carrying a kafilan sword and wooden shield, stamping in time to the agung.
- **Madal iwas monkey dance** (*madal iwas*) — A comic dance piece for children imitating monkey movements.

## Festivals & rituals

The major public festival is Helobung in Lake Sebu in November, featuring horse-fights, t'nalak parades, and gong ensembles; life-cycle rites center on the longhouse platform.

- **Helobung festival** (*Helobung*) — A November festival held in Lake Sebu around the founding anniversary of the municipality, featuring horse-fights, t'nalak fashion parades, and brass-casting demonstrations.
- **T'nalak Festival** — A September festival in Koronadal celebrating the cloth as a provincial emblem with street dancing in t'nalak-patterned costume.
- **Horse-fights** — A distinctive T'boli spectacle in which stallions are set against one another over a mare in season, performed at celebrations and healing rites.
- **Sunggod bride-wealth** (*sunggod*) — A bride-wealth traditionally including a horse, a brass gong, a kafilan, and lengths of t'nalak, negotiated by a professional intermediary at marriage.

## Foodways

The staple is upland rice cultivated by swidden, complemented by maize, cassava, taro, and sweet potato; kesiyu is the signature stew.

- **Halay upland rice** (*halay*) — The staple crop of upland rice cultivated by swidden agriculture, forming the basis of the T'boli diet.
- **Kesiyu stew** (*kesiyu*) — The signature everyday dish, a stew of freshwater fish or chicken with ginger, chilli, and pigeon pea, eaten with hand-pressed rice.
- **Utan vegetable pot** (*utan*) — A mixed vegetable pot of squash tips, taro leaves, and string beans served as a daily side dish.
- **Tinifu sticky rice** (*tinifu*) — Sticky rice steamed in banana leaves, served as ceremonial food at weddings.
- **Mama betel-nut chewing** (*mama*) — Areca nut with lime and piper betle leaf, carried in a hinged brass box as the standard gesture of hospitality.

## Oral tradition & literature

The tud-om is the great T'boli narrative form, a long sung poem performed over several nights ranging from cosmogonic creation accounts to genealogical recitations.

- **Tud-om epic** (*tud-om*) — A long sung poem performed by an elder over several nights, ranging from cosmogonic accounts of D'wata creating the world through mythological journeys to genealogical recitations.
- **Owe personal lament** (*owe*) — A shorter song of personal lament or courtship, standing alongside the tud-om as a major song genre.
- **Todbulol trickster hero** (*Todbulol*) — An epic hero in T'boli folktales whose adventures parallel the Maranao Darangen cycle but focus on the loom, the horse, and the mountain.
- **Lengon proverbs** (*lengon*) — Proverbs exchanged as entertainment and courtship testing among the T'boli.
- **Bat riddles** (*bat*) — Riddles exchanged as entertainment and courtship testing among the T'boli.

## Language & religion

Tboli belongs to the Bilic subgroup of Malayo-Polynesian Austronesian, written in Latin orthography since the mid-twentieth century; traditional polytheistic religion is now syncretic with Catholicism and Protestantism.

- **Tboli language** (*Tboli*) — A South Mindanao Austronesian language closely related to Blaan and Tiruray, now written in Latin orthography with apostrophes marking the glottal stop.
- **D'wata high god** (*D'wata*) — The high god in traditional T'boli polytheistic religion, creator of the world.
- **Fu Dalu abaca spirit** (*Fu Dalu*) — The spirit of abaca in T'boli belief, said to reveal t'nalak designs in dreams to master weavers.
- **Tao mulung shaman-healer** (*tao mulung*) — The female shaman-healer who mediates with nature and ancestor spirits in T'boli traditional religion.
