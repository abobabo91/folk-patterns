## Overview

The Lun Bawang are an indigenous ethnic group of Central Northern Borneo. They live in the southwest of Sabah, including the Interior Division and Labuan; in northern Sarawak, especially Limbang Division; in the highlands of North Kalimantan, including Long Bawan, Krayan, Malinau and Mentarang; and in Brunei’s Temburong District. In Sarawak they are officially recognised as natives and categorised among the Orang Ulu. In Sabah and the Krayan highland they are also called Lundayeh or Lun Daye, while other names include Lun Lod, Lun Baa’ and Lun Tana Luun. They traditionally practise agriculture, animal husbandry, hunting and fishing. Their language belongs to the Austronesian, Western Malayo-Polynesian, North Bornean, North Sarawak and Dayic family. Their folk culture is documented through rice cultivation, food preservation, beadwork, clothing, festivals, oral tradition and Christian-era community institutions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

They do describe clothing, beadwork and ornaments below.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional men’s clothing included *kuyu talun*, jackets made from tree bark. A cloth wrapped around the forehead was called *sigar*, while the loincloth was called *abpar*. Men also tied a long machete, *pelepet*, to the waist, particularly when it was carried to tribal wars.

Women wore *pata* on the head, *beret* on the waist and *bane* around the neck. *Gileng* or *pakel* was worn as an ornament on the hands and wrists. The *pata* was a cap made entirely of beads and was worn as a status symbol.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention the *pelepet*, a long machete tied to the waist, and hollow bamboo stalks used to store pickled meat and fish.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The documented forms of body adornment are *bane*, worn around the neck, and *gileng* or *pakel*, worn on the hands and wrists. The beaded head cap *pata* functioned as a status symbol.

## Music & performance

The sources name *ngiup suling*, described as a bamboo musical-instrument band. It is presented as part of the programme of Irau Aco Lun Bawang, an annual festival in Lawas, Sarawak.

The sources also mention the Lun Bawang language’s oral character and record *some Lun Bawang Spirit Chants* in the bibliography of the Lun Bawang language article.

## Dance & theatre

The sources state that traditional dances and costumes are showcased during the harvest festival in Sipitang district of Sabah, as part of GATA, or Gasing and Tamu Besar.

## Festivals & rituals

Lun Bawang people celebrate **Irau Aco Lun Bawang**, also called the Lun Bawang festival, annually on the first of June in Lawas, Sarawak. It traditionally celebrates the rice harvest and now presents a variety of Lun Bawang cultural events, including *Ruran Ulung*, a beauty pageant contest, and *ngiup suling*, a bamboo musical-instrument band.

In Sipitang district of Sabah, Sabahan Lundayeh celebrate the harvest festival **Kaamatan** biennially during **GATA**, expanded in the source as Gasing and Tamu Besar. Traditional dances and costumes are presented alongside those of other native communities, including the Murut, Kedayan and Bruneian Malay people.

In Indonesian parts of northern Kalimantan, the predominantly Christian Lun Bawang community traditionally celebrates **Irau Rayeh**. The source describes it as an Easter festival and celebration and states that it has been observed since the 1950s.

Rice harvest and communal feasting are also connected with *irau*. An excess of rice harvest was traditionally consumed at a large irau feast, signifying wealth and fortune.

The Lun Bawang and related groups traditionally practised a secondary treatment of the dead called *mitang butung*.

## Foodways

Rice is central to Lun Bawang economic and culinary life. The community cultivates hill rice called *lati’ tana’ luun* and paddy-field rice called *lati’ ba*. Cooked rice is wrapped in banana leaves as *Luba’ Laya*, and rice is also brewed into *burak*, a rice wine. The production of rice was associated with prestige and financial status, while the consumption of excess harvest at an *irau* feast signified wealth and fortune.

*Nuba Laya* or *Nuba Tinga* is cooked Bario rice that is mashed and wrapped in leaves of the *Phacelophrynium maximum* plant. It is described as the centrepiece of a meal for Lun Bawang and Kelabit people. Accompaniments include *kikid*, a small bowl of porridge; *labo senutuq*, shredded beef cooked with wild ginger and dried chilli; *a’beng*, deboned shredded fish; and wild jungle vegetables.

*Daun ubi tumbuk* or *pucuk ubi tumbuk* is made from pounded cassava leaves. The leaves may be sautéed with anchovies and chilli, placed in a bamboo tube and roasted over an open fire, or boiled with shallot, fat and salt.

*Kelupis* means “glutinous rice rolls” in English and is commonly produced in large batches for a traditional wedding ceremony. *Sinamu Baka* is a tangy fermented food made only from fermented wild boar meat. *Narar Baka* is Lun Bawang/Lundayeh-style smoked meat, usually wild boar meat, barbecued on a char-griller and eaten with rice and dipping sauces. Halal versions replace wild boar with other game meats such as venison.

Meat and fish are brined or pickled with salt, stored in hollow bamboo stalks for a month and called *telu’*. They are also preserved by smoking. Salt is obtained by evaporating brine from a salt spring called *lubang mein*. Cattle and buffaloes are bred for meat and used as dowry presented to the bride’s family by the groom’s side.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe Lun Bawang as a mainly oral language. They state that very little printed material in the language was produced independently of missionaries or linguists. The first published work written fully in Lun Bawang was a Bible translation from 1982 called *Bala Luk Do*. A Lun Bawang–English dictionary was constructed in 1969 by the University of Washington, and the Kemaloh Lundayeh dialect was compiled in 2006 into a bilingual Lundayeh–English dictionary.

The Lun Bawang language article records a version of the Lord’s Prayer, identified as “Our Father,” in Lun Bawang and gives an English translation from Matthew 6:9–13. The sources also list *Upai Kasan: A Lun Bawang Folktale* and describe a Lun Dayeh engagement negotiation in a scholarly publication.

## Language & religion

The Lun Bawang language is called *Buri Lun Bawang* or *Buri tau*, meaning “our language.” The language article also calls it *Buri’ Tau*, the Lun Bawang language, Lundayeh language and Putoh. It belongs to the Malayo-Polynesian family; the broader classification given in the ethnographic article is Austronesian, Western Malayo-Polynesian, North Bornean, North Sarawak and Dayic, associated with the Apo Duat or Apad Uat family. Lun Bawang is mainly oral. The sources state that it has six vowels, nineteen consonants and five diphthongs.

Lun Bawangs were mostly animist before the 1920s. Under the White Rajahs in Sarawak, Christian missionaries, particularly the Borneo Evangelical Mission, gained access to settlements in the interior and highlands and preached Christianity. The majority of Lun Bawangs are Christians, predominantly associated with the BEM. Smaller numbers belong to the True Jesus Church, the Seventh-day Adventist Church, the Roman Catholic Church, Islam or Buddhism.
