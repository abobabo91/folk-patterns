## Overview

The Shan are a Tai-speaking people of eastern Myanmar. The 34 objects selected here come from museum records with a Shan attribution. They include dress, bags, containers, arms, sound-making objects and ritual items. Shan State is also home to other peoples; collection place alone does not establish a Shan maker or user.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The selected embroidered caps and bags show several treatments of cloth and ornament; no single pattern defines the whole community.

### Clothing & dress

- **Embroidered caps** — British Museum caps show several constructions and decorative treatments.
- **Jacket, hats and shoes** — The selected garments document particular examples rather than a standard Shan costume.
- **Carrying bags** — British Museum and Gothenburg records include embroidered and decorated bags.

### Architecture

The selected portable objects do not document a specific Shan building type. The British Museum's historical account describes Shan principalities but gives no basis for a universal house form here.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

- **Lacquer betel box and jar** — Two British Museum containers show lacquered forms used for storage or serving.
- **Teapot and gourd-shaped vessel** — The Gothenburg museum records these pottery pieces as Shan.
- **Water bottles, balance and weight** — Other selected objects add carrying and measuring equipment.
- **Dao and sheaths** — Several British Museum records document blades and their fittings.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The selection has one ornament and a tattoo design stamp. Their records document individual forms, not a standard set of jewelry or a community-wide tattoo practice.

## Music & performance

- **Gong and bell** — Museum objects show two sound-making forms without documenting a complete musical repertoire.

## Dance & theatre

- **Peacock dance** — A linked photograph is identified as a Shan peacock dance; the sources here do not establish a theatre repertory.

## Festivals & rituals

- **Poi Sang Long** — A photograph describes a Shan Buddhist novice ordination celebration in Thailand.
- **Tattoo design stamp** — A carved wooden stamp has magic squares, red ink traces and a spirit figure; the British Museum identifies a Shan maker.
- **Charm and small figures** — The collection includes an inscribed protective charm, a ceremonial doll and figures with varying degrees of attribution certainty.

## Foodways

The British Museum describes rice cultivation on the Shan Plateau. The selected teapot, water bottles and betel box document containers, while a linked photograph records a rice dish called Shan htamin. The objects do not establish a full cuisine or recipes.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources here do not document a named Shan oral genre. A British Museum manuscript does record written Buddhist texts from the Shan States.

## Language & religion

A British Museum manuscript from the Shan States uses Shan script for Shan, Burmese and Pali text, including Buddhist sutta summaries. The Library of Congress places Shan manuscripts within wider Tai writing traditions. These sources document specific religious and literary practices without describing every Shan community.

## Glossary

- *Tai* — A name used within Shan communities and the wider language family.
- *Tai Yai* — A name for Shan people used in Thailand.
- *dao* — A blade form represented in the British Museum selection.
- *Poi Sang Long* — A Shan novice ordination celebration documented in the linked photograph.
