## Overview

The Idaʼan — also written *Idahan* — live on the east coast of Sabah, in Malaysian Borneo, where their language is spoken around Sagama and villages west of Lahad Datu, in Ulu Tungku and villages east of Lahad Datu, and in the districts of Kinabatangan and Sandakan. Their language belongs to the Malayo-Polynesian family. The sources used here give no population figure. What makes the group notable in cultural-historical terms is documentary: the earliest known work in the language is a manuscript dated 1408 A.D., written in the Jawi script, which records an Idaʼan man named Abdullah in Darvel Bay embracing Islam — making this one of the earliest known regions in Malaysia to do so. The material record shown in this atlas is a British Museum holding of weapons, plaited hats and baskets, barkcloth garments, bamboo instruments and a sacred pottery jar.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The catalogue records behind this atlas identify Idaʼan textiles only by material and state, not by named weaving tradition, and the Wikipedia article on the language describes no textile practice. What the objects establish is the material range rather than a repertoire of named cloths.

**Barkcloth garments** — Beaten bark is the fabric of a sleeveless jacket, of a man's dress combining barkcloth with vegetable fibre, and of a hat made of barkcloth and twine. Barkcloth here is a garment material in its own right, not a substitute for woven cloth.

**Grass cloth** — A fringed cloth made of grass is held in the collection, worked from plant material rather than spun thread.

**Woven cloth with metal** — A woman's dress is made of textile fitted with a metal chain, so that metalwork forms part of the garment itself.

**Unfinished textile** — One record is an unfinished fibre textile, the kind of object that preserves the working method rather than the finished piece.

**Ticking shroud** — A cloth item of ticking and thread is catalogued, with uncertainty, as a shroud, which would place cloth inside funerary use.

### Clothing & dress

Idaʼan dress in the collection divides along lines the catalogue itself marks. A man's dress is made of barkcloth with vegetable fibre; a woman's dress is of woven textile with a metal chain attached; a sleeveless jacket is of barkcloth, unassigned by gender. Headgear is the best-represented category and shows two distinct making traditions. Several hats are plaited from rattan without further description. A repeated conical type is plaited from bamboo and carries a deliberate colour scheme: three stripes of dark plaiting run vertically from the edge of the hat to its tip, with three strips of red-dyed bamboo running between them, and a thick piece of bamboo is lashed around the rim to hold the shape. That this same hat appears in the collection several times over indicates a standard, reproducible form rather than a single maker's improvisation. A further hat is made of barkcloth and twine. The sources say nothing about belts, footwear, or any ceremonial costume distinct from daily wear.

### Architecture

Neither the language article nor the catalogue records describe Idaʼan house forms, building materials, roofing or architectural ornament, so the sources used here do not cover the built environment.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The strongest single category is arms. A sword of brass, wood and rattan survives with a scabbard of brass, wood, rattan, hide and hair — the hair a decorative addition to the sheath rather than the blade. A knife is made of bone with metal, and two knife sheaths are of wood and rattan bound with cord or plaited sinnet, one of them incorporating iron. A shield is made entirely of rattan. Projectile equipment includes a bamboo-and-rattan quiver with a cylindrical bamboo lid decorated with rattan strips and tied on with cord; nine arrows, of which two are bamboo with pith tails and poisoned, two poisoned without flights, and five neither flighted nor poisoned; and twelve poisoned darts of bamboo or leaf spine, with pith. Domestic and working objects are mostly plaited or carved: a back-carried basket of bamboo and vegetable fibre with a loose wooden disc inside forming a platform, a carrying basket of plant stems, wood and cane, a wooden and plant-stem container, a bamboo and rattan box tied with sinnet, a bamboo lid for a lime box, a fish trap of cane and bark, and a wooden plough. Ceramics appear once, as a sacred pottery jar classed in the catalogue as ritual equipment.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources record no Idaʼan jewellery, tattooing, hairdressing or other body adornment; the only ornamented personal item is the metal chain attached to a woman's textile dress.

## Music & performance

Two instruments in the collection establish that Idaʼan music was made with bamboo and cane rather than with imported metal. A jaw harp — the museum's "Jew's harp" — is made of bamboo with string, and possibly gum and wax, the plucked lamella type found across island Southeast Asia. A second instrument, catalogued with a question mark as a mouth organ, is built of cane with gum and rattan, an aerophone assembled from cane tubes with resin sealing. Beyond these two objects the sources used here name no Idaʼan song genre, no ensemble, and no performance occasion, so nothing can be said about repertoire, tuning, or the contexts — wedding, funeral, harvest — in which either instrument was played. Both are the kind of intimate, close-range instrument associated with solo playing rather than with ensemble performance, but the catalogue does not say so, and the point is left open here rather than filled in. One documentary fact about the language bears on any future recording work: the Begak dialect is reported as threatened with extinction because younger speakers are shifting to Malay, which means that song texts in that variety are on the same trajectory as the everyday speech.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used here record no Idaʼan dance or dramatic tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

Malaysia has no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions, so there is no inscribed element to attach to the Idaʼan calendar, and neither the language article nor the museum records describe an annual festival cycle. Two objects nevertheless point directly at ritual practice. The first is a pottery jar catalogued as sacred and classed as religious or ritual equipment — a jar held apart from ordinary storage vessels and recognised as such by whoever collected it. The second is a cloth of ticking and thread recorded, with uncertainty, as a shroud, which would place woven cloth in the handling of the dead. The one securely dated religious event in the Idaʼan record is a conversion rather than a rite: the 1408 manuscript describes Abdullah of Darvel Bay embracing Islam, and the Idaʼan followed him, while the Begak and Subpan peoples — who had formed a single group with them — continued in their traditional religion. That split is itself the most consequential ritual fact the sources report, since it separated one community's ceremonial life from that of its closest kin. Birth, coming-of-age, marriage and mortuary observances are not described in the sources used here.

## Foodways

The sources describe no Idaʼan staple crop, cooking method, dish or drinking tradition. Two objects indicate how food was obtained and carried rather than prepared: a fish trap made of cane and bark, and a wooden plough, together with back-carried and hand-carried baskets of bamboo, cane and plant stems, one fitted with a loose wooden disc as an internal platform. A bamboo lid for a lime box belongs to betel preparation rather than to cooking. Nothing in the sources states what was grown with the plough or taken with the trap.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Idaʼan have a long literary history in the documentary sense: the earliest known work in the language is the manuscript dated 1408 A.D., written in Jawi script, giving an account of Abdullah of Darvel Bay and his conversion to Islam. The sources name no Idaʼan epic, folktale cycle, proverb collection or storytelling occasion. What they do record is modern linguistic documentation, which is where the present preservation effort sits: John E. Banker's survey description of the language, published in *Languages of Sabah: A Survey Report* (1984), and Nelleke Goudswaard's full-length grammar of the Begak dialect (2005). Lobel's 2016 classification groups Sungai Seguliud and Begak together as Idaanic varieties closely related to Idaʼan proper.

## Language & religion

*Idaʼan* (also *Idahan*) is a Malayo-Polynesian language of the east coast of Sabah. It has been described as having three dialects, each corresponding to one of the three ethnic groups that originally formed a single community: *Idaʼan* proper, spoken in Sagama and villages west of Lahad Datu; *Begak*, spoken in Ulu Tungku and villages east of Lahad Datu; and *Subpan*, spoken in the Kinabatangan and Sandakan districts. Lobel (2016) treats Sungai Seguliud and Begak as Idaanic varieties. Begak is threatened with extinction as younger speakers switch to Malay. The historical script for the language is Jawi, in which the 1408 manuscript is written. Religiously the community is Muslim: the Idaʼan converted to Islam after Abdullah's conversion, while the Begak and Subpan kept their traditional religion.
