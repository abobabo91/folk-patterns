## Overview

The Bicolano people, also called *Mga Bikolnon*, are the fourth-largest Filipino ethnolinguistic group. Their homeland is Bicol, comprising the Bicol Peninsula and neighboring minor islands in the southeastern portion of Luzon. The Bicol Region contains Albay, Camarines Norte, Camarines Sur, Catanduanes, Sorsogon and Masbate; Bicolanos also live in Quezon, Metro Manila, Northern Samar, Davao Region, Misamis Oriental, Caraga and Soccsksargen. They numbered 6,082,165 in 2020. Their livelihoods include rice, coconut, abacá, spices, fishing and mining. They speak closely related Bikol dialects belonging to the Austronesian, specifically Malayo-Polynesian, language family. Their folk culture includes agricultural practices, Catholic festivals, animistic beliefs, ritual observances, foodways, folk songs, epic traditions and distinctive regional vocabulary.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Sinamay* — Bicolanos seldom weave sinamay or piña for clothing as in the past. Sinamay is now reserved for pillow cases, mosquito nets, fishing nets, bags and other decorative items.

*Piña* — Piña is named as a material formerly woven for clothing.

### Clothing & dress

Bicolanos predominantly use light, western-styled clothes suited to the tropical climate. The typical Bicolano wears clothing similar to that of other Filipinos in urban centers. The sources state that men are often called Bicolano and women may be called Bicolana.

The sources mention that sinamay and piña were formerly woven for clothing, while sinamay is now more commonly used for pillow cases, mosquito nets, fishing nets, bags and other decorative items. No named festival garment or special costume is documented. The annual celebration of Our Lady of Peñafrancia is described through devotional actions, processions and the carrying of the Virgin’s image rather than through particular clothing.

### Architecture

Bicolano houses use light materials suited to the tropical climate. Some people now have bungalows designed to withstand strong typhoons. The sources connect house construction to the region’s erratic climate and typhoon-prone physical environment.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Archaeological excavations and accidental discoveries connected with mining, road-building and railway projects show that the Bicol mainland contains ceramic artifacts. Burial cave findings point to a pre-Hispanic practice of using burial jars. Agricultural equipment includes the carabao-drawn plough and harrow, sickles, *basbas* for threshing rice straw and the *nigo*, a winnowing basket. Fishing equipment includes the *basnig*, *pangki*, *chinchoro*, *buliche*, *sarap*, *sumbiling*, *banwit*, *og-og*, *kitang*, *bunuan*, *inangcla*, *sakag* and *sibid-sibid*.

## Music & performance

Bicolano oral music includes the folk song *Sarung Banggi*, identified as the best-known of the region’s folk songs. Other named forms are *awit*, *sinamlampati*, *panayokyok*, *panambitan*, *hatol*, *pag-omaw*, *rawit-dawit* and children’s songs and chants. The sources connect these songs with folk tales and with heroic stories expressing kindness, determination to conquer evil forces, resourcefulness and courage. The annual celebration of Our Lady of Peñafrancia includes shows at the plaza, and the wider festival involves a fluvial procession.

Bikol language itself contains vocabulary associated with speech registers. The angry register is called *tamanggot* or *rapsak* and is used when angry, shouting or speaking in a high-pitched voice. The sources describe this as a linguistic register rather than a musical genre.

## Dance & theatre

They mention an annual fluvial procession, bicycle races, cockfights and shows at the plaza during the Our Lady of Peñafrancia celebration, but do not characterize these activities as dance or theatre.

## Festivals & rituals

Bicolanos observe an annual festival in honor of Our Lady of Peñafrancia on the third Sunday of September. The City of Naga becomes the center of the celebration. An all-male crowd carries the Virgin’s image on its shoulders to the Naga Metropolitan Cathedral while shouting “Viva La Virgen!” During the next nine days, mostly Bicolano visitors light candles and kiss the cult image.

The celebration combines religious and cultural activities. Shows are held at the plaza, the year’s biggest cockfights take place, bicycle races are held, and a lively boat race precedes the fluvial procession. At noon on the third Saturday of the month, devotees carry the image to the waterfront. On the ninth day, the Virgin of Peñafrancia is brought to the Minor Basilica of the Our Lady of Peñafrancia through a grand fluvial procession on the Naga River. The celebration is described as one of the largest Marian celebrations in Asia.

Other annual rituals named in the sources are *pabasa*, *tanggal*, fiestas and *flores de mayo*. The sources also mention the *pamalaye*, *pantomina* and *tigsikan* as persisting traditional practices.

## Foodways

Bicolano cuisine is known for the prominent use of chili peppers and *gata*, or coconut milk. A classic dish is *gulay na lada*, known outside the region as Bicol express, prepared with *siling labuyo*, described as native small chilies, and coconut milk. Bicolanos are said to have a high tolerance for chili food and highly spiced dishes.

Meals are generally rich in carbohydrates, and vegetables are almost always cooked in coconut milk. Meat dishes named in the sources include *pochero*, *adobo*, *tapa* and *dinuguan*. Commonly eaten fish include mackerel and anchovy. In Lake Buhi, *sinarapan*, also called *tabyos*, is common; the sources describe it as the smallest fish in the world.

Rice, corn and root crops are supplied by the Bicol River basin, described as a rice granary. Coconut and abacá are important products, while copra processing and abacá stripping are generally done by hand. Fish supplies are normally abundant from May through September.

## Oral tradition & literature

The folk epic *Ibalong* explains an earlier name for the people of the region. According to the epic, they were formerly called *Ibalong* or *Ibalnong*, a name believed to derive from Gat Ibal, who ruled Sawangan, now the city of Legazpi, in ancient times. *Ibalong* originally meant “people of Ibal” and was later shortened to *Ibalon*. The name *Bikol* replaced *Ibalon* and was originally *bikod*, meaning “meandering,” a description supposedly associated with the principal river of the area.

Folk tales and folk songs are said to abound. The best-known folk song is *Sarung Banggi*. Heroic stories emphasize kindness, the determination to conquer evil forces, resourcefulness and courage.

## Language & religion

Bikol languages are Austronesian languages, specifically part of the Malayo-Polynesian superfamily. Central Bikol, commonly called Bikol Naga or simply Bikol, is spoken primarily in the Bicol Region of southern Luzon. Speakers are found throughout the provinces of Bicol and beyond, including Metro Manila, Mindoro, Palawan and Mindanao.

Central Bikol has six mutually comprehensible dialects: Canaman, Naga City, Partido, Tabaco-Legazpi-Sorsogon, Daet and Virac. The Canaman dialect is used in literature, Catholic religious rites and mass media. Bikol has vocabulary shared with or influenced by other languages, including Spanish loanwords such as *swerte*, *karne*, *imbestigador*, *litro*, *pero* and *krimen*, and Sanskrit-derived words such as *bahala* and *karma*.

A great majority of Bicolanos are Roman Catholics. Catholic Mass is celebrated daily in many churches, and towns hold festivals honoring patron saints. Minority Protestant and Muslim populations also exist. An undercurrent of animism persists, including beliefs in supernatural beings such as the *tawong lipod*, *duwende*, *onglo*, *tambaluslos*, *kalag*, *katambay*, *aswang* and *mangkukulam*. The sources also mention strong beliefs concerning God, the soul and life after death.
