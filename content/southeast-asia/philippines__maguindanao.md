## Overview

The Maguindanaon are an Austronesian ethnic group from the Philippines and part of the wider Muslim political identity known as Moro. They are traditionally native to Maguindanao del Norte, Maguindanao del Sur, and the Soccsksargen region, where the historical Sultanates of Maguindanao and Buayan once stood. Their historical territories also included parts of Davao and the Zamboanga Peninsula. Maguindanaons live in urban areas throughout the Philippines and in overseas communities, including Malaysia, Singapore, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Europe, North America, Australia, and New Zealand. The name Maguindanao or Magindanaw means “people of the flood plains” or “people of the lake.” They speak Maguindanaon, an Austronesian language of the Danao language family, and are known for metalwork, visual art, gong music, dance, riddles, folktales, and epics.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources do mention ceremonial swords and armaments in connection with Maguindanao metalwork and the **Sagayan** dance, whose performers depict fierce warriors carrying shields with shell noisemakers and double-bladed swords.

### Architecture

The sources describe the historical Sultanates of Maguindanao and Buayan, including Cotabato as a capital and Datu Piang as the location of the Sultanate of Buayan in modern-day Maguindanao del Sur. They also mention *kota*, or forts, in the account of Spanish campaigns against Datu Uto.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Maguindanaons have historically been renowned as metalworkers. They produced wavy-bladed *keris* ceremonial swords and other weapons, as well as gongs. In the **Sagayan** dance, performers carry shields with shell noisemakers in one hand and a double-bladed sword in the other. The sources also mention a magical boat in the legend of Mamalu and Tabunaway, said to cross the river under the brothers’ command and carry goods between them.

### Jewelry & body adornment

In the folktale of Lagya Kudarat, a princess throws a ring and handkerchief to Kudarat after he accidentally causes a *sipa* to fall before her window.

## Music & performance

Maguindanao music includes the gong traditions of *kulintang* and *agung*. The sources specifically name **Binalig a Mamayog** and **Sinulog a Bagu** as examples of Maguindanao music.

Maguindanao literary performance includes sung verse and chanted epic. *Ida-ida a rata* are children’s rhymes sung in chorus, while *tubud-tubud* are short love poems. The *bayok* is a metaphorical lyric-poem form used when cautious or euphemistic expression is required. The sources provide examples of Maguindanao verses and translations, including poetic lines about a swift attempting to catch a hawk and about the difficulty of expressing love.

Maguindanao epics are chanted and antedate Islam, although elements of Islam were later incorporated into them. **Raja Indarapatra** contains characters with supernatural powers and includes the story of Raja Indarapatra and Raja Sulayman saving Mindanao from terrible creatures. **Raja Madaya** includes a narrative about Sultan Ditindegen, Princess Intan Tihaya, a dragon, and Raja Madaya.

## Dance & theatre

The **Sagayan** dance is associated with the Maguindanaons and depicts the steps of the hero Prince Bantugan as he wears his armaments, fights a war, and achieves victory. Performers represent fierce warriors. They carry shields fitted with shell noisemakers and double-bladed swords, using rolling movements in an attempt to defend their master. They do, however, describe epic chanting, sung children’s rhymes, lyric poems, riddling, and folktale narration as forms of literary and performed culture.

## Festivals & rituals

They identify Islam as the religion of most Maguindanaons and describe Arabic as the liturgical language of Islam among madrasah-educated Maguindanaons.

The legend of Mamalu and Tabunaway presents a foundational religious division between the brothers’ followers. When the missionary Sharif Kabungsuwan arrived, Tabunaway embraced Islam while Mamalu retained old beliefs. To maintain peace, Mamalu settled in the mountains and Tabunaway remained in the lowlands along the Pulangi. The story presents the lowland Muslims led by Tabunaway as the Maguindanaon and the highland animists of Mamalu as the Teduray.

## Foodways

They mention goods being exchanged between Mamalu and Tabunaway by means of a magical boat.

## Oral tradition & literature

Maguindanao literary culture includes folk speech and folk narratives. Folk speech is expressed through *antuka*, *pantuka*, or *paakenala* riddles, and through *bayok* lyric poems. Folk narratives include *tudtul* folktales and the epics **Raja Indarapatra**, **Darangen**, and **Raja Madaya**. Islamic literary traditions include the Quran, *tarsila* or genealogical narratives, the *Luwaran* customary laws, *hadith* sayings of Muhammad, and *quiza* religious stories.

Riddles promote friendship and serve as tools for basic pedagogy. Their structure consists of an image and a subject, with comparative, descriptive, punning or puzzle-like, and narrative images. Riddling takes place during group activity, including work or recreation. A person who volunteers as riddler must have a riddle ready or risk *dtapulung*, ridicule that forms part of the riddling tradition. Ambiguous answers may be settled by an old man or a respected person in the *barangay*.

The *tudtul* are short stories involving simple events. **The Lagya Kudarat** tells of Lagya Kudarat, Puteli Sittie Kumala, Sumedsen sa Alungan, marriage, separation, and reunion. **Pat-I-Mata** tells of two brothers, Pat-I-Mata and Datu sa Pulu, and ends with a curse connected to boats on the river.

## Language & religion

Maguindanaon is an Austronesian language spoken by the Maguindanaon people. It belongs to the Danao language family, whose related languages include Maranao and Iranun. The language has three major dialects named Ilud, Laya, and Biwangen; the sources also describe Kawanan and Suk varieties. Maguindanaon is spoken in Maguindanao del Norte and Maguindanao del Sur and in parts of Mindanao, including Zamboanga, Davao, General Santos, Cagayan de Oro, Cotabato, Sultan Kudarat, South Cotabato, Sarangani, Bukidnon, and Metro Manila.

Maguindanaon was the language of the Sultanate of Maguindanao. Thomas Forrest published a vocabulary of the “Magindano tongue” in 1779, and Jacinto Juanmartí published a Maguindanao–Spanish/Spanish–Maguindanao dictionary and reference grammar in 1892. The language is written in the Latin script and was formerly written in the Jawi script.

Most Maguindanaons are Muslim. Arabic is used among madrasah-educated Maguindanaons as the liturgical language of Islam. The sources also describe older beliefs in the legend of Mamalu and Tabunaway and identify highland animists associated with Mamalu as Teduray.
