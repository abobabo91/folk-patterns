## Overview

The T'boli (also written *Tboli*, *Tböli*, or historically *Tagabili*) are an Austronesian indigenous people of the highlands of South Cotabato in southern Mindanao, Philippines. Their heartland lies on the mountain slopes flanking the upper Alah Valley and around Lake Sebu, with coastal communities in Maitum, Maasim, and Kiamba in Sarangani province. Numbering roughly 150,000, they speak Tboli, a South Mindanao language within the Malayo-Polynesian branch of Austronesian. After World War II, settlers from the Visayas and Luzon displaced them from the fertile valley floor onto the surrounding highlands. Under Philippine law they are recognised as an indigenous people, and they are grouped politically with other non-Islamised, non-Christianised Mindanao peoples under the Cebuano umbrella term **Lumad** ("native"). In folk-culture terms they matter for one of Southeast Asia's most celebrated ikat traditions — *t'nalak* — alongside a distinctive lost-wax brass tradition and a dense material vocabulary of beadwork, bells, and combs.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

***T'nalak*** — A warp-ikat cloth woven from abaca (*Musa textilis*) fibre on a backstrap loom, dyed in a three-colour palette of undyed cream (the tied resist), red (from *loko* / *Morinda citrifolia* root), and black (from the leaves of the *k'nalum* tree). Designs are said to be revealed in dreams to the weaver by *Fu Dalu*, the spirit of abaca; the master weavers are known as ***dream weavers***, a title associated most famously with the late Lang Dulay of Lake Sebu, a National Living Treasure (Gawad sa Manlilikha ng Bayan). What distinguishes *t'nalak* from neighbouring Bagobo or Mandaya abaca ikats is the strict tricolour discipline, the taboo against cutting the cloth with metal, and the ritual burnishing of the finished panel with a cowrie shell over beeswax.

***T'boli brass casting*** — Personal ornaments, betel-nut boxes (*kafilan*), and bells cast by the lost-wax (*cire perdue*) method using clay-and-wax moulds and hand-pumped bellows. The tradition is closely linked to the neighbouring Maranao brass industry but retains T'boli-specific forms, especially waist-hung ***bells*** (*singkil*) sewn onto women's skirts and dance sashes.

***T'boli beadwork*** — Dense polychrome beaded collars, sashes, and headbands (*swat*) using imported glass seed beads combined with shell discs, coix seeds, and horsehair. The characteristic ground is deep-red with black, white, and yellow geometric fields.

***Kegal binsiwit*** — Blouse of tie-dyed abaca or cotton worn over the *t'nalak* tube skirt (*k'gal*), often edged with mother-of-pearl sequins and tiny bells.

***Sunggod*** / abaca weaving auxiliaries — Plain-weave abaca yardage and plaited mat work (*tudong*) that supply the everyday textile economy alongside the ritual *t'nalak*.

**Motif vocabulary.** *bulinglangit* (clouds), *g'mayaw* (mythical bird), *kabangi* (butterfly), *sawo* (python skin), *bangala* (man in a house), *tofi* (frog), *klung* (shield), *bangkiring* (hair bun / comb), *sofok mo-el* (mudfish scales), *kleng* (crab).

### Clothing & dress

Women's daily wear centres on the ***k'gal binsiwit***, a long-sleeved blouse of dyed abaca decorated with beads, sequins, and small brass bells, worn with the tubular ***t'nalak*** skirt wrapped and belted at the waist. Over this a woman may drape a heavy beaded collar (***nomong***) and hang multiple strands of *lieg* bead necklaces. The hair is coiled and dressed with the ***suwat***, an ornamental comb of carabao horn or wood inlaid with beads and horsehair. Brass leglets and anklets (***singkil***) with pellet bells are worn so that walking produces a continuous jingle — a marker of a woman of standing. Men wear a collarless jacket (***s'laong kinibang***) with a matching pair of tight trousers (***sawal***), a woven waist sash, and, for ceremony, a beaded headcloth. The great domed hat of plaited rattan and horsehair, ***s'laong kinibang***, is worn by both sexes as a sunshade in the fields and has become an emblematic T'boli object in itself.

### Architecture

The T'boli longhouse, ***gono bong*** ("big house"), is a raised timber-and-bamboo structure on hardwood posts about two metres above the ground, with a steeply pitched thatch roof of cogon grass and open gables. A single large multi-family room runs the length of the house; a raised platform at one end (***desyung***) serves as the seat of honour where the datu receives guests and where the loom is set up. Walls are of split bamboo or bark; floors are of springy bamboo slats. There are no interior partitions — sleeping compartments are defined by hanging *t'nalak* and mosquito nets. Rice granaries (*kumu*) stand on separate posts fitted with wooden disc rat-guards. Around Lake Sebu, houses are often built directly at the water's edge and reached by dugout canoe. Decoration is concentrated at the ridge finials and door lintels, carved with the same *g'mayaw* bird and *sawo* python motifs that appear on textile and brass.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The signature medium is cast brass, not clay. T'boli smiths produce the ***kafilan*** (long single-edged sword) and ***tok*** (dagger) with brass-inlaid hilts and carved horn scabbards; the ***s'ludoy***, a polychaeta-shaped areca-nut cutter; ornate ***betel-nut containers*** with hinged lids; and the small clustered pellet ***singkil*** bells sewn into ceremonial dress. Everyday utensils include plaited rattan winnowing trays (*tudong*), coconut-shell dippers, and carved wooden combs. Pottery is a minor tradition, mostly plain earthenware cooking pots obtained by trade with neighbouring lowland groups. Musical instruments — gongs, jew's harps, tube zithers — double as prestige objects and are inherited within families.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Women layer strands of glass and shell beads (***lieg***) at the neck, brass finger rings, and heavy earplugs of brass or shell (***bak***). The ***blonso***, a broad brass belt of linked plates hung with pellet bells, is worn at weddings and healing rites. Combs (***suwat***) inlaid with white beads and tufted with dyed horsehair function as both hair fastener and status marker. Filed and blackened teeth were traditional but are now rare. Tattooing (***bemble***) once marked the forearms and calves of both sexes with geometric bands echoing *t'nalak* motifs; the practice has largely lapsed but is being revived by younger craftspeople in Lake Sebu.

## Music & performance

The core T'boli ensemble is built on suspended, knobbed bronze gongs of the wider Mindanao ***agung*** family, played as a drone without a melodic gong-row (unlike the Maguindanaon *kulintang*). The five-piece ***klintang*** of small gongs in a row is played in some communities under Muslim influence, but the older, characteristically T'boli setup is the pair of large hanging *agung* struck with padded beaters. Melody is carried by the ***hegelung***, a two-string boat-shaped lute with frets of beeswax, plucked to accompany courtship songs and epic recitation. Other instruments include the ***kubing*** (bamboo jew's harp), ***sloli*** (lip-valley flute), ***s'ludoy*** (parallel-string bamboo tube zither, a women's instrument), ***tnonggong*** (goblet drum of deerskin), and the ***kumbing*** mouth harp used for private, near-inaudible messages between lovers. Song genres include the ***sudsungit***, a sung lament, and the ***tud-om***, a long chanted narrative sung by an elder at wakes and weddings. Neighbouring Maranao *kolintang* and the Maranao **Darangen** epic (UNESCO ICH RL/00159, inscribed 2008) are the closest regional analogues, but T'boli musical practice is quieter, more solo-vocal, and organised around the domestic space of the *gono bong* rather than a datu's court.

## Dance & theatre

T'boli dance is a mimetic tradition tied to animals and to the movement of the weaver at the loom. The ***madal tahaw***, or "bird dance," imitates the *tahaw* (tailorbird) with arms spread and slow crossing steps, performed by young women in full beaded dress. The ***madal be lawan*** is a warrior dance for men carrying the *kafilan* and a wooden shield (*klung*), stamping in time to the *agung*. The ***madal iwas*** ("monkey dance") is a comic piece for children. There is no masked or puppet-theatre tradition of the kind found in Muslim Mindanao or in Java; dramatic content is carried instead by the sung *tud-om* narratives. Dance is performed at weddings, at horse-fights (a distinctive T'boli spectacle in which stallions are set against one another over a mare in season), and at healing rites led by the female shaman (***tao mulung***).

## Festivals & rituals

The major public festival is the ***Helobung*** ("to forget one's cares"), held in Lake Sebu in mid-November around the founding anniversary of the municipality; it centres on horse-fights, *t'nalak* fashion parades, brass-casting demonstrations, and gong ensembles. In September the ***T'nalak Festival*** in Koronadal, capital of South Cotabato, celebrates the cloth as a provincial emblem with street dancing in *t'nalak*-patterned costume. Life-cycle rites are anchored to the *desyung* platform of the longhouse: at birth the umbilical cord is buried with a small brass bell; at marriage the groom's family delivers a bride-wealth (***sunggod***) that traditionally included a horse, a brass gong, a *kafilan*, and lengths of *t'nalak*, negotiated by a professional intermediary. Funerals involve a wake of several days with continuous *tud-om* chanting, after which the corpse was traditionally placed in a hollowed-log coffin and interred beneath or beside the house. Agricultural rites still observed by some families mark the clearing, planting, and harvest of upland rice, with offerings to *Fu Dalu* (spirit of abaca) and *D'wata*, the high creator god.

## Foodways

The staple is upland rice (***halay***) cultivated by swidden, complemented by maize, cassava, taro, and sweet potato. The signature everyday dish is ***kesiyu***, a stew of freshwater fish or chicken with ginger, chilli, and *kadyos* (pigeon pea), eaten with hand-pressed rice. Around Lake Sebu, tilapia introduced in the 1970s has become an economic mainstay, grilled over coals or cooked with coconut cream. ***Utan***, a mixed vegetable pot of squash tips, taro leaves, and string beans, is the daily side. Ceremonial food includes ***tinifu***, sticky rice steamed in banana leaves for weddings, and roasted native chicken presented on a brass tray at bride-wealth negotiations. Betel-nut chewing (***mama***) — areca nut with lime and *piper betle* leaf, carried in a hinged brass box — is the standard gesture of hospitality and social opening. Rice wine and sugarcane wine are drunk from a communal jar through bamboo straws at feasts. There are no Islamic dietary rules; pork is eaten freely, distinguishing T'boli food from that of the neighbouring Maguindanaon and Maranao.

## Oral tradition & literature

The great T'boli narrative form is the ***tud-om***, a long sung poem performed by an elder over several nights, ranging from cosmogonic accounts of *D'wata* creating the world through mythological journeys to genealogical recitations. Alongside it stands the ***owé***, a shorter song of personal lament or courtship. Prose folktales feature tricksters such as *Todbulol*, the epic hero whose adventures parallel those of the Maranao **Darangen** cycle but in a distinctively T'boli register centred on the loom, the horse, and the mountain. Proverbs (***lengon***) and riddles (***bat***) are exchanged as entertainment and as courtship testing. Since the founding of the School of Living Tradition in Lake Sebu by Lang Dulay in the 1990s, and its continuation under weavers such as Barbara Ofong and Sebulan Dulay, oral repertoire has been recorded alongside textile technique, and T'boli-language songbooks are now used in local schools under the Mother Tongue-Based Multilingual Education policy.

## Language & religion

Tboli belongs to the Bilic (South Mindanao) subgroup of the Malayo-Polynesian branch of Austronesian, closely related to Blaan and Tiruray. It has no indigenous script; it is now written in a Latin orthography developed by SIL missionaries in the mid-twentieth century, with an apostrophe marking the glottal stop and the schwa (e.g. *t'nalak*, *k'gal*). Most speakers are also fluent in Cebuano and Filipino, with Hiligaynon common in the Koronadal area. The traditional religion is polytheistic, with a high god *D'wata*, the abaca spirit *Fu Dalu*, and a large cast of nature and ancestor spirits mediated by the ***tao mulung*** (shaman-healer). Catholic and Protestant missions (notably the PMEC and SIL) are now demographically dominant, but ritual around weaving, horse-fighting, and healing preserves the older cosmology in syncretic form.
