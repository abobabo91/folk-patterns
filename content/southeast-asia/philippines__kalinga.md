## Overview

The Kalinga are an indigenous ethnic group whose ancestral domain is in the Cordillera Mountain Range of the northern Philippines. They are mainly found in Kalinga province, which has an area of 3,282.58 square kilometres, although some have migrated to Mountain Province, Apayao, Cagayan, and Abra. The Kalinga numbered 163,167 in 2010. Their territory includes the floodplains of Tabuk and Rizal and the Chico River. Kalinga is a dialect continuum spoken by the Kalinga people alongside Ilocano. Kalinga social life includes kinship systems, economic classes, peace pacts called *Bodong*, distinctive tattooing known as *batok* or *batek*, and named agricultural, architectural, clothing, dance, and ritual traditions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Kalinga tattoo designs on textiles** — The named designs include animal, plant, agricultural, geometric, and weapon forms. The sources state that each design has symbolic meanings or magical and talismanic abilities.

**Motif vocabulary.** *gayaman*, centipedes; *tiniktiku*, centipede legs; *tabwhad*, snakes; *tinulipao*, snakeskin; *chillag*, hexagonal shapes representing snake belly scales; *inong-oo*, coiled snakes; *inud-uchan*, rain; *inam-am*, *inalapat*, and *nilawhat*, fern designs; *binunga*, fruits; *chuyos*, parallel lines; *sinagkikao*, alternating lines; *tinatalaaw*, hourglass shapes representing day and night; *lusong*, rice mortars; *tibul*, a pig’s hind legs; *sinwhuto* or *panyat*, rice bundles; *sina-sao*, criss-crossing designs; *inar-archan*, ladders; *tulayan*, eagles; *tokak*, frogs; and *sinawit*, axe blades.

### Clothing & dress

Kalinga men wear *ba-ag*, or loincloths. Women wear *saya*, a colourful garment covering the body from the waist down to the feet. Women are tattooed on their arms up to their shoulders and wear colourful ornaments, including bracelets, earrings, and necklaces, especially during festivities.

### Architecture

Kalinga houses are called *furoy*, *buloy*, *fuloy*, *phoyoy*, or *biloy*. They are either octagonal, in the case of wealthy households, or square, and are elevated on posts. Some posts reach 20–30 feet. The houses have a single room. Other named buildings include *alang*, or granaries, and *sigay*, or field sheds.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Heirlooms include Chinese plates called *panay*, jars called *gosi*, and gongs called *gangsa*. The sources also name a rattan backpack, *pasiking*, and a betel nut bag, *buyo*.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Kalinga women wear colourful bracelets, earrings, and necklaces, particularly during festivities. Kalinga tattoos are known as *batok* or *batek*, and as *whatok* in Butbut Kalinga. The tradition is associated with Apo Whang-od, described as once being known as the “last mambabatok,” and with her teaching younger artists to continue the tradition.

## Music & performance

The sources name the gong *gangsa* as a Kalinga heirloom.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name two Kalinga dances: *salidsid*, a courtship dance, and *pala-ok* or *pattong*, a war dance.

## Festivals & rituals

They describe several life-cycle and ritual practices. Betrothals are common, including betrothals made as early as birth, although an engagement may be broken if a person does not favour it. After death, sacrifices are made in honour of the spirit of the dead, and *kolias* is celebrated after one year of mourning. The *kontad* or *kontid* is a ritual performed for a child to avoid future harms. *Ngilin* involves avoiding the evil water spirit. Pregnant women and their husbands observe dietary and environmental restrictions, including avoiding beef, cow’s milk, dog meat, streams, and waterfalls because these are said to cause harm to unborn children.

The Kalinga also developed *Bodong*, an institution of peace pacts that reduces traditional warfare and headhunting and serves to initiate, maintain, renew, and reinforce kinship and social ties.

## Foodways

Traditional agriculture includes wet-rice cultivation, called *papayaw*, and swidden farming, called *uwa*. The availability of water permits two planting seasons in the rice terraces. The sources name three rice varieties: *onoy*, *oyak*, and *dikit* or *diket*. Fruit trees, including coconut, coffee, and banana, are grown in orchards called *kakkaju*. Wine called *basi* is made from sugarcane.

Men hunt wild pigs, deer, and wild fowl in the forests. Fish, shells, and other marine life are caught in rivers, streams, and lakes. Robusta coffee became a popular cash crop in the 1970s; production declined after a trader monopoly in the 1980s caused low farmgate prices, and government agencies encouraged renewed planting and harvesting of Cordillera Robusta coffee in the 2010s.

The sources describe dietary prohibitions connected with pregnancy.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that *papangat* leaders are expected to be wise and to possess good oratorical ability. The Kalinga language article lists documented works including *Upper Tanudan Kalinga texts*, *Guinaang Kalinga texts*, and a *Minangali (Kalinga) Digital Wordlist: Presentation Form*, but the sources do not summarize their contents.

## Language & religion

Kalinga is a dialect continuum of Kalinga Province spoken by the Kalinga people alongside Ilocano. Ronald Himes divides Kalinga into Masadiit, Northern Kalinga, and South-Central Kalinga. The sources also identify Butbut Kalinga, Limos Kalinga, Lubuagan Kalinga, Mabaka Valley Kalinga, Majukayang Kalinga, Southern Kalinga, Tanudan Kalinga, and Banao Itneg, with named dialects and locations.

Kalinga belief includes a Supreme Being called *Kabuniyan*, described as the creator and giver of life and as having once lived among the Kalinga. Other spirits and deities include *pinaing* and *aran*, associated with nature, and *kakarading* and *anani*, associated with dead ancestors. A priestess called *manganito*, *mandadawak*, or *mangalisig* communicates with these spirits.
