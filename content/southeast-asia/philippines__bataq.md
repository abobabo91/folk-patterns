## Overview

The Batak are one of about 140 indigenous peoples of the Philippines. They live in the northeastern portions of Palawan, a relatively large island in the southwest of the archipelago, and have inhabited river valleys along the coastline of what is today Puerto Princesa City since ancient times. According to the 2020 census, about 1,500 Batak remain. They are also called Tinitianes and are considered by anthropologists to be closely related to the Aeta of Central Luzon, another Negrito tribe. Their folk-culture significance in the sources lies principally in their hunting-gathering economy, forest knowledge, animist practices, offerings to spirits, and sustainable collection of forest products.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources mention clothing materials only as goods received by the Batak through trade.

### Architecture

They state that the Batak, once nomadic, have settled in small villages and that government efforts in the 1930s attempted to establish reservations in the coastal plains.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention bows and arrows, spears, dogs, and homemade guns as means of capturing animals, and clothing materials, rice, and other goods as items received through trade.

## Music & performance

They state that shamans undergo spiritual possession in order to communicate with spirits and heal the sick, but provide no musical details about these practices.

## Festivals & rituals

The Batak belief system is described as animism, with spirits residing in nature. They classify spirits into the *Panya'en*, who are malevolent, and the *Diwata*, who are generally benevolent but also capricious. Batak make regular offerings to these spirits, and shamans undergo spiritual possession to communicate with them and heal the sick.

Gathering trips into the forest can last several days and have both economic and spiritual value.

## Foodways

Batak subsistence combines foraging, selling forest-derived goods, shifting cultivation, wage labor, and work for Filipino farmers or other employers. Their principal food sources include squirrels, jungle fowl, wild pigs, honey, fruits, yams, fish, mollusks, and crustaceans. These foods mainly come from the forests of the region. The Batak use bows and arrows, spears, dogs, and homemade guns to capture animals.

A few Batak cultivate rice, corn, sweet potato, and cassava. The sources also state that Batak men may work for nearby farmers by clearing weeds, harvesting, or picking coconuts and coffee. Through trade, the Batak have sold rattan, honey, and Manila copal and received clothing materials, rice, and other goods.

## Language & religion

They describe the Batak religious system as animism: spirits reside in nature, and the *Panya'en* are malevolent while the *Diwata* are generally benevolent but capricious. Regular offerings are made to these spirits. Shamans undergo spiritual possession in order to communicate with spirits and heal the sick.

The sources state that rapid depopulation, restricted forest access, sedentary living, and immigration have caused severe cultural disruption. Very few Batak now marry other Batak, and children of marriages with neighboring groups often do not follow Batak cultural ways. The sources describe the Batak as being absorbed into a more diffuse group of upland indigenous peoples, with the loss of tribal identities, spirituality, and culture debated alongside the question of whether they continue to exist as a distinct ethnic entity.
