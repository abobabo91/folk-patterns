## Overview

The Bajo, or Sama-Bajau, are several closely related Austronesian ethnic groups of Maritime Southeast Asia. In Indonesia, Indonesian Bajau communities are widely distributed throughout Sulawesi and Nusa Tenggara and are also found in Maluku Utara Province, including the Bacan Islands, Obi Islands, Kayoa, and Sula Islands. The Indonesian Bajau language is spoken mainly in Sulawesi, while related Sama–Bajaw languages are spoken in the Philippines, Indonesia, and Malaysia. Sama-Bajau communities have historically maintained seaborne lifestyles based on trading and subsistence fishing, using small wooden vessels and, in some areas, houses built over coastal shallows. Their cultural significance in folk life lies especially in maritime mobility, boat dwelling, fishing, oral traditions, and the linguistic diversity of their subgroups.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Sama-Bajau architecture is associated in the sources with houses built on stilts in coastal shallows and, historically, with houses built over the sea. Some boat-dwelling Sama-Bajau lived in *lepa*, handmade boats that also served as homes. In the Philippines, Sama Bihing or Sama Lipid traditionally lived in stilt houses in shallows and coastal areas, while Sama Dea, Sama Deya, Sama Dilaya, or Sama Darat traditionally lived in island interiors. The sources also state that many Sama-Bajau later abandoned boat living for Sama-style piling houses in coastal shallows. No specific roof forms, decorative systems, or urban architectural traditions are documented.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do identify wooden maritime objects and vessels, including the *perahu*, *djenging*, *lepa*, *vinta*, *jungkung*, *timbawan*, *biduk*, and *bogo-katik*. The *lepa* is described as a handmade boat in which many boat-dwelling Sama-Bajau lived. Other named livelihood objects include fishing boats and fishing gear distributed through livelihood programs. Traditional economic activities also include mat-weaving, cassava processing, pearl-related jewellery-making, and the use of fish-aggregating devices as an alternative to explosives.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They mention jewellery-making, especially from pearls, as a traditional source of income among some Sama-Bajau refugee groups in the Philippines.

## Music & performance

The Bajo language article states that the lyrics of *Kiriring Pakiriring*, popularly known as *Dayang Dayang*, were written in the Simunul dialect of Southern Sinama. It also records that Central Sinama and Southern Sinama were among the languages used in the 2012 Filipino drama film *Thy Womb*. These references document linguistic and cultural representation.

## Festivals & rituals

They do state that even boat-dwelling Sama-Bajau continue to practise agricultural rituals despite being exclusively fishermen. The sources also describe oral traditions concerning royal ancestors, lost princesses, migration, and the formation of subgroups. In one account among the Bajau Kubang of Semporna, the place called “Kubang” is associated with assembling or gathering. No calendar dates or detailed ritual procedures are supplied for these practices.

## Foodways

They describe subsistence fishing and the historical exchange of fish for farm goods. Sama Dea, Sama Deya, Sama Dilaya, or Sama Darat communities are described as cultivating rice, sweet potato, cassava, and coconuts for copra through traditional slash-and-burn agriculture. Other sources of income include selling grated cassava, known as *magliis*. The Sama-Bajau also historically traded sea products such as trepang, pearls, and shark fin.

## Oral tradition & literature

Sama-Bajau oral traditions and *tarsila*, or royal genealogies, commonly describe an original land-dwelling population whose subjects were ordered to find a lost royal daughter. After failing, they supposedly remained nomadic to avoid the king’s anger. A Borneo tradition describes Johorean royal guards escorting a princess named Dayang Ayesha toward Sulu, while another account among the Bajau Kubang of Semporna names the siblings Haklum Nuzum and Salingayah Bungsu and connects their migration with Sulu, Johor, “Sambuanga” or Zamboanga, Omaral or Omadal Island, and Bum-Bum Island.

Indonesian Sama-Bajau oral histories place greater importance on the Sultanate of Gowa. Other Philippine traditions say that the Sama-Bajau were accidentally towed into what is now Zamboanga by a giant stingray. The epic poem *Darangen* records that an ancestor of the hero Bantugan was a Maranao prince who married a Sama-Bajau princess.

## Language & religion

The Sama–Bajaw languages form a group of Austronesian languages spoken by Sama-Bajau peoples in the Philippines, Indonesia, and Malaysia. Indonesian Bajau is spoken mainly in Sulawesi, and related varieties are distributed through Sulawesi, Nusa Tenggara, Maluku Utara, and other areas. The sources list Indonesian Bajau, West Coast Bajau, Balangingi, Central Sama, Southern Sama, Pangutaran Sama, Mapun, Yakan, and Abaknon among the languages or varieties associated with the group. Several dialects are identified, and communities often classify their language according to the village or island where it originates.

The sources state that many Sama-Bajau are Muslim, while some communities historically practised animist folk religions either exclusively or alongside Islam. They also describe cultural and religious relationships with the Tausūg and the Moro peoples. No historical script or detailed account of Bajo religious practice is provided.
