## Overview

The Khmer are Cambodia's dominant people, descendants of the great Angkor Empire (802–1431). Their village life centers on the Buddhist temple (wat), and they maintain some of Southeast Asia's most elaborate court traditions: classical dance, shadow theatre, and silk weaving. Khmer identity extends beyond Cambodia to ethnic communities in Thailand and Vietnam, united by language, religion, and a distinctive material culture rooted in Angkorian aesthetics.

## Material culture

Khmer material culture draws its vocabulary from Angkorian stone carvings—temple towers, apsaras, nagas, and celestial beings—translated across textiles, metalwork, jewelry, and architecture.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Khmer silk weaving, especially the ikat technique called hol, uses a distinctive golden silk and uneven twill that softens motif edges.

- **Hol ikat** (*hol*) — The signature Khmer weft ikat using an uneven three-warp/one-weft twill that creates a diagonal sheen and soft motif edges, distinguishing it from neighboring Lao and Cham ikats.
- **Sampot hol skirt** (*sampot hol*) — A tubular wrap skirt woven in hol technique, worn daily and ceremonially, in five traditional colour grounds (red, yellow, green, blue, black) with reserved silk motifs.
- **Gold-thread brocade skirt** (*sampot chorabap*) — A supplementary-weft brocade with gold or silver-wrapped thread on silk, historically reserved for royalty and court dancers, creating dense jewel-like patterns.
- **Pictorial ikat hanging** (*pidan*) — A narrative weft-ikat hanging hung behind Buddha images in temples or over wedding beds, depicting Jataka scenes, Buddha's life, Angkor towers, and celestial beings.
- **Checked cotton scarf** (*krama*) — A ubiquitous gingham-checked scarf in red-and-white or blue-and-white, worn as headwrap, sash, or child-sling by men and women as a marker of Khmer daily identity.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress centers on the wrapped lower garment (sampot) in cotton or silk, paired with scarves and draped shoulder-cloths.

- **Wrapped skirt** (*sampot*) — A rectangular cloth wrapped and tucked at the waist, worn by women in plain cotton daily and in hol silk for temple and ceremonial wear.
- **Legged wrap** (*sampot chang kben*) — A longer cloth passed between the legs and tucked at the back, historically unisex court dress still worn by men and women for weddings and ordinations.
- **Diagonal shoulder-cloth** (*sbai*) — A shoulder-cloth draped diagonally across the torso for formal occasions, also used in wedding reenactments where the groom holds its hem.
- **Embroidered blouse** (*av pak*) — An embroidered upper garment worn with wrapped skirts for everyday and special occasions.
- **Dancer's crown** (*mkot*) — A towering crown worn by classical dancers and worn by brides, modelled on the finials of Angkorian temple towers.

### Architecture

Traditional raised timber houses with thatched roofs contrast sharply with the monumental terraced temple-mountains (prasat) of the Angkorian tradition.

- **Raised house** (*ptas khmae*) — A timber-framed structure raised two to three metres on hardwood posts, with palm-thatch or tile roof and shuttered windows, keeping the living floor above monsoon floods.
- **Temple-mountain** (*prasat*) — Monumental structures built of laterite and sandstone, terraced to represent Mount Meru, encrusted with relief programmes depicting celestial and narrative scenes (Angkor Wat, the Bayon, Banteay Srei, Preah Vihear).
- **Buddhist temple compound** (*wat*) — A sacred complex present in every village, housing an ordination hall (vihear), open pavilion (sala), monks' quarters (kuti), and often a stupa (chedi) for ashes.
- **Roof finial** (*andong reach*) — A distinctive gable finial evoking curled nagas or flame motifs of temple roofs.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Historic kiln traditions produced glazed stonewares; rural potters still hand-build vessels, while silversmiths craft repoussé ceremonial objects.

- **Ancient glazed ware** (*Kulen and Angkorian kilns*) — 9th–13th century green-glazed and brown-glazed stonewares including zoomorphic vessels (owl jars, elephant jars) that circulated across the Khmer Empire.
- **Hand-built pots** (*Kampong Chhnang earthenware*) — Utilitarian round-bottomed cooking pots and water jars hand-built without a wheel using paddle-and-anvil technique in the village of Kampong Chhnang.
- **Repoussé vessels** (*Kampong Luong silver*) — Raised silver betel-boxes, offering bowls, and animal-form containers (peacocks, elephants, tortoises) used in weddings and temple offerings.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Khmer jewelry draws from Angkorian relief vocabulary in gold and filigree, while sacred tattooing with yantra and script confers spiritual protection.

- **Pectoral collar** (*sangvar*) — A gold collar drawing on Angkorian relief vocabulary, worn for formal occasions.
- **Sacred tattoo** (*sak yoan*) — Geometric and figured tattoos applied by monks or healers using yantra diagrams, Pali syllables in old Khmer script, and protective figures (hanuman, tigers, nagas).
- **Protective amulet** (*katha*) — Amulets of Buddha images, temple soil, or inscribed metal foil worn on cords around the neck or waist for spiritual protection.

## Music & performance

Three principal ensembles—the gong-chime pinpeat for ritual, the softer mahori for entertainment, and the phleng kar for weddings—anchor Khmer music.

- **Temple-court gong ensemble** (*pinpeat*) — A gong-chime ensemble with xylophones (roneat ek, roneat thung), gong-chimes (kong thom, kong toch), oboe (sralai), barrel drum (sampho), and large drums (skor thom), accompanying classical dance, shadow theatre, and ritual.
- **String-and-voice ensemble** (*mahori*) — A softer ensemble featuring bowed spike fiddles (tro sau, tro ou), long-necked lute (chapey dong veng), hammered dulcimer (khim), and bamboo flute (khloy).
- **Wedding repertoire** (*phleng kar*) — A specific repertoire of some ninety pieces played across the multi-day wedding ceremony, using the full range of Khmer instruments.
- **Solo verse tradition** (*chapey dong veng*) — A male singer with a long-necked lute improvises satirical and moralising verse, inscribed on UNESCO's Urgent Safeguarding list.

## Dance & theatre

Classical court dance codifies thousands of gestures, while shadow theatre uses large pierced-leather panels and masked dance-dramas petition spirits for agricultural fertility.

- **Royal Ballet of Cambodia** — A codified court tradition of 4,500 gestures performed almost exclusively by women in gilt costume and mkot crowns, dramatising the Reamker and Jataka episodes (UNESCO ICH, RL/00060).
- **Large-hide shadow theatre** (*sbek thom*) — Two-metre pierced-leather panels depicting entire scenes, manipulated by dancers before a fire-lit screen while a pinpeat plays and a narrator chants the Reamker (UNESCO ICH, RL/00108).
- **Articulated shadow puppets** (*sbek touch*) — A smaller cousin of sbek thom using jointed puppets rather than large single-scene panels.
- **Masked dance-drama for rain** (*Lkhon Khol Wat Svay Andet*) — An all-male masked dance-drama performed annually at a Mekong temple to petition neak ta spirits for rain and good harvest (UNESCO ICH, USL/01374).

## Festivals & rituals

The lunisolar Buddhist calendar structures the festival year around New Year, water reversal, ancestor veneration, and agricultural cycles.

- **Khmer New Year** (*Chol Chhnam Thmey*) — Marked around 13–16 April, ending the harvest with sand-stupa building at the wat, water-throwing, and games (chol chhoung, leak kanseng, bos angkunh).
- **Buddha's triple commemoration** (*Visak Bochea*) — A May festival commemorating the Buddha's birth, enlightenment, and parinirvana with temple observances and offerings.
- **Ancestor veneration festival** (*Pchum Ben*) — A fifteen-day festival in September–October when families visit seven pagodas to offer rice-balls (bay ben) to the wandering spirits of ancestors released from the lower realms.
- **Water Festival** (*Bon Om Touk*) — Celebrated in November with three days of pirogue racing on the Tonle Sap to celebrate the river's current reversal.
- **Battlefield martial art** (*Bokator*) — A Khmer martial art with animal-derived stances, traced to Angkorian bas-reliefs, displayed at festivals and now formally taught (UNESCO ICH, RL/01868).

## Foodways

Rice and freshwater fish form the base; fermented mudfish paste (prahok) and an aromatic paste (kroeung) are identity markers distinguishing Khmer cuisine.

- **Fermented fish paste** (*prahok*) — A fermented mudfish paste whose pungency underpins soups, dips (prahok ktis), and marinades, marking Khmer identity against Thai and Vietnamese neighbours.
- **Aromatic paste base** (*kroeung*) — A pounded paste of lemongrass, galangal, kaffir lime, turmeric, garlic, shallot, and (in red versions) dried chilli, used in curries and stews.
- **Fish curry in banana leaf** (*amok trey*) — Fish steamed in banana-leaf cups with kroeung and coconut cream, often called the national dish.
- **Tamarind soup** (*samlor machu*) — A sour soup featuring tamarind as the primary sour element, served with rice and vegetable accompaniments.
- **Fish-and-noodle breakfast** (*nom banh chok*) — Rice noodles under a fish-and-kroeung gravy, considered the classic breakfast dish.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Reamker (Khmer Ramayana) and Jataka tales are the foundational narratives, supplemented by folktales, origin myths, and didactic verse codes.

- **Khmer Ramayana** (*Reamker*) — The pre-eminent narrative whose episodes furnish plots for classical dance, shadow theatre, and temple relief; Angkor Wat's outer galleries carry a nearly complete Reamker cycle in stone.
- **Buddha's previous lives** (*Jātaka tales*) — The second great narrative pool, painted on wat walls and recited by monks, illustrating moral lessons through the Buddha's incarnations.
- **Trickster folktales** (*Judge Rabbit*) — Stories of Sophea Tonsai (Judge Rabbit) as moral fables, representing an indigenous folktale cycle of common origin across Southeast Asia.
- **Origin myth of the Khmer people** (*Preah Thong and Neang Neak*) — The tale of a merchant-prince and a naga princess whose union founded the Khmer, reenacted at every wedding when the groom holds the hem of the bride's sbai.
- **Tragic love epic** (*Tum Teav*) — A verse epic from the 17th–19th centuries depicting a tragic love story, considered the national literary work.

## Language & religion

Khmer, an Austroasiatic language, is written in one of Southeast Asia's oldest continuously used scripts; Theravada Buddhism blends with indigenous ancestor and land-spirit worship.

- **Austroasiatic language family** (*Khmer*) — The language, related to Mon and Vietnamese, with major dialects (Central, Northern Khmer Surin, Southern Khmer Krom, Western Cardamom) and an ancient script developed from Pallava Grantha by the 7th century.
- **Sacred script variant** (*aksar khom*) — The older sacred script used for Pali texts and yantra tattoos, preserving pre-modern orthography.
- **Guardian spirits of place** (*neak ta*) — Indigenous land-spirit guardians receiving regular offerings alongside the Buddha in syncretic Khmer Buddhist practice.
- **Ancestor spirits** (*meba*) — Ancestral spirits venerated in Khmer household and temple ritual, particularly during Pchum Ben festival.
- **Ritual healer-astrologer** (*kru khmae*) — Traditional healers and astrologers who mediate the supernatural world through herbal medicine, tattoo application, and divination.
