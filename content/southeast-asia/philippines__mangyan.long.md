## Overview

Mangyan is the collective name for eight indigenous groups of Mindoro island in the Philippines, each with its own tribal name, language and customs: from north to south the Iraya, Alangan, Tadyawan, Tawbuid (called Batangan by lowlanders on the island's west side), Buhid and Hanunoo, with the Ratagnon at the southernmost tip and the Bangon on the east, who speak the western dialect of Tawbuid. The total population may be around 280,001, though official figures are hard to establish given remote terrain and groups with little outside contact. Their languages belong to the Austronesian family and are mutually unintelligible, sharing on average only 40% of their vocabulary. Once the only inhabitants of Mindoro and originally coastal dwellers, they moved inland and upland to avoid Tagalog and Spanish settlement and Moro raids, and now live along highland rivers, descending periodically to trade. In folk-culture terms they matter for a living syllabic script, a sustained poetic form, and indigo-dyed embroidered textiles.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources describe Mangyan textile work only in outline, and name one design. The peoples of southern Mindoro were, in the pre-Hispanic era, exceptional in weaving, pottery and writing; the northern groups are described as simpler in their way of living. Terms and materials differ from tribe to tribe, and the most exceptional designs come from the Hanunó'o.

**Indigo-dyed cloth with *pakudos*** — Hanunó'o textiles are dyed indigo blue and carry an embroidery design called *pakudos* placed at the back of the garment. The same design also appears on their woven bags.

Plaited and basketry work is documented through the objects themselves rather than by tradition name: a rice-harvesting basket of *buri* palm leaf with cane and bark, containers and baskets of split cane, a hexagonal plaited basket lid of vegetable fibre, a shoulder bag combining *buri* palm and *nitu* vine, a rattan winnowing tray, a *buri* palm sleeping mat, and a rattan hammock. Belts are woven rather than cut and sewn: a woman's belt of vine (*salatid tag ina*) woven in a continuous round, and a woven vegetable-fibre money belt (*apdus*) made with a double layer so that the weave itself forms the pocket.

### Clothing & dress

Dress differs by gender. Men wear the *bahag*, a loincloth covering the lower body; women wear a skirt with a shirt above. The museum objects here follow that division: a woman's skirt and a woman's blouse, both of textile identified as probably cotton, together with a man's head cloth of the same probable material. Also collected are an embroidered white jacket, a woman's band worn on the torso made of vegetable fibre, and the two woven belts described above — the vine belt *salatid tag ina* and the double-layered money belt *apdus*, whose pocket held cash. The sources do not distinguish ceremonial from everyday dress, and name no footwear. What they do emphasise is that garment terms and materials vary between the eight groups, with the finest designs — the indigo grounds and the *pakudos* embroidery worked at the back — belonging to the Hanunó'o of southern Mindoro.

### Architecture

The sources used do not describe Mangyan house forms, materials, roofing or architectural ornament. They note only that Mangyan communities live in relative seclusion along rivers in the highlands of Mindoro, and that Mangyan ancestral lands amount to a claim over 40,000 hectares of forest, of which titled portions include 3,270.78 hectares in Sabang in Pinamalayan town and Buong Lupa in Gloria town.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery is named as one of the three crafts in which pre-Hispanic southern Mindoro excelled, and clay appears in a smoking-pipe bowl (*Kwaku*). Bamboo carries much of the household repertoire: a lime container incised with geometric designs — collected with a jew's harp stored inside it — and a bamboo-and-fibre lid for a container called *sukluban bangon*. Coconut shell is worked into a food bowl and into a bowl serving as a rice measure. Other objects include a gourd lime holder with vegetable fibre, a *buri* palm leaf lid, a torch or pitch candle of pitch, leaf and rattan, and a pouch-cum-pocket-belt of vegetable fibre, wood and cord. Tools and weapons cover a wooden-and-cane knife sheath, a woman's digging knife of wood and metal, a wooden axe handle, a harpoon spear of metal, wood, cane and twine, a bundle of ten bamboo hunting spikes still bearing adhering earth, and a bamboo quiver. Two items are improvised from modern materials: a fishing spear so thin it is shot through the barrel of a ballpoint pen, drawn by a rubber band knotched at the butt, and fishing goggles (*salamin*) whose glass lenses are waxed into wooden frames and held by a red rubber head band with nylon twine.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The ornaments recorded here are of beads, plant fibre and leaf rather than metal. A necklace called *siwayang* is strung from blue, yellow and orange glass beads. A man's armband is made of vegetable fibre, and a pair of men's ear pendants of beads and cotton thread. A neck-ring combines vegetable fibre with beads. A woman's pair of ear plugs is made of leaf. The sources record no tattooing, hair practice or ritual function for any of these pieces.

## Music & performance

The instruments collected from the Mangyan are a small, portable set, several of them made of bamboo and two of them strung with human hair. A **jew's harp** of wood and resin is documented, and one such harp was kept inside an incised bamboo lime container — the instrument travelling with the betel kit. There is a bamboo **flute**. A **violin** is made of wood and human hair, played with a bow of bamboo and human hair. A **guitar** of wood is also recorded. One further bamboo object is catalogued ambiguously as a "dibble clapper", uncertain between digging implement and percussion.

Vocal performance is documented through poetry rather than song collections. The Hanunó'o practise *ambahan*, a rhythmic poetic expression with a meter of seven syllables, presented through recitation and chanting or inscribed on bamboo — so that the same composition exists as sound and as incised text. The broader Mangyan repertoire includes song poems that carry taboos, beliefs and customs, and that account for the origins of those customs and the importance of keeping them. Riddles were told in the evening as mind games and comical entertainment. The sources place storytelling after sunset, since telling folk tales in daylight was believed to rot the teeth.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used do not describe Mangyan dance or dramatic traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

Ritual life in the sources is agricultural and tied to rice. Rice cultivation is accompanied by specific rites at every phase, because the rice spirits (*kalag paray*) must be appeased to secure a harvest. Their caretaker is *Binayo*, a sacred female spirit, married to *Bulungabon*. The named rites are the *panudlak*, the rite of first planting; the rite of rice planting itself; and the harvest rites, consisting of the *magbugkos*, the binding of rice stalks, and the *pamag-uhan*, which follows the harvest.

The wider spiritual system names *Mahal na Makaako*, the Supreme Being who gave life to all human beings merely by gazing at them, and *Binayi*, owner of a garden where all spirits rest. *Bulungabon* is aided by twelve fierce dogs that chase erring souls and drown them in a cauldron of boiling water.

Two dates matter in the recent political calendar of Mangyan land rights. In December 2010 the Hanunuo, Gubatnon and Ratagnon (Hagura) Mangyan of Occidental Mindoro received a certificate of ancestral domain title from President Benigno Aquino III. In July 2022 the Tadyawan and Tau-buid became the first indigenous communities in Oriental Mindoro to receive their CADT from the National Commission on Indigenous Peoples. Mangyan ancestral lands have been subject to militarization and land-grabbing, and Mangyan communities to violent attacks, arrests and other human rights violations. The sources describe no annual festival cycle and no birth, marriage or funeral rites; they note only that burial caves hold porcelain and pottery from pre-Hispanic Chinese trade.

## Foodways

The Mangyan are mainly subsistence agriculturalists. They plant a variety of sweet potato, upland (dry cultivation) rice and taro, and they trap small animals and wild pig; farming, fruit gathering and hunting are their sustenance. Those living in close contact with lowland Filipinos sell cash crops, specifically bananas and ginger. Rice is differentiated enough to have its own body of knowledge: among the stories the Mangyan told about daily life were accounts describing the varieties and characteristics of the rice they cultivated, alongside stories about the medicines they used.

The collected objects fill in the handling of food rather than its preparation. A *buri* palm leaf and cane rice-harvesting basket, a rattan winnowing tray and a coconut-shell bowl used as a rice measure cover harvest through portioning; a second coconut-shell bowl serves as a food bowl. Betel chewing is attested by two lime containers, one of incised bamboo and one of gourd with vegetable fibre, and tobacco by a clay pipe bowl (*Kwaku*). The sources name no cooked dish, no ceremonial food and no drink tradition.

## Oral tradition & literature

Mangyan stories were historically transmitted orally, with a selected body preserved as poems written in syllabic script and carved into materials such as bamboo with a sharp object. The categories have no English equivalents and are known by descriptive labels. **Song poems** and **folk beliefs and customs** carry taboos and explain the origins of practices. **Riddles** served as evening entertainment. Folk tales divide into three kinds: *suyot*, the oldest of the oral traditions, comprising legend, folklore and fable and tellable only after sunset; *sugklanon*, acquired through Bisayan influence; and *tultulanon*, accounts of historical events. The Hanunó'o *ambahan*, in its seven-syllable meter, is both chanted and incised. Three of the objects here are exactly that literature as artefact: two bamboo tubes bearing verse inscriptions, catalogued as documents, and a bamboo sliver with incised characters registered in 1948. Most present understanding of Mangyan custom derives from these oral and written accounts.

## Language & religion

Mangyan languages are Austronesian, mutually unintelligible, and share on average 40% of their vocabulary. Tawbuid and Buhid are closely related and unusual among Philippine languages in having an /f/ phoneme; Tawbuid divides into eastern and western dialects, and Western Tawbuid may be the only Philippine language with no glottal phonemes, lacking both /h/ and /ʔ/. Lowlanders are called *damuong*. The groups write with Hanunó'o script; the system, *surat Mangyan*, is a pre-Hispanic syllabary of Indic origin, still practised and still taught in Mangyan schools in Oriental Mindoro. The Hanunó'o take their name from a word meaning "true", "pure" or "genuine", asserting strictness in preserving ancestral practice. The traditional worldview is primarily animistic; around 10% are Christian, Catholic and Evangelical Protestant, and the New Testament has been published in six Mangyan languages.
