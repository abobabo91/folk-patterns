## Overview

The Kayeli people, also known as *Orang Kayeli*, are an ethnic group mainly living on the southern coast of the Kayeli Gulf on the Indonesian island of Buru. They are concentrated in the northeastern part of the island, particularly along the gulf and in the valley of the Apo River, which flows into it. The Kayeli formed during Dutch colonization through the relocation of indigenous inhabitants from other parts of Buru and the development of thirteen villages around a Dutch fort. Their population declined rapidly during the twentieth century; the sources give approximately 800 people in the early twenty-first century and also a total of about 600. Kayeli was an Austronesian language, but it is now extinct. The group is predominantly Sunni Muslim, with remnants of pre-Islamic pagan beliefs.

## Material culture

### Architecture

In 1658, the Dutch built the first permanent settlement and a military fort on the southern coast of the Kayeli Gulf. The fort became the administrative center of Buru for two centuries, and approximately thirteen villages were built around it after indigenous inhabitants were forcibly relocated to the area.

## Festivals & rituals

The Kayeli are predominantly Sunni Muslim, and the Dutch East India Company administrators signed a contract with tribal leaders guaranteeing their right to practise their Islamic religion. The sources also mention remnants of pre-Islamic pagan belief systems, to a lesser extent than among other ethnic peoples of Buru.

## Foodways

During the first two centuries of Dutch colonial rule, the absolute majority of the Kayeli people were exploited on carnation plantations. After carnation ceased to be the main agricultural crop of Buru, economic activity diversified. Much of the population cultivated sago palm for the production of sago and cultivated Eucalyptus for aromatic oil; fisheries also became significant.

## Oral tradition & literature

A 1983 literature review indicated approximately 1,000 speakers of Kayeli, while a field survey conducted in 1989 located only four speakers. The most detailed study of the language was conducted in the 1980s by Charles E. Grimes and Barbara Dix Grimes, who used Kayeli in analysing the causes of the recent extinction of some Malayo-Polynesian languages.

## Language & religion

Kayeli was an extinct Austronesian language once used by the Kayeli people of Buru. Two dialects were recognized: *Leliali* (Liliali) and *Lumaete* (Lumaiti, Mumaite, Lumara). By 1995, only three elderly people could speak the language, and they had not used it for three decades; other natives had shifted to Ambonese Malay. A 1989 survey found four speakers, two men and two women, all in their sixties, who had not used the language for more than 30 years. The last speaker of the Leliali dialect died in 1989, and no speakers of Lumaete could then be located. Slightly more than 400 basic Kayeli words were assembled, including loanwords from Malay, Arabic, Portuguese, and Sanskrit. The Kayeli people are predominantly Sunni Muslim, with remnants of pre-Islamic pagan belief systems.
