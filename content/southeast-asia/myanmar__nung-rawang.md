## Overview

The Rawang, also known historically as the “Khu-Nung” or “Nung,” are a Tibeto-Burman ethnic group inhabiting far northern Kachin State of Burma (Myanmar), especially Putao District. Myanmar is home to an estimated 65,000–70,000 Rawang, while related groups live in neighboring parts of China and India. Their history connects the Tibetan Plateau and northwestern China with the Three Parallel Rivers region of Yunnan, the Upper Mekong Valley, and the remote valleys of the May Hka and Mali Hka rivers. The Rawang are closely related to the Nu and Derung people. Their folk-cultural importance in the available source lies principally in migration traditions, oral history, religious and funerary chants, naming practices, and clan customs.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

They do state that women of the Daru (Dvru) clan adopted facial tattooing to identify them after Tibetan raids in the Mongdam area of the China-Burma border, where members of the clan were taken as slaves to Tibet.

### Architecture

They record two specific traditions associated with oral history: the Daur clan claimed to be unable to build houses in forests because of fear of Tibetan encroachment and adopted camouflaged camps in forests; as late as the British era, in 1910, the Daru were said to live in nests in the trees.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Facial tattooing is documented among women of the Daru (Dvru) clan. The practice was adopted to identify women after Tibetan raids in the Mongdam area of the China-Burma border, during which members of the clan were taken as slaves to Tibet.

## Music & performance

The sources identify funerary and religious chants as an important part of Rawang oral tradition. These chants trace the path of the soul and retrace the original migration routes of the Rawang people and their ancestors over time. Because the language is archaic during such retellings, many of the locations and migration routes mentioned in them have lost their meaning or identification.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record funerary and religious chants that follow the path of the soul and preserve memories of ancestral migration routes.

Oral history also records relations and conflicts involving neighboring peoples. Rawang accounts describe early clashes with Naga tribes during hunting expeditions. The Mishmi people settled in the general area and absorbed a few Rawang clans, while the Mishmi and Rawang held good relations in the area of Li-Pu-Dam at the headwaters of the Mali kha River. These accounts form part of the available evidence for Rawang historical memory.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral history is central to the available account of the Rawang. According to cultural research and oral traditions, their ancestors migrated from the Tibetan Plateau and northwestern China southward into the Three Parallel Rivers region of Yunnan. During the second millennium, they moved southwest into northern Burma and settled in the remote valleys of the May Hka and Mali Hka rivers.

Rawang oral history recounts settlement in the land of Putao in upper Myanmar, early clashes with Naga tribes during hunting expeditions, and relations with the Mishmi people around the Mali kha River. It also preserves accounts of Tibetan raids in the Mongdam area, the enslavement of members of the Daru clan, and the adoption of facial tattooing by Daru women. Funerary and religious chants are said to retrace ancestral migration routes, although archaic language has caused many place names and routes to lose their meaning or identification.

## Language & religion

The Rawang are identified as part of the Nungish-speaking branch of the Tibeto-Burman family. The term Rawang is argued to be a shortening of *rvméváwng*, translated as “Middle River,” referring to movement from the north through Mekong routes and valleys. China recognises the group as part of the Derung people.

The sources refer to Rawang languages and to the archaic language used in funerary and religious chants. They do, however, identify funerary and religious chanting as connected with beliefs concerning the path of the soul and ancestral migration.
