## Overview

The Pangasinan people, also known as Pangasinense or *Totoon Pangasinan*, are an ethnolinguistic group native to the Philippines. They live mainly in Pangasinan and adjacent La Union and Tarlac, as well as Benguet, Nueva Ecija, Zambales, and Nueva Vizcaya; communities also occur elsewhere in the Philippines and in the Filipino diaspora. They numbered 1,823,865 in 2010, while the estimated population in Pangasinan province is 2.5 million. Their language, Pangasinan, is an Austronesian language belonging to the Malayo-Polynesian family and the Pangasinic sub-branch. The name Pangasinan means “land of salt” or “place of salt-making,” from *asin*, “salt.” The sources document the group especially through its language, literature, writing system, and historical identity.

## Music & performance

They mention *Malinak Lay Labi* (“Calm is the Night”) as a traditional folk song in Pangasinan, and *Malinac ya Labi* as a work by Julian Velasco with an English translation. The sources also list *Say Cancanta*, which includes translations of English songs such as “Joy to the World” and “What A Friend We Have in Jesus.” No instrument names, musical ensembles, performance occasions, or ceremonial uses are described.

## Oral tradition & literature

Written and oral literature in Pangasinan flourished during the Spanish and American period. Writers named in the sources include Juan Saingan, Felipe Quintos, Narciso Corpus, Antonio Solis, Juan Villamil, Juan Mejía, and María C. Magsano. Felipe Quintos wrote *Sipi Awaray: Gelew Diad Pilipinas* (*Revolución Filipina*), a history of the Katipunan revolutionary struggle in Pangasinan and surrounding provinces. Narciso Corpus and Antonio Solis co-wrote *Impanbilay na Manoc a Tortola*, a short love story published in 1926. Juan Villamil translated José Rizal’s “Mi último adiós” into Pangasinan, while Pablo Mejia edited *Tunong* in the 1920s and wrote *Bilay tan Kalkalar nen Rizal*. Magsano published *Silew* and wrote *Samban Agnabenegan*. Pangasinan Courier and Pioneer Herald published articles and literary works in Pangasinan.

## Language & religion

Pangasinan is an Austronesian language and one of the eight major languages of the Philippines. It belongs to the Malayo-Polynesian branch and is the sole member of the Pangasinic sub-branch. It is spoken principally in Pangasinan and northern Tarlac, as well as in parts of La Union, Benguet, Nueva Vizcaya, Nueva Ecija, and Zambales. Pangasinan has a verb–subject–object word order and is agglutinative. The modern alphabet has 28 letters, including the 26 letters of the basic Latin alphabet, the Spanish letter ñ, and the digraph ng. The ancient writing system was called *Kuritan* and was related to Tagalog Baybayin; it was derived from the Javanese Kawi script and the Vatteluttu or Pallava script of South India. The sources state that prior to Spanish colonization the Pangasinan people believed in a pantheon of unique deities. They also document Christian publications in Pangasinan.
