## Overview

The Sangirese, also known as Sangir, Sangirese, Sangihé, Sangi, Sangil, or Sangih, are native to the Sangir Islands in the northern chain of islands in Sulawesi and the southern part of Mindanao. They are concentrated in North Sulawesi in Indonesia and the Region of Dávao in the Philippines, with migrant communities on mainland Sulawesi, North Maluku, Ternate, Halmahera, and other areas. Sangirese livelihoods include fishing, nutmeg growing, farming, seafaring, shipbuilding, trade, and wage labour in industrial crop enterprises. Their language belongs to the Austronesian family and the Philippine group within it. Their culture combines maritime life, female-line land transmission, dance traditions, ancient ritual practices, and historical connections across Indonesia and the Philippines.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The main settlements of the Sangirese are located in coastal zones. Their houses were formerly erected on stilts, but these have gradually been replaced by modern houses built in the typical Indonesian style. Families living in the same village form a community called *soa*. Resettled Sangirese maintain ties with their *soa*, helping preserve identity through shared language and culture.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources mention that earlier populations in the Sangihe Islands developed agriculture and produced fabrics and ceramics. They also document blacksmithing, forestry production involving rattan and ebony wood, weaving, and the use of small dolls called *urǒ*, which residents hung to protect cultivated fruits such as coconuts from theft.

## Music & performance

Sangirese dance is generally accompanied by a musical band and female rhythmic singing. The sources identify no specific instruments or named song genres. The sacred *Sasahara* language, meaning “sea speech,” developed in the first half of the 20th century and was widely spoken among Sangirese sailors or pirates. It includes many words borrowed or distorted from other languages. Manado Malay, the lingua franca of North Sulawesi, is also widely spoken among the Sangirese and is particularly influential in Tahuna and Manado.

The sources describe Sangirese people as fishermen, seafarers, shipbuilders, traders, and maritime migrants connecting Sulawesi, the Maluku Islands, and the Philippines.

## Dance & theatre

Sangirese folklore is associated with dance art. Named local dances include *gunde*, *alabadiri*, *masamper*, and *ampawayer*. Earlier performances involved ritual gatherings, while in the present they are also associated with public holidays. Sangirese dance consists of smooth body movements performed by dancers. Organized dances involving large groups are usually accompanied by a musical band and female rhythmic singing.

## Festivals & rituals

Ancient Sangirese belief systems were polytheistic and included belief in spirits of nature and ancestors, ritual worship of inanimate objects, and magic. Distinguished shamans or priests acted as mediators between humans and spirits, protected patients and children, and performed miracles. Despite the spread of Islam and Christianity, many ancient rituals continued to be practiced.

They state that dance gatherings, once ritual in character, are now also held during public holidays. The sources record marriage as an important social institution and state that it was entered relatively late in a society that had reached high density by the 20th century. Historically, buying a bride was an important institution of public organization, and the ransom could consist of whole plots. No detailed birth, coming-of-age, funeral, or wedding rites are described.

## Foodways

Sangirese livelihoods include fishing, hunting, farming, and transit marine trading. Main crops include tubers, root crops, bananas, and sago. Taro was cultivated on mountain slopes and near rivers. Agriculture was considered mainly women’s work, while men commonly engaged in shipbuilding, seafaring, and trade. The sources also identify nutmeg growing and the cultivation of industrial crops in areas where Sangirese people worked as wage labourers.

The main diet is fish with vegetables.

## Oral tradition & literature

Sangirese folklore is described as famous for its dance art. They state that the small dolls called *urǒ*, hung to protect coconuts and other cultivated fruits from theft, are associated with a legend in which the dolls “pursue a thief.” The Sangirese consider themselves to have originated directly from Sangir Island, with Gumansalangi identified as their primogenitor and a cultural hero who lived around the 14th to 15th century.

The Sangirese language article lists a Sangirese and Dutch publication, “SANGIREESCHE TEKSTEN (Vervolg),” by N. Adriani from 1894.

## Language & religion

Sangir, also called Sangihé, Sangi, Sangil, or Sangih, is an Austronesian language spoken on islands linking northern Sulawesi with Mindanao. It belongs to the Philippine group within the Austronesian language family. Sangirese people also speak Talaud and Indonesian, as well as their dialects. Manado Malay is commonly used and may function as a first language. Sangirese has lexical influence from Ternate, Spanish, Dutch, and Malay. The *Sasahara* language is a sacred sea speech developed in the first half of the 20th century.

Islam spread from the Maluku Islands and North Sulawesi in the 15th to 16th century. Protestant Christianity expanded after the Sangihe Islands opened to Protestant missionaries in 1857. The majority of Sangirese people today profess Protestantism, while most Sangirese in the Philippines are Sunni Muslims. As of 2024, about 87% of Indonesian Sangir people from the Sangihe Islands Regency and Sitaro Islands Regency profess Christianity, about 12% are Muslim, and the remainder profess native beliefs.
