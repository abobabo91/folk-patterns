## Overview

The Yali are a major tribal group in Highland Papua, Indonesia. They live east of the Baliem Valley, mainly in Yalimo Regency, Yahukimo Regency, and surrounding regions. Their settlement territory extends between the Ubahak River in the east and the Sibi, Yahuli, and Podeng rivers in the west. Anggruk and Kosarek are major settlements, isolated by difficult geography and reached mainly by air or by several hours of walking. The population figures cited for the Yali vary: estimates for 1991 were 15,000 or 30,000, while the 2010 Indonesian census recorded 133,812 people, including Ngalik people. The Yali speak Yali, a Papuan language of Indonesian New Guinea, with considerable dialectical differentiation. Their folk-cultural profile includes distinctive clothing, village architecture, clan organization, origin traditions, shifting cultivation, hunting, gathering, and Christian religious change.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no Yali textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Yali men traditionally wear a penis sheath called *humi*, together with rattan rings around the waist. This combined attire is called *sabiyab*. The humi is long and thin, made from a dried bottle gourd, and fastened with rattan. The rattan rings also function as fire starters. More rings indicate greater prestige and bravery because rattan grew outside Yali territory and was difficult to obtain.

Men sometimes wear hair nets with a pointed end at the neck. These are decorated with feathers and furs from cassowary, yalme, and cuscus; sugarglider fur is used to decorate the tips of penis gourds. The skin is painted with clay or charcoal, and boar tusks are made into necklaces or nose accessories. Women wear a short reed skirt called *kem* or *kem lahuog* and carry a noken called *sum* on their heads. T-shirts and trousers, or skirts and blouses, are becoming more common.

### Architecture

The Yali live in villages called *opumbuk*. An opumbuk consists of several named building types: *yowi*, a hut for men; *homea* or *homi*, a hut for women; *wam ibam*, a pig house for livestock; and *usa yowi*, a sacred house used for boys’ and men’s rituals.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Yali body adornment includes rattan rings worn around the waist, feathers and animal furs attached to hair nets and penis gourds, boar-tusk necklaces, and boar-tusk nose accessories. Rattan rings communicate prestige and bravery as well as serving a practical function. Men’s skin is painted with clay or charcoal.

## Music & performance

They do describe ritual and social contexts in which particular buildings and customs were important. The *usa yowi* is a sacred house for boys’ and men’s rituals. Weddings are occasions for slaughtering pigs. The Yali also have origin traditions concerning Yeli, a mythical character associated with the creation of the universe, and traditions concerning the brothers Winda and Waya, or Kabak and Pahabol in Anggruk and Ninia Districts.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe several social and ritual practices. Weddings are associated with the slaughter of pigs, which the Yali otherwise keep extensively. The *usa yowi* is a sacred house for boys’ and men’s rituals.

Yali society is organized through exogamous moieties. The Yali believe they descend from two brothers, Winda and Waya; in Anggruk and Ninia Districts, these brothers are called Kabak and Pahabol. Every clan belongs to one moiety, and marriage between clans within the same moiety is strictly prohibited and considered incest, called *pabi*. In the past, the punishment for incest was death; after Christian missionaries discouraged the practice, punishment became more lenient.

Clans are called *unggul*. Groups of clans form larger *unggul uwag* groups, which may join with another group to form a *wilig lumpaleg*, sometimes called a confederation. The largest named grouping is *ap ahe*, referring to clans that have spread into other regions and may now belong to groups such as the Lani and Dani while still being regarded as related.

## Foodways

Sweet potatoes and taro are Yali staple foods. They are grown through shifting cultivation. Hunting and gathering provide additional food. Fruits are not part of the common diet. Pigs are kept extensively and slaughtered for festivities such as weddings.

The sources also describe a gendered division of food production. Men are responsible for hunting and building houses, while women grow and gather food.

## Oral tradition & literature

Yali origin traditions include the story of Yeli, a mythical character who was impervious to death even after being cut. Humans, animals, trees, and other living things were said to have sprung from his severed body parts, and he was revered as the creator of the universe. Before disappearing in the west, toward the Baliem Valley, Yeli taught the Yali their customs and asked to be worshipped. His name gradually changed in pronunciation to become Yali.

Other origin traditions concern the brothers Winda and Waya, or Kabak and Pahabol in Anggruk and Ninia Districts. Clan members believe in a common origin myth, while larger groupings may carry multiple origin myths with the same clan names.

## Language & religion

Yali, also known as Yaly, Jalè, or Jaly, is a Papuan language of Indonesian New Guinea. The language has substantial dialectical differentiation. The sources identify three varieties: Pass Valley, also known as Abenaho, North Ngalik, and Western Yali; Ninia, also known as North Ngalik and Southern Yali; and Angguruk, also known as Northern Yali. Pass Valley includes the subdialects Pass Valley, Landikma, and Apahapsili. Almost nothing is known about the language, and even its pronouns were not attested for the classification cited in the source.

Siegfried Zöellner, a German missionary, translated the Bible into Yali between 1960 and 1973. Today the Yali are Christian, mainly Protestant. Christian missionaries stopped several feuds between villages, and old war rituals and ancestor cults were forgotten.
