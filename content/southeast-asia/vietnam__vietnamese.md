## Overview

The Vietnamese, also known as the Viet or Kinh people, are native to modern-day northern Vietnam and expanded southward during the last millennium. They are concentrated in the lowlands, especially around the Red River Delta, with Hanoi as its capital, and are the dominant ethnic group in most provinces of Vietnam. The Viet accounted for 85.32% of Vietnam’s population in the 2019 census; the sources do not provide a current total population figure. Vietnamese belongs to the Vietic subgroup of the Austroasiatic language family and is Vietnam’s official language. Vietnamese folk culture combines ancestor veneration, family and community obligations, local worship traditions, mythology, music, theatre, cuisine, clothing, and arts shaped by Austroasiatic, Chinese, Cham, Khmer, and French influences.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Silk painting* is a Vietnamese visual-art tradition using silk as a medium. Vietnamese artists combined Western composition principles with Eastern traditions such as calligraphy and brush painting; Nguyễn Phan Chánh, Lê Phổ, Mai Trung Thứ, Trần Văn Cẩn, and Lê Văn Đệ are identified with the medium.

*Lacquer painting* uses resin from the Sơn tree, applied in numerous layers over several months to produce colour and visual effects. Nguyễn Gia Trí is noted for researching pigments and developing new lacquer colours.

*Vietnamese silk* was also used for clothing and pajama-like peasant costumes. Vietnamese art sources describe cloth weaving in the period of the Đông Sơn culture, while silk painting became especially prominent during the development of modern Vietnamese art.

*Vietnamese woodblock prints* use organic materials for paint, which is applied to wood and pressed onto paper. The process is repeated with different colours.

**Motif vocabulary.** Geometric patterns, farming scenes, warriors with feather headdresses, ships, musicians.

### Clothing & dress

The *áo dài* is the most widely recognized Vietnamese national costume. It consists of a long gown with slits on both sides, worn over cotton or silk trousers. It is worn by both genders, although today it is mainly worn by women; white áo dài is required for girls in many high schools. The *áo tràng vạt* was a traditional cross-collared robe worn before the 19th century, while the *áo ngũ thân* replaced it during the Nguyễn dynasty. The *áo tứ thân*, or “four-part dress,” was widely worn by commoner women with the *áo yếm* bodice. Peasant clothing included silk pajama-like costumes called *áo cánh* in the north and *áo bà ba* in the south.

Headgear included *Khăn vấn*, a plain cloth wrapped around the head, the *Đinh Tự* hat, and the *nón lá* conical hat. Peasants often went barefoot, though wooden clogs and sandals were worn, especially on formal occasions. Traditional clothing is now generally reserved for special occasions, apart from the white áo dài worn by many high school girls.

### Architecture

The sources describe prehistoric Vietnamese houses as stilt-houses, represented on Đông Sơn bronze drums. Similar houses can still be found in Vietnam. Chinese architectural influence shaped pagodas, temples, communal houses, scholar-bureaucrat residences, aristocratic buildings, and imperial palaces, while French colonization introduced villas, government buildings, and opera houses.

The Temple of Literature, or *Văn Miếu*, in Hanoi was constructed during the Lý dynasty and dedicated to Confucius and his disciples. It consists of courtyards, buildings, pavilions, and stone steles placed on stone turtles. The complex also contained the *Quốc Tử Giám*, or National University, which functioned from 1076 to 1779. Vietnamese folk-religion buildings include *miếu*, *đình*, *đền*, *điện*, *đài*, *tịnh*, and *phủ*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Vietnamese ceramics and porcelain developed through native traditions and Chinese influence. Ceramics from the Lý, Trần, and Hồ period used the “three colors” concept and became famous across East and Southeast Asia. During the Nguyễn dynasty, imperial courts across Asia imported Vietnamese ceramics.

The Đông Sơn culture was known for advanced bronze-casting skills and elaborately decorated Đông Sơn drums. The drums depict farming, warriors, ships, musicians, and other scenes of everyday life. Common household objects include chopsticks, rice pots, clay cooking pots, ancestor altars, and the wooden puppets used in water puppetry.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used describe tattooing and teeth blackening among ancient Yue groups living in southern China and northern Vietnam. Chinese writers reported that inhabitants of Đại Việt tattooed their foreheads, crossed their feet, blackened their teeth, and went barefoot.

## Music & performance

Vietnamese music has been used in religious activities, daily life, and traditional festivals. It includes regional traditions from northern, central, and southern Vietnam and reflects Chinese, Cham, Buddhist, Western, and other influences. Named instruments include *đàn bầu*, a monochord zither; *đàn tranh*, a long zither; *đàn nhị*, a two-stringed fiddle; *đàn nguyệt*, a two-stringed fretted moon lute; *đàn tỳ bà*, a pear-shaped four-stringed lute; *đàn tam*, a three-stringed lute; *kèn bầu*, an oboe; *T'rưng*, a bamboo xylophone; and *K'ni*, a one-string vertical fiddle played by the Jarai people.

*Nhã nhạc* is court music associated with the Trần dynasty through the Nguyễn dynasty. It was influenced by Ming Chinese music and developed in the royal court in the 1430s. Court music included civil and military dances and was performed in imperial rituals. *Ca trù*, also called *hát ả đào*, is a complex form of musical poetry, generally performed by women and associated historically with imperial and communal-house performances for scholars and elites. It was recognized by UNESCO as a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity since 2005.

Folk genres include *quan họ*, *ca trù*, *hát tuồng*, *hát chèo*, *hát chầu văn*, *hò*, *hát xẩm*, *hát xoan*, *bài chòi*, *đờn ca tài tử*, *ca Huế*, and *trống quân*. *Chầu văn* is rhythmic, trance-oriented music used to invoke spirits during ceremonies. *Vọng cổ* is a popular *tài tử* melody composed in 1919 by Mr Sáu Lầu of Bạc Liêu.

## Dance & theatre

Vietnamese theatre includes *hát tuồng*, also known as *hát bội*, a traditional opera influenced by Chinese opera. It developed from royal entertainment into travelling performances for commoners and peasants. *Cải lương* is a modern folk opera originating in southern Vietnam and uses extensive vibrato techniques. *Hát chèo* is generally satirical musical theatre, traditionally performed by northern Vietnamese peasants in village squares or courtyards, with dance and touring groups.

Water puppetry, or *Múa rối nước*, originated in the 10th century and became especially popular in northern Vietnam. A split-bamboo screen hides puppeteers, while wooden puppets stand in water and are moved with long poles beneath the surface. Performances use epic story lines and scenes from Vietnamese life.

Traditional dances include the lion dance, imperial lantern dance, fan dance, and platter dance. Court dances developed over centuries and required considerable skill; imperial court music and dance were also associated with ceremonies wishing the emperor or empress longevity and the country prosperity.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used mention traditional festivals, Lunar New Year, weddings, funerals, longevity-wishing ceremonies, and ancestor offerings.

Ancestor worship is widely practised. Most Vietnamese, regardless of religious denomination, are described as maintaining an ancestor altar at home or at a business. Food may be placed on the altar during special occasions such as Lunar New Year. Weddings, funerals, festivals, and longevity-wishing ceremonies are occasions for feasts.

Vietnamese funeral practice includes a wake or vigil, washing and dressing the body, placing rice and coins in the mouth, enveloping the body in white cloth, placing it in a coffin, and holding a funeral ceremony. Traditional mourning includes a procession to the burial ground, visits to the tomb after three days, and later observances after 49 days, 100 days, one year, and two years. The sources also describe modern ceremonies as simplified.

## Foodways

Vietnamese cuisine is based substantially on rice, fish sauce, vegetables, herbs, and fresh ingredients. Its five fundamental tastes are sweet, salty, bitter, sour, and spicy. Common ingredients include lemongrass, ginger, mint, Vietnamese mint, brown sugar, long coriander, Saigon cinnamon, bird’s-eye chili, soy sauce, lime, and Thai basil. Cooking generally uses little oil and makes extensive use of vegetables.

*Phở* is a rice-noodle soup originating in North Vietnam, commonly prepared with beef or chicken soup, bean sprouts, scallions, spices, and sauces. Other named dishes include *bún riêu*, *bánh cuốn*, *bún chả*, *chả cá Lã Vọng*, *bún bò Huế*, *bánh khoái*, *bánh khọt*, and *bún mắm*. Regional cooking differs: northern food is generally lighter and less spicy, central food is more strongly spiced and decorative, and southern food uses more sugar, coconut milk, fresh herbs, and seafood.

A typical family meal includes *cơm trắng*, a main dish, *rau*, *canh*, *nước chấm*, relishes, and dessert. Dishes are generally shared communally, while younger people wait for elders to eat first. *Nước mắm*, fermented fish sauce, is the most commonly used condiment. Feasts serve boiled rice, main dishes, and desserts collectively; a basic northern feast consists of ten dishes.

## Oral tradition & literature

Vietnamese mythology and folklore have been transmitted through oral traditions and writing. The creation story of *Lạc Long Quân* and *Âu Cơ* describes one hundred eggs, from which descendants became the Vietnamese people. The story is also known as *Con rồng cháu tiên*, “Descendants of the Dragon and the Immortal.”

Other popular narratives include *Thần Trụ Trời*, the legend of *Sơn Tinh* and *Thủy Tinh*, the betrayal of *An Dương Vương*, *Hoàn Kiếm Lake* and *Lê Lợi*’s magical sword, *Ông Táo*, the origins of *bánh chưng*, and the Four Elements of the Turtle, Dragon, Unicorn, and Phoenix. Vietnamese mythology includes the Four Immortals: *Thánh Gióng*, *Tản Viên Sơn Thánh*, *Chử Đồng Tử*, and *Liễu Hạnh*.

Literature was historically written in *chữ Hán* and *chữ Nôm*. Important works include *Truyện Kiều*, *Cung Oán Ngâm Khúc*, *Chinh phụ ngâm*, and *Quốc âm Thi Tập*. Since the 1920s, literature has mainly used the Vietnamese alphabet and has included novels, poems, short stories, dramas, and modern Vietnamese fables.

## Language & religion

Vietnamese is an Austroasiatic language in the Vietic subgroup and is the official and national language of Vietnam. It is tonal, predominantly monosyllabic, and uses a Latin-based alphabet called *chữ Quốc ngữ*. Earlier writing used *chữ Nôm*, based on Chinese characters, while *chữ Hán* and Classical Chinese were used for formal writing. Vietnamese contains substantial Sino-Vietnamese vocabulary and also has French influence. The sources describe northern, central, and southern variation, including Hanoi and Saigon pronunciation.

Vietnamese religious life includes folk religion, Mahayana Buddhism, Confucianism, Taoism, Catholicism, and syncretic movements. Folk religion is organized around local worship traditions devoted to *thần*, including nature deities, tutelary deities, ancestral gods, and deified heroes. *Đạo Mẫu* centres on mother goddesses and includes shamanic practices, temples, rituals, and spiritual mediums. *Cao Đài* combines local-spirit worship with Buddhism, Confucianism, Taoism, and elements of Catholicism, Spiritism, and Theosophy.
