## Overview

The Lao are the dominant people of the Mekong lowlands, with a material culture defined by technically sophisticated textiles and a music tradition centered on the khene. Their ritual calendar blends Theravada Buddhism with older animist spirit worship, and their foodways revolve around sticky rice eaten communally. Buddhism structures the male life course through monastic ordination, while oral tradition preserves the Phra Lak Phra Lam (Lao Ramayana) and other narratives tied to the Mekong landscape.

## Material culture

Lao material culture emphasizes textiles and metalwork over ceramics, with weaving techniques that combine multiple ikat and brocade methods within single cloths.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Lao weaving combines supplementary-weft brocade, warp ikat, and weft ikat, often all in one cloth, making it one of mainland Southeast Asia's most complex textile traditions.

- **sinh** — The tube skirt worn by nearly all Lao women, made from three horizontally joined sections with the hem border (tin sinh) carrying dense patterning that marks the weaver's region and skill.
- **Lao silk** (*silk*) — Fine lustrous filament silk historically raised in villages, with Xam Neua and Luang Prabang as the highest-status centers, dyed with natural colors from lac insects, indigo, and ebony fruit.
- **Mudmee Lao** (*mat mi*) — The Lao weft-ikat technique in which weft threads are tied, dyed in patterns, and then woven to create slightly blurred, feathered figures, distinguished by darker palettes and tighter zoomorphic patterns.
- **pha biang** — A long ceremonial shawl worn diagonally across the chest, typically in silk with supplementary-weft or brocaded ends, worn by women and by men at temple.
- **Tai Lue weaving** (*Tai Lue*) — The tradition of closely related Tai Lue peoples from northern Laos, distinguished by red-ground supplementary-weft cloths with stepped diamonds and rows of small stylized figures.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday women wear a plain sinh with a fitted blouse, while ceremonial dress adds a heavily brocaded sinh with months of patterning, a silk pha biang, and silver jewelry.

- **salong** — A length of cloth historically wrapped between the legs to form loose trousers, worn by men alongside a collarless shirt, returning for ordination and New Year rites.
- **monk robes** (*robes*) — Dyed in the ochre-saffron range using jackfruit heartwood for novice and full monk ordination.
- **silver belt** (*belt*) — A thick ceremonial belt with repoussé plaques, worn with silver bracelets as part of full formal dress.

### Architecture

Vernacular Lao houses are raised on wooden posts two to three meters high, with woven bamboo or plank walls, an open front veranda as the social center, and an under-house void used for weaving.

- **sim** — The principal ordination hall of a Buddhist monastery, with a rectangular plan, tiered roofs that sweep close to the ground, and gable ends topped with curled cho fa finials.
- **That Luang** (*that*) — A gilded lotus-bud stupa on a stepped square base, the most famous example located in Vientiane and central to the Bun That Luang festival.
- **wat** — A walled Buddhist monastic compound containing the sim, stupas, and other buildings, the religious and social heart of every Lao village.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Lao material culture relies more on metalwork than ceramics; everyday pottery is low-fired earthenware for cooking and storage.

- **sticky-rice steamer** (*huat*) — A conical ceramic vessel for steaming glutinous rice, paired with the woven bamboo rice basket (tip khao) that keeps steamed rice warm at table.
- **betel box** (*khan mak*) — An ornate silver container for betel ingredients, produced in Luang Prabang and Vientiane as part of the broader silver-working tradition.
- **khan tray** (*khan*) — A tall pedestalled silver tray used to present offerings at Buddhist rites and merit-making ceremonies.
- **scripture chest** (*ho tai*) — A lacquered and gilded wooden chest that houses palm-leaf Buddhist sutras in monastery libraries.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Lao ceremonial jewelry centers on silver, with heavy hollow-linked belts, filigree bracelets, and disc-drop earrings as standard forms, complemented by amuletic pendants and the baci blessing cord.

- **pin hairpin** (*pin*) — A silver hairpin worn through a coiled bun as part of ceremonial dress.
- **takrut amulet** (*takrut*) — A small cylindrical pendant inscribed with Pali text, strung on braided cords and worn at the neck for spiritual protection.
- **baci string** (*baci*) — A cotton cord knotted around the wrist during the sou khwan soul-calling rite, functioning as both adornment and blessing that recipients typically leave on until it falls off naturally.

## Music & performance

The khene, a free-reed bamboo mouth organ, is the defining Lao instrument and anchors nearly every folk-song genre, from extemporized lam poetry to ceremonial ensemble music.

- **khene** (*khaen*) — A free-reed bamboo mouth organ of paired vertical pipes with small metal reeds, inscribed by UNESCO in 2017 as a Lao cultural treasure and used as accompaniment in all major vocal traditions.
- **lam** — Extemporized sung poetry in which a vocalist (mor lam) improvises verses over khene accompaniment, with regional styles including lam saravane, lam siphandone, and lam khon savan.
- **mor lam** — A vocalist who improvises rhymed verses in real-time exchanges during lam performances, now also a mass-market popular idiom in northeastern Thailand.
- **ranat xylophone** (*ranat*) — A wooden xylophone used in instrumental ensembles alongside gongs, spike fiddles, and barrel drums.
- **piphat** — Court music of Luang Prabang that follows the broader mainland Southeast Asian gong-chime tradition and accompanies ritual, ordination, and royal dance.

## Dance & theatre

Classical Lao dance derives from the royal court of Luang Prabang and centers on lakhon phra lak phra lam, the Lao Ramayana dance-drama with stylized gestures, gilded headdresses, and painted masks.

- **lakhon phra lak phra lam** — A dance-drama version of the Ramayana in which stylized gestures and masks depict Phra Lam, Phra Lak, the demon king Hapkhanasouane, and Hanuman, with masked players taking demon and monkey roles.
- **lam vong** — A slow circular couple dance performed at weddings, New Year, and village celebrations, with pairs rotating counterclockwise while making stylized hand movements in front of the chest.
- **Bun Bang Fai masked procession** (*phi ta khon*) — Ritual masked performance that survives most vividly in the rocket festival and in masked processions of the Lao borderlands.
- **shadow puppetry** (*nang talung*) — A performance tradition more strongly associated with southern Thailand but performed in southern Lao villages.

## Festivals & rituals

The Lao ritual calendar follows the lunar year as the Hit Sip Song (twelve customs), anchored by major festivals like Bun Pi Mai (New Year) and Bun Bang Fai (Rocket Festival) that blend Buddhist practice with spirit propitiation.

- **Bun Pi Mai** — The three-day Lao New Year festival in mid-April in which Buddha images are bathed with scented water and sand stupas are built in temple courtyards.
- **Bun Bang Fai** — The Rocket Festival held in the sixth lunar month, in which villages launch enormous homemade bamboo rockets to petition the sky god Phaya Thaen for monsoon rain.
- **Bun Ok Phansa** — The end of Buddhist Lent in the eleventh lunar month, marked by illuminated boat processions on the Mekong called Lai Heua Fai.
- **Bun That Luang** — The great stupa festival at That Luang in Vientiane held in the twelfth lunar month, combining merit-making and pilgrimage.
- **baci / sou khwan** (*sou khwan*) — The soul-calling ceremony in which elders knot cotton strings around the wrists of an honoree to summon back the thirty-two khwan (soul components) at births, ordinations, weddings, and departures.

## Foodways

Sticky rice is the Lao staple, steamed in a conical bamboo basket and eaten by hand from a shared basket, distinguishing Lao meals from central Thai and Vietnamese rice traditions.

- **sticky rice** (*khao niaow*) — The Lao staple, steamed in a conical bamboo basket over a clay pot and eaten by hand from the shared tip khao, central to every meal.
- **laap** (*larb*) — Minced meat or fish tossed with toasted rice powder, lime, fish sauce, chilies, mint, and shallots, considered the national dish.
- **padaek** — A coarse fermented fish sauce with visible rice bran and fish pieces that is the foundational seasoning of Lao cooking, distinct from the thinner Thai and Vietnamese versions.
- **tam mak hoong** — The Lao green papaya salad pounded in a clay mortar and typically dressed with fermented fish sauce and crab.
- **or lam** — A Luang Prabang stew of buffalo or beef with sakhan wood, eggplant, and long beans, served at celebrations and merit-making.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Phra Lak Phra Lam (Lao Ramayana) is the core literary text, transmitted in palm-leaf manuscripts, oral recitation, and dance-drama, with the Mekong landscape as its setting.

- **Phra Lak Phra Lam** — The Lao Ramayana, transmitted in palm-leaf manuscript and oral recitation and dance-drama, localizing the Rama story to the Mekong valley.
- **Sang Sinxay** — A seventeenth-century epic composed by court poet Pang Kham, following the hero Sinxay's rescue of his aunt from a winged demon and widely quoted in Lao culture.
- **Vessantara Jataka** (*Phra Vet*) — A Buddhist Jataka tale recited in full at the Bun Phra Vet festival, forming part of the backbone of Lao religious storytelling.
- **nithan** — Folktales and etiological legends from village oral tradition, including stories of the naga of the Mekong and local phi spirits.
- **bailan manuscripts** (*bailan*) — Palm-leaf manuscripts written in the old Tham script that preserve religious and literary texts in monastery libraries.

## Language & religion

Lao is a tonal Kra–Dai language written in an Indic-derived abugida, with major dialects running north-to-south along the Mekong, and Theravada Buddhism layered over an animist substrate of phi (spirits).

- **Lao script** — An Indic-derived abugida related to Thai, used for the modern Lao language, while the older Tham script preserves ecclesiastical texts.
- **phi ban** — The guardian spirit of each village, maintained through a shrine and ritual offerings that syncretize animism with Buddhist practice.
- **khwan** — One of thirty-two soul components called back during the sou khwan ritual at births, ordinations, weddings, and other life transitions.
- **Theravada Buddhism** (*Buddhism*) — The dominant religion, practiced in the same lineage as Thai and Cambodian traditions, structuring the male life course through monastic ordination and alms-giving.
- **tak bat** — Buddhist alms-giving at dawn in which laypeople offer sticky rice and dishes to monks lining village streets as an act of merit-making.

## Glossary

- *sinh* — The tube skirt worn by Lao women, woven in three sections with the hem border carrying dense patterning.
- *mat mi* — Lao weft-ikat technique where threads are tied and dyed before weaving, creating blurred, feathered patterns.
- *pha biang* — A long ceremonial shawl worn diagonally across the chest, typically in silk with brocaded ends.
- *khene* — A free-reed bamboo mouth organ with paired vertical pipes and metal reeds, the defining Lao instrument.
- *lam* — Extemporized sung poetry performed over khene accompaniment, the core Lao vocal tradition.
- *mor lam* — A vocalist who improvises rhymed verses during lam performances in real-time exchanges.
- *sim* — The principal ordination hall of a Buddhist monastery, with tiered roofs and curled finials.
- *that* — A gilded lotus-bud stupa on a stepped square base, the most sacred form of Buddhist monument.
- *wat* — A walled Buddhist monastic compound containing an ordination hall, stupas, and other buildings.
- *khao niaow* — Sticky glutinous rice, the Lao staple steamed in a conical basket and eaten by hand communally.
- *padaek* — A coarse fermented fish sauce with visible rice bran and fish pieces, the foundational Lao seasoning.
- *larb* — Minced meat or fish tossed with toasted rice powder, lime, fish sauce, chilies, mint, and shallots; the national dish.
- *sou khwan* — A soul-calling ceremony in which elders knot cotton strings around the wrist to summon back soul components.
- *baci* — A cotton cord knotted around the wrist during soul-calling rites, functioning as adornment and blessing.
- *phi* — Spirits in the animist substrate of Lao belief, including territorial, ancestral, household, and pathological types.
- *khwan* — One of thirty-two soul components in Lao and Southeast Asian belief, called back during ritual ceremonies.
- *phi ban* — The guardian spirit of a village, maintained through shrines and ritual propitiation.
- *naga* — A serpent-water spirit depicted in textile motifs, often as a paired 'nak khut' figure.
- *tak bat* — Buddhist dawn alms-giving in which laypeople offer food to monks as an act of merit-making.
- *bailan* — Palm-leaf manuscripts written in the old Tham script, preserving religious and literary texts.
- *Phra Vet* — The Vessantara Jataka, a Buddhist birth tale recited in full at the Bun Phra Vet festival.
- *nithan* — Folktales and etiological legends from village oral tradition about spirits and the Mekong landscape.
