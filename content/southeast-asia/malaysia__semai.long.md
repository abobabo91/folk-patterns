## Overview

The Semai are a semi-sedentary people of the interior Malay Peninsula, concentrated in Perak — Tapah, Bidor, Gopeng, the Batang Padang and Kampar districts — and in Pahang around Kuala Lipis, Raub and the Cameron Highlands. They belong to the Senoi grouping and are the largest of its constituent peoples, and one of the largest indigenous groups on the peninsula; the Center for Orang Asli Concerns counted 34,248 Semai in 2000, while the Semai language is reported as spoken by about 60,438 people. Their language, *Semai*, is Austroasiatic (Mon-Khmer), closely related to Temiar, spoken by the Temiar immediately to their north; the Jah Hut border them to the south. In folk-culture terms the Semai are known above all for an ethic of non-violence and deference, described by the anthropologist Robert Knox Dentan from fieldwork in the 1960s, and for a material repertoire built almost entirely from bamboo, cane, rattan and bark.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here — the Wikipedia articles on the Semai and their language, and the British Museum catalogue records for the objects shown — do not describe named Semai weaving or plaiting traditions, motif names, or dyeing techniques. What they document instead is bark and fibre work. Barkcloth is attested by two object classes: a grooved wooden **barkcloth-beater** (a mallet used to pound bast into sheet cloth) and a long narrow **barkcloth bag**. Plant-fibre cordage appears as girdles of black twine with a loosely attached "wreath" of leaves, and as a woman's waist belt of fibre, grass and leaf. Basketry is represented by a leaf basket, a cane fish trap, and a series of plaited caps and lids made for blowpipe quivers, some combining bamboo, rattan, cane and leaf (recorded in one case as possibly screwpine). House walls, in the settlement description, are woven — the same plaiting skill applied at architectural scale.

### Clothing & dress

Semai dress in the record shown here is minimal and fibre-based rather than tailored. Women wore a waist belt of fibre, grass and leaf, and girdles of black twine carrying a loose wreath of leaves — plant matter worn as ornament rather than as covering. Headgear is represented by a head-dress of bark and string. The collection includes no shirts, skirts, sarongs or footwear, and the sources name no vernacular garment terms, no ceremonial costume distinct from daily wear, and no distinction between men's and women's ceremonial dress. The one substantial cloth object is the long narrow barkcloth bag, which with the barkcloth-beater indicates that beaten bark, not woven cloth, supplied what flat textile the Semai used. Circumcision ceremonies are named among the occasions for the *Sewang* dance, but the sources say nothing about dress worn at them.

### Architecture

Semai settlements hold 30 to 200 people, and a household is normally a nuclear family, sometimes with a few extended relatives attached. Dwellings are built of wood and bamboo, with woven walls and roofs thatched with palm leaves. The interior is a single social space: there are no visible bedrooms, and children sleep together with everyone else in the main hall. The only internal division is a curtain of wooden beads screening the parents' chamber — a device the coastal Malays share in a version made of seashells, and the deutero-Malays in batik cloth. There are no locks or any other physical barrier to entry. The curtain itself carries the rule: drawn down, it signals that entry is unwanted; drawn to the sides and tied open, it admits. Entering while the curtain is closed, without express permission, is a transgression and is held to bring natural retribution. Dentan's account stresses that no line divides public from private space in a Semai house, so that "the Western concept of privacy, domestic or otherwise, is not to be found" — a spatial order also found among rural Malays descended from Semai and other Orang Asli marriages.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Semai material culture as collected is bamboo, cane, wood and horn, with no ceramics and almost no metal. The blowpipe complex dominates: bamboo quivers bound with rattan, their interiors fitted with cane tubes to hold darts, closed by caps and lids of bamboo, cane or plaited basketry; wooden blowgun darts; and poison darts of reed. Bladed and hafted weapons are bamboo — complete and unfinished spear-heads, a multiple dagger and its bamboo sheath. Domestic objects include a cane fish trap (women fish with basket traps, men hunt with blowpipes, poison darts and spears), a leaf basket, a wooden hair comb, a bamboo cup used to hold water or seed for caged birds, the grooved barkcloth-beater, and a fire-piston of wood with a horn receptacle. Machetes, used to clear forest for swidden planting, are mentioned but not among the objects shown.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ornament is carved bamboo, worked at the ears, nose and hair. The collection holds cylindrical bamboo ear-rings with incised decoration, a bamboo ear-ring or ear-plug, bamboo hair-pins, and a bamboo nose stick incised with encircling lines — the same shallow-cut linear decoration that appears on the bamboo flutes. Non-bamboo pieces are a rattan bracelet, a necklace strung from seeds and teeth, and a woman's armlet of brass wire, the single metal ornament recorded. A wooden hair comb and the bark-and-string head-dress complete the repertoire. The sources record no tattooing, no body painting, and no named types for any of these ornaments.

## Music & performance

The instruments collected from the Semai are bamboo, wood and shell, and they cluster in three families. Flutes are the largest group: cylindrical bamboo flutes with one end open above the embouchure and the other pierced by a single hole, some carrying a separable cylindrical end-cap, and several decorated with incised work; plainer bamboo flutes are also recorded. Jew's harps appear in three materials — wood, wood with vegetable-fibre string, and turtle-shell. A bamboo horn and two further bamboo instruments (one of bamboo and string) complete the set. No vernacular instrument names, tunings, song genres or singer roles are given in the sources.

Performance context is documented for one tradition. The *Sewang* is danced at birth celebrations, funerals, circumcision ceremonies, healings of sickness and other occasions bound up with belief; some of these events run for three to six days. Dreaming is treated as a purpose in itself in Semai life: children's games are described as exertion that tires the body and so prepares it for sleep and the dreams that follow.

## Dance & theatre

The Semai are known for the **Sewang**, their dance tradition, performed at births, funerals, circumcisions, and the healing of illness, with some of these events lasting three to six days. The sources name no other dance, and no masked, puppet or dramatic theatre tradition. What they do describe at length is a performative institution that is not theatre but is public and formal: the *becharaa*, a dispute assembly held at the headman's house, which can run for days and in which the disputants and the whole community work through the causes, motives and resolution of a quarrel, closing with the headman charging one or both parties not to repeat the behaviour lest it endanger the community. The Semai say "there are more reasons to fear a dispute than a tiger."

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe no annual or calendrical festival cycle for the Semai. Ritual life as recorded is occasional and life-cycle bound, and the *Sewang* dance is its vehicle: birth, funeral, circumcision, and the healing of sickness, in events that may extend over three to six days.

Marriage is the best-documented rite of passage, though as practice rather than ceremony. Semai marriage is overwhelmingly monogamous — fewer than five percent of women are married polygynously — and exogamous, the East Semai barring marriage with consanguineal kin and the West Semai barring anyone descended from one of a person's grandparents, so that spouses usually come from different settlements. Residence afterwards is ambilocal: the couple shuttles between the wife's and the husband's kin every few weeks, lengthening each stay until they settle, and the reason the Semai themselves give is homesickness. Even settled couples take week- or month-long visits to their own kin; divorce and remarriage are fairly common.

One weather rite is recorded. *Chuntah* is performed to drive evil spirits away: in the middle of a storm a man collects rain in a bamboo container until it is nearly full, then gashes his skin and lets his blood run into it.

## Foodways

The Semai are semi-sedentary horticulturalists practising swidden cultivation. They clear forest with machetes, burn it, and plant; after two or three harvests, when the ground is worn, they move to a new plot. The staples are rice and manioc or cassava root. Hunting and fishing supplement the harvest, along with chickens raised for the household, and goats and ducks raised to sell to Malays. Women take most of the fish, scooping them with basket traps; men hunt with blowpipes, poison darts and spears. Large game — deer, pigs, pythons, binturong — is shared out across the community.

Distribution matters more in the sources than cuisine. Food sharing is among the most significant forms of interaction between Semai, and it is governed by *punan*, the taboo on making another person unhappy by imposing one's own wishes or denying theirs. Refusing a request for food is *punan*; so is asking for more than the giver can spare. Small catches go to the nuclear family and near neighbours, larger ones to the whole settlement, which both builds relationships and raises each household's chance of a good meal.

Dietary rule follows the Semai animal classification: *cheb* for the feathered and flying, *'ka* for creatures with rounded scales or moist skin living in or near water, and *menhar* for those living on the ground or in trees, a category that also takes in fungi. Animals straddling two classes are restricted — snakes are inedible because they live on land like *menhar* yet have scales and lack legs like *ka*. The sources name no dishes and no drink traditions.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources record no epic, song-cycle or named storytelling genre. What they do document is narrative used to raise children. Semai children are not punished or coerced: if a child refuses a parent's request, the matter ends there. Control works instead through fear — of strangers, and of violence in nature such as thunderstorms and lightning — and through stories of sprites (*mambang* in Malay) and forest spirits that exact retribution when their sanctity is violated, a logic the sources liken to karma. The cherished virtue is *mengalah*, giving in or giving way to others to keep the peace of the village, and children are taught it young, along with a wariness of their own aggressive impulses. When a child is angry a parent shouts "*Tarlaid!*" — an act that is *tarlaid* can rouse the anger of spirits and bring on natural calamity. A parent may raise a fist or a machete in threat, but does not follow through; the most a child receives is a tap. Two Semai sayings are recorded: "There is no authority here but embarrassment," and the dispute-and-tiger proverb quoted above. The Semai language is documented in the RWAAI archive at Lund University and in an SIL International dictionary, and the 2017 film *Asli*, directed by David Liew, is the first to carry Semai in about half its dialogue.

## Language & religion

Semai is an Austroasiatic language of the Aslian branch, one of the few Aslian languages not endangered, with roughly 2,000 monolingual speakers and three main varieties: Northern, Central and Southern Semai. Its phonology is noted for a highly irregular pattern of expressive reduplication, copying only from the edges of the reduplicant's base to form a minor syllable. A 1995 genetic study at the National University of Singapore found the Semai closely related to the Khmer of Cambodia, consistent with the Mon-Khmer classification, and closer to Javanese than to their Malay neighbours. Semai religion is animist. A thunder deity, *Enku*, presides; a small eyeless snake is called Thunder's headband; and the *Nāga*, huge subterranean dragons associated with rainbows, ravage villages during thunder-squalls. The Semai worldview holds the surrounding jungle to be full of terrifying forces and malevolent beings, so that safety lies only in the community — the ground of their insistence on harmony and non-violence. Several Malaysian state governments run religious agency programmes to convert the Semai to Islam.
