## Overview

The Alune, also known as Sapalewa or Sapolewa, are one of the long-established ethnic groups of Seram Island, Indonesia. About 21,300 Alune live in 27 villages in the western-central area of the island. They originated, together with the Wemale, in a common group called Patasiwa. Their language is of Malayo-Polynesian origin and is also known as Sapalewa or Patasiwa Alfoeren. Despite the small number of speakers, Alune has several dialects, including the prestigious Rambatu speech variety. The available sources document a culture historically connected with forest products, sago, shifting cultivation, bark-fibre clothing, necklaces, warrior clothing, long swords, carpentry, elaborate houses, coming-of-age celebrations, and the Hainuwele legend. The sources also state that consumerism, political and religious restlessness, and conflict in Indonesia have significantly changed Alune culture and lifestyle.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Bark-fibre loincloth* — Alune adults traditionally wore a short loincloth from the waist down, made of bark-fibre and similar to Polynesian tapa cloth. It reached above the knees and often had decorative patterns.

*Tapa cloth* — The source uses Polynesian tapa cloth as a comparison for the Alune bark-fibre loincloth. It does not provide a separate Alune term, production sequence, or documented distinction beyond the material and form of the garment.

The sources used do not document additional Alune textile traditions, named weaving techniques, natural dyes, or further pattern-bearing cloths.

### Clothing & dress

In daily life, Alune adults traditionally wore a short bark-fibre loincloth from the waist down. The garment reached above the knees and often carried decorative patterns. The sources describe little clothing for both men and women, attributing this to the humid environment.

On special occasions, Alune men wore elaborate warrior clothes and carried long swords. The sources do not give a specific name for this clothing, nor do they describe head coverings, belts, footwear, or separate ceremonial garments for women. Women wore necklaces, but the sources do not identify a vernacular name, design, or ritual use for them. Women also spent much of the day collecting products from the forest, and children often accompanied them.

### Architecture

The ancestral Alune houses were large and elaborate. They were built of wood, sticks, and palm leaves. The sources do not provide a vernacular name for the houses or describe their roof form, internal arrangement, foundations, decorative system, or settlement layout. They also do not document later urban architecture or named building types.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum records show objects associated with Alune material culture, including a ladle for preparation of palm sugar made of coconut shell, wood, and rattan; tongs made of wood; a strainer for preparation of palm sugar made of bamboo; another palm-sugar ladle made of bamboo, wood, and rattan; and a slit gong made of bamboo. The catalogue also records a traded bracelet made of metal and a female’s bracelet made of shell. The sources do not document Alune ceramics or provide vernacular names for these objects.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources state that Alune women wore necklaces. The museum catalogue records a female’s bracelet made of shell and a traded bracelet made of metal. No source gives names for these jewelry types, or describes their designs, production methods, exchange contexts, ritual functions, tattoos, hair practices, or other forms of body adornment.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources state that the coming-of-age celebration for Alune girls was an important occasion. They do not provide a name for this celebration, its age threshold, sequence of rites, participants, dress, food, songs, or season.

## Foodways

Alune traditional subsistence depended on forest products. Their diet was based on the sago palm, and they also practiced shifting cultivation. The museum catalogue records a ladle, tongs, and strainer used in the preparation of palm sugar. These objects were made of coconut shell, wood, rattan, bamboo, or combinations of those materials.

The sources do not name an Alune dish or beverage, and do not describe cooking methods beyond the catalogue references to palm-sugar preparation. They do not document papeda, dietary rules, ceremonial foods, tea or coffee traditions, preservation methods, or food served at life-cycle occasions.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Hainuwele legend is an origin myth from Alune and Wemale folklore. It was recorded by the German ethnologist Adolf Ellegard Jensen during a 1937–8 expedition to the Maluku Islands. The sources do not provide the narrative text, its performance context, named characters beyond the title, or a classification of its episodes.

The available sources do not document Alune epic poetry, proverbs, riddles, storytelling occasions, contemporary literature, or preservation projects. They also do not explain how the Hainuwele legend is transmitted in present-day Alune communities.

## Language & religion

Alune is an Austronesian language of west Seram in the Maluku archipelago of Indonesia and is described in the sources as a language of Malayo-Polynesian origin. It is also known as Sapalewa or Patasiwa Alfoeren. The language has a few dialects, and the Rambatu speech variety is identified as the most prestigious.

The sources describe two phonological features: /d/ can be heard as a trill [r] in word-initial and intervocalic positions and may fluctuate to an affricate [dʒ]; /n/ can be heard as a velar nasal [ŋ] before velar stops. The sources do not document a historical script, religious affiliation, sacred vocabulary, or specific spiritual practices. They state only that political and religious restlessness, together with resulting conflict in Indonesia, affected islands of the Maluku area.
