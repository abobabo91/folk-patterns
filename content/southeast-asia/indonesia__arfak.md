## Overview

The Arfak people are an ethnic group inhabiting the Arfak Mountains on the Bird's Head Peninsula in West Papua, Indonesia. Their territory lies in Pegunungan Arfak Regency, at an altitude of 2,950 metres (9,680 ft) above sea level. The mountains border Manokwari Regency to the north, South Manokwari Regency to the east, Teluk Bintuni Regency to the south, and South Sorong Regency to the west. Pegunungan Arfak Regency can be reached through Manokwari Regency or South Manokwari Regency. The Arfak are divided into four sub-groups—Hatam, Meyah, Sougb, and Moile—with distinct languages and territorial divisions. Their documented cultural forms include stilt houses, bows and arrows, dances, songs, clan organization, hunting, and traditional healing.

## Material culture

### Architecture

**Thousand-legs house** — Arfak traditional stilt houses have walls made of wood, roofs made of straw, pandan or sago leaves, and floors made of bamboo or nibung splits. They are commonly called “thousand-legs house,” or *Rumah kaki seribu*, and have different names in the languages of the sub-groups: *mod aki aksa* in Meyah, *igmam* in Hattam, and *tu misen* in Sougb.

The house is both a dwelling and a venue for cultural events. Its upper part is used for living and household activities, while the lower part is used for livestock and firewood storage.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do identify the bow and arrow as weapons used among the Arfak and their four sub-groups. The complete bow and arrow set is called *inyomus* in Sougb and *inyomusi* in Irai village.

## Music & performance

They do state that the Tumbu Tanah Dance is accompanied by songs telling stories of plants, farming, the history of the Arfak people, beliefs, and past wars.

Songs therefore appear in the available material primarily as part of dance performance. The documented subject matter connects performance with agriculture, the natural environment, collective history, beliefs, and warfare.

## Dance & theatre

**Tumbu Tanah Dance** is performed by all four Arfak sub-groups. It has two names according to location: when performed on streets it is called *Tumbuk Tanah*, and when performed near houses it is called *Tumbuk Rumah*. Its accompanying songs tell stories of plants, farming, the history of the Arfak people, beliefs, and past wars.

**Magasa Dance** is also known to outsiders as the Snake Dance. Its name refers to winding, snake-like movements that follow the lyrics of the song. It is performed during weddings, harvest season, and the welcoming of important guests. Men and women dance in pairs, holding hands, jumping, and stomping. The dance tells stories of romance, heroism, and nature’s beauty.

**Red Fruit Dance** is performed only by Arfak youths and represents the beauty of the land.

## Festivals & rituals

They do identify several performance contexts. The Magasa Dance is performed during weddings, harvest season, and the welcoming of important guests. These occasions connect dance with marriage, agricultural seasonality, and public hospitality.

The sources also state that songs accompanying the Tumbu Tanah Dance tell of plants, farming, Arfak history, beliefs, and past wars. The Red Fruit Dance is performed by Arfak youths and represents the beauty of the land. The material does not describe birth rites, coming-of-age ceremonies, funerals, initiation rituals, marriage procedures, sacrificial practices, or named annual festivals.

## Foodways

They do state that the Arfak are skilled hunters and traditional healers, and that the Meyah language distinguishes food growing underground from food growing on trees and vines in its noun-class system.

## Oral tradition & literature

The available material describes oral subject matter within Arfak dance performance rather than a separate literary genre. Songs accompanying the Tumbu Tanah Dance tell stories of plants, farming, the history of the Arfak people, beliefs, and past wars. The Magasa Dance tells stories of romance, heroism, and nature’s beauty through song and movement.

## Language & religion

The Arfak Mountains contain four sub-groups with similar cultures: Hatam, Meyah, Sougb, and Moile. Each group speaks a different language, and the groups cannot communicate with one another in their native tongues. Hatam and Moile share some grammatical similarities with the main Arfak language.

Meyah, also called Meyakh, is a West Papuan language spoken in North Manokwari District, Manokwari Regency, West Papua, Indonesia. It is agglutinative and head-marking, has no grammatical cases, and uses subject-verb-object word order. Meyah has five vowels and thirteen consonants and uses two phonemic pitch levels: rising high and falling high. Sougb is described as the only other known pitch-accent language in the Bird’s Head Peninsula.

The sources mention beliefs and practitioners of black magic.
