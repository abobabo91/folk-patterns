## Overview

The Penan are a nomadic Indigenous people living in Sarawak and Brunei, with only one small community in Brunei. The Penan number around 16,000, although only approximately 200 still live nomadically. They are divided into two loosely related geographical groups: Eastern Penan, living around the Miri, Baram, Limbang and Tutoh regions, and Western Penan, living in and around Belaga district. Their language belongs to the Kenyah subgroup within the Malayo-Polynesian branch of the Austronesian language family; Penan is also known as Punan-Nibong. In government censuses they are broadly classified as Orang Ulu, translated as “Upriver People,” and more broadly as Dayak. Their folk-culture significance in the sources lies in hunting and gathering, forest knowledge, the principle of *molong*, blowpipe technology, basketry, mats, textiles, and continuing resistance to deforestation.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Woven rattan mat** — A British Museum catalogue record describes a rectangular mat woven of rattan, with two opposing corners left undyed and two dyed dark. Alternating “bird” and “blow-pipe” or “moon” patterns run across its length.

**Rattan basketry** — The museum catalogue records baskets made of rattan and nylon, as well as a basket made of rattan and string. The sources identify these as objects associated with Penan material culture.

**Rattan strap** — A strap made of rattan is recorded among the museum objects.

**Penan fabric** — A Museum of World Culture record describes a textile dated 1957, made from bast from *Musa textilis*. It uses shades of brown, grey, black and blue-black and consists of two sewn pieces. The record identifies it with the eastern Penan.

**Rattan objects** — The catalogue includes several objects made from rattan, including baskets, a mat, a strap, parang sheaths, parang handles and bindings.

**Motif vocabulary.** “Bird” pattern, “blow-pipe” pattern, “moon” pattern.

### Clothing & dress

The Penan use animal hides, skin, fur and other animal parts for clothing and shelter. A British Museum record describes a knife handle ending in a red tuft of hair or red-dyed fibre. A poem quoted in the Wikipedia article refers to people “clothed in Chawats [loincloths],”.

### Architecture

Penan communities were predominantly nomadic up until the 1950s. Since 1950, state government programmes and foreign Christian missionaries have encouraged settlement in longhouse-based villages similar to those of other Indigenous groups in Sarawak. The sources also mention shelters in the forest: a British Museum glass negative shows Penan men resting in a shelter in Borneo.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Penan use a *kelepud*, or blowpipe, for hunting. The Wikipedia source describes it as made from the Belian Tree and bored with a bone drill; the wood is not split, so the bore must be precise over a distance of 3 metres. Its darts are made from the sago palm and tipped with poisonous latex from the Tajem tree, identified in the source as *Antiaris toxicaria*. Museum records include a sheath containing ninety-seven blow-pipe darts with sharpened and poisoned tips, three darts with poisoned tips and pith at their ends, three arrows with metal heads coated with poison, and a metal spear-head for a blow-pipe.

Other recorded objects include a parang made of metal, wood, rattan and gum; a bush knife called *tongol*, with a wooden handle, rattan binding, metal blade and tree latex called *mat*; parang sheaths made of wood and rattan; parang handles made of wood and rattan; and a knife with a metal blade inserted into a long bone handle.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The museum records mention a red tuft of hair or red-dyed fibre attached to a knife handle.

## Music & performance

The sources mention the Penan language, customs and survival skills in connection with Bruno Manser, but provide no account of musical practice.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources state that Penan burial sites were destroyed by logging and that plants and animals could be viewed as sacred, as embodiments of powerful spirits and deities. The originally animistic hunter-gatherer Penan were Christianised by missionaries of the Borneo Evangelical Mission; many were baptised and settled. In Brunei, half of the Penan community have been converted to Islam, even if only superficially.

## Foodways

Penan foodways are closely connected with hunting, gathering and the forest. The Penan eat plants, which are also used as medicines, as well as animals. Their diet includes sago, described as starch from the sago palm, jungle fruits and hunted animals. The listed prey include wild boar, barking deer, mouse deer, snakes—especially the reticulated python or *kemanen*—monkeys, birds, frogs, monitor lizards, snails and insects such as locusts.

The principle of *molong* means never taking more than necessary. The source states that this practice places little strain on the forest, on which Penan communities rely for what they need. Everything caught is shared in what the article describes as a highly tolerant, generous and egalitarian society. The sources state that nomadic Penan are said to have no word for “thank you” because help is assumed; *jian kenin*, meaning “feel good,” is typically used in settled communities as an equivalent.

Some younger generations cultivate rice and garden vegetables, while many people continue to rely on forest foods.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources record verbal and written complaints made by Penan communities to logging companies and local government officials, as well as the 2002 Long Sayan Declaration, in which the Penan outlined their wants and requirements to the Sarawak State Government.

The Penan struggle against logging has also been communicated through testimony and public advocacy. A Penan spokesman is quoted describing the forest as the community’s money and bank because it provides food. Bruno Manser learned the Penan language, survival skills and customs during six years living with the Penan and helped communicate their cause to the outside world. The sources also mention the documentary *Rumah Nor* and Bruce Parry’s documentary *Tawai: a Voice From The Forest*.

## Language & religion

Penan belongs to the Kenyah subgroup within the Malayo-Polynesian branch of the Austronesian language family. The Penan language is also known as Punan-Nibong and is described as a language complex spoken by the Penan people of Borneo. The Penan languages are related to the Kenyah languages; the source states that Western Penan is closer to Sebop than to Eastern Penan.

The originally animistic hunter-gatherer Penan were Christianised by missionaries of the Borneo Evangelical Mission, baptised and settled. In Brunei there is only one small Penan community, and half of those Penan have converted to Islam, even if only superficially. The sources also state that plants and animals may be viewed as sacred, as embodiments of powerful spirits and deities.
