## Overview

The Tai Dam are an ethnic minority predominantly from China, northwest Vietnam, Laos and Thailand. They are part of the Tai peoples and are ethnically similar to Thai, Lao and Shan communities. In Vietnam they are called Thái Đen and are included among the Thái people, alongside Thái Đỏ, Thái Trắng, Phu Thai, Tày Thanh and Thái Hàng Tổng. The group of Thái peoples is the third largest of Vietnam’s 54 recognized ethnic groups. Tai Dam means “Black Tai”, referring to the black clothing especially worn by women. Their language is a Tai language similar to Lao, but it has its own writing system. Tai Dam culture includes wet-rice cultivation, distinctive black clothing, spirit worship, oral traditions, named marriage and funeral rites, and the xoe dance.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Black silk clothing** — Tai Dam women wear traditional black skirts and headdresses. The black silk is embroidered with flowers and beautiful patterns, and the belt is typically bright green.

**Pieu shawl** — The *pieu* is a shawl worn by Black Tai women, distinguished in the source by its colorful embroidery.

**Tube-shaped black skirts** — Women’s blouses fit with tube-shaped black skirts. The source identifies the skirts as part of the characteristic dress associated with Tai Dam women.

**Silver-button blouses** — Tai women wear short, colorful blouses decorated down the front with lines of silver buttons shaped like butterflies, spiders and cicadas.

**Green silk belts** — The belt is a green-colored silk band worn with women’s clothing.

**Cloth weaving** — Tai Dam cultivate cotton, indigo and mulberry as subsidiary crops, especially for cloth weaving.

**Motif vocabulary.** Butterflies, spiders and cicadas are named as shapes used for silver buttons; flowers and beautiful patterns are named as embroidery designs.

### Clothing & dress

Tai women wear short, colorful blouses with lines of silver buttons down the front. The buttons are shaped like butterflies, spiders and cicadas. These blouses are worn with tube-shaped black skirts and a green-colored silk belt. Women also wear a key chain around the waist. On festival occasions, they can wear an additional black dress with an underarm seam or a pullover form with an open collar that reveals the silver buttons. These dresses are nipped at the waist and have large shoulders and decorative cloth attached at the underarms or the front of the shoulders. Black Tai women wear the embroidered *pieu* shawl.

Tai men wear shorts with a belt and a shirt with an open collar and two pockets. At festivals, people wear long black dresses with split underarm seams and an internal white blouse. A head turban is worn as a headdress; in ceremonies, it should be the length of an arm.

### Architecture

Tai houses are stilt houses with several roof designs. One form has a round, convex roof resembling a turtle shell, with two ends, and is called *khau cut*. Other houses have a four-paneled roof, a rectangular floor and corridors. Another form has a long, high roof, with rooms at either end used as halls. A fourth form has a low roof and narrow interior and is close to the Muong house style.

The source also describes rites connected with a new house. The *Lung Ta* shows the host the new house and kindles a new fire. People perform spiritual rites at the site, read spiritual texts to drive away bad luck and bring good luck, and worship ancestors.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do describe bamboo objects and household equipment: sticky rice is cooked in a steaming pot, rice is fed to a mother using a bamboo tube, and the placenta is placed in a bamboo cylinder and hung on a branch in the forest. Baskets, back carriers and tump lines are used for carrying goods. Tai smoke with bamboo pipes lit by dried bamboo pieces.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The dead are cremated with gold and silver jewelry. After the family finds the burial place, the ashes are sifted with water and the melted jewelry is picked out as keepsakes before the ashes are buried. Women wear key chains around their waists, and Tai women traditionally wear their hair in a bun or chignon after the first wedding ceremony.

## Music & performance

Tai Dam perform the *xoe* dance, play many kinds of flutes, sing verses and perform vivid alternate songs.

Music and performance are presented as part of Tai Dam artistic activity rather than through a separately described professional or court tradition.

## Dance & theatre

The Tai Dam perform the *xoe* dance.

## Festivals & rituals

The Black Tai worship their ancestors during the seventh and eighth months of the Lunar Year. White Tai communities also celebrate the new year according to the lunar calendar. Villagers worship the gods of the land, mountain and water, as well as the soul of the central post of the village. The Tai calendar follows an ancient horoscope or cosmology containing 12 key animals, like the lunar calendar, while the Black Tai calendar has a time difference of six months.

Funerals have two stages. *Pong* involves bringing offerings to the deceased and taking the deceased to the forest for burial among White Tai or cremation among Black Tai. *Xong* calls the spirit back to live in the part of the house reserved for ancestor worship. Tai Dam wear white at funerals as a sign of grief. Coins are thrown into the crowd after the funeral and before cremation. Food including a pig and fruit is often placed before the headstone.

Pregnant women are not allowed to attend funerals because Tai Dam believe spirits may infiltrate the womb and be born through the fetus. Family members are expected to cry, and women are asked to scream loudly. Until after the funeral, mourners cannot take a full shower or bath. They also cannot attend or hold parties such as weddings and graduations for up to one year.

Marriage traditionally involved the selling and buying of marriage and the son-in-law’s residence with the bride’s family. In *up marriage* (*dong khun*), the son-in-law is introduced and brought to live with the girl’s family to test his personality and hard work. He remains at his wife’s home for 8 to 12 years. *Down marriage* (*dong long*) brings the couple and their family to the paternal family.

Women give birth in a seated position. The mother is warmed by a fire, fed rice through a bamboo tube and required to abstain from certain foods for a month. Rituals teach the child gender-specific work, and a *Lung Ta* is invited to name the baby.

## Foodways

Ordinary rice is now the main food of the Tai, while sticky rice continues to be eaten traditionally. Sticky rice is steeped in water, placed in a steaming pot and cooked over a fire. A meal is described as incomplete without ground chili mixed with salt and accompanied by mint, coriander leaves and onion.

Boiled chicken liver, fish gut and smoked fish called *cheo* may be added to a meal. Ruminant meat is accompanied by sauce made from internal organs called *nam pia*. Raw fish is cooked into salad or meat-in-sauce, or simply salted or sauced. Cooking methods include roasting, steaming, drying, condensing, frying and boiling.

Tai food favors hot, salty, acrid and buttery tastes rather than sweet, rich and strong tastes. Hospitality is associated with smoking: people smoke with bamboo pipes lit by dried bamboo pieces and invite others to join before smoking, as they do before a meal.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Tai have many ancient written works concerning their history, traditions, customary laws and literature, although their language is also taught orally.

Tai Dam creation tradition states that the Lo Cam family is the ruling class and the Luong are the priests.

## Language & religion

Tai Dam is a Tai language spoken in Vietnam, China, Laos and Thailand. It is similar to Thai and Lao, including Isan, but is not close enough to be readily understood by most Thai and Lao speakers. Khmer, Pali and Sanskrit additions found in Thai and Lao are largely missing from Tai Dam.

The Tai have their own Brahmic writing system. Tai Dam writing is called Tai Viet and consists of 31 consonants and 14 vowels. The language is tonal. Tone markers emerged in the 1970s in two sets, although the less popular set is described as using modifiers like New Tai Lue or Tai Nuea. In Vietnam, all Tai peoples are taught a standardized Tai language based on the Tai Dam language, using the standardized Tai Viet script.

Tai Dam traditionally rejected Buddhism. Their religion consists of spirit worship, with ancestral spirits especially important. Women maintain altars to deceased parents, and rituals connected with houses, funerals, births and village worship recognize ancestors, land, mountain, water and the soul of the village’s central post.
