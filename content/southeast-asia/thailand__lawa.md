## Overview

The Lawa are an ethnic group in northern Thailand whose population is estimated to be some 17,000. They speak Lawa, an Austroasiatic language belonging to the Palaungic branch. The Western Lawa live in the vicinity of Mae Sariang in the south of Mae Hong Son Province, while the Eastern Lawa are centred on Bo Luang in Chiang Mai Province. The Lawa are sometimes confused with the Lua of northern Laos and Nan Province, Thailand, whose languages belong to the more distantly related Khmuic group. The Eastern Lawa often prefer to be called Lua by outsiders, while Thai usage applies Lua to speakers of different Palaungic languages. Lawa communities that have not been integrated into mainstream Thai society continue a traditional way of life and often profess animism. They are particularly known for ironsmithing.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Historical accounts place the Lawa among the original inhabitants of Northern Thailand, before the Tai migration into these lands. There is evidence that they inhabited cities before the arrival of Tai peoples. Chiang Mai was founded on the location of a 5th-century CE Lawa walled city. Northern Thai legends also connect the Lawa with the founding of other cities. The Doi Tung story attributes the foundation of Ngoenyang, in the 7th century CE, to the Lawa, and Mangrai was described as a prince of Ngoenyang before establishing the Lanna Kingdom in the 13th century.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Lawa are described as having extraordinary craft skills, especially as ironsmiths.

## Festivals & rituals

Those Lawa who have not been integrated into mainstream Thai society are described as often professing animism. Northern Thai legends connect the Lawa with urban foundations and political alliances. In the account given by the 15th-century book *Cāmadevivaṃsa*, the Mon queen Camadevi established Haripunchai in the 7th century CE, and Vilanga, king of the Lawa, attacked her with 80,000 soldiers. After his defeat, she married her two sons to the two daughters of the Lawa king, and the two kingdoms became allies. The Doi Tung story likewise attributes the foundation of Ngoenyang to the Lawa in the 7th century CE.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Lawa appear in northern Thai legends, mainly in connection with the founding of cities. The 15th-century *Cāmadevivaṃsa*, written by the Chiang Mai monk Bodhiramsi, relates that Camadevi, a princess of the Lavo Kingdom, established Haripunchai in the 7th century CE. Vilanga, king of the Lawa, attacked her with 80,000 soldiers; after his defeat, political ties were established through the marriage of her two sons to the two daughters of the Lawa king. The Doi Tung story attributes the foundation of Ngoenyang to the Lawa.

## Language & religion

Lawa is an Austroasiatic language in the Palaungic branch. Lawa and Wa are sister groups within the Waic subbranch of the Austroasiatic language family, and Lawa is related to Blang and the Wa language of China and Burma. There are two distinct varieties, Eastern Lawa and Western Lawa, which are considered separate languages and are not mutually understandable according to testimonies from Eastern and Western Lawa speakers and testing by SIL. Eastern Lawa has dialects associated with Bo Luang and Bo Sangae; Phalok is also described as a dialect of Eastern Lawa and has been extinct since the 1950s according to the UNESCO Atlas of the World's Languages in Danger. Eastern Lawa is spoken in the home by all ages, while government education, village notices, and official business are usually conducted in Central Thai. Most Eastern Lawa are bilingual with at least Northern Thai. The sources describe animism among Lawa who have not been integrated into mainstream Thai society.
