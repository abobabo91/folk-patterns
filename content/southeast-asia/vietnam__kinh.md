## Overview

The Kinh are Vietnam's dominant ethnic group, concentrated in river deltas and coastal plains, who gradually expanded southward from the 11th century. They carry most of Vietnam's intangible cultural heritage, from imperial court music to village festivals and Mother Goddess worship. Their material culture — textiles, prints, lacquer, ceramics — and performing arts (water puppetry, classical opera, folk music) are recognized globally. The Kinh blend Buddhist, Confucian, Daoist, and animist beliefs into a layered religious life centered on ancestor altars and seasonal festivals.

## Material culture

Kinh material culture emphasizes fine textiles, woodblock prints, and ceramics made in village workshops that have operated for centuries.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Silk weaving, brocade, woodblock prints, and lacquer painting preserve Kinh pattern tradition in formal and folk registers.

- **Vạn Phúc silk** (*lụa Vạn Phúc*) — Fine mulberry silk woven in Hanoi's Hà Đông district for over a thousand years, traditionally supplying the imperial court with damasks whose patterns show only in raking light.
- **Brocade** (*gấm*) — Dense figurative panels of dragons, phoenixes, and clouds woven with continuous silk on satin ground, used for mandarin robes and áo dài trim.
- **Đông Hồ woodblock prints** (*tranh Đông Hồ*) — Folk New Year prints from Bắc Ninh province stamped from jackfruit-wood blocks onto seashell-coated paper, with mineral and plant pigments depicting village scenes like jealousy, pig families, and wrestlers.
- **Hàng Trống prints** (*tranh Hàng Trống*) — Hanoi Old Quarter prints using a single outline block with hand-brushed watercolor washes, showing Taoist and Buddhist altar images in a more subdued palette than Đông Hồ.
- **Vietnamese lacquer painting** (*sơn mài*) — Layers of tree resin with crushed eggshell, mother-of-pearl inlay, and gold or silver leaf, sanded to reveal jewel-like images; developed in the 1930s at the Indochina Fine Arts School.

### Clothing & dress

Pre-modern everyday dress was simple, while the fitted áo dài tunic evolved from 18th-century court reforms and carries embroidered or patterned panels.

- **Áo dài** — Tunic-and-trouser ensemble with painted, embroidered, or brocaded front and back skirt panels, increasingly tailored close to the body since the 1930s redesign.
- **Five-panel tunic** (*áo ngũ thân*) — Developed in the 18th century under the Nguyễn lords in Huế, direct ancestor of the modern fitted áo dài, worn by both sexes with a pre-shaped silk turban.
- **Halter bib** (*yếm*) — Traditional women's garment worn under a four-panel tunic in everyday pre-modern dress.
- **Conical hat** (*nón lá*) — Palm-leaf hat with concentric bamboo hoops and stitched leaves; the Huế variant nón bài thơ is inlaid with poetry.
- **Wide flat hat with chin-straps** (*nón quai thao*) — Characteristic hat of Quan Họ singers in Bắc Ninh, featuring long silk chin-straps.

### Architecture

Village and domestic architecture centers on timber-frame structures with tile roofs and dragon finials; Hanoi preserves narrow shophouses called tube houses.

- **Communal house** (*đình*) — The walled village's central structure on wooden columns with swooping tile roof terminating in dragon finials or makara pairs, housing the tutelary spirit and hosting festivals.
- **Domestic house** — Three-to-five-bay single-storey structure with low hipped or gable-and-hip tile roof, raised brick threshold, open central bay with ancestor altar, and side storage rooms; courtyard used for drying rice.
- **Aristocratic mansion** (*nhà rường*) — Huế refinement of domestic form featuring elaborately joined jackwood columns and no metal fasteners.
- **Tube house** (*nhà ống*) — Hanoi's Old Quarter shophouses with 2–5 metre street frontage running dozens of metres deep, with internal light-wells and street-level shop.
- **Imperial citadel of Huế** (*Kinh Thành*) — Paradigmatic Kinh monumental complex (1805–32) laid out on Vauban lines around the Purple Forbidden City.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Kinh ceramics are anchored by centuries-old village kilns producing blue-and-white ware, celadons, and salt-glazed storage jars; bronze foundries cast ritual vessels and bells.

- **Bát Tràng stoneware** (*gốm hoa lam*) — Blue-and-white dishes and altar sets produced east of Hanoi since the 14th century, alongside celadons and a distinctive crackled cream glaze.
- **Chu Đậu ware** — Elegant 15th-century underglaze-blue vessels revived after long dormancy, found in the Hội An shipwreck.
- **Storage jars** — Salt-glazed brown jars from Phù Lãng and Thổ Hà kilns used for pickling and fish sauce fermentation.
- **Bronze ritual vessels** — Bells, incense burners, ritual gongs, and Đông Sơn drum reproductions cast in Đại Bái and Ngũ Xã villages.
- **Brass meal tray** (*mâm đồng*) — Round brass tray that carries a meal, a recognizable everyday object with strong identity content.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Kinh jewelry is restrained compared to highland minorities, featuring gold and jade bangles, ear-hoops, and neck-rings; tooth-blackening was historically universal among women.

- **Jade and gold bracelet** (*vòng*) — Bangles given at weddings and passed matrilineally through generations.
- **Gold ear-hoops** (*khuyên*) — Traditional earrings worn as a form of personal adornment.
- **Rigid hinged neck-ring** (*kiềng*) — Twisted gold neck-ring worn by brides.
- **Jade pendant** (*hồ lô*) — Gourd-shaped jade worn on a neck-chain, often paired with Buddha pendants.
- **Tooth-blackening** (*nhuộm răng đen*) — Iron-and-lacquer varnish applied to teeth, universal among northern women until the early 20th century, marking adult beauty and maturity.

## Music & performance

Kinh music spans from imperial court ensembles (UNESCO-inscribed nhã nhạc) to chamber genres and folk songs, using fiddles, lutes, zithers, bamboo flutes, and drums.

- **Court music** (*nhã nhạc*) — UNESCO-inscribed elegant music of the Nguyễn court at Huế using ensembles of two-string fiddles, moon-shaped lutes, pear-shaped lutes, 16- or 17-string zithers, bamboo flutes, and drums for ceremonial occasions.
- **Chamber vocal genre** (*ca trù*) — Northern vocal tradition on UNESCO's Urgent Safeguarding List, performed by a female singer with wooden clapper, a male lute player, and a connoisseur audience member on a praise drum.
- **Male-female choral alternation** (*quan họ Bắc Ninh*) — Unaccompanied male and female choir responses at village gatherings in Bắc Ninh.
- **Ritual spring song** (*hát xoan Phú Thọ*) — Associated with the Hùng King cult, sung during spring communal-house rites.
- **Folk-music-based theatre** (*cải lương*) — Modern melodramatic theatre emerging in the 1910s Mekong Delta, set to chamber-music accompaniment.

## Dance & theatre

Kinh theatrical genres include comic opera (hát chèo), classical opera modeled on Chinese forms (hát tuồng), and internationally recognized water puppetry (múa rối nước).

- **Village comic opera** (*hát chèo*) — Northern village theatre performed on a mat in the communal-house courtyard, featuring stock clown characters and satirical songs.
- **Classical opera** (*hát tuồng*) — Southern-and-central opera loosely modeled on Chinese jingju, using painted faces coded to character type, stylized battle choreography, and Confucian loyalty plots.
- **Water puppetry** (*múa rối nước*) — Carved wooden puppets manipulated by bamboo rods from behind a screen across a flooded stage, dramatizing rural episodes like fishing and buffalo-fighting to comic-opera music.
- **Court ritual dance** (*múa cung đình*) — Formal dance performed at the imperial court.
- **Saint Gióng festival pageant** (*Gióng*) — UNESCO-inscribed large-scale processional pageant enacting the martial legend of Saint Gióng at Phù Đổng and Sóc temples.

## Festivals & rituals

The lunisolar calendar brings Tết Nguyên Đán (Lunar New Year), pilgrimage to the Hùng Kings, mid-autumn moon festival, and village lễ hội with processions, wrestling, and tugging rites; life centers on the ancestor altar.

- **Lunar New Year** (*Tết Nguyên Đán*) — Paramount festival marked by cleaning ancestor altars, offering the five-fruit tray, cooking square and cylindrical rice cakes, giving red envelopes, and welcoming kitchen gods from their annual report to the Jade Emperor.
- **Death Anniversary of the Hùng Kings** — National pilgrimage (10th day, 3rd lunar month) to Phú Thọ province honoring semi-legendary founders of the Vietnamese state, UNESCO-inscribed 2012.
- **Mid-autumn moon festival** (*Tết Trung Thu*) — Festival giving children lion-dances, lantern processions, and mooncakes.
- **Death-anniversary meal** (*giỗ*) — Centerpiece of Kinh life-cycle rites, offering food to the deceased at the ancestor altar and inviting their return.
- **Mother Goddess spirit-possession rite** (*lên đồng*) — UNESCO-inscribed ritual summoning deities of the Three Realms (Heaven, Water, Mountains-and-Forests) through elaborately costumed mediumship in the Đạo Mẫu cult.

## Foodways

The Kinh table is built on wet rice, fermented fish sauce, fresh herbs, and light oil, with sharp regional differences: northern phở, central Huế spice, southern coconut sweets.

- **Beef noodle soup** (*phở*) — Hanoi's clear anise-and-ginger broth noodle soup, a northern signature dish.
- **Turmeric-dill fried fish** (*chả cá Lã Vọng*) — Hanoi specialty of turmeric-marinated fried fish from a named restaurant.
- **Lemongrass beef noodles** (*bún bò Huế*) — Spicy central specialty from the imperial city of Huế.
- **Square glutinous rice cake** (*bánh chưng*) — Glutinous rice, mung bean, and pork wrapped in đông leaves, tied with bamboo strips, and boiled for twelve hours; commemorates a legendary prince's invention.
- **Fish sauce** (*nước mắm*) — Fermented cornerstone of Kinh cooking, with best varieties from Phú Quốc and Phan Thiết.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kinh oral tradition preserves origin myths, hero legends, and folktales; the literary summit is a 3,254-line verse novel known by heart by many Kinh.

- **Dragon-lord and fairy origins** (*Lạc Long Quân and Âu Cơ*) — Origin myth of the dragon-lord and fairy whose hundred sons are ancestors of the Vietnamese.
- **Child hero legend** (*Thánh Gióng*) — Story of a boy who grew overnight to repel invaders, basis for Gióng festival pageants.
- **Star-crossed lovers legend** (*Ngưu Lang – Chức Nữ*) — Tale of a weaver-girl and cowherd, a constellation myth.
- **Vietnamese Cinderella** (*Tấm Cám*) — Folktale of a mistreated step-daughter who marries a prince.
- **Verse novel** (*Truyện Kiều*) — Nguyễn Du's 3,254-line verse novel composed around 1820 in six-eight meter, known by heart in whole passages by many Kinh and still used for random-book divination.

## Language & religion

Vietnamese is a tonal Austroasiatic language written in Latin alphabet since the 17th century; Kinh religion layers Buddhism, Confucian ancestor veneration, and Daoism over folk animism.

- **Vietnamese language** (*tiếng Việt*) — Austroasiatic tonal language (six tones in the north, five in the south) with 50–70% Chinese-derived lexicon, written in Latin alphabet developed by Jesuits and refined by Vietnamese scholars.
- **Demotic character script** (*chữ Nôm*) — Vietnamese script devised from the 10th century for vernacular writing, used for classical poetry and folktales before Latin-alphabet adoption.
- **Latin alphabet script** (*chữ Quốc ngữ*) — Latin alphabet developed by Jesuit missionaries (especially Alexandre de Rhodes) and refined by Vietnamese scholars since the 17th century.
- **Mother Goddess cult** (*Đạo Mẫu*) — Vivid ritual expression of Kinh folk religion, blending animism with Buddhism and Daoism through spirit-possession rites.
- **Folk religion** (*tín ngưỡng dân gian*) — Animist substrate underlying the Kinh layering of Buddhism, Confucianism, and Daoism.
