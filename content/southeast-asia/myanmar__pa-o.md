## Overview

The Pa'O are a Karenic language-speaking ethnic group primarily inhabiting eastern Myanmar, especially Shan State. They also reside in Kayin State, Kayah State, Mon State, and Bago Division, while many have fled to Mae Hong Son Province in northern Thailand because of ongoing military conflicts in Myanmar. The Pa'O are the second largest ethnic group in Shan State. Their language is Karenic and is spoken by close to 900,000 Pa'O people. Pa'O communities include Lowland Pa'O based in Thaton and Highland Pa'O based in Taunggyi, with as many as twenty-four subgroups. Their folk culture includes distinctive clothing, seasonal festivals, Buddhist ceremonies, marriage customs, funeral practices, music, and agricultural production.

### Clothing & dress

The Pa'O people of upper Myanmar commonly wear black or navy blue. Men’s traditional clothing consists of a turban, a white shirt, a black or navy jacket, and long black trousers. Women’s traditional clothing consists of five pieces: a blouse, a jacket, a longyi that covers the knees, a turban, and two large, conical-shaped hair pins. Both men and women pin a Pa'O flag badge to their jacket; the badge represents the Zawgyi and dragon from their origin story. Men use a large red sling bag to carry knives, hoes, or long choppers, while women use a cane or bamboo sling basket. Pa'O from lower Myanmar wear Burmese-style clothing.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention knives, hoes, long choppers, cane sling baskets, bamboo sling baskets, bamboo stands, and rockets. Rockets used in the *pwe lu-phaing* are currently made from iron and were originally made from bamboo.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 The Pa'O flag badge is worn by both men and women on the jacket.

## Music & performance

Khun Thar Doon (1940–1978) was one of the early recording stars of Pa'O music and established the first Pa'O modern band in the early 1970s. One of his famous songs is “Tee Ree Ree,” a song about Pa'O solidarity that is still sung at traditional festivals. The sources also name Lashio Thein Aung, Saing Saing Maw, and Khun Paw Yann among other artists who may appear in connection with the compilation *Guitars of the Golden Triangle: Folk and Pop Music of Myanmar (Burma), Vol. 2*, published by Sublime Frequencies. Some artists appearing on the compilation cover songs written by Khun Thar Doon.

## Festivals & rituals

The *pwe lu-phaing*, or Fire Rocket Festival, is celebrated from April to July to bring ample rain to villages during the planting season. According to Pa'O tradition, rockets are fired to help clouds make rain, and the village headman determines their size. The largest rockets can contain up to 20 kilograms of gunpowder and have a range of 5–6 miles. Before firing, a rocket is carried once around the local temple on someone’s shoulder. The festival also demonstrates unity and friendship among villages, which gather for one week. “Pwe” means festival, “Lue” means donation, and “Phaing” means to remove sins.

Pa'O National Day, or *Den See Lar Bway*, is celebrated on the full moon day of Tabaung, which falls in March. It commemorates ancestors and past leaders, including King Suriya Janthar, whose birthday is also celebrated on National Day. The day includes a grand parade through Taunggyi and a festival.

Most Pa'O festivals are based on Buddhist festival days. *Poy Sang Long* celebrates the initiation of young boys as novice monks, and ordination as a monk in adulthood is considered a family celebration. During Buddhist Lent, from August to October, Pa'O youth participate in the *Pwe Lip May Bo* ceremony. On monthly full-moon nights, new-moon nights, and both half-moon nights, they surround their local temple with lanterns suspended from strings raised by supporting bamboo stands. The stands carry the lanterns around the temple three times while the candles are lit, as a show of respect to Buddha.

Pa'O marriage customs include parents requesting a daughter’s hand in marriage and guests tying cotton threads around the wrists of the bridegroom and bride while blessing them. Funeral arrangements are handled by villagers; the body remains at home for a couple of nights, food is offered to monks, and card games provide constant companionship to the departed. After two or three days, the family praises the person who died. Common people are buried, while monks are cremated.

## Foodways

The Pa'O predominantly cultivate thanapet leaves, onions, garlic, chili, potatoes, rice, peanuts, beans, sesame seeds, mustard leaves, and green tea. At funerals, food is cooked and offered to monks.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Pa'O origin myth states that the Pa'O are descended from a shaman, or *weizza*, and a female dragon. The Pa'O flag badge represents the Zawgyi and dragon from this origin story.

## Language & religion

Pa'O is a Karenic language sometimes called Taungthu. It is primarily written using a Mon-Burmese script devised by Christian missionaries. The language has dialects associated with Taunggyi and Thaton: Highland or Northern Pa'O for Taunggyi, and Lowland or Southern Pa'O for Thaton. An additional dialect, the Huay Salop dialect, is found in Mae Hong Son province. The Taunggyi dialect has many borrowings from Shan, while the Thaton dialect has been more influenced by Burmese. Speakers of the two dialects can have difficulties with intelligibility and may switch to Burmese.

The majority of Pa'O people follow Buddhism. Some are Christian, and some maintain Animist beliefs. Buddhist practice shapes many festivals, including *Poy Sang Long* and *Pwe Lip May Bo*. The sources describe four phonemic tones in Pa'O: high, high falling, mid, and low.
