## Overview

The Bamar (ဗမာ, also Burmans) are a Tibeto-Burman-speaking people numbering around 35 million, forming roughly 69% of Myanmar's population and constituting its largest ethnic group. Their cultural heartland is the *Anya* ("upstream") region — the central dry zone along the upper Irrawaddy River encompassing Sagaing, Magwe, and Mandalay — with subsequent expansion into the Irrawaddy delta, Bago, Yangon, and Tanintharyi after the Toungoo (16th century) and Konbaung (18th–19th century) dynasties, and again under British colonial resettlement. Bamar identity coheres around the Burmese language and Theravada Buddhism, shaped by a millennium of continuous statecraft from the Pagan Empire (founded by King Anawrahta in the 1050s) onward. In folk-culture terms the Bamar matter as the transmitters of Pali-derived literary tradition, court music (*Mahāgīta*), lacquerware, silk *acheik* weaving, and the syncretic *nat* spirit cult that operates alongside orthodox Buddhism.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Acheik** — *acheik* (also *luntaya acheik*, "hundred-shuttle") is a silk tapestry-weave textile from the Amarapura–Mandalay region, distinguished by dense interlocking wave, meander, and arabesque patterns worked in multiple weft colours using dozens of small shuttles. Historically reserved for the Konbaung royal court, it clothed queens and princesses and now appears in wedding *htamein* (women's longyi).

**Longyi** — the *longyi*, the ankle-length wrapped tube skirt worn by both sexes, is the everyday textile canvas of Bamar life. Men's versions (*paso*) tend toward checks and plaids in cotton or silk; women's (*htamein*) carry finer figured weaves, including *acheik* for formal occasions. The modern form was popularised during the British colonial period, replacing the longer pre-colonial *paso* and *htamein*.

**Kalaga** — appliquéd and embroidered tapestries produced principally around Mandalay, layering velvet, felt, sequins, seed pearls, glass beads, and metallic thread over a cotton ground. Kalaga typically depict scenes from the Jātaka tales, the Ramayana (known locally as *Yama Zatdaw*), zodiac animals, and court processions; older pieces functioned as monastery hangings and royal partitions.

**Burmese lacquer (*yun*)** — although not a woven textile, Burmese lacquerware from Bagan and Kyaukka carries the same repertoire of patterned surface ornament. Layers of *thitsi* (sap of the *Melanorrhoea usitata* tree) are built up over bamboo or horsehair coils and then incised (*yun-de*) with fine linework filled with red, green, and yellow pigments. The engraved motifs form a shared pattern vocabulary with acheik and kalaga.

**Shan-influenced weaving** — Bamar households in Upper Myanmar have long absorbed Shan and Intha weaving traditions through the Mandalay and Inle textile trade, particularly cotton *pinni* jackets (a warm rust-brown handloom cloth) worn by monks and older men, and lotus-stem silk from Inle used in high-status monastic robes.

**Monastic robes (*thingan*)** — the ochre and maroon *thingan* worn by Theravada monks are cut and sewn according to the vinaya's prescribed patchwork pattern, mimicking a paddy field; their production and offering (particularly during *Kathina*) is itself a ritualised textile tradition.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Acheik-hlaing* (wave), *nagayit* (interlocking coils/naga scales), *kanote* (arabesque/scrollwork), *bilu-me* (ogre face), *pyinsa-rupa* (a composite auspicious animal), lotus (*kya*), hintha bird, *shwe-chi-hto* (gold-couched thread motifs on kalaga), zodiac twelve-animal cycle, and *padauk* (flame-of-the-forest blossom).

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress for both sexes centres on the *longyi*: men knot theirs at the front waist, women fold and tuck at the side. Men pair it with a plain shirt or, formally, with a collarless white *eingyi* under a *taikpon* — a fitted jacket comparable in cut to the Manchu *magua* — and a *gaung baung*, a stiff cloth turban with a projecting flap, historically colour-coded by rank. Women wear a fitted blouse (*eingyi*), traditionally sheer and paired with a bodice, over the *htamein*, with a sash (*yinzi*) for formal wear. Formal footwear for both sexes is the *gadiba phanat* (Mandalay velvet sandal), a thonged slipper with a velvet upper. Both women and children — and traditionally young unmarried men — apply *thanakha*, a pale yellow cosmetic paste ground on a stone slab from the bark of *Limonia acidissima* or *Naringi crenulata*, worn in circular patches or leaf-shaped designs on the cheeks as both sunscreen and adornment. Monastic dress consists of the three-part *thingan* set (lower, upper, and outer robe) in ochre or maroon.

### Architecture

Rural Bamar houses in the dry zone are timber post-and-beam structures raised on stilts, with plaited bamboo walls and steeply pitched thatch or corrugated-iron roofs; the space beneath is used for weaving, storage, and livestock. Interior organisation is governed by cardinal directions: the *yotaing*, the southwest main pillar, is treated as the seat of *Mahagiri*, the household guardian *nat*, and a coconut (*on-daw*) is hung there as his emblem. Villages cluster around the *kyaung* (monastery), the largest wooden building in most settlements — traditionally a multi-tiered pyatthat-roofed hall on massive teak posts, with elaborate pierced-wood eaves and gable boards. The tiered *pyatthat* roof (three, five, seven, or nine tiers, always odd) also crowns royal halls and shrine pavilions. Religious architecture is dominated by the *zedi* (stupa) and *pahto* (hollow temple), whose canonical forms were established at Bagan between the 11th and 13th centuries: the bell-shaped Sinhalese stupa, the corncob *sikhara*, and the terraced temple with a central shrine. Whitewashed or gilded stupas — the Shwedagon in Yangon being the paradigm — dominate every Bamar town's skyline. Colonial-era urban housing in Yangon fused Burmese teak carpentry with Anglo-Indian brick shophouses.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Utilitarian earthenware is produced at villages like **Kyaukmyaung** on the Irrawaddy, whose large glazed *Martaban*-style storage jars (called *pegu jars* in the Indian Ocean trade) once shipped oil, fish paste, and water across the Bay of Bengal. Metalwork traditions include cast bronze **Burmese opium weights** — small zoomorphic weights in the form of the *hintha* bird, *toe naya* (chinthe lion), or *pyinsa-rupa* — used across mainland Southeast Asia from the 15th to 19th centuries, and cast bronze temple bells with long inscribed dedications. Silver bowls (*ngwe kwet*) from Sagaing and Mandalay carry deep repoussé Jātaka scenes. Everyday objects that carry cultural identity include the round low lacquer table (*ok*), the tiered lacquer offering vessel (*hsun-ok*) used to bring rice to monks, the betel box (*kun-it*), and the *pat waing* drum-circle stands of hsaing-waing ensembles.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Traditional Bamar jewelry favours 22-karat gold and Mogok rubies, with signature forms including the *hsan zi* (multi-strand necklace), heavy gold hoop earrings, and gem-set rings; pearls from the Mergui archipelago feed a distinct pearl-jewelry tradition. Ear-boring (*nahtwin*) for girls is one of the Twelve Auspicious Rites and is marked by the gift of a first pair of studs, mirroring the *shinbyu* Buddhist novitiation for boys. Amuletic practice includes wearing gold or silver capsules containing inscribed Pali *in* diagrams (yantra squares) and small Buddha images; more esoteric *weizza* practitioners tattoo protective yantras (*let-hpwe* and *sak yant*-type designs shared with Thailand) on the body, and older men in rural Upper Myanmar historically bore full thigh tattoos in blue-black ink depicting geometric compartments filled with mythological animals. The daily cosmetic that defines Bamar bodies most visibly, however, remains *thanakha*.

## Music & performance

Bamar music divides into folk and classical branches. The classical repertoire, the ***Mahāgīta*** ("great song"), is the entire corpus of Burmese court music, transmitted since the Konbaung period and preserved today at the National University of Arts and Culture in Yangon and Mandalay. Its two main performance formats are the outdoor loud ensemble, ***hsaing waing***, and the indoor chamber ensemble. The hsaing waing is built around the *pat waing*, a circle of 21 tuned drums played by a seated musician at its centre, joined by the *kyi waing* (gong circle), *maung hsaing* (larger gong set), *hne* (a shawm), and *si* and *wa* (small bell and clapper for time-keeping). It accompanies theatre, spirit ceremonies, and pagoda festivals. The chamber ensemble centres on the ***saung gauk***, the arched harp with 13–16 silk strings — one of the oldest continuously played harp forms in the world, iconographically documented in Pyu reliefs from Sri Ksetra — accompanied by the *pattala* (bamboo xylophone), the *hsaing* small drum, and vocal declamation. Song genres within the Mahāgīta include the *kyo*, *bwe*, *thachingan*, and *patpyo* categories, each with fixed rhythmic and melodic patterns. Folk traditions include *than-gyat* verse-shouting performed during Thingyan (the New Year water festival) and lullabies and work songs of the paddy fields. Nat propitiation ceremonies (*nat pwe*) demand hsaing waing accompaniment and mediumship by *nat kadaw* spirit-wives.

## Dance & theatre

Bamar dance is codified in the *zat pwe*, an all-night theatrical form combining dance, drama, orchestral music, and comic interludes, and in the *anyeint*, a more intimate variety show pairing solo female dancers with clown-narrators (*lu shwin daw*). The oldest dramatic tradition is ***yoke thé***, the marionette theatre — a lacquered-headed string-puppet troupe of 28 canonical characters (king, prince, princess, hermit, ogre, horse, elephant, monkey, *nats*, and ministers) whose repertoire dramatises the *Zatdaw* (Jātaka) stories and *Yama Zatdaw* (the Burmese Ramayana). Yoke thé's conventions in turn shaped human dance: performers still trained today imitate the jerky articulation of the puppets. Court dance forms include the *duyin* and *byaw* solo dances and the *ozi* and *dobat* drum dances of village festivals. Masked and costumed dances feature in the *nat pwe*, where the *nat kadaw* medium becomes possessed and dances in the character of the invoked spirit. The *Yama Zatdaw*, adapted from the Ramayana via Thai and Mon transmission during the 18th century, is performed in dance-drama with distinctive stylised costumes derived from the Ayutthaya court.

## Festivals & rituals

The Bamar calendar is lunisolar and studded with monthly full-moon festivals. ***Thingyan***, the water-throwing New Year festival, falls in mid-April and lasts four to five days. The full moon of *Kason* (April–May) marks Vesak and the ceremonial watering of the Bodhi tree; *Waso* (June–July) begins the three-month Buddhist Lent (Vassa); *Thadingyut* (September–October) ends it with the Festival of Lights, when candles and paper lanterns commemorate the Buddha's descent from Tavatimsa heaven; *Tazaungmon* (October–November) hosts the Festival of Lights' second phase and the ***Kathina*** robe-offering ceremony, when new *thingan* are woven overnight in weaving competitions at pagodas such as the Shwedagon. Pagoda festivals (*paya pwe*) — the Kyaiktiyo, Shwedagon, and Ananda Temple festivals among them — punctuate the dry season. The Taungbyone Nat Festival in August, near Mandalay, is the largest annual gathering of the *nat kadaw* mediums honouring the Thirty-Seven Min pantheon. Life-cycle rites are structured by the ***Twelve Auspicious Rites*** (*mingala hsebani-ba*), of which the most publicly celebrated are the naming (*kin-bwe*), the first rice-feeding (*htamin-kyway*), ear-boring for girls (*nahtwin mingala*), the boys' novitiation as monks (*shinbyu*) — the single most important ceremony in a Bamar family's life, often combined with a girl's *nahtwin* — and the wedding (*mingala hsaung*).

## Foodways

White rice is the staple of every Bamar meal, joined by a *hin* (curry) of pork, chicken, mutton, or fish, cooked in a base of onion, garlic, ginger, turmeric, paprika, and generous groundnut oil until the oil separates (*si pyan*, "oil returns"). Meals are structured around the shared table with side dishes of *hin cho* (a light soup), boiled or blanched vegetables (*hin thee hin ywet*), fresh and pickled relishes, fried garnishes, and — anchoring everything — ***ngapi***, fermented shrimp or fish paste served as a raw dip (*ngapi yay*) or cooked into pounded chilli-fish relish (*ngapi kyaw*). The national dish is ***mohinga***, rice vermicelli in a lemongrass-and-catfish broth thickened with roasted chickpea flour and banana-pith, eaten for breakfast throughout the country. Other emblematic dishes include ***ohn no khao swè*** (chicken coconut noodles), ***mont di*** (Rakhine fish noodle), ***Shan khao swè*** (Shan noodles), and ***nangyi thoke*** (thick-noodle salad). ***Lahpet thoke***, a salad of fermented pickled tea leaves with fried garlic, peanuts, sesame, dried shrimp, and lime, is ceremonial: pickled tea historically sealed legal disputes and treaties. Sweet and savoury snacks are collectively called ***mont*** (fritters, coconut rice cakes, semolina cakes). Green tea (*lahpet yay gyan*) is drunk free of charge in every teashop; the imported black tea *lahpet yay* is drunk sweet with condensed milk. Communal food offerings — ***satuditha*** (open feasts) and *ahlu pwe* (merit-feasts) — are core to Bamar Buddhist life.

## Oral tradition & literature

Burmese literature has one of the longest continuous traditions in Southeast Asia, beginning with the quadrilingual **Myazedi inscription** of 1113 CE. The pre-colonial historical genre comprises the ***yazawin*** royal chronicles — the *Maha Yazawin*, the *Yazawin Thit*, and the *Hmannan Yazawin* (Glass Palace Chronicle) — and the ***ayedawbon***, campaign memoirs of specific kings. Verse traditions include the *pyo* (Jātaka-based religious verse), *eigyin* (historical cradle songs for royal children), *mawgun* (odes), and *yadu* (nature lyric). The 550 ***Zatdaw*** (Jātaka) tales, and above all the Ten Great Jātakas (*Zatgyi hse-bwe*), supply the plot bank for puppetry, dance-drama, and mural painting. The Burmese *Yama Zatdaw* is the local Ramayana. Folk narrative includes the cycle of tales around **Princess Thumbelina** (*Shwe Sa Ga*), trickster stories of the folk hero *Nga Pauk Si*, and hagiographies of the Thirty-Seven Nats collected in the *Mahagita Medani*. A 20th-century vernacular literary revival — the *Khit San* ("testing the times") movement of the 1930s at Rangoon University — reshaped Burmese prose into its modern form.

## Language & religion

Burmese belongs to the Lolo-Burmese branch of the Tibeto-Burman family and is a diglossic language with a literary high register (retaining Old Burmese grammatical particles) and a colloquial spoken form. Dialects include Standard Burmese (Yangon–Mandalay), *Arakanese* (which preserves the older /ɹ/), *Dawei* (Tavoyan), *Intha*, *Yaw*, and *Taungyo*. The Burmese script is an Indic *abugida* derived via Mon from the South Indian Pallava script, first securely attested in the Myazedi inscription (1113); it seeded the Shan, Ahom, Khamti, Karen, and Palaung scripts. Religiously, roughly 90% of Bamar are Theravada Buddhists, but this coexists with the veneration of the ***Thirty-Seven Min*** (nats), household guardian *Mahagiri*, esoteric *weizza* alchemical-tantric practice, veneration of Mahayana bodhisattvas (*Lawkanat* = Avalokiteśvara, Shin Upagutta, Shin Thiwali), and yadaya astrological rituals drawn from Hindu Saraswati-worship. Smaller Bamar communities are Muslim (historically called *Zerbadee*/*Pati*) or Christian.
