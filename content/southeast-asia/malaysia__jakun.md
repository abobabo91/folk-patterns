## Overview

The Jakun, also called Orang Ulu or Orang Hulu, are an ethnic group recognised as Orang Asli, the indigenous people of the Malay Peninsula in Malaysia. They are the largest subgroup of the Proto-Malay division and the second-largest Orang Asli subgroup overall. In 2010, their population was 31,577. Jakun settlements are concentrated in the southern region of the Malay Peninsula, especially the interior of southwest Pahang and north Johor. They live near jungle resources, including in areas around the Endau River, Lake Chini, Rompin, Kota Tinggi, Segamat, Mersing and Pekan. The Jakun language is a Malayic language within the Malayo-Polynesian language group and is closely related to Malay. Their folk-culture significance lies in their knowledge of jungle resources, animistic relationship with the natural environment, hunting and fishing techniques, village organisation, oral customs and continued transformation under resettlement and the modern economy.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources describe the production of mats, rugs from Pandan leaves, baskets and handbags as activities based on traditional knowledge introduced in some Jakun communities on a commercial basis.

### Clothing & dress

In the past, Jakun people wore a loincloth around the hips. It was made by hand from the bark of trees, and people did not buy clothes.

In the present, Jakun people are described as dressing like ordinary Malaysians. Clothing changes are presented as part of broader social transformation, alongside watching television, listening to radio, driving cars, riding motorcycles and speaking the Malay language.

### Architecture

The traditional Jakun house is described as a hut. Its floor is made of bamboo, its walls are made of tree bark or wooden planks, and its roof is made of Nipah.

Settlement conditions differ between regrouping-plan villages and older settlements. Rancangan Pengumpulan Semula, abbreviated RPS and glossed as “Regrouping Plan,” refers to settlements created for undeveloped settlements and those undergoing development. In RPS settlements, the government provides individual houses, electricity, water supply, communication facilities, public halls, shops, schools, children’s institutions, medical institutions and asphalted roads. Older settlements generally have few houses made from durable materials, lack electricity, obtain water from natural sources and are reached by earthen roads and jungle paths.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do describe tools and everyday objects connected with hunting, fishing and the use of jungle resources.

A *sampit* is a blowgun used for hunting. It is usually made from bamboo tubing and fires sharpened bamboo darts that have been dipped in poison. The sources state that Jakun hunters can shoot a blowgun with deadly precision at 30 meters. A *bubu* is a cage trap made of bamboo and bounded by rocks, used to catch fish in rivers or streams. Its type and shape depend on the size and type of fish being caught.

Other products include baskets, handbags, mats and rugs from Pandan leaves.

## Music & performance

They do mention performance within social and ritual contexts.

A dance is performed during the wedding day. In the middle of the dance, the bride dashes into the jungle and the bridegroom follows her. If he returns without the bride, the wedding is called off and the attendees jeer at him. During the wedding ceremony, the young couple must feed each other with rice.

A Jakun *bomo*, identified in the sources as a *bomoh* in Malay and glossed as a healer or sorcerer, communicates with spirits. The *bomo* holds special ceremonies to communicate with supernatural forces or ancestral spirits.

The Malaysian radio channel Asyik FM broadcasts separate Jakun programs daily.

## Dance & theatre

The sources document a wedding dance. During the wedding, the bride runs into the jungle and the bridegroom chases her. The event is part of the marriage ceremony rather than a named theatrical or entertainment tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe marriage customs, household relations, spiritual ceremonies and practices connected with birth and death.

Family life begins at about 17 years old. When a boy chooses a girlfriend, his family sends a matchmaker to arrange the marriage and set the value of the dowry. The bride’s family later returns about half of the dowry to the bridegroom’s family as a gift. During the wedding, the bride and bridegroom perform the jungle-chase dance and feed each other with rice.

After marriage, if the young couple does not have a house, the husband lives with his wife’s parents. Each wife must traditionally visit her husband’s parents for at least three days each year. This visit is celebrated in conjunction with traditional holidays.

The birth of a child changes the social identity of the parents and grandparents, moving them into a more respectable category. Their names change according to the name of the eldest child or grandchild, with “father” or “mother” added for the parents.

Jakun animistic beliefs hold that people, animals, plants and inanimate objects possess souls. Mountains, hills, settlements, rivers, rocks and caves are included among objects that may have spiritual force. A *bomo* may communicate with spirits and ancestral spirits through special ceremonies.

## Foodways

Jakun foodways are described in relation to hunting, fishing, gathering, agriculture and exchange. In the past, the nutritional diet was based on fish and wild animals. Animals hunted include wild boars, indigenous deer species identified as *pelandok*, *kancil* and *kijang*, lizards, monkeys and other small animals.

Fish are caught in rivers and streams with *bubu* traps made of bamboo and bounded by rocks. The trap’s type and shape depend on the size and type of fish. Jakun people also gather wild fruits, rattan, wood, rubber, wax, camphor and herbs. Some of these products are exchanged for other commodities.

Where agriculture is practiced, small jungle farms are cleared by slash-and-burn methods. Rice and tuber-based crops such as sweet potatoes are grown. Shifting cultivation involves moving to another plot after two seasons and returning to the old plot only after many years. In village plots, crops include yams, lemongrass, cassava and bananas, and animals are raised.

Many Jakun people obtain food through barter with Malay and Chinese people, exchanging jungle products such as *kemenyan*, *gaharu*, dammar gum and rattan for clothing, tobacco, salt, gambier and areca nut palm.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do mention a legend associated with Lake Chini: tourists are attracted to the legend concerning the immersion of an ancient city of Khmer Empire under water.

Jakun traditional knowledge is inherited from ancestors and passed from generation to generation. This knowledge concerns the tropical jungle, natural resources, medicinal plants, hunting, fishing and the practical limits of community territories. The sources state that loss of jungle, environmental degradation and changes in the way of life contribute to the loss of traditional knowledge and skills, and therefore to the loss of identity as a separate people.

Some international and Malaysian organisations attempt to introduce activities based on Jakun traditional knowledge and skills, including cultivation of local medicinal plants, production of mats and rugs, and making baskets and handbags. The Malaysian radio channel Asyik FM broadcasts separate Jakun programs daily.

## Language & religion

Jakun is a Malayic language of the Malayo-Polynesian language group. It is spoken on the east coast and inland of Peninsular Malaysia, around the Pairang River, from Pekan in Pahang to Sri Gading, east to Benut, and northwest to the middle Muar River area around Segamat, Muar and Tangkak in Johor. The language can be divided at least into the Western Johor and Eastern Johor dialects, which differ mainly in pronunciation and vocabulary. A possible Pahang dialect is reported, although more research is required. Jakun has no written language, is gradually being replaced by Malay and is under threat of disappearance.

Most Jakun people adhere to animistic beliefs closely related to their natural surroundings. They believe that souls exist in people, animals, plants and inanimate objects. Traditional medicine is associated with herbs and roots found in the wild. A *bomo* communicates with spirits and is respected by the community. Islamization intensified after 1980. According to JHEOA statistics from 1996, 1,324 of 16,637 Jakun people were Muslims, or 8%; the figure was 20% in Johor and 5% in Pahang. The sources describe this Islamization as often superficial.
