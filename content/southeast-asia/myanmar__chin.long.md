## Overview

The Chin are a cluster of Tibeto-Burman peoples inhabiting the Chin Hills and Arakan Mountains of western Myanmar, with settlements concentrated in Chin State and spilling into Sagaing, Magway, Rakhine, and Ayeyarwady Regions. Chin villages typically sit above 1,000 metres on ridgelines, with major tribal tracts around Tedim, Falam, and Hakha in the north and the Asho, Cho, Khumi (Mro), and Daai communities of the south. The pan-ethnic "Chin" label — a Burmese approximation of the Asho word *khlaung* ("person") — subsumes five main groupings (Asho, Daai, Khami, Laimi, Zomi) and more than 50 recognized subgroups speaking mutually distinct Kuki-Chin languages. Significant diaspora communities live in Mizoram, Indianapolis, and Tulsa. In folk-culture terms, the Chin are indexed here principally for their backstrap-loom weaving, headhunter-era ornament, and a dense repertoire of tribally specific blankets, tunics, and skirts.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Weaving among the Chin is done almost exclusively by women on the body-tensioned backstrap loom, using locally spun cotton and, in the upper tracts, wool and silk traded up from the plains. Ground cloth is typically warp-faced; pattern is built by supplementary weft, warp float, and — in the most prestigious blankets — twining and hand-embroidery over a woven base. Cloths are woven in narrow panels (roughly 30–45 cm wide) and seamed to form blankets, tube-skirts, and tunics.

**Chin blanket** — A generic English label for the large ceremonial cloths (*puan* in the Zomi/Mizo-related tongues, *hnisan* and other terms elsewhere) that serve as wedding gift, shroud, feast-of-merit reward, and status marker. Ground is usually deep indigo or madder-red with dense supplementary-weft bands.

**Chin weaving** — The broader craft complex on the backstrap loom, encompassing skirts, shoulder cloths, tunics, and headcloths. Distinctive from Burman *acheik* silk (which is tapestry-woven on a frame loom) and from Karen weft-ikat tunics in its emphasis on supplementary-weft geometrics on a warp-faced ground.

**Puan** — The wrapped tube-skirt worn by women, related to the Mizo *puan* across the border. The most prized variants — the **Puan Ropui** ("great cloth") tradition shared with the Mizo, and the Hakha-area **Cawnhna Puan** — carry heavy horizontal bands of red and white on black.

**Hakha tunic (*Cawnhnak Ngo* and related)** — Women's long tunics from the Hakha-Laimi tract with dense diamond-and-lozenge supplementary weft, worn with a matching wrapper.

**Zomi Tedim blankets** — Northern (Tedim/Sizang/Sukte) blankets characterized by narrow warp stripes in indigo, red, and cream, and by discrete supplementary-weft motif bands rather than the all-over pattern of Laimi cloths.

**Asho and Cho southern cloths** — Southern Chin (Cho, Asho, Chinbon) weave lighter cotton wrappers with narrow ikat-like stripes and, among the Cho, an association with the face-tattoo tradition of the women who wore them.

**Khumi (Mro) shoulder bags and skirts** — In the Khumi/Mro tradition of the southern hills and the Bangladesh border, women weave short striped skirts and small carrying bags with beadwork edging.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Kawlh* and related diamond/lozenge frames, *disul* zigzag ("python back"), *nangbia* comb-tooth bands, hornbill-feather chevrons, mithun (gayal) horn motifs, star-and-cross fillings, warp-stripe "railroad" bands, tumpal-like triangle borders, and human-figure rows commemorating feast-of-merit hosts.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday men's dress across most of the Chin Hills combines a wrapped loincloth or short *lungi* with a plain cotton jacket; in colder Hakha and Tedim villages a heavy blanket is worn draped across one shoulder. Women wear the *puan* tube-skirt with a fitted blouse and, on cold days, a shawl. Ceremonial dress is where tribal identity reads clearest: a Hakha bridegroom wears a red-and-black *Cawnhnak* blanket pinned at the shoulder; a Tedim chief at a *tong* (feast of merit) wears a striped Sukte blanket and a headdress of hornbill tail feathers. Married women in the Laimi and Zomi tracts wear silver hairpins and heavy amber-and-glass bead necklaces. The most iconic body-marking tradition — now virtually extinct — is the **facial tattoo of southern Chin women** (Muun, Uppriu, Dai, Yindu, Magan patterns), applied in adolescence with soot and thorn needles and read locally as a mark of beauty and tribal affiliation. Cane leg-rings and brass earplugs formerly identified Khumi and Mro women.

### Architecture

Chin villages are ridge-top settlements of pile-built timber houses roofed with thatch or, more recently, corrugated iron. The classic northern Chin house is a two-level rectangular longhouse: a lower livestock and storage floor on wooden posts, and an upper living floor entered by a notched-log ladder, with an open front veranda where a mortar for pounding grain sits and blankets are hung to weather. Interior space centres on the hearth, above which a bamboo rack stores meat, seed maize, and the skulls of sacrificed mithun. The mithun-skull rack itself is diagnostic: a household's rank at the feast-of-merit cycle is legible from the number and arrangement of skulls, and the front post of a wealthy man's house may be carved with human, tiger, and hornbill figures. Village entrances in Laimi and Zomi tracts formerly held a *khua-hrum* stone altar or shrine, focus of the village guardian-spirit cult, and — in headhunter-era villages — a skull post. Southern Chin (Cho, Khumi) houses lie lower on the slopes, are smaller, and traditionally used cremation platforms rather than the northern practice of secondary burial.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Chin are not a strongly ceramic tradition; earthenware storage jars and rice-beer pots are made in a limited number of villages, and prestige jars are imported Shan or Chinese stoneware — the dark-glazed **Martaban-type jars** used to ferment *zu* (rice beer) and passed down as heirlooms. Metalwork centres on the blacksmith's forge that every substantial village once maintained: the *dao* machete, iron hoes, spearheads, and the flintlock and, later, trade musket that transformed 19th-century Chin warfare. Cast brass and bronze are used for gongs (traded up from the Shan states), for bracelets and earplugs, and for the flat pectoral discs worn by Laimi women. Household wood is heavy and utilitarian — mortars, rice-beer straws (*zu-hna*) of hollowed bamboo, and carved rice-measure baskets — but the carved doorposts and ancestor memorial posts of Hakha and Zophei villages are a genuine sculptural tradition.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Chin women's ornament is heavy and stacked: amber and carnelian trade beads from Yunnan and Bengal strung with brass and silver spacers, glass "chevron" beads of Venetian origin found in older Zomi collections, and, in the Laimi tract, disk pectorals of hammered brass or silver. Silver hairpins and comb ornaments are worn wound into a knot at the nape. Ear ornaments range from small silver hoops to the large cylindrical brass or ivory plugs of Khumi women. The most distinctive and best-documented Chin body practice is the tattoo tradition already noted — the spider-web *Muun* pattern of Mindat-area Cho women, the full-face solid tattoo of Uppriu women, the Dai frog-like pattern, and the light chin-line of Magan women — motifs which, along with the weaving repertoire, encode tribal identity. Men in the headhunter era wore tattooed forearms and, on returning with a head, hornbill feathers and a special blanket.

## Music & performance

Chin music is dominated by voice and by the bronze gong. The **flat brass gong** (*dar* / *darkhuang*), traded from Shan and Kachin smiths, is the most important instrument: sets of gongs, sometimes in matched pairs of large and small, sound the rhythm at feasts of merit, funerals, and the New Year's celebration, and a wealthy household's gong stock is a form of stored wealth on par with mithun. Bamboo idiophones and clappers, small hand drums, and — in the southern Chin tracts — the transverse bamboo flute round out the ensemble. Singing traditions are strongly antiphonal: work songs at the swidden field, courtship songs (*hla* in several dialects) exchanged between young men's and young women's lines at village gatherings, and long narrative *khuang cawi* funeral songs recounting the deceased's genealogy, hunts, and feasts. The **Khuang Cawi**, a feast-of-merit dance-and-song complex among the Hakha and Zophei, is the most elaborated performance tradition: the host, having accumulated the required mithun sacrifices, is celebrated with days of gong music, ceremonial rice-beer drinking through bamboo straws, and communal dance. Church-driven choral singing in four-part harmony, introduced by American Baptist missionaries after 1899, has since become a parallel and now dominant public music.

## Dance & theatre

The Chin have no shadow-puppet or masked-drama tradition of the sort found among the Burman, Mon, or Shan. Performance is centred on communal circle and line dances tied to the feast-of-merit and New Year. The **Khuang Cawi** dance carries the host and his wife on a decorated platform while the village circles them; the **Sarlamkai / Rallu Lam** dance of the Zomi-Mizo continuum, an ex-headhunter victory dance, is performed with dao and shield to gong rhythm; the **Bamboo dance** (*Cheraw*), best known from the Mizo side but shared with several Chin subgroups, has pairs of dancers stepping between clapped bamboo poles. Southern Chin (Cho, Asho) traditions include courtship line dances at harvest.

## Festivals & rituals

The old Chin ritual calendar was organised around swidden agriculture and the household-and-village guardian spirit *khua-hrum*. The main annual festival, still observed as a secular-cum-Christian celebration, is **Chin National Day** on 20 February, commemorating the 1948 Falam conference that abolished hereditary chieftainship. Older seasonal festivals include the post-harvest **Khuado** of the Zomi (Tedim and Sizang), a two- to three-day thanksgiving with gongs, rice-beer, and communal dance; the Hakha **Fang Rual** post-harvest feast; and the sowing-season propitiation of *khua-hrum* at the village stone. The **feast of merit** cycle (*tong* / *khuang cawi*) is the life's-work ritual of a Chin big-man: a graded sequence of mithun sacrifices, each conferring the right to a new blanket, a new house-post carving, and — at the top of the cycle — the honorific placement of a stone memorial. Life-cycle rites are marked by the placing of a naming stone at birth, the presentation of a first *puan* at marriage, and, at death, the wrapping of the corpse in the finest household blanket. Northern Chin practised secondary burial; southern Chin (Cho, Khumi) cremated.

## Foodways

Chin cuisine is a hill-swidden cuisine centred on rice, maize, and millet, with taro, sweet potato, mustard greens, pumpkin, and chilies from the swidden. The signature dish is *sabuti* / **sabuti** (Zomi hominy-and-pork stew — dried maize kernels simmered for hours with pork bones and greens). Other well-known preparations include **vok ril** (pork sausage stuffed with rice and blood), *sathu* (fermented soybean paste comparable to the Naga *akhuni*), *ngapi*-style pounded fermented fish borrowed from the plains, and grilled river fish. Meat comes from mithun (*sial*) at feasts of merit, pigs and chickens at smaller occasions, and hunted game. **Zu**, the household rice-beer, is central: drunk communally through long bamboo straws from a shared jar, poured for guests, and offered to *khua-hrum*. Chilies are eaten green, roasted in ash, or pounded into the **Chin chilli** relish (a pounded paste of roasted dried chilli, garlic, and dried fish) that has crossed over into wider Burmese cuisine. Christian conversion has ended most ritual food restrictions, but *zu* remains the marker of a real Chin feast.

## Oral tradition & literature

Chin oral literature is genealogical and migratory in orientation. The **Chinlung** origin myth — the emergence of the ancestors from a rock, cave, or hole — is shared, with local variants, from the Asho of the delta to the Mizo across the border, and anchors the pan-Chin/Zomi/Mizo sense of common origin. The **Khampat banyan legend** — the sapling planted at the *khua-hrum* altar in the Kabaw Valley before the westward migration into the hills, with a pledge to return when its branches touch the ground — is a central Zomi and Laimi historical narrative. Feast-of-merit songs and funeral laments preserve village and lineage genealogies; the Sukte oral tradition preserves the deeds of Khan Thuam and Kam Hau in the 19th-century rise of Tedim. Since the Baptist mission press at Hakha began publishing in the early 20th century, an active written literature has developed in Hakha Chin, Tedim (Zomi), Falam, Mizo, and other Chin languages, dominated in volume by hymnody and scripture but including a growing corpus of ethnography and fiction by Chin authors such as Sing Khaw Khai, Lal Thang Lian, and Vumson.

## Language & religion

Chin languages form the Kuki-Chin (Kukish) branch of Tibeto-Burman, with the main written standards being Hakha Chin (Laimi), Falam, Tedim/Zomi, Mizo (across the border), Cho, and Asho. All use Roman-based orthographies devised by Baptist missionaries from 1899 onward; there is no pre-colonial indigenous script. Traditional religion centred on the village guardian spirit *khua-hrum* and on household ancestor spirits, with sacrifices of mithun, pig, and chicken mediated by ritual specialists. American Baptist mission work from 1899 onward and a large-scale post–World War I conversion movement produced today's landscape, in which the Chin are one of the most heavily Christian populations in Myanmar — predominantly Baptist, with significant Roman Catholic and Anglican minorities, and continuing folk-religious substrata visible in the persistence of *zu* drinking, feast-of-merit prestige logic, and *khua-hrum* place-names.
