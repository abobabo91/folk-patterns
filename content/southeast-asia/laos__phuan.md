## Overview

The Phuan, also known as Tai Phuan, Thai Puan, or Lao Phuan, are a Theravada Buddhist Tai people. They inhabit Xiangkhouang in Laos and live in scattered communities across Laos and Thailand, with approximately 5,000 people in Mongkol Borei District of Banteay Meanchey Province and Battambang Province in Cambodia. The Ethnologue Report gives a total of 204,704 Phuan, divided fairly evenly between Laos and Thailand. Historically, the Phuan formed the principality of Mueang Phuan, also called Xieng Khouang or Siang Khwang, in the Plain of Jars. Their folk-culture profile in the sources centers on handwoven textiles, distinctive clothing, language continuity, and community practices shaped by forced relocations and regional contact.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Pakama** — The *pakama* is a short sarong worn by men. It is handwoven and is especially associated with striped and patterned textiles made by the Phuan.

**Pasin tin jok** — The *pasin tin jok* is a longer women’s skirt. The sources identify it as a Phuan textile garment.

### Clothing & dress

Phuan handwoven clothing includes the striped and patterned *pakama*, a short sarong worn by men, and the *pasin tin jok*, a longer skirt worn by women. In Thailand, the Siamese forced Phuan communities to live apart from Lao and other Tai people and to dress in black clothing.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Some villages in Tha Wang Pha District retain a tradition of knife making.

## Music & performance

The sources identify singing as part of Phuan cultural history through the bibliographic title “Fading Musical Memory: 150 Years of Lao Phuan Singing in Lopburi, Thailand.” The sources also mention that elephants are used to parade initiates into the monastery, usually just before Songkran, but do not characterize this as a musical performance or describe accompanying music.

## Festivals & rituals

One described custom uses elephants to parade initiates into the monastery, usually just before Songkran.

## Oral tradition & literature

A legend states that Mueang Phuan was founded by Chet Cheuang, the youngest son of the mythical progenitor of the Tai peoples, Khun Borom. The sources also preserve an account of Phuan forced relocation in 1876 by Edward Blencowe Gould, who described captives being driven from the Plain of Jars and reported deaths from sickness, starvation, and exhaustion.

## Language & religion

Phuan or Northeastern Lao is a Tai language spoken in Laos, Thailand, and Cambodia. It is closely related to other tribal Tai languages, including Thai Dam and Thai Loei, and shares similarities with Tai Dam and Tai Lan Na. In Laos, Phuan is considered a Lao dialect, while Thais and Isan people generally consider it distinct. It is not losing ground to standard Thai or the local Isan/Lao trade language. The language has distinct vowel transformations and a distinct vocabulary, and its dialects differ in tonal patterns across regions. The Phuan are Theravada Buddhist.
