## Overview

The Simalungun, also known as Simalungun Batak, are one of the sub-ethnic groups of the Batak people. They live mainly in North Sumatra, Indonesia, especially in Simalungun Regency, the surrounding areas, and the autonomous city of Pematang Siantar. They inhabit the “Eastern Batak” lands, bordering Batak Toba territory to the south and west and Karo Batak territory to the north. Simalungun is linguistically closely related to Toba Batak and culturally closest to Karo Batak.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Hiou* — A ceremonial cloth associated with Simalungun dress.

*Ragi idup* — A ceremonial cloth used when a newborn was brought to the *tapian* on the seventh day if the date of birth was auspicious.

*Ragi panei* — A ceremonial cloth used in the same birth ritual context as *hiou* and *ragi idup*.

### Clothing & dress

The sources mention *hiou*, *ragi idup*, and *ragi panei* as ceremonial cloths. A girl wishing to avoid attention at the marketplace could wear a *hiou* to suggest that she was unavailable. The sources also state that Wismar Djaulung Saragih Sumbayak campaigned for the use of traditional Simalungun clothes and music in the church.

### Architecture

Simalungun villages were described as consisting of houses built parallel to rivers from wooden poles and palm leaves. A house could accommodate a single family, called *rumah parsatanggaan*, or as many as twelve families, called *rumah parrumahopattanggaan*, with a designated area for each family. The head of the village lived in the *Rumah Bolon*, the village’s largest and most ornate house. The main village or capital of each kingdom was called *Pematang*. A well-preserved traditional Simalungun village can be seen at Pematang Purba.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention bamboo tubes used to pipe water to the communal *tapian* and to carry water back to the village. The communal *Losung*, or rice block, was used for pounding rice, with a hole allocated to each family. A new *losung* was cut from a tree trunk, decorated with flowers on an auspicious day, and transported into the village accompanied by music.

### Jewelry & body adornment

When a child was named, it was given black, white, and red bracelets for protective purposes.

## Music & performance

The sources state that music accompanied the transportation of a new *losung* into the village. A boy and girl dressed in ceremonial clothes invested the rice block by throwing rice over it, while villagers sang songs. Wismar Djaulung Saragih Sumbayak also advocated the use of traditional Simalungun music in the church, and his efforts contributed to the formation of the distinct Simalungun Protestant Christian Church.

## Festivals & rituals

Villagers held *Robu Tabu* to drive spirits out of the village. During these days, the village was decorated and outsiders were excluded. Religious ceremonies were often held near the communal *tapian*, where villagers bathed and obtained water through bamboo tubes.

Birth was described as an auspicious occasion. A *dukun*, identified in the source as a midwife or witch doctor, drove off spirits and cut the umbilical cord with a bamboo knife. The newborn was swaddled and daubed with rice chewed by the *dukun* before breastfeeding began. The placenta was buried under the house, and a fire was maintained for seven nights to drive off spirits.

On the seventh day, the child was brought to the *tapian*. If the date of birth was auspicious, the mother’s new *hiou*, *ragi idup*, or *ragi panei* was used. If the date was considered bad, all the women of the village carefully brought the baby while setting out to deceive evil spirits. The child received black, white, and red bracelets when named.

## Foodways

Rice was an important staple and was pounded communally in the *Losung*, or rice block. Each family had a designated hole for this activity. When a new *losung* was introduced, it was cut from a tree trunk, decorated with flowers, and carried into the village with music. A boy and girl in ceremonial clothes threw rice over it while villagers sang songs.

The sources mention rice chewed by the *dukun* and placed on a newborn before breastfeeding.

## Oral tradition & literature

Villagers sang songs during the ceremonial introduction of a new *losung*. The sources also mention Wismar Djaulung Saragih Sumbayak, who authored the first Simalungun dictionary and campaigned for Simalungun-language teaching in schools.

## Language & religion

Simalungun has a regional language called Batak Simalungun, which is different from Indonesian but closely related to Toba Batak. Many Simalungun people also speak Toba Batak and Indonesian. *Pardembanan Batak* is described as a branch of the Simalungun Batak or Toba Batak language influenced by Malay and spoken by some Simalungun Batak who have mixed with Malays and adopted Malay customs.

The Simalungun were traditionally ruled by *raja*, or kings, who were considered living gods. *Parsihili* statues were used to take illness away from a person, while *Pasiarhon* statues were used for communing with the dead. August Theis arrived in Sumatra in 1902 and reached the Simalungun region on 2 September 1903. Wismar Djaulung Saragih Sumbayak was baptized by Theis in 1910 and later helped establish the distinct Simalungun Protestant Christian Church.
