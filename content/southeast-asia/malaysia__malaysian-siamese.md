## Overview

The Malaysian Siamese are an ethnicity or community principally residing in Peninsular Malaysia, especially in Perlis, Kedah, Penang, Perak and Kelantan. The region is culturally relatively homogeneous with southern Burma and southern Thailand, but the Anglo-Siamese Treaty of 1909 established the modern Malaysia–Thailand border from the Golok River in Kelantan to Padang Besar in Perlis. In 2000, national statistics cited 50,211 individuals of Siamese ethnicity in Malaysia, including 38,353 Malaysian citizens. Their cultural life is marked by Thai language use, local Malay dialects, Theravāda Buddhism, temple-centered communities and connections with the fourteen provinces of Southern Thailand and the southernmost Burmese.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The community’s principal named religious building is the *Wat*, a place of worship and a center for religious ceremonies, cultural activities and social activities. Many Malaysian Siamese settled around temples, which monks and villagers use for Buddhist ceremonies and rituals.

## Festivals & rituals

Malaysian Siamese religious and community life is closely tied to temples and to Theravāda Buddhist practice. Monks strengthen communities and encourage villagers to participate in traditional Buddhist ceremonies and rituals on important religious days, including *Uposatha Days*, *Magha Puja*, *Visakha Puja*, Buddhist Lent (*Vassa*) and the End of Buddhist Lent (*Kathina*). Most community members settled around temples, which serve as centers for religious ceremonies and cultural and social activities.

## Language & religion

Malaysian Siamese language use resembles that of their relatives in the fourteen provinces of Southern Thailand and the southernmost Burmese. They also speak fluent local Malay dialects, and one could not differentiate a Malay or a Siamese if they were not speaking their mother tongue. The majority can read and write in Thai because Thai-language learning and teaching have taken place in schools established inside village temples since 1943. The community predominantly professes Theravāda Buddhism, centered on the *Wat*. A small Thai-speaking Muslim minority called *Samsam* exists; the government places them in the Bumiputera, specifically Malay, category, and most have assimilated into the Malay population.
