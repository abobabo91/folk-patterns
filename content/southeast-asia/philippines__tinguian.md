## Overview

The Tinguian, also known as Itneg or Tingguian, are an Austronesian ethnic group indigenous to the Philippines. They primarily inhabit the Cordillera Administrative Region in northern Luzon, especially Abra, Kalinga, Apayao, and Mountain Province, and are also present in the upland areas of the Ilocos Region, including Nueva Era, Ilocos Norte, and Ilocos Sur. The group is divided into nine subgroups: Adasen, Mabaka, Gubang, Banao, Binongon, Danak, Moyodan, Dawangan, and Inlaud, also spelled Illaud. Valley Itneg communities live in the lower reaches of Abra and practice wet-rice cultivation, while mountain Itneg communities rely on dry cultivation and root crops. Their culture includes wet-rice and swidden farming, ceremonial feasts, peace pacts, bead adornment, spirit structures, tattooing, and distinctive social and religious institutions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name delicate patterns of blue lines used in women’s forearm tattoos and small patterns used by some men on their arms and legs.

### Clothing & dress

Women wear a wrap-around skirt, *tapis*, reaching to the knees and fastened with an elaborately decorated belt. On special occasions they also wear a short-sleeved jacket. Men wear a G-string, *ba-al*, made of woven cloth called *balibas*. During special occasions they may wear a long-sleeved jacket, *bado*. Men also wear a belt for fastening their knife and a bamboo hat with a low, dome-shaped top. Beads are the primary adornment of the Tingguians and signify wealth.

### Architecture

The Itneg have two general house forms: a two- to three-room dwelling surrounded by a porch, and a one-room house with a porch in front. Houses are usually made of bamboo and cogon. A Tingguian home with wooden floors commonly has a corner floored with bamboo slats where mothers usually give birth. Spirit structures include *balawa*, built during the *say-ang* ceremony; *sangasang*, located near the village entrance; and *aligang*, which contains jars of *basi*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Chinese jars, copper gongs called *gangsa*, beads, rice fields, and livestock are material possessions that help determine social standing. The sources also describe bamboo and cogon house construction, bamboo-slatted flooring, jars of *basi*, and tools including the lance or spear *pika*, shield *kalasag*, head axe *aliwa*, and Bolo knife.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Beads are the primary adornment of the Tingguians and signify wealth. Women’s forearm tattoos could consist of delicate blue-line patterns, although these were often covered by the large amounts of beads and bracelets they wore. Some men tattooed small patterns on their arms and legs; the same patterns were used to brand animals or mark possessions. The Banao subgroups Busao and Burik were described as having elaborate tattoos. The Busao wore large copper pendants on their ears, while the Burik tattooed their bodies in a manner described as resembling a coat of mail. Tattooing among some groups was becoming obsolete, and warrior tattoos associated with successful head-hunts had disappeared among the “civilized” Itneg.

## Music & performance

 They do identify copper gongs called *gangsa* as possessions associated with social standing. Music-specific information beyond this instrument name is not covered by the sources used.

## Festivals & rituals

 They describe ceremonies and life-cycle practices. Wealth and material possessions help determine a family’s or person’s social standing, as well as the hosting of feasts and ceremonies. Shamans form a distinct group only during ceremonial periods. The *say-ang* ceremony is associated with the construction of *balawa*, a spirit structure. Sugarcane wine called *basi* is usually consumed during traditional rituals and ceremonies, and pigs, chickens, and carabaos may be used for food or religious rituals.

Marriage is arranged by parents, usually between distant relatives. Parents select a bride for their son when he is six to eight years old. If the proposal is accepted, beads are tied around the girl’s waist as a sign of engagement. A bride price, *pakalon*, is paid to the bride’s family, initially and again during the actual wedding. No celebration accompanies the wedding, and guests leave immediately after the ceremony. The dead are cleaned and adorned for the journey to *maglawa*, the Itneg afterlife. During the wake, the corpse is placed in a death chair called *sangadel*. Head-hunting was stopped through peace pacts called *kalon*.

## Foodways

Rice is extensively grown, using wet-rice cultivation and swidden, or *kaingin*, practices. Corn is planted as a major subsistence crop and as a replacement for rice. Other foods include camote, yams, coconut, mango, banana, and vegetables. Sugarcane is planted to make *basi*, a wine usually consumed during traditional rituals and ceremonies. Pigs and chickens are eaten or used in religious rituals, while carabaos are killed during large celebrations. Hunting wild animals and fishing are prevalent. Eel and freshwater fish such as *paleleng* and *ladgo*, identified in the source as lobster, are caught to make viands for most families.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They mention *kadawyan*, custom-based justice, and trial by ordeal, but provide no further account of oral literature.

## Language & religion

The native Itneg language is a South-Central Cordilleran dialect continuum, and the Itneg speak Ilocano as a second language. The Itneg believe in numerous powerful supernatural beings, spirits, and deities. The greatest is Kadaklan, who lives in the sky and created the earth, moon, stars, and sun. They believe in life after death, in a place called *maglawa*, and take special care to clean and adorn the dead before the journey there. The corpse is placed in a *sangadel*, or death chair, during the wake. The sources also describe shamans as a distinct social group during ceremonial periods and identify spirit structures connected with ritual practice.
