## Overview

The Wa are a Southeast Asian ethnic group living mainly in northern Myanmar, especially northern Shan State and eastern Kachin State, near and along the border with China, as well as in Yunnan Province. Their ancestral territory is a rugged mountainous region between the Mekong and Salween rivers, crossed by the Nam Hka. Wa communities also live in Thailand, particularly in Chiang Rai and Chiang Mai provinces. The population in China is estimated at 400,000, while the Wa language has approximately 820,000 speakers in total according to David Bradley’s estimate. The Wa language belongs to the Mon-Khmer, or more broadly Austroasiatic, language family. Their folk culture includes animist ritual, animal sacrifice, rice agriculture, distinctive dances, large wooden drums, oral origin narratives, and several writing traditions.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Traditional Wa villages are mentioned in connection with shrines called *Nyiex Moeg*, where a buffalo was sacrificed once every year at a special Y-shaped post called *Khaox Si Gang*. The Wa State’s de facto capital is Pangkham.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention only a large hollow wooden drum used in dance and the Y-shaped ritual post called *Khaox Si Gang*.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 They state that chewing betel with areca nut was formerly an important custom, but give no further description of its implements or social meaning.

## Music & performance

Wa performance culture includes dances accompanied by the beating of a large hollow wooden drum. The sources identify several types of traditional dance, including the hair dance. Dance and festivals have been promoted as tourist attractions by the Yunnan tourism authorities in China.

The Wa Women’s Association promotes dance and other cultural and philanthropic activities. The sources also mention Wa songwriters who use the official Wa orthography in karaoke lyrics of Wa songs. Christian missionary activity produced Wa hymns: the first transcription of the Wa language was devised by William Marcus Young and Sara Yaw Shu Chin in 1931 for translating the Bible, and the first publication was a compilation of Wa hymns in 1933. The Wa New Testament was completed in 1938.

## Dance & theatre

The Wa have different kinds of traditional dances. One important dance is accompanied by the beating of a large hollow wooden drum, and the sources specifically name the hair dance among other Wa dances. These performances and festivals have been promoted as tourist attractions by the Yunnan tourism authorities in China. The Wa Women’s Association also promotes dance.

## Festivals & rituals

Traditional Wa religious practice centered on animist beliefs and ritual blood sacrifice. Villages had a spirit healer called *Tax Cao Chai*, and sickness or other problems were traditionally addressed by sacrificing a chicken, pig, or larger animal according to the magnitude of the affliction. Traditional villages also had shrines called *Nyiex Moeg*. At these shrines, a buffalo was sacrificed once every year at the Y-shaped post called *Khaox Si Gang*, with blood, meat, and skin offered there.

According to a local legend reported in the sources, cutting a human head was intended as a ritual sacrifice to improve the fertility of rice fields. The sources also state that animals were sacrificed at festivities such as weddings and funerary rituals. Wa communities under Buddhist influence developed different traditions.

In Wa origin narratives described by Sir George Scott, the first Wa originated from two female ancestors, *Ya Htawm* and *Ya Htai*, who spent their early phase as tadpoles, *rairoh*, in a small lake called *Nawng Hkaeo*. The lake is located in northeastern Wa territory in the border area between China and Myanmar. They state that monogamous marriage was the norm in traditional Wa society, while sexual freedom for men and women before marriage was customary.

## Foodways

The Wa traditionally practiced subsistence agriculture by cultivating rice, peas, beans, poppies, and walnuts. They bred water buffaloes, which were used mainly for sacrificial purposes.

Animal products have ritual importance in the sources. Chickens, pigs, larger animals, and water buffaloes could be sacrificed in response to illness, other problems, or ritual occasions. At the annual buffalo sacrifice associated with *Nyiex Moeg* and *Khaox Si Gang*, blood, meat, and skin were offered. Animals were also sacrificed during weddings and funerary rituals among traditional spirit-worshiping Wa.

The sources mention chewing betel with areca nut as a formerly important custom.

## Oral tradition & literature

Wa origin narratives state that the first Wa descended from two female ancestors, *Ya Htawm* and *Ya Htai*, who began as tadpoles, *rairoh*, in *Nawng Hkaeo*. The sources also preserve local narratives claiming that the historical Wa States, all of eastern Shan State, and large areas of adjacent present-day China belonged to the Wa in the distant past.

A local legend connects the practice of cutting a human head with a ritual sacrifice intended to improve the fertility of rice fields. The sources emphasize that much of the early history of the Wa is known through local narratives rather than extensive written histories.

The Wa formerly had no script. A bibliography of materials in or about Wa language and culture is associated with the School of Oriental and African Studies, and the Repository and Workspace for Austroasiatic Intangible Heritage provides a Parauk digital archive.

## Language & religion

Wa is an Austroasiatic language spoken by the Wa people of Myanmar and China. It has three distinct varieties described in the sources: Parauk, the majority and standard form; Vo, also called Zhenkang Wa; and Awa. The sources also describe numerous dialects and varieties, including Baraoke, Banhong, Dazhai, Masan, Awalai, Damangnuo, Xiyun, and Wa. The Wa language is recognized as a state language by the government of Wa State.

Wa formerly had no script. Some literate Wa used Chinese characters, while others used the Shan language and its script. William Marcus Young and Sara Yaw Shu Chin devised the first Wa transcription in 1931 using the Latin script. Wa hymns appeared in 1933 and the Wa New Testament was completed in 1938. A revised Bible orthography is used mainly in Burmese Wa areas and among Wa in Thailand. A transcription adapted to pinyin romanization was developed in China in 1956. This is known as PRC orthography or Chinese official orthography.

Many Wa are animists. Traditional religion centered on spirit worship, spirit healers, ritual blood sacrifice, village shrines, and animal offerings. A small proportion follows a form of Buddhism or Christianity, while Christian missionary work among the Wa began at the beginning of the 20th century.
