## Overview

The Orang Asli are a heterogeneous Indigenous population and national minority in Malaysia, forming one of the 95 subgroups of the Orang Asal. They are described as the oldest inhabitants of Peninsular Malaysia and accounted for 0.7% of its population in 2017; their population is approximately 148,000. Settlements are scattered among the mostly Malay population, often in mountainous areas or rainforest, with more than half living in Pahang and Perak and others in Kelantan, Selangor, Johor, and Negeri Sembilan. The Orang Asli comprise three broad categories: Negrito, Senoi, and Proto-Malay, although the boundaries between these categories are not fixed. Their languages belong to the Austroasiatic and Austronesian families. Their cultural identity is linked to physical, economic, social, cultural, territorial, and spiritual ties with the immediate natural environment.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Orang Asli settlements are described as being scattered among the mostly Malay population, often in mountainous areas or rainforest. In the 2006 census, 36.9% lived in remote villages, 62.4% on the outskirts of Malay villages, and 0.7% in cities and suburbs. The sources state that the Malaysian Government relocated many nomadic and semi-nomadic people to permanent housing estates equipped with electricity, running water, and schools.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention prehistoric artefacts and ceramics in the archaeological history of the Malay Peninsula, including ceramics associated with the Neolithic and the archaeological culture of Hòa Bình.

## Music & performance

 They do record radio broadcasting in Orang Asli languages. Radio broadcasts in these languages began in 1959, and Asyik.FM broadcasts daily in Radio Malaysia in Semai, Temyar, Teman, and Jakun languages from 8 am to 11 pm; the channel is also available via the Internet. The sources also mention individual recordings of pop music in Aslian languages that can be heard on Asyik FM. No instrument names, musical forms, ceremonial performance settings, or professional performers are identified.

## Festivals & rituals

 They state that norms of customary laws are observed in daily Senoi life. Since the colonial era, missionaries of world religions have been active among jungle dwellers, and some people among the tribes are adherents of Islam, Christianity, or Baháʼí Faith.

## Foodways

 They state that the traditional economy of the Senoi was based on jungle resources, including hunting, fishing, foraging, and logging. In contact with the Malay and Siamese states, the Senoi were involved in trading and were the main suppliers of jungle produce in the region. The sources also state that the Orang Kuala and Orang Seletar, who live by the sea, are mainly engaged in fishing and the seafood industry. No specific food names or preparation practices are given.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They state that Orang Asli languages lack both natively written literature and official status in Malaysia. Some Baháʼí Faith and Christian missionaries, as well as JAKOA newsletters, produce printed materials in Aslian languages. Private texts recorded by radio announcers use Malay and English writing and are described as amateur in nature. The sources also mention text messages in Orang Asli languages and individual recordings of pop music in Aslian languages.

## Language & religion

The Orang Asli divide linguistically between Austroasiatic and Austronesian languages. Northern groups, including Senoi and Semang, speak Aslian languages within the Austroasiatic family. These include Jahaic, Senoic, Semelaic, and Jah Hut languages. The Proto-Malay group generally speaks Aboriginal Malay languages within the Austronesian family, although Semelai and Temoq belong to the Aslian languages. Named languages in the sources include Semai, Temiar, Lanoh, Mah Meri, Semaq Beri, Semelai, Temoq, Jakun, Duanoʼ, Orang Kanaq, Orang Seletar, and Temuan.

Almost all Orang Asli are now bilingual and, in addition to their native language, are fluent in Malay, the national language of Malaysia. Semai or Temiar commonly functions as a lingua franca between Orang Asli speakers. Lanoh is described as endangered, while Mah Meri is in the greatest danger. Some members of the tribes adhere to Islam, Christianity, or Baháʼí Faith.
