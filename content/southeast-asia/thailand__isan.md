## Overview

The Isan people are an ethnic group native to Northeastern Thailand, a region of twenty provinces also known as the Isan region or *Phak Isan*. Their population is estimated at about 22 million, and approximately 80% are ethnically Lao. The homeland of the Isan language is mainly the Khorat Plateau, whose landscape is separated from the rest of Thailand by the Phetsabun, Dong Phanya Nyen, and Sankamphaeng mountains. The Phu Phan Mountains divide the plateau into northern and southern sections, while the Mekong River forms much of the geopolitical boundary between Thailand and Laos. Isan belongs to the Southwestern branch of Tai languages in the Kra-Dai language family. Its importance in folk culture lies in the persistence of Lao-derived language, music, dance, foodways, literature, and regional identity despite long-term Thaification.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources used describe a shift toward Thai dress norms during the integration of Isan people into Thai national identity.

### Architecture

The sources mention houses, roads, railways, shanty towns, and slums. The region’s geography is described through the Khorat Plateau, mountain ranges, river systems, flooding, droughts, and areas without access to water during the dry season.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention palm-leaf manuscripts, monastery libraries, Thai-script manuscripts, and written materials.

## Music & performance

Music is identified as one area in which cultural assimilation was never complete. The sources state that students can participate in clubs promoting local music sung in the local Lao language, and they describe local music as part of the renewed expression of regional and ethnic identity around the 1990s.

The sources specifically name *mo lam*, described as traveling troupes that relied on written manuscripts for the lyrics of poetry and old stories set to song. These performances could be accompanied by the *khaen* alone or alongside other local instruments. The written material was preserved on palm-leaf manuscripts, and monks continued to teach young boys to read the Tai Noi script before the expansion of Thai-language administration and schooling. The *khaen*, local music, and songs in the local Lao language are the principal named musical elements covered by the supplied material.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention local dances native to the area and student clubs that promote them. They also describe traveling troupes of shadow puppet performers, whose performances used written manuscripts for lyrics to poetry and old stories set to song.

## Festivals & rituals

They mention festivals as contexts in which passages from old literature were read, and they state that traveling troupes performed old stories set to song. The supplied material does not identify festival names, dates, agricultural ceremonies, or ritual objects.

The sources describe Buddhist institutions and religious change. During the integration of Isan into Siam, the state introduced the Siamese religious sangha and Buddhist calendar instead of the Lao sangha and religious calendar, identified as *hit sipsong khong sipsii*. During Thaification, ancient Lao Buddhist manuscripts and records were burned or destroyed, monastery libraries were seized, and old Lao religious texts were replaced by Thai-script, Thai-centric manuscripts.

## Foodways

The sources describe Isan cuisine as the cuisine of Northeastern Thailand, associated with the more arid Khorat Plateau and influenced by the culture of Laos and Khmer cuisine. They state that Isan cuisine is similar to the cuisine of Southern Laos. Thai cuisine more generally emphasizes lightly prepared dishes, aromatic ingredients, and spicy heat, and is organized into the categories *tom* (boiled dishes), *yam* (spicy salads), *tam* (pounded foods), and *kaeng* (curries).

Rice is the central staple of Thai cuisine. Thai meals generally consist of rice with several complementary dishes shared by everyone at the table. The sources identify sticky rice, *khao niao*, as an edible implement, shaped into small balls by hand and dipped into accompanying dishes. This practice is specifically associated with people living in Lanna and Isan. Meals commonly combine contrasting flavors, textures, and preparation methods.

Named dishes and ingredients in the supplied material include *som tam*, a spicy papaya salad; *nam tok mu*; *tom yam*; *tom chuet*; *nam phrik*; *pla ra*, a pungent fermented-fish sauce; *nam pla*, a clear fish sauce; *kapi*, Thai shrimp paste; and *khao khluk kapi*. The sources also name *khao hom mali*, jasmine rice; *khanom chin*, fresh rice vermicelli; and *kaeng khiao wan*, green curry. Fresh herbs and spices include galangal, lemongrass, shallots, coriander, chilies, kaffir lime leaves, and turmeric.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe a substantial Lao literary inheritance formerly written in the Tai Noi script. Before Thaification, monks taught young boys to read Tai Noi from palm-leaf manuscripts. Passages from old literature were read during festivals, while traveling *mo lam* and shadow puppet performers used written manuscripts for poetry and old stories set to song.

The Thai Cultural Mandates and related reforms banned the old written language and removed Lao literature from schools. Numerous temple libraries were seized or destroyed, and old Lao religious texts, local histories, literature, and poetry collections were replaced with Thai-script manuscripts. This interrupted the Isan people’s access to their written language and shared literary history.

From around the 1990s, academics at Isan universities began studying local language, history, culture, and folklore. Their publications brought renewed attention to Lao features of the language and landscape.

## Language & religion

Isan is a local development of the Lao language in Thailand and belongs to the Southwestern branch of Tai languages within the Kra-Dai language family. It is closely related to Lao, Phu Thai, and Tai Yo. The Thai government officially classifies it as a dialect of Thai, while native speakers, Laotian Lao, and many linguists classify it as Lao or as a separate language shaped by its history and Thai influence.

Isan is spoken mainly in Northeastern Thailand. About 88% of people habitually speak it at home, 11% speak both Isan and Central Thai, and 1% speak Central Thai exclusively. The language is used in familiar and informal contexts, while Standard Thai dominates public, educational, official, and professional settings. The Tai Noi script was banned, and the Tua Tham script previously used for Buddhist texts is now known only to a handful of very elderly people and monastery caretakers.

The sources describe Buddhism through the Siamese and Thai sangha, Buddhist calendars, monks, monasteries, and Buddhist manuscripts.
