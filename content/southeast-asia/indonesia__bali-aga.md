## Overview

The Bali Aga, also called Baliaga or Bali Mula, are the indigenous people of Bali and an Austronesian people. They are predominantly located in eastern Bali, especially in Bangli around the mountains of Kintamani, and in East Buleleng and East Karangasem; communities also occur in north-western and central regions. An inventory recorded about 38 traditional villages in seven regencies in 1988–1989, while research published in 2015 found 62 villages across seven regencies. Their relative isolation in mountain areas preserved some original Austronesian elements, especially in architecture, language, ritual, and textile production. The name Bali Mula, meaning “Original Balinese,” is preferred to Bali Aga or Bali Pegunungan, terms regarded as insulting.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Geringsing** — *Geringsing* is Bali’s traditional double-ikat textile. Both cotton warp and weft threads are carefully dyed and cross-dyed before weaving, so the finished pattern appears only as the cloth is woven. Tenganan is the only village that still produces it.

**Jepun** — The frangipani-flower design known as *jepun* is one of the patterns inspired by *patola*, the silk double ikats produced in Gujarat during the height of the Spice Trade.

Geringsing commonly uses red, neutral, and black. The cloths are regarded as sacred and are ascribed supernatural properties, especially for healing and exorcism. *Gering* means “decease” and *sing* means “no.”

**Motif vocabulary.** mandala, a bird’s-eye view of a sacred center from which everything radiates; *jepun*, frangipani flower.

### Architecture

The Bali Aga live in isolated mountain areas, where relative isolation preserved some original Austronesian elements visible in Bali Aga architecture. The sources mention traditional villages and settlement patterns.

## Music & performance

They mention Kawi/Old Javanese and Sanskrit in connection with religious rituals, but not music or performance.

## Festivals & rituals

In Tenganan, a three-day festival called **Udaba Sambah** is held during June or July. Tenganan prohibits divorce and polygamy, unlike other villages.

The Bali Aga/Bali Mula maintain old beliefs called folk Hinduism, which differ in some respects from the Hinduism of other Balinese people. One major difference concerns funerary practice: instead of performing Ngaben cremation, the people of Trunyan leave corpses lying on the ground on a tree called *Taru Menyan* until only bones remain. They believe that the corpses do not smell bad because they are placed around the *Taru Menyan* tree.

## Foodways

Foraging for wild and semi-wild plants, especially in forest areas, formerly increased dietary diversity and provided income through the sale of plants at traditional markets. These edible plants are still described as the main source of food in rural areas, although their use is increasingly neglected and indigenous knowledge of plant uses is being eroded.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources record a legend about Bedulu, where the last king of Pejeng, Sri Aji Asura Bumibanten, was said to possess supernatural powers. He could cut off his head without feeling pain and replace it. After his head fell into a river, a servant decapitated a pig and placed the animal’s head on the king’s body. The embarrassed king hid in a tall tower, but a child discovered the secret. He consequently became known as Dalem Bedulu, or “He-who-changed-head.” Another explanation connects the name Bedulu with Badahulu, meaning “the village upstream.”

## Language & religion

The Bali Aga are an Austronesian people and a multilingual community. Most speak the Balinese Bali Aga dialect, while a minority also speak standard Balinese. The dialect dates back thousands of years and varies from village to village: the version spoken in Tenganan differs from that spoken in Trunyan. Indonesian is used for communication between ethnicities, English is spoken for tourists, and Kawi/Old Javanese and Sanskrit are used in religious rituals.

Their older beliefs are called folk Hinduism. In Trunyan, funerary practice differs from the cremation customary among most Balinese: bodies are placed around the *Taru Menyan* tree until they become bones.
