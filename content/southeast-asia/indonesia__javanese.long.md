## Overview

The Javanese (*Wong Jåwå* in ngoko, *Tiyang Jawi* in krama) are an Austronesian people native to Central Java, East Java, and the Special Region of Yogyakarta, concentrated historically in the volcanic Kedu and Kewu plains on the slopes of Mount Merapi and in the Brantas River valley of East Java. Numbering more than 100 million, they are the largest ethnic group in Southeast Asia and the fourth-largest Muslim ethnic group in the world. Their language, Javanese, is the largest Austronesian language by native speakers. Javanese folk culture matters because it is a layered synthesis: an indigenous animist substratum (*Kejawèn*), a millennium of Hindu–Buddhist court civilization that produced Borobudur and Prambanan, and a Sufi-inflected Islam absorbed from the northern *pesisir* port towns. The refined *alus* aesthetic of the Yogyakarta and Surakarta *keraton* has shaped batik, gamelan, wayang, and dance across the archipelago.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

***Batik*** — wax-resist dyeing on cotton (and finer *primissima* cottons for court cloths), inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List in 2009 (RL/00170). Central Javanese batik from Yogyakarta and Surakarta is defined by *sogan* browns, indigo blues, and cream, in contrast to the polychrome coastal *batik pesisir* of Cirebon, Pekalongan, and Lasem.

***Batik tulis*** — hand-drawn batik applied with the *canting*, a small copper spouted pen. A single fine cloth may take months and remains the prestige technique of the *keraton* workshops.

***Batik cap*** — stamped batik using copper block *cap*, introduced in the mid-19th century to meet demand. Faster and cheaper than *tulis*, it enabled the mass market for daily-wear cloths.

***Kain panjang*** — long unstitched rectangular cloth (roughly 2.5 m) worn wrapped around the lower body by both men and women; the standard canvas for batik design.

***Kain kembangan*** — a category of ritual cloths of the Yogyakarta and Surakarta courts, using tie-dye and plain-color grounds rather than wax resist, worn for specific ceremonial and life-cycle functions.

***Dodot*** — the oversized ceremonial wrap cloth (up to four meters) worn by bride and groom in *keraton*-style weddings and by court dancers in *bedhaya* and *srimpi*.

***Songket Javanese*** — supplementary-weft weaving with metallic gold or silver thread, used for sashes, shoulder cloths, and ceremonial *kain* in aristocratic dress, distinct from the more widely known Palembang and Minangkabau songket in its restrained palette.

***Lurik*** — narrow-loom hand-woven cotton with lengthwise stripes, historically the daily cloth of Central Javanese villagers and still worn as *surjan* jackets and workwear.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Parang* (diagonal "chopping-knife" or S-curve bands, formerly reserved for royalty in the *parang rusak* variant); *kawung* (four-lobed rosettes in a grid, derived from the sugar-palm fruit); *truntum* (small four-petaled flowers scattered on a dark ground, associated with rekindled love and worn by the mother of the bride); *sido mukti* ("becoming prosperous," a wedding cloth with geometric compartments of birds, butterflies, and *meru* mountains); *sido luhur* ("becoming noble," related wedding design); *semen* (fertility motifs with tendrils, *meru* mountains, birds, and *garuda* wings); *ceplok* (grid-based repeated geometric medallions); *nitik* (dotted patterns imitating woven cloth); *mega mendung* (Cirebon coastal cloud-scroll, showing Chinese influence); *lereng* (single-direction diagonal stripes).

### Clothing & dress

For men, formal Central Javanese dress consists of a *beskap* jacket, a *kain panjang* wrapped around the legs, and a *blangkon* — a batik headcloth stiffened and pre-tied in regional styles (Yogyakarta and Solo have distinct shapes). A *keris* is thrust into the sash at the small of the back for ceremonial occasions. The everyday village equivalent is the *surjan*, a striped *lurik* jacket with a mandarin collar. Women wear the ***kebaya*** — a fitted long-sleeved blouse of cotton, voile, or lace worn over a *kemben* breast-cloth, paired with a *kain panjang* or *sarong* held by a *stagen* wrapped waist-sash. Kebaya was inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List in 2024 (RL/02090) as a shared heritage across Southeast Asia. Bridal *keraton* dress features the *dodot* wrap for both partners, elaborate gilded headdresses, and gold *sanggul* hair ornaments. Coastal Javanese dress on the north shore is looser, more polychrome, and shows Chinese and Arab influence in cut and fabric.

### Architecture

The classical Javanese house is classified by roof form. The ***joglo*** is the highest-status house type, defined by a steeply pitched central pyramidal roof (*tajug* profile) rising over four massive teak *soko guru* pillars in the middle of the main room; it is the traditional dwelling of the *priyayi* nobility. The ***limasan*** has a hipped roof of four slopes and is the standard middle-class house form, while the ***kampung*** house has a simple gabled roof and belongs to villagers. The ***pendopo*** is an open-sided pavilion, again with four *soko guru* pillars, used as reception hall in front of a main house or *keraton*. Kraton complexes in Yogyakarta and Surakarta — the palaces of Hamengkubuwono, Pakualaman, Pakubuwono, and Mangkunegaran — organize pendopo, walled courtyards, and split *candi bentar* gates inherited from the Hindu-Buddhist period. Traditional mosques such as the **Agung Demak Mosque**, the **Menara Kudus Mosque**, and the **Great Mosque of Banten** use tiered pyramidal *tajug* roofs (usually three tiers) rather than domes, and often lack minarets; the Menara Kudus tower is built in a red-brick Hindu-Javanese *candi* style. Village houses are timber-framed with woven bamboo *gedhek* walls and clay-tiled roofs.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The signature Javanese metalwork object is the ***keris***, a wavy or straight-bladed dagger with distinct pattern-welded *pamor* patterns in the iron, inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List in 2008 (RL/00112). Every element — blade shape (*dhapur*), pamor pattern, hilt (*ukiran*), and sheath (*warangka*) — carries symbolic weight, and the weapon is regarded as an *ageman*, an heirloom with spiritual charge. Court metalwork also includes ceremonial spears (*tombak*) and gilded regalia (*upacara*). Gamelan instruments — bronze *gongs*, *kenong*, *bonang* kettle-gong rows, and *saron* metallophones — are cast in specialist forges in Bogor, Solo, and Semarang. Domestic wares include earthenware cooking pots from Kasongan (Yogyakarta), which since the mid-20th century has become known for large decorative terracotta vessels. Everyday food-serving objects include the *besek*, a round woven bamboo tray used for *tumpeng*, and lidded rice baskets.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Court and bridal jewelry uses gold and gold-plated silver in the Central Javanese *keraton* style: *sanggul* hair buns pinned with rows of *tusuk konde* hairpins, a five- or seven-pronged *centhung* forehead ornament, *sumping* ear ornaments derived from Hindu-period reliefs, and heavy *kalung susun* tiered necklaces. The *cundhuk mentul* is a set of trembling gold flower ornaments worn upright above the bride's *sanggul*. Everyday adornment is much more restrained: simple gold ear studs, thin bangles, and for older women a *sanggul* bun secured with a plain hairpin. Body modification is not a strong Javanese tradition; ceremonial makeup at Yogyakarta and Solo weddings includes the painted *paes* — dark forehead motifs shaped in specific curves that mark the bride as being of court style.

## Music & performance

The defining Javanese musical tradition is ***gamelan***, inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List in 2021 (RL/01607). A full *gamelan* ensemble consists of tuned bronze percussion — *saron* and *slenthem* metallophones, *bonang* kettle-gong rows, hanging *gong ageng* and *kempul*, *kenong* pot-gongs — together with *kendhang* two-headed drums, bamboo *suling* flute, bowed *rebab*, plucked *celempung* or *siter* zither, and *gerong* male chorus with *pesindhèn* female solo singer. Two tuning systems coexist: the five-tone *sléndro* and the seven-tone *pélog*, and pieces (*gendhing*) are organized in colotomic cycles marked by the great gong. Ensembles accompany court dance, wayang, and life-cycle rituals; the *keraton* of Yogyakarta and Surakarta each maintain sacred archaic ensembles (*gamelan sekaten*) played only during the Sekaten festival marking the Prophet's birthday. Popular song forms include *langgam Jawa* (Javanese-language sung poetry accompanied by gamelan or *kroncong* string ensemble) and *campursari*, a 20th-century fusion of gamelan tunings with Western instruments. Coastal East Java has *jaranan* processional music with horse-trance dance, and the santri north coast maintains sung Islamic devotional forms.

## Dance & theatre

***Wayang*** — the theatre tradition inscribed by UNESCO in 2008 (RL/00063) — is centered on Javanese *wayang kulit*, shadow-puppet theatre using intricately pierced buffalo-hide puppets manipulated by a *dalang* who narrates, voices all characters, and cues the gamelan through an all-night performance. Repertoire draws from the *Ramayana* and *Mahabharata* (the *lakon* cycle) and from indigenous *Panji* romances. Related forms include *wayang golek* (three-dimensional rod puppets, more strongly Sundanese but also Javanese), *wayang klithik* (flat wooden puppets), and *wayang wong* — human dance-drama in which performers take the roles of wayang characters. Court dance from the *keraton* is divided into refined female genres — the sacred nine-dancer *bedhaya* (of which *Bedhaya Ketawang* at Surakarta is performed only once a year) and the four-dancer *srimpi* — and stronger male martial genres such as the *beksan lawung*. Popular village and coastal dance forms include *ronggeng* (female social dance), *reog Ponorogo* (masked lion-peacock dance from East Java with an enormous peacock-feather headdress), and *kuda lumping* / *jaranan* horse-trance dance.

## Festivals & rituals

The Javanese calendar, a lunar system fixed by Sultan Agung in 1633, superimposes a five-day *pasaran* week on the seven-day week to create the 35-day *wetonan* cycle used for reckoning auspicious days. ***Satu Suro*** (or *Siji Suro*) is Javanese New Year on 1 Suro, marked by nocturnal silent processions (*tapa bisu mubeng benteng*) around the *keraton* walls in Yogyakarta and by the ritual cleansing of heirloom *keris* and gamelan (*jamasan pusaka*). ***Sekaten*** is a week-long festival ending on the Prophet Muhammad's birthday, centered on the courts of Yogyakarta and Surakarta, when the sacred *gamelan sekaten* is sounded and the *Grebeg Maulud* procession carries mountainous *gunungan* of rice, vegetables, and cakes from the palace to the Great Mosque. Analogous *Grebeg* processions mark Idul Fitri (*Grebeg Syawal*) and Idul Adha (*Grebeg Besar*). Village *bersih desa* rites cleanse the village once a year, often with a *slametan* communal meal and a wayang performance. Life-cycle rites include *mitoni* / *tingkeban* — the seventh-month pregnancy ritual — infant naming, tooth-filing, the *tetesan* girls' ceremony, and elaborate *keraton*-style weddings with *siraman* bathing, *midodareni* vigil, and *panggih* meeting of bride and groom. The all-purpose *slametan* communal meal, hosted for any significant transition and centered on shared *tumpeng*, is the core Javanese ritual form.

## Foodways

Rice defines Javanese eating: a plate without it is not a meal, and the yellow conical *tumpeng*, colored with turmeric and surrounded by side dishes on a woven *besek* tray, is the required centerpiece of every *slametan*. Central Javanese cooking runs sweet — the Yogyakarta signature dish ***gudeg*** is young jackfruit stewed for hours with palm sugar and coconut milk, served with *krecek* (buffalo-skin crackers in chili sauce), boiled egg, and chicken. East Javanese and *pesisir* cooking runs hotter and saltier: Surabaya's ***rujak cingur*** combines boiled cow's snout with vegetables, fruit, and a thick fermented *petis* shrimp-paste sauce. ***Pecel*** is a peanut-and-chili sauce ladled over blanched vegetables and rice, and ***nasi kuning*** is festive turmeric rice. ***Tempeh*** — soybeans bound into cakes by *Rhizopus* mould — is a Javanese invention documented in the 19th-century *Serat Centhini* and now the archipelago's most important vegetable protein, along with tofu (*tahu*) prepared in dozens of local forms (*tahu petis*, *tahu campur*). Street sweets include *klepon* (pandan rice-flour balls filled with palm sugar), *serabi* pancakes, and *dawet* iced coconut-milk drink with green rice-flour jellies. Herbal drinks called ***jamu*** — turmeric-tamarind *kunyit asam*, ginger *beras kencur*, bitter *pahitan* — are sold door-to-door by *jamu gendong* women carrying bottles in a back-slung basket; the practice was inscribed by UNESCO in 2023 (RL/01972). Tea is drunk sweet and often jasmine-scented; kretek clove cigarettes accompany social occasions.

## Oral tradition & literature

Javanese literature has a continuous written tradition from the 9th-century Old Javanese *kakawin* poems onward. The Old Javanese ***Ramayana Kakawin*** (c. 870 CE) and Mpu Sedah and Mpu Panuluh's ***Bharatayuddha*** (1157 CE) domesticated the Indian epics, and their episodes remain the standard *lakon* material for *wayang kulit*. The indigenous ***Panji cycle*** — romances of the prince of Janggala searching for his lost princess Dewi Sekartaji — spread across mainland Southeast Asia and is enacted in mask dance (*wayang topeng*) as well as puppet theatre. Later court literature includes the encyclopedic ***Serat Centhini*** (compiled 1814 at the Surakarta court), the ***Serat Kalatidha*** of Ronggowarsito, and the *babad* historical chronicles such as the *Babad Tanah Jawi*. Sung poetry uses fixed *macapat* meters (*Dhandhanggula*, *Asmarandana*, *Pangkur*, *Sinom*, and others), each with its own emotional register and prescribed syllable and vowel patterns. Proverbial philosophy circulates in condensed phrases such as *Jer Basuki Mawa Bea* ("all achievement demands sacrifice"), *Rawe-rawe rantas, malang-malang putung* ("what obstructs will be cleared"), and *Tut Wuri Handayani* ("leading from behind by giving strength"), the last adopted as the motto of Indonesia's Ministry of Education.

## Language & religion

Javanese belongs to the Malayo-Polynesian branch of the Austronesian family, heavily loaded with Sanskrit loanwords and organized into speech levels — *ngoko* (familiar), *madya* (middle), and *krama* (high) — whose correct deployment marks social competence. It was historically written in the Brahmi-derived *Hanacaraka* / *Carakan* script and in the Arabic-derived right-to-left *Pegon* script used by *pesantren* Islamic schools; today the Latin alphabet dominates. Around 97% of Javanese are Sunni Muslim, with a Catholic minority concentrated around Yogyakarta, Semarang, and Muntilan, and Protestant congregations in the Gereja Kristen Jawa. The Tenggerese of the Mount Bromo highlands remain Hindu. Underlying and cutting across formal religious affiliation is ***Kejawèn*** (also *Kebatinan*, *Agama Jawa*), a syncretic mystical practice fusing animism, Hindu-Buddhist cosmology, and Sufi Islam, expressed in meditation, ancestor veneration, pilgrimage to sacred graves such as those of the *Wali Songo*, and belief in the tutelary sea-goddess Nyai Roro Kidul of the southern ocean, ritually linked to the Sultans of Yogyakarta.
