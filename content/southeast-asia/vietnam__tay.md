## Overview

The Tày, also known as the Thổ, T'o, Tai Tho, Ngan, Phen, Thu Lao, or Pa Di, are a Central Tai-speaking ethnic group living in northern Vietnam. According to a 2019 census, 1.8 million Tày people lived in Vietnam, making them the second largest ethnic group in the country after the Kinh. Most live in Cao Bằng, Lạng Sơn, Bắc Kạn, Thái Nguyên, and Quảng Ninh provinces, along valleys and the lower slopes of mountains; they also live in parts of Bắc Ninh and Bắc Giang. Their agricultural life is centered on fertile plains, especially rice cultivation, alongside maize and sweet potato. Their folk culture includes Then religion, the *đàn tính* string instrument, Lồng tồng and Tăm khảu mảu festivals, distinctive rice-based foods, and the *chữ Nôm Tày* writing tradition.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Tày households commonly place an altar for ancestors in a central location in the house. The altar room is considered sacred: guests and women who have given birth are not allowed to sit on the bed in front of the altar.

## Music & performance

The *đàn tính* is a Tày string instrument with two or three strings. The Tày also use ritual and song texts associated with *chữ Nôm Tày*, a logographic script used for writing ritual texts and Then songs. The script is similar to sawndip and was created during the reign of the Mạc dynasty on the basis of Chinese characters. Some characters were borrowed directly from Han characters, while others were created locally from Chinese components.

The majority of Tày people practice Then, an indigenous religion involving tutelary gods, gods of the natural environment, ancestors, and progenitors of human groups. Religious ceremonies formerly involved reciting Chinese characters; alongside the characters, glosses are now used because many practitioners can no longer recite them and Vietnamese schools do not teach Chinese characters. The sources identify the lượn works *Tam Nguyên* and *Hồng Nhan Tứ Quý*: Bế Văn Phụng was the author of *Tam Nguyên*, while Nông Quỳnh Vân was the author of *Hồng Nhan Tứ Quý*.

## Festivals & rituals

*Lồng tồng*, literally “Going down the rice paddy,” is celebrated after the New Lunar Year Festival to pray for a good harvest. The festival includes *khảu thuy*, a cake offered to God and the Earth. Glutinous rice is soaked in water of common water hyacinth and red flower-sandbox tree ash, mixed with taro and a kind of wine, then boiled and pounded. It is cut into square-shaped or canarium fruit-shaped pieces and dried in the sun. Before the festival it is fried until swollen, soaked in molasses, and then coated in fried-rice powder. *Pẻng phạ* is also associated with Lồng tồng.

*Tăm khảu mảu*, literally “Braying the young rice,” is celebrated as the rice is ripening. *Khảu nua nàng tô*, glutinous rice with grub, is associated with the New Rice Festival on the moonday of the 8th lunar month. *Slip Sli* takes place on the Monday of the 7th lunar month and includes several cakes. *Pẻng đắng*, literally “ash water cake,” is made for the Double Fifth Festival. *Pẻng khạ*, or God’s cake, is made for the New Year festival, while *pẻng toóc* is also associated with the New Year Festival.

Tày customs were altered by Vietnamese and Confucian patriarchal structures, although some customs persisted. Marriage was preferred within the clan. Young wives lived with their parents until giving birth to their first child. Polygamy with multiple equal wives and legitimacy of issue was practiced.

## Foodways

Tày foodways center on glutinous rice and include cakes prepared for festivals and offerings. *Pẻng rày*, or ant’s egg cake, is made from glutinous rice, called *khảu* in Tày, black ant eggs, called *rày*, and leaves of a kind of fig, called *bâu ngỏa*. *Pẻng hó* is a glutinous rice cake with pork and mung bean. *Nà Giàng khảu sli* is a glutinous cake with peanuts made in Nà Giàng, Phù Ngọc commune, Hà Quảng district, Cao Bằng Province. *Pẻng ngá* is a peanut cake, while *pẻng cuội* is a banana cake. Other foods named for the *Slip Sli* festival are *pẻng nhứa cáy*, *pẻng mịt*, and *pẻng tải*, literally “carrying cake.”

The sources also name *Coóng phù*, *pẻng chì*, *khảu slec*, *pẻng khua*, or smiling cake, *coóc mò*, *ửng sệch phàn*, *đăm đeng*, *khảu lang*, *pẻng cao*, and *khảu thuy*. *Áp chao* is another named food. *Mác lịch* refers to Trùng Khánh chestnut. *Ché khôm* is Cao Bằng bitter tea, identified as *Ilex kaushue*, with *Ilex kudingcha* given as a synonym. *Khau rả* is identified as *Ampelopsis cantoniensis*, and *mác mật* as *Clausena excavata*.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Tày used *chữ Nôm Tày* to write ritual texts and Then songs. The script was created during the reign of the Mạc dynasty and is based on Chinese characters. The sources name the lượn *Tam Nguyên*, authored by Bế Văn Phụng, and *Hồng Nhan Tứ Quý*, authored by Nông Quỳnh Vân. Hoàng Đức Hậu is described as a famous poet composing the first of two parallel lines of verse that nobody could make the second one. Hoàng Đức Triều, whose pen name was An Định, was a communist and poet who worked with Ho Chi Minh; Nông Quốc Chấn is also identified as a famous poet.

## Language & religion

Tày belongs to the Tai languages of the Kra–Dai language family and is the major Tai language of Vietnam, spoken by more than a million Tày people in northeastern Vietnam. Varieties include Central Tày, Eastern Tày, Southern Tày, Northern Tày, Tày Trùng Khánh, Thu Lao, Tày Bảo Lạc, and Tày Bình Liêu. Tày Bảo Lạc is spoken in Bảo Lạc District, western Cao Bằng province, while Tày Trùng Khánh is spoken in Trùng Khánh District, northeastern Cao Bằng province. Tày is also spoken in northern Laos, among emigrants in Vietnam’s Central Highlands region, and by some speakers in western countries.

Tày literacy in their own language is probably around 5% or less. The former ritual writing system was *chữ Nôm Tày*. The current Tày-Nùng orthography was created in 1961 on the basis of *chữ Quốc ngữ* and approved by the government of Vietnam following Decree 206-CP; its alphabet consists of 31 letters. Most Tày practice Then, involving tutelary gods, natural-environment gods, ancestors, and progenitors of human groups. Some Tày have adopted Mahayana Buddhism under Vietnamese and Chinese cultural influence.
