## Overview

The Nu are one of the 56 ethnic groups recognized by the People’s Republic of China. Their population is 36,575, divided into Northern, Central and Southern groups. They live mainly in Kachin State and Yunnan province, with 90% in Gongshan, Fugong and Lanping counties of Yunnan Province. Their homeland consists of high mountains and deep ravines crossed by the Dulong, Irrawaddy, and Nujiang rivers. The Nu speak various languages in the Tibeto-Burman family and do not have a written language of their own, although a script based on the Latin alphabet has been developed with government assistance. Their folk culture, as described by the source, includes distinctive clothing, mountain houses, agricultural practices, animist traditions, Tibetan Buddhism, festivals, songs, dances and storytelling.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Linen clothing is popular among both genders. Women generally wear linen or cotton tunics with sleeves, buttoned on the left, together with long skirts. Young girls often wear aprons over their tunics. Women may wear necklaces of colored plastic beads, as well as head or chest ornaments made with coral, agates, shells and silver coins. Large copper earrings can hang to the shoulder.

Men often wear linen sleeved tunics over shorts. Almost every man wears a string of coral on his left ear and carries a machete on the left side of his waist. When going out, men often carry machetes, bows and arrow bags made from animal felt. Black turbans are worn around the head, although ear-length haircuts are also common.

### Architecture

The Nu build houses near the mountains from bamboo-slips or wooden planks. Wooden houses are more prevalent because of their greater strength. The houses have two stories. The lower floor functions as a barn and contains livestock, food and other stored items. The upper floor contains the living quarters and is divided into inner and outer rooms.

The inner room serves as a bedroom and storeroom. The outer room functions as a kitchen and guestroom.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do describe bamboo and wooden farm tools used for planting, along with machetes, bows and arrow bags made from animal felt. Copper earrings, silver coins and other metal ornaments are mentioned as forms of adornment.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Nu women wear necklaces strung with colored plastic beads. Some wear head or chest ornaments incorporating strings of coral, agates, shells and silver coins. Large copper earrings hang toward the shoulder. Men commonly wear a string of coral on the left ear.

## Music & performance

The Nu gather in the open air during the tribal flower-fairy festival, singing, dancing and telling stories. The festival also includes ball matches resembling football matches and bow-and-arrow competitions.

## Dance & theatre

Dance forms part of the open-air gatherings held during the tribal flower-fairy festival. Participants also sing and tell stories, while ball matches and bow-and-arrow competitions take place.

## Festivals & rituals

The Nu celebrate the tribal flower-fairy festival, mainly among Nu communities in the Gongshan area of Yunnan province. According to the Chinese lunar calendar, it begins on 15 March annually and lasts three days. The festival is connected with a legend of flooding by the Nu River and a Nu girl named A-Rong, who created a rope-bridge inspired by a spider’s web. After refusing the marriage demands of the chief of the Hou tribe, A-Rong escaped into the mountains and eventually became a stone statue in a cave.

During the festival, people pick bunches of azaleas and sacrifice to the fairy maiden at a cave literally known as Fairy People Cave. Afterwards, people drink together at home, dress in traditional clothing and gather outdoors to sing, dance and tell stories. Ball matches and bow-and-arrow competitions are also held.

The Jijilamu festival is a spring festival lasting about 15 days, from the end of lunar December to the beginning of lunar January. It is mainly celebrated by Nu living in Bijiang, Fugong, Gongshan, Lanping and Weixi counties of Yunnan Province. Losar is also celebrated by Tibetan Buddhist Nu. Before Jijilamu, households butcher pigs, make soft-rice dumplings, brew wine and clean their courtyards. On New Year’s Eve, they place corn and dishes of food on a three-legged barbecue, together with three cups and three pieces of meat, and pray for a good harvest and strong livestock.

## Foodways

Agriculture is the Nu’s main occupation. Major crops include maize, buckwheat, barley, potatoes, yams and beans. The sources state that output is low because chemical fertilizers are not used and crop-planting techniques are described as primitive. The annual grain harvest is approximately 100 kg short of per-capita need, so the diet is supplemented by hunting and fishing.

The sources mention soft-rice dumplings, pigs, corn, dishes of food and brewed wine in connection with the Jijilamu festival. On New Year’s Eve, corn and other dishes are placed on a three-legged barbecue, with three cups and three pieces of meat, before family members pray for the coming year.

## Oral tradition & literature

The flower-fairy festival is associated with the story of A-Rong, a Nu girl who created a rope-bridge after being inspired by a spider’s web. The story explains her transformation into a stone statue and the annual observance of the festival. During the celebration, people tell stories in open-air gatherings.

## Language & religion

The Nu speak various languages in the Tibeto-Burman family. They do not have a written language of their own, although the Chinese government has helped develop a script based on the Latin alphabet.

Nu religious life combines Tibetan Buddhism and tribal Animism, which has close affinities with the natural world. A minority have converted to Christianity. Tibetan Buddhism is mainly professed by the Northern Nu, while most Southern and Central groups have retained their Animist faith. Christianity has made some inroads into the Southern group through French missionaries in the second half of the 19th century.
