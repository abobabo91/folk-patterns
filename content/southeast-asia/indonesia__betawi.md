## Overview

The Betawi, also called Batavians or *Orang Betawi*, are an Austronesian ethnic group native to Jakarta and its immediate outskirts. They are descendants of people who inhabited Batavia from the 17th century onwards and formed through the mixing of populations from Indonesia and abroad. The group’s name emerged in the 18th century, while Betawi self-identification developed later. Betawi culture combines Malay, Javanese, Sundanese, Arabic, Chinese, Portuguese, Dutch, and other influences. Betawi Malay is a Malay-based creole spoken in and around Jakarta, although traditional varieties are endangered and are increasingly associated with older speakers in some suburban areas. The group is especially significant for its distinctive language, architecture, music, dance, ceremonies, martial arts, and cuisine.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe ceremonial wedding clothing. Betawi wedding clothing displays Chinese influence in the bride’s costume and Arabian influence in the groom’s costume.

### Architecture

Betawi people traditionally lived in *kampungs* around Batavia, where houses stood among orchards. As Jakarta became denser, many traditional villages became tightly packed urban settlements, although more authentic villages survived on the city’s outskirts, including Setu Babakan, Jagakarsa, Condet, and areas bordering Depok. Traditional houses are found especially in Betawi villages in East and South Jakarta.

Three principal house styles are identified: *rumah bapang* or *rumah kebaya*, *rumah gudang*, and the Javanese-influenced *rumah joglo*. Most Betawi houses have gabled roofs, while the *rumah joglo* has a high-pointed roof. A characteristic ornament is *gigi balang*, a row of wooden shingles applied to the roof fascia. Another feature is the *langkan*, a framed open front terrace used for receiving guests and as an outdoor living space.

In the coastal Marunda area, North Jakarta, *rumah panggung* houses are built on stilts in response to wet conditions, tides, and floods. The sources describe these houses as possibly influenced by Malay and Bugis houses. *Rumah Si Pitung* in Marunda, Cilincing, North Jakarta, is identified as a preserved example.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do mention wooden shingles used in *gigi balang* roof ornamentation and bamboo in the construction of the large masked-puppet effigies called *ondel-ondel*.

## Music & performance

Betawi music reflects the group’s history of contact with Portuguese, Chinese, and other communities. **Gambang kromong** and **Tanjidor**, together with **Keroncong Kemayoran**, are identified as Betawi musical traditions. The sources state that these forms derive from *kroncong* music associated with Portuguese Mardijker people of the Tugu area in North Jakarta. “Si Jali-jali” is named as an example of a traditional Betawi song.

The Betawi musical repertoire therefore includes a strong Portuguese connection alongside broader local influences. The sources also describe Betawi culture as drawing on Sundanese, Javanese, Chinese, and other traditions.

Betawi music is part of a wider cultural sphere in which local traditions have also been promoted through Jakarta’s municipal patronage. The Betawi dialect is frequently spoken in television shows and dramas, and a Betawi-style Christian music video is listed among external materials associated with the group.

## Dance & theatre

The Betawi performance tradition includes masked dance, large effigy performance, and popular theatre. **Ondel-ondel** consists of large bamboo masked-puppet giant effigies. The form is compared with Chinese-Balinese Barong Landung and Sundanese Badawang, both described as masked-dance art forms.

Betawi dance costumes show Chinese and European influences. The movements of **Yapong** are derived from Sundanese Jaipongan dance and include a Chinese stylistic element. **Topeng Betawi**, or Betawi mask dance, is another named dance tradition.

Betawi popular folk drama is called **lenong**. It is a form of theatre whose subjects range from local urban legends and foreign stories to the everyday life of Betawi people.

## Festivals & rituals

Betawi ceremonies include wedding customs, public gatherings, Muslim circumcision, and the **Lebaran Betawi** festival. **Mangkeng** is performed at important public gatherings, especially weddings. Its stated purposes are to bring good luck and ward off rain. The ceremony is performed by the village shaman, also called the Pangkeng shaman.

A Betawi wedding includes **palang pintu**, literally “door’s bar,” a staged demonstration of *silat Betawi*. It presents a choreographed mock fight between the groom’s entourage and the bride’s *jagoan kampung*, or local champion. The groom’s entourage wins as the village champions welcome him to the bride’s home. Wedding clothing combines Chinese influence in the bride’s costume with Arabian influence in the groom’s costume.

Firecrackers are used during weddings, circumcisions, and other celebrations. The custom of bringing *roti buaya*, or crocodile bread, to a wedding is described as probably European in origin. Other named ceremonies include *sunatan* or *khitanan*, Muslim circumcision, and Lebaran Betawi.

## Foodways

Betawi cuisine developed in the port city of Batavia through contact among Indonesian, Chinese, Indian, Arab, Portuguese, Dutch, and other communities. It is influenced by Peranakan, Malay, Sundanese, Javanese, Indian, Arabic, and European cuisines. Rice is a staple, appearing as steamed rice, coconut-milk rice such as *nasi uduk*, or compressed rice cakes including *ketupat* and *lontong*.

Betawi cooking includes deep-frying, stir-frying, barbecuing, and braising, with sweet, sour, and salty flavours. Beef, mutton, and goat are favoured under Islamic halal dietary rules, which prohibit pork consumption. Freshwater fish are common, including snakehead fish and carp, while the sources note that seafood dishes are comparatively scarce for a coastal city.

Named dishes include *soto betawi*, beef offal soup with tomatoes and fried potatoes; *soto kaki*, made with beef or goat leg tendons and cartilage; *kerak telor*, a glutinous-rice cake cooked with egg, shredded coconut, and dried shrimp; *nasi ulam*; *ketoprak*; *gado-gado*; *sayur asem*; *semur jengkol*; *gabus pucung*; *pecak gabus*; *laksa betawi*; *nasi kebuli*; *nasi biryani*; *nasi goreng kambing*; and *nasi goreng domba*. Other foods include *asinan betawi*, *rujak juhi*, *sate kuah*, *soto tangkar*, *sayur besan*, and *pindang serani*.

Snacks, sweets, and drinks include *roti buaya*, *roti gambang*, *kue ape*, *kue cubit*, *kue cucur*, *kue putu*, *dodol*, *emping*, *geplak*, *wajik*, *bir pletok*, *cendol*, *es cincau*, *es selendang mayang*, and *sekoteng*.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that *lenong*, Betawi popular folk drama, draws on local urban legends, foreign stories, and the everyday life of Betawi people. The sources also identify Si Pitung as a legendary bandit, but provide no narrative account of his stories or their performance contexts.

## Language & religion

Betawi, also known as Batavian, Jakartanese, Betawi Malay, Batavian Malay, or Jakarta Malay, is a Malay-based creole spoken in and around Jakarta. It contains substantial Hokkien Chinese, Arabic, Portuguese, and Dutch vocabulary, alongside Sundanese, Javanese, and Balinese influences. The sources identify traditional urban, suburban, and rural varieties, as well as dialects associated with places including Kampung Melayu, Tanah Abang, Karet, Kebayoran, Pasar Rebo, Ciputat, Gunungsindur, Pebayuran, Mauk, and Sepatan. Traditional varieties are endangered, while Colloquial Jakarta Indonesian serves as a lingua franca.

A substantial majority of Betawi people follow Sunni Islam. Islam is described as a major source of Betawi culture and identity. The sources also document Betawi Christians, including communities associated with Portuguese Mardijker ancestry in Kampung Tugu and Christian Betawi communities in Kampung Sawah. Betawi culture therefore includes Muslim, Christian, and Chinese Peranakan elements. The Catholic St. Servatius Church in Kampung Sawah uses Betawi culture and language in its mass.
