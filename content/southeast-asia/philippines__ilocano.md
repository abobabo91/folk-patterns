## Overview

The Ilocano people, also called Ilokáno, Ilóko, Ilúko, or Samtóy, are an Austronesian ethnolinguistic group native to the Philippines. Their historical homeland is the Ilocos Region on the northwestern coast of Luzon, and Ilocano communities are also found throughout northern and central Luzon, including the Cagayan Valley, the Cordillera Administrative Region, and the northern and western areas of Central Luzon. Large populations live in Metro Manila, Mindoro, Palawan, Mindanao, Hawaii, California, and Canada. Iloco is an Austronesian language and one of the major languages of the Philippines. The Ilocanos are associated in the sources with agriculture, seafaring, trade, family and communal ties, Roman Catholic influences, pre-colonial animist-polytheistic traditions, oral literature, life rituals, songs, dances, and resistance to Spanish and American colonial rule.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Ilocano settlements were called *íli*, with smaller groups of houses known as *purók*. The sources describe Ilocano communities as coastal and valley settlements, including settlements in sheltered coves called *luék*, *luëk*, or *loóc* along the Ilocos coastline.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources mention porcelain, rice, silk, cotton, beeswax, gems, beads, precious minerals, and gold among goods traded by Ilocanos and neighboring peoples. Ilocanos used wooden boats known as *biray* or *bilog* for trade and cargo.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention gems and beads as traded goods.

## Music & performance

Ilocano oral performance includes folk songs and war songs. The sources also name *kankanta* as songs and *dállot* as an improvised long poem delivered in a melodic manner. These forms belong to a wider literary and oral tradition that preserves Ilocano identity and expresses the group’s adaptability within Filipino cultural life. They do state that basi, a sugarcane-based alcoholic beverage, was important in rituals surrounding childbirth, marriage, and death.

The sources describe the Ilocanos historically as both agriculturalists and seafarers. Their social and economic life included trade and barter with the Cordillerans, Pangasinans, Sambals, Tagalogs, Ibanags, and foreign traders from China, Japan, and other Maritime Southeast Asian countries. These activities formed part of a maritime trade network extending across the Indian Ocean and South China Sea, but no performance tradition connected with that network is specified.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name *salsala* as dances within Ilocano cultural expression.

The sources also mention literary duels called *bucanegan*. These are presented as part of Ilocano literary and oral culture, alongside songs, poems, proverbs, and dances.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe several life-cycle contexts in which *basi* was culturally important. Basi, a sugarcane-based alcoholic beverage, was integral to rituals surrounding childbirth, marriage, and death. Its production was also a vital industry in Ilocos.

The sources describe early Ilocano religious life as animistic and polytheistic, with the belief that *anitos*, or spirits, resided in the natural environment. Named figures in the Ilocano belief system included Buni, the god of the earth; Parsua, the creator; and Apo Langit, the lord of heaven. Regional variations developed because Ilocano settlements were dispersed, and Ilocano religious traditions were influenced by Cordillerans, Tagalogs, and Chinese culture.

Spanish missionaries later initiated systematic evangelization in Ilocos. By 1574, Vigan had become the center of Spanish administration and Christianization efforts. The sources characterize contemporary Ilocano culture as combining Roman Catholic influences with pre-colonial animist-polytheistic traditions.

The Basi Revolt began on September 16, 1807, in present-day Piddig and spread through the province. It followed Spanish regulation of basi consumption and production. The revolt was led by Pedro Mateo and Saralogo Ambaristo, and participants from Ilocos Norte and Ilocos Sur marched toward Vigan. On September 28, Spanish troops ambushed Ilocano forces near the Bantaoay River in San Ildefonso. The revolt led the colonial government to partition the Ilocos province into Ilocos Norte and Ilocos Sur.

## Foodways

The sources identify basi as a local Ilocano beverage made from sugarcane. It was an alcoholic drink with cultural, social, and economic importance, used in rituals surrounding childbirth, marriage, and death. Production of basi was a vital industry in Ilocos. The Spanish colonial government imposed a monopoly on its production and sale, required producers to sell it at a low price, and regulated its consumption. These measures contributed to the Basi Revolt, also called the Ambaristo Revolt.

Rice was a central agricultural product and a traded commodity. The Ilocanos traded with Cordilleran communities associated with rice terraces in the Cordillera Central, as well as with Pangasinans, Sambals, Tagalogs, Ibanags, and foreign merchants. The sources also mention agricultural practices introduced during Austronesian migrations.

The sources describe Ilocano society as agricultural and maritime. Trade goods included porcelain, rice, silk, cotton, beeswax, gems, beads, precious minerals, and gold.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ilocano literature traces its origins to an animistic past. Creation narratives include figures such as Aran, Angalo, and Namarsua, the Creator, as well as stories about benevolent and malevolent spirits. Folk and war songs were important forms of expression, and the *dállot* was an improvised long poem delivered melodically.

The major named epic is *Biag ni Lam-ang*, meaning “The Life of Lam-ang.” It is described as one of the few indigenous narratives to survive colonial influence and tells of the adventures of the Ilocano hero Lam-ang. The 17th-century author Pedro Bucaneg is associated with the epic and is celebrated as the “Father of Ilocano Poetry and Literature.”

Ilocano cultural expression also includes *dandániw*, or poems; *pagsasao*, or proverbs; *kankanta*, or songs; *salsala*, or dances; and *bucanegan*, or literary duels. During the Spanish regime, Iloco poetry was influenced by Spanish literary forms. Francisco Lopez published the *Doctrina Christiana* in Iloco in 1621, and the *Gramatica Ilokana* was published in 1895. The sources also mention Leona Florentino, Manuel Arguilla, Carlos Bulosan, Sionil Jose, and Isabelo de los Reyes in connection with Ilocano literary history and preservation.

## Language & religion

Iloco, also called Ilóko, Ilúko, Ilocáno, or Ilokáno, is an Austronesian language spoken primarily in the Philippines. It is one of the major languages of the country, serves as a regional lingua franca and second language in Northern Luzon, and is also spoken in the Cagayan Valley, Central Luzon, Mindoro, Palawan, Mindanao, Hawaii, California, and Canada. It belongs to the Malayo-Polynesian branch and constitutes its own branch within the Philippine Cordilleran subfamily.

The sources describe two commonly agreed dialects, Amiánan, or Northern, and Abagátan, or Southern, differentiated by the pronunciation of the letter *e*. Ilocano writing includes the pre-colonial syllabic system known as *Baybayin*, an abugida. The Ilocano version of the *Doctrina Cristiana*, published in 1621, was the first to designate coda consonants with a virama.

Ilocano religion includes pre-colonial animist-polytheistic practices involving *anitos* and named deities such as Buni, Parsua, and Apo Langit. The sources also describe Roman Catholic influence following Spanish colonization and evangelization.
