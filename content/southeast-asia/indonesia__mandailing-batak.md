## Overview

The Mandailing, also known as Mandailing Batak, are one of the sub-ethnic groups of the Batak people. They are found mainly in North Sumatra and West Sumatra, Indonesia, and are also present in Malaysia, especially in Selangor and Perak. The region associated with their name is Luat Mandailing, now almost in Mandailing Natal Regency in North Sumatra. The Mandailing are closely related to the Angkola and Toba. Their language is an Austronesian language spoken mainly in several regencies of Sumatra and in parts of Riau Province. The Mandailing people are almost entirely Muslim, although a small portion of the community embraces Christianity. Their folk-culture significance in the supplied sources lies especially in their family-name system, oral and literary traditions, language varieties, foodways, and the drum ensemble called *Gordang Sambilan*.

## Music & performance

The Mandailing have a traditional ensemble of drums called *Gordang Sambilan*.

Mandailing literary and musical traditions include *Ende Ungut-Ungut*, a form differentiated by theme. The source describes *Ende* as an expression of the heart associated with experiences such as the misery of life caused by death and abandonment. It also contains knowledge, advice, moral teachings, and the kinship system. *Ende* laments use the rhyme scheme AB AB or AA AA, and their attachments usually use many plant names because the language leaves.

The Mandailing language also has musical and dramatic forms connected with recorded entertainment. These include a musical drama of the 1970s on a tape cassette recorder, the drama *Sampuraga namaila marina*, an early VCD fragment containing a Mandailing song album, and an album by Tapsel, Madina, Palas and Paluta.

## Dance & theatre

They do mention Mandailing-language dramatic works. The musical drama *Sampuraga namaila marina* is listed among Mandailing-language entertainment, together with a musical drama of the 1970s recorded on a tape cassette recorder.

The source also states that Soetan Habiaran Siregar explored the languages, dances, and songs from Angkola-Mandailing.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe language used during life-cycle ceremonies. *Hata andung* is a form of literary language used in the tradition of *mangandung*, or wailing, at traditional ceremonies of marriage or death. *Ende Ungut-Ungut* likewise includes expressions associated with death, abandonment, advice, moral teachings, and kinship.

The sources discuss religious change rather than a ritual calendar. The Mandailing people are almost entirely Muslims, and a portion converted to Islam during the Padri War when Muslim clerics from west Sumatra pressured them to adopt the religion. After conversion, Islam became firmly established through integration with the larger Muslim Malay world. The Mandailing retained many native religious practices and adopted an indigenized form of Islam until the modern era, especially in Malaysia, where they were forced to conform to state-sponsored Islam.

A small portion of the community embraces Christianity. Christian teachings first reached the Mandailing region in the Pakantan area in 1821. The Mandailing Christian community is currently around ± 1%, and almost all Mandailing Christians synod at the Angkola Protestant Christian Church.

## Foodways

The supplied sources identify *daun ubi tumbuk*, or mashed tapioca leaves, as a famous food among the Mandailing people. The dish is described as being lush with *bunga kantan*, lemongrass, and coconut milk flavor.

## Oral tradition & literature

Mandailing literary art is transmitted through a distinctive tradition. *Marturi* tells stories in a verbal social context, with stories passed from generation to generation. Its plots use advanced themes and contain much material concerning manners.

*Ende Ungut-Ungut* is differentiated by theme and expresses the heart through experiences including the misery of life caused by death and abandonment. It also conveys knowledge, advice, moral teachings, and the kinship system. Its laments use AB AB or AA AA rhyme schemes. The source states that attachments usually contain many plant names because the language leaves.

The source identifies *poda* as a kind of storyteller that gives advice and moral teachings in a context appropriate for children. In colonial-period literature, Soetan Martua Raja’s *Doea Sadjoli: Boekoe Siseon ni Dakdanak di Sikola* adopted this form.

Named Mandailing and Angkola-Mandailing literary works include *Si Bulus-bulus Si Rumbuk-rumbuk* by Willem Iskander, *Turi-Turian* by Soetan Hasoendoetan, and *Sitti Djaoerah: Padan Djandji na Togoe*, an Angkola Mandailing-language series published in the weekly *Pustaha* in Sibolga between 1927 and 1929. Mangaradja Goenoeng Sorik Marapi wrote *Turian-turian ni Raja Gorga di Langit dohot Raja Suasa di Portibi*. Contemporary Mandailing literature is described as no longer developing since pre-independence because the national education curriculum uses the national language.

## Language & religion

Mandailing Batak, or Mandailing, is an Austronesian language spoken in Sumatra, the northern island of Indonesia. It is spoken mainly in Mandailing Natal Regency, North Padang Lawas Regency, Padang Lawas Regency, eastern parts of Labuhan Batu Regency, North Labuhan Batu Regency, South Labuhan Batu Regency, and northwestern parts of Riau Province. It is written using the Latin script but historically used Batak script.

Pangaduan Lubis proposes five registers: *Hata somal*, used in everyday life; *Hata andung*, the literary language of *mangandung* at marriage or death ceremonies; *Hata teas dohot jampolak*, used in quarrels or to berate someone; *Hata si baso*, used specifically by the *baso* or *datu*; and *Hata parkapur*, formerly used when searching for camphor. The Mandailing also had *Hata bulung-bulung*, a communication tool or language using plant leaves as symbols rather than sound.

The Mandailing people are almost entirely Muslims. A small portion embraces Christianity, and many native religious practices continued within an indigenized form of Islam until the modern era.
