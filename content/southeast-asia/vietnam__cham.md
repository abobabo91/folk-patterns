## Overview

The Cham are the only Austronesian people whose mainland homeland lies in Southeast Asia. Once rulers of the maritime Champa confederation until 1832, they now live as minority communities preserving one of the few living matrilineal Hindu traditions on the mainland alongside a distinctive Islamic strand. Their material culture — especially textiles, temple architecture, and ritual objects — reflects centuries of Hindu-Buddhist and later Islamic influence, with patterns and motifs traceable to Champa royal workshops.

## Material culture

Cham material culture blends Hindu-Buddhist temple aesthetics with everyday utility, from sacred textiles to functional pottery made using methods unchanged for centuries.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Cham weaving preserves supplementary-weft brocade techniques and motif vocabularies directly copied from Champa temple reliefs.

- **Mỹ Nghiệp brocade weaving** (*Mỹ Nghiệp (Ca Klaing)*) — The village near Phan Rang is the principal center for Cham supplementary-weft brocade using body-tensioned looms and traditional indigo, madder red, and turmeric yellows on dark grounds.
- **Tubular wrap-skirt cloth** (*aban*) — The everyday format of Cham weaving, with finest examples carrying banded end-panels with dense pattern registers for ritual and wedding use.
- **Ceremonial sashes and shoulder cloths** (*khan matơm, khan djram*) — Long narrow sashes worn by Ahier and Bani clergy, woven with reserved motif bands at each end and a plain middle field.
- **Temple-sourced motif vocabulary** (*bingu kalik, bingu jôm mưh, makara, apsara*) — Sandstone reliefs from Mỹ Sơn, Po Klong Garai, and Po Nagar supply scroll, lotus, dragon, and celestial-dancer patterns that weavers still reproduce in thread.
- **Weft-ikat ceremonial skirts** (*aban*) — A minor weft-ikat tradition survives for women's ceremonial wrap-skirts in Panduranga.

### Clothing & dress

Cham dress is layered and gender-distinct, with wrapped skirts, long tunics, and headcloths that signal marital status, religious rank, and ceremonial occasion.

- **Women's everyday tunic and skirt** (*aw kamei, aban*) — An ankle-length tunic worn over the tubular wrap-skirt and secured with a woven belt.
- **Men's long tunic and turban** (*aw tah, khan matơm*) — A white or indigo tunic with trousers or wrap-skirt and a white turban whose folding indicates lay, cleric, or priestly status.
- **Married women's headcloth** (*khan*) — Bound under the hair; Bani women in An Giang wear a longer white veil closer to a Malay tudung, reflecting Malacca–Kelantan contact.
- **High priest's robes** (*Po Adhia*) — A white robe embroidered at the hem with red and yellow bingu bands, broad woven sash, and tall turban topped with a tassel.
- **Wedding costume** (*aban*) — Uses the densest brocade a household can afford, with full-register pattern along both ends of skirt and shoulder cloth.

### Architecture

Cham monuments are kiln-fired brick tower-sanctuaries with corbelled interiors and multi-storey roofs; vernacular houses are timber on piles with palm-thatch.

- **Tower-sanctuary complexes** (*kalan*) — Brick structures at Mỹ Sơn, Po Nagar, Po Klong Garai, and Po Rome with square cellae, corbelled false-vaults, and registers of carved sandstone featuring pilasters, false doors, and finials in linga, lotus bud, or makara forms.
- **Traditional Panduranga compound** (*sang*) — A cluster of low timber houses on short piles with palm-leaf thatch; the sang mư yâu (eastern mother house) holds the matrilineal ancestor altar.
- **Bani mosque** (*sang magik*) — Single-storey structures with tiled hip roofs, closer in form to a Cham house than to a domed West Asian mosque.
- **Mekong stilt houses** — An Giang riverine Cham build wooden stilt houses along the Mekong and its arms.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Cham potters and metalworkers produce handmade earthenware and ritual vessels using ancient techniques still practiced in continuous workshops.

- **Bàu Trúc pottery village** (*Paley Hamu Trok*) — One of mainland Southeast Asia's oldest continuously functioning ceramic villages, where women potters build vessels without a wheel, paddle and smooth by hand, and fire in bonfires of rice straw and cow dung.
- **Unglazed earthenware** — Water jars, cooking pots, incense burners, and ritual figurines in warm russet ground streaked black from reduction firing.
- **Bronze and silver ritual vessels** — Temple offerings and small silver amulets produced by Cham metalworkers.
- **Ceremonial dagger** (*kris*) — A kris-like dagger worn by the priesthood.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Cham jewelry is silver and gold, worn for daily life and ceremony, with wedding pieces inherited matrilineally alongside textiles and ancestor cloths.

- **Ear ornaments** (*ana taney*) — Plain hoop earrings for daily use and elongated pendant earrings for ceremony.
- **Priestess ritual jewelry** (*muk pajau*) — Ahier priestesses wear heavy silver hair pins and a beaded chest chain during rituals.
- **Wedding jewelry** — Bracelets, neck rings, and filigree hairpins inherited matrilineally along with the aban and ancestor cloths.
- **Henna application** — Bani women use henna sparingly at weddings, a practice absorbed from Malay Muslim contact rather than an old Cham element.

## Music & performance

Cham ceremonial music centers on paired goblet drums and a shawm ensemble that accompanies every temple procession, with solo fiddle and priestly chant for ritual invocations.

- **Barrel drum pair** (*ginang*) — Double-headed drums played in pairs, struck with one hand and one stick, forming the foundation of temple ensembles.
- **Frame drum** (*baranâng*) — A shallow single-headed drum played alongside the ginang and saranai in Kate processions and Rija ceremonies.
- **Quadruple-reed shawm** (*saranai*) — Cognate with the Indian shehnai and Khmer sralai, played with drums in temple worship.
- **Solo fiddle carved from jackfruit wood** (*kanhi*) — A two-stringed spike fiddle often crowned with an apsara figure, played by the Ong Kadhar during Ahier rites.
- **Priestly recitation and chant** (*damnây, ariya, dauh ru con*) — Invocatory hymn cycles, verse narratives, and lullabies; Bani communities have parallel Qur'anic recitation and doa chant in liturgical Arabic.

## Dance & theatre

Cham dance draws directly from temple relief iconography; the signature form is a slow solo featuring the bent-elbow tribhanga pose and flared fingers.

- **Temple-relief apsara dance** (*Apsara*) — A slow lyrical solo whose bent-elbow tribhanga pose, flared fingers, and raised heel are drawn directly from Champa temple relief.
- **Fan dance** (*Múa quạt*) — Performed by young women at Kate ceremonies.
- **Water-jar balancing dance** (*Múa đội lu*) — Performed by young women at Kate ceremonies.
- **Possession-dance ceremony** (*muk rija*) — A female medium through whom ancestral kings speak, alternating male and female garments as different spirits mount her during Rija Nagar and Rija Praong ceremonies.
- **Sung dramatic genealogy** (*Ong Kadhar*) — A hereditary singer who chants invocation hymns and royal genealogies, functioning as a sung dramatic form.

## Festivals & rituals

Cham ritual life is organized around two major festivals anchored at temple towers, with village-level renewal rites and elaborate life-cycle ceremonies marking matrilineal inheritance.

- **Kate festival** (*Katé*) — Held on the first day of the seventh Cham lunar month (September or October), the largest annual Ahier gathering opens with processions of sacred garments from highland custodians to Po Klong Garai, Po Rome, and Po Nagar, where priests bathe the mukhalinga and lead three days of celebration.
- **Women's festival** (*Cambur*) — Held in the ninth month at the same towers as the parallel women's gathering.
- **New Year renewal ceremony** (*Rija Nagar*) — A village-level rite in the first Cham month featuring possession-dance and renewal.
- **Muslim month of fasting and renewal** (*Ramawan*) — A distinctive observance in which only the acar clergy fast strictly, while lay Cham fast partially and visit ancestral graves at the month's opening.
- **Secondary burial with bone reliquary** (*kut, klaong*) — For Ahier dead, small forehead-bone fragments are kept in a family reliquary as the true seat of the ancestor; matrilineal wedding and girl's coming-of-age karoeh also mark life passages.

## Foodways

Cham cuisine blends Vietnamese, Khmer, and Malay influences with rice as staple and signature fermented beef sausage and coconut curries; religious dietary rules split sharply between Ahier and Bani.

- **Fermented beef sausage** (*tung lò mò*) — The flagship halal charcuterie of An Giang Cham, now sold across Ho Chi Minh City.
- **Beef and coconut curry** (*cà púa, cari cà ri*) — An Giang signature dish showing Malay-Indian influence; ka-pua is a fish-paste stew of Panduranga.
- **Palm products** — Palm sugar and toddy from the sugar palm are important; the Cambodian Cham palm-sugar block is a recognized regional product.
- **Kate ceremonial offerings** — Chicken, sticky rice, banana, and betel placed before the mukhalinga, with three cups of rice wine.
- **Religious dietary divide** — Ahier abstain from beef (Hindu heritage) while Bani abstain from pork; the forced feeding of pork to Muslim Cham and beef to Hindu Cham by Emperor Minh Mạng is remembered as the archetypal cultural violation.

## Oral tradition & literature

Cham literary culture is ancient, with the oldest attested Austronesian text (4th century), classical verse romances adapted from Malay sources, and genealogical chronicles preserved in family and mosque libraries.

- **Earliest Austronesian inscription** (*Đông Yên Châu*) — A 4th-century inscription in Old Cham is the earliest attested Austronesian text anywhere.
- **Long verse romance** (*Akayet*) — Classical epics such as Akayet Dewa Mưno, Akayet Inra Patra, and Akayet Um Mưrup, the last two adapted from Malay hikayat during 15th–17th-century Champa–Malacca contact.
- **Verse narrative** (*ariya*) — Shorter poetic genre for storytelling.
- **Priestly hymn** (*damnây*) — Didactic and devotional verses recited by clergy.
- **Royal genealogical chronicles** (*sakarai*) — Manuscripts held in family and mosque libraries; the Kan Imam San mosques in Kampong Chhnang preserve the largest corpus in Western Cham akhar srak script.

## Language & religion

Cham is a Malayo-Polynesian language written in three scripts and spoken in Eastern and Western dialects; religious life blends a localised Hindu tradition, syncretic Islam, and orthodox Sunni Islam.

- **Eastern Cham script** (*akhar thrah*) — A Brahmi-derived script still taught in Ninh Thuận.
- **Western Cham script** (*akhar srak*) — Preserved by the Kan Imam San sect around Phnom Oudong.
- **Arabic script for Muslim Cham** (*Jawi*) — Used by Muslim Cham of the Mekong Delta.
- **Hindu-Shaivite tradition** (*Ahier*) — A localised tradition centered on the mukhalinga and the deified god-kings; Ahier and Bani communities intermarry and cooperate at agricultural rites.
- **Syncretic Cham Islam** (*Bani*) — Only the acar clergy carry the full ritual load; retains matrilineal inheritance and pre-Islamic ancestor rites alongside Islamic observance.

- *acar* — Bani cleric or religious specialist

- *baranâng* — Shallow single-headed frame drum used in temple ensembles

- *kalan* — Brick tower-sanctuary of Champa temple complexes
- *kanhi* — Two-stringed spike fiddle carved from jackfruit wood

- *khan matơm* — White turban for men whose folding indicates lay, cleric, or priestly status
- *kut* — Family reliquary holding ancestor bone fragments
- *makara* — Aquatic monster figure in temple reliefs and textiles
- *muk pajau* — Ahier priestess
- *muk rija* — Female medium through whom ancestral spirits speak during possession dances
