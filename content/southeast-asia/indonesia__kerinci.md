## Overview

The Kerinci people—*Uhang Kincai* in Kerinci, *Kerinci* or *Kerinchi* in Malay, and *Urang Karinci* in Minangkabau—are an Austronesian ethnic group native to Jambi province. Their principal territory includes Kerinci Regency, Merangin and Bungo regencies, and the city of Sungai Penuh; communities also occur in neighbouring West Sumatra. The traditional area covers around 4,200 km² and has a population of 300,000 people. Kerinci Regency lies in hilly terrain within the Bukit Barisan Range, whose highest peak is Mount Kerinci. The Kerinci language is a Malayo-Polynesian Malayic language spoken chiefly in the Kerinci highlands and surrounding areas, particularly around Kerinci Seblat National Park. The group is especially documented through its language, its historical Incung writing system, and its geographically diverse dialect traditions.

## Music & performance

They do, however, record several performance-related activities conducted in Kerinci within teacher-training and language-support programs initiated by the Jambi provincial government. These include composing and reciting Kerinci poetry, storytelling, public speaking, short-story writing, traditional singing, and solo comedic performance. The same programs include instruction in writing and reading with the Incung script.

The Kerinci language source also refers to the oral literary register of the Tanjung Pauh Mudik dialect. In that dialect, truncated monosyllabic forms are very common in naturalistic speech, while the full forms found in the oral literary register, including the register of the *kunaung*, are considered archaic by its speakers.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe a sacred status associated with Incung manuscripts. Texts written on buffalo horns, bamboo, bark, cloth, and paper are treated as sacred and revered, and this view remains influential in Kerinci cultural life. From the 19th century onward, such manuscripts were kept as sacred objects.

The language source describes the emergence of Incung writing and literature as a holistic cultural expression encompassing nature, humanity, and divinity. It also states that the script was used to record ancestral history, literary prose, customary agreements, and spells.

## Foodways

The language source gives isolated lexical examples involving food and drink, including *pisa* “banana,” *pisa dalon pingga* “banana on a plate,” *pisan kunan toh dalon pingga* “that yellow banana is on a plate,” *binen* “drink,” *minum* “drink,” *ubuêk* “medicine,” *makan* “eat,” and *masak* “cook casually.” These examples illustrate vocabulary and grammatical patterns rather than a documented food tradition.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used document Kerinci oral and written language practices. Incung was historically used to record ancestral history, literary prose described as romantic and elegiac, customary agreements, and spells. The language source further identifies the oral literary register of the Tanjung Pauh Mudik dialect and names the *kunaung* in connection with that register.

Kerinci language-support programs include storytelling, composing and reciting Kerinci poetry, short-story writing, public speaking, traditional singing, and solo comedic performance. In Sungai Penuh, Kerinci language courses have been made compulsory in the school curriculum. The Jambi provincial government has also initiated teacher-training programs that promote Kerinci and teach writing and reading with the Incung script. These activities represent documented preservation and transmission efforts.

## Language & religion

Kerinci, also called *basê Kinci* or *basê Kincai*, is an Austronesian language and a Malayo-Polynesian Malayic language. As of 2000, its speakers were estimated at around 250,000. The language has approximately 130 sub-dialects grouped into seven main dialects: Gunung Raya, Danau Kerinci, Sitinjau Laut, Sungai Penuh, Pembantu Sungai Tutung, Belui Air Hangat, and Gunung Kerinci. Dialect classifications remain debated: one account identifies three major groups—Upper Kerinci, Middle Kerinci, and Lower Kerinci—while another identifies the “i” dialect and the “ai” dialect.

Kerinci was historically written in the Incung script, also called *Suhat Incoung*. In Kerinci, *incung* means “slanted” or “tilted.” The script is related to the Lampung script and the Rencong script and continued in active use until around 1825. Its use gradually declined with the spread of Islam in Kerinci and the onset of the 20th century. The sources mention Islam.
