## Overview

The Kuy are an indigenous ethnic group of mainland Southeast Asia whose lands extend from the southern Khorat Plateau across the Dangrek Mountains and Mun River region to the Mekong River in southern Laos and northern Cambodia. In Cambodia, they live mainly in Preah Vihear, Steung Treng, Siem Reap, and northern Kampong Thom, with a small population in Kratie. They are also found in Thailand, Laos, and Vietnam, and are an ethnic minority in all four countries. Estimates for the three-country region of Thailand, Cambodia, and Laos place the Kuy population at about 400,000, although exact figures are uncertain. Their language belongs to the Katuic subgroup of the Austroasiatic or Mon-Khmer language family. Kuy culture includes iron smelting, blacksmithing, silk work, basket and textile weaving, irrigated rice cultivation, animist beliefs, and forest-related social practices.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Silk weaving** — Some Kuy in Cambodia raise silkworms and weave silk. The sources identify this as a Kuy practice.

**Basket weaving** — Some Kuy artisans are known for distinctive basket weaving skills.

**Textile weaving** — Kuy artisans are also associated with unique textile weaving skills.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no Kuy textile motifs.

### Architecture

They do identify Kuy communities in villages and describe the importance of forests and mountains surrounding those villages, including the “spirit forest” in which the *Ah'ret* live.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**Iron smelting and blacksmithing** are important documented aspects of Kuy material culture in Cambodia, especially among the Kuy Dek or “iron Kuy.” Archaeological evidence places this practice in Kuy territory at roughly A.D. 800. It declined during the French colonial period and ended in the 1940s because of price competition from suppliers in the West.

The sources also name the *Pakam* rope, a lasso traditionally used in elephant catching by the Kuy Ajiang or “elephant Kuy.”

## Music & performance

The documented Kuy performance tradition is the **Klae Mo healing ceremony**. It takes place in the second and third lunar months, identified in the source as January and February, and is led by a ritual leader and spirit medium called the *Mae Or*. The ceremony involves traditional song, dance, and spirit possession.

The sources also describe spiritual practices connected with Kuy communities in Thailand and Cambodia. In Thailand, animism or *satsana phi*, involving the veneration of spirits, is a primary religious or cosmological belief alongside Buddhism. The Kuy Malo and Kuy Malua in Surin and Sisaket provinces believe in the monitor lizard, *takuat*, as a symbol of fertility and guardian spirit. The Kuy Mahai in Ubon Ratchathani believe in the spirit of *Ahya Chamnak*, which serves as a gateway to the forest and grants permission to access natural resources.

Among the Kuy Ajiang, the spirit of *Phra Khru Pakam* resides in the *Pakam* rope used in elephant catching.

## Dance & theatre

The Klae Mo healing ceremony includes traditional dance as part of its ritual sequence, together with song and spirit possession.

## Festivals & rituals

They do identify the Klae Mo healing ceremony, which takes place in the second and third lunar months, January and February. The ritual is led by the *Mae Or*, a spirit medium, and involves traditional song, dance, and spirit possession.

In Cambodia, central Kuy beliefs concern animism and local spirits associated with the forests and mountains surrounding Kuy villages. These spirits are commonly called *Ah'ret*. Kuy consult the *Ah'ret* before important life events, farming, collecting resources from the forest, and social occasions such as marriage. Belief in these spirits exists alongside Buddhist practice.

Women have an esteemed position in Kuy society, helping to ensure community cohesion and spiritual beliefs. Female elders called *Yeak Chaeng* or *Yeak Chheon Chaeng* serve as spiritual and ritual leaders.

## Foodways

The Kuy in Cambodia primarily practice irrigated rice cultivation, while some groups maintain shifting cultivation. The Kuy in Laos primarily practiced subsistence agriculture using shifting cultivation to grow rice, alongside subsistence hunting and fishing. The Lao government began policies to eliminate this form of swidden agriculture after becoming a communist country in 1975.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record a Kuy explanation for the absence of a traditional written script. According to this folklore, the Kuy alphabet was once written on buffalo hide, but a dog ate it, producing the saying *ajor jar jim*, translated in the source as “the dog ate it all.”

The sources also describe contemporary preservation efforts. The Kuy are actively engaged in efforts to preserve Prey Lang forest in Cambodia. The name Prey Lang originated in the Kuy language and means “the forest which belongs to all of us.” The Kuy are active members of the Cambodian Indigenous Youth Association.

## Language & religion

Kuy, also known as Kui, Suay, or Kuay, is a Katuic language within the Austroasiatic family. It is spoken in northern Cambodia, including Preah Vihear, Stung Treng, and Kampong Thom provinces. Cambodian Kuy has been described as having four dialects: *Kuy Mla*, *Kuy Ntua*, *Kuy Ntra*, and *Kuy Thmei*. The source also names *Auk* and *Wa* as subdialects of Ntra, and identifies *Kuy Mla* speakers in Krala Peas, Choam Ksan District, Preah Vihear Province.

Kuy is primarily a spoken language without a traditional alphabet. The Khmer script is used as a substitute in Cambodia. Only 26 percent of Kuy in Cambodia reported being able to communicate in Kuy, while the remainder spoke only Khmer. This has been attributed to restrictions on speaking Kuy during the Khmer Rouge and to Khmer being the primary language of education.

Kuy religious life in Cambodia centers on animism and local forest and mountain spirits called *Ah'ret*, alongside Khmer Buddhism.
