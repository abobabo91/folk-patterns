## Overview

The Lahu (Lahu: *Ladhulsi* / *Kawzhawd*; Burmese: လားဟူလူမျိုး; Chinese: 拉祜族 *Lāhùzú*; Vietnamese: *La Hủ*) are an ethnic group native to China, Myanmar and the rest of mainland Southeast Asia. About 720,000 live in Yunnan province in China, mostly in Lancang Lahu Autonomous County, where they are one of the 56 officially recognised ethnic groups; in Myanmar they are concentrated in the Kengtung District of Shan State. In Thailand they are one of the six main groups classed as hill tribes, and the Tai call them by the exonym *Musoe* (also spelled *Muser*; Thai: มูเซอ), meaning 'hunter'. They are one of 54 ethnic groups in Vietnam, living mostly in three communes of Mường Tè, Lai Châu Province. Their language belongs to the Loloish branch of the Lolo–Burmese subgroup of Tibeto-Burman, within the Sino-Tibetan family. For folk-culture purposes the Lahu matter above all for their subgroup system: the divisions **Lahu Na** (Black Lahu), **Lahu Nyi** (Red Lahu), **Lahu Hpu** (White Lahu), **Lahu Shi** (Yellow Lahu) and **Lahu Shehleh** are named for the traditional colour of their dress, so that clothing itself carries the group's principal internal classification.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources describing Lahu textiles here are museum catalogue records rather than ethnographic accounts, so the traditions below are named after the object types the collection holds rather than after vernacular craft names.

**Indigo-dyed appliqué cloth** — Woven cotton cloth dyed with indigo and decorated with applique is the recurring basis of Lahu jackets in the collection, appearing in men's and women's jackets alike and on at least one bag. The appliqué is worked directly onto the dark ground rather than woven in.

***Phasin*** — The woman's tube skirt, recorded under this name. One example is made of three unequal pieces of tabby cotton cloth with paired warps, carrying polychrome bands woven as stripes: a top section 32 cm deep with a cream band of single warps above evenly spaced polychrome bands on a red ground with red weft; a middle section 27.5 cm deep with a red band crossed by three fine blue bands and thinner polychrome bands below, again with red weft; and a lowest section 30 cm deep of dark blue cloth broken up by five groups of fine applied red bands and lines of white, pale blue or orange stitching at one edge, with blue weft. A second *phasin* is built from five unequal pieces on the same principle — cream band over polychrome bands on red at the top, more closely spaced polychrome bands with a wider blue and lilac band in the middle (both partly faded), and a lower third of three equal dark blue sections broken by three fine applied red bands.

**Embroidered and appliquéd cotton** — A coat in the collection is both embroidered and appliquéd in cotton and fastened with a large metal button; a boy's cap combines cotton and wool with both appliqué and embroidery.

**Woollen belt weaving** — A man's belt in the collection is a woollen band finished with a fringe.

**Cloth-and-bamboo wall hangings** — Two hangings combine cloth with bamboo; one carries the image of an elephant.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Elephant* (the only pictorial motif named in the catalogue records); banded stripes in polychrome, cream, red, blue and lilac; applied fine red banding on dark blue ground.

### Clothing & dress

Subgroup names encode dress: where a Lahu subgroup name refers to a colour — Black, Red, White or Yellow Lahu — it refers to the traditional colour of that group's clothing. The garments documented in the collection are jackets, coats, caps, belts, skirts and bags. Jackets exist in both men's and women's forms made of indigo-dyed woven cotton decorated with appliqué; one jacket of this type closes with a large silver button, and another jacket, of silk and cotton, has a silver buckle. A woman's coat of cotton was collected together with a silver brooch, indicating that metal ornament was worn with, and catalogued as part of, the outfit. A second coat is a jacket embroidered and appliquéd in cotton with a large metal button. Children's dress is represented by a boy's cap of appliquéd and embroidered cotton and wool. The woman's lower garment is the tube skirt or *phasin*, assembled from several horizontal sections of banded cotton (see above). A man's fringed woollen belt completes the recorded male outfit. Bags of appliquéd cotton were carried; the collection also holds an appliquéd passport holder closed with a nylon zip, showing the same technique applied to a modern object.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Everyday objects in the collection are few and made from local plant and textile materials rather than fired clay. A quiver is made of bamboo, the only hunting equipment recorded — a notable pairing with the Tai exonym *Musoe*, 'hunter'. Bamboo appears again structurally in the two wall hangings, which combine cloth with bamboo, one of them bearing an elephant image. Cotton bags decorated with appliqué serve as carrying gear, and the appliquéd passport holder with a nylon zip shows the same decorative technique carried onto a twentieth-century travel object. Silver is the metal of the collection, but it appears as personal ornament and as dress fittings — a silver buckle on a silk-and-cotton jacket, large silver and metal buttons on coats and jackets — rather than as vessels or tools.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver dominates Lahu ornament as recorded here. The collection holds neck ornaments of silver (two examples), a silver brooch, silver ear-rings including a woman's pair, a woman's silver bracelet and a woman's pair of silver bracelets. Beadwork is represented by a woman's necklace of glass beads. Silver also crosses from ornament into dress fastening: a jacket closes with a large silver button and another with a silver buckle. That a woman's cotton coat was accessioned together with its silver brooch suggests ornament and garment were treated as a single assemblage. The sources used do not describe tattooing, hair practice or body painting among the Lahu.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used here give little on the Lahu festival calendar, and nothing on annual or seasonal festivals. What they do document is ritual protection and naming. The Lahu of northeastern Thailand encountered Theravada Buddhist forest monks (*tudong* monks) around the years 1930–1940; the leader of one such group, Mun Bhuridatta, spent time in Lahu territory, and these Lahu asked him for a *gatha* that would protect them from ghosts and demons — a request that shows traditional concern with spirits being addressed through a Buddhist verbal formula. Birth is marked in the naming system. Lahu given names are made of two syllables, one showing gender and one giving information on the day of birth according to the zodiac: a person born on the Ox day is named *Zanu* if a boy and *Nanu* if a girl. Surnames are a later, external imposition — the Lahu used to have only a given name until the Chinese government gave them surnames, and about 90% of Lahu people now bear either Lee or Zhang, two of the most common Chinese surnames. Kinship practice frames these rites: the Lahu have no kin groups above the family, trace descent bilaterally, and typically practise matrilocal residence, so the subgroups function as neither tribes nor clans.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used here do not record Lahu folktales, epics or proverbs, but they document the written and recorded corpus in some detail. Three Latin-script alphabets exist. The **Protestant alphabet** was begun in 1906 for Black Lahu by the American missionaries C. B. Antisdel and H. H. Tilbe in Myanmar, who published the first book in it in 1908; tones were unmarked at first, with tone marks introduced in 1917, the alphabet reaching the Lahu of China in 1921 and being revised in 1950 and 1962. It remains the main alphabet in Myanmar and Thailand. In 1982 Yellow Lahu refugees from Laos living in the United States built an orthography on it for their own speech, which spread among the Yellow Lahu of Southeast Asia, though Black Lahu often hold that the established Black Lahu orthography should serve instead. The **Catholic alphabet**, developed by Catholic missionaries in Burma in the 1930s, differs mainly in its finals and survives among Lahu Catholics in Myanmar. The **pinyin-based alphabet**, begun in 1953 and approved in March 1957, marks tones with the letters l, d, q, r, t, f after the syllable; unused from 1964 to 1980 because of the Cultural Revolution and its aftermath, it was revived and slightly amended in 1989, and is the official script in China. Documentation includes the Lahu Bible and James A. Matisoff's *English-Lahu Lexicon* and his collection of Lahu texts from 1960s Thailand.

## Language & religion

Lahu (endonym *Ladhof*) is a Tibeto-Burman language of the Loloish branch of Lolo–Burmese, strongly isolating, with subject–object–verb order, numeral classifiers, seven tones and no syllable-closing consonants; adjectives are a subclass of verbs. Traditional folk taxonomy splits the language into Black and Yellow Lahu, Red and White being newer clusters originating in messianic movements of the past few centuries. Black Lahu is the standard in China and a lingua franca among Thailand's hill tribes, though only intelligible to Yellow Lahu speakers with difficulty; Lahu Shi speech is notably divergent. Traditional Lahu religion is polytheistic. Buddhism was introduced in the late 17th century and became widespread, and many Lahu in China are Buddhists; Christianity became established in Burma in the 19th century and has been spreading since.
