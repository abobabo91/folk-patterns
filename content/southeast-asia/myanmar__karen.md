## Overview

The Karen live mainly in the hills and the Irrawaddy delta of Myanmar. They also live in nearby regions of Myanmar, Thailand, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, and other countries. Their material culture includes woven cotton textiles, striped clothing, clay bowls, baskets, tools, and musical instruments. Most Karen are Theravada Buddhists who also practise animism, while a smaller Christian population includes Baptists, Catholics, and other Protestants.

## Material culture

Karen material culture is marked by woven cotton textiles, bright stripes, ikat, embroidered cloth, practical tools, and simple clay and bamboo objects.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Karen textile traditions use cotton, ikat, stripes, embroidery, fringe, and repeated geometric and bird patterns.

- **Many-coloured smock frocks** (*Tanmi prok*) — Tanmi prok were described as sleeveless bags or “cage heads” linked with hill Karen tribes.
- **Karen ikat** — This strong woven cotton strip from North Thailand is mostly blue, with brown-and-light ikat stripes and twisted fringes.
- **Warp ikat** — A brown-toned warp-ikat fabric is recorded as a Karen-associated Thai textile.
- **Patterned hanging** — A long cotton hanging has nine patterned bands with birds, zigzags, diamonds, crosses, and plied fringe.
- **Striped cotton cloth** — Striped cotton appears in a man’s jacket cloth, a tunic with red borders, and a sarong with yellow, green, and red stripes.

### Clothing & dress

Karen clothing records show cotton blouses, robes, breeches, tunics, sarongs, turbans, blankets, and loincloths.

- **Sarong** (*lon-gyi*) — The blackish coarse-cotton lon-gyi has yellow, green, and red stripes.
- **Women’s turban** — A black cotton turban has coloured bands and tassels in red, yellow, purple, and green.
- **Unmarried girl’s blouse** — A museum record identifies one cotton blouse as belonging to an unmarried girl.
- **Striped blanket or hood** — A blanket or hood is made from striped cloth folded and sewn down one side.
- **Loincloth** — A long narrow North Thai cotton loincloth has a blue ground, ikat stripes, and twisted fringes.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Karen everyday objects include clay bowls, lacquer and cane boxes, baskets, hunting tools, bells, and knives.

- **Curry and tea bowl** — Clay bowls from the Pekkong district were used for curry and tea and were also used by Red Karen and Zarein (-Latha).
- **Betel box** — A betel box is made from lacquer, basketry, and cane.
- **Rat catcher’s trident** — A rat catcher’s trident is made from bamboo and metal.
- **Karen knife** (*daukau*) — A Karen-associated knife from Chomthong has a sheath.
- **Knife sheath** (*daukrau*) — The sheath of the Karen-associated knife is called daukrau.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The records mention silver ear ornaments and a coloured women’s turban, but give little other detail about adornment.

- **Silver ear ornaments** — A partly finished pair of silver ear ornaments is associated with Red Karen.
- **Coloured turban** — A women’s black cotton turban is decorated with coloured bands and tassels.

## Music & performance

Karen musical objects include string instruments, reed instruments, bells, horns, and bronze drums.

- **Six-metal-string harp** (*teiná*) — The six-metal-string harp called teiná has a sheet-metal-covered sound box and is recorded as 80 centimetres high.
- **Four-string mandolin** (*su*) — A four-string mandolin is called su.
- **Horn-like reed instrument** (*kwai*) — The side-blown kwai has a beating reed and two joined wooden halves.
- **Buffalo bell** (*kauklo*) — The buffalo bell called kauklo is made from bamboo pieces with wooden clappers.
- **Bronze drum** — One bronze drum has elephants and snails along its side and four single frogs on top.

## Festivals & rituals

Karen religious life centres on Buddhist monasteries, merit-making, animist beliefs, and some distinct Leke practices.

- **Karen New Year** — Karen New Year became a public holiday under the British colonial administration in 1938.
- **Alms giving** — Alms giving is a central merit-making practice in Karen Buddhist life.
- **Leke Saturday services** — The Leke sect holds services on Saturdays.
- **Leke vegetarian practice** — The Leke sect practises vegetarianism.
- **Leke pagodas** — The Leke sect builds distinct pagodas.

## Foodways

Karen foodways include wet-rice cultivation, shellfish stories, curry and tea vessels, and vegetarian practice among the Leke.

- **Wet-rice cultivation** — The Karen likely learned wet-rice cultivation from older Mon-Khmer populations.
- **Shellfish cooking legend** — A Karen legend says ancestors learned from the Chinese to open shellfish and obtain the meat.
- **Curry and tea vessels** — Clay bowls recorded in Karen areas were used for curry and tea.
- **Leke vegetarianism** — The Leke sect is described as practising vegetarianism.

## Oral tradition & literature

Karen oral traditions tell of a river of sand, shellfish cooking, and a lost book returned by a white brother.

- **River of running sand** — A Karen legend tells of ancestors crossing a river of running sand, identified by some with the Gobi Desert.
- **Lost book legend** — A popular legend says a lost book would be returned by a white brother and is linked with the arrival of the Bible.
- **Yedagon Buddhist literature** — Karen Buddhist literature developed around Yedagon Monastery on Mount Zwegabin.

## Language & religion

Karenic languages use a modified Mon–Burmese script, while Karen religion combines Buddhism, animism, and Christianity.

- **Sgaw language** (*Sgaw*) — Sgaw is one of the main Karenic language branches.
- **Pwo languages** (*Eastern Pwo; Western Pwo*) — Eastern Pwo and Western Pwo are mutually unintelligible Karenic branches in the source.
- **Karen soul** (*k’lar*) — Karen animism describes k’lar as the soul, which may wander away and cause sickness or misfortune.
- **Thirty-seven spirits** — Karen animism holds that thirty-seven spirits embody each person and that death occurs when they all leave the body.
- **Karen Christianity** — Karen Christians include Baptists, Catholics, and other Protestants, and some Christian practices include animist elements.
