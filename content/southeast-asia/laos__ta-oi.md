## Overview

The Tà Ôi is an ethnic group living in Laos and Vietnam. In Laos, they are concentrated in *muang Ta Oy* of Saravane Province in southern Laos, while Ta’Oi-speaking communities are also reported in Salavan and Sekong provinces. The Ta Oi population in Laos was 45,991 in 2015; the population in Vietnam was 52,356 in 2019. They speak Ta’Oi, also called Ta’Oih or Ta Oi, a dialect continuum within the Katuic branch of the Austroasiatic language family; another source describes it as a Mon–Khmer language. The Ta Oi call themselves *Taoih*, *Ta Uot*, or *Ta-oih*, while the Paco subgroup uses *Can Tua* or *Can Tang*, meaning “highlanders.” Their documented folk culture includes swidden and slope-field cultivation, cotton growing, textile and brocade weaving, beadwork, bamboo and rattan work, musical instruments, spirit rituals, and oral accounts of souls and supernatural beings.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Cotton, cloth and brocade weaving** — The Ta uot are described as growing cotton and weaving cloth and brocades. They also sew or fasten glass beads onto costumes. The Paco are conversant in bamboo and rattan weaving but are described as not being adept in cloth weaving.

**Reng cloth** — *Reng* is a named piece of cloth associated with the house spirit. A family head may place a *reng* cloth or loin cloth in a *kang*, a shoulder basket kept in a corner of the house, after seeing the house spirit in a dream.

**Cloth exchange** — The Paco trade cloth and clothes, along with blacksmith goods, shoulder baskets, and honey, exchanging them for other valuable goods.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe cloth, brocades, costumes decorated with glass beads, loin cloths, and *reng* pieces of cloth. In accounts of bad souls, poor families cut banana leaves into forms placed across the door: the T symbolizes clothing and the V symbolizes pants. Rich families instead throw out two pieces of cloth as presents.

### Architecture

The Ta-oi formerly lived in long houses in which each family had its own room and worshipped its house spirit. The long house had a room reserved for visitors or guests, where a small house hung under the roof served as the shelter of the spirit of the long house, *giang danh*. The *Rong house* is a named communal building associated with village rituals, celebrations, the inauguration of a tiger’s grave house, and rituals involving the house spirit. Tiger grave houses were built on higher ground near human grave houses, surrounded by a stone hedge, and contained a stone on which the tiger’s head was placed.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention blacksmith goods traded by the Paco, bamboo and rattan work, shoulder baskets, water pipes made from *lo-o* bamboo, bowls, jars, cups, trays, knives, and firewood ashes used in accounts concerning the souls of the dead. A *kang* is a shoulder basket made for keeping a sacred item associated with the house spirit.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The Ta uot are described as sewing or fastening glass beads onto costumes. The sources also mention armlets, agate beads, and *sol* and *leo* among offerings associated with the ritual following the capture of a tiger.

## Music & performance

The Ta uot are described as making some musical instruments, specifically drums and pan flutes. Music is not otherwise documented in the supplied material.

Performance contexts are described mainly through ritual speech, prayer, dreams, and village ceremonies. The village head prays during the ritual for the tiger spirit, asking it to protect villagers from death, provide warning through dreams when there is war between villages, and punish anyone who attempts to destroy or burn the village. Rituals for spirits involve offerings, incense sticks, food, rice, and *doak* liquor served in jars and cups.

## Festivals & rituals

The Ta-oi religious system is described as animist, with natural objects understood as animated by spirits or possessing souls. In the past, annual rituals were dedicated to the heavens spirit, *abang*, the earth spirit, *katek*, and the forest spirit, *krum kaek*. A ritual for the heavens spirit, *giang abang*, was held once every 10–12 years. When a village enjoyed prosperity, a bumper harvest, and an absence of illness and death, it held a thanksgiving ritual for *Giang kmuk*. Floods and other natural calamities prompted rituals for the heavens spirit, the earth spirit, the Rong house spirit, and the house spirit.

Rituals dedicated to the heavens spirit required 12 types of foodstuffs, those for the earth spirit required 8, and those for *Giang kmuk* required 5. The listed foodstuffs include buffalo meat, pork, chicken, blood pudding, grilled meat, *mon thai*, soup, and glutinous rice. Rice was placed in bowls, *doak* liquor was served in jars and cups, and incense sticks were burned.

When water was first brought to a village through *lo-o* bamboo pipes, the village head and heads of family clans performed a ritual for the water spirit. A cock was offered, its blood was buried where the water arrived, and the water-pipe pillar was decorated, repaired, and renewed each year. Before the water was first used, the village head performed another ritual with a pig, goat, and chicken as offerings.

The A ja annual celebrations required families to open the *kang* containing the sacred item associated with the house spirit and use it in family and Rong house rituals. Funerals, weddings, forest-spirit rituals, and water-spirit rituals also required attention to the house spirit.

## Foodways

The sources name rice, glutinous rice, buffalo meat, pork, chicken, blood pudding, grilled meat, *mon thai*, soup, and liquor. Rice was placed in bowls during spirit rituals, while *doak* liquor was served in jars and cups. Offerings to the heavens spirit, earth spirit, and *Giang kmuk* were organized by specified numbers of food types: 12, 8, and 5 respectively.

Offerings to the water spirit included a cock, and the first-use ritual for village water included a pig, goat, and chicken. The tiger-spirit ritual for inaugurating a tiger’s grave house also involved a goat, pig, and chicken, placed first on a tray and then in the grave house. When the Rong house was inaugurated, and during new year and new paddy celebrations held each year, food including new rice was presented to the tiger spirit.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources record a Ta Oi legend in which the water spirit creates fish by placing grains of rice in a banana leaf and dropping them into a brook, where they become fish. They also describe dreams as a channel through which the souls of the dead and other spirits communicate with living people. The soul of a dead person may return during rituals or in the dreams of the living, and the tiger spirit may warn villagers about war, crop failure, or epidemics through dreams.

The account of *nang*, or “love medicine,” says that it is resin collected from the feet of the *Ko-tach* bird, described as black and red and similar to a parrot. The sources state that placing the resin in a girl’s shoulder basket or hair would make her follow the person who placed it there. The supplied material does not document epic poetry, named folktale cycles, proverbs, riddles, or contemporary literary preservation projects.

## Language & religion

Ta’Oi, also written Ta’Oih or Ta Oi, is spoken among the Ta Oi people in Salavan and Sekong provinces in Laos and in the municipality of Huế in Vietnam. It is described as a dialect continuum within the Katuic branch of the Austroasiatic language family and elsewhere as a Mon–Khmer language. Varieties named in the sources include Ta’Oi proper, Ong/Ir/Talan, Chatong, Kriang, and Kataang. Kriang is also called *Ngkriang* or *Ngeq*, and Kataang is also called *Katang*. The language is largely analytic, with a pronominal paradigm marked for dative and genitive case; the dative marker and preposition *adeh* occur before a recipient in benefactive constructions.

Ta Oi religious life is described through animism: natural objects may possess spirits, and all things are believed to have souls. Named spirits include the water spirit *giang dak*, house spirit *giang an teng*, spirit of the long house *giang danh*, tiger spirit *giang avo*, forest spirit *brau brieu*, heavens spirit *abang*, earth spirit *katek*, and forest spirit *krum kaek*.
