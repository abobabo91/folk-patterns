## Overview

The Ifugao are an Austronesian ethnic group inhabiting Ifugao province in the Philippines. They live in Lagawe, the provincial capital, and in Aguinaldo, Alfonso Lista, Asipulo, Banaue, Hingyon, Hungduan, Kiangan, Lamut, Mayoyao, and Tinoc. Some have moved to Baguio, where they work as woodcarvers, and to other parts of the Cordillera Region. Significant populations also extend into Cagayan Valley Region, or Region 2, particularly in Nueva Vizcaya, Quirino, and Isabela. The 2020 Census gives an aggregated population of 309,816, including Ifugao, Ayangan, and Tuwali but excluding the Kalanguya people. Ifugao is an Austronesian language of the Northern Luzon subfamily. The group is especially associated with wet-rice terraces, rice-centered ritual life, woodcarving, textiles, oral literature, and the hudhud.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources describe Ifugao textiles as colorful blankets and clothing woven on looms.

### Clothing & dress

Men wear a loincloth called *wanoh*, while women wear a wraparound skirt called *ampuyo*, also known as *tapis*. On special occasions, men wear a betel bag called *pinuhha* and carry their bolo, or *gimbattan*. During the marriage ceremony, called *uya-uy*, a man usually wears a headress called the *kango*, literally “hornbill,” also known as *yang ngo*. The kango symbolizes wealth and is made from the skull and beak of a rufous hornbill, flanked by two sweeping horns resembling the horns of a carabao. It is anchored with rattan fiber and decorated with feathers and beads. A newly woven loincloth is wrapped around it, with its ends hanging to the waist. Wealthy men also wear the kango on other special occasions, including their funeral.

### Architecture

Ifugao houses, called *Bale*, are built on four wooden posts three meters from the ground and consist of one room, a front door called *panto*, a back door called *awidan*, and a detachable ladder called *tete* leading to the front door. Temporary huts, called *abong*, shelter workers in the field or forest.

The house is square in floor plan and has four posts called *tukud*, fitted with cylindrical wooden rat-guards called *halipan*. Transverse girders called *kuling* support floor joists and floorboards called *dotal*. Wallboards are called *goab* and *pamadingan*, while the studs are called *bagad*. The roof is a true pyramid with four triangular sides rising to an apex without a ridgepole. A reed platform called *palan* may provide attic-like storage for unthreshed rice. The house is entered by a ladder that is removed at night. A smaller, modified version with one door serves as a rat-proof rice granary called *alang*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They identify several wooden household objects. Rice granaries called *alang* are protected by wooden guardian figures called *bulul*. Wooden rice spoons with sacred carved images of bulul, representing deities or ancestral spirits called *anito*, are traditional utensils used for eating rice or soups and serving rice wine. The sources also note the Ifugao skill in carving bulul and identify the *hagabi* as a prestige bench of the upper class. On special occasions, men carry the bolo called *gimbattan*.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ifugao heirloom beads are prestigious goods associated with the wealthy Ifugao community. They are used for ritual occasions including funerals, weddings, and rice harvest festivals, and signify respect. Social status is also measured through gold earrings and carabaos, or water buffaloes.

Tattoos among Ifugao men were strongly linked to head-hunting raids. They recorded how many heads warriors had taken in battle and formed part of initiation rites into adulthood. The number, design, and location of tattoos indicated a warrior’s status and prowess. Tattoos were also regarded as magical wards against evil spirits and illnesses. Named motifs include the *kinabu*, a dog usually placed on the chest; *tinagu*, human figures; and *ginawang* or *ginayaman*, centipedes.

## Music & performance

Ifugao musical instruments include gongs called *gangha*; *bangibang*, a wooden instrument struck with another piece of wood; *bikkung*, a thin brass instrument that is plucked; and the stringed instruments *ayyuding* and *babbong*. The sources also name nose flutes called *ingngiing*, mouth flutes called *kupliing* or *ippiip*, and the *pewpew*, a percussion instrument made from bamboo.

The Ifugao have rich oral literary traditions of *hudhud* and *alim*. The hudhud is described as narrative literature and an epic dealing with hero ancestors, sung in a poetic manner. Women recite ballads, while men recite myths. The sources connect music and verbal performance with family life, ritual, and community identity.

## Dance & theatre

The sources describe ritual and combative performances. During headhunting conflicts, the victim’s family performed a war dance called *himong* and vowed revenge. Ifugao traditional wrestling, called *bultong*, is a sport often played during town and provincial fiestas. It falls under the international classification of “belt wrestling.” Criminal cases could also involve ordeals including duels called *uggub* or *alao*, wrestling, a hot bolo ordeal, and a boiling water ordeal called *da-u*.

## Festivals & rituals

Ifugao ritual life is closely connected with rice cultivation. Harvest season calls for thanksgiving feasts, while the concluding harvest rite called *tungo* or *tungul*, meaning the day of rest, entails a strict taboo against agricultural work. Rice wine, rice cakes, and *moma*—a mixture of several herbs, powdered snail shell, and betel nut or arecoline—are consumed during festivities and ritual activities. When harvest begins, an elderly woman says a prayer directed toward Cabunian, the goddess of rice. A protective prayer is said before rice is placed in the granary.

The highest prestige feasts are the *hagabi*, sponsored by the elite *kadangyan*, and the *uyauy*, a marriage feast sponsored by those immediately below the wealthiest, the *inmuy-ya-uy*. The *kadangyans* also sponsor prestige rituals called hagabi and uyauy. The marriage ceremony is called *uya-uy*, and courtship includes *ca-i-sing*, in which men and women occupy separate houses. The men’s house is called the *Ato*, and the women’s house is called the *olog* or *agamang*.

Life-cycle customs include *pahang* and *palat di oban*, performed for a mother to ensure safe delivery. After birth, visitors may not enter the house until *among* is performed and the baby is given a name. *Kolot* and *balihong* are then performed for the health and good characteristics of a boy or girl. The sources also describe *opa*, a ritual for those who are murdered, intended to force their souls toward the place where their ancestors dwell.

## Foodways

Rice is the staple food and a prestige crop. The basic meal consists of rice served with vegetables, fish or snails, flavorings, and sometimes cooked chicken or pig. When rice supplies are low, the Ifugao consume grain or root crops such as sweet potatoes. Fresh berries and other plant products are eaten as snacks.

Rice wine is central to ritual and special occasions. It is called *baya* or *bubud* and is made with homemade yeast and glutinous rice. Another source calls rice wine *bayah*. Rice cakes and *moma* are consumed during festivities and ritual activities. Moma contains several herbs, powdered snail shell, and betel nut or arecoline, and is used as chewing gum by the Ifugaos.

Wooden rice spoons with carved images of bulul are used for eating rice or soups and for serving rice wine. Although the carvings are animistic, the spoons are everyday utensils.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Ifugao are known for narrative literature, especially the *hudhud*, an epic dealing with hero ancestors and sung in a poetic manner. The popular epic *The Hudhud of Dinulawan and Bugan of Gonhadan* is mentioned in connection with an interpretation of Ifugao history. The group also has a rich oral literary tradition of *alim*. Men recite myths, while women recite ballads.

In 2001, the *Hudhud Chants of the Ifugao* was chosen as one of the 11 Masterpieces of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity. It was formally inscribed as a UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2008.

## Language & religion

Ifugao is a Malayo-Polynesian language spoken in the northern valleys of Ifugao in the Philippines. It belongs to the Northern Luzon subfamily and is closely related to Bontoc, Balangaw, and Kankanaey. It is a dialect continuum with four main varieties: Amganad Ifugao, Batad Ifugao or Ayangan Ifugao, Mayoyao Ifugao or Mayaoyaw, and Tuwali Ifugao. The unified Ifugao alphabet includes A, B, D, E, G, H, I, K, L, M, N, Ng, O, P, T, U, W, and Y. Loanwords from other languages, including Ilokano, are replacing some older terminology. Ifugaos also commonly speak Ilocano, Tagalog, and English as alternatives to their mother tongue.

A majority of Ifugao have converted to Roman Catholicism from an earlier animistic religion. Their mythology says they descended from Wigan and Bugan, children of Bakkayawan and Bugan of the Skyworld, called *Kabunyan*. Ifugao belief includes six worlds: Skyworld, Earthworld or *Pugaw*, Underworld or *Dalom*, Eastern World or *Lagud*, Western World or *Daya*, and Spiritual World or *Kadungayan*. Nature and ancestor worship continue in rituals called *baki*, presided over by a *mumbaki*. Priests called *munagao* and *mumbini* guide rites for good fortune.
