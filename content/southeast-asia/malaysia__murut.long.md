## Overview

The Murut people are an Austronesian ethnic group indigenous to Sabah and Sarawak in Malaysia, North Kalimantan in Indonesia, and Temburong District in Brunei. In Sabah they are concentrated in the Interior Division, especially around Nabawan, Tenom, Keningau, Sipitang and Beaufort, with other populations in districts including Tongod, Kinabatangan and Tawau. They also live around the Lawas and Limbang areas of Sarawak, the Nunukan and Malinau regencies of North Kalimantan, and Brunei. The sources describe 29 distinct sub-ethnic groups and approximately half a dozen closely intertwined Murutic Austronesian languages. Murut culture is represented through shifting cultivation, communal longhouses, beadwork, handicrafts, gong music, festivals, warrior traditions, and dances including Magunatip and Lansaran.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Kelarai** — *Kelarai* handicrafts are especially notable in Keningau and Tenom. They are made from salingkawang, bamboo and rattan, with distinctive white and black floral and geometric patterns. Bamboo is painted with black smoke from kerosene lamps and mixed with water obtained from bark known locally as the uvul tree.

**Pinongkoloh** — The Tahol Murut female dress of *pinongkoloh* is usually black, decorated with colourful floral motifs and beads in distinctive patterns and designs. Its components include babaru linantian, tapi' linantian, pipirot linggit, salupai, sinikot, rarangkol, holong and bungkas.

**Apin lantungan** — *Apin* and *lantungan* are Murut terms for woven mats, especially among Tahol Muruts. The sources name three types: apin lantungan, lantungan ulia and lantungan puaran.

**Sampoi cloth** — Woven Sampoi cloth is identified with Murut handicraft production in the account of the Kalimaran festival and its associated folklore.

**Basketwork** — Murut basketwork includes mats, baskets, bags and winnowing mats made from basketry, matting, rattan, cane and bamboo. The sources describe distinctive black and white geometric and floral designs in some handicrafts.

**Tavil** — The *tavil* was formerly a back-cover body armour used against blowpipe attacks. Its woven surface is decorated with sinusu motifs and reinforced by vertical and horizontal lines.

**Beadwork** — Murut clothing and handicrafts use distinctive beadwork. Beads carry symbolic meanings connected with social and spiritual beliefs, women's maturity and their role in the community.

**Motif vocabulary.** Sinusu, minato, sunsulit, tinampinak and zigzag; sinusu is described as the dominant motif on many tavils, while the other names refer to additional motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Murut dress varies according to subgroup, and black is described as a common colour. Traditional male dress included a jacket made of tree bark, a red loincloth and the *lalandau* headdress, which could be worn by both men and women and decorated with Argus pheasant feathers. The lalandau formerly served as the principal head covering for male warriors; the number of feathers signified death and the total number of enemies killed by the wearer.

Murut women wore a black sleeveless blouse and a sarong falling just below the knees. Clothing was decorated with beadwork. Belts included one made from old silver coins and another of reddish-brown glass beads with yellow and blue beads, worn loosely around the waist. The Tahol female *pinongkoloh* includes a pipirot linggit coin belt, salupai fine beads, the sinikot dangling headdress, the rarangkol necklace, holong wrist jewellery and bungkas waist decoration made of white beads.

The *silaung*, *siraung* or *sirung* is a head covering used mainly by Murut sub-ethnics for protection from rain and sunlight during agricultural work.

### Architecture

The Muruts formerly lived in communal longhouses, usually near rivers. Rivers served as transportation networks and were central to the settlement environment. The sources do not provide further details about longhouse plans, roofs, structural divisions or architectural decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Ancient Chinese jars have a prominent place in Murut customs. Known among Muruts as *tajau*, these pottery vessels became places of spirits, while larger jars were formerly used as coffins. The sources also describe the *tikalis*, used in the past for winnowing rice and as a tray during wedding ceremonies; it is now also used as a souvenir, home decoration and dance-performance prop.

Murut baskets include the *buyong*, used formerly to gather forest produce, with regional names including barait, sasaging, takiding and balait. Other named baskets are banso, bundasan, kalong, kapa, kapon, sasar, tapan, takinan and tinkapan. British Museum catalogue records include mats, bags, baskets, winnowing mats, tobacco pipes, an adze, swords, sword-sheaths, darts, quivers and blow-pipes.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Murut body adornment includes beadwork, coin belts, necklaces, wrist jewellery, dangling headdresses and waist decorations. The Tahol dress components include *rarangkol* necklace, *holong* wrist jewellery, *pipirot linggit* belt of coins, *sinikot* headdress and *bungkas* waist decoration. The lalandau may be decorated with Argus pheasant feathers.

British Museum catalogue records also describe bracelets made of rattan and hair-pins made of deer bone, iron, buffalo horn, lead and tinfoil. The sources do not document tattoos, henna practices or other hair practices.

## Music & performance

Murut musical heritage includes several types of *agung* ensembles. These consist of large, hanging, shallow-sided gongs with small differences between the back and front diameters. Agung ensembles are described as more common among Murutic and Dusunic communities than among the Paitanic, Bajau and Iranun peoples named in the source.

Bamboo was used to make musical instruments and compose songs. The *tangkung* is made from wood and resembles a guitar, although it has only two strings; it was usually played during leisure time. *Sompoton* is another Murut instrument, also owned by Dusuns and Kadazans. *Kulundi* is associated with the Tangara Muruts of Inarad in Upper Kinabatangan, especially in religious or cultural ceremonies. The source also refers to gong music as part of Murut cultural expression.

Music is connected in the sources with cultural activities, religious or cultural ceremonies, leisure time, festivals and dance. The sources do not name Murut song genres or provide descriptions of musical scales, performance ensembles beyond agung, or particular funeral and wedding repertoires.

## Dance & theatre

The principal traditional dance among Muruts in Keningau, Nabawan and Tenom is *Magunatip*. The name derives from the Murut word *apit*, meaning “to press between two surfaces.” It is an energetic indigenous warrior dance in which dancers place their feet in and out between clapping bamboo poles without becoming trapped. The dance is associated with Nabaai, Paluan and Timugon Muruts, as well as Kwijau Dusun and Tambunan Dusun.

Other named dances include the Tahol Murut dances *sasayau* and *alang ra illau*; the Bookan Murut *mangalai*; the Kolor Murut *magalai* and their Murut version of *sumazau*; the Nabaai Murut *mansayau*; the Okolod Murut *alang sirung* and *alang kolod*; the Paluan Murut *ansayau*; and the Tangala Murut dances *tinggung agalai* and *sayau agumbak*.

The *Lansaran* is both a traditional game and a dance heritage. Its name comes from *lumasar*, meaning “to soar” or “swing.” Men and women in traditional attire form a circle and jump on a trampoline-like bamboo floor to reach the suspended kinkilat. It was formerly held to welcome Murut warriors returning victorious from *ngayau*. The sources do not describe a Murut theatre tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The Murut harvest festival takes place annually in March. During it, Muruts hold the *Ralaa Kalimaran* beauty pageant, often in Tenom District. They also celebrate *Unduk Ngadau* in May with other ethnicities, including the Murut. Young women, called *ralaa*, from Murut, Kadazan, Dusun and Rungus communities compete for the title at the KDCA Compound in Penampang District.

The Kalimaran festival is connected in Murut folklore with an origin said to be identical to that of Kadazan-Dusun Huminodun. In the account given by the source, Ralaa represents a young Murut woman who was sacrificed and who produced Murut handicrafts such as mats, silaung and woven Sampoi cloth during her lifetime.

Murut wedding and funeral feasts can last several days. Ancient Chinese jars, or tajau, have a prominent place in these customs and are described as places of spirits; larger jars were formerly used as coffins. In the past, a Murut man could marry only after presenting at least one enemy head to the family of the woman he wished to marry. The sources do not describe birth rites or a separate coming-of-age ceremony.

## Foodways

The sources state that the Muruts practised shifting cultivation of hill paddy and tapioca, supplementing their diet through hunting and river fishing. They do not name specific dishes, recipes, cooking methods, beverages or dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe Murut folklore connected with the Kalimaran festival, including the story of Ralaa, a young woman whose sacrifice is associated with the production of handicrafts. They also record traditions concerning ancestral spirits, including the belief among spiritual leaders during the 1915 drought that food shortages were caused by ancestral spirits angered by the taxation system.

The sources do not provide named epics, folktale collections, proverbs, riddles or a documented literary tradition. They mention George Cathcart Woolley’s documentation of Murut basketwork, including motifs and symbolic themes gathered through interviews with Murut communities, and preservation initiatives that teach younger generations handicraft and embroidery-making skills.

## Language & religion

Murutic languages form a family of approximately half a dozen closely intertwined Austronesian languages. The sources distinguish Northern Muruts in Sabah from Southern Muruts in Brunei, Sarawak and North Kalimantan. They also describe lowland and highland groups, with the Timugon language serving as a lingua franca for the lowland and the Tagol Murut language for the highland.

The Muruts were originally pagan-animist. A majority have converted to Protestantism, while others follow Roman Catholicism, other Christian branches or Islam, and some retain traditional religion. Traditional beliefs remain especially strongly maintained among Muruts living in the deeper Bornean jungles. Ancient jars are described as places of spirits, and headhunting formerly held an important role in Murut spiritual beliefs.
