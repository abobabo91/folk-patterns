## Overview

The Rejang, also called *Tun Hejang* or *Tun Jang*, are an Austronesian ethnolinguistic group native to parts of Bengkulu Province and South Sumatera Province in southwestern Sumatera Island, Indonesia. They live especially along the valleys and upper reaches of mountain rivers, including the upper Ketahun River region in Lebong Regency, and along the slopes of the Barisan mountain range. One source gives an estimate of more than 1,3 million people, while another reports estimates ranging from 250,000 to a million and an Indonesian census estimate of around 350,000 in 2000. Rejang is an Austronesian language with five dialects. Rejang culture includes customary law, song and dance, agriculture, distinctive weapons, and religious traditions involving *punjung*, *kedurai agung*, *betarak*, and *tabeus*.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state only that contemporary Rejang people buy clothing from neighboring peoples.

### Architecture

They mention rural hamlets called *talang*, generally consisting of 10 to 15 houses, and state that traditional families are usually large and extended.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The documented everyday implements are primarily bladed weapons that have also become practical tools. The spear, called *kujua* or *kujuh*, was traditionally used for catching fish in clear river waters. The *pitat* is a parang used for clearing land, making pathways, cutting wood, and opening coconuts. Other named weapons are the *badik* or *badek*, the *ke-is* or kris, and the curved *badek slon imeu*, shaped like a tiger’s claw. Kris may be used in martial arts or kept in homes as an amulet.

## Music & performance

Rejang people are known for song and dance art, including popular female dances. Musical performances are mentioned as part of Annual Regency Day celebrations, where artists are invited from various places. The sources also state that Rejang people retained dancing and singing after monetary relations and the arrival of miners contributed to the decline of aspects of traditional life at the end of the 19th century.

## Dance & theatre

Pencak Silat is identified as a Rejang cultural practice. The form originating in Rejang lands is called **Silat Jang Pat Petulai**. The sources also describe Rejang people as known for song and dance art, including popular female dances. No information is supplied about shadow puppetry, mask performance, or dramatic genres.

## Festivals & rituals

The two biggest Rejang celebrations are *Rayo*, or Idulfitri, and *Rayo Ajai*, or Iduladha. These Islamic holidays are occasions for returning to one’s hometown, visiting family, taking vacations with family members, and strengthening family bonds. Parades, processions, and small-scale fireworks are reported during the nights of *Rayo* and *Rayo Ajai*.

On the 27th day of Ramadan, before *Rayo*, Rejang people observe *Opoi Malem Likua*. They light wooden poles covered with coconut husks in front of their houses so that the spirits of ancestors can find their way home for the celebration. Annual Regency Day and Indonesian Independence Day are non-religious festivals. Annual Regency Day includes exhibitions by small and medium-sized enterprises and musical performances; the largest such celebration is held annually in May in Curup, Rejang Lebong Regency. Indonesian Independence Day is celebrated annually in August with street contests, climbing Areca nut palms, sack racing, tug of war, street marching, and other competitions.

Older religious traditions include *kedurai agung*, meaning “big feast,” during which *punjung* is presented. *Punjung* is a pile of agricultural produce, food, and cakes arranged in a form that may reach 2 meters in height. It is an offering for gods worshiped through *kedurai agung*. *Betarak* is a Rejang term for hermitage or prayer to deities, with Mount Kaba identified as one of its main locations. Before clearing jungle, the *tabeus* ritual is performed to seek permission from the ancestral guardian of the jungle.

## Foodways

Agriculture, especially rice cultivation, is the main Rejang livelihood. The sources also describe Rejang people as fishermen and hunters and state that bamboo shoots have long been consumed as a food source in the interior of Bengkulu Province. The account of Taba Anyar Village connects the expression *merajang rebung*—“chopping bamboo shoots”—with one explanation of the names Rejang and Lebong, although the explanation is described as doubtful.

Food and agricultural produce form part of *punjung*, the offering used during *kedurai agung*. The sources also mention cakes and *apem* pancake as offerings to Nyang Serai, the Goddess of Paddy or Goddess of Fertility, together with sacrificial animals and incense. In contemporary religious life, Islam influences funeral procedures, the use of halal food, and the practice of praying and Tahlila in the mosque.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources record several Rejang stories and beliefs. According to a belief reported from Lebong Regency, Rejang means “to cross over,” referring to an origin story in which ancestors came from a distant land and crossed from Borneo to Sumatra. Another story from Taba Anyar Village connects Rejang and Lebong with *merajang rebung*, or chopping bamboo shoots.

The tale of Muning Raib is associated with Curup and explains why Rejang people from Curup are forbidden to enter Mount Kaba, in order to avoid misfortune or calamity. The Dung Ulau Tujuak, or Seven Headed Snake, is described as a guardian inhabiting the *srawung*, an underwater cave beneath Tes Lake in Lebong Regency.

## Language & religion

Rejang is an Austronesian language spoken predominantly in Bengkulu. It has five dialects: Musi or Musai, Lebong, Kebanagung, Rawas or Awes, and Pesisir; the other account names Curup, Lebong, Kepahiang, Utara, and Awes. Rejang was traditionally written with the Rejang script on bamboo, buffalo horn, bark, or copper plates. The script is thought to predate Islam’s introduction to the area in the 12th century CE, while the earliest attested document dates from the mid-18th century. The Latin alphabet was introduced later.

Most Rejang people are Muslims, and the sources estimate that nearly all practice Islam. They describe adherence to Sunni Islam and the Shafi‘i school, with Muhammadiyah and Nahdlatul Ulama as the main Islamic organizations. Earlier beliefs included *diwo*, or gods, *nyang*, or goddesses, and *smat*, feared supernatural beings. Nyang Serai was associated with rice and fertility. The jungle was regarded as a gift from God and a source of life, while the tiger, or *imeu*, was understood as a sacred ancestral incarnation addressed as *datuk*, *ninik*, or *puyang*.
