## Overview

The Lampung, also called Lampungese, are an indigenous ethnic group native to Lampung and parts of South Sumatra, Bengkulu, and the southwest coast of Banten. Their traditional distribution includes regions along the Tulang Bawang river, the east, south, and west coasts of Lampung, and places such as Martapura, Muara Dua, Kayu Agung, Cikoneng, and Merpas. The group speaks Lampung, an Austronesian language or dialect cluster with around 1.5 million native speakers. Lampung society is conventionally divided into the coastal Saibatin Lampungs and the interior Pepadun Lampungs. Their cultural profile includes customary philosophical values, distinctive dialects and scripts, ceremonial textiles, and kris associated with Lampung and South Sumatra.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Woman’s ceremonial skirt (tapis)* — Museum records identify several nineteenth-century Lampung textiles as Woman’s Ceremonial Skirt (Tapis). They use cotton with gold-wrapped thread, silk paillettes, metal, paillettes, or lead.

*Ceremonial textile (tampan)* — A nineteenth-century Ceremonial Textile (Tampan) is recorded in cotton. The catalogue identifies the object as a Lampung ceremonial textile.

*Palepai* — The catalogue records a Palepai maju ceremonial banner from the eighteenth–nineteenth century made with glass and ceramic beads, fiber, cloth, and nassa shells. Another nineteenth-century Ceremonial Banner (palepai) uses cotton, metallic-wrapped yarns, and supplementary weft.

Lampung textiles in the Museum of World Culture records include gold embroidery and woven cotton ceremonial cloth. Their documented colors include orange and black, yellow, blue, white, red-brown, blue-black, red, and brown.

**Motif vocabulary.** Temple angles, suns, sun wheels, birds, mirrors of mica, angles, loops, and squares.

### Clothing & dress

The museum records describe Lampung ceremonial clothing as *högtidsklädnad* and identify garments including sarong and klädnad. One sarong is yellow, blue, and white cotton with gold embroidery and woven decoration depicting suns, sun wheels, and birds. Other ceremonial garments are described in orange and black, red-brown and blue-black, or blue and brown cotton, with gold embroidery and decorations of birds, mica mirrors, angles, loops, or squares. The Metropolitan Museum of Art records identify women’s ceremonial skirts as Tapis and date them to the nineteenth century.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum catalogue records several kris or keris associated with Lampung and South Sumatra. One is a small kris from South Sumatra (Lampung) with an unusual *ganja*, described as the top of the blade. Another early kris from Sumatra has a bird or Garuda? hilt, a damaskered steel blade, a wooden handle, and a brass fitting with filigree coverings; its sheath is described as being in the style of Palembang/Lampung. A further kris from Lampung has a Jawa Demam stylized bird hilt and a blade with five ondulations.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The museum records do document metallic-wrapped yarns, gold embroidery, brass, filigree, mica mirrors, paillettes, and beads as materials or decorations on textiles and kris.

## Music & performance

The Lampung people article records the *adi-adi* as poems expressing characteristic Lampung values. It gives the following text:

*Tandani ulun Lampung, wat piil-pusanggiri*  
*Mulia heno sehitung, wat liom ghega dighi*  
*Juluk-adok gham pegung, nemui-nyimah muaghi*  
*Nengah-nyampugh mak ngungkung, sakai-Sambaian gawi.*

## Festivals & rituals

The sources record customary social values associated with Lampung life. *Nemui-Nyimah* means maintaining contact by visiting one another and gladly receiving guests. *Nengah-Nyampur* refers to active participation in communal fellowship rather than individualism. *Sakai-Sambaian* means giving mutual assistance in the community. *Juluk-Adok* concerns a personality fitting the customary title one bears, while *Piil-Pusanggiri* describes embarrassment at doing despised work in relation to religion and self-esteem.

The Lampung people article states that these philosophical values are contained in the *Kuntara Raja Niti* manuscript. It also says that the values are represented by the five decorative flowers of the Lampung seal.

## Oral tradition & literature

The origins of the Lampung people are linked in the sources to the name of Lampung and to Chinese chronicles. The seventh century is associated with a place in the south called Nampang and with the Tolang Pohwang kingdom, which is said to have covered the area of present-day Tulang Bawang Regency and regions along the Tulang Bawang river. The fifth-century *Taiping Huanyu Ji* chronicles recorded the names To-lang and Po-hwang among the Nan-hai states. Po-hwang was said to have sent envoys to China in 442, 449, 451, 459, 464, and 466.

The sources also state that the Lampung people’s original lineage came from the Sekala Brak kingdom. The *Kuntara Raja Niti* manuscript is identified as the source containing their philosophical life. The *adi-adi* poems express the values of *Piil-Pusanggiri*, *Juluk-Adok*, *Nemui-Nyimah*, *Nengah-Nyampur*, and *Sakai-Sambaian*.

## Language & religion

Lampung is an Austronesian language or dialect cluster in the Malayo-Polynesian branch. It has around 1.5 million native speakers and is divided into Lampung Api, Lampung Nyo, and Komering, although Komering is sometimes treated as a separate language. The Lampung people article describes Api and Nyo as the two major dialects. Api is associated with several Saibatin and Pepadun regions, while Nyo is used by Abung and Tulangbawang communities.

Traditionally, Lampung was written in Rencong script, also called *had Lampung* or *aksara lampung*. The script is described as an abugida with 20 main characters and 13 diacritics, and as related to Pallawa, Arabic, Kerinci, Rejang, Bengkulu, and Bugis writing systems. The sources mention religion in connection with *Piil-Pusanggiri* and do not provide a general account of Lampung religious practice or religious affiliation.
