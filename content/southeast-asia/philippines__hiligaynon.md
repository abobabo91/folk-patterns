## Overview

The Hiligaynon, also called Ilonggo or Panayan, are the second-largest subgroup of the Visayan ethnic group. Their primary language is Hiligaynon, an Austronesian language of the Visayan branch associated with Panay, Guimaras, and Negros. They originated in Iloilo on Panay, in Western Visayas. Hiligaynon communities are also majorities in Guimaras, Negros Occidental, Capiz, South Cotabato, Sultan Kudarat, and Cotabato Province, and the language is spoken in parts of Sarangani Province. A 2010 census recorded Hiligaynon/Ilonggo as 8.44% of the national population. Their folk-culture profile includes weaving, fishing, agriculture, foodways, festivals, regional language varieties, and the continued use of traditional boats and farming tools.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Hablon** — *Hablon* is a specialty cloth woven from jusi and piña fibers. In Iloilo, weaving is based on local fibers, and the industry has traditionally been dominated by skilled women working in weaving cooperatives, notably in Miagao. Knowledge is passed from mother to daughter.

**Piña** — Piña is a textile fiber made from pineapple. By the 1850s, its production formed part of Iloilo’s substantial textile export industry.

**Jusi** — Jusi is a woven material combining piña and silk. It is used in hablon and forms part of the textile tradition historically associated with Iloilo.

**Sinamay** — Sinamay is a textile combining abacá and cotton. It was among the products that contributed to Iloilo’s reputation for textile production.

**Hablon products of Kabankalan** — The products identified with the Kabankalan indigenous peoples include shawls, scarves, bandano tobao, t-shirts, table runners, place mats, and tela.

Hand-loom weaving began historically after trade with the Chinese introduced weaving materials. Iloilo became known as the “textile capital of the Philippines” for its production of piña, silk, jusi, and sinamay. Weaving declined after the rise of sugarcane production and the availability of inexpensive British cotton cloth, but local weavers continue to produce specialty fabrics.

### Clothing & dress

The sources identify *barongs* as traditional men’s formalwear made with hablon. Hablon is also used for decorative household linens and other accessories.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do mention farming tools: farmers in the mountainous interior of central Panay use bolo knives to cut trees and wooden dibbers to plant seeds.

## Music & performance

The sources describe several performance and media contexts. The Ilonggo Arts Festival uses contemporary media including film and radio, alongside public performances, and sponsors dialogue concerning cultural preservation. The sources also identify Angel Magahum Sr. as a Hiligaynon writer, editor, and composer who composed *Iloilo ang Banwa Ko*, described as the unofficial song of Iloilo.

Hiligaynon speech is marked by a song-like intonation. Hiligaynon-language literary and broadcast activity includes radio drama, poetry, novels, theater, editing, and translation. Elizabeth Batiduan Navarro is identified as a Hiligaynon drama writer for radio programs of Bombo Radyo Philippines.

## Dance & theatre

They mention public performances at the Ilonggo Arts Festival and identify Valente Cristobal as a Hiligaynon playwright. The sources also describe Elizabeth Batiduan Navarro as a writer of Hiligaynon drama for radio programs. No additional account of ceremonial dance, dramatic genres, masks, puppetry, or performance choreography is provided.

## Festivals & rituals

The **Dinagyang festival** is celebrated every fourth Sunday of January in Iloilo City. Its name derives from *dagyang*, meaning “merry-making.” Modeled after Ati-atihan in Kalibo, Aklan, Dinagyang venerates the Santo Niño and commemorates the purchase of Panay Island from the Indigenous Ati by ten fleeing Bornean datus, or chiefs.

The **Iloilo Paraw Regatta** is held each year in February. It combines a week-long competition at sea with a festival on land. The boats used are *paraw*, traditional sailboats that have long been used by the Hiligaynon, and competitors include local fishermen. The event is associated with cultural preservation.

Cultural festivals more broadly are described as serving cultural preservation and celebration in response to the homogenizing of Philippine culture, while also supporting local and national tourism.

## Foodways

Hiligaynon meals commonly include fish and other seafood, often cooked with local green produce and spices. Rice is served as part of the meal. Fishing has contributed to Hiligaynon cuisine and diet since before the arrival of the Spanish. Coastal towns in Iloilo have a strong fishing tradition, with fish available in the Guimaras Strait. Inland fishing, especially for prawn, is also practiced.

**La Paz Batchoy** is composed of pork innards, liver, and heart in a broth with noodles, chicharon, and garlic. **Pancit Molo** is a wonton soup with dumplings filled with pork, chicken, and shrimp, with green onions and garlic. **Ibus** is a finger food made from glutinous rice wrapped in coconut leaves in the shape of a roll.

Other soup dishes associated with the Hiligaynon include **Kadyos, baboy, kag lanka**, also called KBL, **Laswa**, and **Kansi**. Agriculture supplies significant quantities of rice and sugarcane. Early Hiligaynon cultivation practices also produced wine from the juice of these crops.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that some facts concerning the settlement of Panay are clouded by folk mythology among the Hiligaynon. They also preserve several terms and expressions through descriptions of language, including *dagyang*, meaning “merry-making,” and the Hiligaynon verbal forms and vocabulary used in grammatical examples.

Hiligaynon literary culture includes poetry, drama, fiction, journalism, editing, and translation. Peter Solis Nery is described as a writer, poet, playwright, novelist, editor, and champion of the Hiligaynon language. Other named writers include Graciano López Jaena, Ramon Muzones, Magdalena Jalandoni, Flavio Zaragoza y Cano, Conrado Saquian Norada, Angel Magahum Sr., Valente Cristobal, Elizabeth Batiduan Navarro, and Genevieve L. Asenjo.

## Language & religion

Hiligaynon is an Austronesian language of the Visayan, or Bisayan, branch. It is concentrated in Western Visayas, Negros Island Region, and Soccsksargen, and is spoken in neighboring provinces and regions. Recognized varieties include Standard Hiligaynon, Urban Hiligaynon, Bacolodnon Hiligaynon, Negrense Hiligaynon, Guimaras Hiligaynon, and Mindanao Hiligaynon. The language is written in the Latin script. Its orthography was standardized in 2022 and is based on current Filipino orthographic rules.

Most Hiligaynons are Christians, the majority of these being Roman Catholics. Smaller populations are Aglipayan, Protestant, and Muslim. Indigenous Hiligaynon faiths were largely eliminated during the Spanish era and survived as Folk Catholicism.
