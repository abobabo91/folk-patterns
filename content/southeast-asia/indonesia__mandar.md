## Overview

The Mandarese are an Austronesian ethnic group native to West Sulawesi. Mandar people also live in South Sulawesi, particularly in the coastal regencies of Majene and Polewali Mandar. Mandar belongs to the Northern subgroup of the South Sulawesi languages, within the Malayo-Polynesian branch of the Austronesian language family. The closest language to Mandar is Toraja-Sa'dan. The Mandarese are closely related to the Bugis, Makassar, and Toraja peoples. Their cultural organization is associated with seven coastal kingdoms, seven river kingdoms, and three additional kingdoms. Fisheries, agriculture, maritime activity, customary festivals, music, foodways, and the *boyang* house are among the practices described in the sources.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state that many women left their traditional work at the loom and began engaging in the fish trade instead.

### Architecture

The traditional Mandarese house is called *boyang*. The sources name this building type.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They identify a traditional two-stringed lute and an outrigger canoe in connection with Mandarese culture, but provide no catalogue description or material account of these objects.

## Music & performance

A traditional Mandarese musical instrument is a two-stringed lute. The Mandarese are described as among the best sailors in Sulawesi and as serving in the sea transportation sector.

The sources mention customary festivals and performances associated with Mandarese culture. These include **Sayyang Pattu'du**, glossed in the source as “Dancing horse,” and **Passandeq**, described as sailing on an outrigger canoe.

## Dance & theatre

**Sayyang Pattu'du** is a Mandarese customary festival whose name is glossed as “Dancing horse.” **Passandeq** is another customary festival, described as sailing on an outrigger canoe.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources identify several customary practices. **Sayyang Pattu'du** is glossed as “Dancing horse,” and **Passandeq** is described as sailing on an outrigger canoe. In South Pulau Laut District, Kota Baru Regency, the Mandarese practice **Mappando'esasi**, glossed as the “Sea bathing” ceremony.

They state that the Mandarese adopted Islam in the early 17th century and are predominantly Muslim.

Mandarese political and cultural identity is also associated with a covenant sworn by ancestors at Allewuang Batu in Luyo. The term *Sipamandar* is explained as meaning brotherhood and unification of the Mandarese community, referring to the formation of one people through that ancestral covenant.

## Foodways

Mandarese foodways include fisheries, agriculture, and the preparation of fish. The Mandarese engage in fisheries by exporting dried, salted, or fermented fish. They also cultivate coconut palm, dried rice, coffee, and tobacco, as well as practicing forestry.

Named Mandarese specialties include *Jepa*, *Pandeangang Peapi*, and *Banggulung Tapa*. The sources identify these as traditional foods.

The sources state that the Mandarese are predominantly Muslim, but do not specifically describe halal food rules, fasting practices, feast-day meals, tea or coffee customs, or distinctions between everyday and ceremonial food. No further food traditions are covered by the sources used.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that Mandar is written in the Lontara script and is now also written in Latin script.

The sources associate Mandarese identity with an ancestral covenant at Allewuang Batu in Luyo and explain *Sipamandar* as brotherhood and unification of the Mandarese community.

## Language & religion

Mandar is an Austronesian language spoken by Mandar people in West Sulawesi and South Sulawesi, particularly in the coastal regencies of Majene and Polewali Mandar. It belongs to the Northern subgroup of the South Sulawesi languages within the Malayo-Polynesian branch of the Austronesian language family. Toraja-Sa'dan is identified as its closest language. Mandar is written in the Lontara script and is also written in Latin script.

Mandarese are closely related to the Bugis, Makassar, and Toraja peoples. The sources state that the Mandarese are predominantly Muslim and adopted Islam in the early 17th century.
