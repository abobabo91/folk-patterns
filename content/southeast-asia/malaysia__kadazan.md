## Overview

The Kadazan are an Austronesian ethnic group indigenous to Sabah, Malaysia. They primarily live in the West Coast Division, especially in Kota Kinabalu, Penampang, Putatan and Papar, as well as in Beaufort, Membakut and other locations in the Interior Division; they also live in Labuk-Kinabatangan and the Federal Territory of Labuan. The Kadazan and Dusun together were estimated at 568,575, while the 1991 Sabah Census recorded around 104,924 Kadazan and 216,910 Dusun before the groups were merged. Coastal Kadazan, also known as Dusun Tangara, is a dialect of Central Dusun. Kadazan culture is closely associated with rice cultivation, the Kaamatan harvest festival, bobohizan ritual specialists, gong music, sumazau dance and distinctive ceremonial clothing.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Penampang Kadazan attire** — The women’s garments consist of a blouse and long skirt made of black velvet or another black fabric, decorated with gold-coloured lace trimmings. The Penampang and Papar forms differ in tailoring, motifs and textiles, although both preserve shared heritage.

**Sinuangga'** — This sleeveless garment is worn by young Penampang Kadazan women. It forms part of a two-piece gown with gold-coloured decoration and traditional accessories.

**Sinompukung** — Middle-aged women wear this garment, which is distinguished by three-quarter sleeves.

**Kihongon** — Elderly women wear this long-sleeved garment, whose design reflects their seniority.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional Kadazan dress represents status, identity and ceremonial function. Penampang Kadazan women wear a blouse and long skirt in black velvet or black fabric, decorated with gold-coloured lace trimmings. Young women wear the sleeveless *Sinuangga'*, middle-aged women the three-quarter-sleeved *Sinompukung*, and elderly women the long-sleeved *Kihongon*. The ensemble includes British Trade Dollar belts known individually as a *himpogot*, a set of three brass girdles known individually as a *tangkong*, and other brass or silver accessories. The garments are further decorated with ornamental gold-coloured buttons known as *batawi* among the West Coast Bajau.

### Architecture

They state that the Nunuk Ragang tree was said to shelter seven joined houses, with each house measuring 12 by 20 feet, but this is part of an origin legend rather than a documented account of ordinary architecture.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Kadazan material culture includes brass and silver accessories, brass girdles, gold-coloured buttons, gongs, copperware and brassware. The sources state that barter trade brought gongs, copperware, brassware, silverware, necklaces and bangles to the Kadazan. In the 18th century, traders from Brunei introduced brass gongs, which became cultural assets and were passed down as heirlooms. The latest types of gongs are made entirely of mild steel, colloquially called *bosi'*, and are mainly produced in Kudat and sold at weekend markets called *tamu*, especially Tamu Donggongon of Penampang.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The *himpogot* is a belt made from British Trade Dollars. The *tangkong* is a set of three brass girdles, regarded as a prized heirloom and valuable cultural asset; aged examples are especially sought after. The sources state that *tangkong* are believed to act as talismans, offering spiritual protection during rituals and public appearances. Women also wear brass or silver accessories and ornamental gold-coloured buttons called *batawi*.

## Music & performance

Kadazan music centres on gongs and other instruments used in ceremonies, weddings, festive occasions, welcoming receptions and funerals. The *tagung* is an individually named gong in a symphony of six handcrafted gongs. Its rhythms include *magagung*, *botibas* and *dunsai*. At wedding receptions, festive occasions and welcoming receptions for dignitaries, the sound may be exhilarating, melodious and soothing; *botibas* can be performed as a variation. At funerals, *dunsai* is solemn and fearsome and serves as a sign of respect for the dead. In the past, gong sounds also signalled emergencies.

The *sompoton* is an aerophone made from a dried gourd and bamboo. It is played by blowing into it and produces a soft and harmonious sound. Other instruments include the *kulintangan*, the *togunggak* and the *gandang*. The *kulintangan* is a set of nine gong chimes played as an accompaniment to the *magagung* rhythm and may be combined with the *tagung*. According to an animistic Kadazan-Dusun legend, bobohizan taught people to fashion bamboo into an ensemble of six musical instruments, with the seventh item being the *gandang*.

## Dance & theatre

*Sumazau* is the traditional Kadazan dance. It is performed by a man and a woman, or sometimes by a large group, and is usually accompanied by six handcrafted gongs. Both genders wear a ceremonial sash during the dance. It is commonly performed at joyous ceremonies and occasions, especially wedding receptions. The dance has localised forms across Sabah, including *Sumazau Penampang* in Penampang, *Sazau Papar* in Papar and *Sazau Paina* in Membakut.

## Festivals & rituals

The major harvest festival is *Kaamatan*, held annually in May. It honours the paddy spirit *Bambazon* and gives thanks for abundance. Rice farming is closely connected with Kadazan spirituality, and bobohizan perform significant rituals to ensure an abundant harvest and protect crops. During Kaamatan, the best-known event is *Unduk Ngadau*, a beauty pageant held at Hongkod Koisaan Hall. Sumandak of Kadazan descent, together with contestants of Dusun, Murut and Rungus descent from different districts, compete for the title. The pageant commemorates *Huminodun*, a mythological character said to have given her life in exchange for a plentiful harvest.

The sources also describe the *magang* ritual, performed by a bobohizan for the appeasement of skulls. In earlier times, skulls were believed to retain powerful spirits and provide protection.

## Foodways

Rice cultivation is the traditional occupation most strongly associated with Kadazan culture. Wet rice cultivation was once the main occupation of the Kadazan living in the flat plains and lowland areas of Papar and Penampang. Rice is central to rituals, identity and social cohesion, and the Kaamatan harvest festival celebrates the paddy spirit *Bambazon* and the abundance of the harvest. The sources also state that various delicacies and alcoholic drinks are prepared through differing home-brewed fermentation processes.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kadazan oral tradition includes the stories of Huminodun and Nunuk Ragang, which were passed down orally, possibly by generations of bobohizan. Nunuk Ragang is associated with a giant red fig tree at a site said to be roughly located in Kg. Tompios, at the confluence of the Liwagu and Kogibangan rivers, east of Ranau and Tambunan. *Nunuk* is the Dusun word for a type of fig tree, while *ragang* derives from *aragang*, meaning “red.”

The legend tells of migration from Nunuk Ragang after the Minorit Push. The Kadazan and Rungus travelled through the Labuk River and later reached Tempasuk, Kota Belud, through the Marak Parak valley of Kota Marudu, before separating. Another Rungus origin story identifies Longguvai as the ancestor of the Tangara people. The sources also preserve oral tradition about Monsopiad, a warrior said to have taken 42 heads.

## Language & religion

Coastal Kadazan, also known as Dusun Tangara, is a dialect of Central Dusun and is primarily spoken in Sabah, especially in Penampang, Papar and Membakut. It is highly mutually intelligible with Central Dusun and is considered by many to be the same language. Coastal Kadazan has adopted loanwords from northern Borneo indigenous languages and Malay and extensively uses the voiced alveolar sibilant fricative /z/. Its use has declined because of the enforcement of Bahasa Malaysia and the use of English by missionaries, although policies have introduced Kadazan and other indigenous languages into public schools.

The majority of Kadazan are Roman Catholics, while traditional animist practices remain important. Bobohizan, also called bobolian or bobolizan, perform rituals and ceremonies connected with crops, spirits, skulls, taboos and customs. The sources state that UNESCO internationally recognised the Kadazan and Dusun as an indigenous group found on the northern part of Borneo in 2004.
