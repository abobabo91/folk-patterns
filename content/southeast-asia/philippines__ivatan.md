## Overview

The Ivatan people are an Austronesian ethnolinguistic group native to the Batanes and Babuyan Islands of the northernmost Philippines. They are genetically closely related to ethnic groups in Northern Luzon and share linguistic and cultural affinities with the Tao people of Orchid Island in Taiwan. In 1990, the Ivatan population was 15,026; in the 2000 census, 15,834 Ivatans were among the 16,421 residents of Batanes. Their culture has been strongly shaped by Batanes’ severe climate, frequent typhoons, drought, and maritime setting. Stone houses, root-crop agriculture, weather observation, fishing, the *vakul*, folk songs, legends, and chants are prominent elements of the documented culture.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The principal named Ivatan garment is the *vakul*, headgear designed to protect the wearer from sun and rain. It is made from *vuyavuy* palm fiber.

### Architecture

Ivatan architecture developed in response to the environmental conditions of Batanes. Unlike the old-type nipa huts common in the Philippines, Ivatans adopted distinctive stone houses made of coral and limestone. Their meter-thick limestone walls protect against the harsh environment and strong winds associated with Batanes, described as a terminal passage of typhoons in the Philippines. The basic cogon grass roof remains in use and is thickly constructed to withstand strong winds. Before Spanish arrival, Ivatans built smaller houses from cogon grass; these were well situated and designed for protection against strong winds. The Spaniards introduced large-scale lime production for the construction of the stone houses. Pre-colonial Ivatans also constructed fortified hills protected by sheer embankments known as *ijang* or *idjang*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Archaeological excavations show that the Batanes Islands participated in an extensive trade in jade artifacts known as *lingling-o*. Archaeological assemblages are divided into four phases. Phase 1, from 2500 to 1000 BCE, includes red-slipped and fine cord-marked pottery. Phase 2, from 1300 BCE to 1 CE, includes circle-stamped and red-slipped pottery, later developing rectangular and “fishnet” designs, as well as Fengtian nephrite sourced from Taiwan. Phase 3, from 500 BC/1 CE to 1200 CE, includes plain red-slipped pottery. Phase 4, from around 1200 CE onwards, includes imported pottery connected with trade contacts with the Song and Yuan dynasties of China.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention jade artifacts called *lingling-o* and Fengtian nephrite sourced from Taiwan. Tattoos, henna, hair practices, and additional adornment traditions are not covered by the sources used.

## Music & performance

The Ivatans have three named folk song styles: *laji*, *kanta*, and *kalusan*. The *laji* are ancient lyrical songs intended to be sung when people are merry or have just finished work. The *kalusan* is sung during work. The sources name *kanta* as a folk song style. The sea and agricultural environment described in the sources provide contexts for work and community life, but no additional musical practices can be added from the material supplied.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe several religious and celebratory contexts. *Uvod*, the pith of the banana stalk, is served with the wine *palek* on festive occasions such as weddings. June 6 is celebrated in Batanes as its founding day.

The sources state that most Ivatans are Catholics, while some people have not converted and practice ancestral worship to their *anitos*. Growing Protestant denominations are especially present in Basco, the capital town of Batanes. No birth, coming-of-age, funeral, or other life-cycle rites are described.

## Foodways

Ivatan foodways are closely connected to agriculture and the sea. Because frequent typhoons and drought can disrupt agriculture, Ivatans traditionally plant root crops able to cope with environmental conditions. The sources name yam, sweet potato, taro, garlic, ginger, and onion. These crops provide higher chances of survival during adverse climate conditions.

The sea is vital to Ivatan life. Ivatans depend on flying fish, called *dibang*, and dolphinfish, called *arayu*, present on the shores of Batanes from March through May. A named delicacy is *uvod*, the pith of the banana stalk. It is served with *palek*, a wine, during festive occasions such as weddings.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ivatan legends are called *kabbata*. The Ivatans also have *rawod*, chants that chronicle the adventures of their forefathers as they escaped a disaster. These are the only named oral-literary forms identified in the sources.

## Language & religion

The Ivatan language is also known as *Chirin nu Ivatan*, “language of the Ivatan people.” It is an Austronesian language spoken in the Batanes Islands and belongs to the Batanic languages, which are perhaps a primary branch of the Malayo-Polynesian family of Austronesian languages. The language is especially characterized by words containing the letter v, including *vakul*, *Ivatan*, and *valuga*.

Ivatan has two dialects: Basco Ivatan, more commonly known as Ivasay, spoken on the main island of Batan, and Southern Ivatan or Isamurung, spoken on the southern half of Batan and on Sabtang. Itbayaten is sometimes considered a dialect. The sources also mention Ibatan on Babuyan Island, whose classification as a dialect of Ivatan or a separate language is not entirely clear.

Ivatan is written using the Latin alphabet. A Department of Education orthography uses the 26-letter Latin alphabet with three extra letters: *ch*, *ñ*, and *ng*. Most Ivatans are Catholics; some practice ancestral worship to their *anitos*, and Protestant denominations are growing, especially in Basco.
