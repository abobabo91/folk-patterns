## Overview

The Kinh (officially *người Kinh*, also called *Việt*) are the majority ethnic group of Vietnam, accounting for roughly 85.3% of the country's population according to the 2019 census — over 82 million people out of a national total that surpassed 100 million in 2023. Historically concentrated in the lowland river systems of the Red River Delta in the north and the Mekong Delta in the south, and along the narrow coastal plains connecting them, the Kinh gradually expanded southward from the 11th century onward through a process known as *Nam tiến* ("southward march"), absorbing or displacing Cham and Khmer Krom populations. Their language, Vietnamese, belongs to the Austroasiatic family alongside Khmer and Mon. In folk-culture terms the Kinh are the substrate that carries most of Vietnam's UNESCO-recognized intangible heritage — court music, folk song genres, Mother Goddess worship, village festivals — and the producers of its best-known textile, print, and lacquer traditions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Silk weaving of Vạn Phúc and Hà Đông** — *lụa Vạn Phúc*, produced in Hà Đông district on the outskirts of Hanoi, is the classical Kinh silk: fine mulberry-fed filament woven on drawlooms into damasks (*lụa hoa*, "flowered silk") whose motifs are visible only under raking light. It has been made in the village for over a thousand years and traditionally supplied the imperial court in Huế.

**Brocade weaving** — Kinh *gấm* differs from the supplementary-weft brocades of highland Tày, Thái and Mường weavers by using continuous silk supplementary wefts on a satin ground, producing dense figurative panels of dragons, phoenixes, and clouds used for mandarin robes, altar cloths, and *áo dài* trim.

**Áo dài** — the tunic-and-trouser ensemble *áo dài* is not itself a textile but a garment form that concentrates Kinh pattern culture: painted, embroidered, or brocaded panels running the length of the front and back skirts, and increasingly since the 1930s (via the *Cát Tường / Lemur* redesign) tailored close to the body.

**Đông Hồ woodblock prints** — *tranh Đông Hồ*, made in Đông Hồ village (Bắc Ninh province), are folk New Year prints stamped from carved jackfruit-wood blocks onto *giấy điệp* paper coated with crushed seashell. Pigments are all mineral or vegetal (indigo, gamboge, sappanwood red, charcoal black). Subjects include the "jealousy" (*Đánh ghen*), the pig family (*Lợn đàn*), and the wrestler (*Đấu vật*).

**Hàng Trống prints** — *tranh Hàng Trống*, made on Hàng Trống street in Hanoi's Old Quarter, use a single key block for outlines and hand-brushed watercolor washes over the top. Compared with Đông Hồ, the palette is more subdued and the subjects lean toward Taoist and Buddhist altar images (the Five Tigers, *Ngũ Hổ*) rather than village genre scenes.

**Lacquer painting** — *sơn mài*, the Vietnamese refinement of East Asian lacquer developed at the École des Beaux-Arts de l'Indochine in the 1930s, layers *sơn ta* (resin of *Rhus succedanea*) with crushed eggshell, mother-of-pearl inlay, and silver or gold leaf, then sands the surface back to reveal a jewel-like image.

**Conical hat leaf-work** — the *nón lá*, and its finer poetry-inlaid Huế variant *nón bài thơ*, is a palm-leaf tradition rather than a textile, but its concentric bamboo hoops and stitched *lá cọ* leaves are a recognizable Kinh pattern object.

**Motif vocabulary.** *long* (dragon, four-clawed for nobles, five-clawed for the emperor), *phượng* (phoenix), *lân* (qilin), *quy* (tortoise) — the four sacred beasts *tứ linh*; *tùng-cúc-trúc-mai* (pine-chrysanthemum-bamboo-plum, the four noble plants); *thọ* (longevity), *phúc* (fortune) and *song hỷ* (double happiness) characters; *mây* (stylized cloud scrolls); *sóng nước* (wave-and-water); *hoa sen* (lotus).

### Clothing & dress

Everyday Kinh dress in the pre-modern countryside was minimal: for men a brown or black cross-collared *áo cánh* jacket over loose trousers, often bare-chested in the fields; for women a *yếm* halter-bib under a four-panel *áo tứ thân* tunic tied at the waist, with a black or brown skirt (*váy*) in the north or trousers in the south, and a flat *khăn mỏ quạ* ("crow's beak") headcloth folded to a point over the brow. The five-panel *áo ngũ thân*, developed in the 18th century under the Nguyễn lords in Huế, is the direct ancestor of today's fitted *áo dài*, worn by both sexes with a *khăn đóng* — a pre-shaped turban of stiffened silk. Ceremonial mandarin dress at the Nguyễn court used embroidered dragon and phoenix roundels indicating rank. Wooden clogs (*guốc mộc*) and the palm-leaf *nón lá* complete the classical silhouette; the *nón quai thao*, a wide flat hat with long silk chin-straps, is characteristic of Quan Họ singers in Bắc Ninh.

### Architecture

The classical Kinh village (*làng*) is walled or hedged with bamboo and organized around a *đình* — a communal house on stumpy wooden columns with a swooping tile roof whose ridge terminates in dragon finials (*rồng*) or *makara* pairs. The *đình* houses the tutelary spirit of the village and hosts festivals. The *chùa* (Mahāyāna Buddhist pagoda) and the *đền* (spirit temple) follow related timber-frame conventions with three or five bays (*gian*). Domestic houses in the northern delta are typically three-to-five-bay single-storey structures under a low hipped or gable-and-hip tile roof, with a raised brick threshold, an open central bay containing the ancestor altar, and side bays used as sleeping and storage rooms; the courtyard in front is used for drying rice. In Huế the aristocratic *nhà rường* refines this form with elaborately joined jackwood columns and no metal fasteners. Hanoi's Old Quarter preserves the *nhà ống* ("tube house") — narrow-fronted shophouses of 2–5 metres frontage running dozens of metres deep, with internal light-wells and a shop at the street. The imperial citadel of Huế (Kinh Thành, 1805–32), laid out on Vauban lines around the Purple Forbidden City, is the paradigmatic Kinh monumental complex.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Kinh ceramics are anchored by **Bát Tràng** stoneware, produced in a village east of Hanoi since the 14th century: blue-and-white *gốm hoa lam* dishes and altar sets, celadons, and a distinctive crackled cream glaze *men rạn*. **Chu Đậu** ware, revived after long dormancy, produced the elegant 15th-century underglaze-blue vessels found in the Hội An shipwreck. **Phù Lãng** and **Thổ Hà** kilns fire salt-glazed brown storage jars used for pickling and fish sauce fermentation. Bronze casting at **Đại Bái** and **Ngũ Xã** villages produces temple bells, incense burners, ritual gongs, and the reproductions of Đông Sơn drums that remain a national symbol. Everyday objects with strong identity content include the *mâm đồng* (round brass tray) that carries a meal, the *ống nhổ* betel-nut spittoon, the black-lacquered wooden ancestor altar, and the *đũa cả* long serving chopsticks.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Kinh jewelry is comparatively restrained beside that of Vietnam's highland minorities. Traditional forms include gold and jade *vòng* bangles given at weddings and passed matrilineally, gold *khuyên* ear-hoops, *dây chuyền* neck-chains carrying a jade *hồ lô* (gourd) or Buddha pendant, and the *kiềng* — a rigid, hinged neck-ring of twisted gold worn by brides. The most historically distinctive body-modification practice is the blackening of the teeth with iron-and-lacquer varnish (*nhuộm răng đen*), universal among women (and many men) in the north until the early 20th century and considered a mark of adult beauty. Betel-and-areca chewing (*ăn trầu*) stained the mouth red and was, with tooth-blackening, the paired sign of maturity; its ritual offering opens engagements and weddings to this day.

## Music & performance

Kinh music spans a full spectrum from imperial court repertoire to village song. *Nhã nhạc* — "elegant music" of the Nguyễn court at Huế, inscribed by UNESCO in 2003 — uses ensembles of *đàn nhị* (two-string fiddle), *đàn nguyệt* (moon-shaped lute), *đàn tỳ bà* (pear-shaped lute), *đàn tranh* (16-string zither, now often 17-string), *sáo trúc* (bamboo flute), and *trống* drums, with the *đại nhạc* and *tiểu nhạc* ensembles marking large and small ceremonial occasions. *Ca trù*, a chamber genre of the north performed by a female singer who beats a *phách* wooden clapper, a male *đàn đáy* lute player, and a connoisseur audience-member on a praise drum (*trống chầu*), is on the UNESCO Urgent Safeguarding List. *Quan họ Bắc Ninh* alternates unaccompanied male and female choral responses at village gatherings; *hát xoan* and *hát xoan Phú Thọ*, associated with the Hùng King cult, use ritual song at spring communal-house rites. *Ví* and *giặm* folk songs of Nghệ Tĩnh accompany rice-planting, boat-rowing, and lullabies. In the south, *đờn ca tài tử* Nam Bộ is a chamber-music tradition of amateur ensembles playing *đàn kìm*, *đàn tranh*, *đàn bầu* (monochord) and *đàn cò*, and it underpins the theatrical genre *cải lương*. Central Vietnam contributes *bài chòi*, a card-game-cum-sung-entertainment recognized by UNESCO in 2017.

## Dance & theatre

Kinh performing arts include several distinct theatrical genres. *Hát chèo* is the northern comic opera of the villages, performed on a mat laid in the courtyard of the *đình* with stock characters (the clown *hề*, the virtuous wife) and satirical songs. *Hát tuồng* (also *hát bội*) is the southern-and-central classical opera modeled loosely on Chinese *jingju*, with painted faces coded to character type, stylized battle choreography, and Confucian loyalty plots. *Cải lương*, "reformed theatre," emerged in the Mekong Delta in the 1910s as a modern melodramatic form set to *đờn ca tài tử* music. The most internationally recognized Kinh performing art is *múa rối nước*, water puppetry: carved wooden puppets are manipulated by bamboo rods from behind a screen across a pond or flooded stage, dramatizing episodes of rural life (fishing, buffalo-fighting, the fairy dance of *Tứ Linh*) to *chèo* music. Ritual court dances include *múa cung đình* and the "eight-flower" *múa bát dật*, revived at Huế festivals. The *Gióng* festival at Phù Đổng and Sóc temples enacts the martial legend of Saint Gióng in a large-scale processional pageant inscribed by UNESCO in 2010.

## Festivals & rituals

The Kinh year runs on the lunisolar calendar. *Tết Nguyên Đán* (Lunar New Year) is the paramount festival, marked by cleaning the ancestor altar, offering the *mâm ngũ quả* five-fruit tray, cooking square *bánh chưng* and cylindrical *bánh tét* rice cakes, giving red *lì xì* envelopes, and welcoming the *Táo Quân* kitchen gods back from their annual report to the Jade Emperor a week earlier. The **Death Anniversary of the Hùng Kings** on the 10th day of the third lunar month is a national pilgrimage to Phú Thọ province, honoring the semi-legendary founders of the Vietnamese state (UNESCO 2012). *Tết Trung Thu*, the mid-autumn moon festival, gives children lion-dances, lantern processions, and *bánh trung thu* mooncakes. *Tết Đoan Ngọ* (5th day, 5th month) is the parasite-killing festival; the seventh-month *Vu Lan* is the Buddhist filial-piety and hungry-ghost season. Village *lễ hội* honor tutelary gods with palanquin processions, wrestling, human-chess (*cờ người*), and the sitting tug-of-war rites inscribed by UNESCO in 2015. Life-cycle rites center on the ancestor altar: the *thôi nôi* first-birthday tray divination, engagement (*lễ ăn hỏi*) with betel-and-areca and gift trays carried by young men, the wedding *lễ cưới*, and above all the *giỗ* death-anniversary meal at which the deceased is offered food and invited to return. The *Đạo Mẫu* Mother Goddess cult, whose spirit-possession rite *lên đồng* is inscribed by UNESCO (2016), summons deities of the Three Realms — Heaven, Water, and Mountains-and-Forests — through elaborately costumed mediumship.

## Foodways

The Kinh table is built on wet-rice (*cơm*), fermented fish sauce (*nước mắm*, best from Phú Quốc and Phan Thiết), fresh herbs, and a light hand with oil. Signature dishes vary sharply by region. In the north: *phở* beef noodle soup of Hanoi with its clear anise-and-ginger broth; *bún chả* grilled pork over rice noodles; *bún thang* multi-ingredient chicken vermicelli; *chả cá Lã Vọng* turmeric-dill fried fish. In the imperial center of Huế: *bún bò Huế* spicy lemongrass beef noodles, *bánh khoái* and *bánh bèo* rice cakes, and a whole tradition of vegetarian temple cuisine (*ăn chay*) developed for the Nguyễn court's Buddhist observances. In the south: *hủ tiếu* clear noodle soup, *bánh xèo* turmeric crepes, *cá kho tộ* caramelized clay-pot fish, and the sweet coconut-milk desserts (*chè*) of the Mekong. *Bánh mì*, the baguette sandwich, is the enduring French colonial hybrid. Ceremonial foods include the *bánh chưng* — glutinous rice, mung bean, and pork wrapped in *dong* leaves, tied with bamboo strips, and boiled for twelve hours — which commemorates Prince Lang Liêu's Hùng-era invention of the earth-cake. Beverages include green tea (*trà xanh*), the lotus-flower-scented *trà sen* of West Lake, rice liquor (*rượu đế*), and the drip-brewed condensed-milk coffee *cà phê sữa đá*.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kinh oral tradition preserves origin myths of the dragon-lord *Lạc Long Quân* and the fairy *Âu Cơ*, whose hundred sons are the ancestors of the Vietnamese; the child-hero *Thánh Gióng* who grew overnight to repel the Ân invaders; the star-crossed weaver-girl and cowherd of the *Ngưu Lang – Chức Nữ* legend; and the tale of *Tấm Cám*, the Vietnamese Cinderella. The classical literary summit is *Truyện Kiều*, Nguyễn Du's 3,254-line verse novel in *lục bát* (six-eight) meter, composed around 1820 and known by heart in whole passages by many Kinh; villagers still practise *bói Kiều*, opening the book at random for divination. Earlier vernacular verse — Hồ Xuân Hương's erotically-charged 18th-century poems, the anonymous *ca dao* couplets sung during work — was written in *chữ Nôm*, the demotic character script. Proverbs (*tục ngữ*) and the *ca dao* corpus were systematically collected in the 20th century by scholars including Nguyễn Văn Ngọc and later Nguyễn Đổng Chi, whose five-volume *Kho tàng truyện cổ tích Việt Nam* remains the standard folktale compendium.

## Language & religion

Vietnamese (*tiếng Việt*) is an Austroasiatic (Mon–Khmer) language, tonal (six tones in the northern standard, five in the south) and largely monosyllabic, with a lexicon 50–70% derived from Chinese through two millennia of contact. It has been written successively in Chinese characters (*chữ Hán*), the demotic *chữ Nôm* script devised from the 10th century for the vernacular, and since the 17th century in *chữ Quốc ngữ*, a Latin alphabet developed by Jesuit missionaries — Alexandre de Rhodes chief among them — and refined by Vietnamese scholars. The three main dialect regions are Northern (Hanoi), Central (Huế, distinctly harder for other Vietnamese to follow), and Southern (Ho Chi Minh City). The Kinh religious landscape layers Mahāyāna Buddhism, Confucian ancestor veneration, and Daoist cosmology over a substrate of animist folk religion (*tín ngưỡng dân gian*), with the Mother Goddess cult (*Đạo Mẫu*) as its most vivid ritual expression. About 6% are Catholic, a legacy of 17th-century missions and the strong Church presence in Bùi Chu and Phát Diệm; Cao Đài and Hòa Hảo, both 20th-century southern syncretic religions, add further layers.
