## Overview

The Khmer are the dominant ethnic group of Cambodia, comprising over 95% of the country's population of roughly 17 million, with substantial communities beyond national borders: the *Khmer Surin* (over one million) in the Thai provinces of Surin, Buriram, and Sisaket on the southern Khorat plateau, and the *Khmer Krom* in the Mekong Delta of southern Vietnam (estimates range from 1.3 to 7 million). They speak Khmer, an Austroasiatic language related to Mon and Vietnamese, written in a script that itself gave rise to Thai and Lao writing. Culturally, the Khmer are heirs to the Khmer Empire (802–1431) and the Angkor tradition; their village life still revolves around the Theravada Buddhist *wat*, and their court arts — silk weaving, classical dance, shadow theatre — remain among the most codified in mainland Southeast Asia.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Khmer silk** — Cambodian sericulture uses a distinctive indigenous yellow silkworm (*krama sar* golden cocoon variety) producing a warm gold-toned raw thread quite different from the whiter Thai or Lao silk. It is the base fibre for nearly all high-status Khmer weavings.

***Hol*** — the signature Khmer weft *ikat*, in which weft threads are tied and dyed in stages before weaving. Cambodian *hol* is unusual in that it uses an uneven twill (three-warp/one-weft) that gives the surface a diagonal sheen and softens motif edges — a technical fingerprint that distinguishes it from the balanced-tabby ikats of neighboring Lao and Cham weavers.

***Sampot hol*** — the tubular or wrap skirt-cloth woven in *hol* technique, worn by women as everyday and ceremonial dress. Colour schemes cluster around five traditional grounds: red, yellow, green, blue and black, with motifs reserved in the natural silk.

***Sampot chorabap*** (also *sampot ​​chorbap*) — a supplementary-weft brocade woven with gold or silver-wrapped thread on silk, historically reserved for royalty and court dancers. The technique layers a discontinuous metallic weft over a plain-weave silk ground to produce dense, jewel-like figuring.

***Pidan*** (also *pedan*) — a pictorial *ikat* hanging, usually hung behind the Buddha image in a *wat* or over the ceremonial bed at a wedding. Unlike the geometric *hol* skirt-cloths, *pidan* narrate: scenes from the *Jātaka* tales, the life of the Buddha, Angkor-style temple towers, apsaras, and processions of elephants and monks, all rendered in reserved weft-ikat.

***Krama*** — the ubiquitous checked cotton scarf, worn by men and women as headwrap, sash, sling for carrying children, towel and sunshade. The gingham-like check in red-and-white or blue-and-white is a near-universal marker of Khmer daily identity, in Cambodia and among the diaspora alike.

**Khmer relief** — while primarily a stone-carving tradition (the bas-reliefs of Angkor Wat, the Bayon and Banteay Srei), the relief vocabulary — apsara friezes, lotus scrolls, *kala* faces, *nāga* balustrades — feeds directly back into textile motif design and remains a shared iconographic library across media.

**Motif vocabulary.** *phka* (flower rosettes), *phka chan* (jasmine star), *dangkeuv* (interlocking diamond lattice), *anlong* (undulating water-serpent line), *bombok* (small dot infill), *chan* (moon disc), *nāga* (multi-headed serpent), *hong* (celestial swan/hamsa), *apsara* (dancing celestial), *rup phnom* (temple-mountain silhouette), *reachasey* (mythical lion).

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress for both sexes centres on the wrapped lower garment. Women wear the *sampot*, a rectangular cloth wrapped and tucked at the waist, in plain cotton for daily wear and *hol* silk for temple and ceremonial occasions. The *sampot chang kben* — a longer cloth passed between the legs and tucked at the back — was historically unisex court dress and is still worn by both men and women for weddings and ordinations. Upper garments include the *av pak* (embroidered blouse) and, for formal occasions, the *sbai* or *phamuong*, a shoulder-cloth draped diagonally across the torso. The *krama* scarf functions as an all-purpose headwrap and neckcloth. Ceremonial court dress, preserved by the Royal Ballet, includes the tight-fitting *sampot sarabap* skirt, elaborate gilt-metal collars, epaulettes and the towering *mkot* crown of the classical dancer, modelled on the finials of Angkorian temple towers. Buddhist monks wear saffron or ochre robes in the standard Theravada three-robe pattern.

### Architecture

The traditional Khmer village house (*ptas khmae*) is a timber-framed structure raised on hardwood posts two to three metres above the ground, roofed with palm thatch or, increasingly, terracotta tiles. The under-house space shelters livestock, hosts the loom and provides shaded working room during the dry season, while the elevated living floor stays above monsoon floods. Roofs are steeply pitched, often with a distinctive gable finial (*andong reach*) evoking the curled *nāga* or the flame motif of temple roofs. Windows are shuttered rather than glazed, and interior partitions are minimal. In sharp contrast stands the sacred architecture: the temple-mountain (*prasat*) of the Angkorian tradition — Angkor Wat, the Bayon, Banteay Srei, Preah Vihear — built of laterite and sandstone, terraced to represent Mount Meru, and encrusted with the relief programmes noted above. The modern *wat* compound, present in every village, houses the *vihear* (ordination hall), *sala* (open pavilion for gatherings), monks' *kuti* quarters, and often a *chedi* stupa holding family ashes. Phnom Penh's colonial-era shophouses and the mid-twentieth-century "New Khmer Architecture" of Vann Molyvann represent urban developments layered on this vernacular base.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Cambodian ceramics have a long history: the **Kulen and Angkorian kilns** (9th–13th centuries) produced green-glazed and brown-glazed stonewares — pots, jars, roof tiles and zoomorphic vessels (owl jars, elephant jars) — that circulated across the Khmer Empire. The tradition largely lapsed after 1431 but survives in the utilitarian earthenware of villages like **Kampong Chhnang**, where women hand-build (without a wheel) round-bottomed cooking pots and water jars using a paddle-and-anvil technique. Metalwork centres on the silversmiths of **Kampong Luong**, who raise repoussé betel-boxes, offering bowls and animal-form containers (peacocks, elephants, tortoises) used in weddings and temple offerings. Everyday household objects include the *chang'e* palm-leaf mat, coconut-shell dippers, and lacquered bamboo food-carriers.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Khmer jewellery draws heavily on Angkorian relief vocabulary: gold *sangvar* pectoral collars, filigree earrings echoing temple finials, and enamelled bracelets. Bridal ornament for the *kar prapun* ceremony includes multiple layers of gilt necklaces, the *mkot* diadem for the bride and a matching cap for the groom, and gold ankle bells. Sacred tattooing — *sak yant* in the shared mainland Southeast Asian tradition, called *sak yoan* in Khmer — is applied by monks or *kru khmae* healers, using geometric *yantra* diagrams, Pali syllables in the old Khmer script (*aksar khom*), and figures of *hanuman*, tigers or *nāgas* to confer protection and invulnerability. Amulets (*katha*) of Buddha images, temple soil or inscribed metal foil are worn on cords around the neck or waist.

## Music & performance

Classical Khmer music divides into three principal ensembles. The ***pinpeat*** is the temple-and-court gong-chime ensemble, structurally cousin to the Thai *piphat* and Lao *pi phat* but with its own repertoire and tuning; it accompanies the Royal Ballet, shadow theatre and Buddhist ritual. Its core instruments are the *roneat ek* (high xylophone) and *roneat thung* (low xylophone), the circular gong-chimes *kong thom* and *kong toch*, the *sralai* quadruple-reed oboe, *sampho* barrel drum and *skor thom* pair of large drums. The ***mahori*** is the softer string-and-voice ensemble for entertainment, featuring the *tro* bowed spike fiddles (*tro sau*, *tro ou*), the *chapey dong veng* long-necked lute, the *khim* hammered dulcimer, and the *khloy* bamboo flute. The ***phleng kar*** wedding ensemble is a specific repertoire of some ninety pieces played across the multi-day ceremony. The *chapey dong veng* also anchors a solo storytelling genre in which a male singer improvises satirical and moralising verse to his own accompaniment — a tradition inscribed on UNESCO's Urgent Safeguarding list. Buddhist chant (*smot*) and the funerary *kantoam ming* ensemble round out the ritual sound-world.

## Dance & theatre

Cambodian classical dance — the **Royal Ballet of Cambodia** (UNESCO ICH, RL/00060) — is a codified court tradition of some 4,500 gestures, performed almost exclusively by women (male roles included), in gilt costume and *mkot* crowns. Its repertoire dramatises the *Reamker* (Khmer Ramayana) and Jātaka episodes. Folk dance (*robam bosabok*, *robam trot*, coconut and fishing dances) forms a lighter register performed at village festivals. **Sbek thom**, the large-hide **Khmer shadow theatre** (UNESCO ICH, RL/00108), uses two-metre pierced-leather panels — each holding an entire scene rather than a single articulated character — manipulated by dancers in front of a fire-lit screen while a *pinpeat* plays and a narrator chants the *Reamker*. Its smaller cousin *sbek touch* uses articulated puppets. **Lkhon Khol Wat Svay Andet** (UNESCO ICH, USL/01374) is an all-male masked dance-drama performed annually at Wat Svay Andet on the Mekong to petition the *neak ta* spirits for rain and a good harvest.

## Festivals & rituals

The Khmer festival year follows a lunisolar Buddhist calendar. ***Chol Chhnam Thmey*** (Khmer New Year) falls around 13–16 April, marking the end of the harvest and involving sand-*stupa* building at the *wat*, water-throwing, and the games *chol chhoung*, *leak kanseng* and *bos angkunh*. ***Visak Bochea*** in May commemorates the Buddha's birth, enlightenment and parinirvana. ***Pchum Ben***, the fifteen-day "Ancestor Day" festival in September–October, is the most emotionally central: families visit seven pagodas to offer rice-balls (*bay ben*) to the wandering spirits of ancestors released from the lower realms. ***Bon Om Touk***, the Water Festival in November, celebrates the reversal of the Tonle Sap's current with three days of pirogue racing on the river in Phnom Penh. ***Meak Bochea*** (February) and the ploughing ceremony ***Bon Chrat Preah Nongkoal***, opening the rice-planting season, round out the major observances. Life-cycle rites include the *phkar sla* betel-offering that opens a wedding, the multi-day *kar prapun* wedding with its *phleng kar* music and Preah Thong / Neang Neak reenactment, the *bonn kaset* ordination of young men as novice monks, and the *bon sop* funerary cremation. **Bokator** (UNESCO ICH, RL/01868), the Khmer battlefield martial art whose stances derive from animal forms and whose lineage the practitioners trace to Angkorian bas-reliefs, is displayed at festivals and now taught formally as heritage. Cambodia also participates in the multinational **Tugging rituals and games** (UNESCO ICH, RL/01080), the sitting tug-of-war performed for agricultural fertility.

## Foodways

Khmer cuisine is built on rice — long-grain jasmine varieties for daily meals, glutinous rice for sweets and ceremonies — accompanied by freshwater fish from the Tonle Sap and Mekong. The signature seasoning is ***prahok***, a fermented mudfish paste whose pungency underpins soups, dips (*prahok ktis*) and marinades, and is a hard identity marker distinguishing Khmer cooking from the fish-sauce-forward palate of the Thai and Vietnamese neighbours. The essential aromatic paste is ***kroeung***, pounded from lemongrass, galangal, *kaffir* lime leaf, turmeric, garlic, shallot and (in the red version) dried chilli. Signature dishes include ***amok trey*** (fish steamed in banana-leaf cups with *kroeung* and coconut cream, often considered the national dish), ***samlor korkor*** (a thick vegetable-and-toasted-rice-powder stew), ***samlor machu*** (sour tamarind soup), ***nom banh chok*** (rice noodles under a fish-and-*kroeung* gravy, the classic breakfast), ***kuy teav*** (rice-noodle soup), ***lok lak*** (stir-fried beef with a black-pepper–lime dip), and ***bai sach chrouk*** (grilled pork over broken rice). Grilled *tarantulas*, crickets and *a-ping* are Kampong Cham snack specialities. Palm sugar and palm wine (*tuk thnaot*) are drawn from the sugar palm that punctuates the rice-paddy landscape. Ceremonial food centres on offerings of rice-balls, sticky-rice cakes in banana leaf (*num ansom chek*, *num ansom chruk*) prepared for Pchum Ben and New Year, and the boiled sweet *num kom*. Weddings feature *ka tieu* noodle courses and whole roast pig.

## Oral tradition & literature

The pre-eminent narrative is the ***Reamker***, the Khmer recension of the *Rāmāyaṇa*, whose episodes furnish the plots of classical dance, shadow theatre and temple relief alike — the Angkor Wat outer galleries carry a nearly complete Reamker cycle in stone. The **Jātaka tales** of the Buddha's previous lives are the second great narrative pool, painted on *wat* walls and recited by monks. Indigenous folktale cycles include the trickster stories of **Judge Rabbit** (*Sophea Tonsai*), a common set of moral fables, and the origin myth of **Preah Thong and Neang Neak** — the merchant-prince and the *nāga* princess whose union founded the Khmer people, reenacted in every wedding when the groom holds the hem of the bride's *sbai*. The *chbap* — didactic verse codes (*Chbap Srey* for women, *Chbap Pros* for men, *Chbap Kerti Kal*) — were memorised in temple schools until the mid-twentieth century and remain part of the literary canon, though *Chbap Srey* is now contested for its patriarchal precepts. Verse epics such as *Tum Teav*, a tragic love story dating to the 17th–19th centuries, are considered the national literary work. Improvised sung poetry by *chapey dong veng* masters like the late Kong Nay carries the oral tradition into the present, and post-Khmer-Rouge institutions such as Cambodian Living Arts have worked to reconnect surviving master artists with new students after the catastrophic losses of 1975–79.

## Language & religion

Khmer belongs to the Mon-Khmer branch of the Austroasiatic family, with major dialects: Central (Phnom Penh and the plain, the standard), Northern (*Khmer Surin* of Thailand), Southern (*Khmer Krom* of the Mekong Delta), and the little-studied Western or Cardamom Khmer, which uniquely preserves a register-vowel system otherwise lost in the modern language. The script, developed from Pallava Grantha by the 7th century, is one of the oldest continuously used writing systems in Southeast Asia; the older sacred variant *aksar khom* is used for Pali texts and *yantra* tattoos. Theravada Buddhism, adopted broadly from the 13th century after earlier Hindu and Mahayana strata, is the dominant religion, but Khmer practice syncretises it with indigenous ancestor and land-spirit worship: *neak ta* (guardian spirits of place), *meba* (ancestors), *mneang phteah* (house guardians) and *mrenh kongveal* (little red-clad guardian spirits) all receive regular offerings alongside the Buddha, and astrologers (*hao-ra*, *kru teay*) and healers (*kru khmae*) mediate the supernatural world.
