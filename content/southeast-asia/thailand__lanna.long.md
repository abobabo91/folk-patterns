## Overview

The Lanna, who call themselves *Khon Mueang* — "people of the (cultivated) land" — are a Tai people of nine provinces in northern Thailand, in the territory of the former Lanna Kingdom. Their oldest documented settlement core lies in the basin of the Kok and Ing rivers in what is now Chiang Rai province, and because they lived by wet-rice cultivation they took the river plains rather than the mountain ranges that cover three quarters of the north. There are roughly six million of them, with a smaller community of 29,442 (2005 census) across the border in Bokeo Province, Laos. Their language, *Kam Mueang*, belongs to the Southwestern branch of the Tai languages within the Kra–Dai family. In folk-culture terms they matter as a people whose identity was historically defined by way of life rather than descent, and whose script, dress, dance and cuisine survived a century of state-driven assimilation into a single Thai nation.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The museum objects gathered here are overwhelmingly cotton cloth, and they show one dominant decorative technique.

**Supplementary-weft patterning (*khit*)** — Bands of geometric ornament worked in a supplementary weft across the ends of otherwise plain tabby cloth. The ground is usually natural cream cotton, sometimes apricot-coloured, woven with paired warps and wefts; the pattern yarns are stranded cotton in red, black, purple and white. Band depths in the collection range from about 4.5 cm to 22 cm, so the same technique carries anything from a narrow accent to a broad panel.

***Pha lop*** — A bedsheet made of two equal pieces of cream tabby cotton joined together, with the patterned bands at one end only. Warp ends are secured with overhand knotting, and looser overhand knots group the warps into macramé tassels.

**Striped and warp-*ikat* cloth** — Skirts in the collection are striped tubes, one with a black band at the top and a white band at the bottom. A long unused length of *ikat* tabby cotton, intended for a skirt, has a 15.5 cm dark blue border along one selvedge and eight composite polychrome stripes with a turquoise stripe at the centre, each separated by a wider light-and-dark blue *ikat* stripe.

**Shoulder cloths** — Long cloths patterned identically at both ends, the centre field left plain or carrying only scattered white dotted patterning. One example's warp ends are left raw, another's are finished with an overhand knot; both are unworn.

**Textured striped blankets** — A blanket of two joined cream cotton pieces carries eight red stripes, each made of three fine stripes, with extended tabby patterning that raises the surface of the cloth.

**Motif vocabulary.** Geometric bands of varying depth; a line of censers divided by a bar; black bird motifs, some with red feet; confronted *naga* (snakes) with their bodies crossed; black stylized floral motifs; and a band resembling a row of red columns and arches.

### Clothing & dress

The garments in the collection are plain in cut and modest in number: a woman's tube skirt of striped cotton, a bodice of cotton with metal studs, men's cotton trousers, and shoulder bags of cotton cloth. The shoulder cloth — one example identified as a man's — is the one garment with sustained decoration, patterned at both ends and left undecorated across the body of the cloth. Historically, Lanna men tattooed themselves above the hips, on the abdomen (*phung*), a custom so distinctive that the Siamese called them *Lao phung dam*, "black-bellied Lao", to separate them from the Lao to the east who did not practise it. Since the mid-1990s revival, Lanna women, mainly of the middle and upper classes, have worn the classic dresses of the north again on special occasions, made of hand-made cotton; in many public institutions and government agencies it is customary to wear clothes made of traditional textiles on Fridays. The sources do not describe head coverings, belts or footwear.

### Architecture

The sources cover the vernacular house only indirectly. What they do document is the temple, through a model of a temple building held in the collection: walls of wood, roofs of woven rattan, the roofs lacquered in red and black with gilding, possibly gold. The model has three roof levels, each with ornamental cresting ending in flame shapes, and the gables are ornamented in red and gold — a stacked, tiered roof profile with decorated ridge and gable as the defining features. Chiang Mai is historically one of the places where Lanna sacred art developed most, with ancient temples and Buddha sculptures. Beyond the temple, the settlement pattern itself is the architectural fact the sources insist on: the Lanna built in the alluvial plains, river valleys and plateaus, organised into local communities called *muang*, and did not build in the wooded mountains, which belonged to peoples who practised slash-and-burn agriculture. Private gardens commonly hold a spirit house, stocked daily with votive offerings.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Silver is the metal the collection documents. Two embossed goblets survive, one hexagonal and part of a betel nut set, with a floral band around the rim and six panels below carrying mythical, animal and human figures — the clearest of them a prince armed with a bow and arrow, alongside a demon, a monkey and a lion king. The second goblet has a band of decoration at the top and four panels below, each with a different fantastical animal figure. The rest of the holdings are the equipment of cotton cloth production and household life: a wooden and metal stand for reeling cotton, carved in the shape of a cat; a basketry basket for bobbins; a wooden cotton-gin; and a sleeping mat of vegetable fibre. A prayer flag combines wood, thread, reed, pith, bamboo, cloth and metal.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources record no jewelry. The one documented body practice is the male abdominal tattoo described above. Tattoos and amulets written in the *Tai Tham* script are held to possess particular powers, and the script itself is believed by the Tai Yuan to have divine powers.

## Music & performance

The sources establish that the Lanna have a music tradition distinct from that of central Thailand and that it is performed publicly today — there are regular performances of Lanna music and dance alongside demonstrations of traditional handicrafts, part of the cultural revival that gathered around the 700th anniversary of Chiang Mai in 1996 — but they name no instruments, song genres or performance occasions. Andrew C. Shahriari's *Khon Muang Music and Dance Traditions in Northern Thailand* (2007) is the study the sources point to for this material.

## Dance & theatre

The Tai Yuan have their own dance tradition, named as such in the sources and set explicitly against that of central Thailand, and it is performed regularly in Chiang Mai as part of the post-1990s revival. The sources do not name individual dances, mask or puppet forms, or distinguish ceremonial from entertainment repertoire.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do not describe an annual festival calendar or life-cycle rites. What they do document is the ritual landscape in which such observances sit. Small sanctuaries dedicated to animist belief are scattered throughout Lanna territory and are still frequented by devotees who ask the spirits for protection, and many private gardens have a spirit house stocked daily with votive offerings. The most widespread cult among the Tai Yuan is that of the spirit *Chao Luang Kham Daeng*, transmitted through two competing legends. In the first he is a human sent by the god Indra to become king and teach Buddhist precepts to his subjects; he founded the city, ruled it, and on his death was placed by the citizens of Chiang Mai at the head of the city's protective spirits. In the second he is the lord of the ogres who guard the treasure of the sacred cave of Chiang Dao — a version thought to descend from the tradition of the Lawa, who had settled the Chiang Mai area before the Tai Yuan arrived. One further ritual object appears in the collection: a prayer flag assembled from wood, thread, reed, pith, bamboo, cloth and metal.

## Foodways

The Lanna have a cuisine very different from that of central Thailand — the sources state this plainly and in the same breath as the separate dance tradition and the surviving language, as one of the markers by which Lanna identity outlasted the Thaification policies. Beyond that, the material basis: the Lanna were wet-rice cultivators who settled the alluvial plains, river valleys and plateaus precisely because paddy could be grown there, and this is what separated them in their own reckoning from the mountain peoples who practised slash-and-burn agriculture. The sources name no individual dishes, cooking methods, ceremonial foods or dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

The first decades of the sixteenth century are considered the high point of Lanna literature, though the classical works of the period were written not in the language of the Tai Yuan but in Pali, the scholarly language. An earlier flowering came in the fifteenth century under King Tilokkarat, which produced a profusion of palm-leaf manuscripts in the *Tai Tham* script, in vernacular Lanna interspersed with Pali and Buddhist Indic vocabulary. Many old sermon manuscripts remain in active use in Buddhist temples, and the script is still taught to Lao Buddhist monks. There is no active production of literature in the traditional alphabet today; when Lanna is written, the standard Thai script is used. A shared Tai legend tells of the possibly mythical king *Khun Borom Rachathiriat* of Mueang Then, whose several sons settled and ruled city-states across Southeast Asia and southern China. Since the mid-1990s, scholars at Chiang Mai University have worked on researching traditions and cultivating cultural heritage, and signs lettered in the Lanna script are again being put up in some places.

## Language & religion

*Kam Mueang* is a Southwestern Tai language of the Kra–Dai family, classified in the Chiang Saen branch alongside Thai and Southern Thai, though the grouping is contested by scholars who argue it reflects the politics of Thaification rather than linguistic history and masks closer historical ties to the Lao-Phutai branch. Thanajirawat (2018) distinguishes five dialect groups by tone split and merger. The traditional script, *Tai Tham* or *tua mueang*, was adapted from the Old Mon script of Hariphunchai; it was driven out of schools in favour of the Thai alphabet, which cannot transcribe all six Lanna tones. The Lanna adopted Theravada Buddhism from the Mon and have practised it for centuries, retaining animist practice alongside it in the form of spirit sanctuaries, household spirit houses and the cult of *Chao Luang Kham Daeng*.
