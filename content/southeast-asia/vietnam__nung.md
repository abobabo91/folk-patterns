## Overview

The Nùng are a Central Tai-speaking ethnic group living primarily in northeastern Vietnam and southwestern Guangxi. In Vietnam, they are concentrated especially in Cao Bằng and Lạng Sơn provinces, while Nùng is also spoken in China and Laos. The Nùng population numbered about 856,412 in 1999, 968,800 in 2009, and 1,083,298 in 2019. They are closely related to the Tày and the Zhuang; in China, the Nùng and Tày are classified as Zhuang people. Their communities include several named subgroups, whose names often correspond to geographic regions and migration histories. The sources document Nùng language varieties, historical migration, relations with neighboring Tai peoples, political history, temples, festivals, and changing cultural policies.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that the Nùng, Tày, and Zhuang have very similar customs and dress and are known as the Rau peoples. A historical description of the Guangyuan zhou Man-barbarian Nong clan says that people wore their hair long and fastened their clothes on the left.

### Architecture

The sources document temples dedicated to Nùng Trí Cao, including Kỳ Sầm Temple on the western outskirts of Cao Bằng. They state that five temples dedicated to Nùng Trí Cao remained active into the 20th century and that Kỳ Sầm Temple was renovated sometime before 2001. Its exterior and interior pillars were retouched, while Chinese-character inscriptions and Quốc ngữ inscriptions were removed.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources mention metal-tipped bamboo javelins, long shields, heavy swords, axes, crossbows, gold, silver, tame elephants, and the mineral wealth associated with Nùng Trí Cao’s territories.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They state only that a historical description of the Guangyuan zhou Man-barbarian Nong clan says that people wore their hair long.

## Music & performance

The sources state that many Zhuang songs refer to Nùng Zhigao as “King Nong.” They also mention festivals held around Kỳ Sầm Temple, including a festival on the tenth day of the first lunar month and another trade-focused festival in the third lunar month during Thanh Minh (Qingming Festival).

## Festivals & rituals

Worship of Nùng Trí Cao was widespread by the 19th century. In 1897, local leaders arranged the renovation of Kỳ Sầm Temple in conjunction with the Nùng clan. A festival was held around the temple on the tenth day of the first lunar month. The sources state that “Han Chinese” from the Qing dynasty came to the region during this festival, so that Quảng Nguyên “resembled nothing more than another region of China.” A second festival focused on trade was held around the temple in the third lunar month during Thanh Minh (Qingming Festival).

The sources also document the religious and political importance of Nùng Trí Cao. As the strongest thổ ty, Nùng Trí Cao and his family members were deified by local communities. A shrine to Nùng Trí Cao, the Great King of Kỳ Sầm Temple, was reportedly erected in the western outskirts of Cao Bằng.

## Foodways

The sources state that Nùng communities cultivated wet rice and that Nùng migrants transformed poor lands through this cultivation. They also describe the mountainous areas of Việt Bắc, where interaction with Viets included trade with Các Lái, Kinh merchants who held government licenses for trade in the uplands. The sources mention gold and cinnabar as products of the territory associated with the Nong clan.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that many Zhuang songs refer to Nùng Zhigao as “King Nong.” They also record popular accounts claiming that Zhigao fled further south into modern northern Thailand, where his descendants thrive, while other groups identify themselves as descended from him. The Zhuang of Wenshan Zhuang and Miao Autonomous Prefecture identify as survivors of Zhigao’s rebel movement, and groups in Dali City, Xishuangbana, and northern Thailand claim descent from him.

## Language & religion

Nùng is a Central Tai language spoken mostly in Cao Bằng and Lạng Sơn provinces in Vietnam and also in China and Laos. It is also known as Nong, Tai Nùng, Tay, and Tày Nùng. The language includes several varieties, including Nùng Phạn Slinh, Nùng Cháo, Nùng Inh, Nùng An, Nùng Giang, and Nùng Vên. Nùng has six tones. Its speakers in China are classified as Zhuang.

The sources state that Nùng communities historically deified Nùng Trí Cao and maintained temples dedicated to him. Worshipers at Kỳ Sầm Temple preferred Chinese, although a Romanized script had been created for the Nùng language.
