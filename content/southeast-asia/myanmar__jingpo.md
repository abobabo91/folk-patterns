## Overview

The Jingpo are a Tibeto-Burman ethnic group living mainly in northern Myanmar’s Kachin State, the Kachin Hills, northeastern India, and southwestern China. Their largest concentration is in Kachin State and the neighbouring Dehong Dai and Jingpo Autonomous Prefecture of China. An estimated 630,000 Jingpo people live in Kachin State, while China recorded 147,828 in the 2010 census; at least 7,900 Singpho live in India. The Jingpo language belongs to the Tibeto-Burman branch of the Sino-Tibetan family and functions as a lingua franca among the Kachin peoples. Jingpo culture is documented through its distinctive dwellings, dress, tea production, animist practices, migration history, and language varieties.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Women often wear black jackets with silver decorations and wool skirts made in bright colors. During the festival known as *manau*, women dress in black jackets with silver decorations and wear wool skirts made in bright red colors. Women gather their hair into a broad knot on the crown of the head, fastening it with silver bodkins, chains, and tassels; maidens tie their tresses into a roll above the nape.

Men often wear white and black pants. Another description records a white shirt with a colorful longyi. Men cover their heads with turbans: youths wear white turbans and adults black turbans. Men also often tie their hair in a large knot on the crown of the head.

### Architecture

Traditional Jingpo dwellings are usually two stories and built from wood and bamboo. The houses are oval in form. The first floor serves as storage and stable space, while the second is used as living quarters. The same arrangement is described for Singpho dwellings: the lower floor is used for storage and as a stable, and the upper floor for living quarters.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do record shields made from buffalo hide, sometimes as long as four feet, and helmets made from buffalo hide or rattan-work. These helmets are described as black and decorated with boar’s tusks. The sources also describe bamboo technology in tea production: tea leaves are placed in the hollow tube of bamboo, which is exposed to fire smoke so that the tea can be kept for years without losing its flavor.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Women’s hair is fastened with silver bodkins, chains, and tassels. Women’s black jackets may have silver decorations, particularly during *manau*. The sources also describe women’s broad hair knots and maidens’ rolled tresses above the nape. No tattoos, henna practices, named jewelry types, or ritual functions of adornment are documented.

## Music & performance

They record that Taiwan’s Jingpo community hosts performances every October in which participants dance in traditional costumes.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention performances held every October by the Jingpo community in Taiwan, where participants dance in traditional costumes.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources identify *manau* as a festival during which women wear black jackets with silver decorations and bright red wool skirts. No date or annual calendar for *manau* is provided.

Before the arrival of Buddhism and Christianity, animism was widely followed. Jingpo animists believe that spirits are present everywhere, including in the sun and animals, and that these spirits bring good or bad luck. All living creatures are believed to have souls. Rituals are carried out for protection in almost all daily activities, including planting crops and warfare.

The sources also describe the contemporary religious distribution in Myanmar: around 90% of Jingpo people follow Christianity, while the remaining 10% are animists or Theravada Buddhists. The majority of Singphos in India follow Theravāda Buddhism.

## Foodways

Tea is widely planted among the Jingpo and Singpho. The Singpho produce tea by plucking tender leaves, drying them in the sun, and exposing them to night dew for three days and nights. The leaves are then placed in a hollow bamboo tube and exposed to the smoke of a fire. This method allows the tea to be kept for years without losing its flavor.

The Singpho depended on yams and other edible tubers as staple food. The sources state that the Singpho tribe and the Khamti tribe had been consuming tea since the 12th century, although they also state that there is no substantial documentation for the history of tea drinking in the Indian subcontinent during the pre-colonial period.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources mention a flood myth in which ancestors survived in a large drum; one theory connects the ethnonym Jinghpaw with *chyinghpaw*, translated as “open drum.” They also record that early ethnographic documents proposed that the name derived from the Tibetan term *sin-po*, alleging that the Kachin were cannibals.

They mention collections of Kachin folktales told in Jinghpaw and Kachin culture and history told in Jinghpaw, both associated with Keita Kurabe and held at PARADISEC. No contemporary literary revival is described.

## Language & religion

Jingpo is a Tibeto-Burman language of the Sino-Tibetan family and is spoken primarily in Kachin State, northeastern India, and Yunnan, China. Jingpo proper is spoken by 1,500,000 people in Burma and 150,000 people in China in the cited source. The Jingpo language is used as a lingua franca among the Kachin peoples. The Jingpo in India speak the Singpho dialect.

Jingpo is written with a modified Latin alphabet. A Burmese alphabet is also used by some speakers but has largely been phased out. The Latin-based writing system consists of 23 letters and was originally created by American Baptist missionaries in the late 19th century. Ola Hanson arrived in Myanmar in 1890, learned the language, and wrote the first Kachin–English dictionary.

Jingpo has four tones in open syllables and two tones in closed syllables. Other languages spoken among Jingpo include Burmese, Standard Chinese, Assamese, and Shan. Religious practice includes Christianity, Theravada Buddhism, and animism, with spirit beliefs and protective rituals connected to everyday activities.
