## Overview

The Asmat are a Papuan people of the southwestern coast of New Guinea, in Indonesia's South Papua province, occupying roughly 18,000 km² of mangrove, tidal swamp, freshwater swamp and lowland rainforest along the Arafura Sea, both within and adjacent to Lorentz National Park. The population was estimated at around 110,000 as of 2020. "Asmat" names both the people and the region, and is an umbrella term for twelve sub-groups — Joirat, Emari Ducur, Bismam, Becembub, Simai, Kenekap/Kaimo, Unir Siran/Keenok, Unir Epmak/Tomor, Safan, Aramatak, Bras/Brazza and Yupmakcain — speaking five or more dialects of the Asmat language family, a sub-family of Trans–New Guinea. The Asmat have an extensive woodcarving tradition, designed to honour ancestors; it is collected by the world's museums, most notably in the Michael C. Rockefeller Collection at the Metropolitan Museum of Art and in the Tropenmuseum in Amsterdam.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

There is no woven-cloth tradition. The fibre arts are plaiting, netting and knotting, and their great work is the *jipae*, a full-body mask costume of rattan and plant fibre: a netted or plaited body covering with a fringe skirt, painted wooden eye plates, and seed, shell and feather decoration, worn during the jipae ritual by men representing the recently deceased. Plaited fibre bags carry painted panels — geometric, curvilinear and shield-motif designs, sometimes a human figure — trimmed with coix seed and feather tassels, and fibre belts are densely worked with seeds and fringed.

### Woodcarving

Wood is the main Asmat medium, carved in openwork and relief and painted in red, white and black. The best known form is the *bisj* (also *mbis* or *bis*) pole: human figures representing deceased ancestors are stacked above a canoe-shaped base, and the top carries an openwork projecting "wing", the *cemen*. The canoe prow symbolises a metaphorical boat taking the spirits of the dead away, and the canoe-shaped base may also refer to the *ci*, the dugout canoe central to daily life in a swampy land. Bisj poles were carved by religious carvers (*wow-ipits*) after a member of the community had been killed and headhunted. Tall war shields are carved with relief human figures and curvilinear motifs and painted with ochre, white and black. Canoe prow ornaments are openwork scrollwork highlighted in white; ancestor figures, figurative staffs and carved panels make up the rest of the sculpture.

### Clothing & dress

Ceremonial dress was built from feather, fibre, seed, shell and bone. Museum records hold cassowary-feather headdresses knotted onto plant-fibre cords and fur-strip head ornaments worked with seed beads, and early documentary photographs show men wearing nose ornaments.

### Architecture

Because much of the land floods daily, Asmat dwellings, *jew*, have typically been built two or more metres above the ground, raised on wooden posts. In some inland regions the Asmat have lived in tree houses, sometimes as high as 25 metres from the ground. Materials for canoes, dwellings and woodcarvings are all gathered locally, so culture and biodiversity are intertwined.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Everyday objects are wood, bamboo, bone and fibre. Museum records hold carved and painted paddles, some with a human figure near the handle; cassowary-bone daggers decorated with feather and seed tufts; wooden spears with carved barbs, some tipped with cassowary claw; sago pounders with a bamboo blade and a carved wooden handle depicting ancestors, used by women to extract sago pith; carved wooden bowls with human-figure finials and openwork figures; and bamboo combs with carved figurative handles.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment is shell, bone, dog teeth, coix seed and feather: carved bone nose ornaments and shell nose ornaments with spiral scrollwork, necklaces of dog teeth on plant-fibre cord, long coiled strands of coix seed, and armlets.

## Music & performance

Museum records hold two instrument types. The hourglass drum is carved from wood, with a lizard-skin membrane bound by rattan and an integral handle carved in openwork with human, animal or bird figures. Trumpets are bamboo, incised with spiral, scroll or linear designs, some infilled with white lime.

## Festivals & rituals

Headhunting raids were an important element of Asmat culture until missionaries suppressed the practice, which according to some accounts persisted into the 1990s. The death of an adult, even by disease, was believed to be caused by an enemy, and relatives sought to take a head in an endless cycle of revenge and propitiation of ancestors. Heads were thought necessary for the rituals in which boys were initiated into manhood, and cannibalism was a subsidiary feature of the rituals that followed. Bisj poles were erected to satisfy the spirits of the dead and send them to the afterlife, *Safan*, across the sea. After a period under the new Indonesian administration from 1964 to 1968 in which ceremonies were officially discouraged, Bishop Alphonse Sowada facilitated the revitalisation of woodcarving and festivals, and shield and bisj ceremonies were incorporated into an adapted Catholic liturgy. Sowada founded the Asmat Museum of Culture and Progress in Agats in 1973 and, in 1981, an annual woodcarving competition and auction held in Agats in early October.

Family life included polygamy, and a man was often expected to marry a male relative's widow so that she and her children kept protection and support. Tobias Schneebaum reported long-term ritual partnerships between men (*mbai*), in which partners also shared wives (*papitsj*); their prevalence has been disputed, and missionary influence has probably reduced both.

## Foodways

The Asmat mainly subsist on starch from the sago palm (*Metroxylon sagu*), supplemented by grubs of the sago beetle (*Rhynchophorus bilineatus*), crustaceans, fish, forest game and other items gathered from their forests and waters.

## Language & religion

The Asmat languages are a group of closely related languages or dialects, most mutually intelligible to some degree — Casuarina Coast Asmat, Yaosakor Asmat, Central Asmat, North Asmat, Tamnim and Citak — forming the Asmat family within the Trans–New Guinea phylum. The neighbouring Kamoro and Sempan speak languages of the same family but are ethnically distinct. The Citak (Yupmakcain and Brazza) call themselves *Kau*, consider themselves a separate people, and are matrilineal. The Asmat have traditionally placed great emphasis on the veneration of ancestors, particularly accomplished warriors.

The first European contact was with the Dutch in 1623, but the Asmat remained almost entirely isolated until the 1950s. A colonial post opened in Agats in 1938 and closed in 1942; the Dutch missionary Father G. Zegwaard re-established it in 1953 as the base for Roman Catholic missionaries. In 2000 the Asmat formed Lembaga Musyawarah Adat Asmat (LMAA), and outside logging and fishing have met significant resistance. In 2022 Apolo Safanpo, a native Asmat, became the first governor of the new South Papua province.
