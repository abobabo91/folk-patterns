## Overview

The Ma'anyan, also called Dayak Maanyan, Eastern Barito Dayak, or Dusun Ma'anyan, are an Indigenous Dayak people of Borneo. They inhabit the eastern side of Central Kalimantan, especially East Barito Regency and parts of South Barito Regency, and also live in northern South Kalimantan, particularly Tabalong Regency. The Dayak Warukin people belong to the Ma'anyan group, as do the Dayak Balangan and Dayak Samihim groups. The Ma'anyan language is an Austronesian language of the eastern branch of the Barito language family. According to the 2003 census, it was spoken by about 150,000 Ma'anyan Dayaks. Agriculture, elaborate funeral ceremonies, shamanic treatment of disease, oral literature, songs, dance, and distinctive foodways are among the cultural subjects documented in the sources.

## Music & performance

The Ma'anyan traditional folk songs named in the source are *Tumpi Wayu*, *Tataku Balinga*, *Miheput*, and *Ngano*. The sources identify these as traditional folk songs. No musical instruments or song genres beyond these names are documented.

## Dance & theatre

The named Ma'anyan traditional dance is *Tari Giring-Giring*. No dramatic, theatrical, mask, puppet, or shadow-puppet tradition is documented.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe elaborate funeral ceremonies as a characteristic of the Dusun Ma'anyan people. Among the Dayak Warukin people, the funeral ceremony associated with the Kaharingan religion is called *mambatur*; among the Ma'anyan Banua Lima people, it is called *marabia*. The Ma'anyan people are also associated in the source with *Aruh Buntang*, identified as a traditional ceremony, but its date, season, purpose, and sequence are not explained.

The historical oral accounts of the Ma'anyan describe the attacks on the Nansarunai kingdom as *Nansarunai Usak Jawa*, meaning “the destruction of the Nansarunai by the Javanese.” The independent state of Nansarunai was established before the 12th century, and the kingdom declined and fell by the year 1389 after two major attacks from Majapahit forces. The sources connect these events with the migration of Ma'anyan groups toward the Central and South Borneo region.

## Foodways

The sources name *Bagamat*, described as giant bat meat gravy cooked with garlic and various vegetables. They also mention *Kalumpe* or *Karuang*, identified as a pounded cassava-leaf salad mixed with eggplant, lemongrass, onion, and garlic. The source associates Kalumpe or Karuang with the Ngaju people rather than presenting it exclusively as Ma'anyan.

No other staple grains, cooking vessels, preparation methods, ceremonial foods, beverages, dietary rules, or food-related festivals are documented.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Ma'anyan sources preserve oral accounts concerning the Nansarunai kingdom and its destruction by Majapahit forces. In these accounts, the attacks are called *Nansarunai Usak Jawa*. The sources also state that, after the Nan Sarunai kingdom was attacked, the Ma'anyan people broke up into several sub-groups. These include Ma'anyan Paku; Ma'anyan Paju Epat/Ampat or Ma'anyan Siong; Ma'anyan Dayu Lasi Muda; Ma'anyan Paju Sapuluh of Kampung Sapuluh; Ma'anyan Banua Lima or Paju Dime; Ma'anyan Warukin; and Ma'anyan Jangkung.

## Language & religion

Ma'anyan, also written Maanyan, Ma'anyan Barito, Maanyan Barito, Ma'anyan Dayak, or Maanyan Dayak, is an Austronesian language belonging to the eastern branch of the Barito language family. It is closely related to Malagasy, although the two languages are not highly mutually intelligible because of centuries-long separation. The source records high lexical similarity with Paku Dayak and Witu Dusun Dayak. Ma'anyan shares many similarities with languages in Madagascar and contains numerous Malay and Javanese loanwords.

The Ma'anyan organization called *Dusmala* combines the Dusun, Ma'anyan, and Lawangan sub-groups. The sources associate the Dayak Warukin funeral ceremony *mambatur* with the Kaharingan religion. They also describe Ma'anyan shamanic treatment of disease. No historical script, complete religious landscape, or broader account of Ma'anyan religious practice is provided.
