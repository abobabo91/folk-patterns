## Overview

The Kelabit are an indigenous Dayak people of the highlands straddling the Sarawak–North Kalimantan border on Borneo, with a minority in neighbouring Brunei, and with close ties to the Lun Bawang. Their homeland around Bario lies slightly over 1,200 metres above sea level; until recently rapids made the rivers useless as roads and the logging tracks stayed far off, so the highlands remained little touched from outside. A marked overland route now links Miri to Bario, though the drive takes over eleven hours through logging-trail junctions and river crossings. With roughly 6,600 people (2013) the Kelabit are the smallest ethnic group in Sarawak; migration to towns over the last two decades has left an estimated 1,200 in the highlands, living in inherited longhouses and farming rice. Their language belongs to the North Bornean branch of Western Malayo-Polynesian.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here document no loom-weaving tradition. What they do record is pattern made by plaiting, binding and beading — grass, rattan, bamboo and bark worked into named forms, several of them collected by the British Museum with their Kelabit names intact.

**Liling** — a lining mat plaited from coarse *berpah* grass, laid down as a floor or bedding layer inside the longhouse. A heavier variant is recorded simply as a mat of coarse *berpah*, *ugam berpah*.

**Bekang dechur** — a carrying basket worn from a headstrap, *sengoloh*. The back is closely woven rattan; the front and its bottom-hinged flap are built from whole rattan lengths twisted and intertwined into a pattern inside a frame of thicker rattan, over a coiled and intertwined base. Materials are *rabun* rattan, bark string from the *kilid* tree and a headstrap of *tamar* grass.

**Kal belad** — literally "flat style", the rattan binding pattern used to lash a knife blade into its wooden handle; it is named on a small long-handled knife, *io tonan narit*, where tree latex and beeswax fix the blade and the rattan holds the wrapping.

**Sun hats of ilad palm leaf** — broad brimmed hats built on a frame of *kusah* rattan, patterned with concentric circles, radiating lines, zigzags and pompoms of coloured thread, or with a beaded centre; some carry lettering worked into the surface. Inner caps are made variously of *poran* bamboo, woven blue cotton cloth (*oloh*) or *berpah* grass.

**Beaded bamboo hats** — finely split bamboo decorated with beads and cloth in abstract patterns, stars and stripes, made in pairs.

**Motif vocabulary.** Concentric circles, radiating lines, zigzags, stars and stripes, and abstract beadwork fields on hats; parallel lines around the lip and v-shaped lines around the rim on pots; incised lines and squiggles on unglazed cooking pots.

### Clothing & dress

In traditional dress men wore loincloths and women a skirt of Tapa cloth, the material taken from the inner bark of a tree; Westernized clothing is now preferred by many. Headgear is the most elaborately worked part of the costume in the museum record: brimmed sun hats of *ilad* palm leaf on rattan frames, finished with coloured thread pompoms, beadwork or applied lettering and fitted with a hook for hanging, and paired bamboo hats decorated with beads, sequins, metal, wool and velvet. Beaded headdresses are worn only by women, while necklaces are worn by both sexes. One jacket in the collection is made of bear and gibbon skin. Beadwork more generally — sixty-nine items of it are recorded on necklaces, hats and belts — signals prestige, as Chinese porcelain vases do. The customs of tattooing and of dulling the teeth have become less common.

### Architecture

A Kelabit village usually consists of a single longhouse in which the whole community lives. These structures reach up to 75 metres in length and are not divided internally into rooms. They are inherited rather than newly built for each household, and the tightly knit community that occupies one is also the unit that farms together. Before conversion to Christianity the Kelabit marked the landscape outside the longhouse as well: megaliths were erected and ditches dug in honour of notable individuals, a practice that ended as the community Christianized.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Two pot forms recur in the collections. *Kudin* are large round-bottomed earthenware jars glazed with *damar* resin, called *natang*, sometimes ruled with parallel lines around the lip and blackened by use. *Tuning* are round-bottomed cooking pots, either resin-glazed or left unglazed and apparently unfired, decorated with incised lines and squiggles or with v-shaped lines around the rim. Blacksmithing is a Kelabit craft — one glass negative shows four men working a forge with stone hammers — and the weapons reflect it: a spear called *boso*, its shaft of local hardwood bound with rattan to a metal head; knives with rattan-lashed handles; a large knife-sheath, *binan tongol*, of *labakan* wood with a smaller sheath (*labuh io*) for a second blade attached, its casing made from the bark of the *reman belabo* plant. Household wood and rattan work includes a low stool, *laan*, carved from one piece of *belaban* wood, a wooden cooking implement (*bogo*), and a winnowing tray, *reno*, of rattan and bamboo sealed with sap and left open at one end. Kelabit stone adzes are quadrangular in form, unlike the round axe types recorded elsewhere in Southeast Asia.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Men's ear-rings, *abe tawak*, are made from a tube of brass (*temaga*) bent into a ring with its overlapping ends left slightly apart. Bangles are cut from shell, *akap*. Necklaces combine fibre with black, white, yellow and red glass beads, and beadwork extends to hats and belts; the ancient prestige goods a leader is expected to hold are Chinese porcelain vases, beads and gongs, along with pearl objects. Necklaces were worn by men and women alike, headdresses only by women. Tattooing is documented photographically rather than textually: glass negatives in the British Museum show the tattooed legs of a Kelabit woman, and three Kelabit women seated on a mat with two of them extending their legs to display the same work. Precious stones interest the Kelabit, and before conversion they kept stone amulets. Teeth were formerly dulled.

## Music & performance

The instruments collected from the Kelabit are almost all made of bamboo, cut so that the sounding parts come out of the tube itself. *Pagang* is a section of *telang* bamboo with two strings carved from its own wall, propped away from the body by small bamboo bridges, with a hole between them covered by a further piece of bamboo fixed to the strings. *Lotong* uses a wide-bore *betong* bamboo section with both end divisions left closed and a slit cut down one side — sometimes decoratively shaped at the middle — with two strings raised from the bamboo on each side of the slit; one example has a dried leaf left inside the body and bark string from the *kilid* tree binding both ends. *Selingut* is a flute of one *telang* section, made either with three triangular stopping holes and a detachable end piece or with six stopping holes and incised, blackened ornament around the blowing end. *Kelingut* is a nose flute of the same bamboo, with three triangular stops and the node left whole except for the blowing hole. *Tobong* is a tubular bamboo gong with a wide slit in one side and a rattan handle attached, struck with a separate beater of *seboko* wood. The one long-necked instrument is the *sape*, carved from a single piece of *menubun* wood with a flat board nailed to the base, six holes burnt through the body, two metal strings raised on small wooden bridges, pegs of *belaban* wood, beeswax for the movable frets and light ornamental carving near the pegs.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used here record no Kelabit dance or theatre traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

The named annual event is the **Bario Food and Culture Festival**, *Irau Nukenen Bario*, held over three days in late July — 25, 26 and 27 July in 2019. Life-cycle practice is bound up with rank and inheritance. Rural Kelabit society divides into three castes: the nobility, ordinary community members, and the descendants of people held in slavery; leaders always come from the nobility and are expected to hold the prestige goods listed above. Inheritance follows a strict order, and an heir carries the obligation to stage expensive commemorative celebrations for the person from whom they inherit — a burden sharp enough to set older brothers and sisters against each other, though the family remains a cooperating unit. Adulthood is a defined status rather than an age: an adult is *lun merar*, literally "big person", and reaching that state requires three things — a spouse with whom to grow rice, children, and rice enough to feed those children and their children. Marriage carries a bridewealth reckoned in buffalo, which are highly valued; seven are traditionally required for an upper-class bride. Before Christianity, megaliths were raised and ditches dug to honour notable individuals, and meat was killed for sacrifice rather than for eating.

## Foodways

Rice is what the Kelabit consider true human food, and it is the main crop; they distinguish wet from dry paddy, with the northern Bario highlands under wet field only and other parts of the highlands carrying dry fields intercropped with other plants. Beyond rice the fields and gardens yield maize, tapioca, pineapple, pumpkin, cucumber, beans, coffee, lemongrass, taro, passion fruit and strawberry. The forest supplies wild fruit and vegetables, and gathering, hunting and fishing are all normal parts of subsistence. Meat is the difficult item: it was traditionally killed only for sacrifice, and even now almost no animals are slaughtered for daily eating, though livestock is increasingly kept for meat and dairy. Named dishes include **Nubak Layak**, mashed rice wrapped in Isip leaf; **Manuk Pansuh** (also Pansoh), seasoned chicken cooked inside bamboo; **Udung Ubih**, tapioca leaves stir-fried with lemongrass; de-boned fish; fish cooked in a soup of brinjal, cucumber and black fungus; and **Labo Senutuq**, shredded beef stir-fried with bamboo shoots and served with a Kantan flower salad. Salt extraction matters to the highland economy, and salt is traded with other peoples for what the Kelabit cannot make themselves.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kelabit narrative reaches print through the Sarawak Gazette, which in 1965 published a Kelabit–Murut creation story about how the world was made by Guma Nepeled, collected by Balang Galih. The documentation effort around the language is substantial: Robert Blust published a Kelabit–English vocabulary in the Sarawak Museum Journal and returned repeatedly to the voiced aspirates, R. S. Douglas compared Kelabit with Kayan and Kenyah dialects as early as 1911, and there are archived collections of Kelabit language documentation materials as well as a Bario Kelabit language wiki. Charlotte Hemmings' 2016 doctoral thesis treats Kelabit voice and syntax.

## Language & religion

Kelabit belongs to the North Bornean branch of the Western Malayo-Polynesian languages and is among the most remote languages of Borneo, spoken in the Bario highlands and across the Sarawak–North Kalimantan border. Its dialects include Lepu Potng, Padas and Trusan. The language is notable in phonological typology for a rare series of true voiced aspirates occurring between vowels, without any matching series of voiceless aspirates — a combination otherwise reconstructed only for Proto-Indo-European, and not present in every Kelabit or Lun Dayeh dialect. It is written in Latin script, introduced to the island by Europeans. After the Second World War the Borneo Evangelical Mission reached the highlands and the Kelabit are now predominantly Christian; ethnic consolidation intensified alongside that conversion. Some adherents of the older beliefs remain. Earlier practice included stone amulets, megaliths and commemorative ditches.

During the Second World War the Kelabit were drawn into the Allied campaign. Tom Harrisson led Semut I, one of four Semut operations, parachuting into the highlands in 1945; armed by the Australian military, the Kelabit and their neighbours were essential to the liberation of Borneo.
