## Overview

The Kapampangan people, also called Pampangueños or Pampangos, are the sixth-largest ethnolinguistic group in the Philippines, numbering about 2,784,526 in 2010. Their traditional homeland is the province of Pampanga, although Kapampangans also live in Bataan, Tarlac, Bulacan, Nueva Ecija, Zambales and other parts of Central Luzon. Kapampangan communities are found in Tarlac City, Bamban, Capas, Concepcion, Dinalupihan, Hermosa, Cabiao, San Antonio, San Isidro, Gapan and Cabanatuan, as well as in Tondo and other parts of the National Capital Region. Kapampangans have also migrated to Mindoro, Palawan, Mindanao, the Ilocos Region and Cagayan Valley. Their language belongs to the Central Luzon languages of the Austronesian family. Folk culture documented in the sources includes festivals, religious observances, cuisine, language traditions and historical practices.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do record the *Aguman Sanduk*, a New Year observance in Minalin during which men cross-dress as women to welcome the coming year. This is described as a festival practice rather than as a named clothing tradition.

### Architecture

They state that the traditional Kapampangan household had a kitchen that was the biggest and most widely used room. The sources also mention the *puni*, a temporary shrine erected during *Mal ay Aldo*, the Kapampangan expression of Holy Week.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The oldest artifact identified in the sources is a 5000-year-old stone adze found in Candaba, said to have been used in building boats. Earthenware and tradeware dating back to 1500 BC have also been found in Candaba and Porac.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They do mention penitents in the *Mal ay Aldo* procession who are covered in blood from self-flagellation, and state that some have themselves crucified every Good Friday at the dried-up swamp of barrio Cutud in San Fernando. This is described as a religious penitential practice, not as adornment.

## Music & performance

Kapampangan performance traditions are closely connected in the sources with festivals and religious observances. The *Curaldal*, described as “street dancing,” accompanies the Feast of Santa Lucia in Sasmuan. The *Batalla Festival* re-enacts a battle between a native Muslim Moor and new colonist Native Capampangan Christians, representing a historical conflict between two religious groups of native Kapampangans. The battle begins at *Ugtung-aldo*, or afternoon, and ends at *Sisilim*, or sunset, with a tune called *BATTALA* by people from Masantol, Macabebe and Lubao.

During *Mal ay Aldo*, the Kapampangan expression of Holy Week, the *pasion*, or story of Christ’s suffering, is chanted in archaic Kapampangan at the *puni*. The melody of the Kapampangan *pasion* was said to have been taken from a traditional epic whose original words were lost and replaced by the story of Christ.

The Kapampangan language article records examples of spoken expressions and pronunciation, including *sisilim* for “dusk,” *terak* for “to dance,” and *Mangan Tana!* for “Let’s eat!” It also states that stress is phonemic and that Kapampangan has five vowel phonemes in its standard form.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name *Curaldal* as street dancing accompanying the Feast of Santa Lucia in Sasmuan. They also describe the *Batalla Festival*, whose performance re-enacts a historical battle between native Muslim and Christian Kapampangans. These are the only named Kapampangan dance or dramatic traditions covered by the sources.

Kapampangan literary and theatrical history includes *Gonzalo de Córdova* and *Comedia Heróica de la Conquista de Granada*, associated with Anselmo Fajardo, and *Alang Dios*, written by Juan Crisóstomo Soto in 1901. Aurelio Tolentino is described as a pioneer of writing literature in Kapampangan and as one of the first nationalist dramatists of the Philippines. His works include *Ing Buac Ning Ester*, a novel published in three parts from 1911 to 1915, and *Napun, Ngeni at Bukas*.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe an annual cycle of festivals connected with Christian feasts, seasonal change, rivers and local history. The *Aguman Sanduk* welcomes the New Year in Minalin, with men cross-dressing as women. The *Pistang Danum* is held in the barrios of Pansinao, Mandasig, Lanang and Pasig in Candaba. Food is served on floating banana rafts on the waters of the Pampanga River. Originally a non-Christian holiday, it now coincides with the baptism of Christ.

The Kapampangan New Year, *Bayung Banwa*, welcomes the coming of the monsoons and the start of the planting season. It is made to coincide with the feast of John the Baptist. In Apalit, the colourful *Apung Iru* fluvial procession was once a thanksgiving celebration in honour of the river and has become the feast of Saint Peter.

The *Mal ay Aldo* celebration is the Kapampangan expression of Holy Week. It includes the erection of the *puni*, where the *pasion* is chanted in archaic Kapampangan. Its highlight is the procession of *magdarame* or *sasalibatbat* penitents, some of whom are crucified every Good Friday at the dried-up swamp of barrio Cutud in San Fernando.

## Foodways

Kapampangan cuisine is called *Lutung Kapampangan* and is described as having gained a favourable reputation among other Philippine ethnic groups, with Pampanga known as the “Culinary Capital of the Philippines.” The Kapampangan kitchen is described as the largest and most widely used room in the traditional household. During Spanish rule, Spanish friars and sailors taught Kapampangans the basics of Spanish cooking, after which Kapampangan cooks developed a blend of local and Spanish influences.

Popular dishes named in the sources include *sisig*, *morcon*, *menudo*, *caldereta*, *estofado*, *embotido*, *asado*, *lengua*, *lechon*, *chicharon*, *afritada*, *sipo egg*, *bringhi*, *tabang talangka*, *tocino* or *pindang*, *pindang damulag*, and the native version of *longganisa*. *Nasing biringyi*, chicken saffron rice, is associated with fiestas in Pampanga because it is difficult to prepare. The sources compare it with Malaysian nasi biryani.

Other dishes include *balo balo* or *burung bulig*, mudfish fermented in rice, from Candaba; *betute tugak*, stuffed frogs, from Mexico in Pampanga and Magalang; *adobung kamaru*, mole crickets sautéed in vinegar and garlic; *calderetang barag*, spicy monitor lizard stew; *kubang asu*, sweet and spicy dog stew, from Macabebe; and *tidtad itik*, duck stewed in blood, from Masantol. Other heritage dishes are *tidtad*, *begukan*, *hornong pistu*, *asadong dila* and *kilayin*.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that the melody of the Kapampangan *pasion* was said to have been taken from a traditional epic, but that the epic’s original words were lost and replaced by the story of Christ. No title for that epic is supplied.

Kapampangan literary history includes works by Anselmo Fajardo, Juan Crisóstomo Soto, Amado Yuzon and Aurelio Tolentino. Amado Yuzon wrote “Crissotan” to immortalize Juan Crisóstomo Soto’s contribution to Kapampangan literature and is described as a Kapampangan Poet Laureate and Nobel Prize nominee for peace and literature. Aurelio Tolentino’s significant works include *Ing Buac Ning Ester* and *Napun, Ngeni at Bukas*. These works were later translated into Tagalog. Zoilo Hilario is described as having dedicated his life to imposing the ABAKADA on Kapampangan writing through the Akademyang Kapampangan, the organization he created.

## Language & religion

Kapampangan, also called Capampáñgan or Pampangan, is an Austronesian language and one of the eight major languages of the Philippines. It belongs to the Central Luzon languages and is primarily spoken in Pampanga and southern Tarlac, with speakers also in northeastern Bataan, Bulacan, Nueva Ecija and Zambales. The language is known honorifically as *Amánung Sísuan*, meaning “breastfed, or nurtured, language.” Its ISO 639-2 three-letter code is pam.

Kapampangan is predominantly spoken in Pampanga and southern Tarlac and is also used in communities in Metro Manila, Palawan and Mindanao. It has borrowed words from Chinese, Sanskrit and Spanish. The sources state that Kapampangans are mostly Christians, with Roman Catholics, Aglipay, Methodists and members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints represented. A few belong to non-Christian religions. Traces of native-Austronesian Anitism, Hinduism and Buddhism remain in folk practices and traditions. A few Kapampangans practice Islam, and the sources connect earlier Muslim practice with links to Bruneian Malays and with the former Muslim Kingdom of Tondo.
