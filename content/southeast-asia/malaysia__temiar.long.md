## Overview

The Temiar are a Senoic people indigenous to the Malay Peninsula and one of the largest of the eighteen Orang Asli groups of Malaysia. They live mainly in the states of Perak, Pahang and Kelantan, with settlements including Jeram Bertam and Kampung Chengkelik at Kuala Betis in Kelantan, and Kampung Jarau Baru at Kemar, Kampung Sungai Cadak in Ulu Kinta, Kampung Tonggang at Tanjung Rambutan, Kampung Ulu Gerik, Pos Poi and Pos Perwor at Lasah, Kampung Temakah at Sungai Siput, and Air Banun in the Hulu Perak District, all in Perak. Estimates of the total population range from about 40,000 to 120,000; most live on the fringes of the rainforest, and a small number have become urbanised. Their language, Temiar, is Central Aslian within the Austroasiatic family, and the name itself means "edge" or "side" — Temiars describe themselves as "people of the edge, outside," that is, of the jungle. The objects gathered here — plaited bamboo pouches, cane baskets, blowpipe darts in bamboo quivers, sleeping mats and bamboo instruments — are almost entirely made from forest materials worked by hand.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources describe no loom weaving or cloth production. What they do document in detail is plaiting in bamboo fibre and cane, which carries the group's whole pattern vocabulary.

**Plaited bamboo-fibre pouches** — Small containers for betel nut, leaf and tobacco, built from very fine split bamboo fibres in undyed natural colour worked together with fibres dyed purple, indigo, blue, green, red or pink. The finest examples combine lid, body and carrying strap in a single plaited piece, and many hold a second, coarser inner pouch of thick bamboo fibre. One betel pouch carries six squares with central star shapes on one face, a twisted decorative band about a third of the way down the body, and a fringe of small plaited squares with bamboo-fibre tassels at the bottom corners. A triple set of pouches joined by one central purple-dyed strap was used to carry betel nuts, tobacco and leaves together.

**Plaited straps** — Carrying straps are patterned in their own right rather than left plain: repeated rows of woven double squares with larger clusters at intervals, repeating square "belt buckle" shapes, woven circular shapes with squares set inside them, and rows of diamonds with squared edges. Borders are often left in natural-coloured fibre, and on one bag the strap is stitched to the sides while on others it is looped through the base.

**Cane and bamboo basketry** — Back baskets are plaited from cane in a narrow weave, cylindrical in form, with two leaf-fibre shoulder straps crossing the body. A related bamboo back-basket has a square base and a round rim reinforced with thicker bamboo, with two handle loops at base and rim for the straps.

**Plaited sleeping mats** — Narrow rectangular mats of plaited cane or fibre, some plain, others patterned by running dyed fibre through the plait so that crossed diagonal lines in purple or in green cut across the rectangle.

**Rice-shaker plaiting** — A large shield-shaped winnowing plate of plaited bamboo fibre with a thick bamboo rim finished with plaited detail; the ground weave reads as brickwork, with three bands of bamboo laid in the opposite direction across the back.

**Fish-trap caning** — An open-work cane cylinder tapering to a point, with two sets of spikes formed into inward-facing pyramids to hold the catch, and a separate cane circle slatted and tied with cane strip to cap the tapered end.

**Motif vocabulary.** Diamond, both alone and hatched; trellis; chequer; triangle; star within a square; square, singly, doubled and in clusters; circle enclosing a square; crossed diagonal lines; brickwork.

### Clothing & dress

The sources record one item of dress: a headband plaited from bamboo fibres, part undyed and part dyed pale green, the undyed fibres set so that they form a repeating diamond. A frill of white polythene is sewn along one edge — the same reach for new industrial materials that appears elsewhere in the collection, where plastic buttons, felt-tip pen and plastic tassels finish plaited bags. Beyond this the sources used do not describe Temiar everyday or ceremonial clothing, and say nothing about differences between men's and women's dress, footwear or belts.

### Architecture

The sources used do not describe Temiar house forms, building materials or settlement layout; they name the villages and administrative posts where Temiar live but not what is built in them.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The household and forest equipment recorded here is made of bamboo, cane and wood, with metal confined to blades and binding wire. Machetes have metal blades and wooden handles and are kept in wooden sheaths, the two halves held together by a metal coil and a binding of plaited cane; one sheath is finished with carved notches along the edge between the first and second cane bindings, matched by notches and a triangular carving on the handle itself. Axes are built up from a bamboo handle into which a central wooden pole is inserted, with a moulded metal blade set into a wooden head and thin plaited cord wrapped around the handle to bind blade to head. Blowpipe darts are carried by the dozen in a cylindrical bamboo quiver closed with a finely plaited fibre lid, a cord handle fixed to a coil of wire around the tube. Rice is shaken clean in the plaited bamboo plate described above, and fish taken in the spiked cane trap.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources record no jewelry, tattooing or hair practice. The nearest thing to personal adornment documented is a comb cut from a half-cylinder of red or stained wood or cane, with three long prongs sharpened at the ends and a small incised design of hatched diamond and triangle at the top; and the plaited headband with its diamond pattern and polythene frill. Red and white glass beads appear once, threaded on fibre at the point of a bag flap as a fastener.

## Music & performance

Two instruments survive in the collection, both made from wide bamboo cylinders. The first is a bamboo-tube zither: the cylinder has split along one side, and two thin cane strings run the length of the tube across a bridge made from a small wooden bar, the strings coiled and looped around the circumference at the far end. The second is a sealed bamboo tube with a D-shaped opening at one end, set below a tongue-shaped projection on the top edge. The ceremonial *Sewang* dance is performed as part of Temiar folk belief. Beyond these, the sources used do not describe Temiar song genres or performance occasions. Marina Roseman's book-length study of Temiar music and medicine, listed below, is the standing scholarly reference.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name one dance: the ceremonial *Sewang*, performed by the Temiar as part of their folk beliefs. They give no account of its choreography, its performers or its occasions, and describe no theatrical tradition. Sue Jennings has written on Temiar dance and its relation to social order, and on Temiar ritual and transformation.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not set out a Temiar festival calendar or describe life-cycle rites at birth, initiation, marriage or death. What they record is that the Temiar are traditionally animists and that the *Sewang* dance belongs to that religious life. Geoffrey Benjamin's fifty-year study of Temiar religion, covering 1964 to 2012, is the source to go to; the dream-centred literature associated with the Senoi is represented by *Dream Theory in Malaya*.

## Foodways

Three dishes are named. *Nasi serempad* is rice cooked inside bamboo. *Umbut bayas masak gulai* is a curry made from palm pith. *Pucuk paku peno'ol* combines the young fronds of the *pucuk paku* fern with tapioca leaves and anchovies, again cooked in bamboo — the same length of green bamboo that serves as cooking vessel, quiver, instrument body and tool handle elsewhere in this collection. The equipment side of foodways is better documented than the recipes: the plaited shield-shaped shaker for cleaning rice, the spiked cane fish-trap, and the several plaited pouches made specifically to carry betel nut and leaf and tobacco, sometimes all three in a single strapped set. The sources describe no ceremonial dishes, no drink traditions and no dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used do not record Temiar folktales, epics, proverbs or storytelling occasions. They do point to the documentation effort: Temiar materials are archived in RWAAI, the Repository and Workspace for Austroasiatic Intangible Heritage, and Geoffrey Benjamin has published on Temiar personal names and on aesthetic elements in the grammar itself.

## Language & religion

Temiar is a Central Aslian language of the Austroasiatic family, spoken in western Malaysia; the Temiar are among the most numerous Aslian-speaking peoples, numbering around 30,000 speakers in 2017. The language has three allomorphic classes of pronouns — stressed, unstressed and bound — and stressed third-person pronouns must appear with a demonstrative, as in *na-doh* "he-here" and *ʔun-tu:y* "they-elsewhere." Noun phrases open with the noun or pronoun and follow it with modifiers and demonstratives or possessor pronouns; verb phrases run negation, auxiliary, main verb, and precede the subject. The Temiar are traditionally animists, and the *Sewang* dance is performed as part of those beliefs.
