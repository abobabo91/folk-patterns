## Overview

The Dayak are the Austronesian indigenous peoples of Borneo, a loose umbrella term covering more than 200 riverine and hill-dwelling ethnic groups spread across the interior of Kalimantan (Indonesia), Sarawak and Sabah (Malaysia), and Brunei. Major sub-groups include the Iban (formerly "Sea Dayak"), Bidayuh (formerly "Land Dayak"), Ngaju, Kenyah, Kayan, Bakumpai, and the Orang Ulu cluster. They speak languages from several branches of Malayo-Polynesian — Land Dayak, Malayic, Sabahan, and Barito — with roughly 170 languages and dialects documented on the island. In folk-culture terms the Dayak matter as one of Southeast Asia's most important centers of ikat weaving, glass and shell beadwork, tattooing, longhouse architecture, and animist ritual (Kaharingan), and as the source of the hornbill iconography that has become emblematic of Borneo.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Pua kumbu** — the ceremonial warp-ikat cotton blanket of the Iban of Sarawak and West Kalimantan. Woven on a backstrap loom by women, the warp threads are tie-dyed with natural pigments (indigo and *engkudu* / Morinda red) before weaving; the finished cloth is used to receive newborns, wrap heirlooms, and — historically — to receive the severed heads brought back from *ngayau* raids.

**Iban ikat (kain kebat)** — the broader Iban warp-ikat tradition beyond ceremonial *pua*, including skirts (*kain*) and jacket panels. It is distinguished from Sumatran and Sumbanese ikat by its dense, curvilinear figuration and by the ritual status of the weaver herself, whose progression through motifs parallels the male warrior's progression through headhunting grades.

**Sungkit and pilih** — Iban supplementary-weft (*sungkit*) and supplementary-warp (*pilih*) techniques, producing raised patterning in red or black on a plain ground. *Sungkit* cloths are considered even more spiritually charged than *pua kumbu* and were used to wrap trophy heads.

**Dayak beadwork** — glass-bead panels worked on baby carriers (*ba'* among the Kenyah-Kayan), sun-hats, skirts, and jacket collars. Ancient stone, shell, bone, and tooth beads were supplemented from the 8th–9th century by imported glass beads in red, yellow, white, turquoise, and black; multicolored beads followed in the 16th–17th centuries. Heirloom beads are known as *pesaka* and are inherited across generations.

**Dayak barkcloth (kapuo / ku'ut)** — beaten bark from *Artocarpus* and *Antiaris* species, formerly the everyday cloth of interior groups such as the Kenyah and Punan before cotton arrived, and still used ceremonially.

**Tattoo as textile of the skin** — while not a woven textile, Dayak *tato* / *tutang* draws from the same motif vocabulary as ikat and beadwork and is treated as a comparable body-covering art (see Jewelry & body adornment).

**Motif vocabulary.** *Kenyalang* (hornbill), *aso* / *aso'* (the dog-dragon composite spirit-guardian), *nabau* (serpent), *engkaramba* (anthropomorphic ancestor figure), *bunga terung* (eggplant flower — the shoulder rosette that opens a man's tattoo cycle), *tuang* (crocodile), *kala* (scorpion), *pucuk rebung* (bamboo shoot), spiral tendrils and coiling fern fronds.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday interior dress was minimal and adapted to a hot, wet forest: men wore the loincloth (*sirat* / *cawat*) of beaten bark or cotton, and women a short wrap-skirt (*bidang* among the Iban, *ta'a* among the Kenyah). Before the 20th century, toplessness was the norm for women, with only heavily built or married women covering the chest. Ceremonial dress transforms the body. Iban men wear the *sirat* with a hornbill-feather headdress and a *baju burung* jacket, and carry the *mandau* / *parang ilang* at the hip. Iban women's *ngepan* consists of a *sugu tinggi* silver crown, layered silver coin belts (*rawai*), a silver corset (*marik empang*), and a heavy *kain kebat* skirt. Kenyah and Kayan noblewomen wear beaded skullcaps and baby carriers dense with *aso'* figures, while men wear a rattan war-cap (*lawung*) trimmed with hornbill and argus-pheasant feathers. Elongated earlobes weighted with *hisang* brass or silver rings — up to twenty per ear on a married woman — were the standard female beauty ideal until the 1960s.

### Architecture

The signature Dayak building is the longhouse — *rumah panjai* (Iban), *betang* (Ngaju), *lamin* (Kenyah/Benuaq), *uma dado'* (Kayan) — a raised communal structure of ironwood (*belian* / *Eusideroxylon zwageri*) posts and axe-hewn planks, roofed with palm-leaf or ironwood shingles, that may exceed 200 meters in length. It is entered by a notched-log ladder from a fortified apron, and is divided into a covered public gallery (*ruai* in Iban) running the full length of the building, and behind it a row of private family apartments (*bilek*). The *ruai* is the setting for festivals, weaving, and the display of trophy skulls in a rattan basket over the hearth. Carved and painted decoration concentrates on ridgepole finials, house posts, and the panels flanking the entrance, using *aso'* dragon-dogs and *kenyalang* hornbills. Freestanding architecture includes the Ngaju *sandung* bone-house on carved pillars, the *sapundu* anthropomorphic sacrificial post, and, in Christianized areas, the Kaharingan *balai basarah* prayer hall standardized after 1980.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Dayak did not develop a village pottery industry of their own; instead, imported Chinese stoneware jars (*tajau* / *tempayan*), traded up-river from the coast between the Tang and Ming periods, became the paramount heirloom object, used to store rice wine (*tuak*), ferment offerings, and hold the bones of the secondary dead. Certain named jar types — the "Gusi" and "Rusa" among the Iban — carried genealogies of their own. Metalwork centers on the *mandau* (also *parang ilang*, *malat*), the Dayak sword: a soft-iron blade with a hard-iron cutting strip forge-welded in through the process of *ngamboh*, fitted with a deer-antler or hardwood hilt carved in *aso'* motifs and sheathed edge-up for a fast draw. Blowpipes (*sumpit*) of drilled ironwood, up to two meters long, are fitted with a spear-blade lashed to the muzzle and shoot darts poisoned with *ipoh* (Antiaris toxicaria) latex. Woven rattan carrying baskets (*ingen*, *ambin*), bamboo cooking tubes, and gong sets round out the household inventory.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver and brass work is worn in bulk on ceremonial occasions: Iban women's *marik empang* beaded and silver-coin corset, the *sugu tinggi* headdress, and layered *rawai* belts of stacked coin discs; Kenyah women's stretched earlobes weighted with *hisang*. Beads reappear as necklaces, bracelets, and the beaded baby carrier. Tattooing (*pantang*, *tato*, *tutang*) is the most elaborate body art. Iban men earn tattoos by achievement — the shoulder *bunga terung* rosette marks the *bejalai* journey away from the longhouse, while throat and knuckle marks were formerly reserved for successful headhunters. Kayan and Kenyah women receive dense thigh and forearm tattoos, applied with soot-blackened citrus thorns, that mark rank; noblewomen's tattoos extended from ankle to mid-thigh in continuous *aso'* and hook patterns.

## Music & performance

The signature Dayak instrument is the *sape* (also *sapeh*, *sampe'*), a two- to four-stringed boat-lute of the Kenyah and Kayan, carved from a single block of *adau* wood, fretted with movable beeswax frets, and originally strung with rattan or palm fiber (now steel). Traditionally played to accompany healing ceremonies and the *datun julud* welcome dance, it has become the pan-Dayak instrument of choice, brought to international audiences by Sarawak's Rainforest World Music Festival. Other named instruments include the *keledi* / *keluri* mouth-organ of gourd and bamboo pipes, the *jatung utang* wooden xylophone hung between longhouse posts, the *pagang* bamboo tube-zither, the nose flute (*suling*), and the *gendang* drum. Bronze gong ensembles imported through the Brunei trade — hanging bossed gongs (*tawak*, *bebendai*) and the *engkerumong* small-gong rack — accompany feasts and warrior displays. Vocal genres include the Iban *pantun* and *renong* sung poetry, the *timang* / *pengap* ritual invocations chanted by a *lemambang* bard through an entire night of the *gawai* festival, and Ngaju *karungut* recitation.

## Dance & theatre

Dayak performance is danced rather than staged; there is no equivalent of Javanese *wayang* puppet theatre. The Kenyah **datun julud** is a slow solo hornbill-imitation dance for women, arms extended with fans of hornbill tail-feathers strapped to the fingers. The Iban **ngajat** is a warrior dance performed with mandau and long shield, culminating in a leap and stylized beheading motion; a female version, *ngajat indu*, is more contained and processional. The Ngaju and Bakumpai **manasai** and **giring-giring** are communal circle dances shaken to bamboo stampers. Ritual masked dance survives in the **hudoq** of the Bahau, Modang, and Busang Dayaks of East Kalimantan — enormous carved-wood masks representing rice-pest spirits and ancestral protectors, danced through the fields at planting to bless the crop.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual calendar tracks the swidden rice cycle. The Iban **Gawai Dayak**, held from 1 June each year and made an official Sarawak holiday in 1965, closes the harvest with rice-wine offerings, *miring* rite, and all-night *pengap* chanting. The Ngaju and Ot Danum **Tiwah** is the great secondary mortuary festival, held years after the primary burial: the bones of the dead are exhumed, washed, and installed in a carved *sandung* bone-house, accompanied by buffalo sacrifice at the *sapundu* post and multi-day gong music, sending the soul to the Upper-world land *Lewu Tatau*. The Kenyah, Kayan, and Bahau observe **hudoq** masked-dance rites at rice-planting. Life-cycle rites include the Iban *melah pinang* wedding with betel-nut exchange, and, historically, the male *bejalai* journey — a young man's obligatory departure from the longhouse to gain experience, wealth, and tattoos before returning. Overshadowing all peace ritual is the **Tumbang Anoi Agreement** of 1894 in Damang Batu, Central Kalimantan, the months-long inter-tribal assembly that formally ended *ngayau* headhunting across the Dayak world.

## Foodways

The staple is hill rice grown by swidden, cooked plain or, distinctively, inside a length of green bamboo lined with banana leaf and roasted directly on the fire (*lemang* / *nasi lemang*, and the Iban *nasi pansuh* / *pansoh manok*, where chicken, lemongrass, and *daun kesum* are stuffed into the bamboo with the rice). Wild forest starches supplement rice: sago flour from *Eugeissona* palm among the Penan and Punan, cassava, and tubers. Freshwater fish from the Kapuas, Mahakam, Barito, and Rejang rivers are grilled, or fermented with rice into the sour *kasam* preserve. Wild boar, deer, and — where taboo allows — python and monitor lizard are hunted with blowpipe and dog. Bitter jungle ferns (*pucuk paku*, *midin*) are stir-fried with garlic and shrimp paste. The ceremonial drink is *tuak*, a rice wine fermented in heirloom *tajau* jars and served from a communal cup at *gawai* and to welcome guests to the longhouse; a stronger distillate is *arak*. Betel-nut chewing (*sirih pinang*) accompanies negotiation and courtship.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Iban preserve one of Southeast Asia's most extensive corpora of ritual oral literature, chanted by the *lemambang* bard: the epic **timang gawai** invocations that summon the gods to the longhouse festival, the *pengap* narratives of *Sengalang Burong* (the hornbill war-god) and his sons-in-law the warrior heroes, and the *renong* sung dialogues. Iban children's genres include *ensera* folktales, *entelah* riddles, and *jaku' silup* proverbs. The Ngaju *Panaturan*, codified as scripture in 1971, gathers the creation cosmology in which the *Batang Garing* Tree of Life connects Upper-world and Under-world. Kayan and Kenyah keep genealogical chants (*takna'*) that recite ancestry back to the founding of a longhouse. Twentieth-century figures such as the Ngaju leader Tjilik Riwut collected and published Dayak ethnography, and contemporary institutions including the Tun Jugah Foundation in Kuching have recorded *pua kumbu* motif names and *timang* recitations from surviving master weavers and bards.

## Language & religion

Dayak languages fall into several Malayo-Polynesian subgroups — Land Dayak, Malayic (including Iban), Sabahan, and Barito (including Ngaju and Ma'anyan) — with roughly 170 tongues on the island, many endemic and endangered. Historical writing was minimal; ritual knowledge passed orally, and modern literacy is in Rumi Malay/Indonesian script. The indigenous religion is *Kaharingan*, an animist system focused on a stratified cosmos (Upper-world / middle earth / Under-world) mediated by shamans (*balian*, *manang*) who undertake ecstatic soul-flight to heal illness and escort the dead. Since Indonesia's 1980 recognition, Kaharingan has been administratively grouped under Hinduism, with the *Panaturan* as its scripture and the *balai basarah* as its temple; a rival council contests the Hindu classification. Christian conversion, begun by the Rheinische and Basler missions in the 19th century, is now the majority allegiance in much of the interior, while the Bakumpai and other groups are Muslim Dayaks retaining Ngaju cultural ties.
