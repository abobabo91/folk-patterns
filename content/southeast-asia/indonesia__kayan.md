## Overview

The Kayan are an indigenous tribe from Borneo, living in Kalimantan, Indonesia, and Sarawak, Malaysia. They are categorised as part of the Dayak people and are grouped with the Kenyah and Bahau under the Apo Kayan people group. The Kayan are part of the Orang Ulu, meaning “upriver people.” Their population may be around 200,000. Their origins are associated with the Kayan river in North Kalimantan, and their communities historically lived along the upper Kayan, middle Kapuas and Mahakam rivers. They are associated with upland rice cultivation, shifting cultivation, blacksmithing, boat building, carpentry, tattoos, stretched earlobes, storytelling and song. The Kayan language belongs to the Malayo-Polynesian branch of the Austronesian language family.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name *tedek* as the term for Kayan tattoo patterns.

### Clothing & dress

They state that both women and men traditionally had stretched earlobes. Tattooing was practiced before the arrival of Christian missionaries: women tattooed mostly girls and women to signify womanhood, while men received tattoos for ornamental reasons.

### Architecture

Traditionally, the Kayan lived in long houses on river banks. A settlement consisted of one or several long houses, which could be as long as 300 meters and accommodate up to 100 families, or 400–600 people. The houses consisted of a common veranda and rooms, and their residents formed a tribal community.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Kayan craftspeople are described as excellent blacksmiths, boat builders and carpenters. *Malat*, or swords, were the main item of exchange. Kayan crafts also included pottery, wood carving, mask making and the production of tans.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Kayan women and men traditionally stretched their earlobes. Tattooing was practiced by women, who tattooed mostly girls and women to signify womanhood; the patterns, called *tedek*, were intricate and detailed. Men received tattoos for ornamental reasons.

## Music & performance

The sources describe the Kayan as having older generations of storytellers and songsmiths. One performance form was *Tekna'*, a verbal art of storytelling through songs. Storytellers used it to entertain fellow villagers and welcome visitors. The form is now described as nearly lost. Traditional Kayan songs include “Alam Lening” and “Lung Kayaan Mendalam.” The sources also identify *Parap* and *Takna'* as ancient Kayan oral art forms, and name Adrian Jo Milang as a practitioner of these forms.

## Dance & theatre

They mention the making of masks as a developed craft.

## Festivals & rituals

Kayan religion included a complex cult involving bounty hunting, human sacrifices and shamanism. Bounty hunting and human sacrifices disappeared at the beginning of the 20th century. The central event was a feast of collected heads, during which warrior initiations and funeral ceremonies were held. Dogs were kept for hunting and ritual purposes. In the 20th century, some Kayan converted to Christianity, while others developed an indigenous religious reform called *Adat Bungan* after World War II. The reform began with the Kenyah Lepo' Tau of Long Nawang.

## Foodways

Kayan agriculture was based on shifting cultivation and the cultivation of upland rice. Other cultivated foods included sago, corn, yams, pumpkin and tobacco. During years of famine, sago was mined. Fishing was traditionally important, and wild animals were hunted with a *sumpitan*. The Kayan also bred pigs and chickens. Dogs were kept for hunting and ritual purposes.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Kayan are described as having older generations of great storytellers and songsmiths. Storytelling through songs was performed in *Tekna'*, which was used to entertain fellow villagers and welcome visitors. The sources also name *Parap* and *Takna'* as ancient Kayan oral art forms. Traditional songs include “Alam Lening” and “Lung Kayaan Mendalam.” Some practices and traditions have died out or nearly vanished through outside influence, colonial contact, missionary activity and modernisation since the 20th century. Younger indigenous people of Sarawak have recently made efforts to revitalise these practices.

## Language & religion

The Kayan language belongs to the Malayo-Polynesian branch of the Austronesian language family. A separate Wikipedia article states that “Kayan language” may refer to the Padaung language of Burma, any of the Kayan languages of Borneo or the Kaian language of New Guinea. The Kayan developed a religion involving a complex cult, bounty hunting, human sacrifices and shamanism. Some Kayan converted to Christianity in the 20th century, while others followed the indigenous religious reform *Adat Bungan*, which began after World War II.
