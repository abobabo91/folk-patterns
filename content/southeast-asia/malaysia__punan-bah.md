## Overview

Punan Bah, also called Punan or Punan Ba, are an ethnic group found in Sarawak, Malaysia, and Kalimantan, Indonesia. They are distinct from the semi-nomadic Penan people and have never been nomadic themselves. Their name derives from two rivers along whose banks they have lived since time immemorial; *Mikuang Bungulan*, *Mikuang*, and *Aveang Buan* are other names used ritually. Punan communities are concentrated around Bintulu, Sarawak, and along the Rajang River, with settlements extending from Merit District to lower Belaga town and into the Kakus, Kemena, and Jelalong river areas. Their population is estimated at around 3000–5000 people. They speak Bah-Biau Punan, an Austronesian language, and maintain distinctive traditions involving longhouses, agriculture, burial practices, social ranks, and religious observance.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Punan Bah communities live in longhouses, which remain important even after migration to urban areas. Many people who moved to places such as Bintulu, Sibu, Kuching, and Kuala Lumpur continued to return to their longhouses, particularly during major festivities. As of 2006, more than 10 Punan settlements or longhouses were found along the Rejang, Kakus, Kemena, and Jelalong rivers. Named settlements include Punan Lovuk Sama, Punan Lovuk Ba, Punan Lovuk Biau, Punan Lovuk Meluyou, Punan Lovuk Lirung Belang, Punan Lovuk Mina, Punan Lovuk Pandan, and Punan Lo'o Buong. Punan Lovuk Lirung Belang was formerly named Rumah Bilong and is now known as Rumah Ado; Punan Lovuk Pandan is also called Rumah Nyipa Tingang, and Punan Lo'o Buong is also known as Rumah Adi.

## Festivals & rituals

Punan Bah continue to return to their longhouses during major festivities, especially the Harvest Festival, known among Punan as the Bungan festival. The name *Bungan* also refers to a cult religion brought by Jok Apui, a Kenyah from Kalimantan, and should not be treated as a separately documented festival tradition in the available material.

The sources describe a distinctive burial custom. In earlier times, Punan did not bury their aristocrats, or *lajar*. Instead, they built a pole called a *kelirieng*, described as having a height of 50 meters, on which they placed their leaders. Fewer than 30 *kelirieng* are estimated to remain standing in Sarawak. Punan also continue to practise a secondary burial ceremony in which the dead body is kept in the longhouse for at least 3–7 days, partly so that relatives who live far away can pay their last respects.

## Foodways

Punan Bah historically based their livelihood on a mixed economy. Swidden agriculture provided hill paddy as the main crop, supplemented by maniok, taro, sugar cane, tobacco, and other tropical plants. Hunting, especially of wild boar, fishing, and gathering forest resources were also important parts of the economy.

They record only the principal crops and subsistence activities listed above. The Punan language source gives phrases concerning meals: *Tupu koman si* means “Do you have your lunch/dinner/breakfast?” No further food terminology is documented.

## Oral tradition & literature

Punan historical and mythical traditions are closely connected to ruling aristocrats, just as the history of European Middle Ages is described in the source as being linked to ruling monarchs. The social order is described as stratified into *laja*, or aristocrats, *panyen*, or commoners, and *lipen*, or slaves; this hierarchy determines historical traditions that have been preserved.

## Language & religion

Punan speak Bah-Biau Punan, one of the Punan languages. Bah-Biau Punan is an Austronesian language spoken by Punan Bah and Punan Biau people in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Brunei. Punan is described as being closer to the language spoken by the Sekapans and Kejamans than to Penan. The sources provide four Punan phrases: *Nu denge?* (“How are you?”), *Nu ngaro no?* (“What is your name?”), *Piro umun no?* (“How old are you?”), and *Tupu koman si* (“Do you have your lunch/dinner/breakfast?”).

Punan traditional religion was a form of animism known as *Besavik*. Christian missionaries arrived during the Brooke era and brought education and modern medicine. Punan communities retained *Besavik* and subsequently adopted the *Bungan* cult religion, brought by Jok Apui, a Kenyah from Kalimantan. By the late 1990s, conversion to Christianity had increased; as of 2006, almost half of Punan were Christian, while elderly and less educated people continued observing *Bungan* religion.
