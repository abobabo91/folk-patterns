## Overview

The Kamein, also known as the Kaman or the Arakanese Muslims, are a Southeast Asian, Indo-Aryan ethnic group indigenous to Rakhine State, Myanmar. They primarily reside in Rakhine State, where approximately 28,000 of the 50,000 Kaman in Myanmar live. The Kaman predominantly follow Islam and are formally recognized by the Burmese government as one of the seven ethnic groups composing the Rakhine national race. They are considered indigenous Burmese citizens and widely hold national identity cards. The Kaman speak Rakhine, and their customs and dress resemble those of the Rakhine. Their cultural history is associated with archery, the Rakhine Royal Army, Islamic and Persian influence, charitable practices, marriage customs, and continuing discrimination connected to religion and ethnicity.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that Kaman customs and dress resemble those of the Rakhine and that Kaman people wear Rakhine-style clothing. The available material therefore documents resemblance to Rakhine dress.

### Architecture

The sources mention a Kaman practice during Thingyan and Myanmar New Year’s Eve of building rest shelters for travelers. They also describe donating lakes for the convenience of water fetching. No house form, building material, roof type, settlement pattern, decorative system, or named architectural tradition is documented.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention lakes donated for water fetching and rest shelters built for travelers, but provide no catalogue of associated objects or construction techniques.

## Music & performance

The sources mention the “Rakhine Princess Lyric,” in which the court poet Adu Min Nyo referred to the Kaman as brave archers of the Rakhine Kingdom. They also state that the Ministry of Education’s distance education syllabus, Ma-412, defines the word Kaman in line 33 as referring to soldiers who were experts in archery and served in the Royal army of the Rakhine kingdom. These references connect Kaman history with lyric and courtly textual traditions.

No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription concerns this ethnic group.

## Dance & theatre

The “Rakhine Princess Lyric” is mentioned as a historical literary work.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources identify Thingyan as the water festival and Myanmar New Year’s Eve as a period associated with an old Kaman tradition of shampooing children. During this period, Kaman charitable practices included donating lakes for the convenience of water fetching and building rest shelters for travelers.

The Kaman are described as predominantly Muslim. They state that the Kaman practiced monogamy and that, before marriage, they consulted astrology to check the fate of the bride and groom and selected an auspicious wedding date. This custom is described as also being practiced by the Rakhine people.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kaman history is connected in the sources with the “Rakhine Princess Lyric,” in which the royal court poet Adu Min Nyo referred to the Kaman as brave archers of the Rakhine Kingdom during the reign of King Ba Saw Pru. The Kaman are also mentioned in the New Dannyawadi History by the venerable monk U Nyar Na.

The sources describe Kaman as originally designating the Rakhine Royal archer regiment rather than a specific ethnic group. They state that, after the fall of the Rakhine Kingdom, the archers remained in groups and that their descendants became the present-day Kaman of the Rakhine region. The sources also discuss the book Indigenous People of Rakhine State by U Hla Tun Pru, who is described as a journalist-turned-politician and former State Council member.

No Kaman folktales, epic poems, proverbs, riddles, storytelling performances, contemporary literary revival, or preservation project is documented in the supplied material.

## Language & religion

The Kaman speak Rakhine. The sources describe them as an Indo-Aryan ethnic group and state that their customs and dress resemble those of the Rakhine.

The Kaman predominantly follow Islam. The sources state that they have sometimes been conflated with the Rohingya despite being recognized as an official ethnic group since the Ne Win era. They describe discrimination connected with religious affiliation and explain that Kaman have faced tensions between the Buddhist majority and Muslim minorities, particularly in Rakhine State.
