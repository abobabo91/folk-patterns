## Overview

The Bontoc, also called Bontok, are an ethnolinguistic group of the central and eastern portions of Mountain Province on Luzon in the Philippines. Their cultural area is bounded by the central and eastern mountains of Mountain Province and includes the municipalities of Bontoc and Sadanga, extending eastward to Barlig Central and Lias. The Bontoc live particularly near the Chico River and its tributaries. They are the second largest group in Mountain Province. They describe a society shaped by village wards, kinship, agriculture, music, ritual, distinctive clothing, body tattoos, and a pre-Christian belief system.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Men wear *wanes*, described as g-strings, and a *suklong*, a rattan cap. Women wear *tapis*, described as skirts. The sources also state that members of the *kadangyan* caste wore specialised clothing. Wealthy families used jewelry made from gold, glass beads, agate beads called *appong*, or shells to display status.

### Architecture

Bontoc social organization was formerly centered on village wards containing about 14 to 50 homes. Three indigenous housing structures are named: the family residence, called *katyufong*; female dormitories, called *olog*; and male dormitories, called *ato* or *ator*. Other structures were associated with agriculture, including rice granaries called *akhamang* and pigpens called *khongo*. Traditionally, all these structures had *inatep*, or cogon-grass roofs.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention household utensils, cooking tools, agricultural tools such as bolos, trowels, and plows, and fish traps made from bamboo or rattan. Weapons include battleaxes called *pin-nang* or *pinangas*, knives, spears called *falfeg*, *fangkao*, or *sinalawitan*, and shields called *kalasag*.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Bontoc jewelry is associated particularly with wealthy families and may be made of gold, glass beads, agate beads called *appong*, or shells. Tattoos are called *fatek*. The sources distinguish three tattoo types: *chaklag*, the tattooed chest of a head taker; *pongo*, tattoos on the arms of men and women; and *fatĕk*, the term for other tattoos of both sexes. Women were tattooed on the arms to enhance beauty or signify readiness for marriage. Men’s tattoos indicated that the wearer was a warrior who had taken heads during battle. One tattooing method involved drawing a pattern with soot and water, pricking the skin with a *chakayyum*, and working soot into the open skin. The tattooist was called a *bumafatek*.

## Music & performance

Music is important in Bontoc life and is usually performed during ceremonies. The sources name the *kalaleng*, a nose flute; the *gangsa*, a gong; the *affiliao*, a bamboo mouth organ; and the *ab-a-fiw*, a Jew’s harp. Songs and chants accompany these instruments.

The Bontoc also perform a circular rhythmic dance that acts out aspects of the hunt. The dance is always accompanied by the *gangsa*. There was no singing or talking during the dance drama, while women participated usually outside the circumference. The event was described as serious but pleasurable for everyone involved, including children.

## Dance & theatre

The sources describe a circular rhythmic dance drama representing certain aspects of the hunt. It was accompanied by the *gangsa*, included women who usually participated outside the circumference, and involved no singing or talking. The event was serious but pleasurable for the participants and children.

## Festivals & rituals

The Bontoc practice community rituals for agriculture and for matters affecting the entire province, including natural disasters. Their kinship groups help regulate these communal relationships and property arrangements. The sources name *begnas* rituals and state that the Bontoc ritual *pasiking* is called *takba*. The *takba* represents an ancestor figure and is an active participant in *begnas* rituals. They state that the Bontoc formerly practiced head-hunting and that tattoos could identify a man as a warrior who had taken heads during battle. They also describe the importance of marriage regulation within kinship groups and the former residence of young men and women in dormitories.

## Foodways

Rice is the main produce of the Bontoc. During dry periods from February to March, when rain is scarce, they usually consume camote, corn, and millet as alternatives to rice. The Bontoc also catch and gather fish, snails, and crabs for consumption or sale.

In earlier times, Bontoc men carried tobacco and matches when hunting wild deer and wild pigs. In the forests they gathered rattan, edible fruits, beeswax, honey, and wild edible or ornamental plants.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that oral tradition tells how *Lumawig* taught the Bontoc their arts and skills, including irrigation of their land, and that oral tradition preserves five great lessons: a man must not steal; one should not gossip; men and women must not commit adultery; one must be temperate in eating and drinking alcoholic drinks; and all people must live simple and industrious lives.

## Language & religion

Bontoc, also called Bontok, is a macrolanguage native to the indigenous Bontoc people of Mountain Province in the northern part of the Philippines. The Bontoc speak Bontoc, Ilocano, and Tagalog. The sources describe five Bontok languages and discuss their phonology, including primary and secondary stress.

The pre-Christian Bontoc belief system centers on a hierarchy of spirits. The highest is a supreme deity called *Intutungcho*. His son, *Lumawig*, descended from the sky, called *chayya*, to marry a Bontoc girl and taught the Bontoc their arts and skills. The Bontoc believe in the *anito*, spirits of the dead who are omnipresent and must be constantly consoled. A seer, called an *insup-ok*, intercedes when someone is sick through evil spirits. The sources also describe *Lumawig* as the supreme god and cultural hero, son of *Kabunian*, and mention sacred sites associated with him and other Bontoc deities. Christianity contributed to changes in the former dormitory-centered social arrangements.
