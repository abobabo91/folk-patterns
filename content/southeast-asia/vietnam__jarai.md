## Overview

The Jarai, also called Dega, are an Austronesian indigenous people living in Vietnam’s Central Highlands, especially Gia Lai and Kon Tum Provinces, with smaller populations in Đắk Lắk Province, and in Cambodia’s northeastern Ratanakiri Province. The name Jarai is glossed as “People of the Waterfalls” or “People of the Flowing River.” The Jarai language belongs to the Malayo-Polynesian branch of the Austronesian family and is related to Cham and Rade. The sources give differing population figures: approximately 332,558 Jarai speakers in one account and approximately 530,000 speakers in another. Jarai culture is represented through matrilineal village organization, animist beliefs involving the Sacred Sword and the Kings of Fire, Water and Wind, distinctive music, dance, funeral architecture and oral traditions.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do state that Jarai people identify each person with a matrilineage and that matrilineal descent can involve the inheritance of property or titles.

### Architecture

Jarai villages traditionally contain between 50 and 500 people and are laid out in a square. Single-occupancy dwellings or communal longhouses, called *roong*, are arranged around a village centre. The centre often contains a communal house, a well, a volleyball net and a rice mill.

Traditional houses are made of bamboo and stand one metre above the ground. More durable wooden houses with steel roofs have become popular. Houses are oriented from north to south and are built in a place considered acceptable to local spirits. They are established according to the matrilineal clan: after marriage, a daughter lives in her mother’s house with her husband, and her own daughter continues this arrangement. A house can be as long as 50 metres, although homes for nuclear families are also common in modern times. In Ratanakiri villages without electricity, small generators are widely used.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do describe traditional household furnishings, including benches and kitchen objects made from wood and bamboo. Modern additions include beds and televisions. A rice mill is often located at the village centre, and small generators are used in villages without electricity.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They do mention wooden pillars placed around traditional tombs, some topped with crude carvings representing spiritual guardians, but these are funerary objects rather than personal adornment.

## Music & performance

Music is a distinctive element of Jarai culture. Village nights and gatherings inside the house of a clan are animated by ancestral music performed with gongs, xylophones such as *t'rưng*, zithers, the mouth fiddle *k'ni* and other traditional instruments. Many of these instruments are made from wood and bamboo.

The Jarai *Trova* is a composition improvised by a musician. In it, the performer tells of the daily challenges of Jarai life while the clan drinks *Srah Phien*, a jar liqueur made from fermented rice. The occasion also provides a setting in which children learn ancient stories about the jungles and ancestral values of Jarai culture. The music and dance are described as monotonous and nostalgic, creating a close relationship with the jungle and the natural environment.

In 1996, Dock Rmah, a prominent Jarai musician living in the United States, received a Folk Heritage Award from the North Carolina Arts Council.

## Dance & theatre

Dance accompanies Jarai music in village and clan-house gatherings. The sources describe Jarai music and dance as distinctive, monotonous and nostalgic, and associate them with the jungle and the natural environment.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe wedding and funeral practices, religious rites and ceremonies associated with Jarai spiritual leaders.

Jarai society is matrilineal. The mother takes the initiative in arranging the marriage of her daughters, and the husband is expected to live in the house of his mother-in-law. Intermarriage with other ethnic groups is described as common, especially where villages are close. In Ratanakiri Province, Jarai people especially intermarry with Tampuan people. Education in Phnom Penh and Ho Chi Minh City has contributed to increasing intermarriage and multilingual families.

Traditional funerals involve small tomb huts containing the possessions of the deceased and offerings. Wooden pillars with crude carvings are placed around the tomb. The burial ceremony is extremely expensive and usually involves the sacrifice of water buffaloes and cows. In Ratanakiri, large animals are increasingly replaced by large objects such as motorcycles, and some deceased people are buried with their motorbike. If the family cannot afford the ceremony, it can be postponed for several years.

After a number of years, the tomb is abandoned. This final ceremony marks the point at which death becomes final and the deceased person’s spirit is released. It can also release a widow for remarriage.

## Foodways

They do identify *Srah Phien*, a jar liqueur made from fermented rice, which is consumed during performances of the Jarai *Trova*.

Funeral ceremonies traditionally involve the sacrifice of water buffaloes and cows. In Ratanakiri, this practice is being replaced by the use of large objects, including motorcycles.

## Oral tradition & literature

Jarai legends are set in the jungles and are described as useful for understanding Jarai culture and history. A recurring element is the story of three kings—Fire, Water and Wind—and a Sacred Sword that came down from heaven and gave the Dega Jarai people great powers. The King of Fire is said to live in Vietnam and to rely on power derived from religious rites. The kings are not political figures but spiritual leaders with shamanic powers.

The sources also state that some elders say the Jarai were born at the Annamite Range and migrated south, dividing into different groups. During performances of *Trova*, musicians tell of the challenges of daily Jarai life, while children learn ancient stories about the jungles and ancestral values.

## Language & religion

Jarai is a Chamic language within the Malayo-Polynesian branch of the Austronesian language family. It is related to Cham and Rade and has also been connected with Malay. The language is spoken in Vietnam and Cambodia, where dialects can be mutually unintelligible. Named dialect groups include Jarai Pleiku, Jarai Cheoreo, Jarai ARáp, Jarai H’dRung and Jarai Tbuan. Related groups listed by the source include HRoi, M’dhur and Hàlang.

Vietnamese Jarai uses a Latin-based script related to Vietnamese spelling, while Cambodian Jarai has no writing system or Khmer-script orthography. A Jarai writing system based on the Vietnamese alphabet was introduced during French Indochina. Christian missionaries later used the orthography to translate the Bible into Jarai.

Traditional Jarai religion is animism. Objects, places and creatures are believed to possess distinctive spiritual qualities. Religious life centres on the Sacred Sword and the spiritual figures of the King of Fire, King of Water and King of Wind. Animism is linked to the jungle and includes animal sacrifices intended to appease spirits. Christianity, Buddhism and Islam have also been associated with Jarai communities in the sources.
