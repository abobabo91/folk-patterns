## Overview

The Bisaya, or Bisayas, are an Austronesian ethnic group indigenous to East Malaysia and Brunei. In Malaysia, their settlements are concentrated in Sabah’s Interior Division, especially on the Klias Peninsula and along the Padas and Klias riverbanks, extending toward the coastal estuary in Beaufort, Kuala Penyu, and Sipitang; communities are also found in the Federal Territory of Labuan and in the Lawas and Limbang districts of Sarawak. In 1970, an estimated 14,000 Bisaya lived in Sabah and 3,312 in Sarawak. The Sabah Bisaya language is closely related to Tatana, while Sarawak and Bruneian dialects share substantial features. Bisaya life has been shaped by farming, fishing, river and coastal settlement, handicraft production, music, dance, festivals, and changing Muslim, Christian, and traditional religious practices.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Songket* appears in modern Bisaya attire as a shorter contemporary sarong made with gold thread.

*Traditional weaving* in Sarawak Bisaya craft is associated with motifs drawn from flora and fauna. The source gives no individual motif names or detailed technical description.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional Bornean Bisaya clothing from Sabah and Sarawak is predominantly black, combined with a red, green, or yellow sash. Garments may feature bold stitching, silver or gold embroidery, and traditional silver buttons. Among Bisaya in Beaufort, women wear the *timbuku*, a “bun” headdress, while men wear the *sigar*, compared in the source with the *tengkolok* headgear. In Lawas and Limbang, men commonly wear the *mianai*, while women wear the *masuk* and *kebamban*. These garments are used at ceremonies, celebrations, thanksgiving rituals, weddings, social events, and the *Adau Janang Gayuh* festival. In Brunei, the *kebamban limo* is a traditional outfit specifically for bridegrooms. Modern attire includes shorter sarongs made of *songket*, gold thread, beaded headscarves, and other accessories.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Bisaya handicrafts use bamboo, rattan, sago palm, wood, animal skins, and *Pandanus tectorius*. The *kalambigas* is placed on a water buffalo’s neck so the animal can pull *rumbia* during agricultural work. The *lasok* is a basket for crop yields and other necessities, while the *salihid* is a bamboo fish trap. The *takiding*, also called *saging*, is a woven basket with shoulder straps used to carry or store fruits, vegetables, and rice. A *nyiru* is a winnowing basket used in agriculture and in cultural performances and ceremonies. Named weapons include the *andiban*, a spear; *parang*, a large knife; *sumpit*, a blowgun; and *kris*, a dagger.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Bisaya clothing may include intricate beadwork, beaded accessories, silver buttons, silver and gold embroidery, and beaded headscarves. The *timbuku* and *sigar* are named head coverings or headdresses. The *berinai* wedding ceremony involves applying henna to the bride’s nails and fingers as a symbol of marriage.

## Music & performance

Traditional Bisaya music, particularly in Sabah and Sarawak, is characterized by gong-based ensembles and bamboo percussion linked to communal celebrations. The principal named instruments are the *kulintangan*, a gong-chime; brass gongs; *gambang*, a wooden xylophone; and *gendang*, drums. The *Kulintangan* festival includes a *gendang* competition and competitions involving other traditional musical instruments. During the *Kaamatan* celebration in Sabah, *kulintangan* performances take place at the Hongkod Koisaan hall.

Music is also associated with dance and harvest observances. *Lilibu*, a traditional dance of the agricultural Sabah Bisaya community in Beaufort, is accompanied by singing connected with the rice harvest and gratitude. Before Islamisation, it was performed to appease the spirit of rice. The *Alai Bubu* dance is known by several other names, including *Bubu Mengalai*, *Main Lukah*, and *Sayau Salahid*. Its source tradition concerns a “dancing” fish trap. Spiritual chants and elements associated with spirituality and black magic were abandoned after prohibitions introduced by the Sabah Islamic Religious Affairs Department in the 1970s; a revival took place in the 1980s, but only a few elderly Bisaya were said to remember the earlier chants.

## Dance & theatre

Named Bisaya dances include *Alai Bubu*, *Lilibu*, and the children’s dances *Anak Kuda* and *Liliput/Tumutu*. *Alai Bubu* is popular among Bisayas in Beaufort, Lawas, and Limbang and originates from a myth about a “dancing” fish trap. Its earlier spiritual elements and chants were abandoned after prohibitions in the 1970s. *Lilibu* is associated with the rice harvest and was formerly connected with appeasing the spirit of rice. *Berasik* is described as a form of traditional healing intended to expel disturbances believed to cause illness or affliction through spirits.

## Festivals & rituals

The Sarawak Bisaya celebrate the annual *Babulang* festival, which includes music, dance, traditional costumes, and water buffalo races. Sabah Bisaya are associated with the *Adau Janang Gayuh* celebration and the *Kulintangan* festival. The latter includes traditional clothing competitions, proa racing, tug of war, rowing, traditional kite flying, top spinning, *gendang* and other musical-instrument competitions, and traditional foods such as *ambuyat*. A state-level Bisaya festival has been held in Sabah since 2024, with competitions in traditional music and dance. During the *Kaamatan* celebration in Sabah, *kulintangan* performances occur at the Hongkod Koisaan hall. Bisaya in Kuala Penyu celebrate the *Pesta Rumbia*, a festival centered on the sago palm.

Bisaya wedding customs in Sabah and Sarawak include *merisik*, *bertunang*, and *berinai*. *Merisik* is conducted by the male side to investigate the prospective bride’s background, establish whether she is already married, introduce the families, and negotiate a dowry. The bride’s family is given three days to decide whether to accept the groom. *Bertunang* follows agreement between the parties, and traditionally up to a year may be given to the male side to prepare the engagement. The wedding procession includes traditional clothing and the carrying of the groom, as well as *pusing naga*, in which a pillar is circled three times.

## Foodways

*Ambuyat* is described as the main traditional dish of the Bisaya and Bruneian Malays in Brunei and Malaysia, with origins in Bisaya culture. It is especially popular among Bisaya in Kuala Penyu District of Sabah. The dish is made from sago flour obtained by processing the trunk of the *rumbia* tree, *Metroxylon sagu*, locally called *ampulung*. Bisaya cuisine includes other sago-based foods, including *umbut*, the heart of the palm. *Kelupis* is another snack associated with the Bisaya as well as the Bruneian Malays, Kedayan, and Dusun. *Bubur kacang hijau*, mung bean porridge, is eaten during Ramadan for *iftar* by Bisaya Muslims in Labuan and Sabah.

Agriculture supplies rice, sago, coconut, ginger, maize, fruits, and vegetables, while fishing, hunting, animal husbandry, and forest products also contribute to subsistence and market exchange. The sources identify Muslim and Christian Bisaya communities.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe the *Tatanak* as a popular Bisaya legend in which Lok Batata and his six siblings are descendants of the Bisaya ethnic group in Borneo. A separate Bisaya legend, recorded by the British colonial administrator and resident of North Borneo Derek Headly in 1950, explains the name “Bisaya” through the exclamation “Bisai-yah!” attributed to the sultan of Brunei. The sources also discuss traditions connecting the Bisaya with the “10 Datus of Borneo” legends of the *Maragtas* and with the *Tarsilah Brunei*, a history and genealogy of the sultanate of Brunei. In Sarawak, digital platforms have been promoted to showcase Bisaya cultural events and preserve heritage in response to challenges associated with modernisation.

## Language & religion

The Bisaya language of Sabah is very closely related to Tatana, described as one of the Dusun dialects, and shares 90% intelligibility with it, mainly because of intermarriage. It has 58% lexical similarity with Sarawak Bisaya dialects and 57%–59% with the Bruneian dialect. Sarawak’s Bisaya dialect shares at least 60% of its features with the Bruneian dialect.

The majority of contemporary Bisaya in Brunei, Sabah, and Labuan are Muslim, while Sarawak Bisaya are mostly Christian. Christian Bisaya, especially in Limbang, belong either to the Catholic Church or the Borneo Evangelical Church. Earlier Bisaya belief was animist, emphasizing nature spirits, ancestors, balance with nature, and care for the dead. It also included oath-taking to settle major disputes and belief in *ipon*, a spirit with the power to hunt and kill wrongdoers. Conversion to Islam began with Lok Batata in the 14th century. Before this, local Bisaya governments were influenced by Buddhist and Hindu beliefs.
