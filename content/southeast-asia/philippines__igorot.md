## Overview

The Igorot are the uncolonized indigenous highlanders of the Cordillera Central in northern Luzon, numbering about 1.8 million across eleven ethnolinguistic groups. They speak related Austronesian languages and practice a layered religion mixing Christianity with animist ancestor veneration. Their material culture — backstrap weaving, wood carving, bronze work, and tattooing — survived intact into modern times. Their social life centers on rice-terrace farming, prestige feasting, and peace-pact diplomacy.

## Material culture

Igorot material identity runs through woven textiles, carved wood, bronze, and gold rather than fine ceramics, with every object marked by precise regional and group distinction.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Backstrap weaving produces narrow-panel skirts and loincloths whose indigo grounds, stripe order, and small supplementary-weft figures identify the wearer's group and municipality precisely.

- **Woman's wrap-around skirt** (*lufid / tapis*) — Woven on a backstrap loom in narrow panels with broad indigo grounds flanked by fine warp stripes that identify the wearer's municipality.
- **Men's loincloth** (*bahag*) — A long woven band passed between the legs and belted, worn shirtless in ceremony as an assertion of tribal precedent; color and stripe order distinguish Kalinga from Bontok.
- **Bontok man's loincloth** (*wanes*) — Dense warp-stripe end-panels whose stripe number and spacing traced rank and ward affiliation.
- **Warp-striped cloth with blocky figures** (*ginaspala / sinamaki*) — Kankanaey and Benguet cloth with supplementary-weft banding producing small human, lizard, and star figures across indigo-and-red grounds.
- **Resist-dyed mortuary blanket** (*ikat death blanket / pinagpagan*) — Itneg and related Kalinga blankets with blurred-edge anthropomorphic and lizard figures that wrap the corpse during the seated wake.

### Clothing & dress

Daily dress before the twentieth century was minimal and regionally precise — men shirtless in loincloth, women in wrap skirt and often bare above the waist — with elaborate layered dress for ceremony.

- **Aristocratic rank belt** (*hape / ginuttu*) — A belt of shell discs worn by Ifugao kadangyan (aristocrats) with blankets of rank in ceremonial contexts.
- **Men's woven hip bag** (*pinagpagan / butong*) — A carrying cloth worn slung across the body, the Ifugao version serving as a prestige textile.
- **Lidded basket-hat** — A small woven hat serving double duty as a tobacco and betel container.
- **Polished gourd helmet-hat** (*tabungaw*) — A bottle-gourd helmet of Abra and Ilocos, lacquered orange and lined with cloth, with a rattan chinstrap.
- **Basketry cap** (*suklang*) — A woven cap, often feathered or crested with boar tusks for men who have taken heads.

### Architecture

Houses are raised on posts with rat guards, windowless and joined without nails so they can be dismantled and moved; the Ifugao bale sets the reference form.

- **Ifugao house** (*bale / fale*) — A windowless one-room pyramidal-roofed structure of pine and cogon thatch, raised on four posts capped with disc-shaped rat guards, reached by removable ladder.
- **Kankanaey rice granary** (*agamang*) — A raised storage structure on rat-guarded posts, graded by cost and status alongside domestic house types like the innagamang and binangi.
- **Kalinga house** (*furoy / buloy / phoyoy*) — Single-roomed and post-elevated, sometimes twenty to thirty feet high, octagonal for the wealthy and square for others.
- **Isneg sheltered undercroft** (*linong / sidong*) — An open area beneath the house that shelters the abulor, a four-post shed where jars of basi are half-buried in earth.
- **Rice-terrace landscape** — Stone- and mud-walled pond-field terraces of Banaue, Hungduan, Mayoyao and Kiangan, the region's greatest constructed artifact.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Material identity runs through wood, bronze, and imported stoneware; the carved bulul rice-guardian figure is the signature object.

- **Rice-granary guardian figure** (*bulul*) — A seated or standing figure carved in narra or ipil and consecrated with blood and rice wine, the signature object of Cordilleran identity.
- **Carved prestige bench** (*hagabi*) — A massive hardwood lounging bench with carved animal-head terminals, commissioned only by kadangyan as proof of a prestige feast.
- **Ritual storage box** (*punamhan*) — A wooden box used for ritual purposes and household storage.
- **Bronze flat gong** (*gangsa*) — Cast by lost-wax process, played in graded ensembles, individually named and treated as prestige property.
- **Head-axe** (*aliwa / gaman*) — A forged tool with distinctive hooked poke, created by blacksmiths.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Gold jewelry runs back to the Iron Age; Kalinga beadwork is the most exuberant surviving practice; tattooing marked life achievement and ritual status.

- **Double-lobed pendant** (*lingling-o*) — An omega-shaped ear or neck pendant of jade, shell, or gold with Iron Age ancestry, reappearing as heirloom gold ling-ling-o.
- **Beaded chest ornament** (*sipattal*) — A broad Kalinga ornament of massed mother-of-pearl plates, agate, carnelian and glass beads, worn with brass and copper armlets.
- **Shell-disc belt** (*ginutu*) — A Kalinga belt of shell discs worn with other body ornaments as a marker of status.
- **Body tattoo** (*fa'tek / batek*) — Hand-tapped with soot and a citrus-thorn needle; Bontok chak-lag' marks a head-taker's chest, pong'o marks arms, and fa'tek covers other work.
- **Boar-tusk ornament** — Boar tusks and hornbill beaks mark men's ritual standing and achievement.

## Music & performance

The defining sound is bronze on bronze: gangsa gongs are played in named ensembles of six or more, while bamboo flutes and jaw harps fill the intimate register.

- **Bronze handheld gong** (*gangsa*) — Played in graded ensembles, one per player, in two styles: gangsa pattung struck with a beater while dancing, and gangsa toppaya palm-muted and seated.
- **Kalinga nose flute** (*tongali*) — A bamboo flute played through the nose in intimate musical contexts.
- **Bamboo jaw harp** (*kulibaw / ab-afiw*) — Used in courtship, played as part of bamboo ensemble traditions.
- **Bundled panpipe** (*diwdiw-as*) — A stopped-pipe ensemble played one per person in village music-making.
- **Stamping tubes for healing** (*tongatong*) — Bamboo percussion struck on stone for ritual healing purposes.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is communal and gong-driven, without masked or puppet theatre; each municipality has a distinct style, and bird-mimicry dances answer the gong pattern.

- **Community wedding dance** (*tayaw*) — A Kankanaey dance performed at weddings by the community.
- **File dance with local variation** (*pat-tong / pattong / balangbang*) — A dance of Mountain Province with a distinct style per municipality, known in modern form as balangbang.
- **Raised-arm eagle dance** (*banga*) — A Kalinga and Bontok bird-mimicry dance in which raised-arm gestures answer the gong pattern; women balance stacked clay pots as a display piece.
- **High-energy rigid dance** (*torayan*) — A Balangao accelerated dance performed in a rigid T-pose that deliberately contrasts with fluid eagle dances of neighbors.
- **Celebratory mass dance** (*bendian / ad-adivay*) — An Ibaloi victory dance now performed as entertainment at cañao feasts by hundreds of men and women in circular formation.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual year is keyed to the rice cycle; prestige feasting is the central institution, and the Kalinga bodong peace pact remains the region's functioning inter-village law.

- **Rice-cycle observance** (*tungo*) — Ifugao rest-day observances punctuate stages from field opening through transplanting to the ani harvest, when the hudhud is chanted.
- **Prestige feast raising families to aristocracy** (*uyauy / hagabi*) — Ifugao rites that raise a family into the kadangyan class and require the carving and hauling of a hagabi bench.
- **Multi-week prestige feast** (*peshit / pedit*) — An Ibaloi prestige feast lasting weeks and consuming dozens of sacrificed animals.
- **Headhunt celebration feast** (*say-am / pildap*) — An Isneg feast following successful headhunt or major occasion, lasting one to five days and hosted by the wealthy; the pildap is the poor household equivalent.
- **Inter-village peace pact** (*bodong*) — A Kalinga pact sealed with gong-playing, oratory, and exchange of blankets and gongs, functioning as the region's inter-village law.

## Foodways

Wet-field heirloom rice from the terraces is the prestige staple; heavily salted and smoke-cured pork (etag) is the region's great preserve and flavor base.

- **Terraced heirloom rice** (*tinawon*) — Wet-field rice from the terraces, the prestige staple supplemented by swidden rice, sweet potato, taro, millet, yam and maize.
- **Signature ritual dish** (*pinikpikan*) — Chicken beaten before slaughter to draw blood to the skin, then singed and boiled with etag and ginger, with the beating originating as augury.
- **Salted and cured pork** (*etag / kiniing*) — Pork salted heavily and smoke-cured or buried in jars for months, the region's great preserve and the flavor base of everything else.
- **Rice and blood sausage** (*pinuneg*) — A sausage combining rice and blood as a ritual and everyday food.
- **Rice wine or sugarcane wine** (*tapuy / bayah / basi*) — Tapuy is rice wine brewed with bubod starter cakes and poured for the bulul and ancestors; basi is Isneg sugarcane wine aged in half-buried jars.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Ifugao hudhud is the region's monumental form: a long chanted narrative of two hundred episodes performed during harvest, weeding and wake.

- **Epic chant narrative** (*hudhud*) — An Ifugao long chanted narrative of some two hundred episodes about culture-heroes like Aliguyon and Bugan, performed during harvest, weeding and wake.
- **Kalinga epic** (*ullalim*) — A Kalinga epic counterpart to the hudhud, centered on the hero Banna.
- **Ritual hair-cutting chant** (*hudhud di kolot*) — An Ifugao chant performed for hair-cutting rites as part of ritual literature.
- **Priest's myth-recitation and prayer** (*baki*) — Ifugao myth-recitations through which a mumbaki priest names the ancestors and deities in order.
- **Improvised sung debate** (*liwliwa*) — A sung debate between a man and a woman, maintained by Kankanaey and Ibaloi as improvised verse.

## Language & religion

Eleven related Austronesian languages belong to the Northern Luzon subgroup; religion is layered between Christianity and persistent animist practice.

- **Regional lingua franca** (*Ilocano*) — Serves as the regional language across dialect boundaries, alongside Tagalog and English.
- **Ifugao pantheon** (*Kabunian*) — The supreme deity of the Ifugao animist pantheon, complemented by ancestor spirits.
- **Ancestor spirit** (*anito*) — Spirits venerated in animist practice, invoked at planting, illness and death.
- **Ritual priest** (*mumbaki / anituwan*) — Priesthoods that still officiate at planting, illness and death, maintaining animist ritual practice.
- **Divination practice** — Chicken and pig-bile divination remains part of animist religious practice alongside Christian belief.
