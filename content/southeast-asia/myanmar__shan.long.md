## Overview

The Shan are a Tai-speaking people of eastern Myanmar, especially the Shan Plateau. *Tai* is a name used within the community; *Tai Yai* is another name used in Thailand. Shan State is also home to other peoples, so an object collected there is not automatically Shan. The 34 objects in this atlas were selected because the collecting museums identify a Shan maker, use, or cultural attribution. Most come from the British Museum and the Museum of World Culture in Gothenburg, Sweden. They show particular collections, places and periods, not a complete inventory of Shan life.

The [British Museum's account of Myanmar's landscapes](https://www.britishmuseum.org/blog/abundance-riches-myanmars-many-landscapes) describes the Shan states as historically distinct principalities in a plateau region of rice cultivation and exchange. Political borders and the broad label “Shan” should not erase local differences. This page stays close to what its selected objects and linked sources can show.

## Material culture

### Textiles, clothing & adornment

The collection contains embroidered caps, several other hats, a jacket, shoes and three carrying bags. One British Museum [skullcap](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_As-7161) has colored embroidery; another cap and two broad hats show that headwear was not a single fixed form. The selected bags also differ: the British Museum records shoulder bags, while the Gothenburg museum catalogues an embroidered bag and a small decorated bag. Their patterns can be studied on the objects themselves without treating one design as a universal Shan motif.

The [British Museum's jacket](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_As1919-0717-213) is one documented garment, not evidence for every community's dress. The selected objects do not establish a standard skirt pattern, a prescribed set of jewelry, or a single male and female costume. The photographic portrait linked in the media section is a historical image of one sitter, made by a European photographer; it is not a garment made by Shan artisans.

### Lacquer, pottery & household goods

A [lacquer betel box](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_1998-0723-136) and a lacquered jar show container making and decoration. Other records include a balance and a small weight. The Gothenburg museum's Europeana records identify a [teapot](https://www.europeana.eu/item/91608/SMVK_VKM_objekt_6282) and a [gourd-shaped earthenware vessel](https://www.europeana.eu/item/91608/SMVK_VKM_objekt_6211). Two British Museum water bottles add different vessel forms. Together these objects document storage, serving and carrying, while their catalogue entries often leave the exact user and setting unspecified.

### Metalwork, arms & ritual objects

The selection includes a [dao](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_As1962-06-6-a), sheaths, a powder flask and other weapon-related pieces. Their museum records establish form and attribution, but do not turn them into evidence of a single Shan military tradition. A [bell](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_As-7182) is described by the British Museum as used by Shan people. Gothenburg's [gong](https://www.europeana.eu/item/91608/SMVK_VKM_objekt_6212) provides another sound-making object; neither record by itself establishes an ensemble or a repertoire.

The [British Museum's wooden body stamp](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_As1931-1205-2) was used to mark out a tattoo design. The museum notes red ink around its magic squares and a spirit figure in the design. Its maker attribution is Shan, while its collection history includes a Burmese owner; these are different facts. An inscribed protective charm, a ceremonial doll and several small figures show further objects with ritual or representational uses. Some figure attributions are qualified in the catalogues, and their precise ceremonial settings should not be inferred from appearance alone.

## Music, dance & festivals

The gong and bell are material evidence of sound-making, with no documented tune or performance in these catalogue records. A [Commons photograph of a Shan peacock dance](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Shan_peacock_dance.jpg) documents one named dance performance.

A second [Commons image of Poi Sang Long](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Poi_Sang_Long_Festiva04.JPG) describes a Shan novice ordination celebration in Thailand. The image's own description connects the procession, decorated children and temple ordination. The celebration provides a specific example of Buddhist practice across the border; it should not be confused with every festival held in Shan State.

## Food & daily life

The [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/blog/abundance-riches-myanmars-many-landscapes) identifies rice cultivation on the Shan Plateau. The teapot, water bottles and betel box suggest particular practices of serving and consumption, though a container alone cannot tell us a recipe or the identity of its user. The linked [Shan htamin photograph](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Shan_Htamin.jpg) records one rice dish in Shan State and identifies where the photograph was taken. Its location in a Pa'O administered area is a reminder that “from Shan State” and “made by Shan people” are not equivalent.

## Language, writing & Buddhism

Shan belongs to the Tai language family. The [Library of Congress's Tai manuscript collection](https://www.loc.gov/collections/tai-painted-manuscripts/about-this-collection/) places Shan manuscripts alongside related Tai language traditions and explains the importance of Buddhist writing in the region. A [British Museum manuscript from the Shan States](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/image/1613133819) contains Shan, Burmese and Pali in Shan script; its contents include summaries of Buddhist suttas. It gives a concrete example of multilingual religious writing rather than a claim that all Shan people used the same texts or were literate in the same script.

The selected tattoo stamp's spirit figure also cautions against describing religious life only through one category. At the same time, the stamp and a few figures cannot support a complete account of local belief or ritual. The sources here are strongest on portable objects, a manuscript and two photographed performances. They are thinner on buildings, spoken stories and present-day community practice.
