## Overview

The Javanese are the largest ethnic group in Southeast Asia and fourth-largest Muslim ethnic group worldwide, native to Java's volcanic plains and river valleys. Their culture is a synthesis of indigenous animism, a thousand years of Hindu-Buddhist court civilization, and Sufi-influenced Islam absorbed from coastal port towns. The refined *alus* aesthetic of the royal courts has shaped batik, gamelan, wayang, and dance across the entire archipelago. Rice-centered foodways, elaborate life-cycle rituals centered on communal *slametan* meals, and a continuous literary tradition from 9th-century poetry to modern sung forms define daily and ceremonial life.

## Material culture

Javanese material culture is built on natural fibers and metals—woven cotton and silk, cast bronze, and worked iron—organized by the refined *alus* aesthetic of the courts, with distinct regional variations between the refined Central Javanese interior and the looser, more colorful coastal north.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Batik wax-resist dyeing on cotton is the signature textile art, refined over centuries into hand-drawn and stamped techniques that define Central Javanese dress and carry symbolic motifs tied to life events and social status.

- **Hand-drawn batik** (*batik tulis*) — Each fine cloth takes months to complete using a *canting* copper pen, remaining the prestige technique of royal court workshops.
- **Stamped batik** (*batik cap*) — Copper block stamping introduced in the mid-19th century enabled mass production of daily-wear cloths at lower cost than hand-drawn work.
- **Wrap cloth** (*kain panjang*) — Long unstitched rectangular cloth about 2.5 meters, worn wrapped around the lower body by both men and women as the standard canvas for batik design.
- **Ceremonial wrap** (*dodot*) — Oversized ceremonial wrap cloth up to four meters, worn by bride and groom in court-style weddings and by court dancers in *bedhaya* and *srimpi*.
- **Striped cloth** (*lurik*) — Narrow-loom hand-woven cotton with lengthwise stripes, historically the daily cloth of Central Javanese villagers and still worn as work jackets.

### Clothing & dress

Formal Central Javanese dress for men combines a batik *kain panjang* wrap with a *beskap* jacket and distinctive regional *blangkon* headcloth, while women wear the fitted *kebaya* blouse over a wrap, with bridal dress featuring elaborate golden headdresses and the *dodot* ceremonial wrap.

- **Formal jacket** (*beskap*) — Formal jacket worn by men with a *kain panjang* wrap and *blangkon* headcloth for ceremonies.
- **Headcloth** (*blangkon*) — Batik headcloth stiffened and pre-tied in distinct regional shapes, with Yogyakarta and Solo each having their own characteristic form.
- **Fitted blouse** (*kebaya*) — Fitted long-sleeved blouse of cotton, voile, or lace worn by women over a breast-cloth and paired with a wrap, inscribed by UNESCO in 2024 as shared Southeast Asian heritage.
- **Striped work jacket** (*surjan*) — Everyday village jacket made from striped *lurik* cloth with a mandarin collar, worn as the casual equivalent of formal *beskap*.
- **Bridal hair bun** (*sanggul*) — Elaborate gilded hair bun ornament worn by brides, often crowned with trembling gold flower ornaments called *cundhuk mentul*.

### Architecture

Javanese houses are classified by roof form, from the high-status *joglo* with its central pyramidal roof and massive teak pillars to the middle-class *limasan* and simple village *kampung* house, all rooted in a Hindu-Buddhist spatial tradition seen in royal *keraton* complexes and tiered-roof mosques.

- **Nobility house** (*joglo*) — The highest-status house type, defined by a steeply pitched central pyramidal *tajug* roof rising over four massive teak *soko guru* pillars, traditionally the dwelling of the *priyayi* nobility.
- **Middle-class house** (*limasan*) — Standard middle-class house with a four-slope hipped roof, intermediate in status between the *joglo* and village house.
- **Open pavilion** (*pendopo*) — Open-sided pavilion with four *soko guru* pillars used as a reception hall in front of a main house or royal palace complex.
- **Menara Kudus Mosque** — Historic mosque with a red-brick Hindu-Javanese *candi*-style minaret tower, using tiered pyramidal *tajug* roofs rather than domes.
- **Bamboo wattle walls** (*gedhek*) — Woven bamboo wall panels used in timber-framed village houses, typically with clay-tiled roofs.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The *keris* wavy-bladed dagger with pattern-welded iron is the signature Javanese metalwork object and spiritual heirloom, while gamelan bronze instruments and earthenware cooking pots form the everyday soundscape and kitchen.

- **Wavy-bladed dagger** (*keris*) — Dagger with a wavy or straight blade bearing distinct pattern-welded *pamor* designs in the iron, regarded as an *ageman* spiritual heirloom and inscribed by UNESCO in 2008.
- **Bronze ensemble** (*gamelan*) — Full ensemble of tuned bronze percussion instruments including *saron* metallophones, *bonang* kettle-gong rows, hanging gongs, and supporting drums and flutes, inscribed by UNESCO in 2021.
- **Bronze metallophone** (*saron*) — Bronze metallophone bars struck with a wooden mallet, a core melodic instrument in the gamelan.
- **Woven bamboo tray** (*besek*) — Round woven bamboo tray used for serving *tumpeng* conical rice and side dishes at communal meals.
- **earthenware cookware** — Cooking pots from Kasongan (Yogyakarta) and large decorative terracotta vessels that have become known since the mid-20th century.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Court and bridal jewelry uses gold and gold-plated silver in elaborate tiered necklaces, forehead ornaments, and hairpins, while everyday adornment remains restrained with simple ear studs and bangles.

- **Hairpins** (*tusuk konde*) — Rows of ornamental hairpins pinning the *sanggul* bridal hair bun in court style.
- **Forehead ornament** (*centhung*) — A five- or seven-pronged gold ornament worn on the bride's forehead as a mark of court status.
- **Trembling flower ornaments** (*cundhuk mentul*) — Set of trembling gold flower ornaments worn upright above the bride's *sanggul* bun.
- **Ear ornaments** (*sumping*) — Ear ornaments derived from Hindu-period temple reliefs, worn as part of court bridal dress.
- **Tiered necklace** (*kalung susun*) — Heavy multi-tiered gold necklace worn by court women and brides as a symbol of status.

## Music & performance

Gamelan bronze percussion ensembles define Javanese music, accompanying court dance, shadow-puppet theatre, and life-cycle rituals in two main tuning systems, with popular modern forms like *langgam Jawa* and *campursari* blending gamelan with sung poetry or Western instruments.

- **Sacred court ensemble** (*gamelan sekaten*) — Archaic sacred ensemble maintained by Yogyakarta and Surakarta courts, played only during the Sekaten festival marking the Prophet's birthday.
- **Five-tone tuning** (*sléndro*) — One of two main gamelan tuning systems, a five-tone scale used in compositions called *gendhing*.
- **Seven-tone tuning** (*pélog*) — Second main gamelan tuning system, a seven-tone scale, with pieces organized in colotomic cycles marked by the great gong.
- **Sung poetry** (*langgam Jawa*) — Javanese-language sung poetry accompanied by gamelan or *kroncong* string ensemble as a popular form.
- **Fusion music** (*campursari*) — 20th-century fusion form blending gamelan tunings with Western instruments, created for modern audiences.

## Dance & theatre

Wayang shadow-puppet theatre with intricately carved buffalo-hide puppets manipulated by a *dalang* narrator is the classical form, while court dance divides into refined female genres like *bedhaya* and stronger male martial forms, with popular village dances like *ronggeng* and *kuda lumping* horse-trance.

- **Shadow-puppet theatre** (*wayang kulit*) — Inscribed by UNESCO in 2008, this all-night theatre uses intricately pierced buffalo-hide puppets narrated and voiced by a *dalang* who also cues the gamelan through stories from the *Ramayana*, *Mahabharata*, and *Panji* cycle.
- **Sacred female court dance** (*bedhaya*) — Refined nine-dancer sacred female dance form of the royal courts, with *Bedhaya Ketawang* from Surakarta performed only once per year.
- **Four-dancer court dance** (*srimpi*) — Refined four-dancer female court dance distinct from the nine-dancer *bedhaya*.
- **Masked lion-peacock dance** (*reog Ponorogo*) — Popular East Javanese masked dance form featuring a dancer wearing an enormous peacock-feather headdress representing a mythical lion-peacock creature.
- **Horse-trance dance** (*kuda lumping*) — Popular village and coastal dance form in which performers ride stick horses and enter a trance state, also called *jaranan*.

## Festivals & rituals

The Javanese lunar calendar creates auspicious days through a 35-day *wetonan* cycle, marking New Year processions, the Sekaten festival with sacred gamelan and mountainous rice processions to mosques, and life-cycle rites centered on the *slametan* communal meal.

- **Javanese new year** (*Satu Suro*) — Javanese New Year on 1 Suro, marked by silent nocturnal processions around *keraton* palace walls and ritual cleansing of heirloom *keris* and gamelan.
- **Prophet's birthday festival** (*Sekaten*) — Week-long festival ending on the Prophet Muhammad's birthday, centered on the courts of Yogyakarta and Surakarta, when the sacred *gamelan sekaten* is sounded.
- **Sacred procession** (*Grebeg Maulud*) — Procession carrying mountainous *gunungan* of rice, vegetables, and cakes from the palace to the Great Mosque during Sekaten festival.
- **Communal meal** (*slametan*) — All-purpose communal meal hosted for any significant life transition and centered on shared *tumpeng* conical rice, the core Javanese ritual form.
- **Seventh-month pregnancy ritual** (*mitoni*) — Life-cycle ritual performed in the seventh month of pregnancy, also called *tingkeban*.

## Foodways

Rice defines Javanese eating, with every meal centered on the yellow turmeric-tinted *tumpeng* conical rice surrounded by side dishes, while regional cooking styles—Central Javanese sweet *gudeg*, East Javanese spiced *rujak cingur*, and ubiquitous *tempeh* and *jamu*—reflect local ingredients and trade influences.

- **Ceremonial rice cone** (*tumpeng*) — Yellow conical rice colored with turmeric and surrounded by side dishes on a woven *besek* tray, the required centerpiece of every *slametan* communal meal.
- **Stewed jackfruit** (*gudeg*) — Yogyakarta signature dish of young jackfruit stewed for hours with palm sugar and coconut milk, served with buffalo-skin crackers in chili sauce, boiled egg, and chicken.
- **Cow's snout salad** (*rujak cingur*) — East Javanese specialty combining boiled cow's snout with vegetables, fruit, and a thick fermented shrimp-paste sauce called *petis*.
- **Soybean cakes** (*tempeh*) — Javanese invention of soybeans bound into cakes by *Rhizopus* mould, documented in 19th-century texts and now the archipelago's most important vegetable protein.
- **Herbal drinks** (*jamu*) — Herbal wellness drinks like turmeric-tamarind *kunyit asam*, ginger *beras kencur*, and bitter *pahitan*, sold door-to-door by *jamu gendong* women and inscribed by UNESCO in 2023.

## Oral tradition & literature

Javanese literature spans from 9th-century Sanskrit-influenced court poems to modern sung poetry in fixed *macapat* meters, with the *Ramayana* and *Mahabharata* epics providing the *lakon* repertoire for wayang puppet theatre and the indigenous *Panji* romances spreading across mainland Southeast Asia.

- **9th-century epic** (*Ramayana Kakawin*) — Old Javanese rendering of the Indian *Ramayana* from around 870 CE, domesticating the epic for local contexts and providing standard material for *wayang kulit* repertoire.
- **12th-century epic** (*Bharatayuddha*) — Old Javanese *Mahabharata* rendering compiled by Mpu Sedah and Mpu Panuluh in 1157 CE, episodes of which remain standard *wayang kulit* material.
- **Indigenous romances** (*Panji cycle*) — Indigenous romances of the prince of Janggala searching for his lost princess Dewi Sekartaji, spread across mainland Southeast Asia and enacted in mask dance and puppet theatre.
- **Encyclopedic court text** (*Serat Centhini*) — Encyclopedic court text compiled in 1814 at the Surakarta court, documenting *tempeh* and other aspects of Javanese life and knowledge.
- **Fixed-meter poetry** (*macapat*) — Sung poetry meters (*Dhandhanggula*, *Asmarandana*, *Pangkur*, *Sinom*) each with prescribed syllable and vowel patterns that convey specific emotional registers.

## Language & religion

Javanese language is organized into three speech levels—*ngoko* (familiar), *madya* (middle), and *krama* (high)—marking social competence, while around 97% of Javanese are Sunni Muslim with a syncretic *Kejawèn* practice fusing animism, Hindu-Buddhist cosmology, and Sufi Islam.

- **Familiar speech level** (*ngoko*) — Informal speech level used with friends and family in Javanese language.
- **High speech level** (*krama*) — High formal speech level used with superiors and strangers, correct deployment of which marks social competence.
- **Syncretic mysticism** (*Kejawèn*) — Syncretic mystical practice fusing animism, Hindu-Buddhist cosmology, and Sufi Islam, expressed through meditation, ancestor veneration, and pilgrimage to sacred graves including those of the *Wali Songo*.
- **Nine saints** (*Wali Songo*) — Nine Islamic saints whose graves are sites of pilgrimage in *Kejawèn* practice and veneration.
- **Sea goddess** (*Nyai Roro Kidul*) — Tutelary sea-goddess of the southern ocean in *Kejawèn* belief, ritually linked to the Sultans of Yogyakarta.
