## Overview

The Khasi are an Austroasiatic ethnic group of Meghalaya in north-eastern India, with significant populations in Assam and parts of Bangladesh. They are concentrated particularly in the Khasi Hills and the eastern part of Meghalaya, where they constitute 78.3% of the region’s population; they comprise about 48% of Meghalaya’s total population. According to the 2011 Census of India, over 1.41 million Khasi lived in Meghalaya, while their population reached 35 thousand in Assam and around 85 thousand in Bangladesh’s Sylhet Division, especially in Jaflong. Khasi is an Austroasiatic language, and the Khasi are notable for their matrilineal society, indigenous religion, distinctive dress, weaving, oral mythology, and living root bridges.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Ryndia** — *Ryndia* is a hand-spun, hand-woven fabric produced in Meghalaya and passed down through generations as an occupation. It is worn by men and women and is culturally associated with the Khasi people. It occurs in an undyed off-white state and in a maroon and mustard plaid design.

**Eri silk** — Eri silk is described as “peace silk” because its production process is considered non-violent. Weaving is primarily done by women, working from home or in clusters, and production in Meghalaya proceeds from start to finish through women’s work.

The sources used do not document additional Khasi pattern-bearing textile traditions.

### Clothing & dress

The traditional Khasi male garment is the *Jymphong*, a longish sleeveless coat without a collar, fastened at the front with thongs. On ceremonial occasions, men wear a Jymphong with a sarong and an ornamental waist-band, and may also wear a turban. Most male Khasis have adopted western attire in everyday life.

The traditional female dress is the *Jainsem* or *Dhara*. Both are elaborate garments made from several pieces of cloth and give the body a cylindrical shape. The Jainsem consists of two pieces of material fastened at each shoulder, while the Dhara consists of a single piece fastened at each shoulder. On ceremonial occasions, women may wear a crown of silver or gold. A spike or peak is fixed to the back of the crown, corresponding to the feathers worn by menfolk. The sources do not document Khasi footwear or separate everyday head coverings.

## Festivals & rituals

Khasi mythology traces the tribe’s original abode to *Ki Hynñiewtrep*, “The Seven Huts.” According to the myth, U Blei Trai Kynrad, “God, the Lord Master,” distributed the human race into 16 heavenly families, called *Khadhynriew Trep*. Seven families remained on earth while nine resided in heaven. A heavenly ladder resting on Lum Sohpetbneng Peak, in the present-day Ri-Bhoi district, once enabled people to travel freely to heaven. This access ended after people were tricked into cutting a divine tree at Lum Diengiei Peak, in the present-day East Khasi Hills district.

The rooster is a symbol because the Khasis believe that it aroused God and cleared the path for the creation of the Universe. It represents morning, a new beginning, and a new sunrise. Before the arrival of Christian missionaries, almost all Khasi people practised an indigenous tribal religion. The sources used do not provide an annual festival calendar or descriptions of birth, coming-of-age, funeral, or named wedding rituals.

## Foodways

The main crops produced by the Khasi are betel leaves, areca nut, oranges, pineapples, plums, litchis, local varieties of rice, and vegetables. The sources used do not document named Khasi dishes, cooking methods, ceremonial foods, tea or coffee traditions, or dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

Khasi mythology includes the account of Ki Hynñiewtrep, the 16 heavenly families, the heavenly ladder at Lum Sohpetbneng Peak, and the divine tree at Lum Diengiei Peak. The myth is presented as a metaphor in which nature and trees manifest the divine on Earth, while their destruction severs ties with the Divine.

The Khasi people were primarily an oral-language community and had no script of their own until the arrival of Welsh missionaries. The Khasi-language sources list Khasi fables, folk-tales, folk songs, and tales among the documented literary materials. Further reading includes *Khasi fables and folk-tales* by Hamlet Bareh, *Khasi Folk Tales* by Mrs. Rafy, and *Khasi Folk Songs & Tales* by Desmond L. Kharmawphlang and A. R. Tripathi. The sources do not describe particular named tales beyond the mythology summarized above.

## Language & religion

Khasi is an Austroasiatic language spoken primarily in Meghalaya. Its closest relatives are identified as Pnar, Lyngngam, and War in the Khasic group of the Shillong Plateau. Khasi is written in the Latin script. Welsh missionaries first used the Bengali script before adopting the Roman script for the Sohra dialect; Thomas Jones arrived in Sohra on 22 June 1841 and wrote down the local language in the Latin script. In 1842, the *First Khasi Reader* and a *Rhodd Mam* catechism became the first Khasi publications. The language uses a 23-letter alphabet, including ï, ñ, and the digraph ng.

Khasi society is matrilineal. Indigenous Khasi religion, *Ka Niam Khasi*, is monotheistic, with U Blei Nongthaw as the one, formless supreme creator. Around 83.14% of the Khasi tribe are Christian, mainly Presbyterian and Catholic, while 15.41% follow Ka Niam Khasi. Catholicism, Anglicanism, and Presbyterianism are among the Christian denominations followed by Khasis. Smaller groups follow Hinduism, Buddhism, Sunni Islam, or no particular faith.
