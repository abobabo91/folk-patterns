## Overview

The Mising people, also called Miri people, are a Tibeto-Burman ethnic group living mostly in Assam and Arunachal Pradesh in Northeast India. Their population is concentrated around Dhemaji, Lakhimpur, and Majuli, with communities also recorded in Sonitpur, Tinsukia, Dibrugarh, Sivasagar, Charaideo, Jorhat, and Golaghat. The 2001 census recorded 587,310 Misings in Assam, while Mising organisations estimated more than 1.2 million in 2012. Their language belongs to the Tani languages and the Tibeto-Burman language group. Mising culture combines riverine agriculture, weaving, distinctive clothing, folk songs, dance, ritual festivals, and oral historical traditions. The community’s name Mising is an endonym meaning “man of the soil”; Miri is an exonym, while Tani means “man.”

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Weaving** — Weaving is an important part of Mising culture, and a loom is found in each rural household. The craft is described as an exclusive preserve of Mising women, who begin training before reaching their teens. Before yarn was available in the market, Misings grew cotton and produced cotton yarn by spinning. Endi yarn, obtained from worms fed on castor-oil leaves, was once commonly used.

**Ri:bi** — The *ri:bi* is a cotton sheet worn over the ege. It is distinguished by narrow stripes.

**Gaseng** — The *gaseng* is another cotton sheet used over the ege and blouse. Unlike the ri:bi, it has broad stripes of contrasting colours.

**Gero** — The *gero* is usually an off-white sheet. It may be wrapped around the waist, around the chest, or down to about the knees.

**Seleng gasor** — The *seleng gasor* is a light cotton sheet worn occasionally instead of a ri:bi or gaseng.

**Endi yarn** — Endi yarn was obtained from worms fed on the leaves of castor-oil plants and was once used in Mising textile production.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name stripes and contrasting colours as textile features.

### Clothing & dress

Men wear cotton jackets called *Mibu Galuk*, light cotton towels, endi shawls, thick loin cloths, and occasionally shirtings. The sword carried by Mising men is called *yoksa*, and the Mising muffler is called *erkok*.

Women wear several garments. The *ege* is a lower garment consisting of a cotton sheet. Over it they wear a *ri:bi* or *gaseng*, both cotton sheets used to cover the ege and blouse. A *gero* is generally off-white and can cover the lower body or the body down to approximately the knees. The *seleng gasor* is worn occasionally instead of the ri:bi or gaseng. The *riya* is a comparatively narrow sheet wrapped tightly around the chest. Married women wear the *segrek*, a loose cloth wrapped around the waist to cover the ege down to the knees. A *po:tub* is a scarf used to protect the head, while *nisek* is a cloth used to carry a baby.

## Music & performance

Mising folk music includes several named song forms. *A:bang* is a verse of hymn-like praise and worship of gods and goddesses, sung by the *Mibu*, or priest, during rituals. Community A:bangs are used in *Pobua* or *Porag*, a ritual festival connected with prayers for better crops, health, and happiness. *Kaban* is one of the oldest forms of Mising folk song and is lamentational music. *Tebo Tekang* is a romantic lyric, while *Siuhung Nitom* is a melancholic song.

*Bini* consists of lullabies sung at home or in the field when babies are taken to places of work. The baby is tied to the mother’s or a young babysitter’s back. *Midang Nitom* is sung when a bride is taken to her new home, often to tease her. *Oi Nitom* is described as the most popular Mising folk-song form. Mising youths sing it while working or moving through fields and woods, and it forms part of the *Mising Soman* dance.

The sources identify drums and cymbals as usual musical instruments for Mising dances. The *ejug tapung* is a wind instrument resembling the snake charmer’s been and accompanies *Ejug Tapung Soman*. During Ali-Ayé-Lígang, young men and women sing and dance to drums, cymbals, and a gong. The gong is not used on any other festive occasion named in the source, and the drums have specific beats for this festival.

## Dance & theatre

Mising dance forms have particular rules. *Gumrag* is performed five times in circles. *Mibu Dagnam* is performed mostly during *Po:rag*, the harvesting festival, in the *Murong*, the community hall of the Misings. During this ritual dance, the priest sings the Ahbang.

*Selloi* is a song and dance performed for fun with drums or cymbals. It marks the beginning of the influx of Mising people from the hills to the plains of Assam. *Lereli* is performed when all sections of Mising society sing and dance for amusement, especially when meeting old friends. *Ejug Tapung Soman* is an ancient dance accompanied by the ejug tapung.

*Gumrag Soman*, also called *Gumrag Paksong*, is performed during Ali-Aye-Lígang. *Lotta Soman* is performed on any occasion as an expression of joy or community celebration.

## Festivals & rituals

The two chief traditional Mising festivals are Ali Aye Ligang and *Po:rag*, both connected with the agricultural cycle. *Ali-Ayé-Lígang* marks the beginning of the sowing season and the start of a new agricultural calendar. Ali-Ayé means “seeds in a row,” while Lígang means “sowing of seeds.” The festival begins on the first Wednesday of February and lasts for five days.

The celebrations begin when heads of families ceremonially sow rice paddy seeds in a corner of their rice fields and pray for abundant crops. At night, young men and women sing and dance in the courtyards of village households, accompanied by drums, cymbals, and a gong. A troupe receives rice beer and fowls from each household. After the singing and dancing, youths hold a feast on the third day.

*Po:rag* is the post-harvest festival. The harvesting of paddy rice in autumn is now usually observed in early winter or early spring, although summer harvesting was formerly common and Po:rag was consequently celebrated in August or September. Mibu Dagnam is performed during Po:rag in the Murong.

*Dobur* is an occasional animistic village rite. A sow and some hens are sacrificed for different purposes, and the form of observance varies. In its most common form, younger men beat the walls of every village house with large sticks, moving from one end of the village to the other, to drive away “ghosts and goblins hiding.” A feast is then held.

## Foodways

Agriculture and animal husbandry are the main sources of income in a consumption-oriented, subsistence economy. Rice is a central crop, and the sources also mention Rabi crops such as potatoes and mustard. Mising agriculture formerly included slash-and-burn cultivation and is now described as a settled peasant economy. Mono-cropping is practiced.

Food and drink appear in festival and ritual contexts. During Ali-Ayé-Lígang, singing and dancing groups receive rice beer and fowls from households, and youths hold a feast on the third day. During Dobur, a sow and some hens are sacrificed, followed by a village feast.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Misings were an oral community until the early 20th century, and the sources state that there are no detailed written records of their earliest habitat and culture. History was transmitted orally through folk songs and stories passed from ancestors to later generations, and this tradition remains present in Mising society.

Some Assamese literature from the Vaishnavite period and the Ahom Chronicles mention the Miri or Misings in connection with Vaishnavite saints and Ahom kings. British accounts beginning in the 19th century also contribute to attempts to reconstruct early Mising history.

Modern education spread through Christian missionaries. In 1885, J. E. Needham prepared a grammar based on the Shaiyang Miri language around Sadiya, and in 1907 J. H. Lorrain completed a Miri-Abor dictionary. The Mising language adapted the Roman alphabet. The primary literary body of Mising is the Mising Agom Kébang, or Mising Language Society.

## Language & religion

Mising is a Tani language within the Tibeto-Burman language group. The Mising, Padam, and Minyong speak dialects of the same language. Mising speakers live mainly in Assam, including Dhemaji, Lakhimpur, Sonitpur, Dibrugarh, Sibsagar, Jorhat, Majuli, Golaghat, Charaideu, Bishwanath, and Tinsukia, as well as districts of Arunachal Pradesh. Educated Misings are bilingual in Assamese and Mising; some speak only Assamese, while a minority speak only Mising. The language uses the Roman alphabet, which displaced the Assamese alphabet as the preferred script.

The neo-Vaishnavite cult of Sankardev significantly influenced the Misings, and xatras were established in Mising-populated areas. Although the Misings are reckoned among Hinduized peoples, they retained customary practices. Donyi-Polo gained some following among Mising people, especially in Arunachal Pradesh. Christianity has also been embraced by part of the population and has contributed to sectarian tensions.
