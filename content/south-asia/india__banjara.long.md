## Overview

The Banjara — also called Lambāda or Lamāna, and by their own people Gor, Gor Mati or Gormati ("own people") — are nomadic tribes found across India, settled in Rajasthan and many other parts of the country. Their language is Lambadi, also called Gour Boli. Historically they were pastoralists, traders, breeders and transporters of goods through India's inland regions, moving in caravans called *tanda* under an elected headman styled *muqaddam*, *nayak* or *naik*, and at times controlling a large share of the country's overland trade. A 1968 survey by the All India Banjara Seva Sangh recorded 27 synonyms and 17 subgroups for the community. In folk-culture terms they matter above all for embroidery and tattooing, which Banjara themselves treat as central markers of identity, and for a ritual and festival life that developed the open, unorthodox character their itinerant existence encouraged.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Lepo** — the embroidery in which Lambani women specialise, stitching pieces of mirror, decorative beads and coins onto cloth. The mirror-and-coin surface is what distinguishes the work; museum bodices carry strips of cotton set with two or three glass mirrors and a row of metal coins below, sewn at the shoulder, on either side of the neck opening and along the bottom front hem.

**Sandur Lambani embroidery** — a textile embroidery unique to the tribe in Sanduru, Bellary district, Karnataka. It holds a GI tag.

**Cowrie applique** — cowrie shells are worked into the edges of embroidered pieces alongside cotton and wool thread. On a woman's *indhoni* neck-piece they form bobbles and tassels along the lower edge and are sewn around three of the four sides; purses, handkerchiefs and arm ornaments in the collection combine cotton with cowrie in the same way.

**Block-printed and tie-dyed skirt cloth** — the circular skirt in the collection is made from block-printed cotton with a wide band of indigo tie-dyed cloth around the hips and a further panel of the same running half the skirt's length. A woman's veil, *odhni*, is tie-dyed silk: two rectangles sewn together, a fuchsia square at centre holding five green circles outlined in purple, a green border, fuchsia stripes at the short ends and white spots throughout.

**Mirrorwork with cut-out applique** — on the *odhni*, red cotton at one corner has squares cut out with mirrored glass set behind to show through, finished with metal coins along the bottom edge. Blouses combine embroidery, mirrorwork and applique in one piece.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name only multicoloured geometric designs, worked on the fully embroidered front of a red cotton bodice and along the embroidered hem band of a skirt; the *odhni*'s dyed pattern of circles within a square and all-over white spots; and a floral pattern on the brass plate of a nose ornament.

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress is one of the traits the community retained after its traditional occupation collapsed, and it varies according to marital status, as does a woman's ornamentation. A bride receives a full set of traditional dress made by her mother. These dresses are extremely elaborate — glass pieces, beads and seashells on a mainly red ground — and are worn for months at a stretch between careful launderings. The garments in the collection bear this out: a circular skirt hemmed with a band of red cotton and multi-coloured embroidery and closed with cloth ties at the waist, with a rectangular panel of dense embroidery near the hem at one side edged in applique strips; an embroidered, mirrored and appliqued blouse; a red cotton bodice open at the back with ties at side and neck. Head coverings include a *dopatta* of cotton and silk with embroidery and mirror-work, a cotton head-cloth called *fhadki*, and the tie-dyed silk *odhni*. Embroidered cotton belts, armbands and a boy's hat and child's jacket complete the range. Men have largely given up their traditional attire of a white dhoti and a red turban, together with earrings, finger rings and the *kanadoro*, silver strings worn around the waist.

### Architecture

Although traditionally migratory, the Banjara historically settled each year in fixed-village accommodation through the monsoon months of June to August; the sources used do not describe the form, materials or decoration of those dwellings.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The everyday objects that survive in collections are embroidered cloth rather than pottery or metal vessels. Central among them is the *indhoni*, the waterpot holder, and the neck-piece that hangs from that head ring to cover the nape of a woman carrying a pot of water — red woven cotton backed with pale brown, entirely covered in orange, white, green, red, blue and black cotton embroidery, with cowrie shells around three sides and white cotton loops along the top edge. Embroidered cotton bags are specialised by contents: bread bags, a paan bag, purses worked with cowrie shells, and a masala and nut holder. Household and transport gear is decorated to the same standard — a *rumal* cover of embroidered cotton, a donkey bag in cotton and wool, and an ox's head-piece worked with embroidery and applique.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ornament was once made of ivory and silver; reduced economic circumstances have caused it to be made of plastic and aluminium. The collection's metalwork is built around currency: a necklace of metal cylinders strung on twisted cotton cord with ten coins mounted between them and a shield-shaped pendant carrying nine small coins on chains, the large coins embossed "ONE RUPEE INDIA" with dates from 1903 to 1918 and "GEORGE V KING EMPEROR" on the reverse, the small ones "TWO ANNAS INDIA" dated 1906 to 1918 with Edward VII or George V. A nose ornament is a brass ring carrying two brass beads and one blue bead, probably glass, separated by four thin round brass plates, with a floral-patterned brass plate at one side. Other pieces include paired woollen armlets each with a bell-shaped metal ornament and a woollen pendant, an arm ornament of cotton and gold thread with cowries, a cotton hair ornament, and a brass and white-metal foot ring. Tattooing, alongside embroidery, is especially prized and forms a significant aspect of Banjara identity.

## Music & performance

Banjara art includes performance arts — dance and music — beside the folk and plastic arts of rangoli, embroidery, tattooing and painting. The Banjara have a sister community of singers known as the **Dadhis**, or Gajugonia, who traditionally travelled from village to village singing songs to the accompaniment of the *sarangi*. Within the community itself, performance is divided by gender at religious festivals: the men sing the devotional songs and perform the temple rituals, while the women do most of the singing and dancing. Women are also expected to perform alongside men when groups put on performances before non-Banjara audiences to raise money for festival celebrations, though most of that money is then consumed by the men as liquor. During the August ritual of Teej, girls sing and dance around the seedling baskets. The sources used name no further instruments or song genres.

## Dance & theatre

**Ghoomar** and **Chari** are traditional dance forms of the Banjaras.

## Festivals & rituals

**Sheetala Bhavani** falls usually in June or July, and at it the Banjara pray for the protection of their cattle. In August they celebrate **Gangaur**, also called Gamoli or Sinjara, a ritual found only in the Marwar region, in which young unmarried girls pray for a good groom: they sow seeds in bamboo bowls and water them three times a day for nine days, and sprouts that grow thick and high are taken as a good omen. **Teej** brings the singing and dancing around the seedling baskets. The community also keeps **Diwali**, the festival of lights, and **Holi**, the spring festival of colours, and worships the goddess Bhavani during **Vijayadashami**, followed by **Ayudha Puja**.

Marriage is the one religious occasion in which women are paramount, in the preparations; the ceremony usually takes place in the bride's family home. Proposals are initiated by the boy's father, usually when the child reaches 18 and is judged capable of running a household, with advice from the *naik* of his *tanda* and close relatives; horoscopes are consulted. An earlier arrangement may be solemnised with a betrothal ceremony, *sagai*, after which the girl stays in her parents' household until puberty. When both sides promise before the *gor panchayat*, the boy's family distributes liquor, betel leaves and nuts to the *tanda* and to the girl's family. Bride price traditionally falls due at betrothal — a community celebration — set by the *gor panchayat* and traditionally eleven rupees with either four bullocks or one bullock and three cattle; a dowry paid by the bride's family is now becoming evident. Weddings are set for slack months, so April and May just after harvest are common. Widows may remarry and divorce is accepted with the consent of the *gor panchayat*. Two embroidered and quilted wedding mats are in the collection.

## Foodways

What the sources record is trade in foodstuffs and the equipment for carrying them: the community moved grain, oilseed, sugarcane, opium, fruit and flowers, and forest produce including gums, chironji, mahua, berries and honey, with the Labana subgroup trading salt, the Multani grain and the Mukeri wood and timber; in the Deccan Plateau and Central Provinces they held a monopoly on the movement of salt before the East India Company. Among the objects shown are embroidered cotton bread bags, a paan bag and a masala and nut holder, and liquor, betel leaves and nuts are distributed to the *tanda* when a marriage is agreed.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used name no Banjara epic or folktale cycle. The nearest documented oral practitioners are the *Dadhis* or Gajugonia singers described above. Preservation effort is concentrated on the language: there have been calls for Lambadi to be recognised in the Constitution of India, and Telangana has introduced two textbooks in Lambadi for primary school children.

## Language & religion

Banjara speak Lambadi, also called Gour Boli. It has no script of its own and is written either in Devanagari or in the script of the local language, such as Telugu or Kannada; many Banjara today are bilingual or multilingual, taking up the dominant language around them, while those in areas of dense Banjara population keep their traditional idioms. Religious life is loosely held — the peripatetic life bred a casual, unorthodox and open attitude to religion, family and women, and practices barred in orthodox Hindu society were freely followed among Banjara. They worship deities including Balaji and Jagadamba and hold Guru Nanak in great respect. Their most important saint is Sevalal, also Sevabhaya, whom colonial British administrators placed in the 19th century and whose original name they gave as Siva Rathor.
