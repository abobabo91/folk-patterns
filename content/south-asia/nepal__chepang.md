## Overview

The Chepang, also known as Chewang, are a Tibeto-Burman ethnic group from the rugged ridges of the Mahabharat mountain range of central Nepal. They are mostly located in Bagmati Province, in Dhading District, Chitwan District, and Makwanpur District, and in Gandaki Province, in Gorkha District and Tanahun District. The 2011 Nepal census recorded 68,399 Chepang, or 0.3% of Nepal’s population. Chepang is also the name of their language, which is spoken by approximately 59,000 people in South-Central Nepal. The language is closely related to Raute and Raji and uses a duodecimal, or base-12, counting system.

## Festivals & rituals

The Chepang observe the Hindu festivals Dashain, Tihar, and Sakranti, as well as their own tribal festival *Nwagi*, also called *Chhonam*. Chhonam is performed on a Tuesday during the third week of Bhadra, described in the source as some day in August and September. It is an auspicious day for eating a new crop, and eating certain agricultural products is prohibited before the celebration.

The Chepang also observe Maghe Sakranti and Saune Sakranti. In the 5th National Gathering of Chepang in 2004, it was stated that they practiced Prakriti, or Nature, with ancestor worship as the most important practice. They worship many deities, including Bhumi, Aita Bare, Gaidu, and Namrung, described as an earth deity.

According to the 2011 Nepal Census, almost 70,000 Chepang lived in the country; the source describes 65% as Hindu, 25% as Christian, and 10% as animist. The Chepangs themselves follow Animism and are strongly influenced by Hinduism and Buddhism, which came from the Tamangs living just north of them. In recent years, many Chepang have converted to Christianity.

## Foodways

Chepang subsistence has historically combined farming, forest gathering, fishing, hunting, and the use of wild foods. Over the past two or three generations, the Chepang have slowly shifted from a semi-nomadic slash-and-burn lifestyle toward a more settled way of life. They increasingly rely on permanent fields of maize, millet, and bananas, although the severe topography has made permanent farming difficult and usually insufficient.

The forest has remained an important, although decreasing, source of food. Historically, wild yams and tubers, fish caught from nearby rivers, bats, and wild birds supplemented carbohydrates and protein. Periodically, wild deer hunted in nearby forests also supplemented the diet.

The festival Chhonam is the auspicious day for eating a new crop. Before the celebration, eating certain agricultural products is prohibited. They state that malnutrition has historically been a problem despite forest supplements, and that Chepang communities were already suffering from severe malnutrition before the 2015 Nepal blockade.

## Language & religion

Chepang is a Tibeto-Burman language spoken by approximately 59,000 people in South-Central Nepal. It is also known as Chepang, while the people themselves call it Chyo-bang. The people are also called Praja, meaning “political subjects.” Another group living across the Gandaki river calls itself Bujheli. Chepang is spoken in Makwanpur District, Chitwan District, southern Dhading District, and southern Gorkha District, and has Western Chepang and Eastern Chepang dialects.

The language has a basic Subject Object Verb word order, with alterations due to context, and can also be described as verb-final. The sources describe three different dialects and state that the language is closely related to Raute and Raji, two undocumented languages spoken in western Nepal. Chepang uses a duodecimal, or base-12, counting system rather than the decimal, or base-10, system.

Chepang religious practice is described as Animism, with strong Hindu and Buddhist influence. Hindu festivals are observed alongside the tribal festival Nwagi or Chhonam, while ancestor worship and the worship of deities including Bhumi, Aita Bare, Gaidu, and Namrung are also documented. The sources state that many Chepang have converted to Christianity in recent years.
