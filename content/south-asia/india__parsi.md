## Overview

The Parsis came to the Indian subcontinent from Persia after the Arab-Islamic conquest of Iran. They live mainly in Mumbai and nearby parts of western India, with communities in other countries. Their culture preserves Zoroastrian beliefs, Gujarati speech, food traditions, textiles and historical stories. Their identity includes descent from Persian refugees and formal admission through the navjote ceremony.

## Material culture

Parsi material culture includes embroidered silk and cotton clothing, shawls, caps, and a few recorded household and religious objects.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Recorded Parsi textiles include embroidered clothing, shawls and a cap with symbolic motifs.

- **Blouse** (*Choli*) — This blouse was made of silk, metal and cotton and had Chinese embroidery.
- **Parsi women’s outfit** — This outfit included a green and red silk tunic with multicoloured embroidery and baggy trousers.
- **Embroidered shawl** — Recorded shawls were made of wool and cotton, with one example using metal thread.
- **Woman’s cap** — This green cotton cap was embroidered with suns, cypress trees, flowers, fish and birds linked with strength, fertility, abundance and life.

### Clothing & dress

The recorded clothing includes a blouse, a silk tunic, baggy trousers, shawls and an embroidered cap.

- **Blouse** (*Choli*) — The blouse is recorded as part of Parsi or Zoroastrian clothing.
- **Silk tunic** — The loose-fitting green and red tunic had several-coloured embroidery on the front of the skirt.
- **Baggy trousers** — The tunic was worn with a pair of baggy trousers.
- **Embroidered shawl** — Embroidered shawls are recorded as part of Parsi or Zoroastrian clothing.
- **Green cotton cap** — The cap was embroidered with suns, cypress trees, flowers, fish and birds.

### Architecture

The sources mention a fire temple at Sanjan and the movement of the Fire of Victory to Navsari and Udvada.

- **Sanjan fire temple** — A fire temple is described at Sanjan.
- **Fire of Victory** (*Atash Bahram*) — The Fire of Victory was moved to Navsari and later to Udvada in 1742 because of continuing disputes.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The recorded objects include an expanding lantern and religious implements.

- **Expanding lantern** — This lantern was made of calico with a copper top and base and a cast brass carrying handle.
- **Religious implements** (*alat*) — Religious implements called alat are mentioned in an account of a group arriving from Greater Khorasan.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources do not document Parsi jewelry or named body-adornment traditions.

## Music & performance

The sources do not document Parsi instruments, songs or performances, but they mention a Parsi epic.

- **Parsi epic** (*Qissa-i Sanjan*) — The Qissa-i Sanjan is mentioned, but the sources do not say whether it was sung, recited or performed.

## Dance & theatre

The sources do not document Parsi dances, theatre or other entertainment traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

Parsi ritual life centers on Zoroastrian beliefs and formal admission through the navjote ceremony.

- **Admission ceremony** (*navjote*) — The navjote formally admits a person into the Zoroastrian religion.
- **Judgment bridge** (*Chinvat bridge*) — Zoroastrian teaching describes judgment at the Chinvat bridge, with passage to Paradise, Hammistagan or Hell.
- **Final battle** — The sources describe a final battle between good and evil, followed by a river of fire and a paradise after resurrection.

## Foodways

Parsi food combines rice, lentils, curries, meat dishes, eggs, salads, sweets and snacks.

- **Chicken Farcha** — Chicken Farcha is fried chicken.
- **Dhansak** — Dhansak uses meat or vegetables in a mixed-lentil or toor-daal gravy and is served with brown rice.
- **Fish in banana leaf** (*Patra ni Machhi*) — Patra ni Machhi is fish filled with green coconut chutney, wrapped in a banana leaf and steamed.
- **Sharp onion-cucumber salad** (*Kachumbar*) — Kachumbar accompanies most meals.
- **Spiced scrambled eggs** (*akuri*) — Akuri is scrambled eggs with spices.

## Oral tradition & literature

Parsi literature preserves stories of migration, settlement and religious exchange.

- **Epic of Sanjan** (*Qissa-i Sanjan*) — This sixteenth-century epic tells of the journey from Iran, refuge from a local ruler and settlement at Sanjan.
- **Sugar in milk story** — The epic tells how a Parsi priest added sugar to milk to show that the newcomers would blend in and enrich local culture.
- **Religious letters** (*rivayats*) — The rivayats are letters containing religious and social questions and replies exchanged between Zoroastrian priests in Gujarat and Iran.
- **Holy book** (*Avesta*) — The Avesta is the Zoroastrian holy book, written in the Avestan language.

## Language & religion

Parsis speak Gujarati and follow Zoroastrianism, with religious traditions linked to the Avesta and Zarathushtra.

- **Parsi Gujarati** — The Parsi variety of Gujarati is associated with Gujarat and Maharashtra and is now subsumed under Gujarati.
- **Zoroastrianism** (*Zarathushti Pantha*) — Parsis follow Zoroastrianism, also called Zarathushti Pantha.
- **Good and evil** (*Ohrmazd; Angra Mainyu*) — Zoroastrian teaching presents good, identified as Ohrmazd, and evil, identified as Angra Mainyu, as opposing forces.
- **Priestly literary language** (*Middle Persian*) — Middle Persian remained the literary language of the hereditary Zoroastrian priesthood until at least the early 11th century.
- **Old religious language** (*Avestan*) — Avestan is the language in which the Avesta was written.
