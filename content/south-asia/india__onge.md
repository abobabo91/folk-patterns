## Overview

The Onge, also rendered Önge, Ongee, and Öñge, are an Andamanese ethnic group indigenous to the Andaman Islands in Southeast Asia at the Bay of Bengal, India. They traditionally hunted, gathered, fished, and cultivated plants, and are designated as a Scheduled Tribe of India. In the 18th century they were distributed across Little Andaman Island and nearby islands, including territory and camps on Rutland Island and the southern tip of South Andaman Island. Today, surviving members are confined to two reserve camps on Little Andaman: Dugong Creek in the northeast and South Bay. Their population declined from 672 in 1901 to barely 100, but increased from 100 to 117 in 2017. They speak Önge, one of two known Ongan languages, and their endangered language and distinctive subsistence history are central to the cultural record available in these sources.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that ancestor worship is an important element in the religious traditions of the Andaman islands and that the native Andamanese religion and belief system is a form of animism. The sources also record a traditional story about the ground shaking and a great wall of water destroying the land. Taking heed of this story, the semi-nomadic Onge survived the catastrophe caused by the 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake by taking shelter in the highlands.

## Foodways

The Onge were fully dependent on hunting and gathering for food and were traditionally hunter-gatherers and fishers, while also practicing plant cultivation. They state that changes in food habits brought about by contact with the outside world were a major cause of the decline in Onge population. The sources also report that, in December 2008, eight male tribal members died after drinking a toxic liquid identified by some sources as methanol, apparently mistaking it for drinking alcohol. The liquid apparently came from a container that had washed ashore at Dugong Creek.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources document traditional Onge stories concerning the ground shaking and a great wall of water destroying the land. These stories informed the decision to take shelter in the highlands during the catastrophe caused by the 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake.

## Language & religion

The Onge speak the Önge language, also rendered Onge, Ongee, Eng, or Ung ॳङे, with the pronunciation [ˈəŋɡe]. It is one of two known Ongan languages, also called southern Andamanese languages, and is spoken by the Onge people on Little Andaman Island. Önge was formerly spoken throughout Little Andaman, on smaller islands to the north, and possibly at the southern tip of South Andaman Island. Since the middle of the 19th century, British colonization and the massive inflow of Indian settlers from the mainland caused the number of speakers to decline, although a moderate increase has been observed in recent years. As of 2006, there were 94 native speakers confined to a single settlement in the northeast of Little Andaman Island, making Önge an endangered language. The sources describe the native Andamanese religion as animistic and identify ancestor worship as important. They state that the Andamanese probably had no government or clan leader and made decisions by group consensus.
