## Overview

The Malayalis are a Dravidian ethnic group native to Kerala and the union territory of Lakshadweep in southern India. Malayalam is their principal language and belongs to the Dravidian language family. Malayalis also live in neighboring Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, in other Indian metropolitan areas, and in migrant communities across the Persian Gulf, Malaysia, Singapore, the United States, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and Europe. According to the Indian census of 2011, approximately 33 million Malayalis lived in Kerala, constituting 97% of the state’s population. The Malabar coast, historically connected to international spice trade, brought sustained contact with Cochin Jews, Saint Thomas Christians, Mappila Muslims, Portuguese, Dutch, French, and British communities. Malayali folk culture consequently includes diverse architectural, literary, musical, ritual, and performing traditions.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Malayali architecture developed in Kerala’s tropical climate, characterized by excessive rain and intensive solar radiation. Buildings traditionally have low walls, sloping roofs, and projecting eaves, and are set in open garden plots to allow air movement in humid conditions. Timber is the principal structural material, and accurate joinery, assembled woodwork, and carving on columns, walls, and roof frames are described as distinctive features. Stonework was generally restricted to the plinth, while laterite was used for walls. Timber roofs were commonly covered with palm-leaf thatching and, less often, tiles in palaces or temples. Kerala murals are paintings made with vegetable dyes on wet walls in subdued shades of brown.

*Nalukettu* is a quadrangular housing form constructed according to the Tachu Sastra, described as the science of carpentry. It contains four wings around a central courtyard called *Nadumuttom*. This courtyard formed the center of domestic life and was used for rituals. A Nalukettu was often associated with a *tharavadu*, an ancestral home for a matrilineal joint family. More elaborate forms were called *Ettukettu*, with eight halls and two courtyards, and *Pathinarukettu*, with sixteen halls and four courtyards. Mattancherry Palace is given as an example of a Nalukettu structure.

They do mention the *idakka*, a small hourglass-shaped ethnic drum, and the *chengila*, a metallic gong used to mark beats in Sopanam music.

## Music & performance

Music formed a major part of early Malayalam literature, which the sources date to the 9th century CE. Kerala is particularly associated with *Sopanam*, religious music that developed through invocatory singing at the *Kalam of Kali* and later inside temples. *Sopana sangeetham* is performed beside the holy steps, or *sopanam*, leading to a shrine’s sanctum sanctorum. It uses plain notes and is accompanied by the *idakka* and *chengila*. The tradition is described as being performed traditionally by men of the Maarar and Pothuval communities, who are Ambalavasi castes engaged in it as a hereditary profession.

The sources also identify Kerala with Carnatic and Hindustani music. Named Malayali musicians include Swati Tirunal, Shadkala Govinda Maarar, Sangitha Vidwan Gopala Pillai Bhagavathar, Chertala Gopalan Nair, M. D. Ramanathan, T.V. Gopalakrishnan, M.S. Gopalakrishnan, L. Subramaniam, T.N. Krishnan, and K. J. Yesudas. Swathi Thirunal, described as the king of Travancore, patronized and contributed to Hindustani music.

Serpent worship is connected with *Pulluvan Pattu*. The *pulluvar* sing in serpent temples and snake groves, and the same song tradition is also called *Sarppapaattu*, *Naagam Paattu*, *Sarpam Thullal*, *Sarppolsavam*, *Paambum Thullal*, or *Paambum Kalam*. *Mappila Paattukal*, or Mappila Songs, are Muslim devotional songs in Malayalam, composed in colloquial Malayalam and in a mixture of Malayalam and Arabic. Film music is described as the most popular form of music in Kerala.

## Dance & theatre

Malayalis use the words *attom* and *thullal* for dance. Their art forms are classified as religious, semi-religious, and secular. Religious forms include *Theyyam* and *Bhagavatipattu*; semi-religious forms include *Sanghakali* and *Krishnanattom*; and secular forms include *Kathakali*, *Mohiniyattam*, and *Thullal*.

Kathakali and Mohiniyattam are identified as Kerala’s two classical dance forms. Kathakali is a dance-drama, while Mohiniyattam is described as a graceful and sensual dance performed solo or in groups by women. *Kutiyattam* is a traditional performing art from Kerala recognized by UNESCO as a Masterpiece of Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity.

*Ottamthullal*, also called the poor man’s Kathakali, was created by Kunchan Nambiar as an alternative to *Chakiarkooth*. *Theyyam* is a ritualistic art form thought to predate Hinduism and associated with folk dances performed during harvest celebrations. It is performed as an offering to gods intended to remove poverty and illness. *Velakali* is another ritualistic art form, mainly performed at temples during festival time, while *Kolkali* is a folk art in which dancers move in a circle, striking small sticks and maintaining rhythm through particular steps.

Muslim Mappilas, Nasrani Mappilas, and Latin Christians have distinct forms. *Duff Muttu*, also called *Dubh Muttu* or *Aravanamuttu*, is a group performance staged during festivals and nuptial ceremonies. *Oppana* is a form of Muslim social entertainment. *Margamkali* combines devotion and entertainment and is performed by groups singing songs in unison call-and-response form. *Parichamuttukali* adapts the martial art *Kalaripayattu*, while *Chavittu nadakom* is a theatrical form associated mainly with Kerala Latin Christians and dating from the second half of the 16th century.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources identify *Onam* as a harvest festival celebrated in autumn. *Vallam Kali*, the racing of country-made boats, is mainly conducted during the Onam season. It includes races involving traditional boats of Kerala, with *Chundan Vallam*, or snake boat, as the principal type. Other named boats include *Churulan Vallam*, *Iruttukuthy Vallam*, *Odi Vallam*, *Veppu Vallam* or *Vaipu Vallam*, *Vadakkanody Vallam*, and *Kochu Vallam*. The Nehru Trophy Boat Race takes place at Punnamada Lake in Alappuzha district. The Champakulam Moolam Boat Race is described as the oldest and most popular Vallam Kali in Kerala. It is held on the river Pamba on the *moolam* day of the Malayalam month *Midhunam*, the day associated with installation of the deity at the Ambalappuzha Sree Krishna Temple.

Malayali family houses may contain snake shrines called *Kavu*. *Sarpam Thullal* is usually performed in the courtyard of houses with such shrines as a votive offering for family wealth and happiness. The sources also describe *Theyyam* as an offering to gods intended to remove poverty and illness, and *Velakali* as a temple performance during festival time.

In the *tharavadu*, a clan deity called *Para Devatha* was revered, and temples were built to honor these deities. *Tharavadu* life followed a matrilineal joint-family system in which the Karanavar, the oldest male member and usually the eldest maternal uncle, made major decisions. The sources also mention festivals and nuptial ceremonies as contexts for Duff Muttu and describe Oppana as Muslim social entertainment.

## Foodways

They describe the Malabar coast as a major center of international spice trade and state that this contact contributed to Malayali culture.

## Oral tradition & literature

Malayalam literature is described as ancient in origin. The Sangam literature is considered an ancient predecessor of Malayalam, while the earliest known literary works in Malayalam are *Ramacharitam* and *Thirunizhalmala*, two epic poems written in Old Malayalam. During the first 600 years of the Malayalam calendar, literature mainly consisted of oral ballads, including *Vadakkan Pattukal*, or Northern Songs, in North Malabar, and *Thekkan Pattukal*, or Southern Songs, in Southern Travancore.

Malayalam literature includes the 14th-century Niranam poets Madhava Panikkar, Sankara Panikkar, and Rama Panikkar. Later literary figures include Cherusseri Namboothiri, Thunchaththu Ezhuthachan, Poonthanam Nambudiri, and Kunchan Nambiar. Ezhuthachan wrote *Adhyathmaramayanam Kilippattu* and *Mahabharatham Kilippattu*, while Poonthanam wrote *Jnanappana*. Kunchan Nambiar introduced the literary form *Thullal*.

The first travelogue in an Indian language is identified as the Malayalam *Varthamanappusthakam*, written by Paremmakkal Thoma Kathanar in 1785. Modern Malayalam literature includes social, political, and economic subjects, and writers such as O. V. Vijayan, Kamaladas, M. Mukundan, Arundhati Roy, and Vaikom Muhammed Basheer have gained international recognition.

## Language & religion

Malayalam is a Dravidian language spoken principally by the Malayali people. It has official-language status in Kerala, Lakshadweep, and Puducherry’s Mahé district, and is one of India’s 22 scheduled languages and 11 classical languages. The mainstream account in the sources holds that Malayalam developed from a western coastal dialect of early Middle Tamil and separated from it between the 9th and 13th centuries. The language’s origin remains disputed.

The earliest script used for Malayalam was Vatteluttu. The modern script developed through the influence and modification of Vatteluttu, Kolezhuthu, and Grantha scripts, with further influence from Tigalari. Arabi Malayalam uses a modified Arabic script, while Suriyani Malayalam uses a variant of the Syriac alphabet.

Malayali society includes Hindu, Muslim, Saint Thomas Christian, Nasrani, and Latin Christian communities. The sources describe Mappila Muslims, Saint Thomas Christians, and Latin Christians as having distinct literary or performing traditions. They also document ritual forms connected to Hindu temple culture, Theyyam, Kali, serpent worship, snake shrines, and family deities.
