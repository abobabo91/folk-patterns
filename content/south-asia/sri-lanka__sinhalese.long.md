## Overview

The Sinhalese people, also known as the Sinhalese or Sinhala people, are an Indo-Aryan ethno-linguistic group native to the island of Sri Lanka. They are the largest ethnic group in Sri Lanka, constituting about 75% of the population and numbering more than 15.2 million. They speak Sinhala, an insular Indo-Aryan language, and are predominantly Theravada Buddhists, although a significant minority follow Christianity and other religions. Since 1815, they have been broadly divided into up-country Sinhalese of the central mountainous regions and low-country Sinhalese of the coastal regions. Their cultural domains include sculpture, fine arts, literature, dancing, poetry, folk beliefs, rituals, architecture, cuisine and martial arts.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Low-country women’s dress** — Low-country Sinhalese women traditionally wear a white long-sleeved jacket and a tight wraparound skirt, usually with a floral or patterned design.

**Up-country women’s dress** — Up-country Sinhalese women wear a similar outfit with a puffed-up shoulder jacket and a tucked-in frill lining the top of the skirt. In Sinhala, this outfit is called *Reda and Hatte*.

**Kandyan sari** — Traditionally, high-caste Kandyan women wear a Kandyan-style sari, also called an *Osariya*. It resembles the Maharashtrian sari, but has a frill lining the lower half and sometimes puffed-up sleeves.

**South Indian-style saree** — Low-country high-caste women wear a South Indian-style saree.

**Patterned cloth wraps** — A British Museum photograph records Sinhalese women and a child in Colombo wearing patterned cloth wraps.

**Dance apron** — A Museum of Ethnography catalogue records an apron used by dancers in demon ceremonies. It is made of hemp, *hana* in Sinhala, and yarn soaked in paint called *kukul sayam*. The apron covers the upper part of the body and is loosely worn, and the record places its manufacture and use especially in coastal Sinhalese areas.

**Tovil costume** — British Museum records describe a devil dancer’s cotton skirt with red, black and white piped decoration, and another with a white top section and a red lower section. These are identified as Tovil dance costumes.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name floral or patterned designs and do not provide a specific textile motif vocabulary.

### Clothing & dress

During recreation, Sinhalese men traditionally wear a *sarong*, called *sarama* in Sinhala, sometimes with a long-sleeved shirt. Women’s clothing varies by region. Low-country women wear a white long-sleeved jacket with a tight wraparound skirt, while up-country women wear a puffed-up shoulder jacket and a frilled skirt top. High-caste Kandyan women traditionally wear the Kandyan sari, or *Osariya*. For formal and ceremonial occasions, women wear the Kandyan style, consisting of a full blouse that covers the midriff and is partly tucked in at the front. In more populated areas, men also wear Western-style clothing, including suits, while women wear skirts and blouses. The sources do not document head coverings, belts or footwear.

### Architecture

Sinhalese architectural history includes the ancient cities of Anuradhapura and Polonnaruwa. The sources name Ruwanwelisaya, Jetavanaramaya and Abayagiriya as architectural examples, and describe Jetavanaramaya as the second tallest brick building in the ancient world after the Great Pyramid of Giza and Abayagiriya as the third tallest. Sigiriya is described as a combination of natural and human-made fortress, with frescoes and many architectural aspects. Sinhalese hydraulic technology included ancient tanks, systematic ponds with fountains, moats and irrigation reservoirs, including Parakrama Samudra, Kaudulla and Kandalama. The sources do not describe vernacular house forms, roof types or ordinary domestic construction in detail.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources mention traditional wooden handicrafts and clay pottery in the hill country. Museum catalogue records include a wooden and metal knife identified as a *pihiya*, a bell called *sinuva* or *ghantaraya* in Sinhala, and an ivory comb decorated with two kinnaras holding vinas. The bell is recorded as being used by Sinhalese and Hindus in Buddhist or Hindu temples throughout Sri Lanka. Other records include ivory figures of a disave or Kandyan provincial governor and of a female in court dress. The sources do not provide a fuller account of household ceramics or metalworking techniques.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources do not provide a general account of Sinhalese jewelry, tattooing, henna, hair practices or other forms of body adornment. Museum records mention earrings and elongated earlobes in the description of a Kolam mask representing a possible vädda princess, but this is a description of a mask character rather than documentation of Sinhalese personal adornment.

## Music & performance

Buddhist festivals use distinctive music played on traditional Sinhalese instruments, although the supplied sources do not name the instruments. Folk poems relating to particular jobs were communal songs with a rhythm, sung during everyday tasks such as harvesting and sowing. These poems were traditionally composed in four lines, with attention to rhyme.

In popular music, Ananda Samarakoon developed the reflective and poignant *Sarala gee* style in the late 1930s and early 1940s. The sources also name Sunil Shantha, W. D. Amaradeva, Premasiri Khemadasa, Nanda Malini, Victor Ratnayake, Austin Munasinghe, T. M. Jayaratne, Sanath Nandasiri, Sunil Edirisinghe, Neela Wickremasinghe, Gunadasa Kapuge, Malini Bulathsinghala and Edward Jayakody as artists of repute.

The supplied museum records document bells used in Buddhist and Hindu temples and costume elements used in demon ceremonies. They do not describe named musical instruments, song structures beyond the folk poems, or performance settings such as weddings, funerals or court music.

## Dance & theatre

Kandyan dance consists of 18 *Wannam*, or dance routines, representing the behavior of animals including the elephant, eagle, cobra, monkey, peacock and rabbit. It is mainly performed during the annual Perahara pageant in Sri Dalada Maligawa, Kandy.

Pahatharata dance is associated with curing illnesses and spiritual clarification. Its principal feature is the use of masks representing gods and demons, together with fire and water used to bless people. Sabaragamuwa dances are described mainly as a style for entertaining people. Folk music and dances differ according to Sinhalese castes and sometimes regionally, and are especially popular among children and girls during the Sinhalese New Year period.

Tovil is a demon-exorcism performance tradition. The museum records include cotton skirts identified as Tovil dance costumes and an apron used by dancers in demon ceremonies. Kolam appears in museum records through masks and puppets. One mask depicts King Maha Sammata, the chief king in the Kolam repertoire; other records describe the demon of blood, *riri yaka*, and characters from the vädda group. String puppets are called *nul rukadaya* in Sinhala.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources state that Buddhist festivals are marked by distinctive music and that more ancient rituals such as *tovils*, or devil exorcisms, continue to be performed. These rituals praise and invoke the powers of the Buddha and the gods to exorcise demons. Pahatharata dance is likewise connected with healing and spiritual clarification.

The Sinhalese New Year is an important seasonal performance context. Folk music and dances are widely performed during this period. *Kiribath*, or milk rice, is prepared for special occasions including birthdays, New Years’ and religious festivals. During *Aluth Avurudu/Puthandu*, identified in the cuisine source as the Sinhalese/Tamil New Year, kiribath is served with sweets including *kavum*, *kokis*, *mung kavum* and *od iba*.

The sources do not describe birth ceremonies, coming-of-age rites, weddings or funerals. They also do not provide a complete annual festival calendar or specific festival dates.

## Foodways

Sri Lankan cuisine centres on rice, coconut, herbs, spices, fish, vegetables and fruits. Seafood is important, and foreign traders introduced additional foods and cultural influences. The strongest recorded external influences are Indian, especially South Indian, Indonesian and Dutch. Kandyan Sinhalese cooking uses local hill vegetables and fruits, while seafood is standard in coastal areas.

The central meal is boiled or steamed rice served with fish or meat curry, together with vegetable, lentil or fruit curries. Common ingredients include coconut milk, grated coconut, cinnamon, black pepper, Maldives fish, goraka, pandan leaf, lemongrass, jaggery made from kithul palm syrup, red rice and white rice. Varieties of rice named in the sources include Samba, Kekulu, Suwandel, Pachchaperumal, Kaluheenati and Madathawalu.

Named dishes include *kiribath*, rice cooked in salted coconut milk; *kottu*, shredded roti bread stir-fried with vegetables and optional egg, meat or cheese; *hoppers*, made from fermented rice flour and coconut milk batter; *string hoppers*, made from rice meal or wheat flour; *pittu*, steamed or roasted rice flour mixed with grated coconut; *pol roti*; *sambols*; *mallung*; *lamprais*; and *kool*, a seafood broth from Jaffna. Sweets include *kevum*, *kalu dodol*, *watalappam*, *aluwa*, *bibikkan* and *thala guli*. Beverages include tea, toddy, arrack, king coconut water, fruit juices, wood apple juice and kirala juice.

## Oral tradition & literature

The *Mahavamsa* is a Pali chronicle compiled by Buddhist monks of the Anuradhapura Maha Viharaya. It describes the origins of the Sinhalese through settlers associated with Prince Vijaya and later settlers from the Pandya kingdom. The early recorded history of the Sinhalese is also chronicled in the *Culavamsa*. These sources cover the ancient kingdoms of Anuradhapura and Polonnaruwa.

Sinhala folk tales include *Mahadana Muttha saha Golayo*, about a foolish figure who travels with his followers and causes trouble through ignorance, and *Kawate Andare*, about a witty court jester and his interactions with the royal court and his son. Sinhala literature includes *Amāvatura*, *Kavu Silumina*, *Jathaka Potha* and *Sala Liheeniya*. Buddhist priests’ *Sandesha Kavyas* are described as sophisticated and versatile works of literature.

In the modern period, writers named in the sources include Martin Wickremasinghe, G. B. Senanayake, Mahagama Sekera, Madewela S. Ratnayake and Munadasa Cumaratunga. The sources also name Wickramasinghe’s children’s novel *Madol Duwa* and Cumaratunga’s *Hath Pana*.

## Language & religion

Sinhala is an Indo-Aryan language within the Indo-European language family. It is primarily spoken by the Sinhalese people and is written in the Sinhalese script. The language has written and spoken varieties and displays diglossia. Sinhala is one of Sri Lanka’s two national languages, along with Tamil, and has been influenced by Pali, Sanskrit, Tamil, Portuguese, Dutch and English. Regional variations named in the sources include Uva, southern, up-country and Sabaragamu variations. The script is an abugida based on ancient Brahmi and is written from left to right.

Theravada is the oldest and predominant school of Buddhism in Sri Lanka. Sinhalese Buddhist practice also includes elements associated with Hindu traditions, ancient indigenous beliefs, spirits, healing rituals and the worship of Hindu deities. Named figures include Kataragama Deviyo, Upulvan and Ayyanayake. A significant Sinhalese Christian community exists in the maritime provinces, with most Sinhalese Christians being Roman Catholic and a minority Protestant.
