## Overview

Sri Lankan Moors, also known as Sri Lankan Muslims and formerly Ceylon Moors, are an ethnic minority group in Sri Lanka comprising 10.5% of the country’s total population. They are concentrated especially in the Ampara, Trincomalee and Batticaloa districts, while substantial communities also live in Colombo, Kandy, Galle, Matara, Negombo and Jaffna. Most are native speakers of Tamil and followers of Sunni Islam as understood by the Shafi'i school of jurisprudence. Many Muslims outside the Northern and Eastern provinces also speak Sinhala as a second language. The community is ethnically differentiated from Sri Lankan Tamils and Sri Lankan Malays and is officially categorized as a separate ethnicity. Its folk-cultural profile reflects Tamil, Sinhala, South Indian and Arabic-Islamic connections, including distinctive language practices, marriage customs, foodways and religious culture.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources mention the wearing of the North African fez during marriage ceremonies. They also record that wearing the *Thaali* was widely prevalent among Muslims as a Tamil and Sinhala custom.

### Architecture

The sources state that Robert Knox, a British sea captain of the 17th century, observed that the Kings of Kandy built mosques for Muslims. They also mention the re-opened Osmania College in Jaffna, once a prominent educational institution for the city’s Muslim community. No source used here describes Moorish house forms, construction materials, roof types, courtyards, decoration, or other vernacular architectural features.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention the *Thaali* and state that its wearing was widely prevalent among Muslims as a Tamil and Sinhala custom.

## Music & performance

They do state that religious sermons are delivered in Tamil, including in regions where Tamil is not the majority language, but do not characterize these sermons as a musical genre or describe their performance.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe a Muslim community strongly shaped by Islamic culture, with many customs and practices according to Islamic law. They record that congregational eating from a large shared plate called the *sahn* and wearing the North African fez formed part of marriage ceremonies. The sources also state that Tamil and Sinhala customs, including wearing the *Thaali* and eating *Kiribath*, were widely prevalent among Muslims.

Marriage ceremonies therefore appear in the sources as an important setting for shared customs and Arabic-Islamic practices.

The history of the community includes major episodes of displacement and return. In October 1990, the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam forcibly expelled the Muslim population from the Northern Province at gunpoint and confiscated their properties. In 2002, LTTE leader Vellupillai Prabhakaran formally apologized for the expulsion. Since the end of the war, Muslims have travelled in and out of Jaffna, some families have returned, and Osmania College was re-opened.

## Foodways

The sources mention *Kiribath*, which they describe as a Tamil and Sinhala custom widely prevalent among Muslims. They also describe the Arabic custom of congregational eating from a large shared plate called the *sahn*. These practices are presented as evidence of cultural connections among Muslim, Tamil, Sinhala and Arab communities.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record that Arwi is a written register of Tamil using the Arabic alphabet and that this register is unique to the Muslims of Tamil Nadu and Sri Lanka.

## Language & religion

Most Sri Lankan Muslims speak Tamil as their native language and follow Sunni Islam according to the Shafi'i school of jurisprudence. Sri Lankan Muslim Tamil is identified as a social dialect of Sri Lankan Tamil. It has phonological, morphological and lexical differences from other varieties of Sri Lankan Tamil because of Arabic influence, including Persian-Arabic loan words.

The sources distinguish two major sub-dialects: North-Eastern Muslim Tamil, found in the Northern and Eastern provinces, and Southern Muslim Tamil, found in the Southern, Western and Central provinces. In Colombo, Muslims in the Western province tend to code-mix Tamil and English. Southern Muslim Tamil replaces the Tamil sound /sa/ with /sha/ and uses voiced plosives such as /b, d, j, g/, while North-Eastern Muslim Tamil uses voiceless plosives such as /v, p, t, c, k/ instead. Southern speech also shortens some Tamil verbs.

Muslims additionally speak Sinhala and English in varying proportions. According to the 2012 Census, 58.7% or 862,397 Sri Lankan Muslims also spoke Sinhala, while 30.4% or 446,146 also spoke English. Arwi uses the Arabic alphabet for written Tamil and reflects earlier relations between Tamil Muslims in Tamil Nadu and Sri Lanka.

The sources describe the community’s culture as shaped by Islamic law while preserving South Asian customs and adopting Arabic-Islamic practices. They also state that the late 19th century saw a phase of Islamization, primarily under the influence of M. C. Siddi Lebbe, who strengthened Muslim identity and provided an ideological framework for Muslim ethnicity in Sri Lanka.
