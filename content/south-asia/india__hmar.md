## Overview

The Hmar are a scheduled tribe ethnic group from Manipur, Mizoram, Assam, and Meghalaya in Northeast India. Their primary language is Hmar, a Tibeto-Burman or Sino-Tibetan language that closely resembles Mizo. The 2011 Indian Census recorded 98,988 Hmar speakers as a first or native language, while the Hmar population, including people who speak Mizo as their first language, is estimated in the source at roughly 200,000–300,000. The Hmar were initially nomadic, moving from place to place in search of “greener pastures,” before settling in Mizoram and dispersing to neighbouring states. Hmar cultural documentation includes oral traditions, folk tales, language studies, religious writing, the Sikpui tradition, and a substantial body of modern literature.

## Material culture

### Architecture

They do record the Sikpui Lung, an old stone structure and monolith associated with a particular festival of the Hmar people, at Champhai, Mizoram.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The monolith is associated with the Hmar festival called Sikpui.

## Music & performance

The sources identify the Sikpui Hlapui as a folk song associated with the Hmar people. The song is also used by the Bnei Menashe people of Northeast India to claim an alleged connection to the state of Israel.

The sources also mention Sikpui Ruoi in the title of an academic paper, “Revisiting Sikpui Ruoi of the Hmar Tribe,” published in *Anthropology Today* in 2016. A cultural festival-cum-Sikpui Ruoi was held from 2–5 December, alongside a general assembly-cum-literary meet held from 15–16 December, according to the bibliographic material supplied.

Hmar literary and religious works include *Hmar Hla Suina*, *Thalai Hlabu*, *Thralai Hlabu*, *Hmar Hla*, *Thukhawchang*, and other publications listed in the source material. Their titles indicate the presence of song and hymn writing.

## Dance & theatre

The mention of Sikpui Ruoi and the Sikpui Hlapui establishes a festival and song association.

## Festivals & rituals

Sikpui is associated with the Hmar people and with the Sikpui Lung, a monolith found at Champhai, Mizoram. The source describes the stone as having been erected by Hmars in the past to commemorate Sikpui. It reproduces an inscription containing the date 28.12.1918 and gives a rough translation referring to the Hmars’ occupation of the place from 28.2.1918, attributed to Zahula Sailo.

The sources also mention Sikpui Ruoi. Immanuel Zarzosang published “Revisiting Sikpui Ruoi of the Hmar Tribe” in 2016, and another cited publication refers to a Cultural Festival-cum-Sikpui Ruoi held from 2–5 December. The same event listing records a 50th General Assembly-cum-Literary Meet from 15–16 December.

Hmar religious history includes the Christianization of Hmar communities by Welsh missionaries in the 1910s, specifically at Senvawn village. Animism is described as having been traditionally dominant.

## Oral tradition & literature

The source material identifies oral tradition as an important field of Hmar documentation. *Oral Traditions of the Hmars* was published in 2011 by the Assam Institute of Research For Tribals and Scheduled Castes. Lal Dena’s *Hmar Folk Tales* appeared in 1995, and Sari Rothangpui Khawbung’s *Folktales Myths and Legends in HMAR Culture: An Interpretative Study* was submitted in 2023 as a Doctor of Philosophy work at The English and Foreign Languages University.

Hmar literature also includes language, religious, historical, and poetic works. Vanlal Tluonga Bapui published *Hmar Grammar* in 1996 and *Hmar Ṭawng Inchukna (A Lexical Study of the Hmar Language & Usages)* in 2012. Lalthlamuong Keivom published *Hla* and other works, including *Hmar Hla Suina*, *Zoram Khawvel* 1–8, *Zangkhaw Bungbu*, *Nun Ram, Ka Nun*, and the Hmar translation of the Bible. Other listed authors include Rochunga Pudaite, Rosiem Pudaite, John H. Pulamte, Darsanglien Ruolngul, Ngurthangkhum Sanate, Hrilrokhum Thiek, and H. Zaneisang.

## Language & religion

Hmar is a Sino-Tibetan language and belongs to the Tibeto-Burman language family. It is spoken primarily by the Hmar people of Northeast India, and speakers often use Mizo as a second language. The Hmar language uses a modified Roman script. The Hmar alphabets are known as *Hmar Hawrawp* and have 25 letters: 6 vowels and 19 consonants. The script has diacritic marks to assist pronunciation.

The Roman script was introduced during the colonial era in India by British missionaries, including Pu Buanga, identified in the source as James Herbert Lorrain. It is locally known as the “A Aw B.” The majority of Hmar people practice Christianity. Hmar communities were Christianized by Welsh missionaries in the 1910s, specifically at Senvawn village, while animism was traditionally dominant. The geographical location of Sinlung, regarded traditionally as the ancestral homeland, remains unverified and subject to scholarly debate.
