## Overview

The Burusho, also known as Burusho, Brusho, Botraj, and Hunzakut, are an ethnolinguistic group native to Yasin, Hunza-Nagar, and other valleys of Gilgit-Baltistan in northern Pakistan. A small minority lives in Jammu and Kashmir, India. Their traditional region is known as Brushal. Burushaski, their language, is a language isolate spoken in the Hunza, Nagar, northern Gilgit, Yasin, and Ishkoman valleys, with a few hundred speakers in India. The Burusho are associated with music and dance, and with educational and social views described as progressive. Their culture has also been discussed through the foodways and longevity claims connected with the Hunza people, although those claims have been disputed and lack reliable documentation.

## Music & performance

The Burusho are described as having a music and dance culture. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription supplied for this profile concerns the Burusho.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used state that the Burusho are known for dance culture.

## Festivals & rituals

The account of the Burusho community in Jammu and Kashmir states that arranged marriages are customary and that this community practices Shiite Islam, but it gives no further description of wedding ritual.

## Foodways

Burusho cuisine, also called Hunza cuisine, consists of traditional food and drink associated with the Burusho people of northern Pakistan. The sources describe a diet containing nuts, fresh vegetables, dry vegetables, mint, fruits, and seeds added with yogurt. Meals are also described as including cooked *daal* with *chappati* for dinner. The sources discuss claims that the Hunza diet produces exceptional longevity, but state that such claims have been discredited and that there is no reliable documentation validating the ages of alleged Hunza supercentenarians. Reports described in the sources associated the population with health problems including goitre, malnutrition, rheumatism, tuberculosis, cancer, and high infant mortality.

## Oral tradition & literature

Burushaski has predominantly been a spoken language. One of the earliest examples of modern Burushaski literature was poetry written by Prof. Allamah Nasiruddin Nasir Hunzai in the 1940s. Ghulamuddin Ghulam Hunzai also contributed to written Burushaski through poetry, a Burushaski translation of the Qur’an, and other religious and literary writings. A sample poem praising the University of Karachi for its role in documenting and preserving Burushaski language and literature is presented in the Burushaski Arabic alphabet with Urdu and English translations.

Tibetan sources record a *Bru zha* language of the Gilgit valley, identified in the source as apparently having been Burushaski. The sources state that no *Bru zha* manuscripts are known to have survived. They also discuss the Tibetan Buddhist text *mDo dgongs ’dus*, which is supposed to have been translated from Burushaski, although its words have not been satisfactorily demonstrated to be related to Burushaski or another language.

## Language & religion

Burushaski is a language isolate. It is spoken in Pakistan in the Hunza, Nagar, northern Gilgit, Yasin, and Ishkoman valleys, and in India in Botraj Mohalla of the Hari Parbat region in Srinagar. The principal varieties are associated with Hunza, Nagar, and Yasin; Jammu & Kashmir Burushaski has developed features that distinguish it from the varieties spoken in Pakistan. The language is also known as Werchikwar and Miśa:ski.

Burushaski was predominantly spoken rather than written. Urdu-derived and Latin-derived orthographies are both used, while modified Persian-Arabic writing systems have also been developed. The sources mention a Burushaski-Urdu Dictionary compiled in 2006, 2009, and 2013. The Burusho community in Jammu and Kashmir is described as practicing Shiite Islam.
