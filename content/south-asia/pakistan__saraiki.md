## Overview

The Saraikis are Indo-Aryan communities native to central Pakistan who identify with the Saraiki language and a shared regional identity. They primarily inhabit southern Punjab and northern Sindh, as well as most of Derajat, where southwestern Punjab, southeastern Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and northeastern Balochistan meet. Saraiki is an Indo-Aryan language of the Lahnda group and was spoken by 28.84 million people according to the 2023 Pakistani census. The language is primarily spoken in southern Punjab, northern Sindh and southern Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. The Saraiki regional identity emerged during the 1960s from a political movement that separated the Derawali, Multani and Riasti dialects from Punjabi. Saraiki culture is represented in the sources through clothing, cuisine, Sufi-associated religious life, dance, music, poetry and writing traditions.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The traditional dress of Saraiki people is the *shalwar kameez*, which is also the national dress of Pakistan. *Sajarak* is identified as an important part of both Saraiki male and female dress.

## Music & performance

The sources describe Pakistani music as incorporating South Asian, Central Asian, Middle Eastern and contemporary Western influences, with Persian, Arab and Turkish traditions among its influences. They also describe Pakistani folk traditions as reflecting local culture and language and as being passed down through generations. These traditions commonly form part of community events, rituals and festivals, with themes including village life, nature, bravery, spirituality, love, longing and separation.

For Saraiki music specifically, Ataullah Khan Esakhelvi and Shafaullah Rokhri are identified as main singers, legends and popular singers from the Saraiki belt. They state that Jhumar is performed to emotional songs and identify Kafi as the name given to the poems of Khawaja Ghulam Farid. Saraiki music is therefore represented in the supplied material chiefly through singers, poetry and dance rather than through documented instrumental practice.

The sources also discuss qawwali as a Pakistani devotional musical form associated with Sufi poetry, improvisation, divine love, longing and spiritual devotion. A qawwali group is described as comprising about 8 to 12 musicians led by a principal singer, with hand clapping and instruments including harmonium, tabla, rubab, sitar, sarangi and tanpura.

## Dance & theatre

*Jhumar*, also called *Jhoomar* and, in the Sandalbar area, *Ghumbar*, is described as a traditional Saraiki and Baloch dance in Pakistan. It is also popular in the Sandalbar areas of Punjab. The dance is slower and more rhythmic, is usually performed at wedding ceremonies, and takes place in a circle to emotional songs. The sources explain the word “Jhumar” through *Jhum* or *Jhoom*, meaning “Swaying.”

## Festivals & rituals

They state only that Jhumar is usually performed at wedding ceremonies and that Pakistani folk traditions commonly form part of community events, rituals and festivals.

## Foodways

Saraiki cuisine is described as the native cuisine of the Saraiki people in South Punjab and as one of the key parts of Pakistani and South Asian cuisines. It shares influences with neighbouring regional cuisines. Multan and Tonsa are identified as the two main hubs of Saraiki cooking.

Curry is called *bhaji* in Saraiki, whether it is made with vegetables, meat or a mixture. Curries made from moringa flower buds, locally known as *sohanjrra'n*, and *jachnar* are considered regional specialities. *Sohbat* is described as a traditional dish of Damaan and of the Saraiki belts of Bhakkar, Layyah, Tonsa, Vehoa and Dera Ghazi Khan. Rice dishes include *pulao*, *bhat*, particularly *dal bhat*, and Cholistani-style pulao made with camel meat.

*Multani chaamp* consists of spiced, skewered lamb chops grilled over charcoal. *Sajji* is identified as a regional speciality, particularly in the Sulaiman Mountains region of Southern Punjab. Common snacks include samosa, pakorra, corn on the cob, dahi bhallay, gol gappay and boiled potatoes mixed with yogurt.

*Sohan halwa* is a traditional speciality of southern Punjab, particularly Multan. It is prepared by boiling water, sugar, milk and cornflour until solidified; saffron provides flavour and ghee prevents sticking. Almonds, pistachios and cardamom seeds may be added. Other sweets include *gurr ala bhat* or *gurr walay chaawal*, *zarda* rice, siwayyan, gulab jaman, barfi, firni, kheer and ras malai. *Thandai* is particularly popular in summer. Mango is a summer fruit, while dates, jamun, shahtoot, falsa, banana and guava are also listed as traditional fruits.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources associate Saraiki literary identity especially with the poetry of the Sufi saint Khawaja Ghulam Farid. His poems, known as *Kafi*, are still famous, and his poetry is described as an emotional source for the language and as an identity symbol.

Shakir Shujabadi is described as a well-recognized modern poet. His named books include *Kalam-e-Shakir*, *Khuda Janey*, *Shakir Diyan Ghazlan*, *Peelay Patr*, *Munafqan Tu Khuda Bachaway* and *Shakir De Dohray*. The Department of Saraiki at Islamia University, Bahawalpur was established in 1989, and the Department of Saraiki at Bahauddin Zakariya University, Multan was established in 2006. Saraiki is taught in schools and colleges and at degree level at the Allama Iqbal Open University at Islamabad. The Al-Khair University at Bhimbir has Pakistani Linguistics Departments offering M.Phil. and Ph.D. in Saraiki.

## Language & religion

Saraiki is an Indo-Aryan language of the Lahnda group and a member of the Indo-Aryan subdivision of the Indo-Iranian branch of the Indo-European language family. It has partial mutual intelligibility with Standard Punjabi and shares vocabulary and morphology with it, while also having important grammatical features in common with Sindhi. It is described as a transitional language between Punjabi and Sindhi.

Dialect names listed in the sources include Central Saraiki, including Multani; Southern Saraiki; Sindh Siraiki; and Northern Saraiki, or Thali. The language is written in Punjab with the Arabic-derived Urdu alphabet, including seven diacritically modified letters for implosives and extra nasals. In Sindh, the Sindhi alphabet is used. The calligraphic styles are Naskh and Nastaʿlīq. Historically, *kiṛakkī* or *laṇḍā* and Multani were used, while Khojiki has also been in use.

Most Saraikis are predominantly followers of Sunni Islam. A small minority follows Christianity in Pakistan. The sources state that there used to be a large Hindu and Sikh minority, many of whom migrated to India after Partition of India in 1947. Saraikistan is renowned for its Sufi heritage; Multan is known as the city of saints and has the shrines of Baha-ud-din Zakariya, Shah Rukn-e-Alam and Shah Yousef Gardez. Uch Sharif has been a centre of the Qadiriyya Sufi order.
