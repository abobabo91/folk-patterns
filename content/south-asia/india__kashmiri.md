## Overview

Kashmiris are an Indo-Aryan ethnolinguistic group who speak Kashmiri and originate from the Kashmir Valley in Indian-administered Jammu and Kashmir. Most Kashmiri speakers live in the Kashmir Valley and surrounding areas of Jammu and Kashmir, where they form the majority; Srinagar is identified as a major city of the region. The sources give approximately 6.8 million speakers of Kashmiri and related dialects in Jammu and Kashmir and among the Kashmiri diaspora in other Indian states, while another source describes around 7 million Kashmiris as Kashmiri speakers. Kashmiri culture includes spoken language, literature, cuisine, architecture, traditions, music and dance. It reflects Hinduism, Buddhism and Islam, as well as Persian and Central Asian influences after Islamic rule.

## Material culture

### Architecture

They do mention the city of Srinagari, identified with Srinagar, which was built during the reign of Ashoka, and describe dwellings at Neolithic sites in the Kashmir Valley, including pits at Burzahom and Gufkral. The Burzahom evidence includes stone hearths at ground level near the mouths of pits, while Gufkral is described as having dwelling pits without hearths or fireplaces inside.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Archaeological evidence connects everyday objects with early settlement in the Kashmir Valley. Burzahom produced stone tools, rectangular harvesters with curved cutting edges, harpoons, mortar and pestle forms, and harvesters made from stone and bone. Period II structures at the site included a dish with a hollow stand and a globular pot. At Gufkral, piercers and scrapers were used in processing animals. Earthen thalis, handis, flat plates, pedestalled cups and edged bowls are reported from Indo-Greek-period and later excavated sites including Semthan, Harwan and Kanispur. The sources also describe wooden boats used in the floating vegetable garden on Dal Lake.

## Music & performance

Kashmiri music includes **Sufiana Kalam**, **Chakri**, **Henzae**, **Rouf** or **Wanwun**, and **Ladishah**. Sufiana Kalam is described as the classical music of Kashmir. It uses its own maqams and is accompanied by the Rubab, Kashmiri saz, Santoor, wasool and dokra. A dance based on Sufiana Kalam is called hafiz nagma.

Chakri is one of the most popular forms of traditional music in Jammu and Kashmir. It is a responsorial song form with instrumental parts and may be performed in folk and religious contexts by Kashmiri Muslims and Kashmiri Pandits. Its instruments include harmonium, Rubab, sarangi, Ghatam or Noet, geger, Tumbaknaer and chimta. Chakri was used for stories including Yousuf-Zulaikha and Laila-Majnun. It ends with Rouf, and its fast concluding notes may also be called Rouf. Chakri is an important part of the Henna Night, or Ma'enzi raat, during weddings.

Henzae is described as an ancient form of singing practiced by Kashmiri Pandits at their festivals. It has archaic features and is presented as the oldest form of Kashmiri folk singing. Ladishah is a sarcastic and humorous form of singing. Singers move from village to village, generally during the harvesting period, composing songs on the spot about local cultural, social and political issues.

The sources name numerous instruments. The Rubab is made from mulberry wood and has three strings; the Santoor is an important accompaniment for Kashmiri folk and Sufiyana music. The Tumbaknaer is a baked-clay drum with a sheep-skin base, while the Daf has a round frame, metal jingles and a head made from plastic or goat skin. Other named instruments include Saz-e-Kashmir, Nout, Sitar, Kashmiri sarang, Surnai, Geger, Dokra, Nagada, Shankh, Swarnai, Khasya, Thaluz and Nai Flute.

## Dance & theatre

**Rouf** is a traditional dance usually performed by women at marriages, other social functions and cultural activities. It is also connected with the ending of Chakri performances. The sources describe Rouf as a dance form rather than only a musical conclusion.

**Hafiz nagma** is identified as a dance based on Sufiana Kalam.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources list primary festivals of Kashmiri Muslims as **Eid al-Adha**, **Eid al-Fitr**, **Shab-e-Barat**, **Shab-e-Meraj**, **Eid-e-Meelad-un-Nabi** and **Nowruz**. Primary festivals of Kashmiri Hindus are **Herath**, identified with Shivaratri, **Khetchmaavas**, **Zyeth Atham**, **Tiky Tsoram**, **Pann** and **Gaab Batt**.

Music and dance are associated with life-cycle and social occasions. Rouf is performed at marriages and similar functions. Chakri is an important part of the Henna Night, or Ma'enzi raat, during weddings. Henzae is practiced by Kashmiri Pandits at their festivals.

Kashmiri food traditions also include seasonal practices. During summer, vegetables are peeled, chopped, salted and sun-dried for winter. The harvesting period is a setting for Ladishah performances. Spring is associated with picnics and blossoms of almond, peach and cherry, while the harvest of lotus stem begins in September. During winter, families gather in kitchens around long-cooked food, including shab deg.

## Foodways

Rice has been a staple food in Kashmir since ancient times, and meat is a central part of the cuisine. Kashmiri cuisine is generally meat-heavy. Pandits prefer lamb or sheep, while Muslims prefer goats, and the sources describe differences in the use of onions, garlic and shallots. Wazwan is a multi-course meal prepared by skilled chefs called Wazas. Wazwan dishes with rice, vegetables and salad are prepared for weddings and special occasions such as Eid.

Named foods include **rogan josh**, **yakhaen**, **ab gosht**, **riste** and **goshtabh**. The sources also mention mutton, goat’s meat, fish, game, beef, rabbit and geese. Vegetables include **haakh**, or collard greens or kale; **monj Haak**, or kohlrabi; **tsochael**, or mallow; **bamchoont**, or quince; **nadur**, or lotus stem; **praan**, or shallots; **mawal**, or cockscomb; and **gor**, or water-chestnut.

Kashmir is known for baking traditions. Named breads and baked goods include **sheermal**, **bakarkhani**, **lavas**, **tsochwor**, **tsot**, **kulcha** and **girdas**. Kashmiri Bakarkhani is described as crisp, layered and sprinkled with sesame seeds. Girdas and lavas are served with butter.

Beverages include **Noon Chai**, also called Sheer Chai, and **Kahweh** or **Kehew**. The sources describe Kahweh and saffron as part of the atmosphere of the autumn harvesting season. Sun-dried vegetables are called **hokh syun**. Varieties include dried tomatoes, fenugreek leaves, lotus stem, shallot leaves, mint, bottle gourd, turnip slices, quince, collard greens and spinach. In the Charar Sharief area of central Kashmir, dried pears called **tang haech** are considered a delicacy.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kashmiri mythology includes folklore, myths and legends associated with the Kashmir Valley. Named mythical creatures include **Yach**, **Vigin**, **Bramrachokh**, **Rantas** and **Pasukh**. **Himal and Nagaray** is identified as one of the most popular examples of Kashmiri folklore.

The sources say that Chakri was used to tell stories such as **Yousuf-Zulaikha** and **Laila-Majnun**. Ladishah singers compose humorous and sarcastic songs on the spot, addressing the cultural, social and political conditions of the village where they perform. The songs are described as reflecting local truth while remaining entertaining.

Kashmiri culture is associated with texts including the Bharata Natya Shastra, the Panchatantra, the Yoga Sutras, the Vigyan Bharaiv Tantra, Sapndi Karkika Tantra Loka and Para-Trisika-Vivarana. The sources also mention verses by Lalleshwari in Kashmiri written in the Sharada script.

## Language & religion

Kashmiri, also called **Koshur**, is an Indo-Aryan language of the Dardic branch. It is spoken primarily in the Kashmir Valley and surrounding hills of Jammu and Kashmir, with speakers also in Azad Jammu and Kashmir. Kashmiri is among the 22 scheduled languages of India and became an official language in the Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir in 2020. Poguli and Kishtwari are closely related and are spoken in the mountains south of the Kashmir Valley.

Kashmiri was traditionally written in the Sharada script. Modern Kashmiri is written primarily in the Perso-Arabic script, as well as Devanagari; Sharada remains associated with religious ceremonies and rituals of Kashmiri Pandits. Persian was used as the court language from the 14th century and was replaced by Urdu in 1889 during Dogra rule.

Kashmiri Hindus are Saraswat Brahmins, known by the exonym Pandit. Kashmiri Muslims and Kashmiri Pandits are described as belonging to the same stock, with Kashmiri Muslims descended from Kashmiri Hindus who converted to Islam. The sources describe Kashmiri culture as shaped by Hinduism, Buddhism and Islam, with Persian and Central Asian influences after Islamic rule.
