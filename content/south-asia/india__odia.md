## Overview

The Odia are an Indo-Aryan ethno-linguistic group native to Odisha, an eastern coastal state of India, where they form a majority. Significant Odia-speaking populations also live in Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand and West Bengal. The 2011 census recorded 37.52 million Odia speakers in India, while the worldwide number is given as 50 million. Odia belongs to the Indo-Aryan language family and is also spoken in parts of Assam, Tripura, Gujarat, Maharashtra and other regions. Odisha became a separate province and the first officially recognized language-based state of India in 1936. Odia culture is represented by distinctive painting, textiles, temple architecture, classical and folk music, dance, puppetry, literature and cuisine.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Sambalpuri kapta** — This is one of four major types of handloom saree named in the source. Odisha sarees use a tie-and-dye technique to create motifs, a method described as unique to the region.

**Sambalpuri Bandha** — This is another named Sambalpuri handloom type. The sources identify Sambalpuri textiles as an important part of Odisha’s artistic culture.

**Sambalpuri Bomkai** — This handloom saree is listed among the major types of Odisha sarees.

**Sambalpuri Saptaper** — This is the fourth major handloom type identified in the source. Odisha sarees are available in colours including cream, maroon, brown and rust.

**Pattachitra** — Pattachitra is a traditional painting of Odisha made on canvas, using natural colours and rich colourful application. Its subjects are mainly mythological and are especially inspired by Jagannatha and Baishnaba sects. The tradition is associated particularly with Raghurajpur, a heritage crafts village near Puri.

**Tala Chitra** — Tala Chitra refers to palm-leaf engravings, named among the visual-art attractions of Odisha.

Motif vocabulary. The sources mention motifs and designs in Pattachitra.

### Clothing & dress

Western-style dress has gained acceptance among men in cities and towns, although traditional garments such as *Dhoti*, *Kurtha* and *Gamucha* remain preferred during festivals and religious occasions. Women are described as wearing the *Sari*, including the Sambalpuri Sari, or the *Shalwar kameez*. Western attire is becoming more popular among younger women in urban areas. The sources discuss colours, varieties and tie-and-dye techniques in Odisha sarees.

### Architecture

Kaḷinga architectural style developed in ancient Kalinga, formerly known as Utkal, and in present-day Odisha. It consists of three temple types: *Rekha Deula*, *Pidha Deula* and *Khakhara Deula*. The Rekha Deula and Khakhara Deula contain the sanctum sanctorum, while the Pidha Deula forms outer dancing and offering halls. The first two types are associated with Vishnu, Surya and Shiva temples, while Khakhara Deula is mainly associated with Chamunda and Durga temples. Named monuments include the Jagannath Temple in Puri, the Sun Temple at Konark, the Lingaraj Temple, Mukteshvara Temple, Rajarani Temple, Parashurameshvara Temple and the Buddhist sites of Lalitgiri, Udayagiri and Baitala Deula.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources name the stone utensils of Nilagiri in Balasore as a cultural attraction. They also state that Odia food is traditionally served on brass or bronze metal plates, banana leaves or disposable plates made from sal leaves. The Jagannath Temple kitchen uses wood-burning clay hearths called *chulas*. No further catalogue of ceramics, metalwork, tools or household objects is provided.

## Music & performance

Odissi music is the traditional classical music of Odisha. It originated as a *seva* in the Jagannatha temple of Puri and was developed by composers including Jayadeva, Upendra Bhanja and Dinakrusna Dasa. The source describes five broad categories: Tribal Music, Folk music, Light Music, Light-Classical Music and Classical Music. It also states that Odissi music is over two thousand five hundred years old and that, by the eleventh century, forms known as *Triswari*, *Chatuhswari* and *Panchaswari* had developed into a classical style.

Named folk forms include *Jogi Gita*, *Kendara Gita*, *Dhuduki Badya*, *Prahallada Nataka*, *Pala*, *Sankirtana*, *Mogal Tamasa*, *Gitinatya*, *Kandhei Nacha*, *Kela Nacha*, *Ghoda Nacha*, *Danda Nacha* and *Daskathia*.

*Pala* is a form of balladry combining theatre, classical Odissi music, Odia and Sanskrit poetry, wit and humour. Its forms include *Baithaki Pala*, in which performers sit; *Thia Pala*, in which they stand; and *Badi Pala*, a competitive form involving two groups. *Daskathia* is identified as another Odia ballad tradition.

## Dance & theatre

Odissi dance is a classical form with a tradition of 2,000 years. It is associated with the divine love of Krishna and Radha and with compositions by Jayadeva, who lived in the 12th century CE. The source states that Odissi dance nearly became extinct during the British period and was revived after India’s independence by proponents including Guru Deba Prasad Das, Guru Pankaj Charan Das, Guru Raghunath Dutta and Kelucharan Mohapatra.

*Chhou* is a tribal martial dance associated with Mayurbhanj princely state and performed in West Bengal, Jharkhand and Odisha. Its three subtypes are Seraikella Chhou, Purulia Chhou and Mayurbhanj Chhou, named after their places of development.

*Mahari Dance* originated in Odisha’s temples and was performed by Devadasis known as Maharis. The dancers were associated with ceremonies connected to Lord Jagannath and with processions such as Ratha Yatra, Jhulana Yatra and Dola Yatra. *Gotipua* is a dance performed by a single boy and is associated with Raghurajpur near Puri. *Jhumair* is a folk dance from North and Western Odisha, performed during harvest season and festivals.

*Ravana Chhaya* is a traditional shadow-puppetry form. Flat figures made of deer skin or goat hide are held behind a white cloth screen at night, while a light casts their shadows onto it.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe a saying in Odia, “Baarah maase, terah parba,” referring to thirteen festivals in a year. Named festivals include Ratha Yatra, Durga Puja, Raja, Maha Shivratri, Kartika Purnima, Dola Purnima, Ganesh Puja, Chandan Yatra, Snana Yatra, Makar Mela, Chhau Festival and Nuakhai. The annual Ratha Yatra is closely connected with the Jagannath Temple in Puri and draws pilgrims and visitors from many places.

The Jagannath tradition is central to religious and festival life. The deities Subhadra, Balabhadra and Lord Jagannath reside in the Jagannath Temple, and the temple’s ceremonial calendar includes Ratha Yatra, Jhulana Yatra and Dola Yatra. *Adhara paṇa*, a milk- and chhena-based drink, is offered to the trinity at the end of Ratha Yatra.

The sources also mention Makar Sankrati, Raja, Kumara Purnima, Prathamashtami, Boula Amavasya, Chitau Amavasya and Pana Sankranti in connection with particular foods.

## Foodways

Rice is the staple cereal and is eaten throughout the day. Lentils, including pigeon peas and moong beans, are major ingredients. Common vegetables include pumpkin, gourd, plantains, jackfruit and papaya, while mustard, cumin, fenugreek, aniseed and kalonji form the five-spice blend *Pancha phutana*. Mustard oil is used in some dishes, while ghee made from cow’s milk is preferred in temples. Odia cuisine is described as using less oil and being less spicy than some other regional Indian cuisines.

Named dishes include *pakhala*, fermented rice with water and yoghurt; *dalma*, made from dal and vegetables; *santula*; *guguni*; *besara*; *machha besara*; *chingudi malai tarkari*; *kankada jhola*; *badi chura*; *poda pitha*; *enduri pitha*; *arisa pitha*; *manda pitha*; *chhena poda*; *rasagola*; *rasabali*; *kheeri*; *khira sagara* and *chhena jhili*. Seafood is eaten mainly in coastal areas, including crab, prawn, lobster and fish.

Temple food has a specific place in Odia culture. The Jagannath Temple’s offering is called *Mahaprasad*, or *Sathie Pauti*, and consists of 60 recipes or dishes. Its dish names are described as a sacred secret known only to the *Mahasuar*, the chief cook. Traditional drinks include *handia*, a rice beer popular among the tribes of Odisha, and *Tanka toraṇi*, a fermented rice-water drink prepared in the Jagannath Temple.

## Oral tradition & literature

Odia literature is associated with the *Charyapadas*, composed in the 7th to 9th centuries, and with the development of *Charya Sahitya*. The first great poet named in the sources is Sarala Das, who wrote the *Sarala Mahabharata*, *Chandi Purana* and *Vilanka Ramayana*. Arjuna Das wrote *Rama-Bibha*, described as the first long poem in Odia.

The Panchasakhas were five poets associated with Utkaliya Vaishnavism: Balarama Dasa, Jagannatha Dasa, Achyutananda Dasa, Ananta Dasa and Jasobanta Dasa. Their works include the *Odia Bhagabata*, *Jagamohana Ramayana*, *Lakshmi Purana*, *Haribansa* and *Gobinda Chandra*. Later literary figures include Jayadeva, Upendra Bhanja, Dinakrushna Dasa and Fakir Mohan Senapati. The sources identify *Pala* and *Daskathia* as oral-performance traditions combining poetry, music, theatre and humour.

## Language & religion

Odia is a classical Indo-Aryan language and the official language of Odisha. It is also an official language of India and the second official language of Jharkhand. Varieties named in the sources include Baleswari, Kataki, Khurda Odia, Ganjami, Sundargadi, Sambalpuri and Desia, alongside community varieties such as Bodo Parja, Bhatri, Reli and Kupia.

Odia uses the Odia script, also known as the Kalinga script, a Brahmic script whose earliest trace is dated to 1051 AD. Its curved appearance is associated with writing on palm leaves. *Karani Script*, or *Karani Lipi*, was used by Karana scribes for administrative and literary purposes during the medieval and early modern periods.

Odisha has continuous traditions of Hinduism, Buddhism and Jainism. The Jagannath Temple in Puri is considered holy by Hindus, Buddhists and Jains. The state also has Christian and Muslim minorities, while the majority of its population is Hindu.
