## Overview

The Gujarati people, or Gujaratis, are an Indo-Aryan ethnolinguistic group native to Gujarat in Western India. Most Gujaratis in India live in Gujarat, while significant communities live in Mumbai, Pune, Delhi, Kolkata, Chennai, Bangalore, Kochi, and elsewhere. Gujarati diaspora communities occur across India and in Pakistan, the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada, East Africa, South Africa, Oman, Southeast Asia, Australia, New Zealand, and other countries. Gujarati is an Indo-Aryan language, descended from Old Gujarati, and is spoken predominantly by Gujarati people. Gujarati culture includes mercantile and seafaring histories, religious pluralism, distinctive dance and music forms, fairs, wedding rites, cuisine, literature, and theatre.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources name clothing and embroidery.

### Clothing & dress

Gujarati dress described in the sources includes the *choli*, *ghaghra*, and *bandhani dupatta* worn by women performing Garba. These garments may be exquisitely embroidered and set with mirrors. Women also wear necklaces, bracelets, and anklets. The sources describe men’s Garba clothing as *kehediyu*, *chudidar*, and a turban. At the Chitra–Vichitra Mela, men generally wear a blue shirt, *dhoti*, and a red or saffron turban, while women wear *ghaghras* and heavy silver jewellery. At the Trinetreshwar Mahadev Fair, tribal youth wear colourful *dhotis*, waistcoats, and turbans, while village women wear colourful finery.

### Architecture

They mention the *mandap*, a decorated canopy used for Hindu weddings, whose four pillars represent the parents of the bride and groom. They also mention temples, mosques, community centres, the BAPS Swaminarayan Temple in Neasden, London, the Jain Temple in Leicester, and the Masjid Umar. In Gujarati cuisine, *undhiyu* is traditionally cooked upside down underground in earthen pots fired from above.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention brass and copper utensils sold at the Bhavnath Mahadev Mela, silver ornaments sold at the Chitra–Vichitra Mela, and embroidered umbrellas associated with the Trinetreshwar Mahadev Fair. The wedding rite described as *Samagree* uses crushed sandalwood, herbs, sugar, rice, ghee, and twigs offered into the sacred fire.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Gujarati adornment described in the sources includes necklaces, bracelets, anklets, heavy silver jewellery, and the ornate silver jewellery worn by women at the Chitra–Vichitra Mela. At that fair, women use liquid *kumkum* to colour their cheeks and lips and outline their eyes with *kajal*. Gujarati Garba dress includes mirror decoration on women’s garments.

## Music & performance

Gujarati folk music includes devotional songs called *bhajan*, whose forms and themes include *Prabhatiya* and Garba. The Barot, Charan, and Gadhvi communities have preserved and enriched storytelling traditions with or without music, including *Doha*, *Sorathaa*, and *Chhand*. *Dayro* and *Lokvarta* are performances in which people gather to hear religious and social messages. *Marasiyas* are an elegiac form of music originating from *Marsiya*. *Fattanna* or *Lagna-geets* are light songs and music played during marriages.

The sources name *Shehnai* and *Dhol* in the Gujarati Hindu wedding procession. The wedding day begins with the playing of the *Mangal-Vadya*, including these instruments. The sources also mention a tradition of *Haveli Sangeet* and identify Faiyaz Khan and Pandit Omkarnath Thakur among classical musicians and composers. Folk music performers named in the sources include Kinjal Dave, Kirtidan Gadhvi, Geeta Rabari, Jignesh Kaviraj, Arvind Barot, Vaishali Harin Gohil, Hemant Chauhan, Bhikhudan Gadhvi, Diwaliben Bhil, Hemu Gadhavi, Harshida Raval, Praful Dave, and Aishwarya Majmudar.

## Dance & theatre

The sources identify Garba, Dandiya Raas, Tippani, Padhar, Sidi, and Dangi as Gujarati folk dances. *Dandiya Raas* is performed by men and women in two concentric circles, one moving clockwise and the other counter-clockwise. Dancers carry two bamboo sticks called *dandiyas*. It is associated with Navratri and is described as energetic, colourful, playful, and romantic.

*Garba* is mainly performed by women in a circular formation, with singing and rhythmic clapping around the goddess Ambaji. Men may also participate. *Padhar*, performed by a rural community around Nal Lake, imitates the movements of roving mariners and undulating sea waves. *Garbi* was originally performed by men returning from battle, accompanied by couplets and amorous songs sung by Charanswar; women now also participate.

The traditional theatre forms named in the sources are **Bhavai** and **Akhyana**. Bhavai is a musical performance of stage plays. *Dayro* also combines singing and conversation and reflects on human nature.

## Festivals & rituals

Gujarati Hindu weddings include a sequence of named rites. *Gol Dhana* marks an engagement through the eating and distribution of jaggery and coriander seeds. The wedding begins with *Varghodo*, the procession from the groom’s house to the bride’s house, followed by *Swagatam*, *Ganesh Puja*, *Madhuparka*, *Kanyaa Daan*, *Vivaaha*, *Mangal Phera*, *Saptapadi*, *Mangal Sutra*, *Suhaag* or *Sindhoordana*, and *Aashirvaad*. During *Saptapadi*, the bride and groom take seven steps around the sacred fire and make seven promises.

The sources name Navratri, Dassara, Diwali, Holi, and Mahashivratri among Gujarati festivals. The Bhavnath Mahadev Mela is held for five days in February during Mahashivratri. Dangs Darbar takes place in March in Ahwa a few days before Holi. Chitra–Vichitra Mela occurs in March, a fortnight after Holi. Dhrang Fair is held in April. Trinetreshwar Mahadev Fair takes place in September–October during the first week of Bhadrapad. Vautha Mela occurs in November during Kartika Purnima, and Shamlaji Melo is also held in November for about two weeks.

The sources describe fairs involving religious rituals, tribal dancing, songs, drums, animal trading, marriage-seeking, food stalls, cattle shows, and craft or ornament sales. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription is supplied for this group.

## Foodways

A typical Gujarati *thali* includes *rotli*, *dal* or curry, rice, and *shaak*. It may also include pulses or whole beans called *kathor*, snacks called *farsaan*, and sweets called *mishthaan*. Named farsaan include *dhokla*, *pathra*, *samosa*, and *fafda*. Staples include homemade *khichdi*, *chaas*, and pickles. A *vaghar* is a mixture of spices heated in oil and used with vegetables and dals.

Gujarati food commonly combines sweet, salty, and spicy flavours. Sugar or jaggery is often added to vegetables and dal. The sources describe a predominantly vegetarian food culture, although Koli Patel, Ghanchi, Muslim communities, and Parsis may include seafood, chicken, and mutton. Coastal Gujarat’s Kharwa community has a cuisine based on fresh and dried fish, including pomfrets, khandwas, gedadas, surmai, prawns, crabs, lobster, and *narsinga*.

Named breads include *rotla*, *bhakri*, *phulka rotli*, *parotha*, *puri*, *thepla*, *dhebra*, and *juvar no rotlo*. Named dishes include *undhiyu*, *kadhi*, *dal dhokli*, *doodhpak*, *shrikhand*, *mohanthal*, *jalebi*, *lapsi*, *ladoo*, *basundi*, and *ghughra*. *Undhiyu* is a mixed vegetable casserole associated with winter and is served at weddings and banquets in its Surti form. Drinks include *jaggery water*, *nimbupani*, and *chhas*. Regular fasting with diets limited to milk, dried fruits, and nuts is also described.

## Oral tradition & literature

Gujarati literature’s history is traced in the sources to 1000 AD. Named writers and poets include Hemchandracharya, Narsinh Mehta, Mirabai, Akho, Premanand Bhatt, Shamal Bhatt, Dayaram, Dalpatram, Narmad, Govardhanram Tripathi, Mahatma Gandhi, K. M. Munshi, Umashankar Joshi, Suresh Joshi, Pannalal Patel, Rajendra Keshavlal Shah, Kavi Kant, and Kalapi. *Saraswatichandra* is identified as a landmark novel by Govardhanram Tripathi.

Gujarati theatre owes much to *Bhavai*, while *Dayro* combines singing and conversation and reflects on human nature. The Swaminarayan *paramhanso* contributed prose such as *Vachanamrut* and poetry in the form of *bhajans*. The Barot, Charan, and Gadhvi communities are described as preserving storytelling traditions.

## Language & religion

Gujarati is an Indo-Aryan language descended from Old Gujarati, which developed from Prakrit. The sources divide its history into Old Gujarati, Middle Gujarati, and Modern Gujarati. Gujarati is written in the Gujarati script, an *abugida* related to Devanagari. The Gujarati alphabet is called *Kakko*. Named varieties include Standard Gujarati, Saurashtra, Amdawadi Gujarati, Kathiawari, Parsi, and Lisan ud-Dawat. Kutchi is often called a Gujarati dialect, although most linguists consider it closer to Sindhi.

Gujarati is the official language of Gujarat and the union territory of Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu. The Gujarati people are predominantly Hindu, with Muslim, Jain, Christian, Sikh, Buddhist, Parsi, Jewish, and Baháʼí communities. Gujarat is described as a home of Hindu, Jain, Muslim, Sufi, and Zoroastrian traditions. Parsis migrated from Greater Iran to Gujarat and Sindh between the 8th and 10th century CE, and Udvada is identified as a primary Parsi holy site.
