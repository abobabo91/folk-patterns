## Overview

The Gondi are a large central Indian group whose historic territory stretched across Gondwana from eastern Madhya Pradesh to western Odisha. Their material culture—huts, hearths, ploughs, drums and god-symbols—was documented as a working whole rather than as isolated crafts. They speak Gondi, an endangered language, and follow their own religion called Koyapunem, founded by Pari Kupar Lingo, though Hindu, Sarnai, Christian and Muslim practices have mixed in.

## Material culture

Gond material culture relies on natural materials—clay, fiber, wood, bamboo, brass and iron—shaped into practical household and ritual objects decorated with geometric and figural motifs.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Woven textiles are rare; the decorative work is carried by leaf and bark work instead.

- **Woollen blanket** — A blanket of woollen cloth, the only substantial woven piece in the household collection.
- **Leaf head-dress** — Composite head-dresses built from wild date leaves with loops, folded triangles, and interlocking leaf hoops, sometimes faced with red and gold tin-foil.
- **Wool waist-string** — A man's waist-string of red-dyed wool strands finished with purple, yellow and white tassels and a loop to fasten it.
- **Bark head-band and head-ring** — A head-band cut from bark and tied with twisted fiber carrying a bark disc, and a separate head-ring of reddish-brown leaves bound with bark strips.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress is simple; ceremonial dress for Diwali and festivals includes peacock-feathered turbans, saffron attire and special leaf and bark head coverings.

- **Cotton blouse** — A woman's blouse of cotton cloth.
- **Peacock-feathered turban** — Worn by Gond communities of Adilabad during Diwali celebrations with saffron attire.
- **Snake-god iron necklace** — Men wear an iron necklace of interlocking figure-of-eight links representing a snake as a god symbol.
- **Flattened grey-metal armlet** — Women wear this as a charm for protection.
- **Pangolin-scale amulet** — A pierced pangolin scale threaded on twisted fiber cord and worn as a cure for pain.

### Architecture

Gond houses are rectangular clay-and-fiber huts with pointed fiber roofs, small windows and doors on the long sides, often set in a yard with hearths and stands.

- **Rectangular hut** — A clay-and-fiber structure with rectangular doors on two long sides, a small blocked window, and rough relief work around the frames.
- **Pointed fiber roof** — Held down by L-shaped twigs laid over it and the whole tied together.
- **Horseshoe-shaped hearth** — Built of earth and decorated with chevron-and-dot patterns at each end.
- **Clay hearth** — Circular body with a mushroom-shaped top, used for cooking and heat.
- **Rainshield** — Overlapping orange-brown leaves sewn together with slivers of wood or bamboo and lashed to a bamboo frame for outdoor work.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Iron, brass, wood and bamboo are worked into tools, god symbols, musical instruments and hunting gear with incised bands and geometric decoration.

- **Tobacco pipe** — Made of earthenware.
- **Friction drum** — Built of earthenware with skin and feathers.
- **Brass round-bellied vessel** — With a flaring lip, scored base and incised bands around the neck.
- **Iron sickle** — With a blade set into thick bamboo for harvesting.
- **Bamboo pellet bow** — With a plaited pouch for hunting, paired with barbed and tanged arrows.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Cast metal dominates jewelry, with brass and white metal worked into hinged bracelets, anklets and ear-ornaments decorated with flowers and ovals.

- **Hinged brass bracelet** — Made in two parts with a central ring throwing off ovals and stemmed flowers, pegged shut with wood and tied with twisted fiber.
- **Interlocking metal anklet** — Pairs of many separate grey-metal plates each pierced twice, threaded on twisted fiber and patterned with dots of different sizes.
- **White-metal ear-ornament** — Cast as a ten-petalled flower with a thick wire through the centre.
- **White-metal neck-ring** — Decorated with coiled wire and fastens with a hook and two rings.
- **Iron penannular band** — One with square section and spiral twist, another twisted with each end curled back, worn as god symbols.

## Music & performance

Village musicians play kettledrums, friction drums, brass cymbals, bamboo pipes and musical horns that match a full ensemble.

- **Kettledrum** — Turned from dark brown wood with cow hide stretched over it and lashed with leather thongs, standing on a small circle at the base.
- **Brass cymbals** — A pair of incised discs joined through their centres by a textile cord.
- **Bamboo pipe** — Closed at one end by the node with a hole pierced below it and four finger-holes at the other, banded with black paint and incised lines.
- **Musical horn** — Made from a buffalo horn with the tip removed, pierced with many holes at the broad end and a twisted fiber loop.
- **Fiddle** (*pardhan*) — Played by the clan bard (pardhan) to calm the violent tendencies of a clan's Persa Pen.

## Dance & theatre

Two named dances mark Gond festivals: the sua dance honours Shiva and Parvati after Diwali in Chhattisgarh, and the Gussadi dance is performed in Adilabad during Diwali.

- **Sua dance** (*sua*) — Performed by women in Chhattisgarh after Diwali in honour of Shiva and Parvati, with the belief that a parrot will carry their sadness to lovers.
- **Gussadi dance** (*Gussadi*) — Celebrated in Adilabad during Diwali, with performers wearing peacock-feathered turbans and saffron attire moving in festive groups.
- **Wooden castanets** — Used as dance equipment with plain pierced lugs for handles, one rounded and one squared.
- **Bamboo rattle** — Pierced by two twisted iron handles strung with iron rings, belonging to hunting rather than dance.

## Festivals & rituals

Major festivals include Pola, Phag, Dassera and Diwali; worship of the clan's Persa Pen is the central rite conducted by village priests and clan members.

- **Pola festival** (*Pola*) — A major Gond festival.
- **Phag festival** (*Phag*) — A major Gond festival.
- **Dassera festival** (*Dassera*) — Celebrated with an image of Ravana riding an elephant carried in procession by Gondi inhabitants of Paraswadi, honouring him as the tenth dharmaguru.
- **Mahua flower ceremony** — The Gonds of Adilabad hold the first ceremonies of the year when Mahua flowers bloom; the flowers yield a purifying liquor.
- **First Fruit festival** — All members of a saga eat with the seniormost member of its seniormost pari, and Persa Pen is worshipped with sacrificial food cooked by soira saga members.

## Foodways

Few everyday foods are recorded; instead the sources emphasise ritual drink, sacrifice and communal meals.

- **Mahua liquor** (*Mahua*) — Pressed from Mahua flowers, considered purifying and tied to the first ceremonies of the year.
- **Sacrificial meal** — Cooked by two members of a soira saga at worship of Persa Pen and served saga by saga in order of precedence.
- **First Fruit meal** — All saga members eat together with their seniormost kinsman at the First Fruit festival.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Gonds have their own version of the Ramayana, the Gond Ramayani, and cycles of myth tied to pilgrimage sites and clan origin stories.

- **Gond Ramayani** — Seven stories set after the main events of the Ramayana, drawn from oral folk legend, with Lakshmana as protagonist finding a bride.
- **Kachchargardh cave cycle** — A narrative and charter for a pilgrimage site in which Mata Kali Kankali's children imprisoned by Shambu and Gaura are freed when the bard Hirasuka Patalir is enlisted by Pari Kupar Lingo.
- **Clan origin myths** — Name the ancestral villages from which each saga dispersed, some still identified with places on the ground.
- **Wedding songs** — Folk literature sung in the Gondi language.
- **Gunjala Gondi Lipi** — Brahmi-based manuscripts dated to about 1750 and rediscovered by University of Hyderabad researchers.

## Language & religion

Gondi is an endangered South-Central Dravidian language; most Gonds follow Koyapunem, the way of nature, founded by Pari Kupar Lingo and honouring the high god Baradeo.

- **Gondi language** (*Gondi*) — A South-Central Dravidian language related to Telugu with two genders and aspirated stops; about three million speakers against nearly thirteen million Gonds.
- **Koyapunem** — The Gond religion, meaning the way of nature, founded by Pari Kupar Lingo and worshipping the high god Baradeo alongside clan, village and ancestral deities.
- **Dorla dialect** (*Dorla*) — One of the main Gondi dialects, divided by the treatment of initial s.
- **Koya dialect** (*Koya*) — One of the main Gondi dialects.
- **Gunjala Gondi Lipi script** (*Gunjala Gondi Lipi*) — A native script for Gondi named after Munshi Mangal Singh Masaram's 1928 Brahmi-based script.

## Glossary

- *Koitur* — What the Gondi call themselves.
- *Koi* — Alternative name for the Gondi.
- *Gondwana* — Historic territory of the Gonds in central India.
- *Gondi* — Language of the Gonds, South-Central Dravidian family.
- *Koyapunem* — The way of nature, the Gond religion founded by Pari Kupar Lingo.
- *Baradeo* — The high god worshipped in Koyapunem.
- *Persa Pen* — A clan's clan god, worshipped through sacrifice and ritual.
- *Saga* — A kin group in Gond society.
- *Pari* — A subsection of a saga.
- *Soira* — A ritual group that dresses the Persa Pen idol and cooks sacrificial food.
- *Mahua* — Flowers that yield a purifying liquor and mark the year's first ceremonies.
- *Pardhan* — A Gond bard who plays the fiddle.
- *Sua* — A women's dance performed after Diwali in honour of Shiva and Parvati.
- *Gussadi* — A festive dance performed in Adilabad during Diwali.
- *Gond Ramayani* — The Gond version of the Ramayana drawn from oral folk legend.
- *Dharmaguru* — A spiritual teacher or guide, a title given to Ravana by Paraswadi Gonds.
- *Pari Kupar Lingo* — The founder of Koyapunem, the Gond religion.
