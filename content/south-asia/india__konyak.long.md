## Overview

The Konyak are a Naga people associated especially with Mon district in northeast India. The [Mon district administration](https://mon.nic.in/history/) also describes Konyak villages across the border in Myanmar. Its account names the *Angh* as a traditional village chief and the *morung* as a communal institution. Konyak is a distinct language, and the [Konyak Literature Board](https://ipr.nagaland.gov.in/node/16813) is publishing and teaching it today.

This gallery contains 45 objects that the British Museum identifies as Konyak, many acquired from villages including Wakching and Longkhai. It is a view through particular historical collections, with many more ornaments, belts and carved objects than textiles or photographs of contemporary life. Two nineteenth-century pieces from Jaboka are catalogued as “Konyak (?)”; their original acquisition notes identify them as objects of the Jaboka Nagas. The [Mon district plan](https://nsdma.nagaland.gov.in/sites/default/files/2024-08/DDMP%20MON%202023.pdf) names Jaboka among its Konyak chiefly villages, but the museum's question marks remain relevant.

## Material culture

### Textiles, dress & adornment

The selection includes a [woven cotton warrior's belt](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_As1954-07-58), a [palm-leaf skirt](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_As1944-03-61), cane dance leggings, belts, gauntlets and several forms of headgear. Materials and uses differ between records; these are examples of dress, not a single Konyak costume. The [Bowers Museum's *nyauni* shawl](https://collections.bowers.org/objects/144791/shawl-nyauni) adds a documented cloth form absent from this gallery. [Nagaland Living Heritage](https://www.nagalandlivingheritage.com/community-page/konyak/textiles) describes the *shengnung* wrap and the chevron-patterned *Anghya Nyekok* associated historically with a chief's family. Its account also shows these forms being woven and adapted now, beyond the period represented by the British Museum objects.

Beads, shell, hair, horn, bone and fibre appear in the museum's [necklet](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_As1954-07-63), ear ornaments, hair ornaments and [cowrie-covered arm gauntlets](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_As1944-03-15-a-b). These catalogue identifications establish the objects and materials; they do not supply a rule for who may wear each form. The [carved wooden head showing facial tattooing](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_As1944-03-32) documents an older visual practice through an object, rather than a photograph of a person. The Mon district's [official history](https://mon.nic.in/tourism/) also describes older tattooed men. Present-day Konyak life should not be reduced to that history.

### Wood, baskets & household objects

A [carved morung-post model](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_As1944-03-27) and a [carved house board](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_As1944-03-62) show how wood was worked for village buildings. The former is a model, not an in-situ post. A roof-ornament model is likewise a small carved representation. The [Mon district plan](https://nsdma.nagaland.gov.in/sites/default/files/2024-08/DDMP%20MON%202023.pdf) describes morungs as places where unmarried young men assembled and village matters were discussed; the objects here cannot reconstruct a complete building.

Carrying and ceremonial baskets show different constructions. A [basket with a carved wooden handle](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_As1954-07-68) contrasts with a [bamboo basket](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_As1954-07-67). The British Museum calls another example a [“head hunter's basket”](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_As1954-07-69); it incorporates a monkey skull and tusk. That wording comes from the acquisition record and describes one historical collecting context. Lime boxes, tobacco pipes, a bamboo comb, a rattan rat trap and a pandanus brush document smaller aspects of daily work and storage. The museum also records carved figures, including a seated man and two morung-post models.

### Metalwork & arms

Spears, a buffalo-hide [shield from Jaboka](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_As-8287) and several helmets are present in the selection. The shield's museum attribution is “Konyak (?)”; its acquisition note calls it a Jabboka Naga shield. A [horned and feathered helmet](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_As1954-07-54) demonstrates the combined use of basketry and animal materials. A bronze armlet shows metal in personal adornment. The [Mon district history](https://mon.nic.in/history/) describes Konyak work in cane, bamboo, brass and firearms; a [Nagaland government craft event](https://ipr.nagaland.gov.in/index.php/NAGA-CRAFTSCAPE-INAUGURATED-AT-KISAMA) presented Konyak gunsmithing and ornaments as living practices. None of those sources establish that every village made every kind of object.

## Music, dance & festivals

Two British Museum [bamboo jaw harps](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_As1944-03-22) are the gallery's sound-making objects. They do not document a melody. Government accounts of [Aoleang Monyu](https://ipr.nagaland.gov.in/konyak) describe log drums, singing and dancing organized around the morung. A [2025 Nagaland cultural programme](https://ipr.nagaland.gov.in/CULTURAL-DISPLAY-MARKS-THE-4th-DAY-OF-HORNBILL-FESTIVAL) identifies *Alea-Boa* as a Konyak dance associated with Aoleang. These named examples are stronger evidence than a general claim about a fixed Konyak music or dance repertoire.

Aoleang is celebrated in early April after sowing, marking a new year and seeking a good harvest. The [Nagaland tourism account](https://tourism.nagaland.gov.in/tribes/) describes six days with preparation, visiting, feasts, dress, dancing and village cleaning. The historical account on the information department's site also describes practices associated with the former period of headhunting. It should be read as a record of traditions, not a description of every contemporary celebration. The [Mon district plan](https://nsdma.nagaland.gov.in/sites/default/files/2024-08/DDMP%20MON%202023.pdf) identifies *Lao-ong Mo* as another festival, in September after the harvest.

## Food & daily life

The [Aoleang account](https://ipr.nagaland.gov.in/konyak) mentions rice beer, meat, vegetables, banana leaves and food exchanged with relatives and neighbours. These details establish festival preparation and sharing; the selected museum objects do not supply recipes or a complete account of everyday meals. Farming and the timing of sowing and harvest are prominent in the festival sources. The small household objects in the gallery point to other daily activities, but their catalogues rarely name their users.

## Language, belief & transmission

The [Mon district administration](https://mon.nic.in/history/) notes that Konyak speech varies between villages. The [Konyak Literature Board's grammar launch](https://ipr.nagaland.gov.in/node/16813) shows active work on written description and education. Aoleang songs and the morung's historical role in village discussion point to forms of knowledge transmitted in speech and performance; the sources here do not identify a specific epic or story cycle.

The [district tourism office](https://mon.nic.in/tourism/) describes Christianity as the faith of most people in Mon today and records earlier beliefs about nature. The Aoleang account retains the language of prayers for crops. These sources concern different periods and settings, so neither a carved object nor a festival description can stand for every Konyak person's beliefs.
