## Overview

The Dimasa, also called the Dimasa Kachari people, are an ethnolinguistic community living chiefly in Assam and Nagaland in northeastern India. Their main areas include Dima Hasao district, the Jatinga Valley and Dhansiri Valley, Diphu City and Howraghat region of Karbi Anglong, West Karbi Anglong, parts of Nagaon, Hojai, Cachar, Hailakandi and Karimganj districts, Dimapur district, and parts of Jiribam district of Manipur. They speak Dimasa, a Boro-Garo language of the Tibeto-Burman family. As of the 2011 census, the language had approximately 137,184 native speakers. The Dimasa are one of the Kachari peoples and established the Dimasa kingdom, one of the early states that followed the decline of the Kamarupa kingdom.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They record one textile-related term, *Rijamphain beren* or *Rmai*, described only as a chest wrapper in connection with the necklace called *Rmailik*. No further information about its material, construction, patterning, or use is provided.

### Architecture

They state that traditional Dimasa sacred shrines are called *Daikho*. These are described as unstructured mythological shrines located in different areas of the traditional Dimasa region and associated with ancestral deities and sacred areas.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention silver and gold only in relation to ornaments: *Phowal* includes real silver metal beads, while *Khamaothai* may be made of silver or gold. Men’s ornaments include *Yaocher* and *Kharik*.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Dimasa ornaments include separate forms associated with men and women. Men use two types, *Yaocher* and *Kharik*. Women’s ornaments include *Phowal*, a necklace made with coral and real silver metal beads; *Jongsama*, a necklace of micro-beads with coral and silver beads; *Rangbarsha*, a necklace made of coins; and *Chandrawal*, a necklace of three silver chains with flower shapes. Other named forms are *Rmailik*, *Likjao*, *Likshim*, *Khadu*, *Khamaothai*, and *Yaoshidam*. These include necklaces of micro-beads or red and black micro-beads, a heavy silver bangle, silver or gold earrings, and a finger ring. *Phowal* is also worn by men.

## Music & performance

The available material identifies *Baidima* as the Dimasa name for dance, explaining the term as *Bai*, meaning dance, and *Dima*, meaning Dimasa. It states that Dimasa Kachari dance forms are complex in character, but provides no further information about accompanying music, instruments, choreography, performers, or occasions.

## Dance & theatre

Any Dimasa dance is called *Baidima*. The name is explained through *Bai*, meaning dance, and *Dima*, meaning Dimasa. The sources describe Dimasa Kachari dance forms as complex in character.

## Festivals & rituals

Since 1994, according to a decision of the Dimasa community of Dima Hasao, the Autonomous Council of Dima Hasao has officially declared 27 January as Busu Dima festival day.

Dimasa religious life is described as following an ancient animistic or shamanic faith, with a syncretism of traditional faith and Hinduism among Dimasas living in Cachar. The earth, air, and sky are described as inhabited by invisible spiritual beings commonly known as *Madai*. Six gods are regarded as ancestral deities with authority over the entire Dimasa land. The sacred areas associated with these powers are called *Daikho*, a word explained as combining *Madai* or *Dai* with *Kho*, understood as the house of God. Their number is widely believed to be twelve.

## Foodways

They state only that land and nature are sources of the Dimasa economy, culture, history, and Dharmic heritage. No food-related vernacular terms are provided.

## Oral tradition & literature

Dimasa royal-clan folklore is mentioned in connection with the etymology of the group’s names, the descent and titles of the king, and the traditional account of settlement. Ancient Dimasa tradition states that the people left their ancestral land after a severe drought and, following long wandering, settled at *Di-lao-bra Tsangi-bra*, the confluence of the Dilao and Sangi, or *Di-tsang*.

The sources mention the Dimasa literary body *Dimasa Lairidim Hosom*, described as the main guiding force behind the use of Latin script in lower-primary education in Dima Hasao district. No further information about its publications or preservation activities is provided.

## Language & religion

Dimasa is a Sino-Tibetan or Boro-Garo language spoken in Assam and Nagaland, India. It belongs to the Boro-Garo group of the Kachari-Kachin-Konyak sub-branch and is closely related to Boro, Kokborok, and Garo. The language is also known as *Grao-Dima*. It is agglutinative or inflectional and has six vowels and sixteen consonants. Latin and Eastern Nagari scripts are used for writing; Latin script was introduced into the lower-primary education system in Dima Hasao district, while Bengali script is used in Cachar. Dimasa religious life includes an ancient animistic or shamanic faith, with Hinduism combined with traditional beliefs among Dimasas in Cachar. Spiritual beings are known as *Madai*, and sacred shrines are called *Daikho*.
