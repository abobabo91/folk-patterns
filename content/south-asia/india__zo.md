## Overview

The Zo people are an ethnolinguistically related group of speakers of Kuki-Chin languages living primarily in northeastern India, western Myanmar, and southeastern Bangladesh. Their settlement area includes contiguous regions of Northeast India, Northwest Burma (Myanmar), and the Chittagong Hill Tracts in Bangladesh. In India, they are particularly prominent in Manipur, Nagaland, Assam, and Mizoram, and some fifty Kuki/Zo peoples are recognised as scheduled tribes. The Zo are also known as the Kuki-Chin-Mizo group and comprise various tribes with shared linguistic and cultural similarities. Their dispersal across international borders resulted from a British colonial policy that drew borders on political rather than ethnic grounds.

## Festivals & rituals

Traditionally, Zo people followed animism and practised ancestral worship. In the 19th century, Christian missionaries converted many Zo people to Christianity, and Christianity, mostly Protestant, is now dominant among them. Minorities practise Buddhism, Judaism, or indigenous beliefs.

## Language & religion

Zo languages belong to the Kuki-Chin branch of the Tibeto-Burman family. Zou, also spelled Zo and known as Zoham or Zokam, is a language of the Northeastern branch of Kuki-Chin languages originating in western Burma and also spoken in Mizoram and Manipur. Zou is sometimes used as a blanket term for languages spoken by the Kuki-Chin-Mizo peoples. Zou is often written in a Latin script developed by Christian missionary J.H. Cope. In 1952, M. Siahzathang of Churachandpur created an alternative script known as Zolai or Zoulai, which has alphabetic and some alphasyllabic characteristics. Zou cultural, political, and literary organizations began adopting it from the 1970s, and the Manipur State Government subsequently supported both Siahzathang and the script. The sources describe a religious transition from animism and ancestral worship toward predominantly Protestant Christianity, alongside Buddhist, Jewish, and indigenous beliefs.
