## Overview

The Naga are not one people but a collection of independent village societies spread across the Patkai hill ranges where India meets Myanmar. Each village was historically fortified and ruled either by councils of elders or hereditary chiefs, and warfare and head-taking shaped nearly all their material culture. British administration arrived from the 1830s onward, and American Baptist missionaries converted the majority to Christianity within a few generations, so Nagaland now has one of Asia's highest proportions of Christians. The material legacy is striking: warp-striped shawls that read like résumés, carved morung houses, log drums, and the paired mithun horns and hornbills that appear everywhere from textiles to house gables.

## Material culture

Naga material culture draws from hill forests and plains trade routes—cotton, bamboo, cane, wood, bone, hornbill feather, shell, carnelian and brass—and nearly every object declares the maker's or wearer's rank and deeds.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Naga weavers create narrow warp-striped cloths on back-strap looms; the stripes and patterns encode rank and achievement rather than just decoration.

- **Lohe shawl** (*lohe*) — Angami shawl whose broad bands and stripe patterns mark the wearer's standing, with the most prestigious versions reserved for men who had completed major feasts.
- **Tsungkotepsu** — The most celebrated Ao men's shawl, a dark ground with a white median band painted with mithun, tiger, elephant, cock or human heads—each figure denoting an entitlement.
- **Sumi ahu** (*ahu*) — Sumi shawl of banded construction in dense red and black, with rank-graded varieties.
- **Rengma azhu** (*azhu*) — Rengma shawl type with graded versions, the senior versions restricted to men of demonstrated status.
- **Mithun horn motif** — Paired inward-curving horns woven, carved, cast or painted on textiles and objects; the single most widespread Naga design element, standing for wealth and feast-sacrifice.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress was minimal in the warm climate; ceremonial dress covered the whole body in rank-declaring ornaments, shawls, kilts and feather headdresses.

- **Angami kilt** — Short dark cotton wrap worn by Angami men, in senior versions ornamented with rows of cowrie shells arranged to signal war or romantic success.
- **Mekhela** — Woman's wrapped body-cloth or skirt worn tubular or as a wrap; Chakhesang ceremonial versions are richly banded and worn with matching shawls at festivals and weddings.
- **Zeliang bead apron** — Small waist apron or skirt panel densely covered with glass and shell beadwork, worn by Zeliang women and girls at dances.
- **Hornbill feather headdress** — Warrior's headdress mounting the black-and-white tail feathers of the great hornbill, often on a cane cap trimmed with bear or goat hair and boar tusk; the number of feathers counted achievements.
- **Sash and baldric** — Woven or cowrie-covered sashes worn diagonally across the chest, part of the full male dance costume together with spear, dao and kilt.

### Architecture

Naga villages were built for defence on ridge spurs with stone walls; the monumental architecture is the morung, the carved men's house, rather than any temple.

- **Morung** (*morung; ariju, dekachang*) — Large gable-roofed timber hall where unmarried young men slept, learned tradition and warfare, and kept the log drum and trophy heads; functioned as school, barracks and clubhouse at once.
- **Morung carving** — Carved and painted decoration of morung front posts and bargeboards in high relief: mithun heads, hornbills, tigers, human figures and rows of heads.
- **Mithun skull frieze** — Row of mithun skulls or carved skull motifs mounted on a rich man's house or morung, recording the animals sacrificed at his feasts of merit.
- **Village gate** (*kharu*) — Monolithic carved wooden gate set in the stone village wall, decorated with mithun horns, human heads and weapons, closed at night against raiders.
- **Chief's house** — Among the Konyak, the Angh's residence is the village's largest structure with an exceptionally long ridge and the heaviest concentration of carving and trophies.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery is modest; the striking work is in cane and bamboo basketry, wood carving and traded or cast brass.

- **Naga log drum** — Hollowed-out tree trunk, sometimes many metres long and carved at one end as a buffalo or mithun head, struck with wooden beaters to signal raids, deaths, fire and harvest to the whole valley.
- **Naga brass hornbill** — Cast or sheet brass hornbill figures and beak-shaped ornaments, mounted on headgear or worn as pendants; brass was a prestige metal worked into heads and discs.
- **Dao** — Broad-bladed working and fighting knife of iron with wooden handle, carried in a bamboo or wooden sheath; the universal tool for jungle clearing, building and butchering as well as a warrior's weapon.
- **Cane and bamboo basketry** — Conical carrying baskets with head straps, storage baskets, mats, rain shields and cane rings and helmets; cane work is the most ubiquitous Naga craft.
- **Rice-beer vessels** — Gourds, bamboo tubes and wooden or pottery containers for fermented rice beer, central to every hospitality, oath-taking and feasting occasion.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Naga ornament is heavy, frontal and legible: what a person wears declares what they or their forebears have accomplished.

- **Konyak headhunter necklace** — Necklace of brass or bronze human heads strung on cord or beads, in some forms one head per killing; the definitive Konyak male ornament and the most direct recall of head-taking.
- **Naga carnelian bead necklace** — Multi-strand necklaces of orange-red carnelian beads traded from the Indian plains, often combined with glass and shell; heirloom wealth and a common bride-price component.
- **Conch-shell armlet** — Thick white armlet or bracelet cut from marine conch, obtained through long-distance trade and marking both wealth and trading reach.
- **Cane-and-boar-tusk armlet** — Upper-arm ornament of tightly coiled cane set with curving wild boar tusks, worn by men and an explicit badge of hunting or martial achievement.
- **Cowrie ornament** — Cowrie shells sewn in rows on kilts, sashes and baldrics, with patterns coded to particular achievements.

## Music & performance

Naga music is overwhelmingly vocal and collective, marked by polyphony: multi-part unaccompanied singing in which a leader's line is answered by a group in drone or parallel parts.

- **Log drum performance** — Team of men strike the log drum in interlocking patterns to accompany group chant and signal community events; hauling a new drum into the village was itself a major communal rite.
- **Bamboo mouth organs and flutes** — Wind instruments used in Naga ensembles, along with jaw harps and single-string fiddles in some communities.
- **Tati** — One-stringed instrument played by the Angami, part of the limited but characteristic Naga instrumental tradition.
- **Buffalo-horn trumpet** — Horn used for signalling, along with bamboo clappers and struck tubes in the Naga sound world.
- **Village and church choirs** — Baptist hymnody introduced four-part singing that Naga congregations adopted with such enthusiasm that choral competition is now itself a Naga specialism at festivals.

## Dance & theatre

Naga dance is martial and grounded, performed in lines or circles by costumed groups rather than soloists, with mimed hunting and harvests embedded in the movement.

- **Angami war dance** — Men advance in a stamping crouch with sharp shouts and abrupt shifts of direction that mimic raid approach and ambush, performed in full feather headdress, sash and kilt with spear and dao in hand.
- **Konyak war dance varieties** — Konyak versions of the war dance are among the best-known forms of Naga martial performance.
- **Women's linked-arm dance** — Women perform in rows with linked arms or hands on shoulders, with swaying step and sustained song; more contained than men's dances.
- **Hunting and harvest mime** — Dances that dramatize tracking, felling, sowing, threshing and the hauling of the log drum; at several festivals the whole agricultural year is danced through.
- **Hornbill Festival performances** — Since the 1990s, staged competitive performances at the Hornbill Festival have become the main arena where village troupes rehearse, standardise and display their dances.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual year follows the agricultural calendar; the ritual career is the feast of merit in which a man gains rank by sacrificing mithun and distributing meat and rice beer to the entire village.

- **Sekrenyi** — Angami purification festival in February in which men ritually bathe and the village renews itself.
- **Moatsu** — Ao festival held after sowing in early May.
- **Hornbill Festival** — Annual state-organised gathering held December 1–10 near Kohima at which every recognised tribe presents dance, song, crafts, replica morungs and food; an invention of the late 1990s that is now the principal instrument of Naga cultural self-presentation.
- **Feast of merit** — A man of means sacrificed mithun and distributed meat and rice beer to the entire village, acquiring the right to wear particular shawls, add carved horns to his house gable and erect standing stones; the sequence could repeat at ascending grades over a lifetime.
- **Genna days** (*genna*) — Ritual prohibition days on which work and movement were suspended; most such observances ended with Christianisation.

## Foodways

The Naga diet rests on rice eaten with smoked meats, foraged greens, bamboo shoot and chilli; the defining flavours are fermentation and intense heat.

- **Axone** (*axone; akhuni*) — Fermented soybean paste, pungent and cooked with smoked pork, one of the two defining flavours of Naga cuisine.
- **Bastenga** — Fermented bamboo shoot, sour and cooked with smoked pork, the other defining flavour.
- **Raja mircha** (*raja mircha; bhut jolokia*) — The king chilli, one of the hottest cultivars in the world, used fresh, dried or in chutney in Naga cooking.
- **Rice beer** (*zutho; zu; madhu*) — Brewed from rice or millet, the medium of all hospitality, oath-taking and feasting, though Nagaland's prohibition law has pushed it to the margins of public life.
- **Smoked pork with fermented bamboo** — The most emblematic Naga dish, combining pork smoked above the hearth with fermented bamboo shoot in a simple preparation with little oil.

## Oral tradition & literature

Before the Roman script arrived with missionaries, everything was oral and transmitted in the morung by elders to young men across years of shared quarters; most origin narratives describe emergence from stone or earth and a tiger, spirit and human born as brothers.

- **Makhel origin site** (*Makhel*) — A place remembered as the common point of dispersal from which ancestral Naga groups separated, appearing in descent narratives across communities.
- **Khezhakeno stone** — A stone site identified as a point of ancestral separation in Naga origin traditions.
- **Sonuo/Sonuolo cycle** — Well-known narrative cycle centered on a maiden, preserved in the Kohima area and part of the Dzükou valley tragic-lovers traditions.
- **Easterine Kire novels** — English-language Naga literature including *A Naga Village Remembered* and *When the River Sleeps*, the most widely read contemporary Naga fiction.
- **Temsüla Ao poetry and short fiction** — Major body of contemporary Naga literary work alongside Kire's novels.

## Language & religion

Naga languages belong to several branches of Tibeto-Burman and are often mutually unintelligible between neighbouring villages; religion is overwhelmingly Baptist Christian, though ancestral animism survives in cultural forms.

- **Nagamese** — An Assamese-lexified pidgin that emerged as the market and inter-tribal language and is now widely spoken across Naga territories.
- **Angami language** — One of the first Naga languages reduced to writing by missionaries, with its own orthography, hymnals and Bible translations.
- **Ao language** — Another early language reduced to writing by Baptist missionaries, with a significant literary tradition beginning in the 1870s.
- **Pfutsana** — The ancestral religion, sometimes glossed by this term, which recognised a creator or high spirit, local place spirits, ritual prohibition days and the transferability of fertility.
- **Heraka reform movement** (*Heraka*) — Indigenous religious alternative associated with the Zeme and the figure of Rani Gaidinliu, persisting as a small reform movement within Naga territories.

## Glossary

- *azhu* — Rengma shawl type, graded by rank
- *bastenga* — Fermented bamboo shoot, sour condiment
- *bhut jolokia* — King chilli, one of the world's hottest pepper varieties
- *dao* — Broad-bladed working and fighting knife
- *dekachang* — Local name for morung in some Naga communities
- *genna* — Ritual prohibition days on which work and movement were suspended
- *kharu* — Village gate among the Angami
- *lohe* — Angami shawl whose broad bands mark the wearer's standing
- *madhu* — Generic term for rice beer
- *Makhel* — Remembered place of common ancestral dispersal
- *mekhela* — Woman's wrapped body-cloth or skirt
- *morung* — Bachelors' dormitory and men's house in a Naga village
- *Nagamese* — Assamese-lexified pidgin used as inter-tribal language
- *Pfutsana* — The ancestral Naga religion, animist in character
- *tati* — One-stringed musical instrument of the Angami
- *tsungkotepsu* — The highest-grade Ao men's shawl, painted with achievement symbols
