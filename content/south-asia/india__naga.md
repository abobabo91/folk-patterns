## Overview

The Naga are a large cluster of peoples living in the forested hills between India and Myanmar, each group with its own language and customs. They are known for making almost everything they need themselves — cloth, hats, medicines, cooking pots — with cloth especially encoding a person's rank and wealth. Village life centered on the bachelors' house and on large feasts where a man could gain status by sacrificing animals and distributing meat to the whole community. Though most are now Christian, older beliefs and practices live on in festivals, dances and everyday taboos.

## Material culture

Naga crafts are rooted in self-sufficiency and status display, with weaving, carving, basketry and metalwork all calibrated to show rank.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Shawls woven on backstrap looms by women are the primary rank documents of Naga society, with specific designs and grades earned through feasts or warfare.

- **Tsüngkotepsü** (*Ao man's shawl*) — Black-and-red field with white central panel painted with figures like mithun, elephant and human heads; worn only by men who took heads or gave feasts of merit.
- **Rongsü** (*Ao feast shawl*) — Red and black banded shawl earned through the Feast of Merit rather than warfare, worn by the feast-giver and his wife.
- **Lohe** (*Angami shawl*) — Distinguished by thick embroidered animal motifs applied over a dark ground.
- **Sütam, Ethasü, Longpensü** (*Lotha men's shawls*) — Graded series where a man moves up through stages of the Feast of Merit cycle, each grade adding permitted bands and figures.
- **Mekhela** (*wraparound skirt*) — Woman's lower garment woven in group-specific stripe systems; designs differ for men and women throughout Naga groups.

### Clothing & dress

Daily dress is simple — short kilt or loincloth for men, knee-length wraparound skirt for women — with most display and rank markers in headgear and ornaments.

- **Hornbill feather headdress** — Tail feathers of the great hornbill worn in ceremonial assemblage, strictly rationed by rank.
- **Cane-and-boar-tusk armlets** — Worn above the biceps as part of full male ceremonial dress for war dance and festival.
- **Conch-shell armlets** — Part of male ceremonial dress worn with shawl, helmet and dao in war dance.
- **Konyak facial tattooing** — Broad dark band across the face with chest and throat work conferred on successful head-takers; very few surviving tattooed men remain.

### Architecture

Villages sit on ridge crests behind stone walls, centered on the morung (men's house) where bachelors lived and learned war and carving.

- **Morung** (*Ao arichu, Angami kichüki*) — Bachelors' dormitory and men's house where boys lived from puberty to marriage; facades carry the most ambitious Naga carving of hornbills, mithun heads and human heads.
- **Morung carving** — Typical designs present hornbill, mithun heads, tiger, a squatting human figure and rows of carved or actual human heads on warrior morungs.
- **House-horns** (*kika*) — Crossed horns mounted on the gable of a house whose owner completed the Feast of Merit, serving as an architectural rank marker like the shawl.
- **Mithun skull frieze** — Actual sacrificed skulls mounted along the front wall or in the house porch, one per feast given.
- **Naga log drum** — A single hollowed tree trunk sometimes carved as a hornbill head or buffalo, up to several metres long, beaten for alarm, war news and festival.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Naga craftwork spans basketry, wood carving, pottery and metalwork, with brass and bell-metal work centered on distinctive figurative forms.

- **Conical carrying baskets** — Tight basketwork with a tumpline for carrying goods; also lidded storage baskets and cane rain-shields.
- **Naga brass hornbill** — Signature brass cast form, alongside small brass human heads strung on Konyak necklaces.
- **Dao** — Broad iron blade in a wood-and-cane half-sheath worn at the hip, simultaneously tool, weapon and personal emblem.
- **Pottery** — Hand-built without a wheel in specialist villages and traded along ridge routes.
- **Brass armlets and bell-metal ear-spools** — Heavy metalwork ornaments made and worn throughout Naga communities.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Beads in glass, stone, shell, tusk, metal, bone and wood are used in profusion and complexity to mark status and identity.

- **Naga carnelian bead necklace** — Long orange-red stone beads traded in from the plains over generations and heritable, the highest-value bead form.
- **Konyak headhunter necklace** — Carries small brass or bronze faces, conventionally one per head taken.
- **Zeliang bead apron** — Dense glass beadwork on a cloth ground covering a woman's front panel.
- **Red-dyed goat's hair ear tassels** — Large ear ornaments worn by both men and women.

## Music & performance

Naga music is group-sung, polyphonic and unaccompanied, with instruments like log drums used to announce raids and important events rather than make melody.

- **Tati** — One-string fiddle-type instrument made and played by Naga communities.
- **Bamboo mouth organs** — Wind instruments made and played for music-making at festivals and in the morung.
- **Log drum announcements** — Log drum beaten in coded rhythms to announce a raid, a death, a successful hunt or the start of festival.
- **Historical narrative songs** — Carry the biography of famous ancestors and the account of specific incidents, serving as the historical record before writing.
- **Seasonal songs** — Tied to the agricultural cycle — sowing, weeding, harvest and rest periods.

## Dance & theatre

All Naga dances are group dances performed in tight synchronisation and always sung, composed by sex and occasion.

- **War dance** — Emblematic male form, athletic and martial, performed in full headdress with dao and spear, built on stamping, high leaps, sudden crouches and shield feints.
- **Women's dances** — Lateral and linked-arm, moving in a slow line or circle with a rocking step.

## Festivals & rituals

Each Naga group keeps its own festival calendar pinned to the jhum agricultural year, with the Feast of Merit as the dominant historical ritual for gaining rank.

- **Sekrenyi** (*Angami*) — Purification festival in late February with ritual bathing at the village well, sacrifice and ten days of feasting and song.
- **Moatsü** (*Ao*) — Festival in early May marking the completion of sowing.
- **Feast of Merit** — Graded sequence where a wealthy man sacrificed mithun and distributed meat and rice beer to the village, gaining the right to higher shawl grades and house-horns.
- **Hornbill Festival** — Government-staged festival (1–10 December at Kisama since 2000) serving as an inter-group meeting ground.
- **Tokhu Emong and Tsükhenyie** (*Lotha and Chakhesang*) — Post-harvest and spring festivals respectively.

## Foodways

Naga cuisine is defined by smoking and fermentation, with rice the staple and smoked pork the signature meat.

- **Smoked pork with anishi** — Smoked pork served with fermented, sun-dried taro-leaf cakes, the dish most strongly identified with the region.
- **Axone** (*akhuni (Sumi)*) — Fermented soybean cake with a formidable smell, cooked with smoked pork or pounded into a chutney.
- **Bastenga** — Fermented bamboo shoot providing the cuisine's base sourness.
- **Raja mircha** (*bhut jolokia / king chilli*) — Heat source used fresh, dried and in a roasted chutney.
- **Galho** — Rice-and-vegetable one-pot porridge, the everyday dish.

## Oral tradition & literature

The entire pre-Christian record is oral — folk tales, genealogies and historical song cycles — with migration narratives the shared backbone.

- **Khezakenoma** — The stone-terraced site in Angami-Chakhesang-Rengma-Sumi migration tradition where three founding brothers quarrelled over a grain-multiplying stone.
- **Tiger-spirit-man sibling tales** — Recur across groups, with the tiger, spirit and first man as brothers who divide the world.
- **Proverbs and riddles** — Were the morung's teaching medium for instruction and knowledge.

## Language & religion

Naga languages are Tibeto-Burman with no indigenous script; traditional animism was transformed by Baptist missions in the mid-19th century, making Nagaland one of India's three Christian-majority states.

- **Tibeto-Burman languages** — Over eighty-nine languages and dialects, mostly mutually unintelligible; greatest language diversity of any ethnic group or state in India.
- **Roman orthographies** — Came with American Baptist missions in the 19th century and remain the writing system.
- **Nagamese** — Assamese-based creole serving as the working lingua franca.

## Glossary

- *tsüngkotepsü* — Ao man's shawl; status document marking heads taken or feasts given
- *rongsü* — Ao shawl earned through Feast of Merit
- *lohe* — Angami shawl with embroidered animal motifs
- *mekhela* — Woman's wraparound skirt woven in group-specific stripes
- *morung* — Bachelors' dormitory and men's house; center of village life
- *kika* — Crossed house-horns marking completion of Feast of Merit
- *dao* — Broad iron blade in sheath; tool, weapon and personal emblem
- *mithun* — Semi-domesticated bovine; sacrifice basis of Feast of Merit
- *Feast of Merit* — Graded ritual sequence for gaining rank through animal sacrifice and feasting
- *anishi* — Fermented, sun-dried taro-leaf cakes; Ao specialty
- *axone* — Fermented soybean cake; Sumi contribution to cuisine
- *bastenga* — Fermented bamboo shoot
- *jhum* — Swidden agriculture; basis of annual festival calendar
- *tati* — One-string fiddle-type instrument
- *raja mircha* — King chilli; main heat source in Naga cooking
- *Khezakenoma* — Stone-terraced site in migration founding narrative
