## Overview

The Koraga are a tribal or indigenous community found mainly in the Dakshina Kannada and Udupi districts of Karnataka and the Kasaragod district of Kerala, in south India. They also live in smaller numbers in Uttara Kannada, Shimoga and Kodagu. The Karnataka areas are often collectively called Tulunaad, corresponding roughly to the boundaries of the erstwhile South Canara district. The Koraga numbered 16,071 according to the 2001 census of India and are classified by the Government of India as a particularly vulnerable tribal group. Their language is an independent Dravidian language. Their folk-culture profile includes basket-making from bamboo and creepers, leaf-built dwellings, drum beating, flute music, dance, and a tribal religious system involving Bhutas, devas and a sun god.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe traditional dress only briefly. The Koraga formerly lived in structures made of leaves called *koppus* and also dressed in leaves.

### Architecture

Koraga traditional structures were made of leaves and were called *koppus*. Around the beginning of the 21st century, Koraga families began moving into simple free houses constructed and sanctioned by Government agencies, although some people remained homeless or lived on government-owned lands. Government-supported Koraga colonies have included tiled houses, concrete lanes and electricity, with financial support from District authorities, Taluk and village panchayats.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They identify basket-making as an important material practice, using forest produce—principally bamboo and creepers—to manufacture baskets. Some Koraga continued this skill while others worked as agricultural labourers, scavengers or sweepers. No named tools, vessels or household forms are recorded.

## Music & performance

Koraga drum beating is one of their important cultural contributions. The drum is called *dollu* or *dolu*, and Koraga people formerly played it during Kambala, village fairs and informal gatherings in their living places. Traditional belief held that Koraga drum beating possessed special powers and that its sound drove away evil spirits. The practice declined because drum beaters were looked down upon by society, but attempts have been made to revive it through performance groups such as Gajamela in Dakshina Kannada, with Government support. These groups also perform at tribal gatherings in coastal Karnataka.

Flute music is another important part of Koraga culture. The sources also describe dance involving both men and women, particularly during celebrations such as Bhoomi Habba, identified as the worshipping of earth.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name no separate Koraga theatre tradition. They state that dance involving both men and women forms an important part of Koraga culture and appears at celebrations such as Bhoomi Habba.

## Festivals & rituals

Koraga cultural performance is associated with Kambala, village fairs, tribal gatherings and Bhoomi Habba. Drum beating formerly took place during Kambala and village fairs, while Bhoomi Habba includes flute music and dance and is described as worshipping earth. Koraga people have also been subjected to the practice called *ajalu*, which the Government of Karnataka defined as differentiating Koraga people from other communities, treating them as inferior human beings, mixing hair, nails and other inedible substances into food and requiring them to eat it. They were also made to run like buffaloes before the beginning of Kambala.

Ajalu was prohibited in 2000 by the Karnataka Koragas (Prohibition of Ajalu Practice) Act, 2000. The sources state that it has nevertheless occasionally been witnessed during Nemotsava or temple fairs at Inna (Karkala) and Saligrama, Udupi.

## Foodways

The Koraga diet is generally of poor quality because of poverty. Rice is a principal food, accompanied by meats such as pork and beef. Pulses and vegetables are increasingly used. Meat is often obtained from animals that have died naturally rather than through slaughter. Malnutrition is common among Koraga children, and Government health authorities have expressed concern about it. Government schemes have sought to improve the nutritional content of Koraga food, but implementation has been poor, and Koraga people have sometimes demanded improvements.

## Oral tradition & literature

Koraga folklore claims that Hubbashika was one of their chiefs. A Kadamba narrative asserts a Chandala origin, presenting the Koraga as descendants of the offspring of a Brahman woman and a Shudra father. The sources state that this narrative was recorded by Brahmans and that its reliability is lacking. Edgar Thurston quoted M. T. Walhouse, who wrote in 1875 that the account represented the Koraga, together with the Kapata, as the lowest of the fifteen Chandala groups.

## Language & religion

Koraga is a Dravidian language and is further classified within the Southern Dravidian family. It is generally spoken rather than written; when written, it uses Kannada script. Koraga people are generally conversant in Tulu and Kannada and use those languages as a medium for producing literature. The sources identify four dialects according to Bhat: *Onti*, spoken in Udupi; *Tappu*, spoken in Hebri; *Mudu*, spoken in Kundapura; and *Ande*, located midway between Tappu and Onti and spoken in Mangalore. Mudu speakers are bilingual in Kannada, while Onti speakers are bilingual in Tulu. Mudu Koraga and Korra Koraga are described as divergent, and Mudu Koraga is not intelligible with Korra Koraga.

The Koraga regard themselves as Hindus while following a tribal religious system. They worship spirits known as *Bhutas*, as well as some devas and a sun god. The sources state that they formerly practiced matrilineal inheritance known as *Aliya Santana*, while they may now be patrilineal under *Makkala Santana*. Their three main subdivisions are Sappina, Ande and Kappada Koraga. These subdivisions are divided into clans called *balis*, and people do not marry within their own clan.
