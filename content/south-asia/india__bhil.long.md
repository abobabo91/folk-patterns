## Overview

The Bhil are a group of indigenous communities of western and central India, concentrated in the southern Aravalli Range of Rajasthan and the western Satpura Range of Madhya Pradesh, with large populations in Gujarat, Maharashtra and Chhattisgarh, and scattered communities as far east as Bengal and Tripura and west into the Sindh province of Pakistan. In Madhya Pradesh they are the inhabitants of Dhar, Jhabua, Khargone and Ratlam districts. They are the largest tribe in India and, in Victoria R. Williams's assessment, its most widely dispersed tribal group; around 10.4 million people were recorded as speaking a Bhil lect in 2011. Those lects are Indo-Aryan, classified as dialects standing midway between Gujarati and the Rajasthani–Marwari languages; the aboriginal language the Bhil originally spoke is lost. The name itself is traced to Dravidian *billa* or *billu*, "bow" — a reference to the bow and arrow's prominence among these groups, and the objects in this atlas bear that out.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources name one pattern-bearing tradition, and it is a painting rather than a textile practice.

**Pithora painting** — The Bhilala subdivision is known for Pithora painting. The sources say nothing further about its technique, surface or iconography.

Bhil painting more generally is characterised by in-filling with multi-coloured dots. *Bhuri Bai* was the first Bhil artist to work in readymade colours on paper; Lado Bai, Sher Singh, Ram Singh and Dubu Bariya are also named as Bhil painters.

The museum objects include worked surface pattern on non-textile material: a wooden column capital carved with diagonal lines, a narrow trellis-like band and a larger abstract or "vegetable" motif; a wooden maize mortar with a notch pattern at the lip; and iron arrowheads carrying a simple punch-mark pattern at the centre, one of which makes the head read as a leaf. One woman's short jacket in the collection has three rows of supplementary-weft decoration across the back hem, and one woman's dress fragment is a rectangle of cotton striped in yellow, red, black and white.

### Clothing & dress

Men's traditional dress is the *pagri*, *angarkha*, *dhoti* and *gamchha*; women wear the *sari* and *ghagra choli*. The catalogued garments are consistent with this and add detail on the plainer end of the range: several men's loin-cloths of cotton, one of them embroidered, a man's *dhoti* described as a cotton loin-cloth, and a man's cotton head-dress. The one tailored piece is a woman's short jacket with long sleeves in burgundy wool cloth, lined with unbleached cotton, with a stand-up collar, hand-stitched buttonholes and supplementary-weft bands at the back hem — a garment made to a cut rather than draped. A woman's dress survives only as a rectangle of striped cotton in yellow, red, black and white. Tattooing is a traditional custom, generally done on women before marriage.

### Architecture

What the collection documents instead is a built form in miniature: the *mandir*, a god's house modelled in terracotta, of which the atlas holds several. One is described in detail — a clay body with a large round belly, a small outwardly flaring stand, a rounded and pointed top, and a small triangular opening cut into one side. Every village has its local deity, the *Gramdev*, and families keep household deities — *Jatidev*, *Kuldev* and *Kuldevi* — symbolised by stones, which is the shrine context these clay houses belong to. The traditional village is led by a headman, the *gameti*, who holds authority and decision-making power over most local disputes.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Terracotta carries most of the weight here, and it is largely votive. The collection holds horse figures, some in parts (body, head, leg) and one whitewashed; a horse-shaped votive figurine from whose back, behind the horse's own head, rises a human head with elongated ears echoing the horse's and wide protruding eyes, with thumb-impressed trim around the horse's top and between the two heads; and figures of elephants carrying a *howdah*. Household equipment in the collection pairs ceramic with tree sap: a lacquer-lined tray used in preparing *rotis*, and three lacquer-lined dishes. Woodwork is represented by a rice measure and a tall mortar for pounding maize, roughly carved with a flared base. Weapons are prominent: split-bamboo bows, one strung with a sappy springy length of small bamboo, another with a rattan strip ending in twisted vegetable-fibre loops; reed-shafted composite arrows with iron heads bound on with leather or fibre and four feather flights, shafts painted red or black; a bird bolt with a bamboo tip; and two buffalo-horn daggers of the 18th–19th century. Bhil households keep self-made bows and arrows, swords, knives and axes for defence and hunting.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Men wear the *kada*, *bajuband*, chain, earrings and *kardhani*; women wear the *hansli* (ring), *zele-zumke*, *narniyan* (bangle) and *nathni* (nose-jewel). The catalogued pieces are mostly women's and mostly base or white metal: cast metal anklets; a penannular brass necklet incised with a linear design on one face; a pair of grey-metal earrings formed as slightly conical discs with bands of twisted wire, a bead-like edging, a starburst field between, and a raised pyramidal boss at the centre with incised marks flanking it; silver earrings with a circular element and a central boss; and white-metal finger rings in relief, one with five spirals. One necklace is strung on twisted fibre from glass beads and shell broken into small pieces — two strands of small red beads, four strands of shell joined at one end, and six strands of slightly larger shell. Tattooing on women before marriage is the documented body-marking practice.

## Music & performance

Folk song and dance are the chief means of Bhil recreation. The instruments named are the harmonium, *sarangi*, *kundi*, *bansuri*, *apang*, *khajria*, *tabla*, *jhanjh*, *mandal* and *thali*, usually made from local materials. Three instruments in the collection show what that means in practice. The *reneti*, a musical bow, is built from a coconut shell drum with skin stretched over it and fastened with string, a pale wood bridge set on the skin, a bamboo neck passing through the shell, and two tuning pegs of wood or bamboo at the far end, with two sets of strings tied to the tuning sticks with twisted fibre. A second, composite instrument joins two cane pipes to a gourd sounding box covered in multi-coloured printed paper, with a long-beaked bird's head attached. A hand-held drum is made by stretching leather over the neck of a water pot and lashing it with vegetable-fibre string, with the bamboo beater tied to the end of that same string. Women's dancing is accompanied by a drum beat. The Bhil song-poem tradition is the subject of Randhir Khare's published collections, listed below.

## Dance & theatre

**Ghoomar** is the traditional folk dance of the Bhil and a symbol of womanhood: young girls take part in it and by doing so declare that they are stepping into the shoes of women. Women dance in traditional Bhili style, to a drum beat, at birth celebrations, at marriages and at certain festivals. The named dances beyond Ghoomar are the *Lathi* or staff dance, the *Dhol* dance, the marriage dance, the Holi dance, the battle dance, the Bhagoria dance, the Deepawal dance, the Sajoni dance and the hunting dance. The sources record no Bhil theatre or puppetry tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The Bhil observe Rakhi, Navratri, Dashera, Diwali and Holi alongside a distinct calendar of their own: *Akhatij*, *Navmi*, *Howan Mata ki Chalavani*, *Sawan Mata ki jatar*, *Diwasa*, *Nawai*, *Bhagoria*, *Gal*, *Gar*, *Dhobi*, *Sanja*, *Indel* and *Doha*. Several of these carry tribal fairs, or *mela*, held at different places across the districts — the Navratri mela and the Bhagoria mela, which falls during Holi, are named. The Bhil community of Udaipur holds the *Gavari* festival each year after Holi. Ritual life centres on the village and household deities described above and on a wide pantheon: *Bhati dev* and *Bhilat dev* as serpent-gods, *Baba dev* as village god, *Karkulia dev* as crop god, *Gopal dev* as pastoral god, *Bag dev* as lion god, *Bhairav dev* as dog god, and also *Indel dev*, *Bada dev*, *Mahadevel*, *Tejaji*, *Lotha mai*, *Techma*, *Orka Chichma* and *Kajal dev*. The buffalo is worshipped as *Bhensasaur*. *Bhopas* are relied on for physical, mental and psychological treatment. The votive terracotta horses and elephants in this atlas belong to this devotional economy. On the life-cycle side the sources name women's dancing at births and marriages, a dedicated marriage dance, and tattooing before a woman's marriage; the 1881 grievances included a ban on the killing of witches.

## Foodways

The staples are maize, onion, garlic and chilli, grown in small fields, supplemented by fruit and vegetables gathered from the local forests. Wheat and rice appear only at festivals and other special occasions. The Bhil are traditionally non-vegetarian, and wild fauna taken with bow, arrow, sword, knife and axe forms a major part of the diet. Alcohol distilled from the flower of *mahua* (*Madhuca longifolia*) is drunk profusely; its manufacture was one of the practices whose prohibition the Bhil protested in 1881, and Govind Guru's Brahminical missionary work later turned some of the community away from both meat and alcohol. Festive cooking draws on maize, wheat, barley, malt and rice. The collection's kitchen objects fit this grain-based economy directly: a tall wooden mortar for pounding maize, a wooden rice measure, and a lacquer-lined ceramic tray used in preparing *rotis*, with three matching lacquer-lined dishes.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources name no Bhil epic. What they do document is a song-poem tradition substantial enough to have been collected and published in its own right: Randhir Khare's *The Singing Bow: Song-Poems of the Bhil* and *Flight of Arrows: Selected Song Poems of the Bhils*, with his *Dangs: Journeys Into The Heartland* covering the region. Folk songs are named alongside dance as the chief means of Bhil recreation. On the visual side, a contemporary revival is documented in painting rather than in letters: Bhuri Bai's move to readymade colours and paper carried the dotted in-filling idiom of Bhil painting onto a gallery surface, and Lado Bai, Sher Singh, Ram Singh and Dubu Bariya followed.

## Language & religion

Bhili is Indo-Aryan, a set of lects treated as dialects of Gujarati and Rajasthani and forming a link midway between Gujarati and Rajasthani–Marwari. Up to 36 dialects are identified, grouped as Northern Bhil (Bauria, Wagdi, Bhilori, Magari), Central Bhil (Bhili proper or Patelia, Bhilodi, Adiwasa and Rajput Garasia, Bhilali, Chodri, Dhodia–Kukna, Dhanki, Dubli), Eastern Bhil or Bareli, Pardhi and Kalto; Gamit, Mawchi and Vasavi are also Bhil-spoken. Bhili is the most commonly spoken language of Dadra and Nagar Haveli at 40.42% of its population. Bhils in Sindh speak Sindhi Bhili and Dhatki. Most Bhil identify as Hindu; the Dang Bhils are Christian, the Nirdhi and Tadivi Bhils Muslim, and others follow *Sonatan*, described as their own religion blending Hindu belief with animistic philosophy. The sources record no historical Bhil script.
