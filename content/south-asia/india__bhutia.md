## Overview

The Bhutias, also known as Drejongpas, are a Tibetan ethnic group native to the Indian state of Sikkim. Most live in Sikkim, while significant communities reside in the Darjeeling and Kalimpong districts of northern West Bengal and in Nepal and Bhutan. They speak Drejongke, a Tibetic language descended from Old Tibetan. The name Drejongpas means “People of the Rice Valley,” while Bhutia is an exonym meaning “People from Tibet.” In northern Sikkim, Bhutias include the Lachenpas and Lachungpas, whose names refer to inhabitants of the “big pass” and “small pass.” Their folk culture includes distinctive clothing, clan organization, village law, Buddhist festivals, dances, songs, folktales, musical instruments, foodways, and house forms.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Kho** — The *kho* is the traditional Bhutia outfit, a loose cloak-like garment fastened at the neck on one side and near the waist with a silk or cotton belt. It is similar to the Tibetan chuba.

**Mo kho** — The *mo kho*, also called *bakhu* in Nepali, is a sleeveless, floor-length dress traditionally worn by Bhutia women over a full-sleeved shirt.

**Pangden** — The *pangden* is a loose sheet of multicolored woolen cloth with geometric designs, tied at the front of the women’s clothing. It is described as a symbol of a married woman.

**Teygho** — The *teygho* is the full-sleeved shirt worn beneath the *mo kho*.

**Kyera** — The *kyera* is a silk belt used to tie the women’s dress at the waist.

**Motif vocabulary.** Geometric designs — the sources describe the *pangden* as bearing exotic geometric designs.

### Clothing & dress

Men wear the *kho* with loose trousers. Women traditionally wear the sleeveless, floor-length *mo kho*, or *bakhu* in Nepali, over the full-sleeved *teygho*. The dress is tied at the waist with the silk belt called *kyera*. A multicolored woolen cloth with geometric designs, the *pangden*, is tied across the front and identifies a married woman. Both men and women wear embroidered leather boots.

The sources describe silk and cotton belts, woolen cloth, and embroidered leather footwear. Pure gold is favored by both women and men, and traditional jewelry is mostly made of twenty-four-carat pure gold.

### Architecture

A traditional Bhutia house is called a *khim* and is usually rectangular. Outside the house is a stone structure called a *sangbum*, used for burning incense. The word “sang” means incense and “bum” means vase, and the structure is shaped like a vase. It is used to burn *sang*, a sacred offering to deities.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Traditional Bhutia jewelry is mostly made of twenty-four-carat pure gold because pure gold is favored by both women and men.

## Music & performance

The Bhutia have traditions of dances, songs, and folktales. Musical instruments named in the sources are the flute, *yangjey*, drum, and *yarga*.

Music and performance are closely connected with religious and festive life. During Losar, fire dances are common in the evenings. Losoong is marked by traditional Cham dancing and merry-making. In Sikkim, dances performed during Losoong often depict narrativized tales from the life of Padmasambhava or Guru Ugyen.

## Dance & theatre

Named Bhutia folk dances are **Denzong-Neh-Na**, **Ta-Shi-Yang-Ku**, **Tashi Shabdo**, **Guru-Chinlap**, **Singhi Chham**, and **Yak Chham**.

Cham dancing is associated with Losoong, which is described as the most important festival among the Bhutias in India. In Sikkim, dance forms performed during Losoong may depict narrativized tales from the life of Padmasambhava or Guru Ugyen.

## Festivals & rituals

The main festival observed by the Bhutias is Losar. It usually takes place in the first week of February and marks the beginning of the Tibetan New Year. Fire dances are common in the evenings during Losar.

Losoong is usually celebrated at the end of the Tibetan year and falls at the end of the tenth Tibetan lunar month, usually in December. It is marked by traditional Cham dancing and merry-making. Losoong is celebrated across monasteries in Bhutan, Nepal, and Sikkim. In Sikkim, dances during the festival often depict stories from the life of Padmasambhava or Guru Ugyen.

Almost all Bhutia festivals and holidays have Buddhist religious significance.

## Foodways

Bhutia people traditionally eat rice with vegetables or meat fried in animal fat. The meat is usually pork or beef, and occasionally mutton or chicken. *Momo* are steamed meat dumplings, while *thukpa* consists of noodles in broth.

*Chhaang* is described as the favorite drink of the Bhutias and is increasingly consumed by other communities living alongside them. It is made from fermented barley or millet and served in a bamboo container called the *Tongba*. Tea with milk and sugar, as well as butter tea, is served on religious or social occasions.

The sources state that Bhutias utilize over 70 species of animal, fungi, and plant.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Bhutia have a tradition of folktales, songs, and dances. The sources name no epic, folktale cycle, proverb, riddle, or formal storytelling genre.

## Language & religion

Drejongke, also called Denjongke or Sikkimese, is a Sino-Tibetan and Tibetic language descended from Old Tibetan. It is spoken by Bhutia people in Sikkim, parts of Koshi province in eastern Nepal, and Bhutan, and is one of the official languages of Sikkim. The language has a lexical similarity of 65% with Dzongkha and 42% with Standard Tibetan. It has also been influenced by Yolmo, Yolmowa, Tamang, Nepali, Tibetan, and English.

Sikkimese is written using Tibetan script and Zhang Yeshe De Script. Until 1975 it was not a written language; after Sikkim became part of India, a written language was developed and introduced in schools. The sources describe dialect differences, including villages in the north where honorifics are generally not used.

Bhutias follow Vajrayana Buddhism, mainly the Nyingma and Kagyu schools. Religious life includes monasteries, incense offerings, Losar, Losoong, fire dances, and Cham dancing. The sources mention Rumtek Monastery in Sikkim and Bhutia Busty Monastery, also called Karma Dorjee Chyoling Monastery, in Darjeeling.
