## Overview

The Meitei, also known as Meetei, are a Tibeto-Burman ethnic group native to Manipur in Northeast India. They primarily settle in the Imphal Valley, while sizeable populations live in Assam, Tripura, Nagaland, Meghalaya, and Mizoram; Meitei communities are also present in Myanmar and Bangladesh. According to the 2011 census, 1,761,079 Meitei language-speakers lived in India, including 1,522,132 in Manipur, where the language-speakers formed the vast majority of the population. Meitei is a Tibeto-Burman language of the Sino-Tibetan family, and is the official language and lingua franca of Manipur. Meitei culture includes the Lai Haraoba ritualistic festival, Manipuri classical dance, Meitei literature, Sanamahism, distinctive foodways, and the Meitei mayek writing system.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources mention textiles as goods sold by women in local markets, but provide no specific textile names, materials, techniques, or motifs.

### Clothing & dress

They state that women tend to dominate local markets as sellers of textiles and traditional clothing.

### Architecture

Traditional Meitei architectural designs and sculptures appear in buildings and institutions, especially temples associated with the traditional Meitei religion. Named examples include Hiyangthang Lairembi Temple, Pakhangba Temple, Kangla, Sanamahi Kiyong Temple, and Thangjing Temple in Moirang. Other structures mentioned are Ima Keithel and Kangla Sanathong. The sources also describe Meitei architectural works broadly as including temples, ancient buildings, palaces, court halls, offices, and entrance gates.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention traditional objects and sites only indirectly, including wooden beams and fishing traps in the Langkol Ching narrative, and the royal Hiyang Hiren, which was crafted from wood according to the Hijan Hirao account.

## Music & performance

Music forms part of several Meitei religious and performing traditions. The Lai Haraoba is a traditional ritualistic theatrical festival consisting of dances, musical performances, and carnivals in temples and streets. It is dedicated to ancient Meitei gods and goddesses, including the Umang Lai and Lam Lai.

Nat, a shortened form of Meitei Nat Sankirtan, is described as a classical form of music of Manipur. It has six ragas and thirty-six raginis and is associated with the Sanskritisation of traditional Meitei culture through Hindu culture. In Meitei Nata-Sankirtana, performers sing and play musical instruments while simultaneously making delicate movements of the body and hands. These movements follow Khuthek Anoi, the “language of hand movements,” and Khongthang Anoi, the “language of footsteps.”

Pung Cholom and Meitei Nata Sankirtana are related to Manipuri Raas Leela. The sources also describe dance and music as vital in the life of Manipuri people, with *jagoi* identified as the Manipuri synonym for dance. Rasa dance is described as a major product of Manipuri culture: Maharaj Bhagyachandra innovated it, and it was first performed in Manipur in 1779 during the full moon of Kartik.

## Dance & theatre

The Manipuri classical dance, also called Manipuri Raas Leela or *Jagoi Raas*, is one of the major Indian classical dance forms and originated from the historical Manipur Kingdom. It is described as a spiritual expression of the worship of the Hindu deity Krishna. The form was formally developed by Meitei Hindu King Ching Thang Khomba, also called Rajarshi Bhagyachandra, in the 18th century CE.

The traditional Meitei dance-cum-martial art form of Thang-Ta uses the steps, gaits, and poses associated with *thang*, meaning “sword,” and *ta*, meaning “spear.” These movements form a foundation for choreographic steps in Lai Haraoba, Manipuri Raas Leela, Meitei Sankirtana, and Pung Cholom.

Lai Haraoba includes ritualistic and theatrical dances, among them Khamba Thoibi Jagoi, meaning “Khamba Thoibi dance.” The sources identify this as one of the cultural elements that inspired the development of Manipuri Raas Leela. Meitei theatre also includes adaptations of classical Greek, Roman, and Sanskrit plays, including Malemnganbi, Lengshonnei, Hojang Taret, Bacchae, Urubhangam, Shakuntala, and Bhagwatajjukiyam.

## Festivals & rituals

The Meitei follow a traditional calendar called *Maliyafam Palcha Kumsing*, with twelve months and a seven-day week. Important days include Cheiraoba on the first of Sajibu month; Emoinu Eratpa on the twelfth of Wakching month; Heikru Hidongba on the eleventh of Langban month; Kwaak Taanba on the tenth of Mera month; Mera Chaorel Houba on the first of Mera month; Mera Hou Chongba on the fifteenth of Mera month; and Ningol Chakouba on the second of Hiyangei month. Yaoshang falls on the fifteenth of Lamtaa month and the full moon.

Lai Haraoba is an ancient ritualistic music and dance festival performed to please the Umang Lais and Lam Lais. *Jagoi*, originally spelled Chatkoi, is the traditional form of dancing performed by devotees to please the deities. The sources list several forms, including Chukpharon Jagoi, Khamba Thoibi Jagoi, Laiching Jagoi, Laihou Jagoi, Leima Jagoi, Leisem Jagoi, Maibi Jagoi, Panthoibi Jagoi, and Thougal Jagoi.

The Meitei perform four types of death ceremony: air ceremony, involving disposal to space; earth ceremony, involving burial; fire ceremony, involving burning; and water ceremony, involving disposal to water bodies.

## Foodways

Rice, vegetables, and fish are staple foods of the Meiteis. Meat is consumed, but in traditional Meitei dishes it is not used in non-vegetarian dishes; at traditional and cultural gatherings, fish, snails, oysters, crabs, and eels are described as the non-vegetarian elements used. Rice is the main carbohydrate source and is typically served with vegetables, fish, freshwater snails, crabs, oysters, and eels.

Manipuri Sareng, also known as Wallago attu or Helicopter catfish, Hilsa, also called ilish or Tenualosa ilisha, freshwater snails, and edible oysters are identified as delicacies. Vegetables may be prepared as stews called *Kangsoi*, with little or no oil, or stir-fried with spices as *Kanghou*. Roasted, smoked, sun-dried, or fried fish is often added to stews and curries.

An important ingredient is *Ngari*, fermented fish. Roasted ngari is used in *singju*, a kind of salad; *morok metpa*, a chilli chutney; and *eromba*, boiled and mashed vegetables with chillies. Fermented bamboo shoots called *soibum*, fresh bamboo shoots called *Ushoi* or *Shoidon*, and fermented soya beans called *hawaijaar* are also important. Meals are served with fresh aromatic herbs.

A typical everyday meal includes rice, vegetable or fish curry, *morok metpa* or *eromba* with herbs, *champhut*, a steamed or boiled vegetable, and Kanghou. Common meat curries include *yen thongba*, chicken curry; *nganu thongba*, duck curry; *oak thongba*, pork curry; and *shan thongba*, beef curry.

## Oral tradition & literature

The earliest sections of the Cheitharol Kumbaba record the spread of Meiteis across Kangleipak, the Meitei term for Manipur, and the assimilation of other clans, also called Yek Salai, into a confederacy. Mythical narration is described as integral to nearly every genre of Meitei literature.

The *Khamba Thoibi Sheireng*, meaning “poem on Khamba Thoibi,” is regarded as the national epic of the Manipuris. It is based on the ancient romantic adventure tale of Khamba and Thoibi, is associated with Hijam Anganghal Singh, and contains 39,000 verses. Other narrative works and folktales named in the sources include the Nungpan Ponpi Luwaopa, Chainarol, the story of Petanga, Sandrembi and Cheisra, Emoinu, Hanuba Hanubi Paan Thaaba, Pebet and Hoidong Lamboiba, Thabaton and Keibu Keioiba, Lai Khutshangbi, Uchek Langmeidong, Eeta Thangmei, and Tapta.

Meitei mythology includes stories concerning Khunu Leima, Nganu Leima, Panthoibi, Nongpok Ningthou, and other deities. The sources also describe folktales involving a pigeon, a hornbill, and a golden parrot. The Meitei language and script have been the subjects of revival movements, including the classical language movement and efforts associated with the revival of Meitei mayek.

## Language & religion

Meitei, also known as Manipuri or Meiteilon, belongs to the Tibeto-Burman branch of the Sino-Tibetan language family. It is the official language and lingua franca of Manipur and an additional official language in four districts of Assam. It has a literary tradition of not less than 2,000 years. Meitei proper, Loi, and Pangal are identified as its three main dialects.

Historically, Meitei was written in the indigenous Meitei mayek script. The script was replaced by the Bengali script in the 18th century and later revived. Meitei mayek is also called Kanglei script or kok sam lai script. In 2021, its use for writing Manipuri was officially adopted by the Government of Manipur alongside Bengali script.

Sanamahism, also known as Meiteism or Lainingthouism, is the ethnic religion of the Meitei people. It is polytheistic and named after Lainingthou Sanamahi. Principal deities include Lainingthou Sanamahi, Sidaba Mapu, Pakhangba, Leimarel Sidabi, and Imoinu Ahongbi. Many Meiteis follow Hinduism as well as Sanamahi traditions and rituals, including worship of Sanamahi in the south-west corners of their homes.
