## Overview

The Konkani people, also called Konkanites, are an Indo-Aryan ethnolinguistic group native to the Konkan region of the Indian subcontinent. Konkani is spoken along the western coast of India, especially in Goa, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Kerala, Gujarat, and the territories of Daman, Diu, and Silvassa. Konkani-speaking populations are also found in cities and regions including Mangalore, Kochi, Ahmedabad, Karachi, New Delhi, and Surat. The 2011 Census Department of India figures listed 2,256,502 Konkani speakers in India. Konkani is one of the 22 scheduled languages of the Indian Constitution and has been the official language of Goa since 1987. Its many dialects and scripts reflect migrations, religious change, and long contact with Marathi, Kannada, Malayalam, Portuguese, and other languages.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They record that Hindu and Catholic communities in Goa experienced major cultural and linguistic changes during Portuguese rule.

### Architecture

They do record the destruction of Hindu temples in Goa during Portuguese rule. In 1567, 300 Hindu temples were destroyed in Bardez after the majority of local Hindus had converted to Christianity; temples at Assolna and Cuncolim were also destroyed in 1583.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The historical sources mention stone and wood implements among prehistoric groups in Goa and Konkan, and refer to inscriptions on stone and copper plates.

## Music & performance

They mention hymns in the history of Konkani literature and language. A hymn dedicated to Lord Narayana is attributed to the 12th century AD, and another hymn is described as belonging to the later 16th century. The sources also state that Konkani was studied and promoted by Catholic missionaries in Goa during the 16th century, including Thomas Stephens, who used it as a communication medium.

The sources describe religious and linguistic contexts rather than musical performance. They state that the Portuguese required fluency in Portuguese from Goan applicants to the priesthood and their immediate relatives in 1745. They also record that Hindu rituals, including marriages, sacred-thread wearing, and cremation, were prohibited from public performance under Portuguese rule from 1567. No information is supplied about instruments, dance accompaniment, funeral music, court performance, or contemporary Konkani musical organizations.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record several historical religious practices and commemorations. During Portuguese rule, laws enacted from 4 December 1567 prohibited the public performance of Hindu rituals such as marriages, sacred-thread wearing, and cremation. Hindu temples at Assolna and Cuncolim were destroyed in 1583 after the majority of local inhabitants had converted to Christianity.

The sources describe 9 April as World Konkani Day, also called Vishwa Konkani Dis. It commemorates the death anniversary of Vaman Raghunath Varde Valaulikar, known affectionately as Shenoi Goembab, who worked to unite Konkani speakers and revive Konkani literature. The sources also record the first All India Konkani Parishad in Karwar in 1939 and state that at least 29 subsequent adhiveshans were held.

## Foodways

The sources document Malvani cuisine as the standard cuisine of the South Konkan region of Maharashtra and Goa. It overlaps with Maharashtrian and Goan cuisine and uses coconut in forms including grated coconut, dry-grated coconut, fried coconut, coconut paste, and coconut milk. Its masalas may include dried red chilies, coriander seeds, peppercorns, cumin, cardamom, ginger, and garlic. Kokum, dried kokam or amsul, tamarind, and raw mango or kairi are also used. The Malvani masala is a dried powder made from 15 to 16 dry spices.

Fish dishes dominate Malvani cuisine. Solkadhi is a pink-colored drink made from kokam fruit and coconut milk. It is also described as an appetizer usually served with kombdi vade, fish, and mutton Malvani. *Kombdi vade* or chicken Malvani consists of Malvani chicken curry, vade, onion, lemon, and solkadhi. Vade are fluffy fried breads made from jowar or rice and lentils flour.

Other named dishes include *mori masala*, or shark curry; *bangda fry*, made from fried mackerel; *Malvani mutton curry*; *kavda curry*, made from the local Konkani bird called Kavda; *bombil fry*, or Bombay duck fry; *paplet saar*, made with pomfret; *phanasachi bhaji*, made from raw jackfruit, chilies, and grated coconut; *kajuchi or fakachi usal*, a cashew curry; *prawn curry*; *kolambi fry*; and *kalya vatanyancha samara*, a black pea curry made with coconut and Malvani masala.

The sources also name *dhondas*, a baked cucumber preparation made with rice flour, coconut, and jaggery; *ghaavane*, a rice-flour pancake; *khaproli*, a pancake dipped in sweet coconut milk; *tandalachi bhakri*, made from rice flour; *ambolya*, made from fermented rice and udad daal batter; *shirvale*, rice-flour noodles served with sweetened coconut milk; and *ras-poli*, a fried pancake served with sweetened coconut milk.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do describe a substantial literary and language-revival history. Vaman Raghunath Varde Valaulikar wrote works in Konkani and is regarded as a pioneer of modern Konkani literature. His efforts sought to unite Konkani Hindus and Christians regardless of caste or religion.

Madhav Manjunath Shanbhag, an advocate from Karwar, travelled through Konkani-speaking areas seeking to unite the fragmented Konkani community under the banner of “one language, one script, one literature.” The first All India Konkani Parishad was organized in Karwar in 1939. The sources also state that the Sahitya Akademi recognized Konkani as an independent language in 1975 and that Konkani literature received recognition through an annual award.

## Language & religion

Konkani belongs to the Indo-Aryan branch and forms part of the Marathi-Konkani group of the southern Indo-Aryan languages. It is spoken in many dialects, which are often only partially mutually intelligible. Contemporary Konkani is written in Devanagari, Kannada, Malayalam, Persian, and Roman scripts. The Goan Antruz dialect in Devanagari has been promulgated as Standard Konkani.

The language has been influenced by Prakrit vernaculars, Sanskrit, Dravidian languages, Kannada, Marathi, Malayalam, and Portuguese. It was called Canarim, Concanim, Gomantaki, Bramana, and Goani, while Portuguese sources used Língua Canarim, Língua Brahmana, and Língua Concanim. Konkani-speaking communities include Hindus, Catholics, and Muslims. The sources describe ancient traces of Shamanic religion in Goa, worship of a mother goddess, anthill worship, Buddhism, Jainism, Shaivism, Christianity, and Islam in different historical periods.
