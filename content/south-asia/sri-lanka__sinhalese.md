## Overview

The Sinhalese are an Indo-Aryan people native to Sri Lanka. Since 1815, they have been broadly divided into up-country and low-country communities. Their culture includes sculpture, literature, dance, poetry, rituals, architecture, cuisine and martial arts. Theravada Buddhism is central, alongside Hindu traditions, indigenous beliefs and Christianity.

## Material culture

Sinhalese material culture includes regional dress, ceremonial costumes, ancient buildings and waterworks, wooden crafts, pottery and temple objects.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Sinhalese textiles include regional clothing, patterned wraps and costumes used in demon ceremonies and dances.

- **Up-country women’s outfit** (*Reda and Hatte*) — This outfit has a puffed shoulder jacket and a frill tucked around the top of the skirt.
- **Dance apron** (*hana; kukul sayam*) — This coastal dance apron is made from hemp and yarn soaked in paint.
- **Tovil costume** (*Tovil*) — Tovil costumes include cotton skirts with red, black and white decoration or red-and-white sections.
- **Patterned cloth wraps** — A museum photograph shows Sinhalese women and a child in Colombo wearing patterned cloth wraps.

### Clothing & dress

Sinhalese dress differs by region, caste and occasion, with sarongs, regional women’s outfits and the Kandyan sari among the main forms.

- **Men’s sarong** (*sarama*) — Men traditionally wear a sarong, sometimes with a long-sleeved shirt during recreation.
- **Kandyan sari** (*Osariya*) — High-caste Kandyan women traditionally wear a sari with a frill along the lower half and sometimes puffed sleeves.
- **Low-country women’s dress** — Low-country women wear a white long-sleeved jacket with a tight wraparound skirt.
- **South Indian-style saree** — Low-country high-caste women wear a South Indian-style saree.
- **Western-style clothing** — In populated areas, men may wear suits and women may wear skirts and blouses.

### Architecture

Sinhalese architecture is known for ancient cities, monumental buildings, fortresses and large waterworks.

- **Anuradhapura** — Anuradhapura is one of the ancient cities linked with Sinhalese architectural history.
- **Polonnaruwa** — Polonnaruwa is another ancient city linked with Sinhalese architectural history.
- **Sigiriya fortress** — Sigiriya combines a natural and human-made fortress with frescoes and many architectural features.
- **Ancient tanks and reservoirs** — Sinhalese hydraulic technology included tanks, ponds with fountains, moats and irrigation reservoirs.
- **Parakrama Samudra** — Parakrama Samudra is one of the named ancient waterworks.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources mention hill-country pottery, wooden crafts and several ceremonial or household objects.

- **Wooden handicrafts** — Traditional wooden handicrafts are associated with the hill country.
- **Clay pottery** — Traditional clay pottery is also associated with the hill country.
- **Wooden and metal knife** (*pihiya*) — A museum record identifies a wooden and metal knife as a pihiya.
- **Temple bell** (*sinuva; ghantaraya*) — This bell is used by Sinhalese and Hindus in Buddhist or Hindu temples throughout Sri Lanka.
- **Ivory comb** — An ivory comb is decorated with two kinnaras holding vinas.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources give little information about Sinhalese personal adornment and mainly mention adornment on a Kolam mask.

- **Earrings and elongated earlobes** — A Kolam mask representing a possible vädda princess is described with earrings and elongated earlobes.

## Music & performance

Sinhalese music includes festival music, work songs and modern popular styles.

- **Festival music** — Buddhist festivals use distinctive music played on traditional Sinhalese instruments.
- **Work poems** — Folk poems about particular jobs were four-line communal songs sung during tasks such as harvesting and sowing.
- **Reflective popular song style** (*Sarala gee*) — Ananda Samarakoon developed the reflective and poignant Sarala gee style in the late 1930s and early 1940s.

## Dance & theatre

Sinhalese dance and theatre include animal dances, healing performances, demon exorcisms and masked puppet traditions.

- **Kandyan dance routines** (*Wannam*) — Kandyan dance has 18 Wannam representing animals such as the elephant, eagle, cobra, monkey, peacock and rabbit.
- **Healing dance** (*Pahatharata*) — Pahatharata uses masks of gods and demons, fire and water to help cure illness and bring spiritual clarification.
- **Demon-exorcism performance** (*Tovil*) — Tovil is a performance tradition for exorcising demons.
- **Kolam theatre** (*Kolam*) — Kolam uses masks and puppets, including figures such as King Maha Sammata and riri yaka.
- **String puppets** (*nul rukadaya*) — Kolam string puppets are called nul rukadaya in Sinhala.

## Festivals & rituals

Sinhalese ritual life combines Buddhist festivals, demon exorcisms, healing practices and New Year celebrations.

- **Demon exorcism** (*tovil*) — Tovils praise and invoke the Buddha and the gods to exorcise demons.
- **Sinhalese New Year** (*Aluth Avurudu/Puthandu*) — The Sinhalese/Tamil New Year is a major time for folk music, dance and special foods.
- **Milk rice offering** (*kiribath*) — Kiribath is prepared for birthdays, New Year celebrations and religious festivals.
- **New Year sweets** (*kavum; kokis; mung kavum; od iba*) — New Year kiribath is served with sweets including kavum, kokis, mung kavum and od iba.

## Foodways

Sinhalese food centers on rice, coconut, spices, fish, vegetables and fruits, with strong Indian, Indonesian and Dutch influences.

- **Milk rice** (*kiribath*) — Kiribath is rice cooked in salted coconut milk.
- **Shredded roti stir-fry** (*kottu*) — Kottu is shredded roti stir-fried with vegetables and optional egg, meat or cheese.
- **Rice-flour cakes** (*pittu*) — Pittu is steamed or roasted rice flour mixed with grated coconut.
- **Seafood broth** (*kool*) — Kool is a seafood broth from Jaffna.
- **Rice varieties** (*Samba; Kekulu; Suwandel; Pachchaperumal; Kaluheenati; Madathawalu*) — Named rice varieties include Samba, Kekulu, Suwandel, Pachchaperumal, Kaluheenati and Madathawalu.

## Oral tradition & literature

Sinhalese literature includes Buddhist chronicles, folk tales, classical works and modern novels.

- **Great Chronicle** (*Mahavamsa*) — The Mahavamsa is a Pali chronicle compiled by Buddhist monks and describes Sinhalese origins and ancient kingdoms.
- **Later Chronicle** (*Culavamsa*) — The Culavamsa continues the recorded history of the Sinhalese.
- **Foolish traveler tale** (*Mahadana Muttha saha Golayo*) — This folk tale follows a foolish figure whose ignorance causes trouble for him and his followers.
- **Court jester tale** (*Kawate Andare*) — This folk tale concerns a witty court jester, the royal court and his son.
- **Children’s novel** (*Madol Duwa*) — Madol Duwa is a children’s novel by Martin Wickremasinghe.

## Language & religion

Sinhala is Sri Lanka’s main Sinhalese language, while Theravada Buddhism is joined by Hindu, indigenous and Christian traditions.

- **Sinhala language** (*Sinhala*) — Sinhala is an Indo-Aryan language with written and spoken varieties, regional forms and a script written from left to right.
- **Sinhala script** — The Sinhala script is an abugida based on ancient Brahmi.
- **Theravada Buddhism** (*Theravada*) — Theravada is the oldest and predominant school of Buddhism in Sri Lanka.
- **Kataragama deity** (*Kataragama Deviyo*) — Kataragama Deviyo is one of the named figures in Sinhalese religious practice.
- **Other named deities** (*Upulvan; Ayyanayake*) — Upulvan and Ayyanayake are also named figures in Sinhalese religious practice.
