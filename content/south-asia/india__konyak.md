## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| Who | A Naga people with a distinct Konyak language. |
| Where | Especially Mon district, Nagaland, with villages across the Myanmar border. |
| How many | No current population estimate is established by the sources used here. |
| Language | Konyak; speech varies between villages. |
| Religion | Christianity is widespread in Mon today; older practices appear in historical and festival sources. |
| Known for | Baskets · Carved wood · Dress and ornaments · Aoleang |

## Overview

The 45 British Museum objects selected here are identified as Konyak. Many came from villages such as Wakching and Longkhai. They document particular historical collections, with many portable objects and little evidence of present-day life. Two nineteenth-century pieces from Jaboka have a qualified “Konyak (?)” museum attribution; their acquisition notes identify them as Jabboka Naga objects, and the Mon district plan names Jaboka among Konyak chiefly villages.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The gallery has belts and a palm-leaf skirt but no shawl. A [Bowers Museum *nyauni*](https://collections.bowers.org/objects/144791/shawl-nyauni) documents one Konyak shawl. [Nagaland Living Heritage](https://www.nagalandlivingheritage.com/community-page/konyak/textiles) describes the *shengnung* wrap and the chevron-patterned *Anghya Nyekok*, historically associated with a chief's family and adapted by weavers today.

### Clothing & dress

- **Belts and skirt** — A woven cotton warrior's belt, a palm-leaf skirt and other belts show several forms and materials.
- **Gauntlets and headgear** — Cane, bamboo, hair, bone and fibre appear in selected dance leggings, gauntlets and helmets.

### Architecture

A carved house board came from a *morung*. Carved morung-post and roof-ornament models represent parts of a building; the models are not photographs of an intact structure. The Mon district plan describes morungs as places where unmarried young men assembled and discussed village matters.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

- **Baskets** — Bamboo and fibre baskets vary in form; one ceremonial basket incorporates a monkey skull and tusk.
- **Lime boxes and pipes** — Carved containers and tobacco pipes document storage and use.
- **Comb, rat trap and brush** — Bamboo, rattan and pandanus make smaller tools in the selection.
- **Spears and shield** — Museum records document arms; the Jaboka shield has a qualified Konyak attribution.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The objects include a bead necklet, shell and cane arm gauntlets, ear ornaments and a bronze armlet. A carved wooden head depicts facial tattooing documented in older Konyak material; one object cannot establish who was tattooed or how widely the practice continued.

## Music & performance

Two bamboo jaw harps are the gallery's instruments. Government descriptions of Aoleang also mention log drums and singing; the objects alone do not document a melody.

## Dance & theatre

A [Nagaland cultural programme](https://ipr.nagaland.gov.in/CULTURAL-DISPLAY-MARKS-THE-4th-DAY-OF-HORNBILL-FESTIVAL) names *Alea-Boa* as a Konyak dance associated with Aoleang. The other sources here describe festival dancing without establishing a theatre tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

- **Aoleang Monyu** — A six-day festival in early April after sowing, with preparation, feasts, visits, dress, song, dance and village cleaning.
- **Lao-ong Mo** — The Mon district plan places this festival in September after the harvest.
- **Historical basket** — The British Museum uses the acquisition label “head hunter's basket” for one object; that label describes an older collecting context.

## Foodways

The [Aoleang account](https://ipr.nagaland.gov.in/konyak) mentions rice beer, meat, vegetables, banana leaves and food exchanged with relatives and neighbours. The selected objects do not establish recipes or a complete everyday cuisine.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe festival songs and the morung's historical role in village discussion. The Konyak Literature Board published a descriptive grammar in 2025.

## Language & religion

Konyak speech varies by village. The Mon district tourism office describes widespread Christianity today and earlier beliefs about nature. The Aoleang account records prayers for crops. These sources describe different periods and settings.

## Glossary

- *Angh* — A traditional Konyak village chief.
- *morung* — A communal institution associated with young men and village discussion.
- *nyauni* — A Konyak shawl documented by the Bowers Museum.
- *shengnung* — A wrap described by Nagaland Living Heritage.
- *Anghya Nyekok* — A patterned wrap historically associated with a chief's family.
- *Aoleang Monyu* — The Konyak festival in early April.
- *Lao-ong Mo* — A festival after the harvest.
