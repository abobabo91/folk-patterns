## Overview

The Mizo people, historically called the Lushais, are a Tibeto-Burman ethnic group primarily from Mizoram in northeast India. Communities also live in Manipur, Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Myanmar, Bangladesh, the United States, Canada and Israel. The Mizo language, also known as *Duhlián ṭawng*, belongs to the Tibeto-Burman or Sino-Tibetan family and functions as the official language and lingua franca of Mizoram alongside English. Mizo identity includes several clans and tribes, including Hmâr, Lusei, Râlte, Lai and Mara. Oral traditions connect the people with Chhînlung, while historical accounts describe migrations through southern China, Burma, the Kabaw Valley and the Lushai Hills. Mizo folk culture includes distinctive handwoven textiles, gong and drum music, songs, dances, ritual systems, cuisine and oral literature.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Puan** — *Puan* is the principal traditional Mizo fabric, produced through handloom weaving. It remains worn at important festivals and festivities, although it is now predominantly associated with women’s dress.

**Puanchei** — *Puanchei* is a multicolored striped puan used especially as a traditional wedding garment for Mizo brides. Its horizontal stripes are called *Sakeizangzia* and are said to allude to tiger stripes.

**Ngotekherh** — *Ngotekherh*, traditionally known as *Puan Hruih*, consists of black and white stripes. It is worn during festivals including Chapchâr Kût, Mim Kût and Pâwl Kût.

**Pawndum** — *Pawndum* is generally woven in black, yellow and white stripes. It may form part of a bride’s trousseau and is also used in mourning, including to cover a husband’s body after death.

**Tawlhloh Puan** — *Tawlhloh Puan* is an indigo-colored textile with red and green stripes. It was traditionally worn by warriors on the battlefield and is now treated as prestige attire.

**Hmaram** — *Hmaram* is one of five puans described as important in distinguishing Mizo culture and associated with a geographic indicator tag.

**Thuam Chhawm** — *Thuam Chhawm* is the practice in which a woman brings puan to the groom’s house after marriage and names it.

**Motif vocabulary.** Sakeizangzia — the horizontal stripes of a puanchei, associated with tiger stripes.

### Clothing & dress

The puan was traditionally worn by both men and women. Men also wore jewellery before the onset of Christianity, but Western clothing contributed to the decline of puan in male dress and to the shedding of male jewellery by the 1960s. Women continued to wear puan after the establishment of Christianity. *Puanchei* is especially associated with Mizo brides, while *Ngotekherh* is worn at festivals. *Pawndum* is connected with a bride’s trousseau, mourning and funerary practice. *Tawlhloh Puan*, formerly associated with warriors, is now prestige attire. The sources describe changes in women’s clothing through employment and Western fashion, including skirts, coat suits, dresses, frocks, uniforms and facial cosmetics.

### Architecture

The sources mention the *zawlbûk*, or young men’s dormitory, in connection with village life, drinking and the keeping of drums. They also refer to houses with features whose construction was traditionally associated with the title of *thangchhuahpa*: windows, a raised veranda and a front room. In the Khuangchawi account, a special box called *khuanghlang* was used to carry the feast host and his wife to the chief’s courtyard.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Hmâr communities traditionally participated in blacksmithing, carpentry, brass working and pottery. Mizo gong traditions include brass instruments such as the *darkhuang*, *darbu*, *darmang* and *darbenthek*. Wooden and plant-based objects include the *bengbung* xylophone-like instrument, the *talhkhuang*, bamboo instruments, temporary ritual posts called *seluphan*, and the *zupeng*, a container of rice beer used in the *Sedawi* ritual.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources state that jewellery was worn and adorned by men before Christianity. They also describe traditional hair practices: Lusei and Duhlian identity was associated with how hair was tied at the back of the head, while Pawi was a name given by Lusei to people who tied their hair on the top of the forehead.

## Music & performance

Mizo music is primarily vocal and is accompanied by drums, gongs, bamboo wind instruments and other percussion. The word *rimawi* was coined in 1966 for instrumental music, while *hla* means song and appears in the names of many song categories. Mizo musical history is divided into *pipute hla*, the pre-missionary songs of the ancestors; *sakhaw thar hla*, songs of the missionary era; and *tunlai hlate*, contemporary songs.

Named folk-song forms include *dar hla* or gong songs, *bawh hla* or war chants, *hlado* or hunting chants, *thiam hla* and *dawi hla* for ritual invocation and incantation, *puipun hla* or festive songs, *lengzem zai* or love songs, and *hnam hla*, nationalist songs. *Khawhar zai* is associated with death and bereavement, while *lengkhawm zai* is a Christian congregational tradition combining Mizo melody with Christian themes.

The principal drum is the *khuang*, made from a hollowed log covered at both ends with animal hide. Its sizes include *khuangpui*, *khuanglai* and *khuangte*, while an elongated drum is called *kawlkhuang*. Gong types include the large *darkhuang*, the three-gong *darbu*, the timekeeping *darmang* and paired *darbenthek*. Wind instruments include *rawchhem*, *tawtawrawt*, *phenglawng*, *tumphit*, *buhchangkuang* and *hnahtum*. Stringed instruments include *tingtang*, *lemlawi* and *tuiumdar*. Christian choral music uses tonic sol-fa notation, and the *Kristian Hla Bu* contains 600 hymns.

## Dance & theatre

Named Mizo dances include **Cheraw**, **Khuallam**, **Chheihlam**, **Chailam**, **Tlanglam**, **Sarlamkai**, **Chawnglaizawn**, *Zangtalam*, *Rallu Lam* and *Sakei Lu Lam*. Cheraw, known as the bamboo dance, is performed by women stepping between and across pairs of bamboo sticks held by seated participants. Before Christianity it was associated with rites intended to appease harmful spirits and to secure a deceased child’s safe passage to *pialrâl*.

**Khuallam**, the guest dance, was performed by groups of ten or more honoured invitees entering a feast called *khuangchawi*. **Sarlamkai**, associated with Pawi and Lakher communities, was connected with war and ceremonies after raids or battles. **Chai** is performed during Chapchâr Kût, with boys and girls standing alternately in a circle. **Chheih lam** is performed by one dancer while others sit in a circle, clap and accompany the dancer with drums.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe Chapchâr Kût, Mim Kût, Pâwl Kût, Thalfavang Kût and Khuangchawi. Chapchâr Kût is associated with the Chai dance; Mim Kût and Pâwl Kût are named among the festivals at which Ngotekherh is worn. Rice beer called *zû* was prepared for festivals, marriages and village feasts.

*Khuangchawi* was described in traditional religion as the highest festival and the final stage in entering *pialrâl*. It was performed in October, during *Khuangchawi thla*, when the moon was brightest and there was no work to be done in the jhums. The ceremony required two fully grown *sial* for the village, one mother sial for children and two male pigs for the household. Preparation took approximately three months, and guests entered through a dance called Khuallum.

The life-cycle and religious rites described include *Sakung*, performed by a newly married person establishing a separate household; *Chawng*, involving a community feast and conferring the titles *Chawngpa* and *Chawngnu*; *Dawi no chhui*, a sequence of family feasts; and *Sedawi*, involving the spearing of a sial. The sources also describe communal singing in the home of a bereaved family for at least three days and nights. Christianity, introduced through missionaries, transformed Mizo ritual and musical life.

## Foodways

Rice is the staple food of the Mizo people. Meals commonly combine rice with meat and vegetables, and fish, chicken, pork and beef are popular. Cooking is described as using available oil, with many meals containing relatively little oil and many vegetables. *Bai* is a vegetable stew; versions may include sesame, garlic, onion, herbs, *bekang* or *sa-um*. *Sawhchiar* combines rice with beef, pork or chicken.

Named side dishes include *rep*, smoked meat mixed with chillies, local herbs and leafy greens; *chhum han*, mixed steamed vegetables; *hmarcha rawt*, a chilli chutney; and *bekang*, fermented soybeans. Lunch foods include *sanpiau*, rice porridge with meat, sauce and Mizo spices; *chhangban*, sticky local rice bread; *artui chhipchhuan chow*, fried egg with noodles; and *alu chop*.

Fermented foods include *bekang-um*, *sa-um*, *chhi-um*, *ai-um*, *aite-um*, *tam-um* and *tuai-um*. Traditional rice beers include *zupui*, *zufâng*, *tin-zu* and *rakzu*. *Zû* was made from husked rice through distillation and was consumed collectively rather than commercially, especially in *zawlbûks*, at festivals, marriages and feasts.

## Oral tradition & literature

Mizo oral tradition includes songs and tales concerning Chhînlung, the ancestral homeland or migration place. The *hla* of Chhînlung has been passed from generation to generation, and songs describe departure from the old settlement. Mizo folklore includes stories of natural places and figures such as Kungawrhi puk, Sibuta Lung, Phulpui Grave, Chhingpui Memorial and Chhurbura, a culture hero whose stories combine foolishness and wisdom.

A distinctive literary genre is *Rambuai*, referring to poetry, songs and literature associated with the Mizo Insurgency of 1966–1986. It addresses social and cultural trauma connected with sexual violence, air attacks, village groupings and curfews. Mizo literature has written and oral traditions and developed mainly from the Lusei dialect, with influence from Pawi, Paihtê and Hmâr. The Mizo Hlakungpui Mual, established in 1986, commemorates poets and writers; four phases of monuments have honoured 36 individuals.

## Language & religion

Mizo, also known as *Duhlián ṭawng*, is a Sino-Tibetan language generally classified within the Central Kuki-Chin languages. It is based mainly on the Lusei dialect and includes words from surrounding clans such as Hmâr and Pawi. The Mizo alphabet is based on the Roman alphabet and has 25 letters. A written script for Lushai was created in 1874, and the present form was devised by Christian missionaries using the Hunterian system of transliteration.

Before Christianity, Mizo communities followed a religion described as Lushai Animism, *Sakhua* or Khua worship. Its spiritual figures include Pathian, Khuanu, Khuavang, Pu Vâna, Vanchungnula, Khaltu and various *Ramhuai*. Religious specialists included the *sadâwt*, *tlahpawi* and *bawlpu*. Christianity was introduced through missionaries and became central to Mizo cultural transformation.
