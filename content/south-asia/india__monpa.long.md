## Overview

The Monpa are a major people of Arunachal Pradesh in northeastern India and one of the 56 officially recognized ethnic groups in China. Most Monpas live in Arunachal Pradesh, especially in Tawang and West Kameng districts; smaller populations live in East Kameng, Bhutan, and parts of Tibet, including Tsona County, Pêlung in Bayi District, and Mêdog County. The sources give differing population figures: one passage states 50,000 in Arunachal Pradesh, while another gives around 60,000; around 9,000 Monpas are stated to live in Tibet, and 11,143 were recorded in Le, Lebo, Lebugou, or Lebugou township of Cona or Tsona City in 2020. Their languages belong to the Tibeto-Burman family and are written with the Tibetan script. Their folk-culture significance in the sources lies in wood carving, weaving, paper manufacture, wooden and bamboo work, Tibetan Buddhist practice, festivals, agriculture, and pastoral life.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Weaving** — The Monpa are known for carpet making and weaving.

**Bamboo weaving** — Bamboo weaving is identified as a Monpa craft. The museum records include a rectangular box made from split bamboo strips, woven mostly in a herringbone pattern in yellow bamboo with a counterpoint design in green. Its rounded corners result from the continuous weaving.

**Woodblock printing** — The Monpa manufacture paper from the pulp of the local sukso tree. At the Tawang Monastery, a printing press produces religious texts on local paper and wooden blocks, generally for literate Monpa Lamas. Museum records describe hand-made paper from the daphne bush printed with wooden blocks.

**Paper manufacture** — Paper is made from the pulp of the local sukso tree; museum records also describe paper made from the Daphne bush. The records do not establish whether these names refer to different plants.

**Prayer-flag printing** — Museum records describe rectangular woodblock prints on hand-made paper, with ink prepared from soot from an oil lamp. One print has a central “wind horse” carrying flaming jewels, the Four Protective Animals at the corners, invocatory texts in Tibetan, auspicious symbols, and the Banner of Victory.

**Amulet printing** — The museum records include woodblock prints depicting royal male and female figures, two arrows, and what is probably a distaff with a ball of wool. These were printed on hand-produced paper from the Daphne bush.

### Clothing & dress

The traditional dress of the Monpa is based on the Tibetan chuba. Men and women wear headwear made of yak hair with long tassels. Women commonly wear a warm jacket and a sleeveless chemise reaching to the calves; the chemise is tied around the waist with a long, narrow piece of cloth. A cap with a single peacock feather in a felt hat is also mentioned.

### Architecture

Monpa houses are constructed of stone and wood, with plank floors. They often have carved doors and window frames. Roofs are made with bamboo matting, which helps keep the houses warm during winter. Sitting platforms and hearths are found in living rooms. The sources describe the Tawang Monastery as an important religious institution and mention gompas.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Monpa are known for wooden bowls and bamboo weaving. The British Museum records include a rectangular bamboo box with a lid, a moulded and unfired clay votive stupa, and small clay votive stupas made from metal moulds. One stupa has a central upper dome and eight round-domed stupas set on a lotus-petal base. Another is described as having a central and uppermost miniature stupa surrounded by concentric rings of smaller stupas; the monk who produced it gave the total as 108.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Monpa ornaments are made of silver, corals, and turquoise.

## Music & performance

The sources describe religious and festival performance contexts. Pantomime dances are the principal feature of the Ajilamu festival. During Choskar, Buddhist lamas read religious scriptures in the gompas for a few days, after which villagers walk around cultivated fields carrying sutras on their backs. The sources also mention printed religious texts, amulets, prayer-flag prints, and prayer-wheel sheets. No source supplied here covers Monpa singing, instrumental music, musical ensembles, or performance at weddings, funerals, courts, or tea houses.

## Dance & theatre

Pantomime dances are the principal feature of the Ajilamu festival. The Choskar festival includes a processional practice: after Buddhist lamas read religious scriptures in the gompas, villagers walk around cultivated fields with sutras on their backs. The sources present this as a religious and agricultural observance rather than as theatre.

## Festivals & rituals

Principal Monpa festivals include the Choskar harvest festival, Losar, and Torgya. During Losar, people generally offer prayers at the Tawang Monastery for the coming of the Tibetan New Year.

Choskar is associated with cultivation and the protection of crops. Buddhist lamas read religious scriptures in the gompas for a few days. The villagers then walk around cultivated fields with sutras on their backs. The stated purpose is to pray for better cultivation and the prosperity of villagers and to protect grain from insects and wild animals.

Torgya is identified in a British Museum catalogue record as a festival a month before the Tibetan New Year. A rectangular bamboo box with a lid was made for Torgya.

The Monpa also observe religious practices connected with votive stupas, amulets, prayer flags, prayer wheels, and the Tawang Monastery. A museum record describes a sheet of hand-made paper printed with the mantra “Om mani padme hum” for inclusion inside a prayer-wheel located around the outside of a monument. Another record describes a similar print with “Om mane padme hum,” surrounded by symbols, including the Eight Auspicious Symbols and symbols denoting offerings.

The sources state that, at birth, there is no strict preference for a boy or a girl.

## Foodways

Monpa food habits are shaped by extreme climatic conditions. They consume substantial amounts of cheese, salt, and meat with alcohol to cope with extreme cold. Butter tea is mentioned, as are locally made distilled liquors from maize, millet, barley, buckwheat, or rice. These liquors are known as Chang, Baang-Chang, and Sin-Chang.

The Monpa practice shifting and permanent cultivation. Crops include paddy, maize, wheat, barley, millet, buckwheat, peppers, pumpkin, and beans. Cattle, yaks, cows, pigs, sheep, and fowl are kept as domestic animals.

The sources state that commercially processed store-bought foods and fast foods such as chips, burgers, and samosa, along with cappuccino coffees and commercial alcohols, are widely prevalent among younger people. This is associated with tourism, western culture, and changes in youth diet. No source supplied here documents a ceremonial food or a named festival dish.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that a printing press at the Tawang Monastery produces religious texts on local paper and wooden blocks, generally for literate Monpa Lamas. Museum records describe printed amulets, prayer-flag prints, and sheets bearing Tibetan mantras.

## Language & religion

Monpa languages are often treated collectively as the “Monpa languages,” although this is not a genealogical term and includes several different languages. The sources place them within the Tibeto-Burman family and state that they differ significantly from the Eastern Tibetan dialect. They are written with the Tibetan script. Languages and varieties associated with the Monpa include Kho-Bwa, East Bodish, and Tshangla; Tawang is an East Bodish language and a variety of Dakpa. Other named languages include Sherdukpen, Lish, Sartang, Bugun, Dirang, Morshing, Kalaktang, and Brokpa.

The Monpa generally adhere to the Gelug sect of Tibetan Buddhism, adopted in the 17th century under the influence of the Bhutanese-educated Merag Lama. In Zemithang, Monpas adhere to the Nyingmapa strand of the Mahayana/Tantrayana tradition. Historical accounts in the sources also mention Nyingma and Kagyu influence, the adoption of the Tibetan alphabet for religious purposes, and the central role of Tawang Monastery.
