## Overview

Maldivians (Dhivehi: ދިވެހިން, romanized: Dhivēhin) are an Indo-Aryan ethnic group and nation native to the Maldive Islands. They constitute the Republic of Maldives and the island of Minicoy in Lakshadweep, India, and share a common ancestry, history, culture, and language. More than 250,000 belong to the main group, approximately 60,000 to the southern group, and about 10,000 to the people of Minicoy, known as Mahls. The main group inhabits atolls from Ihavandhippolhu (Haa Alif) to Haddhunmathi (Laamu), while the southern group lives in Huvadhu, Fuvahmulah, and Addu atolls. The Maldivian language is central to identity and is an Indo-European language with Dravidian influences. Folk culture includes distinctive dialects, coconut- and tuna-centred folklore, Islamic festivals, foodways, dress, mats, weaving, and performing arts with Indian and African roots.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Libaas* — A red top with a gold neck trim worn by Maldivian women with a long black skirt.

Mats and other objects — Local materials are used to produce mats and handheld or display objects.

### Clothing & dress

Traditionally, Maldivian men wear a *mundu* with a shirt, in a style described as similar to that of Malayali people. Maldivian women wear a red top with a gold neck trim called a *Libaas* and a long black skirt.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources mention local materials used to make mats and handheld or display objects. During *Keyn* meals, food is served in a large wooden dish called a *Malaafaiy*. Beverages are placed in glasses, water is served in a ceramic jug, and the hand is washed using a copper jug into a copper basin.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention the gold neck trim of the women’s *Libaas*.

## Music & performance

The sources describe traditional Maldivian performing arts as having Indian and African roots. They also mention *Mauloodhu*, a prayer accompanied by a festive meal, and the *Mauloodh Haruge*, a dining hall specially made for this event.

Martial arts are known as *hevikamuge kulhivaruthak*, while *gulhamathi hifun* is traditional wrestling. These names identify physical practices rather than musical traditions. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription is supplied for the Maldives.

## Festivals & rituals

Most Maldivian festivals are related to Islam, while some belong to older Maldivian traditions, including a kite flying festival. Naming a newborn child, *Mauloodhu*, the Eid festival, and circumcision of the male child are events accompanied by celebrations. *Mauloodhu* includes a prayer and a festive meal.

Two named observances are connected with the Maldivian language. 10 December is marked as *Kandu Rōdi duvas*, and 14 April as *Gamu Rōdi duvas*; Maldivian language day has been marked on the latter date from 2011 onwards.

## Foodways

Rice is the major staple food in most Maldivian households. It is usually cooked and served with *Garudiya*, a tuna soup. The sources also describe a traditional meal called *Keyn*, prepared for *Mauloodhus*. One *Keyn* serves 10–12 people and includes rice, curries, salads, grilled fish, coconut cream, coconut syrup, bananas, puddings, and other foods.

*Keyn* is set out in a large wooden *Malaafaiy*. Individual plates are filled with curries, salads, and other items and arranged around the rice. The dish is covered with a lid, wrapped in a white cloth tied at the top, carried into the *Mauloodh Haruge*, and placed on straw mats. Food is consumed using the fingers of the right hand only. At the end of the meal, the hand is washed with water from a copper jug into a copper basin.

Named sweets include *Bocholhi*, made from rice flour, grated coconut, and coconut palm syrup; *Godhan Furhu Boa Folhi*, a pancake made with flour, coconut, eggs, coconut cream, jasmine water, jasmine flowers, coconut palm syrup, cinnamon, cardamom, and oil; and *Han’dulu Aurus*, made from rice, coconut palm syrup, jasmine water, and jasmine flowers. *Han’dulu Furhu Kubus* uses patna rice, coconut, coconut palm syrup, caster sugar, and banana leaf. Other named preparations are *Hukkaru*, *Huni Folhi*, and *Karukuri Banbukeyo*.

## Oral tradition & literature

Maldivian folklore describes dependence on the coconut tree and tuna fish. One legend says that a great sorcerer, or *fandita*, caused coconut trees to grow from the skulls of buried first settlers. The word *naashi*, meaning coconut shell, is also the word for skull in Dhivehi. The coconut tree occupies a central place in the present-day Maldive national emblem.

Tuna is said to have been brought to Maldivian waters by a mythical seafarer, or *maalimi*, called *Bodu Niyami Kalēfanu*, who travelled close to the *Dagas*, the mythical tree at the end of the world. Other origin traditions concern princes, royal couples, hunters, a man-beast, exile, settlement, and the establishment of kingdoms. The sources name five versions of the Koimala myth, including the Ceylonese Prince Koimala, Indian prince, Malakamana, Manadhoo, and Hulhumeedhoo accounts. They mention old manuscripts, oral tradition, and chronicles including the *Lōmāfānu* and *Rādavaḷi*.

## Language & religion

The Maldivian language is central to Maldivian identity. It is an Indo-European language, specifically the southernmost Indo-Aryan language, and shows influences from Dravidian languages as well as loanwords. The main group speaks the standard dialect associated with Malé and the central atolls. Huvadhu, Mulaku, and Addu each have distinctive dialects, while Minicoy has the *Maliku* dialect, also called *Mahl*, which retains features of an older Maldivian and shows Malayalam influences. The dialects of the southern group differ significantly from other dialects.

Islam is the state religion, and Maldivians are entirely Muslims adhering to the Sunni school of thought. Daily life is regulated according to Islam, and government policies are based on Islamic law, or Shari'a.
