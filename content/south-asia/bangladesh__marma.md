## Overview

The Marma are an ethnic group native to parts of south-eastern Bangladesh and southern Tripura in India. In Bangladesh, they primarily reside in the Bandarban, Khagrachari and Rangamati Hill Districts, where they are the second-largest ethnic community in the Chittagong Hill Tracts. They belong to the same community as the Rakhine people. According to the 2022 census, Marmas are the second-largest ethnic group in Bandarban District, the third-largest in Rangamati Hill District and the fourth-largest in Khagrachhari District. Around 20,000 Marmas have settled in Myanmar. Their language belongs to the Tibeto-Burman family: Rakhine is spoken by the Rakhine and Marma peoples and has the Sittwe–Marma, Ramree and Thandwe dialects. Marma culture includes distinctive clothing, Theravada Buddhist ritual, agriculture, festivals and funeral practices.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Marma men wear a sarong called *lungyi*, while Marma women wear a sarong called *thabein*. The sources describe these garments as part of Marma clothing. The culture of the Marmas is described as similar to that of the Rakhine people, including language, food, clothes, religion, dance and funeral rites.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that Marmas traditionally practice slash-and-burn cultivation on the hills.

## Music & performance

They state that Marma culture includes dance and that, during the Sangrai festival, Marmas take part in traditional sports, dances and cultural activities. The sources also describe meetings concerning community issues during Sangrai Akya, the second day of the New Year celebration. No information is provided about the music accompanying these activities, the performers, the contexts of musical performance, or the transmission of musical knowledge.

The sources describe the Marma language as Rakhine, a Tibeto-Burman language spoken primarily in western Myanmar, parts of south-eastern Bangladesh and parts of southern Tripura in India. Rakhine is closely related to Burmese and is spoken by the Rakhine and Marma peoples. The Sittwe–Marma dialect is identified as one of three dialects of Arakanese and is described as accounting for about two thirds of speakers. No specific Marma musical tradition is associated with this dialect in the supplied material.

## Dance & theatre

They state that Marmas participate in dances and cultural activities during Sangrai Akya, the second day of the New Year festival. The sources do not distinguish ceremonial and entertainment dance, describe choreography, or identify performance specialists. They also state that Marma culture is similar to Rakhine culture in dance.

## Festivals & rituals

The Marma New Year is called *Sangrai* and follows the Burmese calendar. It begins on the first day of Bohag and is celebrated over three days. On the first day, called *Painchwai* or *Akro*, homes are decorated with flowers. On the second day, *Sangrai Akya*, Marmas participate in traditional sports, dances and cultural activities, hold meetings about community issues, and go to monasteries for the bathing or cleansing ritual of a Buddha statue. On the third day, *Sangrai Atada*, they cook *hangbong*, a vegetable dish made from more than 100 ingredients.

Another part of Sangrai is *Sangrain Relong Pwe*, the water-pouring event in which young Marma men and women spray each other with water. The festival is also associated with *pangchowai*, described as flower Sangrai, *sangrai ji*, candle lighting and *buddhasnan*. The sources state that the festival is organized on the Bengali New Year occasion of the month Boishakh.

Other festivals named in the sources are Buddha Purnima, Kathina and Oyahgyai or Prabarana Purnima. Birth, death and marriage are also identified as traditional festival occasions. After death, Marma elders are cremated, while younger deceased Marmas are buried.

## Foodways

Marmas mostly depend on agriculture and traditionally practice slash-and-burn cultivation on the hills. The sources name *sangraimu*, a form of traditional cake made for the New Year, and *hangbong*, a vegetable dish prepared on the third day of Sangrai from more than 100 ingredients. The dish is also called *pachan* in Bangla.

The sources describe Sangrai water pouring and monastery rituals. They state that Marma food culture is similar to that of the Rakhine people, without specifying particular Rakhine dishes or cooking methods. The sources also mention delicious cakes made at home during Wagyai or Prabarana Purnima. No further information about their ingredients or preparation is supplied.

## Oral tradition & literature

Marma oral tradition maintains that, before settling in Arakan, their forebears lived in the region of Pegu and were therefore of Mon origin. The supplied source describes this claim as largely unverified. It also states that Marma clan names indicate connections with the royal court of Arakan and that Marmas firmly believe their ancestors originated from Arakan.

They mention the Rakhine Minrazagri Ayedaw Sadan and the Dhanyawaddy Ayedawbon as texts connected with the historical use of the name Mranma.

## Language & religion

Rakhine, also known as Arakanese, is a Tibeto-Burman language spoken by the Rakhine and Marma peoples. It is closely related to Burmese. The language has three dialects: Sittwe–Marma, Ramree and Thandwe. Rakhine is written using the Burmese script, which descends from Southern Brahmi. Most Marma speakers are only literate in Bengali, while Rakhine speakers are taught Rakhine pronunciations using written Burmese.

Marmas principally follow Theravada Buddhism, with ritual practices involving deities. Some also practice Animism and Christianity. Buddhist observances named in the sources include Buddha Purnima, Kathina, Oyahgyai or Prabarana Purnima, monastery visits and the bathing or cleansing of Buddha statues.
