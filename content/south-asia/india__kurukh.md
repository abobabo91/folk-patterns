## Overview

The Kurukh, also known as Oraon, Uraon, Dhangad, and by other local names, are a Dravidian ethnolinguistic group indigenous to the Chhotanagpur Plateau and adjoining areas, especially Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, and West Bengal. Kurukh communities also live in Assam, Tripura, Bangladesh, Nepal, and Bhutan. According to the 2011 census of India, the Scheduled Tribes Kurukh population in India numbers 3,623,512. Traditionally dependent on forests and farms, many Kurukh have become settled agriculturalists, while others work as migrant labourers or in tea gardens. Their folk culture includes named festivals, dances, musical instruments, village institutions, religious practices, marriage customs, and distinctive clothing and tattoo traditions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name elaborate symmetrical tattoo patterns around the forearms, ankles, and chest, but provide no motif names or glosses.

### Clothing & dress

At the turn of the 20th century, Kurukh men wore a loincloth tied around the hips. Women who were less influenced by other communities wore a cloth reaching just above the knee and covering the chest. In the present day, women traditionally wear a thick cotton sari with detailed stitched borders made with purple or red thread. Men wear a thick cloth with similar detailed borders as a dhoti or lungi. The sources describe no specific footwear, belt, or separate ceremonial costume. Tana Bhagats, a religious movement formed by Oraon saints Jatra Bhagat and Turia Bhagat, still wear a khadi kurta, dhoti, and Gandhi topi with a tricoloured flag in the cap.

### Architecture

They identify the *Dhumkuria* as a traditional informal educational institution and youth dormitory, and the *Akhra* as the public and common meeting place where people discuss matters and solve disputes. A *pandal* is constructed in front of the bride’s father’s house during a wedding.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that the community uses plough agriculture and that Kurukh livelihoods have traditionally depended on forests, forest goods, and farms.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Traditional Kurukh tattoos consist of elaborate symmetrical patterns around the forearms, ankles, and chest. Tana Bhagats wear a Gandhi topi with a tricoloured flag, and perform puja to the Mahadeo and to the tricolour bearing a chakra symbol.

## Music & performance

The Oraon people are described as having a range of folk songs, dances, tales, and traditional musical instruments. Men and women participate in dances at social events and festivals. The principal named instruments are the *Mandar*, *Nagara*, and *Kartal*. In Kurukh, a song is called *Dandi*.

Music and dance occur in social and festival settings. Wedding performance is especially visible in the marriage account: on the wedding day, the groom arrives with his friends at the bride’s house, where they hold a dance. While the couple undergoes the marriage procedures, the rest of the party continues to dance.

## Dance & theatre

Named Kurukh dances include war dances between two *Parhas*, *Karma* dance or *Karam dandi*, *Khaddi* or *Sarhul* dance, *Phagu*, *Jadur*, *jagra*, *Matha*, *Benja Nalna*, and *Chali*. *Benja Nalna* is identified as a wedding dance, while *Chali* is identified as a courtyard dance. The dances are performed by both men and women at social events and festivals.

## Festivals & rituals

The Kurukh celebrate traditional festivals of the Chota Nagpur plateau, including *Sarhul*, *Karma*, *Dhanbuni*, *Harihari*, *Nawakhani*, and *Khariyani*. *Sarhul* is also associated with the *Khaddi* or *Sarhul* dance.

Kurukh marriage is usually arranged by the parents. The parents negotiate a bride price. On the wedding day, the groom arrives with his friends at the bride’s house, and a dance takes place. A *pandal* is built in front of the bride’s father’s house. The bride and groom stand on a stone, with grain beneath it above a plough yoke. A cloth is thrown over the couple, who are doubly screened by the groom’s friends. The groom then applies *sindoor* to the bride’s forehead, sometimes receiving it in return. Water is poured over the couple, after which they change in a separate area of the house. When they emerge, they are considered married.

The village organisation includes the *Parha*, with offices such as *Pahan*, *Panibharwa*, *Pujar*, *Bhandari*, and *Chowkidar*. These officials have roles in religious ceremonies, festivals, and dispute resolution.

## Foodways

They state that Kurukh communities traditionally depended on forests and farms, and that their agricultural system uses plough agriculture rather than *jhum*.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe the Oraon people as having folk songs, dances, and tales. They mention a documentary and folklore interest in Oraon culture, including the film-maker Ritwik Ghatak’s 1957 preparatory test film *Oraon*, concerning Adivasis of the Ranchi region in Jharkhand and the Oraons of Rani Khatanga Village in Jharkhand.

Kurukh literary activity includes writing in Devanagari, the Kurukh Banna script, and Tolong Siki. In 1991, Basudev Ram Khalkho released the Kurukh Banna script in Odisha. In 1999, Narayan Oraon invented the Tolong Siki script specifically for Kurukh; it received official recognition from the state of Jharkhand in 2007. The Kurukh Literary Society of India has helped spread Tolong Siki in Kurukh literature.

## Language & religion

Kurukh is a North Dravidian language belonging to the Dravidian language family. It is spoken by the Kurukh and Kisan people and is found in India, Bangladesh, Nepal, and Bhutan. The language is written in Devanagari, Kurukh Banna, and Tolong Siki. It has two dialects, Oraon and Kisan; the sources state that Oraon is being standardised while Kisan is endangered.

The Oraon follow Sarnaism, a traditional religion based on nature worship, and Kurukhar also believe in Animism. Religious rituals are performed under the shade of a sacred grove. The sun is worshipped as *Biri*, the moon as *Chando*, and the earth as *Dharti Aayo*, described as earth as mother. *Chando Biri* are words used in Sarna pujas, while Dharmesh is the supreme almighty god. The sources also record Hinduism, Christianity, Adi Dharam, Addi Bassi, Islam, Buddhism, Sikhism, and other religious affiliations among the Scheduled Tribes Kurukh population.
