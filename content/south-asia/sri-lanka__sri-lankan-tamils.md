## Overview

Sri Lankan Tamils are Tamils native to Sri Lanka, also known as Ceylon Tamils or Eelam Tamils. They form a majority in the Northern Province, a plurality in the Eastern Province, and a minority elsewhere; 70% of those living in Sri Lanka live in the Northern and Eastern provinces. According to the 2012 census, there were 2,270,924 Sri Lankan Tamils in Sri Lanka, representing 11.2% of the population. They are distinguished from Tamils of India by customs, traditions, dialects, and social organization. Their history is associated with the Jaffna kingdom and the Vanni chieftaincies, and evidence places Tamil-speaking populations on the island from at least around the 2nd century BCE.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Sri Lankan Tamil architecture is represented in the sources primarily by religious buildings. Pallava rule was associated with the erection and structural development of several *kovils* around the island, especially in the north-east. Pallava Dravidian rock temples remained a popular and influential architectural style in the region for several centuries. Excavations also found a limestone *Kovil* from the era of Raja Raja Chola I on Delft island, together with Chola coins.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Archaeological evidence includes megalithic burials, pottery, iron technology, and megalithic graffiti shared by Sri Lanka and Southern India during the protohistoric period. Ceramic sequences similar to those of Arikamedu were found at Kandarodai, also known as Kadiramalai, on the north coast and dated to 1300 BCE. Potsherds with early Tamil writing from the 2nd century BCE were found from Poonagari in Kilinochchi District to Tissamaharama. The sources also mention locally issued coins from Tissamaharama, produced between the 2nd century BCE and the 2nd century CE, some bearing Tamil personal names.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe Hindu Saivism, Tamil Buddhism, Jainism, and village deity worship among Tamils in the first century CE. They also state that kudi groups in eastern Sri Lanka collectively owned places of worship such as Hindu temples. Most Eastern Tamils follow customary laws called Mukkuva laws, while Northern Tamils follow customary laws called Thesavalamai; both were codified during the Dutch colonial period.

The sources record religious activity at named places. A coastal route established in the 6th century CE connected the Jaffna peninsula with Saivite religious centres in Trincomalee, including Koneswaram, and further south with Batticaloa, including Thirukkovil. A trilingual stone tablet erected at Galle in 1409 CE recorded offerings to Buddha, Allah, and Tenavarai Nayanar, the God of Tamils.

## Foodways

They state that Vanni communities practiced tank-based cultivation and that hunting and raising water buffalo and cattle accompanied agriculture. A 1890 census listed 711 irrigation tanks in the Vanni area. The sources also state that people on the east coast grew cinnamon and other spices, fished for pearls and seed pearls, and traded heavily with Kozhikode of Kerala. These references do not provide a foodways profile.

## Oral tradition & literature

Sri Lankan Tamil literature on religion and the sciences flourished during the medieval period in the court of the Jaffna Kingdom. The sources mention the *Manimekhalai*, described as a historical poem, in connection with Nāka-Tivu of Nāka-Nadu on the Jaffna Peninsula and its international pearl and conch trade. The *Mahavamsa*, also described as a historical poem, refers to Tamil rulers bringing horses to the island in watercraft and to the invasion of the island by Ellalan around 145 BCE.

The sources also mention the *Nampota*, whose present form dates to the 14th or 15th century CE, and which recognized the Tamil Kingdom, including parts of modern Trincomalee District, as a Tamil region called Demala-pattana. Robert Knox described the island’s Tamil country in *An Historical Relation of the Island Ceylon*, published in 1681 CE.

## Language & religion

Sri Lankan Tamils speak Tamil, with dialects noted for archaism and the retention of vocabulary no longer used in Southern India. Sri Lankan Tamil dialects are differentiated regionally, including the Negombo Tamil dialect, Eastern Tamil varieties, and Jaffna or Northern Tamil. Tamil-Brahmi and Tamil-Prakrit scripts were used to write Tamil on the island during the prehistoric period, and early Tamil writing appears on potsherds from the 2nd century BCE.

Sri Lankan Tamils are mostly Hindus, with a significant Christian population. In 1981, about 80% were Hindus following the Shaiva sect; most of the remainder were Roman Catholics who converted after the Portuguese conquest of the Jaffna Kingdom. There is also a small Protestant minority, and the 2012 Sri Lanka Census recorded 22,254 Buddhists among Sri Lankan Tamils. Pentecostal churches and Jehovah’s Witnesses are active among internally displaced and refugee populations.
