## Overview

The Brahuis, also spelled Brahvis, are a tribal group principally found in the central regions of Balochistan province of Pakistan, with smaller communities in Afghanistan and Iran and additional communities in Turkmenistan, Iraq, Qatar, and the United Arab Emirates. In Pakistan they inhabit a narrow belt from Bolan Pass near Quetta through the Brahvi Hills to Ras Muari on the Arabian Sea, especially the Mastung, Kalat, Nushki, and Lasbela districts. They are mainly pastoralists and have traditionally been nomads. The 2023 census of Pakistan enumerated 2.78 million Brahvi-speakers across Pakistan, while an estimate from 1996 suggested approximately 700,000 Brahvi tribesmen. Their language, Brahvi, belongs to the Dravidian family and is geographically isolated from the other Dravidian languages. Their historical importance includes the Khanate of Kalat and their distinctive position as a northern Dravidian-speaking population in Balochistan.

## Oral tradition & literature

Brahui oral historical material is described as limited and contested. Bravui lore speaks of a migration from Syria to Kalat followed by the overthrow of one Sewa dynasty, but the source characterizes this as a piecemeal borrowing from Baloch traditions. Historical ballads are described as nonexistent in the language. The sources therefore state that reconstructions of Brahvi pre-history depend on linguistics and genetics rather than a substantial indigenous historical literature.

No significant corpus of Brahvi literature exists. The earliest extant work is *Tuḥfat al-Aja īb* (“Gift of Wonders”), a translation from Persian by Malikdad Gharsin Qalati, dated approximately 1759–1760. Malikdad Gharsin Qalati is described as a court poet of Nasir Khan I. The Perso-Arabic script used for Brahvi was developed approximately 1900 through the efforts of Mulla Nabo-Jan and Maulana Fazl Mohammed Khan Darkhani, who used it to spread Islamic revivalist ideas. *Talár* is identified as the first daily newspaper in the Brahvi language and as an attempt to standardize and develop Brahvi for modern political, social, and scientific discourse.

## Language & religion

Brahvi, also spelled Brahui, is a northern Dravidian language primarily spoken by the Brahvi people of central-southern Balochistan, Pakistan. It is geographically isolated from the other Dravidian languages, with the nearest described as being over 1,500 kilometres away in South India. The language has three dialects with no significant variation: Sarawani, spoken in the north; Jhalawani, spoken in the southeast; and Chaghi, spoken in the northwest and west. Another account distinguishes Jhalawani, centered on Khuzdar, and Sarawani, centered on Kalat, by the pronunciation of *h*.

Brahvi has extensively borrowed from Balochi and other languages of the area, including Persian and Pashto. Its lexicon is described as 35% Perso-Arabic, 20% Balochi, 20% Indo-Aryan, 15% Dravidian, and 10% unknown in origin. Brahvi is primarily written in the Arabic script and is also written in the Latin script. In Pakistan, an Urdu-based Nastaliq script is used. The Roman-based orthography *Brolikva* was developed by the Brahvi Language Board of the University of Balochistan in Quetta and adopted by the newspaper *Talár*. The sources state that the Brahvi population adopted Islam.
