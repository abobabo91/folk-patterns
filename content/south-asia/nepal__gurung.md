## Overview

The Gurung, also known as Tamu, are a Tibetan ethnic group living in the hills and mountains of Gandaki Province of Nepal. At the time of the 2011 Nepal census, 798,658 people, or 2.97% of Nepal’s population, identified as Gurung. Gurung is a Sino-Tibetan language of the Tibeto-Burman family, also spoken in parts of India, Bhutan, Singapore, and Hong Kong. Gurungs have historically followed a semi-nomadic lifestyle, herding sheep and yaks in the Himalayan foothills, while many have diversified into other professions. Their folk-cultural profile includes orally transmitted history and tradition, Tamu Lhosar, Gurung religious specialists, regionally varied language, and clothing associated with mountainous conditions and locally available materials.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Handwoven wool and nettle-fibre textiles** — Historically, Gurung clothing was made from handwoven wool and plant fibres such as nettle. Cotton and other fabrics were increasingly incorporated through trade.

**Bhangra** — The *bhangra*, also called *ryenga* or *nhigi*, is a handwoven wrap traditionally made from nettle fibre. It is worn across the chest and often serves as a carrying pouch.

**Patuki** — The *patuki* is a sash tied around the waist as part of women’s dress. A related men’s garment, the *patuka*, is worn as a sash or belt.

### Clothing & dress

Women’s traditional attire includes the *cholo* or *chaubandi cholo* blouse, the *phariya* or *lungi* skirt, the *patuki* sash, the *ghalek*, worn across the body from shoulder to waist, and the *pachhyauri*, a head or shoulder cloth. Men’s attire includes the *kachhad* or *kaas*, a white wrap-around lower garment, worn with the *bhoto*, a short-sleeved shirt with front ties. Men also wear the *patuka* around the waist and the *bhangra*, *ryenga*, or *nhigi* across the chest. Caps and woollen coverings were commonly worn in colder highland areas. Traditional clothing has largely declined in everyday use in contemporary Gurung communities and is now primarily worn during festivals, rituals, and cultural events.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ornamentation is an important part of Gurung dress. Women’s adornment includes *dori* worn in the hair, *kantha* or *muga ko mala* necklaces, earrings, nose ornaments, anklets, and bangles.

## Festivals & rituals

*Tamu Lhosar* is the main festival of the Gurungs. It is celebrated every year on the 15th of *Poush*, identified in the source as December/January, to celebrate the new year.

Gurung religious practice includes Buddhist rituals performed by *Lamas* at birth, funerals, other family rituals such as *Domang* and *Tharchang*, and *Lhosar*. Lamas perform Buddhist ceremonies primarily in Lamjung, Parbat, Kaski, Manang, Mustang, and elsewhere. Gurung Dharma includes *Ghyabri*, also called *Ghyabring*, and *Pachyu*, also called *Paju*. Some Gurung villages have retained remnants of a pre-Buddhist form of the Bon religion and aspects of an older shamanic belief system that served as a counter to Bon.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that Gurung history and details related to culture and tradition are usually passed from one generation to another by word of mouth. They also report an oral tradition according to which the name Gurung derives from the Tibetan word “Gru-gu,” meaning “to bring down,” reflecting migration from the Tibetan plateau to the southern slopes of the Himalayas.

## Language & religion

Gurung, also known as *Tamu Kyi* or *Tamu Bhāṣā*, is a Sino-Tibetan language of the Tibeto-Burman family. It is commonly classified within the Tamangic subgroup and has substantial regional variation because of geographically separated hill communities. Varieties are sometimes grouped into eastern and western dialect clusters, although the boundaries are not rigid. Gurung is tonal and generally follows subject–object–verb structure. It has traditionally been transmitted orally and is now also written using Devanagari and community-developed writing systems.

Four scripts have been proposed for Gurung: a system based on the Tibetan script, Devanagari, *Khemaa lipi*, also called *Tamu Khema Phri* or *Khema Phri*, and a Romanized script. Most Gurung speakers today are bilingual in Nepali. Religious life includes Buddhist rituals, Gurung Dharma, remnants of Bon, and aspects of an older shamanic belief system.
