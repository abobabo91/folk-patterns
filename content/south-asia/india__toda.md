## Overview

The Toda are a Dravidian ethnic group living in Tamil Nadu in southern India, especially in the Nilgiri Hills. Their population has generally remained between 700 and 900, with historical Census of India figures ranging from 597 in 1931 to 879 in 1951. Toda settlements are called *mund* and consist of small thatched houses on pasture slopes where domestic buffalo are kept. Their economy has traditionally centered on cattle-herding and dairy-work, and buffalo milk is both an economic product and a central element of religious life. Toda culture has attracted sustained attention because of its distinctive appearance, customs, pastoral economy, religious practices, houses, songs and language. Toda belongs to the Dravidian language family and is described as typologically aberrant and phonologically difficult.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Toda embroidery** — Toda embroidery is a distinctive practice passed through generations. The Registrar of Geographical Indication has given it geographical-indication status, intended to ensure uniform pricing and protect the work from low-quality duplication.

**Toda art forms** — The fronts of Toda huts are decorated with rock mural painting.

### Clothing & dress

Toda dress consists of a single piece of cloth worn as a wrap over a dhoti by men and as a skirt by women, together with a shawl wrap. Funeral gatherings are described as occasions when people wear their best clothes.

### Architecture

Toda settlements are called *munds* or *mund*, and traditionally contain between three and seven small thatched houses. The huts, called *dogles*, have an oval, pent-shaped construction and a sliding door placed inside the hut. They are approximately 10 feet high, 18 feet long and 9 feet wide. Bamboo is fastened with rattan to form the frame, while thicker bamboo canes are arched into the bent shape and thinner canes are tied parallel across it. Dried grass forms the thatch, and each hut is enclosed by a wall of loose stones. The front and back are usually made from dressed stones, mostly granite. A very small entrance, about 3 feet wide and 3 feet tall, requires people to crawl inside and serves as protection from wild animals. The front is decorated with Toda rock mural painting. Each settlement also has temples called *Paluvarsh*, with parabolic or barrel-vaulted forms, and *Poovarsh*, with cylindrical walls and a long conical roof. Many Toda abandoned these huts for concrete houses in the early 21st century, while a movement developed to rebuild traditional barrel-vaulted huts. From 1995 to 2005, 40 new huts were built in this style.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

## Music & performance

Toda religious and funerary rites provide the social context for complex poetic songs about the cult of the buffalo. These songs are composed and chanted as part of the community’s religious life. They do, however, describe a religious system in which buffalo herding and dairy-work are closely connected to ritual: twice-daily milking and churning of butter, seasonal movement between pastures, burning dry pastures and giving salt to the herd are all ritualized. Holy dairies function as temples, and the milk is divided into low-grade *tarvali* and high-grade *kudrpali*.

Funerals are described as celebratory events involving festivities and dancing. People gather in their best clothes, and outsiders are typically invited to participate.

## Dance & theatre

Toda funerary rites include festivities and dancing, and the religious and funerary setting is the principal performance context described in the sources. No named Toda dance, theatrical form, mask tradition, shadow-puppet tradition or dramatic genre is identified.

## Festivals & rituals

Toda religious practice is organized around a pantheon whose principal figures are the goddess *Tökisy* and the underworld god *Ön*. Each clan has its own *nòdrochi*, a deity regarded as the clan’s ruler during the period when Todas and gods lived together. The sources state that there are between 1,600 and 1,800 gods. Every settlement has temples of two types, *Paluvarsh* and *Poovarsh*.

Ritual life is directly tied to buffalo herding and dairy-work. The dairy process is ritualized from twice-daily milking and butter-churning to seasonal pasture changes, the burning of dry pastures and the giving of salt to the herd. The milk is divided into *tarvali* and *kudrpali*, with a sacred bell identifying buffalo used to produce *kudrpali*. The sources also describe a ritual calf sacrifice held every year, associated with a story in which the god *Kwoto* tricked the gods into eating the flesh of a slain buffalo calf.

Toda funerals are described as more celebratory than western funerals. The dead are prepared with slaughtered buffalo for the afterworld, and the animals are chased and captured before slaughter so that men can demonstrate their prowess. Separate areas are dedicated to funeral ceremonies for men and women, and a hut is made where the body is prepared. Diviners work in pairs to explain misfortunes, such as the burning of a dairy, and may prescribe a ritual or prayer or identify a sorcerer believed to have caused the event.

## Foodways

Toda foodways center on buffalo milk and dairy products. The Todas are vegetarians and do not eat meat, eggs that can hatch or fish. Buffalo milk is processed into butter, buttermilk, yogurt and cheese, and is also drunk plain. Rice is a staple eaten with dairy products and curries.

Buffalo milk is divided in the holy dairy into low-grade *tarvali* and high-grade *kudrpali*. The dairy is also the workplace of the priest or milkman, who processes the buffalo’s gifts. Because dairies function as temples, dairy production has both economic and religious significance. The sources describe ritual calf sacrifice and the slaughter of buffalo in connection with religious and funerary practices, while also stating that the Todas do not eat meat.

## Oral tradition & literature

Toda religious and funerary rites provide the setting for complex poetic songs about the cult of the buffalo. These songs are composed and chanted, and Toda scholarship includes works titled *Toda Songs* and *Songs of the Toda*.

Several religious narratives are recorded. One tells how *Ön* and his wife *Pinârkûrs* went to the Kundahs in the Nilgiri hills and set up an iron bar. *Ön* pulled buffalo from the earth, while *Pinârkûrs* pulled out the animals that formed the ordinary herd. The first Toda man emerged holding the tail of the last buffalo pulled out by *Ön*, and the first Toda woman was created from one of the man’s ribs. Another story concerns *Kwoto* and the origin of ritual calf sacrifice.

## Language & religion

Toda is a Dravidian language spoken by the Toda people in the Nilgiri Hills of South India. It is associated with the Toda-Kota subgroup of South Dravidian, although Bhadriraju Krishnamurti does not consider there to be a single Toda-Kota branch. Toda has 16 vowels, an unusually large number for a Dravidian language, and an unusually large number of fricatives and trills. Its seven places of articulation are described as the most among Dravidian languages. The language is noted for its phonological difficulty and distinctive consonant system.

Toda religion includes a pantheon headed by *Tökisy* and *Ön*, clan deities called *nòdrochi*, sacred buffalo, temple-like dairies and hill deities. Mountains and hills are important because grass sustains buffalo herds and because the hills are believed to be the homes of the gods. Hills associated with gods feature stone circles called *pun*.
