## Overview

The Santal, also called Santhal, are an Austroasiatic-speaking Munda ethnic group of the Indian subcontinent. They constitute the largest tribal community in Jharkhand and West Bengal and are also present in Odisha, Bihar, Assam, and Tripura. They are the largest ethnic minority in northern Bangladesh’s Rajshahi and Rangpur divisions and have a sizeable population in Nepal. In India, their Scheduled Tribe population in five states was 6,570,807 according to the 2011 Census of India. The Santals speak Santali, the most widely spoken language of the Munda subfamily of Austroasiatic languages. Their culture includes distinctive village institutions, festivals, music, dance, puppetry, carved houses, ritual objects, and painted scrolls.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name animal, hunting, dancing, and geometric designs on house walls.

### Clothing & dress

The sources provide little information about everyday or ceremonial clothing. They state that Santal society includes *phul*, a ritual friendship with members of other ethnic groups, and mention *poeta*, people who wear a thread on the chest in worship. The catalogue records include a woman’s white-metal anklet, bronze anklets and armlets, bronze and metal necklets, and a palm-leaf ear-plug decorated with foil and red seeds. These records document objects associated with the catalogue’s Santal or “Sonthals” identifications.

### Architecture

Traditional Santal homes have walls ornamented with carved designs of animals, hunting scenes, dancing scenes, and geometric patterns. Santal bridal palanquins are also finely carved. A characteristic Santal village contains a sacred grove called *Jaher* on its edge, where spirits live and annual festivals take place. The grove is left undisturbed except during festivals and contains natural, uncut stones representing the *bongas*. The *Majhi Than* is a raised mound of earth covered with a thatched roof outside the headman’s house; it is associated with the spirits of the headman’s ancestors. Local affairs are handled by a village council led by a *manjhi*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources describe Santal agricultural tools and household objects only in limited contexts. During the British period, Santals took loans to buy iron tools, seed grain, and oxen. The British Museum catalogue includes a battle-axe with a dark wood handle and iron head, an arrow with a pale wood head and bamboo shaft, and a model wooden oil-press made from horizontal and vertical wooden pieces. The catalogue also records a painted *pata* scroll on paper showing a tiger’s god, a shrine, dancers, warriors, and musicians.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Museum catalogue records document several forms of Santal-associated body adornment. These include a woman’s anklet made of white metal, bronze anklets with interlocking sections and incised geometric or floral patterns, and a pair of bronze armlets with ridges, punched patterns, and square rosettes. The records also describe a white-metal or zinc necklet with incised floral and banded decoration, a broad bronze necklet with geometric patterns, and a palm-leaf ear-plug rolled into a cylinder and covered with gold and green foil and small red seeds.

## Music & performance

The Santal people are described as loving music and dance. Traditional music and dance have been influenced by mainstream Indian and Western culture, but traditional forms remain in use. Santal music differs from Hindustani classical music. The Santal traditionally accompany many dances with two drums, the *Tamak’* and the *Tumdak’*. The *Tumdak’* is a hand-struck, two-headed drum with a clay body and leather straps. The *Tamak’* is a single-headed kettle drum struck with two sticks; its head is usually 14–16 inches in diameter and it is described as having special spiritual meaning and power.

Other instruments named in the sources include *Sarpa*, *Buan flute*, *Sarenga*, *Banam*, *lerda*, *jhal*, *Tiriwaw*, *Tirio*, *Harmonium*, *Dhodro banam*, *Phet banam*, and *kabkubi*. The *Tirio* is made from bamboo and has seven holes. The *Dhodro banam* is a one-stringed bowed lute often carved with anthropomorphic heads, while the *Phet banam* has three or four strings. The *kabkubi* is a one-stringed plucked instrument used by Christian Santal musicians as a substitute for the *Tamak’*.

Traditional tunes are heard especially during Santal festivals. Most songs and tunes are associated with particular festivals and the dances accompanying them. Their meters and rhythms reflect those dances. Santal melodies usually remain within an octave and often use scales of five or six pitches.

## Dance & theatre

Santal dances are traditionally accompanied by the *Tamak’* and *Tumdak’*. The sources state that many tunes are directly connected with festival dances, so their meters and rhythms reflect the movements and structures of those dances.

*Chadar Badar*, also known as Santal puppetry, is a folk show involving wooden puppets placed in a small cage that functions as the stage. The museum catalogue records also include *pata* scroll paintings showing dancers and musicians, including a devotional scene with a “tiger’s god,” a shrine, and dancers, and another scene with worshippers, warriors fighting, and musicians.

## Festivals & rituals

*Sohrai* is the principal festival of the Santal community. Other important festivals named in the sources are *Baha*, *Karam*, *Dashain*, *Sakrat*, *Mahmore*, *Rundo*, and *Magsim*. The sources do not provide dates for these festivals, but state that Santal dances are traditionally accompanied by the *Tamak’* and *Tumdak’* during festival occasions. Tunes used for the *Sohrae* harvest festival are referred to by that name, and festival tunes are generally connected with the dances they accompany.

Santal villages contain the sacred grove called *Jaher*, where spirits live and annual festivals take place. The grove is established when the village is founded and is left undisturbed except during festivals. Natural stones inside it represent the *bongas*. The *Majhi Than*, outside the headman’s house, is associated with the spirits of the headman’s ancestors; during summer, a jug of water is placed there so the spirits can drink.

Marriage is treated as important for the entire village as well as for the couple. The sources name seven recognised kinds of marriage. The most elaborate is *hapramko bapla*, or ancestor’s marriage, while the most widely practised is *kesimek’*. In *kesimek’*, the couple’s intentions are investigated by the *jog majhi*, the bride and groom are consulted, and the families negotiate a bride price. A symbolic marriage contract involves cash and *handi*, or rice-beer. The bride later returns to her natal village carrying a pot with white clay and greets her mother. The sources do not describe birth or funeral rites in detail, although they mention funerals among the major life-cycle events attended by ritual friends.

## Foodways

The sources state that *handi* is rice-beer and that it is given to guests during the *kesimek’* marriage negotiations. Guests also share *pera hor handi* when the bride returns to her natal village. The historical account says that Santal settlements were turned into paddy fields.

## Oral tradition & literature

Santal folklore claims that the people came from *Hihiri*, which scholars have identified as *Ahuri* in Hazaribagh district. The tradition recounts movement to the Chhota Nagpur Plateau, then to *Jhalda*, *Patkum*, and finally *Saont*. Santal religious tradition includes a creation story in which *Marang Buru*, *Malan Budhi*, *Pilchu Haram*, and *Pilchu Budhi* appear, and the children of seven couples become the progenitors of Santal clans.

The sources also identify published collections and studies of Santal oral literature, including P. O. Bodding’s *Santal Folk Tales* and *Santal Riddles and Witchcraft among the Santals*, Cecil Henry Bompas and P. O. Bodding’s *Folklore of the Santal Parganas*, and Indu Roy Chaudhury’s *Folk Tales of the Santals*. W. G. Archer collected and analysed hundreds of Santal songs in the mid-twentieth century.

## Language & religion

Santali is an Austroasiatic language of the Munda subfamily. The Santal are an Austroasiatic-speaking Munda ethnic group. The sources describe Santal religious life as centred on *bonga*, spirits associated with the village, household, ancestors, and sub-clans. Benevolent and harmful spirits are distinguished, and the *bonga* are described as intermediaries between *noa puri*, the visible world, and *hana puri*, the invisible reality. The creator is called *Marang Buru* or *Thakur Jiu*.

The sources report that, among the Scheduled Tribe population counted in 2011, 63.15% were reported as Hindu, 23% as Sarna, 7.28% as *Sari Dharam*, and 5.46% as Christian. They also describe *Sarna Dharam* as an umbrella term for tribal religions and *Sari Dharam* as a religion based on truths. In the doctrine of *Sari Dharam*, *Um-hor* are followers of Christianity, *Saka-hor* followers of Hinduism, and *Bonga-hor* followers of *Sari Dharam*.
