## Overview

The Chitrali, also known as the Kho, are an Indo-Aryan ethnic group native to northern Pakistan. Their main homeland is the Chitral region of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, with communities also in the Ghizer region of Gilgit-Baltistan. Khowar is their principal language and belongs to the Dardic family. Khowar is spoken by around 800,000 people in Pakistan and is also used in the Gupis-Yasin and Ghizer districts of Gilgit-Baltistan and in the Upper Swat district. The Kho expanded through Chitral from the Mulkhow and Torkhow valleys, later moving into southern Chitral, Yasin, Ghizer and the Kalasha valleys. Their documented folk culture includes regional foodways, Chitrali clothing, polo, a spring festival, folklore concerning supernatural beings, and a distinctive language and writing tradition.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Traditional Chitrali clothing includes the *shalwar kameez* and the *pakol*, described as a Chitrali hat. The sources identify these garments as part of traditional Chitrali clothing but do not distinguish everyday and ceremonial forms.

## Music & performance

They do, however, identify polo as a popular sport and pastime among the Kho. Polo traditionally played by the Kho has no strict rules or organisation. The Shandur Polo Festival is held annually during summer at the Shandur Polo Ground, described as the world’s highest polo ground, at an altitude of 3,700 meters. The Shandur Pass itself is at 3,800 meters. The tournament is played under freestyle rules between teams from Gilgit-Baltistan and Chitral.

## Festivals & rituals

The Kho celebrate a spring festival known as *Jashan-e-Qaqlasht* at the Qaqlasht Meadows in Upper Chitral. The sources describe it as a spring festival. The Shandur Polo Festival is held annually during summer at the Shandur Polo Ground, where teams from Gilgit-Baltistan and Chitral play a polo tournament under freestyle rules.

The sources state that the Kho historically practised a faith akin to that observed by the Kalash people today. In the 14th century, many Kho converted to Islam, although some previous customs continued to persist.

## Foodways

Chitrali cuisine is the cuisine of the Kho and is based on wheat flour, vegetables, milk and meat. It has regional variation and resembles the cuisine of neighbouring Gilgit-Baltistan. Salty buttered tea is popular in Chitral, as are green tea and a very bitter black tea called *troq chai*. Honey, cheese and fresh fruits are staples of the Chitrali diet. Lamb and chicken *karahi* reflect the influence of Punjab, while Uzbek dumplings called *mantu* are also popular. Pilaf, usually made with beef that is preboiled until extremely tender, is described as an essential dish at large gatherings; the meat is served on top of the rice. Dried apricots, called *chambor*, and dried mulberries, called *kitori*, are important parts of the local diet and are exported to other parts of Pakistan, Afghanistan and Arab states of the Persian Gulf.

Breads include *paratha*, an unleavened whole-wheat flatbread fried on a *tawa* with butter or cooking oil; *tikki*, a thick leavened loaf baked over coals in a special mold; *khesta*, made from fermented liquid batter and cooked on a griddle; *phulka*, described as the standard South Asian chapatti; and *rishiki*, thin pancakes or crepes made from whole wheat flour, water and eggs. Sweet *rishiki* is served with honey and cottage cheese.

Dry meat dishes include *rondijhzu*, barbecued or spit-roasted goat or lamb seasoned with salt, and *taaw kahak*, chicken roasted on a griddle under additional pressure. *Taaw machhi* uses the same pressure-grilling method for fish, especially brown trout, rainbow trout and schizothorax. Soups are especially important and are usually eaten as the main dish with bread and fruit. They include *kalli*, a minced-beef noodle soup; *leganu*, made with tiny lentil-flour dumplings; *lajhaik*, a thick soup of whole wheat and barley grains with lamb, beef or duck; *khhamalogh*, made from crushed sheep, goat or cattle skulls; and *kawirogh*, a light soup of dried wild capers and meat.

Other named dishes are *ghalmandi*, layered flat breads filled with cottage cheese, coriander and chives and covered with melted butter and walnut oil; *cheer aa shapik*, a similar dish with white sauce; *qalaibat*, whole wheat flour cooked with lamb fat; *shroshrp*, an unsweetened halwa made from germinated wheat grain flour; and *pushur tikki*, a meat-filled loaf. Variations include *phhenak tikki*, filled with cottage cheese, *pandir tikki*, filled with matured cheese, and the spinach-filled vegetarian *shaakh mujhzi*.

## Oral tradition & literature

Chitrali folklore places great emphasis on supernatural beings. The region is sometimes called *Peristan* because of a common belief in fairies, or *peri*, inhabiting the high mountains. *Barmanou* is described as a popular local legend and as the indigenous equivalent of the Yeti of the Himalayas.

## Language & religion

Khowar, also known by the exonym Chitrali, is an Indo-Aryan language of the Dardic group. It is primarily spoken by the Kho and functions as the lingua franca of Chitral. It is also spoken in Gupis-Yasin, Ghizer and Upper Swat, and significant Khowar-speaking populations live in Islamabad, Karachi, Lahore and Peshawar. The Kalash people also speak it as a second language.

The native name is *Khō-wār*, meaning “language of the Kho people.” Other names recorded in the sources include Chitrālī, Qāshqārī, Kashkār, and Arnyiá or Arniya. Khowar is typically written in the calligraphic Nastaʿlīq script, using an orthography derived from the Urdu alphabet. Six new letters were added to the 37-letter Urdu alphabet, producing a 43-letter Khowar script. Khowar nouns have four case forms, and the language has an animacy-based noun classification system.

The Kho are primarily Hanafi Sunni Muslims, with a substantial population of Isma'ili Muslims in upper Chitral. The sources also describe an ancient faith akin to that of the Kalash and the persistence of some earlier customs after conversion to Islam.
