## Overview

The Rajasthani people, or Rajasthanis, are Indo-Aryan peoples native to Rajasthan, a state in Northern India. Rajasthan includes arid desert districts and greener eastern areas; the sources specifically mention the Marwar, Mewar, Shekhawati, Hadoti, Malwa, and Thar Desert regions, as well as cities including Jaipur, Jodhpur, Udaipur, Bikaner, Jaisalmer, and Alwar. Standard Rajasthani or Standard Marwari is spoken by over 25 million people in different parts of Rajasthan according to the 2011 figure given in the sources, while Marwari is described as the most spoken Rajasthani language, with approximately 8 million speakers. Rajasthani languages belong to the Western Indo-Aryan language family. Their folk culture includes hereditary musician communities, epic ballads, scroll painting, puppetry, regional cuisines, dance traditions, textiles, jewellery, forts, palaces, and temple architecture.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Maru-Gurjar painting* is a Rajasthani painting tradition that developed under royal patronage and reflects the royal heritage of ancient Rajasthan. Its major forms include phad paintings, miniature paintings, kajali paintings, and gemstone paintings.

**Phad paintings** are colourful scroll paintings made on cloth. They illustrate performances by Bhopa singer-reciters and are associated with vibrant colours and historical themes. The Phad of God Devnarayan is described as the largest among the popular Pars in Rajasthan, measuring 170 square feet, while Pabuji Ki Phad is painted on a 15 x 5 ft. canvas.

**Rajasthani miniature painting** developed in princely courts from the late 16th Century, combining indigenous influences with Persian, Mughal, Chinese, and European influences. The principal schools are Mewar, Marwar, Hadoti, and Dhundar.

**Mewar painting** includes the Chavand, Nathdwara, Devgarh, Udaipur, and Sawar styles.

**Marwar painting** includes the Kishangarh, Bikaner, Jodhpur, Nagaur, Pali, and Ghanerao styles.

**Hadoti painting** includes the Kota, Bundi, and Jhalawar styles.

**Dhundar painting** includes the Amber, Jaipur, Shekhawati, and Uniara styles.

**Block printing and tie-dye** are named among Rajasthan’s textile traditions. The sources specifically identify block prints, tie-dye prints, Bagru prints, Sanganer prints, and Zari embroidery.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no specific recurring textile motifs beyond the historical themes and figures represented in Phad paintings, including God Devnarayan, Pabuji, Gogaji, Prithviraj Chauhan, and Amar Singh Rathore.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional men’s clothing includes earrings, moustaches, dhotis, kurta, angarkha, and paggar or safa. The *safa* is a headgear resembling a turban, and its wearing style and colour vary according to caste and age. A traditional chudidar payjama frequently replaces the dhoti in different regions. Mewar is associated with the tradition of the paggar, while Marwar is associated with the safa. The sources state that women dress according to caste culture. *Poshak* is traditionally worn by Rajput, Rajpurohit, and Charan women. Dress styles vary across Rajasthan, and the sources distinguish regional ways of wearing the dhoti in Marwar, Shekhawati, and Hadoti.

### Architecture

Rajasthan is noted for forts, temples, palaces, and havelis. The sources describe Māru-Gurjara architecture, also called the Solaṅkī style, as a distinctive style that began in Rajasthan and neighbouring Gujarat around the 11th century. It treats architecture sculpturally and is especially associated with Hindu and Jain temple architecture. The Dilwara Jain Temples of Mount Abu, built between the 11th and 13th centuries CE, are identified as major examples. The style features large amounts of sculpture, especially numerous small, sharply carved figures and friezes with repeated animals, sometimes carrying human riders. Rajasthani architecture also includes jharokha enclosed balconies and chhatri open pavilions. Named buildings include Amer Fort, Jaisalmer Fort, Mehrangarh Fort, Chittor Fort, the City Palaces of Jaipur and Udaipur, and numerous Jain and Hindu temples.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources identify Jaipur blue pottery as particularly noted for its colour. Rajasthan is also described as having traditions of furniture with intricate carvings and bright colours, along with handicrafts made from marble, wood, leather, semi-precious stones, and dimensional stones. Agriculture-related tools are made by communities including Lohar and Sikligar; Lohar are blacksmiths, while Sikligar traditionally made and polished arms used in war and now create agricultural tools. Wealthy Rajasthanis historically used silver and gold ornaments in interior decoration, including on curtains, seat cushions, and handicrafts.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Rajasthani people have traditionally worn jewellery made from various metals and materials. Women historically wore gem-studded gold and silver ornaments. Wealthy Rajasthanis also used gem-studded gold and silver on swords, shields, knives, pistols, cannon, doors, and thrones. Men are described as traditionally wearing earrings.

## Music & performance

Rajasthani music comes from a region associated with important centres of Indian musical development, including Udaipur, Jodhpur, and Jaipur. The sources identify diverse musician castes, including Langas, Sapera, Bhopa, and Manganiar. Langas traditionally performed mostly for Muslim audiences and styles, while Manganiars had a more liberal approach. Manganiars and Langas are also described as prominent communities known for their musical traditions.

Traditional music includes women’s Panihari songs, which describe chores, especially those centred on water and wells. Songs performed by various castes commonly begin with an *alap*, followed by a recital of a couplet called a *dooba*. Epic ballads tell stories of Devnarayan Bhagwan, Gogaji, Ramdeoji, Pabuji, and Tejaji. Folk songs also mark seasonal change, the coming of the Monsoon, and the harvest season, while other songs concern daily activities such as sowing Jeera or references to Podina.

Named instruments include the sarangi, dholak, sitar, harmonium, and algoza. The sarangi is a bowed string instrument used to accompany vocal performance; the dholak is a two-headed drum; and the algoza is a double-flute instrument played by blowing into both flutes simultaneously. Jaipur-Atrauli Gharana is associated with Alladiya Khan, who was trained in Dhrupad and Khyal styles. Allah Jilai Bai is identified as a famous Rajasthani Maand singer of the Bikaner Gharana.

## Dance & theatre

Ghoomar is a traditional dance performed by women and is described as originating in the Marwar region. It involves graceful movements and twirling in colourful ghagras. The sources also identify Kalbeliya, performed by the Kalbeliya community, as an energetic and acrobatic dance that has gained international recognition. Bhavai involves balancing multiple pots on the head while performing intricate steps. Chari involves women balancing brass pots with lit lamps on their heads.

Kathputli is a traditional string-puppet performance native to Rajasthan. It is performed at village fairs, religious festivals, and social gatherings, and appears in Rajasthani folk tales, ballads, and folk songs. The sources state that the art is associated with the Bhat community and with narratives from Indian epics, folklore, and historical events. Kings and nobles of Rajasthan encouraged the art, while performances glorified the achievements and ancestry of patrons.

## Festivals & rituals

The main religious festivals named in the sources are Deepawali, Holi, Gangaur, Rakshabandan, Teej, Goga Navami, Makar Sankranti, Ganesh Chaturthi, and Janmashtami. Rajasthan’s desert festival in Jaisalmer is celebrated once a year during winter. It includes dancing, singing ballads of valour, romance, and tragedy, fairs with snake charmers, puppeteers, acrobats, and folk performers, and a prominent role for camels.

The sources connect several foods and offerings with religious festivals. Ghevar is offered as *Naivedhya* to Shiva and Parvati during Teej. Suji Laapsi and Puri form *Naivedhya* for Durga Ashtami, while Moong Dal Laapsi is common during Diwali. Panjeeri is prepared on Janamashtami as *Naivedhya* for Krishna’s mothers Devaki and Yasodha. Varieties of Laadu are offered to Ganesh, and Besan Chakki is offered as *Naivedya* in Hanuman temples. Gujhiya is associated with Holi, Sutra pheni with Karwa Chauth and the months Kartik and Margashirsha, and Gud Gatta with Makar Sankranti.

## Foodways

Rajasthani cuisine developed in an arid region where water and fresh green vegetables were scarce. Food that could last several days and be eaten without heating was preferred. Wheat and millets form the staple diet, while lentils, beans, gram flour, and dairy products are widely used. The cuisine includes dal baati churma, panchratna dal, papad ro saag, ker sangri, gatte ro saag, bikaneri bhujia, mirchi bada, kanda kachauri, malaidar special lassi, Lashun ki chutney, Mawa lassi from Jodhpur, Alwar ka mawa, Malpauas from Pushkar, and rasgulla from Bikaner.

Dal Baati Churma combines lentils, Baati bread, and Churma. Ker Sangri uses dried Ker berries and Sangri beans. Rajasthani Panchratna Dal combines toor dal, urad dal, moong dal, chana dal, and moth dal. Other named foods include Gatte ro saag, Papad ro saag, Bajra Khichdi, Raab, Ghaat, Khoba Roti, Missi Roti, Baatis, Ghevar, Laapsi, Panjeeri, Gujhiya, and Churma.

Cooking uses barbecue, grilling, baking, and clay ovens. Household grills are called *Sigdi* or *Angithi*, while clay ovens are called *bhatthi* or *tandoor*. The sources state that Hindu and Jain Rajasthanis are predominantly vegetarian, while Rajputs traditionally prefer a largely non-vegetarian diet. Beef is described as taboo within the majority of the culture.

## Oral tradition & literature

Rajasthani folk literature includes ballads, songs, proverbs, folk tales, and panegyrics. Epic ballads tell of Devnarayan Bhagwan, Gogaji, Ramdeoji, Pabuji, and Tejaji. Phad paintings illustrate narrative performances by Bhopa singer-reciters, while Kathputli performances present stories from Indian epics, folklore, and historical events.

The first mention of Rajasthani literature is associated with the 778 CE novel Kuvalayamala, composed in Jalor by Jain acharya Udyotana Suri. Udyotan Suri referred to the language as Maru Bhasha or Maru Vani. Modern Rajasthani literature is associated with Suryamal Misrana, whose important works include Vamsa Bhaskara and Vira satsaī. The Vira satsaī is a collection of couplets about historical heroes. Other named poets include Bakhtavara Ji, Kaviraja Murari Dan, Maharaja Chatur Singh, and Hinglaj Dan Kaviya.

Komal Kothari researched and recorded local practices of Western Rajasthan musicians for roughly 25 years. A collection of nearly 1200 recordings of ballads, oral epics, and storytelling by hereditary musician castes was digitized through the Modern Endangered Archives Program.

## Language & religion

Rajasthani languages are a group of Western Indo-Aryan languages spoken primarily in Rajasthan and also in parts of Malwa, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Haryana, Punjab, Sindh, and Nepal. Standard Rajasthani or Standard Marwari is described as a common lingua franca, while Marwari is the most spoken Rajasthani language. Other named varieties include Malvi, Mewari, Dhundhari, Harauti, Nimadi, and Bagri. Rajasthani is written in the Devanagari script in India; the Mahajani script, or Modiya, was used earlier, and a variant of the Sindhi script is used in Pakistan.

Rajasthani society is predominantly Hindu, with sizeable Muslim, Sikh, and Jain minorities. Shaivism and Vaishnavism are followed by a majority, while Shaktism is represented through Bhavani and her avatars. Popular deities include Surya, Krishna, and Rama. Rajasthan also has traditions of religious syncretism: Baba Ramdevji was adored by Muslims and Hindus, while Dadu Dayal preached unity between Ram and Allah.
