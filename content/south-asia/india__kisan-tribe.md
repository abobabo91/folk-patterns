## Overview

The Kisan are a tribal group found in the Indian states of Odisha, West Bengal and Jharkhand. They are traditional farmers and a food-gathering people. The tribe mainly lives in northwestern Odisha, especially in the districts of Sundergarh, Jharsuguda and Sambalpur. Other populations live in Malda district in western West Bengal and in Latehar and Gumla districts of western Jharkhand. A small number also live in Nepal’s Jhapa district, where the population is around 1,000. According to the 2011 Census of India, the largest registered population was in Odisha. The Kisan speak Kisan, also known as *kunha*, as well as Odia and Sambalpuri; Kisan is generally understood as a dialect of Kurukh, within the North Dravidian group of the Dravidian languages.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources document ceremonial clothing in connection with marriage. During the marriage ceremony, the groom, bride and their parents wear red, yellow and white clothing; black is not worn.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

In the marriage ceremony, rice is cooked in a new pot, but the sources provide no further information about pottery or its manufacture.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The marriage account states that the groom applies vermillion to the bride’s forehead, but gives no further description of the material or its wider use.

## Music & performance

The sources mention music in relation to marriage celebrations. After the marriage ceremony, celebration, dancing and feasting continue throughout the night. In earlier practice, post-ceremony dances and celebrations were accompanied by *mandar* drums; in more recent practice, loudspeakers are used.

## Dance & theatre

The marriage ceremony includes a mock fight staged by the bride’s and groom’s relatives on the outskirts of the village. After the ceremony, there is dancing and feasting throughout the night.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that marriage can take place only after the harvest, when there is little work in the fields.

Kisan marriage involves several recognized forms, including marriage by negotiation, marriage by capture, love marriage, marriage by intrusion, marriage by adoption and marriage by exchange. Negotiated marriage is the most common and is called *benja*. An *agua* mediates between the two families, and the village headman is consulted. The groom’s family visits the bride’s house with rice, *handia* and animals. The families later settle the bride price, called *kania muli hessu* or *sukha mula*, which is paid in rice. The date of marriage is fixed with the consent of the *kalo*, the village priest.

On the marriage day, the groom’s *barat*, or wedding procession, arrives with *handia*. The bride is escorted to the groom’s village. The relatives stage a mock fight, after which the bride is welcomed into the groom’s house. In the evening, the couple are led to a *pandal*. The *kalo* worships the *Dharme Belas*, described as supreme gods. The couple’s clothes are tied together, and they walk around the *pandal* 7 times while invoking the *Dharme Belas*. Divorce and remarriage are permitted under the conditions described in the source.

## Foodways

The Kisan are described as traditional farmers and a food-gathering people. Rice is important in the marriage process: the groom’s family brings rice to the bride’s house, the bride price may be paid in rice, and rice is cooked in a new pot during the ceremony.

*Handia*, identified as rice-beer, is served during marriage negotiations and appears in the wedding procession. In more recent practice, members of the *barat* are served *mahua* liquor instead of *handia*, along with meat and rice, at the bride’s house.

## Language & religion

The Kisan speak Kisan, also known as *kunha*. The language is sometimes treated as an independent member of the North Dravidian group of the Dravidian languages, although the scholarly opinion presented in the source is that it is a dialect of Kurukh. Kisan speakers also use Odia and Sambalpuri.

The marriage ceremony includes worship of the *Dharme Belas*, described as supreme gods. The ceremony is conducted by the *kalo*, or village priest, who fixes the marriage date with the families’ consent and performs the worship during the wedding.
