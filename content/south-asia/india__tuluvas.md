## Overview

The Tulu people, or Tuluvas, are a Dravidian ethnic group native to Tulu Nadu in south-western India. Tulu Nadu comprises Dakshina Kannada and Udupi districts in Karnataka, part of Kasaragod district in Kerala, and includes the cities of Mangalore, Udupi and Kasaragod as centres of Tulu culture. Mangalore is described as the commercial hub. The 2011 Indian census reported 1,846,427 native Tulu speakers living in India. Tulu is a southern Dravidian language with a substantial oral literature, including epic poems, ritual songs and narratives. Tuluva culture is especially represented by Yakshagana, Bhuta Kola, Nagaradhane, Kambala and Tuluva Paddanas. The group also maintains a distinctive matrilineal inheritance system known as *Aliyasantana*.

## Music & performance

Tulu oral culture includes *Paddanas*, stylised oral epic poems sung during the Hindu rituals of Bhuta Kola and Nagaradhane. They are largely legends about gods or historical personalities. The longest named example is *Siri Paddana*, concerning a woman called Siri who demonstrates strength and integrity during adverse times and attains divinity. The sources state that the Paddana depicts the independent nature of Tulu womenfolk and that the entire Paddana was written down by Finnish scholar Lauri Honko of the University of Turku.

Other forms of Tulu oral tradition include riddles, bhajans and *Kabitol*. Riddles often concern kinship and agriculture. Bhajans are sung in temples across the Tulu region and are dedicated to gods and goddesses; they are performed in the Carnatic style and in a style similar to that used in Yakshagana. *Kabitol* consists of songs sung during the cultivation of crops, with *O Bele* identified as a notable example.

The sources also describe Tuluva Paddanas as sung narratives forming part of closely related singing traditions in the Tulu language. They are performed on occasions describing the evolution of Tulu tribes and Tulu culture. No musical instruments are identified in the sources.

## Dance & theatre

The principal named theatrical tradition is *Yakshagana*, prevalent in coastal Karnataka and northern Kerala. Tulu-language Yakshagana is popular among the Tuluva people and can function as temple art. The sources identify groups attached to the Kateel Durga Parameshwari Temple and the Udupi Krishna Temple. Eight professional Yakshagana troupes perform in Tulu during the Yakshagana season and outside it, in Karnataka and elsewhere. More than 2,000 Yakshagana artistes take part in performances in Mumbai annually.

Tulu plays are described as major entertainment for admirers of art and culture in Tulu Nadu. They are generally centred on the comic genre and are also popular in Mumbai and Bangalore.

## Festivals & rituals

Tulu culture includes the annual ceremony of *kola* or *nema*, which celebrates the festival of bhutas. Bhuta-aradhana, meaning “spirit worship” or “soul worship,” is associated with supernatural or spiritual entities, especially ancestors. Bhutas may be local deities, representatives of characters from the Puranas, deified human beings or strictly local characters. Named examples include Panjurli, Pili-bhuta, Bermer, Lekkesiri, Vishnumurti, Gulige, Annappe, Koti-Chananye, Male-Chandi, Ullaldi, Malaraye, Marlu-Jumadi, Potte, Posa-bhuta, Vokku-Ballala and Muttappe.

*Nagaradhane* is another ritual tradition associated with the Tulu people. The sources also identify *Aati kalenja* and *Kambala* among distinctive features of Tuluva culture.

The Tuluva New Year is called *Bisu Parba*. It falls on the same day as Baisakhi, Vishu and the Thai New Year.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tulu oral traditions include *Paddanas*, riddles, bhajans and *Kabitol*. The Paddanas are oral epic poems sung in a highly stylised manner during Bhuta Kola and Nagaradhane. They commonly concern gods or historical personalities. *Siri Paddana* tells of Siri, who shows strength and integrity during adverse times and attains divinity. The sources also identify the legend of Koti and Chennayya and the *Epic of Siri* as forms of Tulu oral literature.

Written Tulu literature includes the Tulu translation of the Sanskrit epic *Mahabharata*, called *Mahabharato*, written by Arunabja. Other named works are *Devi Mahatmyam*, *Sri Bhagavata* and *Kaveri*. Modern Tulu literature is written using the Kannada script. *Mandara Ramayana*, written by Mandara Keshava Bhatt, is identified as a notable modern work. Tulu periodicals named in the sources include *Madipu*, *Mogaveera*, *Saphala* and *Samparka*. The Tulu Sahitya Academy was established by the state government of Karnataka in 1994, and the Kerala Tulu Academy was established in Manjeshwaram in 2007.

## Language & religion

Tulu is a Dravidian language belonging to the southern branch of the Dravidian language family. Its speakers are concentrated in Dakshina Kannada, southern Udupi and northern Kasaragod. The language has four named dialects: Common Tulu, Brahmin Tulu, Jain dialect and Adivasi dialect. Closely related Tuluoid languages include Koraga, Kudiya and Bellari.

Historical inscriptions of Tulu are found in the Tigalari script. The oldest available inscriptions are dated to the 7th and 8th century AD. The Tulu script is descended from Brahmi through the Grantha script and is a sister script of Malayalam. The Kannada script gradually became the contemporary script for Tulu, and the Tulu characterset was approved by the Central Institute of Indian Languages in 2021.

Tulu religious life includes Bhuta Kola, Nagaradhane, temple bhajans and Yakshagana connected with temples. Bhuta worship may involve non-Brahmin worshippers, while in many villages Brahmins conduct annual ceremonies for spirits regarded as protectors. Tuluvas follow a matrilineal inheritance system called *Aliyasantana*, in which inheritance passes from uncle to nephew, with exceptions identified in the sources.
