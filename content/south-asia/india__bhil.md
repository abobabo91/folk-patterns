## Overview

The Bhil are the largest and most widely scattered indigenous group in India, spread across western and central regions. They speak Bhili, an Indo-Aryan language with many dialects, and their culture centers on hunting, farming, and strong ties to forest resources. Their name comes from their historical skill with the bow and arrow, which shaped their material culture and identity.

## Material culture

Bhil material culture reflects their forest life and hunting heritage, combining practical items made from local materials—wood, bamboo, clay, and animal products—with distinctive painted and dotted patterns.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Pithora painting is the named pattern tradition, characterized by colorful dot in-filling.

- **Pithora painting** (*Pithora*) — A painting tradition of the Bhilala people known for detailed dotwork.
- **Bhil dotted painting** — Bhil painting fills designs with multi-colored dots; artists like Bhuri Bai moved this style to modern paper and ready-made colors.

### Clothing & dress

Men wear pagri, angarkha, dhoti and gamchha; women wear sari and ghagra choli in everyday dress.

- **Pagri** — A man's head-dress worn as part of traditional Bhil dress.
- **Angarkha** — A man's garment in traditional Bhil dress.
- **Dhoti** — A man's loin-cloth, sometimes embroidered, worn in traditional dress.
- **Ghagra choli** — A woman's traditional dress consisting of a skirt and blouse.
- **Women's tattooing** — Tattooing on women before marriage is a traditional body-marking custom.

### Architecture

The mandir, a small clay god's house, represents built forms; villages are led by a headman called the gameti.

- **Mandir** — A small terracotta god's house kept at household shrines, with a round belly, flaring stand, pointed top and triangular opening.
- **Gramdev** — The local village deity worshipped by every Bhil community.
- **Household deities** (*Jatidev, Kuldev, Kuldevi*) — Family gods symbolized by stones and kept as shrines in Bhil homes.
- **Village headman** (*gameti*) — The traditional leader of a Bhil village who holds authority and settles disputes.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Terracotta dominates, mostly votive horses and elephants for shrine offerings; weapons and household items are made from wood, bamboo and metal.

- **Votive horse figures** — Terracotta horses sometimes showing a human head rising from the horse's back, made for shrine offerings.
- **Votive elephants with howdah** (*howdah*) — Clay figures of elephants carrying a howdah (a seat or carriage), used as shrine offerings.
- **Bows** — Split-bamboo bows made from local materials, strung with bamboo or rattan, used for hunting and defense.
- **Arrows** — Reed-shafted composite arrows with iron heads, leather or fiber bindings, and feather flights, often painted red or black.
- **Buffalo-horn daggers** — Curved blades from the 18th–19th century used for hunting and defense.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Men wear kada, bajuband, chains and earrings; women wear hansli, zele-zumke, narniyan and nathni in base or white metal.

- **Kada** — A metal bracelet or arm ornament worn by Bhil men.
- **Bajuband** — An arm ornament worn by Bhil men.
- **Hansli** — A metal ring worn by Bhil women.
- **Narniyan** — A bangle worn by Bhil women.
- **Nathni** — A nose jewel worn by Bhil women as body adornment.

## Music & performance

The Bhil play many instruments—sarangi, bansuri, tabla, and others—usually made from local materials, with folk song and dance as chief recreation.

- **Reneti** — A musical bow made from a coconut shell drum with skin, a wooden bridge, bamboo neck and tuning pegs.
- **Sarangi** — A stringed instrument played in Bhil music.
- **Bansuri** — A bamboo flute used in Bhil folk music.
- **Hand-held drum** — A drum made by stretching leather over a water pot and lashing it with vegetable-fiber string.
- **Bhil song-poem tradition** — A substantial oral tradition of folk songs and poems collected and published by Randhir Khare.

## Dance & theatre

Ghoomar is the traditional women's folk dance and symbol of womanhood; other named dances include Lathi, Dhol, and hunting dances.

- **Ghoomar** — The traditional Bhil folk dance performed by young girls and women, marking the transition to womanhood.
- **Lathi dance** (*Lathi*) — A traditional Bhil staff dance.
- **Dhol dance** (*Dhol*) — A traditional Bhil dance accompanied by drum.
- **Bhagoria dance** (*Bhagoria*) — A Bhil dance performed during the Holi season.
- **Hunting dance** — A traditional Bhil dance related to hunting practices.

## Festivals & rituals

The Bhil observe Hindu festivals alongside their own calendar including Akhatij, Bhagoria, and Gavari, with ritual life centered on village and household deities and a wide pantheon of gods.

- **Bhagoria** — A Bhil festival and fair held during Holi with dancing and community gathering.
- **Gavari festival** (*Gavari*) — An annual festival held in Udaipur after Holi by the Bhil community.
- **Navratri mela** — A tribal fair held during the Navratri festival in different places across Bhil districts.
- **Gramdev and household deities** (*Gramdev, Jatidev, Kuldev, Kuldevi*) — Village and family gods that form the center of Bhil ritual and daily devotion.
- **Bhopas** — Healers and ritual specialists relied upon for physical, mental and psychological treatment in Bhil communities.

## Foodways

Maize, onion, garlic and chilli are staples; the Bhil traditionally hunt wild animals with bow and arrow, and drink mahua alcohol.

- **Maize** — The primary grain staple of the Bhil diet, pounded in wooden mortars.
- **Roti** — Unleavened bread made from grain flour, prepared on lacquer-lined trays.
- **Mahua alcohol** (*mahua*) — Alcohol distilled from the flower of the mahua tree, drunk widely in Bhil communities.
- **Wild game** — Hunted animals taken with bow, arrow, sword, knife and axe, forming a major part of the Bhil diet.
- **Forest gathering** — Fruit and vegetables gathered from local forests to supplement small-field farming.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Bhil have a rich song-poem tradition collected by Randhir Khare; contemporary Bhil painters like Bhuri Bai carry the dotted painting style to modern media.

- **Bhil song-poems** — Folk songs and poems substantial enough to be collected and published; Randhir Khare's *The Singing Bow* and *Flight of Arrows* are major publications.
- **Bhuri Bai** — The first Bhil artist to paint in readymade colors on paper, bringing the dotted style to contemporary galleries.
- **Lado Bai** — A Bhil painter following the dotted in-filling tradition of Bhil painting.
- **Sher Singh** — A Bhil painter working in the dotted painting tradition.
- **Ram Singh and Dubu Bariya** — Bhil painters continuing the dotted in-filling tradition in modern media.

## Language & religion

Bhili is an Indo-Aryan language with up to 36 dialects; most Bhil are Hindu, some are Christian, Muslim, or follow Sonatan, their own religion blending Hindu and animistic belief.

- **Bhili language** (*Bhili*) — An Indo-Aryan language spoken by Bhil people, classified as dialects between Gujarati and Rajasthani–Marwari.
- **Northern Bhil dialects** (*Bauria, Wagdi, Bhilori, Magari*) — Four dialects of the Northern Bhil language group.
- **Central Bhil dialects** (*Bhili proper, Bhilodi, Adiwasa, Bhilali, Chodri, Dhodia–Kukna, Dhanki, Dubli*) — Dialects of the Central Bhil language group.
- **Sonatan** — A religion followed by some Bhil that blends Hindu belief with animistic philosophy.
- **Hinduism, Christianity, Islam** — Most Bhil identify as Hindu; Dang Bhils are Christian, Nirdhi and Tadivi Bhils are Muslim.
