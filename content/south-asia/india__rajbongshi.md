## Overview

The Rajbanshi, also called Rajbongshi and Koch-Rajbongshi, are associated with Lower Assam, North Bengal, eastern Bihar, the Terai region of eastern Nepal, Rangpur Division of North Bangladesh, and Bhutan. They have historically sought an association with the Koch dynasty, and the name Rajbanshi refers to a claimed royal lineage. Worldwide population estimates given in the source are 11–12 million. In West Bengal, Rajbanshi is classified as a Scheduled Caste, while the community is classified as OBC in Assam and Bihar; in Nepal, it forms part of the Plains Janjati. The group speaks Kamatapuri, also known as Rangpuri, an eastern Indo-Aryan language. Its folk-culture significance in the sources lies especially in oral knowledge, agriculture, music, dance, song, medical practices, house building, culture, and language.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources state that the Koch Rajbongshi community has an oral tradition concerning the building of houses, with knowledge ideally transferred from one generation to another.

## Music & performance

Music is described as integral to Koch-Rajbongshi culture. The principal musical forms named in the source are **Bhawaiyya**, **Chatka**, **Chorchunni**, **Palatia**, **Lahankari**, **Tukkhya**, and **Bishohora Pala**, among others.

The named instruments include string instruments such as *Dotora*, *Sarindra*, and *Bena*; double-membrane instruments such as *Tasi*, *Dhak*, *Khol*, *Desi Dhol*, and *Mridanga*; gongs and bells such as *Kansi* and *Khartal*; and wind instruments such as *Sanai*, *Mukha bansi*, and *Kupa bansi*. The catalogue records supplied for this atlas contain no museum objects associated with these instruments.

The source also describes an oral tradition that includes music and song, with knowledge ideally transferred from one generation to another.

## Dance & theatre

The sources identify dance as part of the Koch Rajbongshi oral tradition and culture. The musical forms **Chorchunni**, **Palatia**, **Lahankari**, **Tukkhya**, and **Bishohora Pala** are listed, but the supplied material does not explain whether any of them involve theatre, dance, narrative performance, or particular ceremonial settings.

No source describes masks, puppetry, staged drama, shadow theatre, dance costumes, choreography, or distinctions between ritual and entertainment performance.

## Festivals & rituals

The historical account does document ritual and social practices connected with the Rajbanshi Kshatriya movement. Large numbers of Rajbanshi took a ritual bath in the *Karatoya River* and adopted practices associated with the twice-born, including wearing the sacred thread, *Upanayana*, and adopting a *gotra* name. The account also says that the period of *asauch* was shortened from 30 days to 12. The community gave up practices described as forbidden in Hindu religion, including drinking liquor and rearing pigs. These practices are presented in relation to social upliftment and the assertion of Kshatriya status rather than as a general festival calendar.

## Foodways

The sources describe Rajbongshi traditional occupation as mixed farming. Many were agricultural labourers, called *halua*, or sharecroppers, called *adhiar*. The sources also name the agricultural and landholding relationships in which some Rajbongshis worked: *dar-chukanidars*, *chukandiars*, *jotedars*, and *zamindars*.

No museum catalogue records supplied for this atlas document food-related objects.

## Oral tradition & literature

A 2019 research account cited in the source describes a Koch Rajbongshi oral tradition encompassing agriculture, dance, music, medical practices, song, house building, culture, and language. The source says that the community ideally transfers this knowledge from one generation to another.

The historical account also records legends created to support a claimed Kshatriya origin. According to that account, the group connected itself to a story in which ancestors originally belonged to the Kshatriya *varna*, left their homeland because of fear of annihilation by the Brahmin sage *Parashurama*, took refuge in *Paundradesh*, and later became known as *Bhanga Kshatriyas*. The source presents this narrative as an ideological basis for the movement rather than as independently established history.

## Language & religion

Rajbanshi people speak Kamatapuri, while the related language source identifies Rangpuri as an eastern Indo-Aryan language of the Bengali-Assamese branch. It is spoken in Rangpur Division in Bangladesh, northern West Bengal, and western Goalpara of Assam. Many Rangpuri or Kamta speakers are bilingual in Bengali and Assamese in their respective regions. The language source gives the names *Rangpuri*, *Kamtapuri*, *Bahe*, *Deshi bhasha*, and *Anchalit bhasha*. According to the same source, Rangpuri forms part of the Central-Eastern Kamta group and, with Goalpariya/Deshi, Kayort, Rajbanshi, and Surjapuri, belongs to the KRDS group of languages.

The historical sources describe increasing Brahminical influence on Koch society from the 17th century and the adoption of Hindu practices during the Rajbanshi Kshatriya movement. They also mention the *Kalika Purana*, *Yogini Tantra*, Kshatriya identity, sacred-thread wearing, *Upanayana*, and *gotra* affiliation.
