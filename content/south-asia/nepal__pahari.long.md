## Overview

The Pahadi people, also called Pahari, Parbatiya, or Parbate, are an Indo-Aryan ethnolinguistic group native to the hills and Himalayan foothills of Nepal. Historically, these terms referred specifically to the Khas people, although “Pahari” is also used geographically for people from Nepal’s hill and Himalayas region regardless of ethnicity. Pahari communities speak Eastern Pahari languages, including Nepali. Their history is associated with the Khas people, the Kaśa Kingdom, the Shah dynasty, and the political and military development of Nepal. Pahari culture includes hillside terrace cultivation, wool spinning, weaving, painting, music, dance, caste-based social organization, and Hindu, shamanistic, and oracular practices.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Kullu shawls** — Woolen shawls are commonly produced in the Pahari region. Kullu shawls are known for their patterns and use of natural dyes.

**Chamba rumals** — These handkerchiefs are identified as a Pahari textile tradition. The sources name their use.

**Woolen carpets and blankets** — Handloom weaving produces woolen carpets and blankets in the Pahari region. Most Paharis spin wool, while members of lower castes weave fabrics.

**Bangadwari** — Traditional nuptial wall paintings in Chamba retain stylistic characteristics of 16th-century Northern Indian art and reflect an established regional tradition before the mid-17th-century developments in Pahari painting.

**Pahari painting** — Pahari painting is an Indian miniature-painting tradition originating in the lower Himalayan hill kingdoms of North India. It developed from the 17th through the 19th centuries in centres including Basohli, Mankot, Nurpur, Chamba, Kangra, Guler, Mandi, and Garhwal.

**Basohli painting** — The Basohli school is associated with bold, intense painting, double-storey building structures in square-format backgrounds, lotus flowers, elaborate shikharas, and other decorative elements. Its subjects included the *Rasa-Manjari*, *Ramayana*, *Ragamala*, and *Gita Govinda*.

**Kangra painting** — Kangra paintings are known for delicate, lyrical treatment, realism, soft colour textures, fine borders, and carefully painted trees, leaves, flowers, birds, buildings, and landscapes.

**Motif vocabulary.** Radha and Krishna — eternal-love subjects; lotus flowers — a Basohli decorative element; shikharas — elaborate architectural forms in Basohli compositions; barahmasa — seasons or months; nayikas — heroines; *Ragamala* — a literary and musical theme.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe Pahari clothing as adapted to the cold mountain climate. Woolen garments are common. Men wear *chogas*, described as long cloaks, while women drape shawls or pashmina scarves. Himachali caps, distinguished by visible colours and geometric patterns, are identified as a regional feature of Pahari attire. In Kashmir, the *pheran* is a traditional loose gown worn by both men and women during winter.

### Architecture

In Pahari painting, however, Basohli compositions are distinguished by double-storey building structures in square-format backgrounds and elaborate shikharas. The sources also mention temples and fortresses decorated with mural paintings under the Dogras, including the temples of Krimchi and Babbor and the fort at Bahu.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources mention pottery, wood carving, and woodwork as Pahari crafts. Pahari farmers raise water buffalo, sheep, goats, and cattle, and lower-caste Paharis are associated with goldsmithing, leather working, tailoring, music, drumming, and sweeping.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Pahari paintings are described as including jewellery rendered with fine brushstrokes, particularly in Kangra compositions.

## Music & performance

Music has a central role in Pahari cultural life, especially during celebrations, festivals, and rituals. Folk music commonly addresses nature, love, separation, and devotion. The sources identify the *dhol*, a double-sided drum; the *nagara*, a kettledrum; the flute; and the *chimta*, a percussion instrument with jingling discs. These instruments are commonly used for folk songs.

Among Nepalese Paharis, most higher-caste people are described as military men, farmers, and civil servants, while lower-caste people may work in music and drumming. Pahari painting and culture are associated with oral traditions, and the decline of Pahari dialects has also endangered ballads and folk tales transmitted through oral methods.

## Dance & theatre

*Nati*, originating from Himachal Pradesh, is a Pahari dance known for slow, rhythmic movements. It is performed during festivals and celebrations, often by long lines of dancers moving in unison to traditional music.

## Festivals & rituals

Pahari music and dance occur within celebrations, festivals, and rituals. Paharis native to districts such as Rajouri and Poonch observe the Vaisakhi festival, which marks the harvest season. Paharis in Jammu and Kashmir also observe Muslim religious festivals such as Eid.

Among Nepalese Paharis, death is treated through both burial and cremation. Low-status individuals, including children and some women, are buried. Indigenous healers known as *Jhankri* are buried with their fontanelle pierced so that their spirit can rise to the spirit world. Other people, particularly high-caste and wealthy individuals, are cremated according to classic Hindu tradition.

The sources describe diverse marriage customs, including monogamy, polyandry, polygyny, and group marriage. Girls may be betrothed before the age of 10, although cohabitation with husbands begins when they reach maturity. Widow remarriage is formally prohibited by social norms, while the institution called *Jari* involves a woman taking a paramour and leaving her first husband. The second husband pays the first husband *Jar dine* for the loss of his wife.

## Foodways

The sources describe Pahari foodways in relation to local ingredients, seasonal availability, and regional variation. Common foods include lentils, rice, vegetables, and dairy products. Named dishes include *pahari murga*, chicken cooked with milk and yogurt and seasoned with coriander, ginger, garlic, cardamom, cinnamon, and fennel; *chainsoo*, a lentil-based dish from the Garhwal region made from black gram; and *pahari chicken snack*, in which chicken is stir-fried with mint, coriander, garlic, ginger, green chillies, and garam masala.

*Babru* is a variation of poori made by adding baking powder to dough, filling it with cooked black gram dal, and deep-frying it. *Chana madra* consists of chickpeas slow-cooked in yogurt-based gravy with cardamom, cloves, turmeric, and cumin. *Shakuli* is a traditional papad made from refined flour, salt, water, and oil, steamed and dried in the sun. *Aloo palda* consists of potatoes in gravy made from onions, whisked yogurt, and ground rice.

The sources also name Chamba Chukh, fried fish from the Chamba region, Kullu trout, *kahwa*, *kehew*, and *Siddu*. Kullu trout is marinated with spices and pan-fried, while *Siddu* is steamed bread made from wheat flour and stuffed with lentils or vegetables.

## Oral tradition & literature

Pahari dialects, ballads, and folk tales have traditionally been transmitted orally. The sources state that this transmission has been disrupted by modern lifestyle changes, migration, and the diminishing use of local dialects. Pahari literary works and traditions are described as being at risk of loss.

Pahari painting draws on literary and religious subjects including the *Ramayana*, *Gita Govinda*, *Rasa-Manjari*, and *Ragamala*. The central theme of Pahari painting is the eternal love of the Hindu deities Radha and Krishna.

## Language & religion

Pahari is an encompassing term for languages, dialects, and language groups found largely in the lower Himalayas. In Nepal, Pahari people speak Indo-Aryan languages such as Nepali, and the Pahari people of Nepal are associated with Eastern Pahari languages. The sources also describe Northern Indo-Aryan languages as a proposed group including the Indo-Aryan languages of Nepal, Uttarakhand, and Himachal Pradesh.

Most Nepalese Paharis are Hindus, except for shamanistic and oracular *Matwali* Khasa Chhetris. Hindu Paharis are organized through caste distinctions, including the higher clean Dvija castes and lower unclean Dalit castes. The Dvija include Bahun and Chhetri castes. The sources also describe local animistic practices among Pahari communities in remote areas, while Pahari communities more broadly follow Hinduism, Islam, or Buddhism.
