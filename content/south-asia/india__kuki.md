## Overview

The Kuki people, also called Kuki-Zo people, are an ethnic group living in the Northeastern Indian states of Manipur, Nagaland, Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram, as well as in Bangladesh and Myanmar. In Northeast India they are present in all states except Arunachal Pradesh. The Kuki people form one of the largest hill-tribe communities in this region, and the Chin people of Myanmar and the Mizo people of Mizoram are described as kindred tribes. Collectively, these peoples are termed the Zo people. Some fifty tribes of Kuki peoples in India are recognised as scheduled tribes. The sources present Kuki culture through village institutions, agriculture, youth education, Christianity, customary governance, language, dance, sport and oral practice.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They mention that *Sawm-nu*, within the Sawm institution, cared for chores including washing the boys’ garments, but provide no further information about clothing forms, materials or styles.

### Architecture

Kuki village life included the *Sawm*, a community centre for boys and a centre of learning, and the *Lawm*, a traditional youth club where boys and girls engaged in social activities and acquired practical knowledge. The sources also mention the *Kuki Inn* in Imphal, which was constructed to serve as the office of the Kuki Company.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention a heavy wooden implement used for pounding and de-husking paddy in the sporting activity *Suhtumkhawh*, but provide no broader catalogue of tools or material forms.

## Music & performance

The sources describe several contexts in which Kuki music and performance occur. A popular folk song summarised the position of Khan Thuam during the expansion of the Sukte chieftaincy. The *Lawm* institution also supported sporting and social activities, including *Kung–Kal*, *Ka’ng Ka’p*, *Ka’ngchoi Ka’p*, *Suhtumkhawh* and *So’ngse*. These activities included high jump, top games, javelin throwing with a heavy wooden implement for pounding and de-husking paddy, and shot put.

After the harvest season, the *Lawm-se’l* was celebrated as a Lawm meeting and commemoration. The event was accompanied by dance and the drinking of rice-beer, sometimes continuing for days and nights.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention dance in connection with the *Lawm-se’l*, a commemoration held after the harvest season. The event included dance and rice-beer drinking and could continue for days and nights. No named dance vocabulary, choreography, dramatic tradition, theatre form, mask practice or ceremonial distinction is described.

## Festivals & rituals

The Lawm meeting was celebrated after harvest, accompanied by dance and drinking rice-beer, and a pillar was erected as a commemoration.

The sources describe major changes in religious and ritual life following the arrival of Christianity. Before conversion in the early 20th century, the Chin, Kuki and Mizo peoples are described as animists, and ritual headhunting is mentioned among their practices. Christian missionaries entered Manipur in the late 19th century. William Pettigrew, described as the first foreign missionary, arrived in Manipur on 6 February 1894 and was sponsored by the American Baptist Mission Union. Watkins Robert organised the Indo-Burma Thadou-Kuki Pioneer Mission in 1913, and its name changed to North East India General Mission in 1924.

## Foodways

They mention rice-beer in connection with the *Lawm-se’l*, a post-harvest commemoration accompanied by dance and drinking. The Lawm also transmitted practical knowledge concerning farming, hunting and fishing.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources mention a popular folk song concerning Khan Thuam, a powerful Sukte chieftain who rose in the area south of present-day Manipur around 1830. The song summarised the political position of Khan Thuam during territorial expansion and raids along the border of Manipur. No text, title, performer or musical form is supplied.

The sources also refer to traditional Meitei hymns in which the Kuki king is praised along with the Meitei king. They mention chronicles, including the chronicles of Tripura and *Cheitharol Kumbaba*, the court chronicle of the Manipur kings, which records various Kuki tribes and clans from 1404 onwards. These are historical references rather than a documented Kuki literary canon.

## Language & religion

Thadou, also called Kuki or Thado Chin, is a Sino-Tibetan language of the Northeastern sub-branch of Kuki-Chin. It is spoken by the Thadou people in Manipur and Assam. The language is also known by the names Thado, Thado-Pao, Thado-Ubiphei, Thādo, Thaadou Kuki and Chin. Its named dialects include Hangshing, Khongsai, Kipgen, Saimar, Langiung, Sairang, Thangngeo, Haokip, Sitlhou and Singson, also written Shingsol. The Saimar dialect was reported as spoken by four people in one village in Tripura. The language has partial mutual intelligibility with Paite, Hmar, Vaiphei, Simte, Kom and Gangte.

Before conversion in the early 20th century, Kuki religious practice is described as animist. Christianity spread after the arrival of missionaries and the Anglo-Kuki War of 1917–1919; the majority of Kukis are now Christians, most belonging to Protestant denominations, especially Baptist. Since the late 20th century, some have followed Messianic Judaism. The Bnei Menashe are described as a small group within Manipur and Mizoram made up of Mizo, Kuki and Chin peoples. A Kuki Muslim community is also described in Tripura.
