## Overview

The Tamils, also known as *Tamilar*, are a Dravidian ethnic group whose ancestry is traced mainly to the southern part of the Indian subcontinent. They speak Tamil and form the majority in Tamil Nadu and Puducherry. They also constitute significant proportions of the populations of Sri Lanka, Malaysia and Singapore: 15.3% in Sri Lanka, 7% in Malaysia and 5% in Singapore; they constitute about 5.7% of the Indian population. Tamil-speaking populations also exist in South Africa, Mauritius, Fiji, Southeast Asia, the Middle East, the Caribbean and parts of the Western World. Archaeological evidence from Tamil Nadu indicates more than 3,800 years of continuous human occupation. Tamil culture preserves extensive traditions of literature, temple architecture, sculpture, music, dance, food and religious practice.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Thanjavur painting* — This form of Tamil painting originated in the 16th century CE. A cloth base coated with zinc oxide is painted with dyes and decorated with semi-precious stones and silver or gold threads.

*Kanchipuram silk sari* — This silk sari is made in the Kanchipuram region in Tamil Nadu and is worn by many women in South India for bridal and special occasions. It was recognized as a Geographical indication by the Government of India in 2005–2006.

*Kovai Cora cotton* — This is a specific type of cotton sari made in the Coimbatore region.

*Thanjavur painting* — The form is distinguished by its decorated surface, in which painted cloth is embellished with semi-precious stones and metallic threads.

Tamil literature also describes women’s saris, pavaadai, dhavani, veshti and lungi, but the sources used do not document the other indexed textile traditions.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name the veni pattern of flowers in the hair, the kolam only as an indexed search term rather than in the supplied source material, and the geometric and religious themes of Tamil painting without identifying additional motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Tamil women traditionally wear a *sari*, a draped garment measuring from 4.6 m to 8.2 m in length and from 0.61 m to 1.2 m in breadth. It is wrapped around the waist with one end draped over the shoulder, leaving the midriff uncovered. Colourful silk sarees are worn on traditional occasions. Young girls wear a long skirt called *pavaadai* together with a shorter sari called *dhavani*. Men wear a *veshti*, a rectangular piece of non-stitched cloth wrapped around the legs and knotted at the waist, often white with brightly coloured borders. A hip belt can hold it in place. A *dhoti* is a similar long white garment wrapped around the waist and legs. A *lungi* is tubular, with stitched ends and colourful batik patterns, and is common in the countryside. Urban people generally wear tailored or Western clothing, including Western-style school uniforms. The sources do not describe head coverings or footwear.

### Architecture

Dravidian temple architecture has a central sanctum, or *garbhagriha*, topped by a pyramidal tower called a *vimana*. Porches, or *mantapas*, lead toward the sanctum, while large gate-pyramids called *gopurams* stand in the quadrangular enclosures surrounding the temple. The complexes can also contain pillared halls and water tanks or wells. Gopurams are monumental, ornate entrance towers topped by *kalasams*, or finials.

The Group of Monuments at Mahabalipuram, built by the Pallavas in the 7th and 8th centuries, contains more than forty rock-cut temples, monoliths and rock reliefs. Madurai and Kanchipuram are notable centres of Dravidian architecture. The Srirangam Ranganathaswamy Temple has a 236 feet (72 m) high Rajagopuram. The Brihadisvara Temple at Thanjavur is an exception in which the vimana is not smaller than the gopuram. The sources do not describe Tamil house forms, roofs or workshops.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Tamil material culture includes stone temple sculpture, bronze icons and bronze statues. Chola bronze statues were produced through a lost-wax process: models made from beeswax and sal tree resin were encased in clay, fired so that the wax melted, and filled with molten metal. The museum catalogue records a wooden *valari* with a flattened round handle and a curved blade widening toward its flat tip, as well as brass and wood scribe’s knives and styluses, a brass water vessel, a steel and leather mace, a wooden betel-box and a wooden coconut scraper from Sri Lanka whose metal blade is missing. A museum catalogue also records a metal trident, or *triśūla*, and a metal vessel used for *Sambrani*.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources record a *Malligai Arumbu Malai*, or Jasmine Bud Necklace, from the 19th century, made of gold with rubies strung on black thread. They also record a gold ear pendant called *Kathija*. Tamil women’s ceremonial dress is described with jewellery and special anklets called *salangai*, made of small bells. Hair is plaited in a particular manner and decorated with flowers in a pattern called *veni*. The sources do not document tattoos, henna, armlets or other named body-adornment forms.

## Music & performance

The ancient Tamil country had a musical system called *Tamil Pannisai*. Sangam literature, including *Silappatikaram* from the 2nd century CE, describes music notes and instruments. A Pallava inscription from the 7th century CE is among the earliest surviving examples of Indian music in notation. It describes the string instrument *veena* and the singing of musical hymns called *Thirupadigam* in temples. From the 9th century CE, Shaivite hymns called *Thevaram* and Vaishnavite hymns called *Tiruvaymoli* were sung with instruments.

Traditional instruments named in the sources include *parai*, *tharai*, *yazh*, *murasu*, *nadaswaram* and *thavil*. The nadaswaram is a reed instrument often accompanied by the thavil, a drum, and both are major instruments in temples and weddings. *Melam* is a group of percussion instruments from ancient Tamilakam played during events and functions. *Villu Paatu* is a musical storytelling form in which narration is interspersed with music from a string bow and accompanying instruments. *Gaana* is a combination of folk musics sung mainly in Chennai.

Carnatic music developed later with rhythmic and structured compositions by Thyagaraja, Muthuswami Dikshitar and Shyama Shastri. The annual *Tyagaraja Aradhana* is held in Tiruvaiyaru. During the Tamil month of Margazhi, music concerts called *katcheris* are generally conducted, including the Madras Music Season by the Madras Music Academy and Chennaiyil Thiruvaiyaru. Folk singing remains popular, especially in rural areas, and the *urumi melam* is played with an urumee and nadaswaram.

## Dance & theatre

*Bharatanatyam* is a major Indian classical dance genre originating in Tamil Nadu. The dancer wears a colourful silk sari, jewellery, small-belled *salangai* anklets and a flower-decorated hairstyle called *veni*. The dance uses bent legs, footwork, hand gestures called *abhinaya*, hand mudras and expressive movements of the eyes and face.

Tamil folk dances include *Karakattam*, in which dancers balance decorated pots on their heads; *Kavadiattam*, in which dancers carry a kavadi; *Kolattam*, performed with crossed sticks; *Kummi*, in which the hands replace the sticks; and *Mayilattam*, which imitates a peacock. Other named dances are *Oyilattam*, *Paampu attam*, *Paraiattam*, *Puliyattam*, *Puravaiattam*, *Bhagavatha nadanam*, *Chakkaiattam*, *Devarattam*, *Kai silambattam*, *Kuravanji*, *Sevaiattam* and *Urumiattam*.

*Koothu* is street theatre combining dance, music, narration and singing. Performers wear wooden headgear, special costumes, heavy anklets, face painting and make-up. It is performed during festivals in open public places and is often dedicated to Mariamman or Draupadi. *Bommalattam* is puppetry using rod- and string-operated marionettes behind an oil-lamp-lit screen. The sources do not cover shadow puppetry.

## Festivals & rituals

*Pongal* is a major multi-day harvest festival celebrated in the month of Thai according to the Tamil solar calendar, usually on 14 or 15 January. It is dedicated to Surya, the Sun God, and takes its name from the ceremonial dish Pongal, made from newly harvested rice boiled in milk with jaggery. *Mattu Pongal* celebrates cattle, which are bathed, decorated, garlanded and taken in processions. *Jallikattu* is a traditional event during this period in which a bull is released into a crowd and participants attempt to hold its hump.

*Puthandu* is Tamil New Year and marks the first day of the Tamil calendar in April. *Karthikai Deepam* is a festival of lights observed on the full moon day of the Kartika month, in November or December. *Thaipusam* is held on the first full moon day of the Tamil month of Thai and is dedicated to Murugan. *Aadi Perukku* is celebrated on the 18th day of Adi and pays tribute to water’s life-sustaining properties. *Panguni Uthiram* marks the full moon of Panguni and celebrates the wedding of various Hindu gods. *Vaikasi Visakam* commemorates the birth of Murugan.

The worship of Amman and Ayyanar deities is organized during the month of Adi in temples across Tamil Nadu. Temple practice also includes rituals involving village deities, sacrifices, possession and exorcism. The sources used do not describe birth, coming-of-age or funeral rites in detail.

## Foodways

Rice is the staple food and is served with *sambar*, *rasam* and *poriyal*. Traditional meals are served on banana leaves, and food is eaten seated on the floor with the fingertips of the right hand. Hospitality is a major social obligation, and offering food to guests is described as one of the highest virtues. Tamil cuisines include Chettinadu, Kongunadu, Nanjilnadu, Pandiyanadu and Sri Lankan Tamil varieties.

Chettinadu cuisine is known for meat dishes and generous use of spices. Kongunadu cuisine uses fewer spices and includes coconut, sesame seeds, groundnut and turmeric. Nanjilnadu cuisine is milder and based mainly on fish and vegetables. Sri Lankan Tamil cuisine uses gingelly oil and jaggery with coconut and spices. Fish is traditionally consumed along the coast, while other meats are preferred inland.

Popular dishes include *idli*, *dosa*, *upma*, *idiappam*, *pongal*, *paniyaram*, *parotta*, *biryani*, *kuzhambu*, *kootu*, *thuvaiyal*, *pachadi*, *vadai*, *appalam*, *payasam* and *koottu*. Coffee and tea are staple drinks; preparation of filter coffee is described as a ritual. The sources describe both vegetarian and meat dishes but do not provide a comprehensive system of dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tamil literature is one of the oldest bodies of literature in South Asia. Early literature was composed in three poetic assemblies called Tamil Sangams and classified into *iyal* poetry, *isai* music and *nadagam* drama. Sangam literature used the genres *akam*, concerning the internal world, and *puram*, concerning the external world, and described five landscapes.

The oldest surviving book is *Tolkāppiyam*, a treatise on Tamil grammar. Early literature includes the *Ettuttokai*, or Eight Anthologies, and the *Pattuppattu*, or Ten Idylls. Later works include the Five Great Epics and Five Minor Epics, while *Tirukkural* is a book on ethics by Thiruvalluvar. The epic *Cilappatikaram*, dated to 100–300 CE in the mythology source, tells the story of Kannagi and her revenge against the Pandyan King of Madurai after the mistaken execution of her husband Kovalan.

Bhakti literature developed through the hymns of the Alvars and Nayanmars. The *Naalayira Divya Prabandham* collects the hymns of the Alvars. The sources also describe *Villu Paatu* as a storytelling performance. Tamil literature and palm-leaf manuscripts preserve religious, medical and magical writings.

## Language & religion

Tamil belongs to the southern branch of the Dravidian languages and is one of the longest-surviving classical languages in the world, attested since c. 300 BCE. It is an official language of Tamil Nadu, Puducherry, Singapore and Sri Lanka. The language has Old Tamil, Middle Tamil and Modern Tamil periods. Its historical writing systems include Tamil Brahmi, *vaṭṭeḻuttu*, Grantha and Pallava scripts; the current script has 12 vowels, 18 consonants and one special character.

Tamil has regional varieties including Madras Bashai, Kongu Tamil, Madurai Tamil, Nellai Tamil, Kumari Tamil, Batticaloa Tamil, Jaffna Tamil and Negombo Tamil. Hinduism is the major religion among Tamils, alongside Christianity, Islam, Jainism and Buddhism. Tamil mythology combines Dravidian culture with mainstream Hinduism. Named deities include Murugan, Tirumal, Kotravai, Ayyanar, Mariamman, Meenakshi, Shiva and Vishnu. The sources describe village-deity worship, temple ritual, possession, exorcism and sacrifice. Siddha medicine is a traditional system associated with the Tamils and with Siddhars.
