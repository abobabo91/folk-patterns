## Overview

The Kodavas, also called Coorgs, are an endogamous Dravidian ethnolinguistic group from Kodagu in the southern Indian state of Karnataka. They are traditionally land-owning agriculturists with patrilineal organization and martial customs, and have cultivated paddy fields, maintained cattle herds, and grown coffee plantations. The Kodava language belongs to the South Dravidian subgroup of the Dravidian language family and is spoken in Kodagu, also known as Coorg. The 2011 Census of India recorded 96,918 people returning Kodava as their mother tongue and 16,939 returning Coorgi/Kodagu, giving a total of 113,857 in the parent group. Kodava folk culture is particularly documented through its agricultural festivals, weapon traditions, clan houses, sacred groves, oral songs, foodways, and distinctive dress.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Kodava men traditionally wear a wraparound robe called the *Kupya*, now chiefly seen on ceremonial occasions. Men also carry ceremonial knives and have distinctive martial practices. Kodava women wear the sari with its pleats at the back and its loose end pinned at the right shoulder. During Kaveri Sankramana, married women wear new silk saris. The sources describe no specific head coverings, belts, footwear, tattoos, or hair practices.

### Architecture

Kodava settlements in Coorg consist of *Okka* family groups scattered across agricultural and forest holdings. Traditional *Ainmane* houses serve as focal meeting points in the rural landscape. The sources describe the houses as ancestral homes and identify the *Nellakki Nadubade* as the central hall of the house and a place of community worship. During Puttari, ancestral homes and houses are decorated with marigold flowers and green mango leaves, and houses were painted by whitewashing in earlier times. Clan lands also contain *Kaimada* shrines dedicated to the first ancestor, Guru Karana.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Weapons are important identity-bearing objects in Kodava culture. The Kodava/Coorg emblem contains the *Odi Kathi*, or machete, the *Peeche Kathi*, or dagger, and a shotgun. Kodavas worship weapons including swords, bows, arrows, and later guns. During Kail Poldh, weapons are removed from the prayer room, cleaned, decorated with flowers, placed in the central hall, and worshipped. The *Kaimada* shrines contain weapons made of wood or metal.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention jewels in connection with the Kanni Puje during Kaveri Sankramana. A vegetable representing the goddess Kaveri is wrapped in red silk and decorated with flowers and jewels, chiefly the *Pathak*, identified as a Kodava *Mangalasuthra*.

## Music & performance

Kodava oral song traditions include the *Palame*, also called the *Balo Patt* or *Dudi Patt*. These folk songs were transmitted orally across generations and are associated with marriage and death ceremonies, seasonal festivals, and the honoring of local deities and heroes. Traditionally known as *Balo Pat*, the songs are performed by four men who beat *dudis*, or drums, as they sing. Kodava folk dances are performed to the beat of many of these songs.

The *Pattole Palame*, a collection of Kodava folksongs and traditions compiled in the early 1900s by Nadikerianda Chinnappa, was first published in 1924. It was originally written using the Kannada script and later translated into English by Boverianda Nanjamma and Chinnappa. The sources describe the collection as a major record of Kodava folklore, with nearly two-thirds consisting of orally transmitted folksongs.

## Dance & theatre

Kodava folk dances are performed to the beat of the *Palame* songs and the *dudis*. Martial war dances are among the distinctive practices associated with Kodava men. During the village festival called *Naad namme*, groups of men and children dress in different attires, including tiger and women’s attire, and perform a procession with bands through the village. The procession visits houses overnight and entertains villagers.

They distinguish neither a separate ceremonial dance repertoire from an entertainment repertoire beyond the festival and martial contexts described.

## Festivals & rituals

Kodava festivities center on agriculture and military tradition. The three main festivals peculiar to Kodagu are Kail Poldh, Kaveri Changrandi or Kaveri Sankramana, and Puthari.

Kail Poldh, the Festival of Arms, is celebrated on 3 September. It follows the transplantation of the rice crop and marks the time when weapons are taken from the prayer room, cleaned, decorated with flowers, and worshipped. The festival includes feasting, drinking, physical contests, marksmanship, and rural sports. The family gathers in the *Mand*, or open ground, where shooting skills and contests are conducted.

Kaveri Sankramana normally takes place in mid-October, when the sun enters Tula Rasi. At Talakaveri, a fountain fills a larger holy tank, and people gather to bathe in the water. The water, called *Theertha*, is carried to homes throughout Kodagu. Married women perform the Kanni Puje to a vegetable representing the goddess Kaveri. Vegetarian food is prepared and alcohol is prohibited on this festival.

Puttari, or *pudiya ari*, meaning “new rice,” is the rice harvest festival. It takes place in late November or early December on the night of a full moon. Houses are cleaned and decorated, foods are offered to the ancestors, and the family proceeds to the paddy fields. A gunshot marks the beginning of the symbolic harvest, accompanied by the chant “Poli Poli Deva.” A communal dinner follows a week later.

Other observances include *Karana Kodupo*, offerings and feasting for the family ancestor, *Pasanamurthy Therre Kattuva*, and the village festival *Naad namme*.

## Foodways

Boiled rice, called *koolu*, is a staple food for lunch and dinner. Rice is also prepared as *kanji* or *koolu* and served with curries and additional dishes. Coconut, jackfruit, plantain, mango, fruits, and vegetables are widely used. The sources describe pork, chicken, river fish, and varieties of bush meat as commonly consumed, and state that meat and alcoholic drinks are not prohibited.

*Pandhi Curry* is a Kodava pork curry, commonly made with hunted wild boar. Its deep brown color comes from *Kachumpuli*, a vinegar made from *Garcinia cambogia*. It is served with *Kadumbuttu*, steamed rice balls. Other named foods include *Otti*, a rice flatbread; *Paaputtu*; *Thaliya Puttu*; *Noolputtu*, rice noodles served with traditional Coorg chicken curry; *Bymbale*, or bamboo shoots; wild mushrooms; leafy vegetables; ferns; crabs; raw mango curry; tender jackfruit curry; jackfruit-seed curry; and *Akki Payasa*.

During Puttari, families prepare *thambuttu*, a sweet made with ripe bananas and roasted rice flour, *kadambutt pandhi curi*, and yam with jaggery water and coconut. During Kaveri Sankramana, the menu includes dosa, vegetable curry, usually sweet pumpkin curry called *kumbala kari*, and *payasa*. That festival is strictly vegetarian and alcohol is prohibited.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kodava family histories, rituals, and other records were written on palm leaves called *Pattole*, meaning palm leaf. The folk songs of the Kodavas, the *Palame*, were transmitted orally across generations. They preserve traditions concerning marriage, death ceremonies, seasonal festivals, local deities, and heroes.

The *Pattole Palame*, compiled by Nadikerianda Chinnappa in the early 1900s and first published in 1924, is identified as an important collection of Kodava literature and folklore. Appachcha Kavi and Nadikerianda Chinnappa are described as important poets and writers of the Kodava language; B D Ganapathy and I M Muthanna are also named as writers. The sources state that Kodava had no significant written literature until the twentieth century. The *Pattole Palame* was originally written in the Kannada script and later translated into English by Boverianda Nanjamma and Chinnappa.

## Language & religion

Kodava is an endangered South Dravidian language spoken in Kodagu district. Its two dialects are Mendele, spoken in northern and central Kodagu outside Kiggat naadu, and Kiggat, spoken in southern Kodagu. The language is closely related to Malayalam, Tamil, Tulu, and Kannada. It is also known as Kodagu, Coorg, Coorgi, Kodaga, and Kodava thakk.

Kodava has been written in the Kannada script and, especially near Kerala, in the Malayalam script. It can also be written in the Latin alphabet. The thirke script is described as a Brahmic abugida associated with an early stage of Kodava. Dr. IM Muthanna developed a script in 1971, and the Karnataka Kodava Sahitya Academy accepted and recommended it as the official script in 2022.

The Kodavas are Hindus. Their local trinity consists of the patron goddess Kaveri, the chief preceptor Igguthappa, and Guru Karana, the revered common ancestor. They worship ancestors, nature, weapons, spirits, snakes, and local deities. Sacred groves called *Devakadu* were maintained on village lands, where hunting and cutting trees were prohibited. Clan shrines called *Kaimada* honor ancestral spirits, and offerings are made to the family *Karana*.
