## Overview

The Baloch are an ethnic group native to the Balochistan region of South and West Asia, occupying parts of Pakistan, Iran, and Afghanistan, with diaspora communities in Central Asia, the Arabian Peninsula, Oman, the Arab states of the Persian Gulf, Turkmenistan, East Africa, and other parts of the world. They were traditionally nomadic pastoralists. The majority reside in Pakistan: about half live in the Pakistani province of Balochistan, while 40% are settled in Sindh and a smaller number in Pakistani Punjab. As of 2008, an estimated eight to nine million Baloch lived in Afghanistan, Iran, and Pakistan, divided among over 130 tribes. Their language, Balochi, is an Indo-European language of the Indo-Iranian and Northwestern Iranian groups. Baloch folk culture includes needlework, weaving, folklore, music, dance, foodways, jewelry, and oral traditions preserved in Balochi.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Balochi rugs and floor coverings** — Rug weaving is a common profession among Baloch tribes in Afghanistan. Balochi rugs and floor coverings are sold in the Herat local market and worldwide.

**Balochi weaving** — Balochi weaving is described as renowned throughout the world, with fine specimens of rugs and carpets.

**Baloch needlework and embroidery** — Needlework and handicrafts are identified as the art of Baloch women in Afghanistan.

**Women’s embroidered clothing** — *Za Asteen Guptan* is a type of clothing worn by Baloch women in Afghanistan and designed with Baloch needlework and embroidery.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe Baloch women in Afghanistan as wearing *Za Asteen Guptan*, clothing designed with Baloch needlework and embroidery. They also describe Baloch women’s gold ornaments and the use of a gold brooch called *tasni* to fasten the two parts of the dress together over the chest. The sources state that Baloch dressing has been influenced by the wider country and mention the dressing style of Baloch women.

### Architecture

 Ibn Khordadbeh’s description of Makran, as summarized in the source material, says that Baloch houses were made of wood.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention local jewelers making gold brooches in different shapes and sizes, and describe heavy gold earrings fastened with gold chains. The sources also mention camel ownership, breeding, and camel-riding competitions in Nimroz province.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Gold ornaments, especially necklaces and bracelets, are an important aspect of Baloch women’s traditions. A favored item is *dorr*, a heavy earring fastened to the head with gold chains so that its weight does not harm the ears. Women also wear a gold brooch called *tasni*, made by local jewelers in different shapes and sizes and used to fasten the two parts of the dress over the chest.

## Music & performance

Baloch music includes named musical forms and instruments. *Zahīrōk* is described as one of the musical forms of the Baloch; at the beginning, it was sung only by two groups of Baloch women. *Suroz* and *Ghaychak* are identified as popular instruments among Balochs, including craftspeople, folk artists, folk musicians, and dance groups.

The sources also describe older performance practices. In ancient times, especially during the pre-Islamic era, Baloch women commonly performed dances and sang folk songs at different events. The tradition of a Baloch mother singing lullabies to her children is presented as a means by which knowledge has been transferred from generation to generation since ancient times.

Folk music is listed as a significant element of Baloch culture, alongside Balochi handicrafts, drama, and Balochi cinema. Baloch Culture Day includes folk music among its activities.

## Dance & theatre

The sources state that Baloch women performed dances and sang folk songs at different events in ancient times, especially during the pre-Islamic era. They also identify dance groups among the communities associated with *suroz* and *ghaychak*. Baloch Culture Day programs include dance, folk music, craft exhibitions, and other activities. They mention drama and Balochi cinema as significant parts of Baloch culture but provide no further description.

## Festivals & rituals

Baloch Culture Day is celebrated annually on 2 March by Baloch people, with festivities centered on Baloch culture and history. The Baloch Council of Afghanistan is described as a Baloch socio-cultural organization that celebrates Baloch Culture Day every year.

Balochistan celebrates its culture day on 2 March, when people from different villages gather to organize cultural programs. These events include folk music, dance, craft exhibitions, and other activities. The sources also state that Balochistan is the only state in Pakistan where the *Jashn-e-Kalat* festival is celebrated. This festival includes circus performances, rifle shooting, and other cultural programs and shows, and is held to honor the diversified culture of Balochistan. They describe *Baad* as an unlawful marriage custom and dispute-resolution practice in Harnai district, in which a girl is given as compensation to a victim’s family without her consent; the decisions are made by a *jirga*, which heads the tribal court.

## Foodways

Baloch cuisine is associated with the Baloch people of Balochistan across Afghanistan, Iran, and Pakistan and with diaspora communities worldwide. The food culture varies regionally. Meat is described as the most commonly consumed food. Many meat dishes are cooked over indirect heat with limited spices in order to preserve the food’s taste.

Named meat dishes include *sajji*, *dampukht*, and *tabaheg*. *Tabaheg* is an entire goat marinated with dried pomegranate and salt and preserved across generations. *Sajji* is also mentioned as a dish familiar in other parts of the country.

Fish is commonly consumed in the coastal region of Balochistan. *Mayg o bhat* means “fish and rice” and is identified as the most common fish dish. The sources describe several kinds of bread, varying by region. *Kaak*, also called *kurnoo*, is made from wheat wrapped around a hot round stone and cooked around a fire in a hardened ball of bread that can last for days.

Desserts vary by area or tribe. *Gwadari halwa* is described as a popular dessert cooked in a round, aged metal pot.

## Oral tradition & literature

Baloch folklore consists of folk traditions developed in Balochistan over many centuries. Most are preserved in the Balochi language and address tragic love, resistance, and war. Ballads narrate conflicts and wars fought by different clans and celebrate the valor of tribal chiefs and heroes.

*Hani and Sheh Mureed* is described as a tragic love story about the deep love between Hani and Sheh Mureed and the social pressures that lead to their separation. *Mir Hammal Jiand* is a significant figure in Baloch folklore, Baloch culture, and Balochi literature, particularly in connection with conflicts with Portuguese colonial forces in the 16th century. He is celebrated as a heroic leader and compared with other Baloch leaders such as Mir Chakar Rind.

Baloch folklore includes stories about nature, anthropomorphic animals, love, heroes, villains, mythological creatures, and everyday life. Baloch mythology intertwines with beliefs and geography and includes supernatural elements. *Ashkash* appears in several verses in the *Shahnameh* as commander of the Baloch army.

## Language & religion

Balochi is an Indo-European language belonging to the Indo-Iranian branch and the Northwestern Iranian group. It is spoken primarily in Balochistan in Pakistan, Iran, and Afghanistan, with speakers in Oman, the Arab states of the Persian Gulf, Turkmenistan, East Africa, and diaspora communities. Its main varieties are Eastern or Soleimani, Southern or Makrani, and Western or Rakhshani; Koroshi is also classified as Balochi.

Balochi was not a written language before the 19th century. Persian script was used where necessary, British colonial officers first wrote Balochi in the Latin script, and Baloch scholars later adopted the Persian alphabet. The first collection of poetry in Balochi, *Gulbang* by Mir Gul Khan Nasir, was published in 1951. A Balochi Standard Alphabet was later standardized by the Balochi Academy Sarbaz.

The majority of Iranian Baloch are Sunni Muslims, in contrast with Iran’s predominantly Shia Muslim population. Baloch culture sources also describe belief in supernatural powers, including the wind and sea, and in the effects of *nazzar*, the evil eye, and *jinn*.
