## Overview

The Sora, also called Saora, Saura, Savara, Sabara, or Soura, are a Munda ethnic group of eastern India. They live mainly in southern Odisha and northern coastal Andhra Pradesh, especially in Gajapati, Rayagada, and Bargarh districts of Odisha and in Srikakulam, Vizianagaram, and Visakhapatnam districts. They also inhabit the blocks of Gunupur, Padmapur, and Gudari, with a high concentration in the Puttasingi area, approximately 25 km from Gunupur NAC. The Sora speak Sora, a South Munda language of the Austroasiatic family. Their folk culture includes terraced rice farming, shifting cultivation, distinctive dress, village political offices, oral traditions, dance and music, and a shamanic religion involving female priests, ancestral spirits, and supernatural entities called the *kittung*.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Sora clothing is described in relation to everyday dress. Adult men wear a *gavancha*, while women wear saris. The Soras are also called Lanjia Souras because of a dress pattern in which a loin cloth hangs behind the wearer and can be mistaken by a stranger for a tail.

### Architecture

Savara villages consist of houses with mud walls and sedge-grass roofs, usually situated in foothills. The sources also state that Sora settlements are found in the Eastern Ghat hills and that the people practice sedentary rice farming on terraced paddy fields. No named house type, decorative program, settlement plan, public building, or urban architectural tradition is described.

## Music & performance

Dance and music are described as integral parts of Sora aesthetic life. Sora religion includes dances performed by the *kuran*, the female priests or shamans, during rituals connected with the *kittung*. These rituals can also involve annual observances, cattle sacrifices, and offerings.

The Sora shamanic tradition gives performance a religious and social function. A shaman, usually a woman, serves as an intermediary between the worlds of the living and the dead. During a trance, her soul is said to descend to the underworld, leaving her body available for the dead to use as a vehicle for communication. Spirits speak through her mouth, while mourners gather around her, arguing with the dead, laughing at their jokes, or weeping at their accusations.

The sources also describe weekly markets, called *shandies*, as important to social life, economy, and cultural exchange with other tribes and with Western culture.

## Dance & theatre

The sources state that dance forms part of Sora aesthetic life and that dances occur in the rituals performed by *kuran* for the *kittung*.

## Festivals & rituals

Sora traditional religion attributes natural phenomena to gods, deities, or spirits, and customary law, values, and norms are strongly respected because of the fear of personal or communal harm. Supernatural entities called the *kittung* are believed to inhabit natural and human landscapes, including forests, mountains, villages, rivers, and particular locations.

The Sora shamans, or *kuran*, are female priests whose tasks include fulfilling the *kittung* through annual rituals, cattle sacrifices, dances, and offerings.

Sora social life includes marriage by bride capture, elopement, and negotiation. Families are usually nuclear, although joint or extended families also occur. The sources state that the Sora family is polygamous and that the household economy revolves around the woman, who helps her husband with ploughing and harvesting in addition to household work. No detailed birth, initiation, wedding, or funeral ceremony is described.

Christianity was introduced by Canadian and American Baptist missionaries in the early 1900s, and the first Sora converts were baptised in 1905. In some areas, shamanic and animist beliefs have become distant memories, and around Puttasingh some ancestral graveyards have been destroyed. Sora Christians were affected by the 2008 Kandhamal violence, during which a Sora church was burned and its pastor physically assaulted.

## Foodways

Sora agriculture includes sedentary rice farming on terraced paddy fields and *podu* cultivation, a slash-and-burn system practiced especially in the hills of Srikakulam district. Because level land is limited, forested land is cleared and millet and pulses are planted between tree stumps. After a couple of years, the land is allowed to recover while another area is cultivated, and the original plot is eventually used again.

Where irrigation is possible, the Sora construct terraces and grow rice. The sources therefore identify rice, millet, and pulses as cultivated foods.

## Oral tradition & literature

Sora has very little formal literature but an abundance of folktales and traditions. Much knowledge is passed orally from generation to generation. The Sora people’s speech remained unwritten until the colonial period, although a small number of lexical items were recorded by Colonel Dalton in 1872 and Pendercast in 1881. Fred Fawcett later included a short Sora wordlist in his 1887 ethnographic description of the Sora tribe.

The first systematic descriptive grammar of Sora appeared in 1931 and was authored by the Telugu scholar G. V. Ramamurti. Sora also has a few songs and folk tales that are usually transmitted orally.

The Sora language was featured in the 2008 PBS Ironbound documentary *The Linguists*, which followed G. D. S. Anderson and K. David Harrison during fieldwork in southern Odisha. The documentary attempted to document several moribund languages and included a visit to a Sora village.

## Language & religion

Sora is a South Munda language belonging to the Austroasiatic family. It is spoken mainly in Ganjam, Gajapati, and Rayagada districts of southern Odisha and in northern coastal Andhra Pradesh, with communities in nearby districts and other parts of India. It is also found in Vizianagaram, Parvatipuram Manyam, and Srikakulam districts. Many Sora speakers are bilingual in Odia or Telugu.

The sources name several dialects or varieties, including Lanjia-Sora, Imani, Kansid, Kampu, Tenkala, Sarda, and Juray-Sora. Sora is written in the native Sora Sompeng script, as well as Latin, Odia, and Telugu scripts. The Sora Sompeng script was developed in 1936; the sources associate its development with Mangei Gomango and also state that Malia Gomango and his son-in-law, Mangei Gomango, were involved in its creation and printing.

Sora traditional religion is a form of shamanism. The Sora are described as animists who believe in numerous deities and ancestral spirits. Female *kuran* serve as shamans or priests and communicate with the *kittung*. Christianity, introduced by missionaries in the early 1900s, has superseded traditional beliefs in many areas.
