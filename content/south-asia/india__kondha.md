## Overview

The Khonds, also spelt Kondha and Kandha, are a Dravidian ethnic group and indigenous tribal community in India. They are the largest tribal group in Odisha and also have Scheduled Tribe status in Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Jharkhand, and West Bengal. They are divided for census purposes into hill-dwelling and plain-dwelling Khonds, while identifying through specific clans. The 2011 census recorded 1,743,406 people in the eight states; 93.35% resided in Odisha. Khonds speak Kui and Kuvi, both Dravidian languages written in the Odia script. Their folk culture is closely connected with forest resources, shifting cultivation, clan organisation, hunting, ancestor worship, nature worship, oral teaching, and ritual relationships with the earth, hills, valleys, and rivers.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state that women assist with cultivation, harvesting, household work, and the sale of produce, while men commonly forage or hunt.

### Architecture

They state that the Khonds are forest and hill dwellers and that the Dongria clan inhabits the steep slopes of the Niyamgiri Range of Rayagada district and extends over the border into Kalahandi.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention household activities including fetching water, cooking, food service, cultivation, harvesting, food preservation by smoking fish and meat, and the use of medicinal plants, without describing the objects involved.

## Music & performance

The Kondha sources describe singing and dancing as part of the education and enculturation of adolescents. Girls and boys sleep at night in separate dormitories, where they learn social taboos, myths, legends, stories, riddles, and proverbs amidst singing and dancing throughout the night. Girls are instructed in housekeeping and bringing up children, while boys learn hunting and the legends of their brave and martial ancestors. The Khonds’ martial traditions are discussed in relation to hunting, warfare, and military recruitment: bravery and skill in hunting determine the respect a man receives, and many Khonds were recruited by the British during the First and Second World Wars as jungle warfare experts and warriors.

## Dance & theatre

The sources state that adolescent girls and boys learn through singing and dancing in their respective dormitories, alongside instruction in myths, legends, stories, riddles, proverbs, social taboos, household responsibilities, hunting, and the legends of martial ancestors.

## Festivals & rituals

Traditional Khond religion combined totemism, animism, ancestor worship, shamanism, and nature worship. It involved worship of mountains, rivers, the Sun, and the Earth. *Baredi* is identified as a place of worship. Named rituals include *Jhagadi*, *Kedu* or *Meriah Puja*, *Sru Penu Puja*, *Dharni Penu Puja*, *Guruba Penu Puja*, *Turki Penu Puja*, and *Pitabali Puja*.

*Matiguru* involved worship of the earth before sowing seeds. *Guruba Puja*, *Turki Puja*, and in some cases *Meriah Puja* were connected with land fertility. The sources describe *Meriah Puja* as involving human sacrifice and state that it was performed to appease Dharni, the earth. *Saru penu puja* involved the sacrifice of fowls and a feast. In *Dehuri*, goats and chickens were sacrificed. *Gurba Penu Puja* and *Turki penu puja* were performed outside the village.

*Pitabali Puja* was performed by offering flowers, fruits, sandal paste, incense, ghee-lamps, ghee, sundried rice, turmeric, buffalo or a he-goat, and fowl. The Traditional Khond religion gave the highest importance to the Earth goddess, creator and sustainer of the world, whose gender changed to male as Dharni Deota. His companion was Bhatbarsi Deota, the hunting god. A buffalo was sacrificed to them once a year. Before hunting, the Khonds worshiped the spirits of the hills and valleys where they hunted. They state that remarriage was common for divorced or widowed women and men, and that marriage commonly crossed clan boundaries.

## Foodways

Khond subsistence combines hunting and gathering with agriculture, especially shifting or slash-and-burn cultivation locally called *Podu* or *podu chasa*. The economy includes trapping game and collecting wild fruits, tubers, and honey. The Khonds grow rice, lentils, beans, vegetables, and millets, and are described as excellent fruit farmers. They cultivate pineapple, oranges, turmeric, ginger, and papaya. Forest products collected include mango, jackfruit, Mahua, Guava, Tendu, and Custard apples.

Millets and beans, together with game and fish, form the staple diet. The Khonds grow native cultivars of fragrant and coloured rice, while parboiled rice is preferred as a staple. Food is usually cooked with oil extracted from sal and mahua seeds. Fish and meat are smoked for preservation. The sources also state that the Khonds use medicinal plants and remain mainly dependent on forest resources for survival.

Turmeric grown in the Kandhamal district by the Khonds is a registered Geographical Indication product. The sources describe a dietary shift from millets to carbohydrate-rich processed foods and associate it with obesity and diabetes among tribals.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Khond dormitory is an important setting for oral teaching. Adolescents learn myths, legends, stories, riddles, and proverbs through night-long communal activity involving singing and dancing. Boys learn the legends of brave and martial ancestors, while the wider process transmits social taboos, values, and the way of the tribe.

The sources identify Kuvi-Khond folktales, Kuvi folk literature, Kondh oral epics, Kondh myths, and oral epics of Kalahandi as areas of documentation. The further-reading list includes *The religion of the Kuvi-Konds, their customs and folk-lore*, *A Grammar of the Kuvi Language with Texts and Vocabulary*, *Kuvi Folk Literature*, and *Oral Epics of Kalahandi*. It also lists *Kondh Myths and Oral Epics of Odisha* and *The Creation Myth of the Kutia Kondh: A Sacred Narrative*.

## Language & religion

Khonds speak Kui and Kuvi as native languages. Both are Dravidian languages written in the Odia script and are most closely related to the Gondi language. Khond religious traditions historically combined totemism, animism, ancestor worship, shamanism, and nature worship, with sacred relationships to the Earth, mountains, rivers, the Sun, hills, valleys, and forests.

According to the 2011 census, 90.19% of the Khond population in the eight states was Hindu and 9.28% Christian; 2,578 were Muslim, 437 Buddhist, 181 Sikh, and 89 Jain. A further 3,151 followed other religions, primarily Nature worship, while 2,578 stated no religious affiliation. Many Khonds converted to Protestant Christianity in the late nineteenth and early twentieth century through the Serampore Mission, while others converted to Islam. Hindu deities including Kali, Durga, Shiva, Jagannath, Ram, Krishna, and Balram were incorporated into Khond religious practice. The practice of traditional Khond religion has practically become extinct today, but Khond tribal identity remains central to social and ethical culture.
