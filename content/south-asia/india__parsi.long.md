## Overview

The Parsis or Parsees are an ethno-religious community in the Indian subcontinent, descended from Persian refugees who migrated during and after the Arab-Islamic conquest of Iran. They live chiefly in Mumbai and in towns and villages south of Mumbai, with communities also recorded in Karachi, Chennai, Pune, Bangalore, Kolkata and Hyderabad. Parsi communities have also settled in Pakistan, China, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, the United States, the United Kingdom and Canada. According to the 2011 census of India, there were 57,264 Parsis in India. Their mother tongue is Gujarati, and the Parsi variety of Gujarati is described as a variety spoken in Gujarat and Maharashtra. Their importance in folk culture lies in the maintenance of Zoroastrian religious identity, community customs, foodways, textiles and historical traditions within Indian society.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Choli* — A blouse made of silk, metal and cotton, recorded by the British Museum as having Chinese embroidery.

*Parsi women’s outfit* — A green and red silk tunic, embroidered on the front of the skirt in several colours, worn with a pair of baggy trousers. The catalogue identifies it as part of a Zoroastrian woman’s outfit.

*Shawl* — Embroidered shawls are recorded in wool and cotton, with one catalogue entry also specifying metal thread.

*Woman’s cap* — A green cotton cap embroidered on the outside with suns, cypress trees, flowers, fish and birds. The catalogue associates these motifs with strength, fertility, abundance and life in Zoroastrian beliefs.

The sources used do not document gara embroidery, Parsi gara, sudreh, kusti, dagli, Parsi paghdi, or other named Parsi textile traditions.

**Motif vocabulary.** Suns, cypress trees, flowers, fish and birds; the catalogue associates these with strength, fertility, abundance and life in Zoroastrian beliefs.

### Clothing & dress

The museum records identify a blouse, a green and red silk tunic, a pair of baggy trousers and embroidered shawls as elements associated with Parsi or Zoroastrian clothing. The tunic is loose-fitting and has several-coloured embroidery on the front of the skirt. A green cotton cap is embroidered with motifs including suns, cypress trees, flowers, fish and birds. The sources do not describe everyday dress, men’s clothing, footwear, belts, ceremonial dress, or the use of the terms sudreh, kusti, dagli or Parsi paghdi. The sources also do not describe the occasions on which the recorded garments were worn.

### Architecture

The sources describe a fire temple at Sanjan and the later movement of the “Fire of Victory” to Navsari. They also state that continuing disputes concerning the Atash Bahram led to the fire being moved to Udvada in 1742. ## Festivals & rituals

Zoroastrianism is described as the religion of the Parsis. In ancient Persia, Zarathushtra taught that good, identified as Ohrmazd, and evil, identified as Angra Mainyu, were opposite forces. The sources describe judgment at the Chinvat bridge, with passage to Paradise, Hammistagan or Hell. They also describe a final battle between good and evil, followed by a river of fire and a post-resurrection paradise.

The sources identify the *navjote* ceremony as the formal admission of a person into the Zoroastrian religion. They state that the definition of a Parsi is generally understood to involve descent from the original Persian refugees and formal admission through this ceremony. The *Qissa-i Sanjan* says that the early community was allowed to settle in India after demonstrating similarities between its faith and local beliefs, and that it had to speak the local language, follow local marriage customs and carry no weapons. The sources do not describe annual festivals, wedding rites, funerals, birth practices, or the festivals named Navroz, Jashan or Muktad.

## Foodways

Parsi cuisine is described as the traditional cuisine of the Parsi people who migrated to the Indian subcontinent from Persia. A basic Parsi lunch consists of rice eaten with lentils or a curry. Curry may be made with coconut, and *ras* is described separately; curry is usually thicker than ras. Dinner is described as a meat dish, often accompanied by potatoes or another vegetable curry. *Kachumbar*, a sharp onion-cucumber salad, accompanies most meals.

Named dishes include *Chicken Farcha*, fried chicken; *Dhansak*, made with lamb, mutton, goat, chicken or vegetables in a mixed-lentil or toor-daal gravy served with brown rice; and *Patra ni Machhi*, fish stuffed with green coconut chutney, wrapped in a banana leaf and steam cooked. Other listed dishes are *Sali Murghi*, *Saas ni Machhi*, *Kolmi no Patio*, *Jardaloo Sali Boti*, *Khichri*, and *Tamota ni Ras Chaval*. Egg dishes include *akuri*, scrambled eggs with spices, and *pora*, a Parsi omelette.

Desserts include *sev*, *ravo*, *malido*, *faluda*, *kulfi* and *Lagan nu Custard*. Snacks include *bhakhra*, *batasa*, *dar ni pori*, *doodh na puff* and *khaman na ladva*. Traditional breakfasts during the 1930s in Mumbai and South Gujarat villages included *khurchan*, offal meats cooked with potatoes in a spicy gravy, together with fried or half-fried eggs. In agrarian communities, this was accompanied by coconut toddy. The sources also describe a present trend toward light eating, no red meat and vegetarianism.

## Oral tradition & literature

The *Qissa-i Sanjan* is a sixteenth-century Parsi epic and the foundational legend explaining the integration of the Parsis into India. It recounts the journey from Iran, the granting of refuge by a local ruler, and the settlement at Sanjan. Its account includes the story of a Parsi priest adding sugar to a vessel of milk to demonstrate that the newcomers would blend in and enrich local culture without causing disruption.

The *Qissa* is described as the only existing account of the early years of Zoroastrian refugees in India, composed at least six centuries after their tentative arrival. Its account of the landing is vague or contradictory, and three possible dates—716, 765 and 936—have been proposed. The text is nevertheless described as important for its depiction of Parsi identity and its relationship with the dominant culture.

The sources also mention the *rivayats*, epistles containing religious and social questions and replies sent between Zoroastrian priests in Gujarat and co-religionists in Iran. These transmissions span 1478–1766. The Zoroastrian holy book is the *Avesta*, written in the Avestan language.

## Language & religion

Parsis are associated with Gujarati, and the Gujarati-speaking Parsi community is described as distinct from the Dari-speaking Irani community. The Parsi variety of Gujarati was treated by Ethnologue as a separate language before 2023, with the ISO 639-3 code prp; it is now subsumed under Gujarati. The sources also mention Persian, Middle Persian, Sanskrit and Avestan. Inscriptions at the Kanheri Caves suggest that Middle Persian remained the literary language of the hereditary Zoroastrian priesthood until at least the early 11th century.

Parsis follow Zoroastrianism, also called Zarathushti Pantha. Their religious tradition includes the Avesta, the teachings of Zarathushtra, the opposition of Ohrmazd and Angra Mainyu, the Chinvat bridge, and the fire temple. The sources state that Parsi and Irani religious principles, convictions and customs have no substantial differences.
