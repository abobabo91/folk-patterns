## Overview

The Kotas, also called Kothar or Kov by self-designation, are an indigenous ethnic group of the Nilgiri Mountains in Tamil Nadu, India. They have historically been a small population, not exceeding 1,500 individuals spread across seven villages for the last 160 years. Six villages lie in the Nilgiris district of Tamil Nadu and one in the Wayanad district of Kerala. Kota villages are called *Kokkal* in the Kota language and Kotagiri by outsiders. The Kotas speak Kota, or *Ko-v Ma-nt*, a Dravidian language closely related to Toda. Their folk-culture importance is especially visible in their combination of pottery, agriculture, leather working, carpentry, blacksmithing, music, village ritual, and specialized service relationships with neighboring Toda, Kurumba, and Irula communities.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Architecture

Kota villages consisted of seven relatively large nuclear villages interspersed between Toda and Badaga settlements. Each village was organized through three exogamous clans, with each clan occupying a street called *Kerr* or *Kerri*. They do record that, since the 1940s, temples of Tamil style have been built to accommodate the worship of mainstream Hindu deities adopted into the Kota pantheon.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery and metalwork are central to Kota occupational life. Kotas were traditionally potters, blacksmiths, carpenters, leather workers, agriculturalists, and musicians serving neighboring groups. Women were exclusively engaged in making pottery. Kota religion and culture revolved around the smithy: during the seed-sowing ceremony, a forge and furnace were built within the main temple, and smiths made votive items such as axes or gold ornaments for the deity.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention gold ornaments as votive items made by smiths during the seed-sowing ceremony and silver disks as the forms in which major Kota deities were worshiped in temples.

## Music & performance

Music is one of the established traditional occupations of the Kotas, particularly in their historical service relationships with neighboring groups. Kota informants stated that they supplied battle drums during periods of war. Instrumental music is specifically associated with the village of Kolmel, where it is connected with the Kamatraya festival. The village of Thichgad is known for its women’s song and dance, while funerals are especially associated with Menad.

Music also has a religious and ritual role. Women who become possessed through flute music are called *Pembacol* and are consulted during important village decisions. The Kota religious system includes the possession of a god, *so-ym*, through the intermediary *navya-navkaru*, who assists the head priest.

Kota music historically formed part of a wider interdependent system. Kotas provided services to Toda neighbors in return for buffalo milk, hides, ghee, and meat, and maintained trading and ritual relationships with Kurumba and Irula neighbors.

## Dance & theatre

The village of Thichgad is famous for women’s song and dance.

## Festivals & rituals

Kota ritual life includes religious festivals dedicated to major deities and to particular agricultural moments. Pre-colonial Kota religious festivals included prayers to the rain god *kakakaku kana-bado* and to the titular deity *Kamatra-ya*. The Kamatraya festival is especially associated with the village of Kolmel, while village elders determine when festivals are to be held.

The seed-sowing ceremony combines agriculture, smithing, and temple ritual. A forge and furnace were built inside the main temple, and smiths produced votive axes or gold ornaments for the deity. The head priest, *mundika-no-n*, and the headman, *gotga-rn*, generally came from a particular family, or *kuyt*, and these offices passed from father to son. The *mundika-no-n* was assisted by the *navya-navkaru*, through whom the god communicated with the people while being possessed.

Kota funerals consisted of two ceremonies. The first, called the Green funeral, involved cremation of the body. The second, called the dry funeral, involved exhumation of buried bones from the first ceremony followed by the sacrifice of semi-wild buffaloes. The second funeral is no longer widely practiced.

## Foodways

Kotas ate meat, unlike Todas, and their traditional food included Italian millet, known in the Kota language as *vatamk*. It was served on almost all ceremonial occasions, while rice was the preferred daily food. Rice was served with *udk*, a sambar-like preparation made from locally available pulses, vegetables, and tamarind juice.

Beef was seldom eaten. Eggs, chicken, and mutton were consumed when available, together with locally grown vegetables and beans. The sources characterize Italian millet as a grain important throughout arid regions of India.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources record a Kota origin myth in which Kotas, Todas, and Kurumbas were placed in the Nilagiris area at once as brothers by the Kota god. They also report a Kota tradition, recorded by Friedrich Metz, that alluded to migration from a place called Kollimale in Karnatakas. These accounts form part of the historical and religious narratives associated with Kota identity. They do record extensive scholarly study of Kota language and culture, especially the work of Murray Barnson Emeneau in Dravidian linguistics and Richard Kent Wolf’s study of Kota music, time, space, and social life.

## Language & religion

Kota, or *Ko-v Ma-nt*, belongs to the Dravidian language family and is closely related to Toda. It diverged from the common South Dravidian stock in years BCE. Robert Caldwell identified it as an independent Dravidian language in the 1870s, and Murray Emeneau studied it in detail. The language preserves words showing strong affinity to Archaic Tamil. Most Kota children study Tamil at school and are bilingual in Kota and Tamil; previously, Kotas were multilingual in Kota, Toda, and Badaga.

Kota religion differs from Hinduism in its worship of non-anthropomorphic male deities and a female deity. The major deities are *A-yno-r*, divided into big, or *Doda-ynor*, and small, or *kuna-yno-r*, together with the father god and mother goddess *Amno-r*. The father god is also called *Kamati-cvara* or *Kamatra-ya* in some villages. These deities were worshiped as silver disks in specific temples. Since the 1940s, mainstream Hindu deities have also been adopted into the Kota pantheon, and temples of Tamil style have been built for their worship.
