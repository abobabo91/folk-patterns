## Overview

The Warli, also called Varli, are an indigenous Adivasi people of western India. They live in mountainous and coastal areas along the Maharashtra–Gujarat border and surrounding areas. The Warli speak Varli, an unwritten Indo-Aryan language usually classified as Marathi, though it is sometimes classified as Konkani or Bhil. Their folk-cultural importance in the sources lies chiefly in their ritual wall paintings, which use a restricted graphic vocabulary of circles, triangles and squares, and in the historical and social practices represented in those paintings. The sources also describe their animistic beliefs, later adoption of Hindu beliefs through acculturation, agricultural life, marriage customs and organized resistance to colonial and landlord exploitation.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name the circle, triangle and square as the basic graphic vocabulary of Warli wall painting. The circle represents the sun and the moon; the triangle derives from mountains and pointed trees; the square indicates a sacred enclosure or a piece of land.

### Clothing & dress

The sources provide only limited information about Warli dress. Warli women wear toe rings and necklaces as signs of being married.

### Architecture

Warli ritual paintings are usually made inside huts. The walls are constructed from a mixture of branches, earth and cow dung, producing a red ochre background. They do state that the Warli traditionally lived together in small-scale groups led by a headman.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do describe a bamboo stick chewed at the end so that it becomes supple like a paintbrush. This implement is used to apply the white pigment of Warli wall paintings, which is made from rice paste and water with gum as a binding material.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources document Warli women’s toe rings and necklaces, which signify that the wearer is married.

## Music & performance

They do describe dances as subjects represented in Warli ritual paintings. These paintings also depict hunting, fishing, farming, festivals, trees and animals.

Warli wall painting is itself associated with ritual and social occasions. The paintings are made inside huts and are produced for special occasions such as weddings or harvests. Until the late 1970s, this artistic practice was preserved by women. In the 1970s, Jivya Soma Mashe and his son Balu Mashe began painting outside the context of a specific ritual, pursuing painting as an artistic activity.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is mentioned only as one of the subjects shown in Warli ritual paintings, alongside hunting, fishing, farming, festivals, trees and animals.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that Warli wall paintings are made for special occasions such as weddings and harvests. In these ritual paintings, the central form is the square, known as the *chauk* or *chaukat*. The sources identify two principal types: **Devchauk** and **Lagnachauk**.

Inside a **Devchauk** appears **Palaghata**, the mother goddess, who symbolizes fertility. Male gods are unusual in the paintings and are frequently associated with spirits that have taken human form. The square and the scenes surrounding it form the central structure of each ritual painting. The surrounding imagery includes hunting, fishing, farming, festivals and dances.

They do state that marriage is marked in women’s dress by toe rings and necklaces. Some Warli practice polygyny. The sources also describe harvests as occasions for ritual painting.

## Foodways

The sources state that contemporary Warli are mainly agriculturists and cultivate crops including rice and wheat.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Warli language is described as unwritten.

## Language & religion

Varali, also written Warali, Warli and Varli, is an Indo-Aryan language spoken by the Warli people. The sources describe it as unwritten. It is usually classified as Marathi, but is sometimes classified as Konkani or Bhil. The Warli language belongs to the southern zone of the Indo-Aryan languages.

The Warli have their own animistic beliefs, life, customs and traditions. Through acculturation, they have adopted many Hindu beliefs. In Warli ritual paintings, **Palaghata** is represented as a mother goddess associated with fertility. Male gods are unusual in these paintings and are frequently related to spirits that have taken human shape.
