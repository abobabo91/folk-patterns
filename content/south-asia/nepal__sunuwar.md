## Overview

The Sunuwar, also known as Koinch or Kõinchs, are a Tibeto-Burman ethnic group and a Kirati tribe native to Nepal, parts of India including West Bengal and Sikkim, and southern Bhutan. The group speaks the Sunuwar language, also called Koinch, a Kiranti language of the Sino-Tibetan language family. The Kõinchs number 82,705 in total; the 2011 census of Nepal recorded 55,712 Sunuwar, or 0.2% of Nepal’s population. Sunuwar-speaking communities are concentrated around the river banks of Likhu Khola in the Okhaldhū۠ngā and Rāmechāp districts. Their folk-culture documentation in the sources concerns language, writing systems, religion, and social customs.

## Music & performance

The Sunuwar language article records that Sunwar people called “Khangsa” sign language with voice and direct action for foreign people who do not understand Sunuwar, but it provides no further account of its performance or cultural setting.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources state that, according to the 2001 census of Nepal, 17% of the tribe followed the Kirant religion and adopted the Mundhum (Kiranti) culture.

## Foodways

The language article states that Sunuwar villages visit neighboring village markets to purchase goods such as spices, sugar, tea, and salt.

## Language & religion

Sunuwar, or Koinch, is a Kiranti language of the Sino-Tibetan language family spoken in Nepal and India by the Sunuwar people. It is also known as Kõits Lo, Kiranti-Kõits, Mukhiya, and Koic. About 40,000 speakers are residing in eastern Nepal. The language is spoken around Sunuwar villages near Likhu Khola, including Vacul, Pahare, and Kũbhu Kãsthālī; the majority of Sunwar speakers live between Pahare and Vacul.

The Sunuwar alphabet, also called the Jenticha script or Koĩts script, was promoted in 1932 by Karna Bahadur Sunuwar, who lived from 1926 to 1991. It received official recognition in Sikkim and Eastern Nepal, where it is taught in schools. The script has 35 base letters and its own decimal digits. Tikamuli is an alternative writing system developed by Tikaram Mulicha; its use is limited to Nepal, while the wider community prefers the Koits script. Most literate speakers in Nepal use the Devanagari abugida. The sources also state that Sunuwar had no traditional written language in Nepal.

The sources identify the Kirant religion and Mundhum (Kiranti) culture.
