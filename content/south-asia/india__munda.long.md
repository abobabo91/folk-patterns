## Overview

The Munda are an Austroasiatic-speaking ethnic group of the Indian subcontinent whose native language is Mundari, a member of the Munda subgroup. They are concentrated in the eastern Indian states of Jharkhand, West Bengal, and Odisha, particularly in districts including Khunti, Ranchi, Simdega, Paschim Singhbhum, Gumla, Purbi Singhbhum, Ramghar, Sundargarh, Sambalpur, Jalpaiguri, Paschim Medinipur, and North 24 Parganas. They also live in Chhattisgarh, Bihar, Assam, Tripura, Mizoram, Bangladesh, and Nepal. Munda languages are spoken by about eleven million people in India, Bangladesh, and Nepal. Munda culture includes seasonal agricultural festivals, clan-based social organisation, dance, song, oral traditions, ritual stone erection, and distinctive village administration.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no Munda textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The museum catalogue records a girl’s apron made of cotton, described as fringed and having two plaited ties. It also records a boy’s short-sleeved cotton shirt and a boy’s cotton loin-cloth with tassels.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum catalogue records a steel and bamboo halberd, bamboo bows, and several bamboo arrows. The arrows have feather flights and either broad bamboo bolts or tanged metal heads; some bindings and painted surfaces are described as black. Other catalogue records include a metal comb surmounted by a bird, a bone spoon with a handle terminating in an elephant, a bone spoon formerly having such a handle, a metal tobacco-container with a wooden stopper, and an oil lamp carved from local sandstone. The lamp has a circular basin for oil, a pointed spout, a flared geometric section, and notches along its edges. Silver-alloy bowls or salt-cellars were beaten out from rupee coins.

## Music & performance

The Munda have folk songs, dances, tales, and traditional musical instruments. Both sexes participate in dances at social events and festivals. The sources identify the *naqareh* as a principal musical instrument and state that Munda terms for dance and song are *susun* and *durang*, respectively. Named folk dances include **Jadur**, **Karam Susun**, and **Mage Susun**. Mundari music is described as similar to the music of Sadan. The winter *Mundari Mage* song has a rhythm similar to the *Nagpuri Fagua* song, whose seasonal association is described as winter and spring.

The sources also describe music and dance as part of social and festival life. Munda enjoy marriage ceremonies with feasts, drinks, and dance, while seasonal agricultural festivals provide further contexts for performance. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription concerns this ethnic group.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name **Jadur**, **Karam Susun**, and **Mage Susun** as Munda folk dances. Both sexes participate in dances at social events and festivals, and dance is also part of marriage celebrations.

## Festivals & rituals

The Munda celebrate the seasonal agricultural festivals **Mage Parab**, **Phagu**, **Karam**, **Baha parab**, **Sarhul**, and **Sohrai**. Some seasonal festivals coincide with religious festivals, but the sources state that their original meaning remains. The Munda deity is **Singbonga**.

Munda ritual life includes ceremonies connected with birth, death, engagement, and marriage. Munda practice clan exogamy and tribal endogamy, and monogamy is the norm. Bride price is prevalent. A marriage ceremony begins with **Sagai** and ends with **Bidai**, and the occasion includes a feast, drinks, and dance. The sources describe *Patthalgari*, or stone erection, as an old tradition among Munda people of Jharkhand. A large inverted U-shaped dressed headstone is buried at the head of a grave or at the entrance to a village, and the family tree of the dead person is inscribed on it.

The sources distinguish several forms of *patthalgari*: **Horadiri**, a stone on which the family tree is written; **Chalpadiri** or **Saasandiri**, a stone marking a village boundary and its limits; **Magodiri**, a headstone for a social criminal who committed polygamy or an unsocial marriage; and **Ziddiri**, a stone placed over the burial of a placenta and the dried navel part of a newborn.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Munda have folk songs and tales. The sources state that folk literatures are abundant among Munda languages, while written literature before the 19th century was lacking.

John-Baptist Hoffmann studied the language, customs, religion, and life of the Munda people. With the help of Menas Orea, he published the 15-volume *Encyclopaedia Mundarica*. Its first edition was published posthumously in 1937, and a third edition appeared in 1976. Hoffmann also published the first Mundari language grammar in 1903. *The Mundas and Their Country* by S. C. Roy was published in 1912. *Adidharam*, by Ram Dayal Munda and Ratan Singh Manki, describes Munda rituals and customs in Mundari with a Hindi translation.

## Language & religion

Mundari is the native language of the Munda people and belongs to the Munda subgroup of the Austroasiatic language family. Munda languages are spoken in India, Bangladesh, and Nepal and are divided into North Munda and South Munda branches in one classification. North Munda includes Korku and the Kherwarian languages. The sources identify Mundari, Ho, Santali, Korku, Kharia, Sora, Juang, Gtaʔ, Remo, Birhor, and Gorum among the Munda languages.

The Munda languages generally have head-final clause structure, no grammatical gender as a productive category, numeral classifiers, and synthetic and agglutinating structures. Multilingualism is widespread, with Hindi, Bengali, Sardi, Odia, Marathi, Telugu, and other regional languages used in administration, trade, and education. The Munda deity is Singbonga. The sources also describe religious elements in marriage, including Sindurdaan and the use of turmeric, as Hindu elements borrowed into Munda tradition.
