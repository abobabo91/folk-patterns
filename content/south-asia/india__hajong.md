## Overview

The Hajong are an ethnic group of Northeast India and northern parts of Bangladesh. Most Hajongs live in India, where they are predominantly rice-farmers and have the status of a Scheduled Tribe. They are the fourth largest tribal ethnicity in Meghalaya. Their settlements extend through the foothills of the Garo Hills and Khasi-Jaintia Hills, including parts of Assam, Meghalaya, and Bangladesh. In India, Hajongs live in the Garo and Khasi Hills of Meghalaya, the South-West Garo Hills District, and the Dhubri, Goalpara, and Dhemaji districts of Assam, extending into Arunachal Pradesh. In Bangladesh, they are found mainly in the northern Dhaka division and along the borderland from Sherpur district to Sunamganj district. Hajong is an Indo-Aryan language with Tibeto-Burman roots, spoken by more than 175,000 ethnic Hajongs according to one source. Their folk culture is closely connected with rice farming, weaving, basketry, woodwork, music, religious rites, and seasonal festivals.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Pathin** — *Pathin* is a horizontally striped, colourful rectangular cloth worn by women as a wrap-around skirt covering the body from the bust to the calf. Its stripes alternate between different colours, with red stripes and thick horizontal borders. The textile has two principal stripe sets: the *kan*, at the upper and lower ends, and the *gao*, the larger central portion.

**Rangapathin** — *Rangapathin* is a pathin in which red is the principal colour. It is associated in the source with young women. Middle-aged women tend to wear pathins with fewer stripes in shades of green.

**Bana** — Every Hajong household traditionally has a loom called *bana*. Two types are named: *salbana* and *sipnibana*. The sipnibana is operated solely by hand and does not require the use of the feet.

**Kompes** — *Kompes* is a brocaded scarf chiefly used by men. Women also use it to tie the waist, and men keep their necks warm with it during winter.

**Argon** — *Argon* is a traditional brocaded shawl used by both men and women to cover the body during winter.

**Pasra** — *Pasra* is an embroidered light shawl.

**Gamsa** — *Gamsa* is a garment similar to the Assamese gamosa.

Women’s weaving is an important household activity. Hajong women weave clothing for themselves, their children, and other family members. Knowledge of weaving was traditionally regarded as a requirement for maidens before marriage, although this expectation is described as less strictly followed under Western influence.

**Motif vocabulary.** The named textile structure consists of *kan*, the upper and lower stripe sections, and *gao*, the larger central stripe section.

### Clothing & dress

Women chiefly wear *pathin*, a wrap-around skirt extending from the bust to the calf. In the source, women of the upper class are described as wearing a long pathin reaching the floor, while women of the lower class wear a shorter form reaching the ankle. The pathin is horizontally striped and colourful, with alternate layers between red stripes and thick horizontal borders. It is also called *pate* or *pâthni*. Young women wear the red-dominant *rangapathin*, while middle-aged women tend to wear pathins with fewer stripes in green shades.

When working in the fields, women use *kompes* as a *banong*, or belt. Men wear *ningti*, also called *bhijâ kapur*, as a woven cloth worn in the fashion of a dhoti. Men also wear *buksuli*, the traditional shirt. During winter, both men and women use the brocaded shawl *Argon*, while men use *kompes* to keep the neck warm. Other named garments include *gamsa*, similar to the Assamese gamosa, and *pasra*, an embroidered light shawl.

### Architecture

Traditional Hajong houses consist of separate buildings arranged around a courtyard. Households customarily construct four or five houses on the sides of the courtyard, protecting its middle portion. The courtyard is used for religious rites and, in every household, for threshing grain after harvest. The kitchen is separate from the main dwelling.

A typical house has an earthen plinth and two thatched roofs on both sides. Bamboos and timbers are used occasionally; floors are earthen, and walls are made from split bamboo plastered with cow dung. The main dwelling and dining hall is the *Bhat ghor*, also called *mâijâ ghor*. Other named buildings are the *Akhli ghor*, or kitchen; *Kasri ghor*, a dormitory with provision for guests; *Khupra ghor*, also called *jura ghor*, a bedroom for a married son or daughter; *Chang ghor*, a granary; *Dhiki ghor*, a husking house; *Guli ghor*, a cattle shed; and *Dyao ghor*, a shrine for household deities. The house of an *Udhikâri* is an exception to the otherwise similar village house pattern. *Mak Mas* and *Phalgun Mas* are described as favourable months for constructing a new house.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that Hajongs are skilled in woodwork and basketry and make their farming tools and household items themselves. Their material culture includes implements needed for rice farming and many bamboo fishing implements.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Most Hajong ornaments are made of silver, although gold, ivory, coral, and conch shells are also used. Married women wear conch-shell bangles called *haka* and the wedding ring *manik angthi*. Men traditionally wear wedding rings called *manik angthi* and a gold chain; men may also wear a red waist thread called *bâstâ* or *bâitâ*, *lugun* over the left shoulder, and rosaries made of sandalwood, golden apple, and holy basil.

Named women’s ornaments include *Galahicha*, a torc; *Mugâ mala*, a gold necklace with alternating red and black beads; *Gujurâti* or *Harsura*, a floral chain of gold or silver; *Chondrohar* or *Sunchisura*, a silver necklace with three to five floral chain rows; and *Sikâ mala*, a necklace made of coins. Other ornaments are *Katbaju* armlets, *Nol Kharu* bangles, *Buila* silver bangles, *Bak kharu* open ankle rings, *Bak Gunjuri* bell-bearing ankle rings, *Bonko* zigzag anklets, *Koromphul* earrings, *Kankurya* curved earrings, *Kanpasa* flat hooked earrings, *Not* nose rings worn on the left side by married women, and *Nolok* septum nose rings, including *titlipata*, *kumrâbisi*, and *jibâli*.

## Music & performance

Traditional Hajong music includes *gitâlu gahen*, *gupni gahen*, and songs connected with agriculture and religious rites. The named instruments are *Dhuluk*, a broad drum with membranes at each end played from both ends; *Basi*, a flute; *Khul*, a pair of small brass cymbals; *Dutra*, a stringed instrument; *Dhapa kurtal*, a pair of large cymbals; *Hurindo*, a fiddle; *Hamuktal*, an instrument made with apple snail shells; and *Gugna*, a lamellophone consisting of a flexible bamboo tongue attached to a frame.

Music and singing also form part of the festival called *Chorkhila* in India, known as *chormaga* in Mymensingh. During Chorkhila, groups of young people go from house to house or from village to village, playing music and singing folk songs. They sometimes perform stories from the *Ramayana*. In return, the groups receive rice or money. The source presents this performance both as entertainment and as a social occasion at which young people can observe prospective brides and grooms. Traditional songs also accompany agricultural activities and religious rites.

## Dance & theatre

They describe Chorkhila performances in which groups of young people travel between houses or villages while playing music, singing folk songs, and sometimes presenting stories from the *Ramayana*.

## Festivals & rituals

*Pusnâ* is one of the most important Hajong festivals. It marks the end of winter and the month of Pus and celebrates Makar Sankranti, with feasts lasting for a week. Hajongs also celebrate Hindu festivals including Durga Puja and Kamakhya Puja.

Several traditional festivals and rites are connected with local religious practice. *Bastu pujâ* does not involve idol worship. It is held outside the village in an area called *Bastu hali* or *Bastu than*, where tortoises and pigeons are sacrificed for *Bastu dyao*. *Chorkhila* is celebrated during October in the South-West Garo Hills District of Meghalaya. Groups of young people perform music, songs, folk stories, and sometimes episodes from the *Ramayana*.

The Hajongs celebrate the pre-monsoon harvest festival *Biswâ*. *Kani pujâ* and *Kâtkâ pujâ* are performed on the last day of the month of Srabon and Kati. The day of Sharad Purnima is called *Kujâi Ghor*. Traditional rituals are performed by a *Dyushi* or *Nungtang*, a Hajong shaman.

The sources describe birth customs as following Hindu customs. Marriage is usually arranged through negotiation, although romantic love and widow remarriage are allowed. Monogamy is prevalent; polygyny is rare. Marriage outside the tribe is strictly forbidden, and the community gives a bride price called *pon* or *khalti*.

## Foodways

Rice is the staple food, eaten with lentils and vegetables. On special occasions, rice is ground into fine powder and used to make steamed or fried rice cakes called *pithâ*. Tortoise has traditionally been a favourite meat. The sources also state that one religious section, the *Khatal*, prohibits the use and preparation of rice beer.

Named dishes include *Dingpura*, sweet rice cooked in a special type of bamboo; *Libahak*, dishes made with ground rice; *Bukni Bhat*, fermented rice; and *Bisi Bhat*, steamed sticky and sweet rice. *Bhâtuwahak* consists of dishes made with rice flour and fermented fish. *Putâmas* are small fishes steamed in banana leaves, while *Chunsâhak* is a cooked vegetable prepared for a special guest. *Tupla Bhat* is rice cooked in banana leaves. *Kharpani* is a vegetable boiled with dried fish and soda. *Chungâhak* refers to dishes cooked in bamboo.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Hajongs have no recorded history, and the historical references described in the source take the form of legends, folktales, and traditional beliefs. Hajong folklore tells of ancestral migration from Hajo and of settlement in the foothills of the Garo Hills and Khasi-Jaintia Hills. According to a Hajong legend, the people are *Suryawanshi*, called *Surjobungsi* in Hajong, and descend from *Surjodyao* or *Bila*, the Sun god.

Folklore also describes habitation beginning at the Hajong village *Hwârkuna* in the northeastern foothills of the Garo Hills and ending at *Jumakuna* in the southeastern foothills of the Jaintia Hills. These traditions are connected with the account of twelve thousand Hajongs who fled Hajo and settled in the northern foothills of the Garo Hills.

During Chorkhila, performers sing folk songs and sometimes tell or enact stories from the *Ramayana*.

## Language & religion

Hajong is classified as an Indo-Aryan language with Tibeto-Burman roots or a possible Tibeto-Burman substratum. It has similarities with Assamese and Bengali and is spoken across parts of Northeast India and Bangladesh. One source states that it is spoken by more than 175,000 ethnic Hajongs, while the separate language article gives approximately 80,000. Hajong has 23 consonant phonemes, 8 vowel phonemes, and 2 approximants. It is written in the Eastern Nagari, Bengali-Assamese, Bengali, Assamese, and Latin scripts, according to the different descriptions in the sources. The language has Sanskrit loanwords and includes the vowel /ɯ/, represented in Latin script by “w” and in Eastern Nagari by “অৗ”.

Hajongs are predominantly Hindu. Their present religious customs combine Hinduism with an older folk religion and traditional animistic religion, producing a syncretic form of Hinduism. Two documented sections are *Khatal* and *Hajong*. The Khatal section is divided into *Bastom* and *Khutri*; the hereditary *Udhikâri* priest class belongs to Bastom. Traditional rituals are performed by a *Dyushi* or *Nungtang*.
