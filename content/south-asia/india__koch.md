## Overview

The Koch are a small trans-border ethnic group of Assam and Meghalaya in India and northern Bangladesh. They consist of nine ethno-linguistic groups: Tintekiya, Wanang, Koch-Rabha/Kocha, Harigaya, Margan, Chapra, Satpari, Sankar and Banai. The groups are generally endogamous, while their clans, called *nikini*, are matrilineal and strictly exogamous. Some Koch preserve the Boro-Garo language called Koch, while others use local Indo-Aryan varieties. Koch is a Sino-Tibetan language spoken mainly in Meghalaya, Lower Assam and northern Bangladesh. The group is significant for the survival and promotion of its language, animistic religions, non-Hindu customs and traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

The Koch people who have preserved their languages are described as following animistic religions and non-Hindu customs and traditions.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources mention Koch language-promotion organizations and materials. Koch Krorang Mathop in Tura, Meghalaya publishes Koch materials, including books and an annual Koch magazine, while Koch Development Forum works to promote the Koch language.

## Language & religion

Koch is a Sino-Tibetan language spoken by the Koch people of India and Bangladesh, especially in Meghalaya, Lower Assam and northern Bangladesh. It is written with Assamese, Bengali and Roman scripts. The language is spoken in parts of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Bangladesh, and the sources describe five surviving dialects: Wanang, Tintikiya, Harigaya, Chapra and Margan. The Koch groups use their own mother tongues within the home and group, often use the Harigaya variety among Koch groups, and use Hajong, Assamese, Bengali, Garo, Hindi and English outside the community. The language is described as “Definitely Endangered.” The Koch people discussed here preserve animistic religions and follow non-Hindu customs and traditions.
