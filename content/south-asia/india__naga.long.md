## Overview

The Naga are not a single people but a cluster of related hill communities—Angami, Ao, Sumi, Lotha, Konyak, Chakhesang, Phom, Rengma, Zeliang, Tangkhul, Mao, Khiamniungan and others—spread across the Patkai ranges where the Indian states of Nagaland, Manipur, Arunachal Pradesh and Assam meet Myanmar's Sagaing Region and Chin State. Their languages all belong to the Tibeto-Burman family but are often mutually unintelligible even between neighbouring villages, which is why the Assamese-based pidgin *Nagamese* and, increasingly, English serve as bridges; Kohima, the Angami hill town that became Nagaland's capital, is the conventional centre of gravity. Total population is commonly estimated at two and a half to three million. Historically each village was a sovereign political unit fortified on a ridge, governed either by a council of elders (as among the Angami and Ao) or by a hereditary chief of near-sacral status (as among the Konyak, whose *Angh* held rights over land, labour and marriage). Warfare between villages, and the taking of heads as a source of fertility-bearing potency, shaped almost everything that survives as material culture—the stripe codes of shawls, the boar-tusk and cane armlets awarded for valour, the brass heads worn on necklaces, the carved posts of the men's houses. British expeditions from the 1830s onward gradually brought the hills under administration; American Baptist missionaries, arriving from Assam in the same century, converted the great majority within a few generations, so that Nagaland today reports one of the highest Christian proportions of any territory in Asia. The material legacy is distinctive out of all proportion to the population: narrow warp-striped shawls woven on the back-strap loom, in which colour, band count and painted or embroidered figures declared a man's feasts, hunts and killings; mithun (the semi-domesticated bovine *Bos frontalis*) rendered as a stylised pair of curving horns on everything from house gables to woven bands; the hornbill, whose tail feathers crowned warriors and whose beak became a headdress ornament; carnelian and conch traded up from the plains and worn as heirloom wealth; and enormous hollowed log drums that once announced raids, deaths and harvests to the whole valley.

## Material culture

Naga material culture is built from what the hill forests and the plains trade routes supplied: cotton and nettle fibre, cane and bamboo, wood, bone, boar tusk, hornbill feather and beak, cowrie and conch shell, carnelian and glass beads, and brass obtained in exchange. Almost every object is also a statement of rank, because in a society without writing, entitlement was carried on the body and on the house.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Naga textile is a warp-striped rectangular cloth woven in narrow panels on a back-strap tension loom and stitched together, and its stripes are read rather than merely admired.

- **Back-strap loom band** (*Naga back-strap band*) — Narrow warp-faced panel woven on a loin loom whose tension comes from a strap around the weaver's back; panel widths of roughly a foot mean most shawls and body-cloths are two or three panels seamed together.
- **Angami loin loom** — The Angami working of this loom, associated with tightly beaten warp-faced cloth in black, red and white, and the source of the region's best-known heavy shawls.
- **Lohe shawl** (*lohe*) — Angami shawl type whose broad bands and stripe arrangements mark the wearer's standing, with the most restricted variants reserved for men who had completed the appropriate feasts.
- **Tsungkotepsu** (*tsungkotepsu*) — The celebrated Ao men's shawl: a dark ground with a median white band on which figures are painted in black—mithun, tiger, elephant, cock, human heads and sometimes a spear—each denoting an entitlement, so that the cloth is effectively a worn curriculum vitae. Traditionally it could be worn only by a man who had taken heads or given a feast of merit.
- **Ao warrior shawl** — The broader family of Ao men's cloths of which *tsungkotepsu* is the highest grade, graded downward through plainer striped versions for men without such claims.
- **Sumi ahu** (*ahu*) — Sumi shawl of banded construction, with rank-graded varieties; Sumi weaving is noted for dense red and black banding.
- **Lotha shawl** — Lotha men's cloths of which the most prestigious grades correspond to the number of feasts given, the highest commonly carrying embroidered or appliquéd animal figures.
- **Phom red shawl** — Phom cloth in deep red with dark bands, in some accounts a mark of a man who had taken part in successful raiding.
- **Rengma azhu** (*azhu*) — Rengma shawl type, likewise graded, with the senior versions restricted to men of demonstrated status.
- **Mithun horn motif** — The paired inward-curving horn, the single most pervasive Naga design element, appearing woven, carved, cast and painted; it stands for the wealth that mithun represent and for the feasts at which they were sacrificed.
- **Human-head motif** — Schematic frontal head, sometimes in rows, carried on textiles, brass ornaments and house carving; a reference to head-taking and to the fertility believed to follow from it, retained after Christianisation as an ancestral emblem rather than a live claim.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress was minimal in the warm hill climate and elaborate on ceremonial occasions, when the whole body became a display of rank.

- **Angami kilt** — Short black or dark cotton wrap worn by Angami men around the waist, in senior versions ornamented with lines of cowrie shells whose arrangement signalled success in war or in love.
- **Mekhela** (*Chakhesang ceremonial mekhela*) — Woman's wrapped body-cloth or skirt, worn tubular or as a wrap; the Chakhesang ceremonial versions are among the most richly banded, worn with a matching shawl at festivals and weddings.
- **Zeliang bead apron** — Small waist apron or skirt panel densely covered with glass and shell beadwork, worn by Zeliang (Zeme–Liangmai) women and girls at dances.
- **Hornbill feather headdress** — Warrior's headdress mounting the black-and-white tail feathers of the great hornbill, often on a cane or basketry cap trimmed with bear or goat hair, boar tusk and sometimes a hornbill beak; the number of feathers was itself a count of achievement. Hunting pressure and conservation law mean wooden and synthetic substitutes are now general.
- **Sash and baldric** — Woven or cowrie-covered sashes worn diagonally across the chest with shawl and kilt, part of the full male dance costume together with spear and dao.

### Architecture

Naga villages were built for defence—on ridge spurs, behind stone walls and carved wooden gates—and their monumental architecture is the men's house rather than any temple.

- **Morung** (*morung*; *ariju*, *dekachang* and other local names) — The bachelors' dormitory and men's house: a large gable-roofed timber-and-thatch hall in which unmarried young men slept, learned oral tradition, craft and warfare, and kept the log drum and trophy heads. It functioned as school, barracks and clubhouse at once, and its decline under missionary discouragement is the single largest rupture in Naga social architecture.
- **Morung carving** — Carved and often painted decoration of the morung's front posts, bargeboards and king-post: mithun heads, hornbills, tigers, human figures and rows of heads, executed in high relief on massive timbers.
- **Mithun skull frieze** — Row of mithun skulls or carved skull motifs mounted on the façade of a rich man's house or morung, recording the animals sacrificed at his feasts of merit; carved house-horns or crossed gable finials serve the same function.
- **Village gate** (*kharu* among the Angami) — Monolithic carved wooden gate set in the stone village wall, decorated with mithun horns, human heads and weapons, closed at night against raiders.
- **Chief's house** — Among the Konyak, the *Angh*'s house is the largest structure in the village, with an exceptionally long ridge, deep front verandah and the heaviest concentration of carving and trophies.
- **Standing stones** — Menhirs and stone seats erected to commemorate feasts of merit and the dead, ranged along village approaches and on ceremonial platforms.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery is comparatively modest and locally distributed; the striking work is in cane and bamboo basketry, in wood and in traded or cast brass.

- **Naga log drum** — Hollowed-out tree trunk, sometimes many metres long and carved at one end as a buffalo or mithun head, housed at the morung or on an open platform and struck with wooden beaters by a team of men; used to signal raids, deaths, fire and harvest, and to accompany group chant. Hauling a newly cut drum into the village was itself a major communal rite.
- **Naga brass hornbill** — Cast or sheet brass hornbill figures and beak-shaped ornaments, mounted on headgear or worn as pendants; brass was an imported prestige metal worked into heads, hornbills and discs.
- **Dao** (*dao*) — Broad-bladed working and fighting knife of iron with a wooden handle, carried in a bamboo or wooden sheath; the universal tool for clearing jungle, building and butchering as well as the warrior's weapon.
- **Cane and bamboo basketry** — Conical carrying baskets borne on the back with a head strap, storage baskets, mats, rain shields and the cane rings and helmets that armoured a warrior; cane work is the most ubiquitous Naga craft and survives most strongly.
- **Wood carving and furniture** — Carved wooden mortars, seats, headrests, rice-beer vessels, and the sculpted figures set up at graves and on commemorative platforms.
- **Rice-beer vessels** — Gourds, bamboo tubes and wooden or pottery containers for the fermented rice beer central to every hospitality and ritual occasion.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Naga ornament is heavy, frontal and legible: what a person wears states what they, or their forebears, have done.

- **Konyak headhunter necklace** — Necklace of brass or bronze human heads, in some forms one head per killing, strung on cord or beads; the definitive Konyak male ornament and the object that most directly recalls the head-taking complex.
- **Naga carnelian bead necklace** — Multi-strand necklaces of orange-red carnelian beads traded up from the Indian plains, often combined with glass and shell; heirloom wealth transmitted between generations and a common bride-price component.
- **Conch-shell armlet** — Thick white armlet or bracelet cut from marine conch, obtained through long-distance trade and therefore a marker of both wealth and reach.
- **Cane-and-boar-tusk armlet** — Upper-arm ornament of tightly coiled cane set with the curving tusks of a wild boar, worn by men and in most communities an explicit badge of hunting or martial achievement.
- **Ear and hair ornament** — Ear ornaments of horn, shell, glass, brass or hornbill feather quills; and, among Konyak and Phom men, tattooed faces and chests recording success in raiding—a practice that ended with headhunting itself, leaving only elderly bearers by the twenty-first century.
- **Cowrie ornament** — Cowrie shells sewn in rows on kilts, sashes and baldrics, their patterns coded to particular achievements.

## Music & performance

Naga music is overwhelmingly vocal and collective, and its most distinctive feature is polyphony: unaccompanied multi-part singing in which a leader's line is answered by a group in drone or in parallel parts, performed while working, hauling logs, or feasting. Songs are the principal archive of genealogy, war and migration. Instruments are comparatively few but characteristic: the log drum struck in interlocking patterns; bamboo mouth organs and flutes; jaw harps; a single-string fiddle or bamboo zither in some communities; the *tati* or similar one-stringed instrument of the Angami; buffalo-horn trumpets used for signalling; and bamboo clappers and struck tubes. The Tetseo Sisters and similar contemporary groups have taken Chakhesang polyphonic song onto national stages, while the Baptist chapel introduced four-part hymnody that Naga congregations adopted with such enthusiasm that choral singing is now itself a Naga specialism, with village and church choirs competing at festival level. No Naga tradition is currently inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, though Naga song and the log-drum complex are frequently cited in Indian national heritage documentation.

## Dance & theatre

Naga dance is martial, grounded and performed in lines or circles by costumed groups rather than by soloists. Men dance with spear and *dao* in hand, in full feather headdress, sash and kilt, advancing in a stamping crouch with sharp shouts and abrupt shifts of direction that mimic the approach and ambush of a raid; the war dance of the Angami and the Konyak varieties are the best-known forms. Women's dances are more contained, performed in rows with linked arms or hands on shoulders, with a swaying step and sustained song. Hunting and harvest mimes—tracking, felling, sowing, threshing, the hauling of the log drum—are common, and at several festivals the whole sequence of the agricultural year is danced through. There is no tradition of scripted theatre, masked drama or puppetry comparable to that of mainland Asia; dramatic content is carried instead by mime within dance and by the recitation and enactment of narrative in the morung. Since the 1990s, staged competitive performance at the Hornbill Festival has become the main arena in which village troupes rehearse, standardise and display these dances.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual year is agricultural, and the ritual career is the feast of merit. Each community has its own principal festivals: *Sekrenyi* of the Angami, a purification festival in February at which men ritually bathe and the village renews itself; *Moatsu* of the Ao, held after sowing in early May; *Tsungremmong*, the Ao harvest festival; *Tuluni* of the Sumi in July, marked by exchanged rice beer and meat; *Aoleang* of the Konyak, the spring new-year festival of the first days of April; *Tokhu Emong* of the Lotha and *Ngada* of the Rengma after harvest; and *Lui-ngai-ni*, the seed-sowing festival of the Nagas of Manipur. Overarching all of these is the **Hornbill Festival**, staged annually at Kisama near Kohima from 1 to 10 December, a state-organised gathering at which every recognised tribe presents dance, song, crafts, architecture in the form of replica morungs, and food—an invention of the late 1990s that has become the principal instrument of Naga cultural self-presentation. The older individual rite was the **feast of merit**, in which a man of means sacrificed mithun and distributed meat and rice beer to the entire village, acquiring in return the right to wear particular shawls and ornaments, to add carved horns to his house gable, and to erect a standing stone; the sequence could be repeated at ascending grades over a lifetime, and it was the mechanism by which wealth was converted into rank and redistributed. Christianisation ended the sacrificial and head-taking dimensions, and ended most divination and genna observance (ritual prohibition days), but left the festivals themselves, now reframed as cultural and often opened with prayer and choral hymn.

## Foodways

The Naga diet rests on rice—wet-terraced in the Angami country, where the terraces of Khonoma and Kohima are among the most elaborate in the eastern Himalaya, and grown by shifting jhum cultivation elsewhere—eaten with boiled and smoked meats, foraged greens, bamboo shoot and chilli. The two flavours that define the cuisine are fermentation and heat: *axone* or *akhuni*, fermented soybean paste, pungent and cooked with smoked pork; *bastenga* or fermented bamboo shoot, sour and used in the same way; dried and smoked pork and beef cooked simply with these ferments and little oil; and the *raja mircha* or king chilli (bhut jolokia), one of the hottest cultivars in the world, used fresh, dried or in chutney. Pork is the prestige meat and smoked pork with fermented bamboo shoot the most emblematic dish; dog, and in some communities other game, are eaten in certain areas, a practice that draws periodic national controversy. Rice beer—*zutho* or *zu* among the Angami, *madhu* generically—is brewed from rice or millet and is the medium of all hospitality, oath-taking and feasting, though Nagaland's prohibition law and Baptist temperance have pushed it to the margins of public life. Cooking is predominantly boiling and smoking rather than frying, and meat is preserved above the hearth through the year.

## Oral tradition & literature

Before the Roman script arrived with the missionaries, everything was oral, and the morung was the institution that transmitted it: young men learned genealogies, migration narratives, war songs, riddles, proverbs and the etiquette of feasts from elders across years of shared sleeping quarters. Most Naga groups trace descent from a common point of dispersal, frequently identified with a site remembered as *Makhel* or with the stone at Khezhakeno, from which the ancestral groups are said to have separated; origin narratives commonly involve emergence from stone or from the earth, and a tiger, spirit and human born as brothers, whose division of the world explains the relationship between the village, the forest and the dead. The Kohima area preserves the well-known cycle around the maiden Sonuo/Sonuolo and the tragic lovers of the Dzükou valley. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century writing began with missionary orthographies, hymnals and Bible translations—Ao and Angami were among the first languages reduced to writing—and an English-language Naga literature has since emerged, of which Easterine Kire's novels, notably *A Naga Village Remembered* and *When the River Sleeps*, are the most widely read; Temsüla Ao's poetry and short fiction is the other major body of work. Colonial ethnography—J. H. Hutton on the Angami and Sema, J. P. Mills on the Ao, Lhota and Rengma, Christoph von Fürer-Haimendorf on the Konyak—remains, for all its period assumptions, the densest documentary record of pre-Christian practice.

## Language & religion

Naga languages form several branches within Tibeto-Burman; Angami–Pochuri, Ao, Sumi, Lotha, Rengma, Konyak–Chang–Phom, Zeme and Tangkhul are among the recognised clusters, and the internal diversity is such that speakers of two villages a day's walk apart may share no mutual intelligibility. Nagamese, an Assamese-lexified pidgin, emerged as the market and inter-tribal language of the plains contact zone and is now widely spoken; English is the official language of Nagaland and the medium of education, church and publishing, so that literacy in one's own language is often thinner than literacy in English. Religion is overwhelmingly Christian, predominantly Baptist, following American Baptist mission work that began among the Ao in the 1870s and spread through the hills over the following century; church membership is high and church structures substantially organise village social life. The ancestral religion, sometimes glossed as *Pfutsana* or simply described as animist, recognised a creator or high spirit alongside local spirits of place, worked through village priests and diviners, observed *genna* days of ritual prohibition on which work and movement were suspended, and understood fertility—of crops, livestock and people—as transferable, which is the logic underlying head-taking. A small Heraka reform movement, associated with the Zeme and the figure of Rani Gaidinliu, persists as an indigenous religious alternative. What has survived Christianisation is the social and aesthetic architecture: the shawl codes, the festivals, the polyphonic singing, the mithun and hornbill, now held as heritage rather than as cult.
