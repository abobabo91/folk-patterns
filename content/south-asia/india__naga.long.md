## Overview

The Naga are a cluster of Tibeto-Burman-speaking peoples of the Patkai and Naga Hills, the forested ridge country straddling the India–Myanmar watershed between the Brahmaputra plain and the Chindwin valley. They form the majority of Nagaland state and of Myanmar's Naga Self-Administered Zone, with large populations in Manipur, Arunachal Pradesh and Assam, and in Sagaing Region and Kachin State. "Naga" is an exonym — Assamese *Noga*/*Naka*, glossed as "people with pierced ears," and Meitei *Hao* — that the British applied broadly; by December 2015 some eighty-nine groups were classified as Naga by various sources. They speak over eighty-nine mutually unintelligible languages. In folk-culture terms the Naga matter for an unusually dense convergence of village-level self-sufficiency: as Verrier Elwin put it, they made their own cloth, hats, raincoats, medicines and cooking vessels — with cloth, in particular, encoding rank so precisely that a shawl reads as a biography.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Weaving is women's work across every Naga group, done on a back-strap loom. A defining structural trait: a Naga shawl is woven as three separate narrow panels and stitched together, so the design has to be planned across seams before a thread is thrown. Each shawl takes days.

**Tsüngkotepsü** — the Ao man's shawl, and the most legible status document in the region. A black-and-red field crossed by bands, with a central white or undyed median panel painted (not woven) with figures: mithun, elephant, tiger, human heads, spear, dao. Only a man who had taken heads or given the full sequence of Feasts of Merit could wear it; the painted median band is what distinguishes it from every woven-only shawl around it.

**Rongsü** — the Ao counterpart earned through the Feast of Merit rather than through warfare, red and black banded, worn by the feast-giver and, by extension, his wife.

**Lohe** — the Angami shawl, distinguished from the Ao types by thick embroidered animal motifs applied over a dark ground rather than painted or supplementary-weft figures.

**Sütam, Ethasü, Longpensü** — the graded Lotha men's shawls. These form an explicit ladder: a man moves up the series as he completes successive stages of the Feast of Merit cycle, each grade adding permitted bands and figures. The *Lotha shawl* series is the clearest case of textile-as-rank in the Naga corpus.

**Rongkhim and Tsüngrem Khim** — Yimkhiung shawls; *Süpong* is the Sangtam equivalent. **Ahu** is the Sumi men's cloth, and the **Phom** are associated with a deep red shawl. The **Rengma** *azhu* completes the set of group-specific men's cloths our gallery indexes.

**Mekhela (wraparound skirt)** — the woman's lower garment, woven in group-specific stripe systems; designs for wraparounds and shawls differ for men and women throughout. The **Chakhesang ceremonial mekhela** and the **Angami kilt** (a short black men's skirt, often cowrie-studded) are the two best-documented ends of this range. Naga shawls made in Nagaland are the subject of a Geographical Indication application filed by the Indian Chamber of Commerce.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Mithun* horns (the semi-domesticated bovine, *Bos frontalis*, whose sacrifice underwrites the Feast of Merit); *hornbill* feather and beak; human head (the headhunting trophy, reduced to a frontal lozenge-face); elephant; tiger; spear and *dao* (the working machete); cowrie rows (ranked wealth counters, sewn not woven); sun-and-moon roundels; the striped band system itself, whose count and colour order is the group signature.

### Clothing & dress

Daily dress is minimal and the shawl does the heavy lifting: men wore a short kilt or loincloth with the shawl wrapped over the shoulder, women a knee-length *mekhela* wrapped and tucked, with a smaller body-cloth above. Headgear is designed by both sexes and is where the display budget goes. The full male ceremonial assemblage — worn for war dance and festival — combines a cane helmet faced with boar's tusk and mithun horn, **hornbill feather headdress** (the tail feathers of the great hornbill, strictly rationed by rank), **cane-and-boar-tusk armlets** worn above the biceps, **conch-shell armlets**, cowrie-sewn kilt or baldric, and a *dao* in a decorated holder. Konyak men additionally carried facial and chest tattooing marking head-taking, and brass pendant heads on the necklace. Women's festival dress substitutes dense bead collars and brass or bell-metal ear ornaments for the feather work.

### Architecture

Villages sit on ridge crests and spur tops — defensive siting from the intervillage warfare period — behind stone walls and a carved wooden gate. The central institution is the ***morung*** (Ao *arichu*, Angami *kichüki*), the bachelors' dormitory and men's house where boys lived from puberty to marriage and learned song, carving, discipline and war. Morung facades and their king-posts carry the most ambitious Naga carving: **morung carving** typically presents a hornbill, mithun heads, tiger, a squatting human figure and, on warrior morungs, rows of carved or actual human heads. Houses are timber-framed, walled in split bamboo or planks, with steep thatch roofs; among the Angami and Konyak a house whose owner has completed the Feast of Merit earns crossed **house-horns** (*kika*) on the gable — an architectural rank marker identical in logic to the shawl. A **mithun skull frieze** of the actual sacrificed skulls is mounted along the front wall or in the house porch, one per feast given. The **Naga log drum** — a single hollowed tree trunk, sometimes prow-carved as a hornbill head or buffalo, up to several metres long — sits in its own shed at the morung and is beaten by a crew for alarm, war news and festival.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Craftwork spans basketry, wood carving, pottery, metalwork, jewellery-making and bead-work. Basketry is the everyday high art: tight conical carrying baskets with a tumpline, lidded storage baskets, and cane rain-shields. Pottery is hand-built without a wheel in a few specialist villages and traded outward along ridge routes. Metalwork centres on brass and bell-metal: the **Naga brass hornbill** and the small cast brass human heads strung on Konyak necklaces are the signature forms, alongside heavy brass armlets and bell-metal ear-spools. The *dao* — a broad iron blade in a wood-and-cane half-sheath, worn at the hip — is simultaneously tool, weapon and personal emblem. Drinking vessels are bamboo tubes, gourds and carved wooden mugs used for rice beer.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Beads are the Naga medium of choice, used, in the standard phrase, in variety, profusion and complexity, in glass, shell, stone, teeth and tusk, claws, horn, metal, bone, wood, seed, hair and fibre. The **Naga carnelian bead necklace** — long orange-red stone beads, traded in from the plains over generations and heritable — is the highest-value form, sometimes strung with brass heads or conch discs. The **Konyak headhunter necklace** carries small brass or bronze faces, one conventionally per head taken. **Zeliang bead apron** work covers the woman's front panel in dense glass beadwork on a cloth ground. Ear ornaments are large: red-dyed goat's hair tassels, bamboo plugs, cotton wool tufts. Konyak facial tattooing (a broad dark band across the face, with chest and throat work) was conferred on successful head-takers; women in several groups carried leg and arm tattooing at marriage. The surviving tattooed Konyak men are now very few.

## Music & performance

Instruments made and played by Naga communities include the *tati* (a one-string fiddle-type instrument), bamboo mouth organs, bamboo flutes, trumpets, cattle-skin drums and the log drum. The log drum is less melodic instrument than village voice: beaten in coded rhythms to announce a raid, a death, a successful hunt, or the start of festival. Song genres divide into romantic songs, historical narrative songs that carry the biography of famous ancestors and the account of specific incidents, and seasonal songs tied to the agricultural cycle — sowing, weeding, harvest, and the rest period after. Because the Naga languages had no writing, these songs were the historical record, and the oral tradition is kept alive through folk tales and songs. Naga singing is characteristically polyphonic and unaccompanied, performed by a group in the field or at the morung. Early Western missionaries opposed folk song among Naga Christians, treating it as bound up with spirit worship, war and immorality, and substituted translated Western hymns — a substitution that drove indigenous music into steep decline across the Naga hills. The current revival works against that break: choir culture is enormous in Nagaland, and the Hornbill Festival has become the main public stage for older repertoire.

## Dance & theatre

Naga dance is group dance, performed in tight synchronisation and always sung: all dances are accompanied by songs and by war cries from the dancers themselves. Composition is by sex — some dances for men, some for women, some mixed — and by occasion, with festivals and religious occasions the standard settings. The war dance is the emblematic male form: athletic and martial, performed in full headdress with *dao* and spear, built on stamping, high leaps, sudden crouches and shield feints, and punctuated by shouted cries that in origin announced a successful raid. Women's dances are lateral and linked-arm, moving in a slow line or circle with a rocking step. There is no masked or scripted theatre tradition among the Naga comparable to the mask dances of the eastern Himalaya; the dramatic content sits inside the war dance and inside the sung narratives instead.

## Festivals & rituals

Each Naga group keeps its own festival calendar, pinned to the jhum (swidden) agricultural year rather than to a pan-Naga date. The Angami **Sekrenyi** falls in late February and is a purification festival: ritual bathing at the village well by the young men, sacrifice, and ten days of feasting and song. The Ao **Moatsü** (early May) marks the completion of sowing, and **Tsüngremmong** (August) precedes the harvest. The Sumi **Tuluni** in July celebrates the year's first fruits with rice beer shared between betrothed couples. The Lotha **Tokhu Emong** and the Chakhesang **Tsükhenyie** fill the post-harvest and spring slots respectively. Since 2000 the Government of Nagaland has staged the **Hornbill Festival** (1–10 December, at Kisama) as an inter-group meeting ground; **Lui Ngai Ni**, in February, is the Naga seed-sowing festival of Manipur and serves the same inter-ethnic function there.

The dominant ritual complex historically was the **Feast of Merit**: a graded sequence in which a wealthy man sacrificed mithun and distributed meat and rice beer to the whole village, gaining at each stage the right to a further shawl grade, house-horns, and a stone-pulling monument. Headhunting — abolished under British administration in the 19th century along with intertribal violence — was the other route to rank, and the two systems together determined what a man could wear, carve on his house and sing about. Life-cycle rites are now largely Baptist in form, but bride-price in mithun, and burial with the deceased's personal ornaments, persist in practice.

## Foodways

Naga cuisine is defined by smoking and fermentation. Rice is the staple, grown in jhum fields and, among the Angami, in some of the most elaborate wet-terrace systems in the Northeast. Meat — pork above all, plus mithun, dog, and river fish — is smoked over the hearth on a bamboo rack and kept for months; smoked pork with *anishi* (fermented, sun-dried taro-leaf cakes, an Ao speciality) is the dish most strongly identified with the region. *Axone* (*akhuni*), fermented soybean cake with a formidable smell, is the Sumi contribution and is cooked with smoked pork or pounded into a chutney. *Bastenga* (fermented bamboo shoot), fermented fish, and fermented soybean give the cuisine its base sourness in place of tamarind or vinegar. Heat comes from the *raja mircha* (bhut jolokia / king chilli), used fresh, dried and in a roasted chutney. Cooking is overwhelmingly boiling, steaming in leaf parcels and smoking — very little oil, very few spices beyond chilli, ginger, garlic and *Zanthoxylum*. *Galho*, a rice-and-vegetable one-pot porridge, is the everyday dish. Rice beer — *zutho* or *thutse* among the Angami, *ruhi* elsewhere — is the ritual drink of every feast and festival, though Nagaland's prohibition law and Baptist temperance have pushed it to the margins of public life.

## Oral tradition & literature

Nothing was written until the missionary orthographies of the later 19th century, so the entire pre-Christian record is oral: folk tales, genealogies and the historical song cycles that narrate whole stories of famous ancestors and particular incidents. There is no single pan-Naga epic — the diversity of languages and the pattern of clan absorption during successive migrations worked against one — but migration narratives are the shared backbone, most famously the Angami-Chakhesang-Rengma-Sumi tradition of dispersal from the stone-terraced site of **Khezakenoma**, where the three founding brothers quarrelled over a grain-multiplying stone. Tiger-spirit-man sibling tales, in which the tiger, the spirit and the first man are brothers who divide the world, recur across groups. Proverbs and riddles were the morung's teaching medium. Contemporary revival runs through the Naga Heritage Village at Kisama, work on Naga folklore by regional universities, and an English-language literary scene (Temsüla Ao, Easterine Kire) that rewrites the oral corpus and the conflict years for readers outside the hills.

## Language & religion

The Naga languages are Tibeto-Burman, classified under the Chin-Naga group or the Sal group depending on the branch, and comprise over eighty-nine languages and dialects that are mostly mutually unintelligible — the greatest language diversity of any ethnic group or state in India. There is no indigenous script; Roman orthographies came with the American Baptist missions. Nagaland declared English its official language in 1967, and *Nagamese*, an Assamese-based creole, is the working lingua franca. Traditional religion was animist: veneration of sun, moon, trees and stones, a Supreme Being above a population of nature spirits, and sacrifice to keep them settled. American Baptist mission from the mid-19th century transformed this comprehensively; over 87% of Nagas now identify as Christian, making Nagaland one of India's three Christian-majority states. Older belief survives inside festival practice and taboo observance rather than as separate cult. The 2011 census recorded 5,176 Hindus in the state; small Buddhist minorities exist. In Myanmar the word "Naga" separately denotes the serpent beings of Buddhist mythology and religious art.
