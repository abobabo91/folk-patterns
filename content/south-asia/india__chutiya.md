## Overview

The Chutia people are an ethnic group native to Assam, India, historically associated with the Chutia kingdom. Their historic political centre was Sadiya, in the eastern Brahmaputra Valley, and their settlements later extended across Upper Assam, Central Assam, Darrang, Nagaon, North Guwahati, and the Barak Valley. The Chutia kingdom was incorporated into the Ahom kingdom in 1523–24, after which Chutia families, artisans, nobles, and other inhabitants were dispersed and resettled in different regions. The Chutias constitute one of the core groups forming the Assamese people. The community historically belonged to the Bodo–Kachari group, with suspected Shan admixtures, and underwent Sanskritisation, Hinduisation, and later cultural change connected with Ekasarana Dharma and assimilation with neighbouring populations.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Brick-building was already taking place in the Sadiya region during the early medieval period. A 2024 study grouped bricks from Pratimagarh and Padum Pukhuri within a technological horizon broadly assigned to the seventh–tenth centuries CE, although this evidence does not by itself establish Chutia political authority. Chutia-period archaeological remains include fortified settlements, temple remains, earthen forts, and ancient brick-built tanks in the Sadiya–Lohit–Dibang region. Named sites associated with this landscape include Tamreswari Temple, Bhismaknagar, Malinithan, Gomsi, Ita Fort, Naksaparvat, Rukmininagar, Buroi, Moiramora Doul, and Padum Pukhuri. Direct epigraphic links include the 1442 inscription of Sri Srimata Muktadharmanarayana at Tamreswari and inscribed bricks bearing the names Sri Sri Lakshinarayana and Sri Sri Pratyakshanarayana.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Chutia kingdom was associated with copper-plate inscriptions and brick-built structures. After the conquest, Suhungmung carried away a large body of blacksmiths from the Chutia kingdom and settled them in the Bosa (Doyang) and Ujoni regions, where smithies produced knives, daggers, swords, guns, and cannons. The blacksmith establishment during his reign is stated to have numbered about 3,000.

## Music & performance

The sources do mention the Saliki clan’s traditional association with training myna birds to imitate speech and songs. Occupational and service groups are recorded in relation to the Ahom state, including the Charingia Naoboicha, who operated royal boats, and the Buruk Chutias, whose duties were comparable to those of the chaodangs or royal guards. These records concern administration and service rather than performance.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record religious and social distinctions within the community. Hindu Chutias included people initiated into Vaishnavism by Sankardev and Madhavdev. Those following stricter Vaishnavite observances were popularly called Kesa-ponthi, while Poka-ponthi retained more of the older tribal customs. Chutias living around the Mayamara Satra were initiated into its form of Ekasarana Dharma.

The sources also describe household and community practices. Chutia people generally lived in joint families, sometimes with more than one hundred members. Villagers traditionally helped families during marriages, deaths, and other special occasions, a practice known in some areas as *hejari* and in others as *howri*. The Haar Chutia are associated in community tradition with former sacrificial rites at the principal shrine at Sadiya, although the source states that this tradition does not by itself establish the historicity of the practice.

Among Ahom Chutias, the 1905 Assam District Gazetteers records the Chaklong marriage rite and presents it as evidence of assimilation into Ahom social and cultural practices.

## Foodways

They do record dietary restrictions associated with particular groups. The Buruk clan traditionally avoided goat meat and bottle gourd. The 1901 Census of India records the Borahi Chutias as avoiding goat meat. Among Hindu Chutias, stricter Vaishnavite observances associated with the Kesa-ponthi restricted animal meat and alcohol in religious rites, while the Poka-ponthi retained more older tribal customs.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources record several community traditions connected with clans, places, rulers, and historical movements. The Buruk clan derives its name from the odal tree, whose fibre was used to make ropes, while the Lophai name is explained through the cultivation of *lopha*, a leafy vegetable. The Dihing-goya are associated with settlements along the Dihing River, and the Sesa-gharia with the Sessa River. Oral tradition connects the Haar Chutia with former sacrificial rites at the principal shrine at Sadiya.

Local tradition at Ghilamara in present-day Lakhimpur associates the locality with a Chutia rebel leader named Ghila, who is said to have been killed there by Ahom forces during the reign of Sukhaamphaa (1552–1603). The Darrang Raj Vamsavali records that members of the former Chutia ruling family took refuge among the Miris and Mishmis after the conquest. It also records their settlement at Bahbari, present-day Sootea, after an appeal to Nara Narayan.

The primary literary body of Deori is called “deori chucheba chengcha” (Deori Sahitya Sabha), but the sources distinguish the Deori language and community from the Assamese-speaking Chutia community.

## Language & religion

The Chutia people speak Assamese today, while the original Tibeto-Burman language associated with them has disappeared. Earlier scholars associated the Deori language with the Chutias and described Deori-Chutia as the original language of the community. Modern linguistic studies cited in the sources do not equate the present-day Deori language and community with the Assamese-speaking Chutia community and state that the two communities are ethnically and linguistically distinct.

Deori is described as a Tibeto-Burman language spoken by the Deori people of Assam and Arunachal Pradesh. Of the four territorial Deori groups, only the Dibongiya have retained the language; Patorgoyan, Tengaponiya, and Borgoyan have shifted to Assamese.

Chutia religious history includes Sanskritisation, Hinduisation in the Vaishnava tradition, initiation into Vaishnavism by Sankardev and Madhavdev, and later affiliation with Ekasarana Dharma. The sources also describe divisions among Hindu Chutias, Ahom Chutias, Borahi Chutias, and Deoris, shaped by religious affiliation, intermarriage, and association with neighbouring communities.
