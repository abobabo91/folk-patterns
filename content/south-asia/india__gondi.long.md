## Overview

The Gondi, or Gond, who call themselves *Koitur* or *Koi*, are an ethnolinguistic group of central India spread across Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Chhattisgarh, Uttar Pradesh, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Bihar and Odisha. Their historic territory, Gondwana, ran from eastern Madhya Pradesh to western Odisha and from northern Telangana to the southeastern corner of Uttar Pradesh, taking in the Satpura range, the Bastar division and the valleys of the Godavari, Indravati, Wardha and Wainganga. The 2001 census counted 8,501,549 Gonds; almost 13 million returned themselves as Gond in 2011. Their language, *Gondi*, belongs to the South-Central branch of the Dravidian family. They matter here because their village religion, clan structure and household material culture — hut, hearth, plough, drum, god-symbol — were documented as a working whole rather than as isolated crafts.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources available for this profile — Wikipedia's articles on the Gondi people and the Gondi language, and the British Museum catalogue records of the objects shown here — describe almost no woven textile tradition, and none of the named painting or weaving traditions commonly attached to the Gonds appears in them. What the museum records do contain is a small number of cloth and fibre objects and a body of leaf- and bark-work that carries the decorative load elsewhere borne by textiles.

**Woollen blanket** — a blanket of woollen cloth is among the collected household goods, the only substantial woven piece in the set.

**Cotton blouse** — a woman's blouse of cotton cloth; the catalogue notes the material and nothing of its cut or decoration.

**Leaf head-dress** — composite head-dresses built from wild date leaves, in which loops of leaf ring the head-band, folded leaves are massed into triangles and a further layer fans across the crown, with pendants of interlocking leaf hoops hanging from the base. One example adds tin-foil in red and gold over the folded triangles and a flat semi-circular gilt panel above them.

**Wool waist-string** — a man's waist-string of several strands of red-dyed wool, finished with purple, yellow and white tassels at one end and a loop at the other to fasten it.

**Bark head-band and head-ring** — a head-band cut from a strip of bark, tied with twisted fibre and carrying a bark disc fastened at the top; separately, a head-ring of reddish-brown leaves bound with bark strips.

**Motif vocabulary.** The catalogue names only geometric and figural motifs on hard materials: chevron-and-dot banding on a hearth; triangles within a serrated circle radiating from a shield boss; ovals and flowers on stems springing from the ring of a hinged brass bracelet; dots of graded size covering the interlocking plates of an anklet; a ten-petalled flower forming the face of a woman's ear-ornament; incised bands around brass vessels, a platter and a pair of cymbals; and black painted and incised bands along a bamboo pipe.

### Clothing & dress

The sources say little about everyday dress beyond a woman's cotton blouse and a man's red woollen waist-string with multicoloured tassels. Ceremonial and festival dress is better attested. For Diwali, the Gond communities of Adilabad dance wearing peacock-feathered turbans and saffron attire. Head coverings of leaf and bark form a distinct class: wild date leaves are attached vertically to a circular band and tied at the top, with a second fanned layer held by interwoven leaf strips and pendant hoops falling from the base, and in the tin-foil version the same structure is faced in red and gold. Men wear a snake-god symbol as an iron necklace of interlocking figure-of-eight links; women wear a flattened grey-metal armlet as a charm and cast white-metal ear-ornaments. A composite amulet made from a pangolin scale, pierced at both sides and threaded on twisted fibre cord, was collected as a cure for pain. The sources record no footwear.

### Architecture

Two clay-and-fibre models of Gond houses were collected, and they are the fullest architectural evidence in the sources. Both show a rectangular hut with a rectangular door opening in each of the two long sides and a small window, blocked, with rough relief work worked around the door and window frames. The roof is pointed and made of fibre, held down by L-shaped twigs laid over it. One model is roughly painted pale yellow overall. The other is set in a modelled yard of clay, partly fenced with wooden twigs, holding three small clay hearths with circular bodies and mushroom-shaped tops, and two small square stands to one side of the yard, one of them raised. A separate hearth was collected as an object in its own right: horseshoe-shaped, built of earth, and decorated with a chevron-and-dot pattern at each end. For working outdoors there is a rainshield — overlapping orange-brown leaves sewn together with slivers of wood or bamboo and lashed to a bamboo frame with fibre.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Earthenware appears as a tobacco pipe and as the body of a friction drum. Brass is worked into a round-bellied vessel with a flaring lip, its base scored and its neck ringed with incised bands, and into a platter with hammered banding. Iron carries the tools and the sacred objects alike: a sickle whose blade is set into a thick length of bamboo, a socketed spear-head that is itself a god symbol, a sword with decoration along the lower blade and a simple finger-guard, and a seated agricultural figure with a bird or animal head turned upward. Wood and bamboo furnish a model plough, a model harrow with iron parts, a model bed of wood and bamboo, a measuring vessel, a cattle clapper of bamboo, wood and vegetable-fibre cord, and a shallow openwork quail-trap of plaited split bamboo whose entrance is spiked with long bamboo slivers. Hunting gear includes a bamboo pellet bow with a plaited pouch, barbed and tanged arrows, a hide shield with a brass boss, a leather sword-sheath, a wood-and-hide powder flask and a leather water-bottle.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Cast metal dominates. A hinged brass bracelet made in two parts is pegged shut with wood and tied with twisted fibre, its central ring throwing off ovals and stemmed flowers. Anklets come as pairs of many separate interlocking grey-metal plates, each pierced twice, threaded on twisted fibre and fastened at one side with a stick; each plate is patterned with dots of different sizes and the band widens from top to bottom. Women's ear-ornaments are cast in white metal as ten-petalled flowers with a thick white-metal wire through the centre. A white-metal neck-ring carries coiled wire decoration and fastens with a hook and two rings. Iron ornaments serve as god symbols: the men's figure-of-eight snake necklace, closed chains of interlocking iron links representing a snake, and penannular iron bands, one of square section with a spiral twist, another twisted with each end curled back.

## Music & performance

The collected instruments cover most of a village ensemble. A kettledrum is turned from dark brown wood with cow hide stretched over it and lashed down with leather thongs; strips of leather run down the sides to form a small circle at the base, on which the drum stands, and twisted vegetable-fibre cords are tied to each side of the drum face and knotted together. A friction drum is built of earthenware with skin and feathers. Brass cymbals come as a pair of incised discs joined through their centres by a textile cord. A pipe is cut from bamboo, closed at one end by the node with a hole pierced below it and four finger-holes at the other, and banded with black paint and incised lines. A musical horn is made from a buffalo horn with the tip removed, pierced with many holes at the broad end, one carrying a twisted fibre loop. A fiddle-bow of wood, its upturned foot bound with horse-hair under a cotton strip and a red cotton strip at the middle, is the surviving trace of a bowed instrument: the *pardhan*, the Gond bard, plays a fiddle to calm the violent tendencies of a clan's *Persa Pen*, and in the Gond account of the Kachchargardh caves the bard Hirasuka Patalir plays a *kingri* to give the imprisoned children the strength to push away the boulder.

## Dance & theatre

Two named dances appear in the sources. In Chhattisgarh, women perform the *sua* dance, named after the word for parrot, after Diwali, in honour of Shiva and Parvati; it carries the belief that the parrot will carry their sadness to their lovers. In Adilabad, Diwali is celebrated with the *Gussadi* dance, whose performers wear peacock-feathered turbans and saffron attire and move in festive groups. The museum set includes the women's dance equipment: wooden castanets, each with a plain pierced lug for a handle — one rounded, one squared. A rattle of bamboo, pierced by two twisted iron handles strung with iron rings, belongs to hunting rather than to dance.

## Festivals & rituals

*Pola*, *Phag* and *Dassera* are named as major Gond festivals, and Diwali is marked in Chhattisgarh and Adilabad with the dances above. At Dussehra the Gondi inhabitants of Paraswadi in Gadchiroli district carry an image of Ravana riding an elephant in procession, honouring him as the tenth *dharmaguru* of their people and protesting the burning of his effigies. The Gonds of Adilabad hold the first ceremonies of the year when the *Mahua* flowers bloom; the flowers yield a liquor held to be purifying. At the First Fruit festival, all members of a *saga* eat with the seniormost member of its seniormost *pari*. Worship of the clan's *Persa Pen* is the central rite: the clan priest sacrifices while two members of a *soira* saga dress the idol and cook the sacrificial food, and the meal is served to each saga separately, in the order in which its ancestors emerged from the cave. Before any festival, the village guardian *Aki Pen* and the village mother goddess are worshipped. Three men officiate — the *baiga*, village priest; the *bhumka*, clan priest; and the *kaser-gaita*, leader of the village. Weddings circle the couple around a post of *Mahua* wood. A pre-monsoon hunting ceremony reads the quantity of blood spilled as a forecast of rain. Death is met with anger, because it is held to be caused by demons; the dead are buried with their possessions, though Hinduization has made cremation more common, and stones representing the souls of the dead, *hanals*, are kept in a *hanalkot* at the foot of a *saja* tree.

## Foodways

The sources describe almost no cuisine. What they attest is drink and sacrifice: liquor pressed from *Mahua* flowers, considered purifying and tied to the first ceremonies of the year; animals sacrificed to *Gansam*, the protector of villages from tigers; and the sacrificial meal at the worship of *Persa Pen*, cooked by two members of a *soira* saga and served saga by saga in order of precedence. At the First Fruit festival, saga members eat together with their senior kinsman. A rule of diet follows from the religion: Gonds may eat animals, but not those that stand as a totem. The collected objects that bear on food are a wooden measuring vessel, a brass vessel and a brass platter, an earth hearth and the three clay hearths modelled in the house yard, and a bamboo quail-trap.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Gonds have their own version of the Ramayana, the ***Gond Ramayani***, drawn from oral folk legend: seven stories set after the main events of the Ramayana, with Lakshmana as protagonist, in which he finds a bride. The Gondi language carries a folk literature of wedding songs and narrations. The cycle around the Kachchargardh caves — Mata Kali Kankali's children imprisoned by Shambu and Gaura, fed for twelve years by a pond and a mythical bird, freed when Pari Kupar Lingo enlisted the bard Hirasuka Patalir, who was crushed by the boulder — is both narrative and the charter for a pilgrimage site. Clan origin myths name the ancestral villages from which each *saga* dispersed, some still identified with places on the ground. The Gonds also kept their own terms for the sun, moon, Milky Way and constellations, on which their calendar rested. Preservation now runs through writing: Munshi Mangal Singh Masaram's 1928 Brahmi-based script, the *Gunjala Gondi Lipi* manuscripts dated to about 1750 and rediscovered by University of Hyderabad researchers, and recent children's books and online videos.

## Language & religion

*Gondi* is a South-Central Dravidian language related to Telugu, with a two-gender system and aspirated stops that distance it from Proto-Dravidian. Its main dialects are Dorla, Koya, Madiya, Muria and Raj Gond, divided by the treatment of initial *s*, kept in the north and west and shifted to *h* or lost in the south and east. It is endangered: about three million speakers against nearly thirteen million Gonds, and Hindi, Chhattisgarhi, Marathi and Odia dominate education and work. It is written in Devanagari and Telugu script, and in two native scripts. Most Gonds follow their own religion, *Koyapunem*, "the way of nature", founded by Pari Kupar Lingo, worshipping the high god Baradeo alongside clan, village and ancestral deities; some follow Sarnaism, a minority are Christian or Muslim, and Brahminical Hinduism has shaped much of the whole.
