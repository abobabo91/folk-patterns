## Overview

The Limbu are a Kirati ethnolinguistic group indigenous to the Himalayan region of eastern Nepal, northeastern India and western Bhutan. In Nepal, they live especially in Koshi Province, including Dhankuta, Ilam, Jhapa, Morang, Panchthar, Sankhuwasabha, Sunsari, Taplejung and Tehrathum; significant numbers also live in Sikkim, West Bengal, Assam, Nagaland, Bhutan and Myanmar. The 2021 Nepal census recorded 4,14,704 Limbu people, while the source gives an estimated population of 6,00,000. Limbu is a Sino-Tibetan language. The Limbu refer to themselves as Yakthung and their language as Yakthungpan. Their folk culture includes distinctive houses, the Sirijunga script, oral religious traditions, ritual dances, harvest celebrations, musical instruments, agricultural practices and named foods.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that Limbu women weave and knit clothing for traditional dresses. They do describe several forms of symbolic decoration connected with clothing and ritual. The red mud paint used on the skirting of a traditional house represents the *patuka*, or belt, worn by Limbu women. Decorative wood carvings on doors and windows represent gold jewelry worn by Limbu women. The *Silam Sakma*, a ritualistic element used by *phedangmas*, is worn by community members on the left chest during an event.

### Architecture

A traditional Limbu house is described as a symbolic representation of *yuma*, a goddess of the Limbu community. Its doors and windows may contain wood carvings depicting flowers used by the Limbu during rituals, as well as decorations representing gold jewelry worn by Limbu women. The wall skirting is generally painted manually with red mud paint, symbolizing the *patuka* belt.

The main structural feature is the *muring-sitlam*, the central pillar or column on the ground floor. Limbu people believe that the *yuma* goddess resides there, and ritual prayers and offerings are performed around it, usually twice a year. Traditional houses are found in eastern Nepal and western Sikkim, India. Geometrical shapes such as circles, triangles and squares may be painted in patterns and different colors on building facades. The *Silam Sakma* symbol, used by *phedangmas*, is diamond-shaped and has nine concentric diamond layers supported by vertical and horizontal axes. It appears on entry gates and balcony railings. Traditional houses are endangered because wood carving has become expensive and local craftsmen have declined.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do mention agricultural tools, including the *halo* plough and *Kodalo* hoe. The *Phedza* is used to prepare meats. The *Taa* is made of brass, has a diameter of 25 cm, and consists of a pair of cymbals weighing one kilo. The *Phedza*, *halo*, *Kodalo* and *Taa* are the only named everyday or material objects in the sources that can be described here.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention gold jewelry worn by Limbu women. Wood carvings on the doors and windows of traditional houses are said to represent this jewelry. The *Silam Sakma* is worn on the left chest during an event, but the sources describe it as a ritualistic symbol rather than jewelry.

## Music & performance

Limbu musical instruments include **Chyabrung**, **Yalambar Baja**, **Chethya/Yethala**, **Mephrama**, **Miklakom**, **Niyari Hongsing Ke**, **Negra**, **Phakwa**, **Phenjekom**, **Puttungey**, **Simikla**, **Tetlafakwa**, **Ting**, **Tungeba**, **Ungdung** and **Yea Pongey**. The *Phamuk* is a melody instrument consisting of three bamboo pipes, each about 4 cm thick, attached side by side. The *Taa* is a brass cymbal instrument played by unmarried Limbu women in *Ke Lang*.

Limbu singing includes **Khyali**, a conversational song in which young men and women sing poetic expressions in a sweet tone. Traditional love songs include **Sakpa Palam Samlo**, sung during the fast *Kusakpa Yaalang* dance; **Kemba Palam Samlo**, sung during the slow *Kemba Yeaaˀlang* dance; and **Domke Akma Palam Samlo**, sung during everyday chores and the *Domke Akma* dance. **Hakpare Samlo** is sung by middle-aged men and women interested in *Mundhum*, while **Nisammang Sewa Samlo** is a devotional song performed during religious functions. Retellings of *Yumawad*, a religious scripture transmitted orally by religious leaders, are also included in traditional music and contain social stories, dreams and accounts of everyday life.

## Dance & theatre

The sources describe several named Limbu dances. **Yalakma**, also called *dhan nach* in Nepali, is associated with weddings and the rice harvest. Men and women dance in a slow circle, and the dance may continue for many hours. **Kelangma**, also called **Chyabrung**, uses complex footwork synchronized with drums; anyone may join.

The **Ke Lang** or **Chyabrung dance** is described as a dance performed after the origin of life and imitates animals, insects and other living beings. **Yea Kakma** is an agricultural dance performed in the evening after crops are reaped, while **Damke Akme** is performed while sowing crops. **Nahangma** is a war dance performed during a religious function. Only adult males and *Shamani* priests may take part, and participants carry objects including a *Phedza*, a domesticated young adult male Junglefowl, a sword and shield, or a bow and arrow.

**Aambepojoma Kamketlungma** depicts the historical war of ten Limbu. **Phungsok Lang** and **Tongsing Lang** are mysterious and ancient dances performed only by *Shamani* priests.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do describe several religious, agricultural and life-cycle practices. Limbu wedding ceremonies are mostly arranged by parents, and asking for a woman’s hand is an important ceremony. Marriage generally takes place within the community but outside the clan; marriage within one’s own clan is prohibited for up to three generations, and cross-cousin marriage is not allowed. After the wedding, members of the man’s family visit the woman’s house with a piglet and alcoholic and non-alcoholic beverages. Major wedding ceremonies take place in the groom’s house.

The *Yalakma* dance may accompany weddings, harvest celebrations or other social occasions. Agricultural dances are connected with reaping and sowing crops. In the *Nahangma* ritual, *Manggena* is performed at home before the war dance. A stone treated as a god with red *tika* is placed on a banana leaf, a large adult black homegrown pig is killed and presented to *phedangba*, and a Junglefowl is given to each person according to age and gender. The *phedangba* gives a vision of each person, after which a *phedangma* beheads the Junglefowl and sprinkles its blood.

The Limbu traditionally bury their dead, although cremation is becoming more common through the influence of Hinduism. Ritual prayers and offerings are performed around the *muring-sitlam* pillar, usually twice a year.

## Foodways

Limbu foodways include homegrown livestock, grains, vegetables, fermented foods and alcoholic beverages. The sources state that Limbu people commonly eat *dhal bhat tarkari* with pickle: *dhal* is lentil soup, *bhat* is rice, *tarkari* is vegetables and *achar* is pickle. They use the *Phedza* to prepare meats. Pork, fish and yak are named as traditional meats, and livestock also have religious uses.

Named foods include **Chembikeek sumbak**, oil-fried *kinema* with spices; **Chhurpi**, made from yak and buttermilk; **Filinge achar**, Niger-seed pickle; **Gundruk**, fermented leafy vegetables with soup; **Kaan sadeko**, fried pork ears; **Khareng**, maize, millet or wheat roti; **Khoreng**, baked roti made from wheat, millet, buckwheat or rice flour; and **Kinema**, fermented beans with soup. Other dishes include **Macha ko siddra**, dried river fish; **Mandokpenaa thee**, a fermented millet beverage served with *Tongba*; **Mula ko acchar**, radish pickle; **Pena manda**, millet flour cooked in more water; **Phando**, a chutney made from soybean and chilli powder; **Poponda**, finger-millet flour wrapped in leaves; **Faksa Dameko**, pan-seared pork; **Pork sekuwa**; **Sakhekya**, dry beef; **Sekuwa**, pork, chicken or vegetable skewers; **Sungur ko khutta daal**, pork feet in lentil soup; and **Yangben-Faksa**, pork curry with *Yangben*.

**Tongba** is a traditional drink, while *Rakshi* is a traditional alcoholic beverage. Guests may also be offered *Lassi*, water and homemade fruit juices. Religious foods include **Yumé**, meats cooked in blood, and dishes involving *Yangben*. The sources mention food taboos among different tribes.

## Oral tradition & literature

*Mundhum* is an oral scripture and religious book through which Limbu social rules and regulations are transmitted. *Yumawad* is a religious scripture kept alive by religious leaders and handed down verbally from generation to generation. Its retellings include social stories, dreams and everyday life, and some are incorporated into traditional music. The sources also state that Limbu customs and traditions are conventionally said to have been established in the distant past by *Sawa Yethang*, described as a council of eight kings.

The history of the Limbu is said to be written in *Bangsawoli*, also known as *Bansawali*, a genealogy of which some ancient families have kept copies. Limbu language and literature have undergone revival through the Sirijunga script, the promotion of Yakthung culture and contemporary publications. *Tanchoppa*, meaning “Morning Star,” has been published as a monthly newspaper or magazine since 1995.

## Language & religion

Limbu, also called *Yakthungpan*, is a Sino-Tibetan language spoken in Nepal, northeastern India and expatriate communities in Bhutan. Its four main dialects are **Phedape**, **Chhathare**, **Tambarkhole** and **Panthare**. The language is postpositional, ergative-absolutive and polysynthetic in nature. The Language Commission of Nepal has recommended it as an official language in Koshi Province, and Sikkim recognizes it as an additional official language for preserving culture and tradition.

The Limbu writing system is called the **Sirijunga script**. One account says it was designed by Limbu King Sirijanga Hang in the 9th century; another places its development during the Buddhist expansion in Sikkim in the early 18th century. It was later redesigned and reintroduced by Te-ongsi Sirijunga Xin Thebe. The script has been influenced by Tibetan and Devanagari.

Limbu religion is known as **Yuma samyo**, and Limbu people follow the Yuma religion. *Mundhum* and *Yumawad* are central oral religious traditions. Some Limbu have converted to Christianity, and Phalgunanda established the reformist **Satyahang** religion. Burial is traditional, while cremation has become more common through Hindu influence.
