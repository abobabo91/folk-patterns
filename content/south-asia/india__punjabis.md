## Overview

The Punjabis are an Indo-Aryan ethnolinguistic group associated with the Punjab region, which comprises areas of northwestern India and eastern Pakistan. The region is connected with the five rivers Beas, Chenab, Jhelum, Ravi, and Sutlej, tributaries of the Indus. Punjabi identity is primarily linguistic, geographical, and cultural rather than exclusively tribal or religious. Punjabi people follow Islam, Sikhism, Hinduism, or Christianity, with regional differences between Pakistani and Indian Punjab. Punjabi-speaking people also live in a large overseas diaspora, including communities in Canada, the United States, the United Kingdom, the Middle East, East Africa, Australia, New Zealand, Southeast Asia, and Italy. Punjabi is an Indo-Aryan language, written mainly in Shahmukhi in Pakistan and Gurmukhi in India.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Phulkari** — *Phulkari* is a form of folk embroidery traditionally worked in floral patterns, though its designs also include geometrical shapes, symbols, and motifs relevant to Punjabi culture. It is stitched with woven silk and colourful thread and is commonly applied to dupattas and chunis, as well as suits, dresses, and accessories.

Phulkari has been worn by women for hundreds of years in vibrant colours. Women wear it during religious and cultural celebrations such as Vaisakhi and Lohri, and during wedding celebrations such as the Jago.

**Motif vocabulary.** Floral patterns, geometrical shapes, symbols, motifs.

### Clothing & dress

A Punjabi suit consists of a qameez, or top, and salwar, or bottom. The combination is also called a salwar kurta, salwar suit, or Punjabi suit. Shalwars are trousers that are usually wide at the waist and narrow toward a cuffed bottom, held by a drawstring or elastic belt. The kameez is a long shirt or tunic whose side seams are left open below the waist for freedom of movement.

Women wearing the shalwar-kameez may also wear a long scarf or shawl called a *dupattā* around the head or neck. It can serve as a form of modesty and may cover the head in a temple or in the presence of elders. The shalwar-kameez is widely worn and is the national dress of Pakistan.

A *dastār* is a form of headgear associated with Sikhism and Punjabi and Sikh culture. Sikh men and women who keep the Five Ks wear the turban to cover their long, uncut hair, or *kesh*. The dastār represents equality, honour, self-respect, courage, spirituality, and piety.

The *kaintha* is a traditional necklace usually made of gold or steel. It is worn with the shalwar-kameez, together with a shawl, chunni, or vest. Men and women traditionally wear it at the Mayian and Jaggo ceremonies and while performing Bhangra and Giddha.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They identify gold and steel as materials used for the kaintha necklace.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The *kaintha* is a traditional necklace usually made of gold or steel. It has a pendant that stands out from the rest of the necklace and is accompanied by matching colour schemes and yarn at the back to hold it together. It forms part of Punjabi clothing and is worn by men and women at Mayian and Jaggo ceremonies, as well as during Bhangra and Giddha performances.

The sources also describe Sikh hair practice. Sikh men and women who keep the Five Ks retain long, uncut hair, or *kesh*, which is covered by the dastār.

## Music & performance

Bhangra describes dance-oriented popular music with Punjabi rhythms, developed since the 1980s. Sufi music and Qawali are also identified as important genres in the Punjab region, particularly in Punjab, Pakistan.

The sources describe the musical setting of Bhangra through its connection with agricultural work and harvesting. Farmers performed Bhangra while carrying out agricultural activities, using movements associated with their work. Traditional Bhangra is performed in a circle with traditional dance steps and is now also performed outside the harvest season.

Punjabi musical life is connected with both religious and regional diversity. Sufi music and Qawali are associated with Punjab, Pakistan, while Bhangra is presented as a popular form linked to Punjabi rhythms and the culture of agricultural labour.

## Dance & theatre

**Bhangra** is one of the most famous dances originating in the Punjab. It was historically performed by farmers during the harvesting season and while agricultural chores were being completed. The movements provided a way to express accomplishment and welcome the new harvesting season. Traditional Bhangra is performed in a circle using traditional dance steps, although it is now performed on occasions beyond harvest.

**Giddha** is named as another traditional Punjabi dance. The sources mention it alongside Bhangra in connection with the wearing of the kaintha.

Punjabi dances may be performed by men or women, individually or in groups, and may be accompanied by traditional musical instruments.

## Festivals & rituals

Punjabi Muslims typically observe Islamic festivals, while Punjabi Sikhs and Hindus typically observe Lohri, Basant, and Vaisakhi as seasonal festivals. Muslim festivals follow the lunar Islamic calendar, or Hijri calendar, and their dates fall earlier by 10 to 13 days from year to year. Hindu and Sikh seasonal festivals follow specific dates in the luni-solar Bikrami calendar, also called the Punjabi calendar, although their dates vary in the Gregorian calendar while remaining within the same two Gregorian months.

Baisakhi, Basant, and Lohri are also observed by some Punjabi Muslims, although this participation is described as controversial. Islamic clerics and some politicians have attempted to prohibit it because of the religious basis of the Punjabi festivals and their classification as *haram*, or forbidden in Islam.

The sources associate Phulkari with Vaisakhi, Lohri, and the wedding celebration called the Jago. The kaintha is traditionally worn by men and women at the Mayian and Jaggo ceremonies.

## Oral tradition & literature

The folk tales of Punjab include **Heer Ranjha**, **Mirza Sahiban**, and **Sohni Mahiwal**.

Punjabi identity has undergone processes of integration and assimilation, with community building and social cohesion becoming important alongside older clan and tribal structures.

## Language & religion

Punjabi is an Indo-Aryan language spoken natively by the Punjabi people. It is the most popular first language in Pakistan, with 80.5 million native speakers recorded in the 2017 census, and the 11th most popular in India, with 31.1 million native speakers recorded in the 2011 census. It is also spoken by a significant overseas diaspora.

In Pakistan, Punjabi is written in the Shahmukhi alphabet, based on the Perso-Arabic script. In India, it is written in the Gurmukhi alphabet, based on Indic scripts. Punjabi is unusual among Indo-Aryan and Indo-European languages in its use of lexical tone. Related dialects named in the sources include Pahari-Pothwari and Chhachhi.

Punjabi people follow Islam, Sikhism, Hinduism, or Christianity. Punjabi Muslims mainly live in Pakistan and generally write Punjabi in Shahmukhi. Punjabi Sikhs and Hindus predominantly live in India, where Gurmukhi is associated with Sikhs and the scriptures of Sikhism. Sikhism originated in the Punjab region during the 15th century. The sources also describe the historical presence of Buddhism, Jainism, and the Ahmadiyya community in the Punjab.
