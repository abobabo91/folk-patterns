## Overview

The Banjara are nomadic tribes historically known as pastoralists, traders, and transporters of goods across India in caravans called tanda. They moved under elected headmen and at times controlled much of India's overland trade. Their itinerant life shaped an open, unorthodox approach to religion, family, and women that set them apart from orthodox Hindu society. Today they are known above all for their distinctive embroidery, tattooing, and elaborate traditional dress.

## Material culture

Banjara material culture centers on textiles—especially embroidery and mirror-work—which remain central to identity even as the community's traditional occupation has largely disappeared.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Lepo embroidery—stitching mirror, beads, and coins onto cloth—is the signature textile art of Banjara women.

- **Lepo embroidery** (*lepo*) — Mirror-and-coin embroidery that distinguishes Banjara textile work, stitched onto cotton strips at shoulders, neck openings, and hems.
- **Sandur Lambani embroidery** (*Sandur Lambani*) — A unique textile embroidery from Sanduru, Bellary district, Karnataka, with GI tag protection.
- **Cowrie applique** — Cowrie shells worked into embroidered pieces, forming bobbles and tassels on neck-pieces and other garments.
- **Block-printed and tie-dyed cloth** — Indigo tie-dyed and block-printed cotton used for skirts and veils, often combined with fuchsia and green dyes.
- **Mirrorwork with cut-out applique** — Mirrored glass set behind cut-out sections of red cotton, finished with metal coins along edges.

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress, worn for months at a stretch and heavily embellished with glass, beads, and seashells on a red ground, marks marital status and is a key identity marker the community retained.

- **Circular skirt** — Hemmed with red cotton and multi-coloured embroidery, closed with cloth ties, with dense embroidered panel near hem.
- **Embroidered blouse** — Red cotton bodice open at back with ties at side and neck, combining embroidery, mirror-work, and applique.
- **Dopatta** — Cotton and silk head covering with embroidery and mirror-work.
- **Odhni** — Tie-dyed silk veil with fuchsia and green circles, white spots, and mirrored cut-outs finished with metal coins.
- **Kanadoro** — Silver strings traditionally worn around the waist by men, now rarely worn.

### Architecture

Though traditionally migratory, Banjara settled in fixed-village accommodation during monsoon months, but sources do not describe these dwellings.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Everyday objects are primarily embroidered cloth rather than pottery or metal, with functional pieces decorated to high standards.

- **Indhoni** — Waterpot holder with attached neck-piece of red woven cotton entirely covered in embroidery and cowrie shells.
- **Bread bag** — Specialised embroidered cotton bag for carrying bread.
- **Paan bag** — Embroidered cotton bag for carrying paan (betel leaf preparation).
- **Masala and nut holder** — Embroidered cotton container for spices and nuts.
- **Rumal cover** (*rumal*) — Embroidered cotton cover for household and transport items.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ornament was historically silver and ivory, now often plastic and aluminium; metalwork pieces incorporate coins and are worn as necklaces, arm ornaments, and foot rings.

- **Coin necklace** — Metal cylinders strung on twisted cotton cord with coins mounted between them, dated 1903–1918.
- **Shield pendant** — Shield-shaped pendant carrying nine small coins on chains with rupee and anna denominations.
- **Nose ornament** — Brass ring with beads and brass plates, decorated with a floral-patterned brass plate at one side.
- **Woollen armlets** — Paired armlets with bell-shaped metal ornaments and woollen pendants.
- **Tattooing** — Tattooing alongside embroidery forms a significant aspect of Banjara identity and is especially prized.

## Music & performance

The Banjara have sister singers called Dadhis who traditionally travelled singing to the sarangi, and within the community performance divides by gender at religious festivals.

- **Dadhis singers** (*Dadhis*) — Sister community of singers, also called Gajugonia, who travelled village to village singing to the sarangi.
- **Sarangi** — String instrument traditionally accompanying Dadhi singers.
- **Teej singing and dancing** (*Teej*) — August ritual in which girls sing and dance around seedling baskets for nine days.

## Dance & theatre

Ghoomar and Chari are traditional Banjara dance forms, though their steps, costumes, and performance occasions are not documented.

- **Ghoomar** — Traditional Banjara dance form.
- **Chari** — Traditional Banjara dance form.

## Festivals & rituals

Festivals mark the ritual calendar and life transitions, with August bringing multiple celebrations and marriage being the one occasion where women hold paramount importance.

- **Sheetala Bhavani** — Festival in June or July when Banjara pray for protection of their cattle.
- **Gangaur** — August ritual unique to Marwar region where unmarried girls sow seeds in bamboo bowls and water them for nine days to divine marriage prospects.
- **Teej** — August festival when girls sing and dance around seedling baskets.
- **Sagai** — Betrothal ceremony formalizing earlier marriage arrangement, after which the girl remains in parents' household until puberty.
- **Gor panchayat** — Banjara council that sets bride price, approves divorces, and advises headmen on family matters.

## Foodways

Sources do not describe Banjara cooking or staple dishes, but the community historically traded grain, oil, fruit, salt, and forest produce across India.

- **Chironji** — Forest produce traded by Banjara caravans.
- **Mahua** — Forest produce traded by Banjara caravans.
- **Salt trade** — The Labana subgroup specialized in salt trade; Banjara held a monopoly on salt movement in the Deccan and Central Provinces before the East India Company.

## Oral tradition & literature

No Banjara epic or folktale cycle is documented; oral tradition is primarily preserved through Dadhi singers and efforts to recognize Lambadi language in schools.

- **Dadhis singers** (*Dadhis*) — Documented oral practitioners who sing traditional songs.
- **Lambadi textbooks** — Telangana has introduced two textbooks in Lambadi for primary school children as part of language preservation.

## Language & religion

Banjara speak Lambadi with no script of its own, and their loosely held, unorthodox Hindu practice reflects the openness bred by their peripatetic life.

- **Lambadi language** (*Lambadi*) — Language spoken by Banjara, also called Gour Boli, written in Devanagari or local scripts like Telugu or Kannada.
- **Gour Boli** — Alternative name for Lambadi language.
- **Balaji** — Deity worshipped by Banjara.
- **Jagadamba** — Deity worshipped by Banjara.
- **Sevalal** — Most important saint in Banjara tradition, also known as Sevabhaya or Siva Rathor.
