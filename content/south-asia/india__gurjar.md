## Overview

The Gurjar (also Gujjar, Gujar, or Gurjara) are an agricultural ethnic community living mainly in India, Pakistan, and Afghanistan. They are divided into various clan groups and have historically combined agriculture, pastoralism, and nomadic activities. Gurjars are linguistically and religiously diverse, and many speak the Indo-Aryan language Gujari. In India they live in regions including Rajasthan, Gujarat, Haryana, Madhya Pradesh, Punjab, Maharashtra, Jammu and Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, and Uttar Pradesh. In Afghanistan they are concentrated in northeastern areas including Kapisa, Baghlan, Balkh, Kunduz, Takhar, Badakhshan, Nuristan, Laghman, Nangarhar, and Khost. The sources describe both settled cultivators and pastoral communities, including the semi-nomadic Van Gujjars of the Shivalik Hills.

## Material culture

### Architecture

They mention a fort in Parikshitgarh in Meerut district, also known as Qila Parikishatgarh, which is ascribed to the Gurjar king Nain Singh Nagar, and the Metcalfe House in Delhi, which was sacked by Gurjar villagers, but provide no architectural description.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention water buffalo, milk production, and an Andak animal in connection with the Van Gujjars and Gurjars of Badakhshan.

## Music & performance

Gurjar Goth is described as collective chant-like singing performed by Gurjar men and women in Rajasthan. Its themes generally concern **Devnarayan Katha**. Gujari folklore includes songs, ballads, and folktales known as *Dastans*. Hundreds of folk songs have been recorded and published, including “Nooro,” “Tajo,” “Nura Beguma,” “Shupiya,” “Kunjhdi,” and “Mariyan.”

The sources name no musical instruments or specific performance venues. They do, however, document a substantial modern Gujari literary and media sphere. Poets including Sain Qadar Bakhsh, Noon Poonchi, Mian Nizam ud Din, Khuda Bakhsh Zar, Zabih Rajourvi, Shams ud Din Mehjoor Poonchi, Mian Bashir Ahmed, Javaid Rahi, Rafiq Anjum, Milki Ram Kushan, Sarwari Kassana, and Naseem Poonchi contributed to Gujari poetry, prose, or criticism. All India Radio, Doordarshan Kendra, Radio Kashmir Jammu, Radio Kashmir Srinagar, Radio Kashmir Poonch, seven radio stations of Pakistan, and PTV broadcast Gujari programmes or news bulletins.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document religious observances and community rites in limited terms. Rajasthani Gurjars worship Surya, Devnarayan, Shiva, and Bhavani. Devnarayan is described as an avatar of Vishnu, and Devnarayan Katha forms the principal subject of Gurjar Goth singing.

In Haryana, the community has elaborate guidelines for solemnizing marriages and holding other functions. A *mahapanchayat*, described as “the great panchayat,” decided that people seeking dowry would be excommunicated from society.

The Van Gujjars practise seasonal transhumance. During winter they migrate with herds of semi-wild water buffalo to the Shivalik Hills at the foot of the Himalayas; during summer they move to alpine pastures higher in the Himalayas. The sources present this movement as part of their pastoral way of life rather than as a named festival.

## Foodways

They do record milk selling as the primary source of income for the Van Gujjars. These pastoralists treat their animals with great care and do not eat them or sell them for meat.

In the northeastern province of Badakhshan, Gurjar people used Andak meat to treat coronavirus disease during the COVID-19 pandemic because clinics and other health facilities were lacking in their areas. The Gurjar Tribe Council considered the meat of the Andak animal *haram*, meaning forbidden, while many Gurjar people said they had no choice.

## Oral tradition & literature

Gujari folklore includes songs, ballads, and folktales called *Dastans*. The sources name the folk songs “Nooro,” “Tajo,” “Nura Beguma,” “Shupiya,” “Kunjhdi,” and “Mariyan,” but do not summarize their narratives. Gurjar Goth, a collective chant-like form sung by men and women in Rajasthan, generally takes Devnarayan Katha as its subject.

The sources also identify a modern tradition of Gujari creative writing involving poetry, prose, and criticism. Gujari publications include encyclopedias, dictionaries, grammars, fiction, non-fiction, poetry, folklore, and research. The Jammu and Kashmir Academy of Arts, Culture and Languages established a Gujari Department in its Central Office in the 1970s. The Government of Azad Kashmir, the University of Azad Jammu and Kashmir, and the Allama Iqbal Open University have begun studies intended to protect and preserve the Gujari language of Azad Kashmir.

A Gojri Bahali Programme, or Gojri Revival Programme, was launched in Pakistan to encourage Gujjars who had stopped speaking Gujari to readopt it as their mother language. On 25 December 2022, Gujjars in Lower Dir District requested that Gujari be taught in schools.

## Language & religion

Gujari is an Indo-Aryan language classified within the Rajasthani languages under the Western Indo-Aryan language subfamily. It is spoken by Gurjars in northern India and Pakistan and eastern Afghanistan. The language is written in Perso-Arabic and Devanagari scripts; Nastaliq is also used, particularly in Pakistan, while Devanagari is more common in several Indian states. The sources identify two major dialects, Eastern Gujari and Western Gujari, as well as varieties including Van Gujari, Bakerwali Gujari, and Banihari Gujari.

Gujari is linguistically related to Urdu, Hindi, Marwari, Gujarati, Mewati, Punjabi, Pahadi, Haryanvi, and Hindko. Its folklore and literature include songs, ballads, folktales, poetry, prose, and criticism. Gujari was recognized by the government of India-administered Jammu and Kashmir in 1999 through inclusion in the sixth schedule of the former state constitution, and it was admitted as a mother tongue by the Jammu and Kashmir Board of School Education in 2006.

Gurjars mostly follow Islam, followed by Hinduism; the 1988 estimate cited by the source gives 53% Muslims, 46.8% Hindus, and 0.2% Sikhs in the Indian subcontinent. Rajasthani Gurjars worship Surya, Devnarayan, Shiva, and Bhavani.
