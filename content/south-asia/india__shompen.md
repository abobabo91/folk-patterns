## Overview

The Shompen, also called Shom Pen, are Indigenous people of the interior of Great Nicobar Island, part of the Indian union territory of the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. In 2001 their population was estimated at approximately 300; another estimate gives approximately 400, although no census had been conducted for the Shompen language. They live in the hilly hinterland of the Great Nicobar Biosphere Reserve and practice a hunter-gatherer subsistence economy, while also keeping pigs and farming yams, roots, vegetables, and tobacco. The Shompen are designated as a Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Group within the list of Scheduled Tribes. Their language or languages are poorly documented and are classified differently: they have been described as related to Southern Nicobarese varieties, a language isolate, or a Southern Nicobaric language.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Bark cloth** — Bark cloth is used for loincloths, skirts, and occasionally shawls.

### Clothing & dress

Men wear a short, thin loincloth made of bark cloth, covering the genitals without a “tail” of cloth in front. Women wear a knee-length skirt of bark cloth and occasionally a shawl of bark cloth covering the shoulders. Traditional attire includes only clothing below the waist, and both sexes are barefoot. The sources describe bead necklaces, bamboo armbands, bamboo ear plugs, and a loincloth belt. A man usually carried a bow and arrows, a spear, a hatchet, a knife, and a fire drill through his loincloth belt.

### Architecture

Shompen huts are built to house 4 people, and villages consist of 4 to 5 families. Once a child is grown enough, he makes his own hut. Lowland Shompen huts are built on stilts, with walls of woven material on a wood frame and roofs of thatched palm fronds. Highland Shompen houses are built on the ground from the same materials. Interiors are covered with mats, with sleeping mats at one end and tools and utensils hung on the walls and rafters. Cooking is done outside. In the late 1980s, the Shompens were living in ten groups ranging in size from 2 to 22 individuals, scattered across the interior of the island.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do describe bows and arrows, numerous types of spears, spear throwers, fire drills, hatchets, knives, mats, and tools and utensils hung on house walls and rafters. The Shompen do not use quivers but carry arrows by hand.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Men’s decoration is limited to bead necklaces and armbands. Women wear bamboo ear plugs called *ahav*, bead necklaces called *naigaak*, and bamboo armbands called *geegap*.

## Festivals & rituals

 They record that a polling station was set up in the Shompen area for the election of 2014 and that Shompen people participated in the democratic process for the first time.

## Foodways

The Shompen practice a hunter-gatherer subsistence economy. They hunt wild game such as pigs, birds, and small animals, and forage for fruits and forest foods. They also keep pigs and farm yams, roots, vegetables, and tobacco. Cooking is done outside the house.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They state that very little reliable information has been added since the nineteenth century, partly because access to the Nicobar Islands has been restricted for foreign researchers since Indian independence.

## Language & religion

Shompen, or Shom Peng, is a language or group of languages spoken on Great Nicobar Island. At least two Shompen languages have been reported, and the varieties may not be related to each other. The language is poorly described, with much of the available material coming from the nineteenth century and later publications described as limited or problematic. Shompen has been classified as related to Southern Nicobarese varieties, a language isolate, or a Southern Nicobaric language rather than a separate branch of Austroasiatic.
