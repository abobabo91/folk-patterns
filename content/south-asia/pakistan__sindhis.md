## Overview

Sindhis are an Indo-Aryan ethnolinguistic group who speak the Sindhi language and originate from Sindh, a region of present-day Pakistan. Their historical homeland lies south of the Indus and includes southeastern Balochistan, the Kacchi Plain, Lasbela and Makran, the Bahawalpur region of Punjab, the Kutch region of Gujarat, and Jaisalmer and Barmer in Rajasthan. Sindhis also live in India, particularly in Gujarat, Maharashtra and Rajasthan, as well as in Afghanistan, Sri Lanka, Hong Kong and Singapore. India’s 2011 census recorded an estimated 2,770,000 Sindhis, while Afghanistan was described as having an estimated 25,000. Sindh contains ruins of the Indus Valley Civilization, including Mohenjo Daro, and Sindhi culture has retained a distinctive identity despite geographical separation.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Sindh contains ruins and remains associated with the Indus Valley Civilization. Mohenjo Daro, built around 2500 BCE, had standardized bricks, street grids and covered sewer systems. The ancient Indus cities were noted for baked-brick houses, elaborate drainage systems, water-supply systems and clusters of large non-residential buildings. Mohenjo Daro was abandoned in the 19th century BCE, rediscovered in the 1920s, and designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1980.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe the conquest of Sindh under Muhammad ibn Qasim in 712 CE as commemorated in modern Pakistan on the 10th day of Ramadan as Yom-e Bab ul-Islam. They also state that the Shri Ramapir Temple in Tandoallahyar holds an annual festival that is the second largest Hindu pilgrimage in Pakistan. No further calendar of Sindhi seasonal festivals is provided.

The sources describe Islam as the religion of the majority in Sindh, with Muslims making up over 90% of the population. They discuss Sufi saints, mystics and shrines, including the popular legend that 125,000 Sufi saints and mystics are buried on Makli Hill near Thatta. They also state that Sindhi Hindus migrated to India after partition in 1947, and that Hinduism survived as a significant minority through Muslim rule until partition.

## Oral tradition & literature

Sindh has a strong Sufi literary association in the supplied material. Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai is identified as a Sufi poet who lived in Sindh, and the sources state that he composed *Shah Jo Risalo*, a classic Sindhi text, during the period when lower Sindh was governed from Thatta under the Mughal Empire and upper Sindh was ruled by the Kalhora dynasty. The sources also identify Sindhi scholars connected with hadith, fiqh, poetry and scientific translation, including Abu al-'Ata Sindhi and Abu Mashar Sindhi.

The sources mention a popular legend concerning 125,000 Sufi saints and mystics buried on Makli Hill near Thatta.

## Language & religion

Sindhi is an Indo-Aryan language. The name Sindhi derives from the Sanskrit *Sindhu*, translated in the source as “river” or “sea body.” The Greeks used “Indos” for the Indus River and the surrounding region. The historical spelling “Sind” was discontinued in 1988 by an amendment passed in the Sindh Assembly, after which “Sindh” became the spelling used in the source. In Balochi, traditional terms for Sindhis include *Jadgal* and *Jamote*.

Islam has a long history in Sindh, beginning with the conquest associated with Muhammad ibn Qasim in 712 CE. The majority of Muslim Sindhis follow Sunni Hanafi fiqh, with a Shia Ithna 'ashariyah minority. Sufism has strongly influenced Sindhi Muslim culture, and Sufi shrines are widespread across Sindh. Hinduism and Buddhism were predominant before the Arab Islamic conquest; Buddhism later declined and disappeared in the region, while Hinduism continued as a significant minority. The sources also mention approximately 10,000 Sikhs in Sindh.
