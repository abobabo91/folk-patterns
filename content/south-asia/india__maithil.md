## Overview

The Maithils, also known as Maithili people or Tirhutiya, are an Indo-Aryan cultural and ethno-linguistic group whose native language is Maithili. They inhabit the Mithila region, comprising Northern and Eastern Bihar and Northeastern Jharkhand in India, and Madhesh Province together with some Terai districts of Bagmati and Koshi Provinces in Nepal. Darbhanga is described as one of Mithila’s core centers, while Madhubani is identified as the place where Mithila painting originated. The region is associated with the Videha Kingdom, Hindu learning, the birthplace of Sita, and distinctive clothing, household structures, religious practices, festivals and cuisine.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Mithila Painting** — The sources identify Madhubani as the place where Mithila painting originated and describe it as a major part of Maithili culture.

**Jamdani** — Jamdani is named among the traditional attire of women in Mithila.

**Banarisi** — Banarisi is listed as a traditional textile associated with women’s clothing in Mithila.

**Bhagalpuri** — Bhagalpuri is named among the textiles used in traditional women’s attire. No further catalogue or technical description is provided.

### Clothing & dress

Men’s clothing includes *kurta* and *dhoti*, worn with a Mithila Painting-bordered maroon-coloured *Gamchha*. The Gamchha is described as a symbol of passion, love, bravery and courage. Men also wear a gold nose ring, a *Balla* on the wrist and a *Mithila Paag* on the head. The sources associate the nose ring with prosperity, happiness and wealth and connect it with Lord Vishnu.

Women traditionally wore white or yellow sarees with a red border. The *Laal-Paara*, a traditional red-bordered white or yellow saree, is worn on some special occasions. Women also wear *Shakha-Pola* with a *lanthi* in the hand; this is described as mandatory after marriage in Mithila. Women’s traditional attire includes Jamdani, Banarisi and Bhagalpuri textiles. During Chhaith, women wear a pure cotton dhoti without stitching.

### Architecture

Traditionally, Maithils lived in *Badaghars*, described as longhouses accommodating large families of many generations, sometimes numbering 40–50 people. Household members pooled their labour, contributed their income, shared expenditure and used one kitchen. In the courtyard of a Maithil family, a *Dalaan* provided a place for relaxation and gathering by family members, relatives and close neighbours. It was also used for cultural activities of the family and neighbourhood.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention a household courtyard, a Dalaan, a shared kitchen and the stove used in festival food practices, but provide no further account of their construction or material culture.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Men wear a gold ring on the nose, described as a symbol of prosperity, happiness and wealth and as inspired by Lord Vishnu. They also wear a Balla on the wrist. Women wear Shakha-Pola with a lanthi in the hand after marriage in Mithila.

## Music & performance

They mention Jyotirishwar Thakur as a 14th-century poet, playwright and musician who composed the earliest prose work in the Maithili language, the *Varna Ratnakara*. They also identify Nagarjun as a renowned Maithili poet, Sharda Sinha as an Indian folk singer and Maithili Thakur as an Indian singer and politician. No descriptions are supplied of their instruments, repertories, performance settings or relationships to weddings, religious ceremonies, courts or other gatherings.

## Dance & theatre

Jyotirishwar Thakur is described as a poet, playwright and musician.

## Festivals & rituals

Many festivals are celebrated throughout the year in Mithila. Chhath Puja, Durga Puja and Kali puja are described as among the most important celebrations. Local observances include Ashtajam Puja, Lakhram Puja and Chaurchan Puja, which are followed by the Maithils in devotion to God.

The sources describe clothing connected with particular occasions. Women wear the Laal-Paara on some special occasions, while during Chhaith they wear a pure cotton dhoti without stitching. In the food calendar, Satuani is observed on the eve of Jur Sital, identified as the Maithil New Year. Women prepare a feast including kadhi-badi-bhaat and tarua. After cooking, the stove is cleaned, worshipped and left unused for the following day. Bari-bhaat prepared the day before is eaten cold with curd or raw mango chutney. Tilkut is prepared with dahi-chura and exchanged among relatives.

Mithila’s religious practices include worship of Baraham Baba, Gosaun Devata, Brahma Baba, Goddess Bhagawati, Kuldevata and Kuldevi.

## Foodways

Maithil cuisine is the traditional cooking style of Maithils in the Mithila region of India and Nepal. It includes rice, wheat, fish, meat, spices, herbs and natural edibles. Preparations are served together on a platter and consumed at once. The staple meal is *bhat* with dal, roti, tarkari and achar, prepared from rice, lentils, wheat flour, vegetables and pickles. Mustard oil is the traditional cooking medium. *Panchforan* combines jeer, ajwain, mangrail, sounf and methi.

Mutton and fish are traditional non-vegetarian foods, while chicken is regarded as an inferior option. Mutton and fish have religious and cultural significance as sacrificial offerings to Durga and Kali. Vegetarian dishes include *saagak jhor*, *sanna*, *bhajia*, *tarua*, *Daail-jhimni* and *Thadia saagak teeman*. Fish dishes include *Machh-bhat* and *Machhak jhor*.

Mithila Makhana is a fox nut variety grown in the region’s ponds. It is widely used and distributed by the Maithil community during Kojagara Puja. A proverb identifies fish, betel leaf and fox nut—*maachh, paan, makhaan*—as the three defining foods of Mithila. Breakfast foods include chai with chura and ghugni, poori with aloo dum, jilebi, chini wala roti, pua and suzi ke halwa. Snacks and sweets include chure ka bhujia, makai ke lawa, masalgar murhi, jhilli, samosas, launglati, kachari, pyaaji, kheer, malpua, laddoo and khaja.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources associate Mithila with the Ramayana and identify Janaka as King of Mithila and Sita as Princess of the Mithila Kingdom and wife of King Rama. The region is described as the birthplace of Sita in relation to Hindu tradition, while Sitamarhi and Janakpur are both identified as places many people claim as her birthplace.

Maithili literary history includes Vidyapati, a 14th/15th-century Maithili and Sanskrit poet-saint; Jyotirishwar Thakur, a 14th-century poet, playwright and musician; and Nagarjun, described as a renowned Maithili poet. The sources also name Maithili writers and cultural figures including Ramdhari Singh ‘Dinkar’, Phanishwar Nath ‘Renu’, Acharya Ramlochan Saran and Maithili Thakur.

## Language & religion

Maithili is the common language of the Maithil people. It is described as an Indo-Aryan language, one of the recognised regional languages of India listed in the Eighth Schedule of the Indian Constitution, and the second national language of Nepal in the Interim Constitution of Nepal. The *Tirhuta* script, also known as the *Mithilakshar* script, has historically been used for Maithili. During the 20th century, most Maithili writers gradually adopted Devanagari, although Tirhuta is still sometimes used by religious pundits for ceremonial letters and documents.

Maithil religious practice is based on orthodox Hinduism, and Mithila is described as a principal seat of Hindu learning. Local traditions include worship of Baraham Baba and Gosaun Devata. Brahma Sthan, also called Dihawar Sthan, is associated with an old pipal tree and the worship of Brahma Baba, regarded as the protector God of the village. Maharani Sthan is associated with Goddess Bhagawati, while Gosaunik Ghar is the household place for worship of Kuldevata and Kuldevi. Shaivism is described as one of Mithila’s oldest continuing traditions.
