## Overview

The Bonda, also known as the Bondo, Bondo Poraja, Bhonda, or Remo, are a Munda ethnic group living in the isolated hill regions of the Malkangiri district of southwestern Odisha, India, near the junction of Odisha, Chhattisgarh, and Andhra Pradesh. The source describes them as a Scheduled Tribe and gives a population of approximately 12,000 in the 2011 census, while also distinguishing Upper Bonda and Lower Bonda populations. Their language belongs to the Munda branch of the Austroasiatic language family and is closely related to Gutob. Bonda culture is documented through distinctive dress, body adornment, marriage and death customs, barter practices, oral tradition, and a language whose traditional written system is not recorded.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Bonda women are generally semi-clothed and wear a *ringa*, a length of cloth covering the waist. Their torsos are covered with strings of colourful beads. Women have shorn heads and wear two headbands: *turuba*, made of grass, and *lobeda*, made of beads. The *turuba* secures the *lobeda* and prevents it from slipping.

Women also wear metal neck bands called *khagla*, made from aluminum, and bead necklaces called *Mali*. The sources describe the *khagla* and *Mali*, together with the *ringa*, as clothing for the upper body. Women wear bangles on their arms, while both men and women wear brass earrings called *limbi* and aluminum finger rings called *orti*.

Bonda men and women have different adornment practices according to age and marital status. From the ages of eight or nine, males may wear headbands called *ornaghboh*, bangles called *sungrai*, necklaces called *thangimali*, earrings called *unsurul*, and rings called *sanbah*. For bachelors or newly married men, wearing these ornaments is customary; once married, men typically do not continue adorning their bodies with additional ornaments.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do record aluminum *khagla* neck bands and *orti* finger rings, brass *limbi* earrings, and the use of cows, land, trees, and animals in marriage payments, debts, and debt bondage.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Bonda body adornment is especially associated with women’s clothing and forest life. Women wear aluminum *khagla* neck bands, bead necklaces called *Mali*, brass *limbi* earrings, aluminum *orti* finger rings, and bangles. The sources state that women’s ornaments are thought to help protect them from injuries and attacks by wild animals while hunting and foraging in the forest.

Women shave their heads and wear the grass *turuba* and beaded *lobeda* headbands. Men’s age- and marital-status ornaments include *ornaghboh*, *sungrai*, *thangimali*, *unsurul*, and *sanbah*.

## Music & performance

 They state that the government-school curriculum uses prayers and songs as part of the process of bringing the Bonda into the mainstream. The sources also mention that men brew and consume liquor from rice, palm, and the mahua flower, but they do not associate this practice with a named musical tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do document marriage and death customs. A form of dowry called *Gining* is paid for brides, and the items used in *Gining* help determine how many arranged marriages will take place. The number of cows depends on the social status of the girl. Bonda boys are expected to marry between the ages of 10 and 12, while Bonda girls largely marry boys who are at least five to ten years younger than themselves.

Divorce is called *Lung Sisi*. In cases where a Bonda woman is divorced for adultery, the former husband may demand double the price paid for the marriage. The village council determines the severity of the case and reaches a decision based on the number of cows returned. If a man caused the wrong resulting in divorce, he can no longer marry through an arranged-marriage system.

When a death, or *mora*, occurs, it is customary to sacrifice a cow on the tenth day. This practice is called *Gaitang*. The sources also state that obligatory marriages, deaths, fines, and other socio-religious practices contribute to economic hardship and debt.

## Foodways

 They state that crop production is hardly able to feed the population and that, in order to overcome starvation, the Bonda, or *Ku duburu Remo*, take out loans called *Kalantar* or *Badi*. These loans are often used to pay for bride prices, fines, and socio-religious rites.

Men brew and consume liquor made from rice, palm, and the mahua flower. The sources also mention that Bonda people forage for food in the forest and that women hunt and forage. No further food names or culinary practices are described.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Bonda sources preserve a clothing-origin legend relating to the *Ramayana*. According to the account, Bonda women encountered Sita while she was bathing at a pond in the Bonda hills. After they sniggered on seeing her naked, Sita cursed them to remain naked and to have shaven heads. When the women pleaded for forgiveness, Sita gave them a piece of cloth torn from her sari. The legend explains the wearing of the *ringa* and the practice of shaving women’s heads. They state that the Bonda language has no traditional written system recorded and that the absence of a script or text for Remo contributes to the threat of language extinction. The Government of Odisha established the Bonda Development Agency in 1977 in response to cultural decline.

## Language & religion

Bonda, also known as Bondo or Remosam, is a south Munda language of the Austroasiatic language family. It belongs to the Southern subgroup of the Munda branch and is part of the Gutob-Remo branch. The Bonda people call themselves *Remo*; the word is glossed in the sources as “people” or “human,” and *Remosam* means “the human language” in their native tongue.

The language is spoken in Odisha and has Plains Remo and Hills Remo subdivisions. Plains Remo is localized in 35 villages throughout the Khairpat within the Malkangiri district, while Hills Remo is localized in the Jeypore Hills region. Bonda is a spoken language with no traditional written system recorded. It follows the SOV, or Subject + Object + Verb, sequence, although other word orders are possible.

The chief god of the Bondo people is called Mahāprabhu, meaning “Great Lord.” The sources also state that outside influences introduced new gods, while school prayers and songs participate in the process of mainstreaming.
