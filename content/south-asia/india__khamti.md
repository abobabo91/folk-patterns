## Overview

The Tai Khamti, also known as the Hkamti Shan or simply Khamti, are a Tai ethnic group of India, Myanmar and China. In India, they are found chiefly in Namsai district and Changlang district of Arunachal Pradesh, with smaller numbers in Lakhimpur district, Dhemaji district and Munglang Khamti village in Tinsukia district of Assam. Those living around the Tengapani basin of Arunachal Pradesh are described as descendants of migrants who came during the eighteenth century from the Shan region of Hkamti Long, in the western source of the Irrawaddy River Valley. According to the 2011 census of India, the Tai Khamtis numbered 16,944. Their language is Southwestern Tai, closely related to Thai and Lao, and their culture includes a distinctive script, raised-floor houses, named garments and foods, swords, religious sculpture, dramatic dance and the annual festival Sangken.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Phanoi* — A multi-colored sarong worn as part of traditional men's dress. The source identifies its material culture as clothing.

*Sinh* — A deep-colored long sarong worn by women, made from cotton or silk.

*Sinn* — A plain black long wrap-around sarong worn by married women.

*Langwat* — A shorter green wrap-around cloth worn over the *sinn* by married women.

*Phamai* — A colored silk scarf included in women's attire.

The sources name these textiles and their colors or materials.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional men's clothing consists of a full-sleeved cotton shirt called *siu pachai* and a multi-colored sarong called *phanoi*. Women's attire includes the long-sleeve shirt *siu pasao*, the deep-colored long sarong *sinh*, made from cotton or silk, and the colored silk scarf *phamai*. Married women wear a plain black long wrap-around sarong called *sinn*, with the shorter green wrap-around cloth *langwat* over it.

The Khamti wear their hair in a large knot supported by a white turban called *pha-ho*. Chiefs wear long silk coats, and their hair is drawn up from the back and sides into a roll four to five inches in length. This roll is encircled with an embroidered band whose fringed and tasseled ends hang behind.

### Architecture

Khamti houses are built on raised floors and have thatched roofs. They are constructed low enough that the walls remain concealed. Wooden planks are used for flooring, while the walls are made from bamboo splices.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do document the plough *thaie*, drawn by a single animal such as an ox or buffalo, and, in ancient times, even an elephant. Other named objects include swords called *pha-nap*, poisoned bamboo spikes called *panjis*, spears, bows and arrows, shields, firearms resembling old flint muskets and horse pistols. Khamti priests carve religious statues from wood, bone or ivory, and Khamti craftspeople shape ivory handles for weapons.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Khamti jewelry includes bright amber earplugs, coral, beaded necklaces, silver hairpins, bangles and gold ornaments. Khamti men traditionally tattoo their bodies. Hair is worn in a large knot supported by the white turban *pha-ho*. Chiefs have a distinctive hair roll encircled by an embroidered band, with fringed and tasseled ends hanging behind.

## Music & performance

The sources describe music mainly in relation to dance and festival performance. The Cockfight Dance, *Kaa Kong Tou Kai*, is accompanied by drumbeats from the *kongpat*, cymbals called *paiseng* and gongs called *mong-seeing*. The performance is enacted by two or four dancers wearing headgear shaped like a cock's head and imitates a cockfight, a tradition once used to entertain kings.

Drums, dances and festivities accompany the procession during Sangken, the main Khamti festival. The sources also state that many Tai-Khamti dances have religious themes and that their folk dances have roots in Southeast Asian countries such as Thailand and Myanmar.

## Dance & theatre

*Ka Poong Tai* is described as one of the main dramatic art forms of the Tai-Khamti. Unlike many traditional Arunachali dances, Khamti dance is a dramatic performance reflecting Khamti culture.

The Peacock Dance, *Kaa Kingnara Kingnari*, depicts the graceful movements of a mythical half-human, half-peacock creature from the Himalayas and reflects Buddhist beliefs in nature. The Cockfight Dance, *Kaa Kong Tou Kai*, imitates a cockfight and uses cock-shaped headgear, drumbeats, cymbals and gongs. The Deer Dance, *Kaa-Toe*, is celebrated during the light festival in October and marks the return of the Buddha after preaching to his mother and other spirits. The Demon Dance, *Kaa Phi Phai*, portrays the victory of good over evil: the Buddha achieves enlightenment despite the attempts of Mara, the king of evil spirits, to disturb his meditation. The sources associate these performances with religious belief and entertainment.

## Festivals & rituals

The main Khamti festival is Sangken, celebrated on 14 April. During the festival, people of all tribes, castes, cultures and races participate in rituals involving the splashing of clean water, which symbolizes peace and purity. Buddha images are taken for a ceremonial bath, and a procession is accompanied by drums, dances and other festivities. The celebration lasts for three days. Locals prepare homemade sweets and exchange gifts.

Other festivals named in the sources are Poi-Pee-Mau, identified as the Tai Khampti New Year, Mai-Kasung-Phai, Khoa-Wa and Poat-Wa. The Deer Dance is celebrated during the light festival in October, called *Nuen-Sip-Eit*, and marks the Buddha's return after preaching to his mother and other spirits.

## Foodways

Rice is the Khamti staple food and is usually complemented by vegetables, meat and fish. The Khamti cultivate paddy rice called *khow*, mustard or sesame seeds called *nga*, and potatoes called *man-kala*. They use a plough called *thaie* in settled agriculture.

The Khamti drink a beer made from rice called *lau*, although it is not served during festivals. Named dishes include *khao puk*, made from sticky rice and sesame seeds; *khao lam*, described as bamboo rice; *paa sa*, a fresh river fish soup with special herbs; *paa som*; and *nam som*. Beef is considered taboo.

Homes typically have a prayer room where people pray every morning and evening, offering flowers called *nam taw yongli* and food called *khao tang som*. The Tai-Khamti are credited in the source with being the first people to introduce tea drinking to India, while the same source notes that substantial documentation for the history of tea consumption in the Indian subcontinent before the colonial era is lacking. It states that the Singpho and Khamti have consumed tea since the 12th century.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources mention Jataka tales, folktale collections and the study of literature, but they do not summarize particular Khamti stories, epics, proverbs or riddles. *Lengdon's Legacy: Tai Khamti Folktales From Arunachal Pradesh* is identified as a 2010 collection edited by Tertia Sandhu and published in India by the National Book Trust.

Khamti script is traditionally taught in monasteries alongside the Tripitaka, Jataka tales, codes of conduct, doctrines and philosophy, history, law codes, astrology and palmistry.

## Language & religion

Khamti is a Southwestern Tai language spoken in India and Myanmar. It is a Daic language belonging to the Kadai, Kam-Tai, Tai, Southwestern, Northwest branch, and is closely related to Thai, Lao and Shan. The name “Khamti” translates as “place of gold,” from *kham*, “gold,” and *-ti*, “place.” Three dialects are named: North Burma Khamti, India Khamti and Sinkaling Khamti. Khamti speakers are bilingual, often in Assamese and Burmese.

The Tai Khamti follow Theravada Buddhism and are described as strong believers in it. Their writing system, called *Lik-Tai*, closely resembles the Northern Shan script of Myanmar and is derived from the *Lik Tho Ngok* script. It includes 35 letters, consisting of 17 consonants and 14 vowels. The first printed book in the script was published in 1960; it was revised in 1992 by the Tai Literature Committee in Chongkham and modified in 2003 with tone markings by scholars from Northern Myanmar and Arunachal Pradesh.
