## Overview

The Birhor, also called Birhul, are a tribal or Adivasi forest people traditionally known for a nomadic way of life. They live primarily in Jharkhand, India, especially in areas corresponding to the former Hazaribagh, Ranchi and Singhbhum districts, and are also found in Orissa, Chhattisgarh, West Bengal and Maharashtra. The sources describe their population as around 10,000, while other estimates cited in the sources place it below 20,000. Their language is Birhor, a highly endangered member of the Munda group within the Austroasiatic language family. Birhor subsistence has traditionally depended on forest resources, including hunting, gathering, honey collection and the manufacture of vine-fibre ropes. Their settlements, livelihood, language and cultural practices have all been affected by settlement policies, forest degradation and urban encroachment.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Birhor temporary settlements are known as *tandas* or *bands*. They consist of at least half a dozen conical huts made from leaves and branches. These structures are associated with the group’s traditionally mobile way of life.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Birhor household possessions traditionally included earthen utensils, digging implements, hunting and trapping implements, rope-making implements and baskets. Aluminium and steel later entered Birhor huts. Birhors also make ropes from the fibres of a particular species of vine and sell them in markets near agricultural communities.

## Festivals & rituals

Birhor traditional religious beliefs are described as similar to those of the Hos. Mundari deities, including Sing Bonga, identified as the Sun God, and Hapram, identified as ancestral spirits, hold an important place in their traditional beliefs. Hinduism and Pentecostal Christianity are also making significant inroads into Birhor society.

## Foodways

The Birhor economy has traditionally involved nomadic gathering and hunting, particularly of monkeys. They also trap rabbits and titirs, described as a small bird, and collect and sell honey. Forest products including honey, fruits and fibres contribute to subsistence.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Birhor language and cultural context are described as poorly documented and threatened, while the RWAAI Repository and Workspace for Austroasiatic Intangible Heritage and its Birhor materials are identified as documentation resources. The Birhor Living Dictionary is also listed among the external resources associated with the language.

## Language & religion

Birhor is a highly endangered Munda language within the Austroasiatic language family. It is spoken by the Birhor people in Chhattisgarh, Odisha, West Bengal and Maharashtra, while Birhor communities are also found in Jharkhand. Most Birhors speak Hindi, and a small minority know Ho. Birhor has similarities with Santali, Mundari and Ho. Birhors also use Sadri, Santali, Ho and Mundari in the areas where they move. The sources report a first-language literacy rate of 0.02 percent in 1971 and Hindi literacy of around 10 percent. Traditional beliefs include Sing Bonga and Hapram; Hinduism and Pentecostal Christianity are also present.
