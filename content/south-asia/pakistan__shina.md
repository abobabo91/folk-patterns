## Overview

The Shina, also known as Gilgitis, are an Indo-Aryan ethnolinguistic group primarily residing in Gilgit-Baltistan and Indus Kohistan in Pakistan, as well as in the Gurez and Dras regions of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh in India. Their geographic area of predominance is called Shinaki. In Pakistan, they are the major ethnic group of Gilgit-Baltistan, and many Shina people have also settled in Karachi and Islamabad for employment, business, and education. The Shina language belongs to the Dardic branch of the Indo-European family. Its speakers are estimated at 600,000 in Gilgit-Baltistan and Kohistan in one source, and at 1,146,000 in Pakistan according to Ethnologue (2018) in another. Shina culture documented here includes the Chili festival, cedar worship, changing religious practices, and a distinctive spoken and written language.

## Music & performance

The “Shina people” article identifies Hasan Raheem as a singer-songwriter and Ali Sadpara as a mountaineer.

## Festivals & rituals

The Shina festival of **Chili** marks the commencement of wheat sowing. The source compares it with Lohri and Makar Sankranti, celebrations elsewhere in the Indian subcontinent. Chili formerly had a connection with the worship of the cedar. Cedar worship is described as prevalent among historic Hindu communities of the Himalayas, extending from the Hindu Kush region to Himachal and Uttarakhand. The cedar is known as Deodar, a term derived from the Sanskrit words Devadaru, meaning “wood of the gods,” and from deva, “god,” and dāru, “wood, etym. tree.” The cedar is also sacred in Kafiristan.

The sources describe earlier Hindu and Buddhist practices among the Shina. In Gilgit, Hunza, and Nagar, Hindu Shins formerly practised sati, which ceased before A.D. 1740. The source states that 1877 was the last year in that region when Shina men underwent Hindu cremation rites.

## Foodways

They do record a historical dietary restriction concerning beef and cow’s milk. Even after the majority of the ethnic group converted to Islam, orthodox Shins continued to neither eat beef, drink cow’s milk, nor touch any vessel containing it, because a dead cow or a suckling calf was considered especially unclean. Purification was necessary if garments touched such a vessel or animal.

## Oral tradition & literature

Shina was mostly a spoken language and was not traditionally a written language, although the supplied language article states that orthography was standardized in the late 2010s and that primers and dictionaries endorsed by the government of Gilgit-Baltistan were published. The sources name Sasken Shina, a 1992 Sociolinguistic Survey of Shina, and Shina Language Textbooks for Class 5 and Class 6 as further reading or language materials.

## Language & religion

Shina is an Indo-Aryan language of the Dardic branch in the Indo-European family. It is primarily spoken by the Shina people of northern Pakistan, especially in Gilgit-Baltistan and Kohistan, with smaller communities in the Neelam Valley of Azad Jammu and Kashmir, the Gurez valley of Jammu and Kashmir, and the Dras valley and far north of the Kargil district in Ladakh. Outlying varieties or related languages named in the sources include Brokskat, Kundal Shahi, Palula, Sawi, Ushojo, and Kalkoti.

Shina was unwritten until a few decades ago, and its orthography was standardized only in the late 2010s. Two main orthographic conventions are described: one in the Pakistani-administered area of Gilgit-Baltistan and in Kohistan, and another in the Indian-administered area of Dras, Ladakh. The language has three contrasting tones—level, rising, and falling—and distinguishes vowel length and nasalization.

The Shina historically practised Hinduism and Buddhism. Islam reached the Shina around the 12th century, with waves of conversion during the 14th, 16th, and 17th centuries. By the late 19th century, most Shina were adherents of Islam. A small minority of related groups, chiefly the Brokpa community, continue to practise Buddhism, although the majority of them are adherents of Islam. The sources also describe cedar worship among historic Hindu communities associated with the Shina festival of Chili.
