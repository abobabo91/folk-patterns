## Overview

The Magars, also spelled Mangar and Mongar, are a Tibeto-Burman ethnic group native to Nepal and Northeast India. Their first home was west of the Gandaki River, around Gulmi, Arghakhanchi, and Palpa; the medieval Magarat extended from Palpa to Rukum Rolpa. In the 2021 Nepal census, 2,013,498 people identified as Magar, representing 6.9% of Nepal’s population. Magars are distributed especially through Gandaki, Lumbini, and Karnali provinces and are also found in India, the United Kingdom, Hong Kong, Singapore, Malaysia, Brunei, Myanmar, the United Arab Emirates, Japan, Australia, Saudi Arabia, the United States of America, and South Korea. Their languages belong to the Bodic branch of the Tibetan family. Magar folk culture includes named dress, food, festivals, songs, dances, religious practices, and several dialect traditions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources name clothing and ornaments.

**Kachhad** — A wrap-on-loincloth worn by men.

**Phariya** — A garment worn by women.

**Lunghi** — A women’s garment listed alongside the *phariya*.

**Chaubandhi cholo** — A closed blouse worn by women.

**Patuka** — A heavy waistband worn by women.

**Mujetro** — A shawl-like garment worn on the head.

**Bhangra** — A men’s garment named with the *kachhad*, *bhoto*, and Nepali topi.

### Clothing & dress

Men wear the *kachhad*, a wrap-on-loincloth, together with a *bhangra*, a *bhoto* or shirt of vest, and the usual Nepali topi. Women wear the *phariya* or *lunghi*, the *chaubandhi cholo* or closed blouse, and the heavy *patuka* waistband. Their head covering is the *mujetro*, described as a shawl-like garment. Dress also appears in performance: in Sorathi or Maruni dance, the main dancer wears a woman’s dress, and the dress is ritually arranged with flowers, rice, and other items on a brass plate or winnower before the dance begins.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do mention a brass plate or winnower used in the ritual preparation of the costume and ornaments for Sorathi or Maruni dance.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Magar women wear *madwari* on the ears, *bulaki* on the nose, and *phuli* on the left nostril. Other named ornaments include the silver coin necklace *haari*, the *pote* of yellow and green beads with a *tilhari* gold cylinder, and *jantar*, *dhungri*, *naugedi*, *phul*, *kuntha*, and *raiya*. Women also wear silver and glass bangles, together with *sirbandhi*, *sirphuli*, and *chandra* on the head. The latter are large pieces of gold beaten into elongated and circular shapes. Magar men generally wear few ornaments, although some wear silver earrings called *gokkul*, hanging from the earlobes. Magar girls wear an amulet or locket necklace. Women of the lower hills and high-altitude areas wear silver ornaments with embedded *muga* stones and *kantha*.

## Music & performance

Magars have contributed to Nepali folk songs and dances, and both men and women participate in folk song and dance. In Sorathi or Maruni dance, one person beats a *Madal* or *Rani Madal*. The sources describe the drummer’s role as especially important. Another performer mimics the main dancer and is considered one of the dancer’s protectors; this person wears a mask and entertains the crowd through gestures.

Sorathi or Maruni is practiced during the Tihar festival. The performance is connected with mythology and is believed to have divine origin. Before dancing begins, the group worships the *Madal*, the dancer’s dress, and the ornaments to be used. The performers then offer obeisance to gods and goddesses including Saraswati, Ram, and Sita. The ritual ends with blessings for the family that has offered alms to the dancing group.

Kaura, also known as *Kaanraha* in the Magar language, is a folk musical performance indigenous to the hilly regions of Nepal. It involves rhythmic movements accompanied by traditional music and is performed during festivals or cultural celebrations. The sources state that Kaura originated in the Magar community of Rishing Ranipokhari, Tanahun, and was originally called *Kaanraha*.

## Dance & theatre

Sorathi or Maruni is one of the best-known Magar folk dances. It is performed during Tihar, with a main dancer dressed as a woman, another person mimicking the dancer, and a drummer playing a *Madal* or *Rani Madal*. A masked performer entertains the audience through gestures and is considered a protector of the dancer. The costume and ornaments are placed with flowers and rice on a brass plate or winnower before the performance, giving the dress a ritual position. Worship, obeisance to gods and goddesses, and blessings for the family offering alms form part of the performance.

Kaura, or *Kaanraha*, is a Magar folk musical performance involving rhythmic movement and traditional music. It originated in Rishing Ranipokhari, Tanahun. Hurra is another Magar dance, performed during Tihar. *Hurra Naach* originated in the Magar community of Mudebas Village of Dhankuta. It has been observed from a time described by the source as unknown, although the culture is widely believed there to have begun in 1850 AD.

## Festivals & rituals

Maghe Sankranti is one of the most important annual festivals of the indigenous Magar community. It is also a government-declared national festival of the Magar community and the Tharu community, a status dated in the source to 2009 AD. It is celebrated on the first day of Magh, the tenth month of the Nepali calendar, in mid-January, marking the transition from winter to spring. In Magar terminology, the festival commemorates the end of *udheli*, or “down,” a six-month period beginning in mid-July, and the beginning of *ubheli*, or “up,” another six-month period beginning in mid-January. The celebration includes gatherings, invitations to *chelibetis*, traditional Magar songs and dances, and the preparation of *batuk*, also commonly known as *bara*.

Bhume Puja, also called Bal puja, is celebrated by Kham Magar people from Lumbini and Karnali provinces. *Bhume* means “Earth” in Nepali. Kham people worship the land through *bhumi puja*, water sprouts through *shim*, forests through *ban puja*, and the weather, seeking better crop yields and cattle. The Earth is regarded as God, mother, and protector. People perform the associated dance from mid-May through mid-June, with the main celebration during the first week of June.

Other named festivals include Chandi Purnima, Baisakhe Purnima, Mangsir Purnima, Jestha Purnima, Chaiti, Rungma, Keja, and Yacha. *Chhaigo* is celebrated as Lhosar and is considered the New Year for the Magar community according to the Naagchi Sambat.

## Foodways

*Batuk*, commonly known as *bara*, is identified as a traditional food of the Magar people and is especially associated with Maghe Sankranti and other celebratory occasions. It is shaped like a western doughnut. Black lentils are soaked for over twenty-four hours and ground into a thick paste. The paste is mixed with salt, pepper, and turmeric and fried in oil. A round form is made with the palm, and a small hole is made in the centre.

The sources connect Maghe Sankranti with gatherings, invitations to daughters and other female members of the family, traditional songs and dances, and the preparation of *batuk*. They also describe the annual movement of livestock between high pastures as a historically important economic activity associated with the festival’s *udheli* and *ubheli* periods.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources record several origin narratives and mythical stories. The Magars of Bahra Magarat east of the Kali Gandaki River are said to have originated in Seem. Two brothers, Seem Magar and Chintoo Magar, were separated after fighting; one remained in Seem, while the other went to Kangwachen in southern Sikkim. Another narrative concerns Sintoo Sati Sheng, his queen, and the poisoning of 1,000 Bhutia people at a place called Tong Song Fong, interpreted as “where a thousand were murdered.”

Kaike Magar origin stories describe a woman from an unspecified village of Kalyal kingdom, her son, and his angel bride. Their three sons became ancestors of the Buddha, Roka, and Gharti clans. A fourth ancestral story concerns a shepherd who discovered a baby boy receiving milk from a female goat in a hollow bamboo tree; the boy became the ancestor of the Jhankri clan. The language was called “Kaike,” meaning “language of the Gods.”

## Language & religion

Magar languages are rooted in the Bodic branch of the Tibetan family. The group has two major dialects and several subdialects. Baraha Magaratis speak Dhut, while Athara Magaratis speak Pang and Kaike. Magar Dhut speakers include Rana, Ale, and Thapa; Magar Kham speakers include Budhathoki, Pun, Roka, and Gharti; and Magar Kaike speakers include Tarali Magar of Dolpa and Magar clans residing in Dolpa and Karnali districts. The *Magar Akkha* script is used in Sikkim, and the source states that it is closely associated with Brahmi script.

According to the source, 79% of Magars follow Hinduism, alongside Buddhism, animism, and ancestor worship. Shamanism is identified among the original religions or beliefs. A *dhami*, or faith healer, is called *Dangar*, while a *jhankri* is another kind of faith healer and was traditionally a spiritual and social leader. Many Magars follow Tibetan Buddhism, with priests known as *Lama Guru*. The sources describe religious syncretism combining shamanistic and Buddhist rituals with Hindu traditions.
