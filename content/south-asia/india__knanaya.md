## Overview

The K'nānāya, also known as the Southists or *Tekkumbhagar*, are an endogamous ethnic group within the Saint Thomas Christian community of Kerala, India. They are distinguished from the Northists, or Vaddakkumbhagar, and number about 300,000 in India and elsewhere. Their origin is traced in community tradition to the arrival of the Syriac merchant Thomas of Cana, or Knāi Thoma, who led Jewish-Christian migrants from Mesopotamia to Kodungallur in AD 345. The community later developed settlements and churches in places including Udayamperoor, Kaduthuruthy, Kottayam, Chunkom, Kallissery, Uzhavoor, and Thodupuzha. During the 20th and 21st centuries, many Knanaya migrated westward, forming communities in non-Malayalam-speaking areas, including Houston and Chicago. Their folk culture is documented through songs, church traditions, ritual titles, manuscripts, and historical accounts.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do record a ceremonial context in which the barber performs the *Chandam Charthal*, or grooms beautification ceremony, during a Knanaya marriage. In this ritual, the barber petitions the assembly three times for permission to shave the bridegroom. The sources also state that the Knanaya historically held the title of “protectors of seventeen castes,” and that this authority continues to be displayed during Knanaya marriage ceremonies when participants ask permission before carrying out their designated roles. No specific garments or bodily adornments are identified.

### Architecture

Knanaya architectural history is represented chiefly by churches and church settlements. Before the Synod of Diamper in 1599, the Southist community held five churches: Udayamperoor, Kaduthuruthy, Kottayam, Chunkom, and Kallissery. The designation *Ancharapallikar*, or “Owners of Five and a Half churches,” referred to these five churches and to the half privilege and share held in churches co-owned with Northist Saint Thomas Christians.

In 1456, the Knanaya approached the Kingdom of Vadakkumkoor to rebuild Kaduthuruthy St. Mary’s Valiya Palli. Masons extended its walls and added a *gopuram*, or entrance tower. In the 17th century, the community built St. Stephens Church in Uzhavoor. After the destruction of Cranganore, a portion of the community founded Chunkom in Thodupuzha, where they built St. Mary’s Knanaya Church in 1579. Kottayam St. Mary’s Knanaya Valiya Palli was established after Knanaya from Kaduthuruthy were invited to Kottayam in 1550.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The most important documented Knanaya objects are historical records and ecclesiastical objects. The Thomas of Cana copper plates recorded the community’s arrival and granted his followers 72 social, economic, and religious rights from Cheraman Perumal. The plates were present in Kerala during Portuguese colonization but were later lost. The community invoked them as evidence of descent from Thomas of Cana’s mission and of its historical rights.

Knanaya churches also preserve two stone crosses known as the Persian crosses. They are inscribed in Pahlavi and Syriac and are dated between the eighth and tenth century. The sources further record that the Knanaya of Chunkom were known to mold “famous pottery,” but provide no detailed description of its forms, materials, or decoration.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They mention the *Chandam Charthal* marriage ceremony, in which a barber shaves the bridegroom, but give no further account of bodily decoration or jewelry.

## Music & performance

Knanaya musical culture is documented through historical folk songs written on palm-leaf manuscripts. Community scholars cite these songs as evidence of Jewish-Christian heritage, and the sources state that they were first written on palm-leaf manuscripts in the 17th century. The song *Nallor Orosilam*, translated in the source as “The Good Jerusalem,” recounts that the migrants prayed at the tomb of the Jewish Prophet Ezra before departing for India.

The historical song *Alappan Adiyil* records the reconstruction of Kaduthuruthy Church and includes the colophon date 1456. Another song, *Innu Nee Njangale Kaivitto Marane*, translated as “Have You Forgotten Us Today Oh Lord?”, records the dispersal of the Knanaya after the destruction of their township and churches at Cranganore. These songs function as repositories of migration history, religious memory, settlement history, and community identity.

## Dance & theatre

The documented marriage ceremony *Chandam Charthal* is a ritual of grooms beautification.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document marriage rituals and the historical ritual authority associated with the title “protectors of seventeen castes.” During Knanaya marriage ceremonies, participants taking part in designated roles ask permission before fulfilling them. In the *Chandam Charthal*, the barber petitions the assembly three times before shaving the bridegroom.

The community’s ritual and historical identity is also connected with churches and church reconstruction. The rebuilding of Kaduthuruthy St. Mary’s Valiya Palli is associated with the date 1456, while St. Mary’s Knanaya Church at Chunkom was consecrated in 1579. The Persian crosses at Kottayam Valiya Palli are ancient relics brought from Kaduthuruthy Church.

## Foodways

They state only that Knanaya in Chunkom carried out commerce in the region, regularly trading with the Kingdom of Travancore and the Tamil Dynasty of Madurai, and that the community was known there to mold “famous pottery.”

## Oral tradition & literature

Knanaya oral tradition includes migration narratives, folk songs, and accounts of descent from Thomas of Cana. The community tradition states that Syriac Christian migrants who arrived with Thomas of Cana were Jewish-Christians. Songs preserve this memory, including *Nallor Orosilam*, which refers to the Jewish Prophet Ezra, and *Innu Nee Njangale Kaivitto Marane*, which recalls the destruction of Cranganore and the dispersal of the community.

The historical song *Alappan Adiyil* records the reconstruction of Kaduthuruthy Church and preserves the colophon date 1456. These songs were written on palm-leaf manuscripts in the 17th century. The sources also record a two-wives legend associated with Thomas of Cana, in which one wife is presented as Syriac and the other as a native Saint Thomas Christian woman. Fr. Antonio Monserratte described these stories in 1579 as the “lie of the land,” while Archbishop Francisco Ros called the legend a “fable” in 1611. The sources present the legend as a disputed explanation of the division between Southists and Northists.

## Language & religion

The term K'nānāya derives from the Syriac adjectival epithet *K'nā'nāya*, meaning “Canaanite,” and members of the community are also called *Knāi* or *Knā* in reference to Thomas. The Knanaya are also known in Malayalam as *Tekkumbhagar*, generally translated as “Southist.” The sources mention Malayalam, Syriac, Pahlavi, Chaldean, and the Syriac manuscript tradition, including the oldest surviving Syriac manuscript of India, Vatican Syrian Codex 22.

The majority of Knanaya are members of the Syro-Malabar Church, specifically the Kottayam Archeparchy, and the Syrian Orthodox Church, specifically the Knanaya Archdiocese. Their religious history is tied to Saint Thomas Christian traditions, the Church of the East, churches dedicated to Saint Thomas, Saint Mary, Saint Kuriakose, and Saint Stephens, and the Persian crosses preserved at Kottayam Knanaya Valiyapally.
