## Overview

The Sentinelese, also known as the North Sentinel Islanders, are Indigenous people who inhabit North Sentinel Island in the Bay of Bengal, in the Andaman Islands of the northeastern Indian Ocean. The island lies about 64 km west of Port Blair and has an area of about 59.67 km². Population estimates are uncertain: the 1971 census estimated around 82 people, while a 2016 handbook estimated between 100 and 150. The Sentinelese are one of the six Indigenous and often reclusive peoples of the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. They are hunter-gatherers who use bows, arrows, spears, canoes, fishing nets, baskets, and other locally made objects. Their language is undescribed and is presumed to belong to one of the Andamanese language families.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources mention simple geometric designs and shade contrasts engraved on weapons, but name no specific motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Both sexes wear bark strings. Men tuck daggers into waist belts. The Sentinelese also wear ornaments such as necklaces and headbands, but are described as essentially nude.

### Architecture

Usual habitations include small temporary huts erected on four poles, with slanted roofs covered with leaves. In 1967, an expedition documented 18 lean-to huts made from grass and leaves, showing signs of recent occupation through still-burning fires at the corners.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Sentinelese use bows, arrows, spears, adzes, cane baskets, fishing nets, bamboo pots, and wooden buckets. Canoes are used for lagoon-fishing and are propelled with long poles rather than paddles or oars. The sources report no evidence of knowledge of metallurgy apart from cold forging to make tools and weapons, although metal-working was observed during a 1967 expedition. The Sentinelese salvaged iron from shipwrecks, and residents accepted aluminium cookware left by the National Geographic Society in 1974. A board divided into 64 squares, with alternate squares studded with stone and shell, was found at a camp, though its origin and purpose remain unknown.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources document necklaces, headbands, bark strings, waist belts, and daggers worn as body adornment or equipment. They also describe shared traits in body decoration between the Sentinelese and the Onge. No named jewelry forms, tattoos, hair practices, ritual ornaments, or ceremonial adornment are documented.

## Music & performance

They record that women have been seen dancing while slapping both palms on their thighs and tapping their feet rhythmically in a bent-knee stance. During a 2018 encounter, John Allen Chau wrote that the Sentinelese communicated with “lots of high-pitched sounds” and gestures, and he recorded high-pitched sounds resembling “ba, pa, la, and as.”

## Dance & theatre

The sources document one dance practice: women have been seen to slap both palms on their thighs while simultaneously tapping their feet rhythmically in a bent-knee stance. No dramatic traditions, theatre, masks, puppetry, or named dances are documented.

## Festivals & rituals

They describe contact events, gift exchanges, hunting, fishing, temporary habitation, and responses to outsiders.

## Foodways

The Sentinelese are hunter-gatherers. They use spears with bows and arrows to hunt terrestrial wildlife and employ more rudimentary methods to catch local seafood, including mud crabs and molluscan shells. They are believed to eat many molluscs, given the abundance of roasted shells found in their settlements. The 1967 expedition documented raw honey and wild fruits, as well as fishing nets and bamboo pots. The sources state that the Sentinelese are not known to engage in agriculture. No named dishes, staple grains, beverages, cooking rules, ceremonial foods, or dietary restrictions are documented.

## Oral tradition & literature

An Andamanese scholar, Vishvajit Pandya, noted that Onge narratives often recall voyages by their ancestors to North Sentinel to procure metal. This is documentation about Onge narratives rather than a recorded Sentinelese oral tradition. No contemporary literary revival or preservation project is described.

## Language & religion

Sentinelese is an undescribed language, and essentially nothing is known about it because of the lack of contact between the Sentinelese and the rest of the world. It is presumed to belong to one of the Andamanese language families and is thought to be related to the Ongan languages, such as Jarawa, rather than to Great Andamanese. The Jarawa and Sentinelese languages are mutually unintelligible, and Onge-speaking visitors were unable to recognise the language spoken by the inhabitants. No words are known with certainty, although Dāūwacho-chégálé-bāī is tentatively attested as a personal name.
