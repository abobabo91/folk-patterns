## Overview

The Shoshone or Shoshoni, also known by the endonym *Newe*, are an Indigenous people of the United States. Their four large cultural and linguistic divisions are Eastern Shoshone in Wyoming, Northern Shoshone in southern Idaho, Western Shoshone in California, Nevada, and northern Utah, and Goshute in western Utah and eastern Nevada. They traditionally speak Shoshoni, a Numic language in the Uto-Aztecan language family. As of 2020, 17,918 people in the USA were identified as Shoshone, including 3,638 in Nevada and 3,491 in Wyoming. Their material culture includes hunting weapons, bags, clothing, and objects represented in museum collections, while their language, regional bands, historical movements, and preservation programs are documented in the sources.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Laxskinnsväska** — A museum catalogue records a salmon-skin bag made about 1960 by Cora George at Fort Hall, Idaho, for the Tukuryka Shoshone. The bag has a decorative band around its opening and a light-skin strap attached to its short sides; the salmon skin is described as hard and stiff.

The sources used do not document additional Shoshone textile or pattern traditions, including coiled basketry, willow basketry, burden baskets, tule mats, rabbit-skin blankets, or parfleche.

### Clothing & dress

The museum records depict Shoshone men wearing tunics, leggings, moccasins, cloth shirts, hide leggings, blankets, jackets, trousers, and neck ties. A British Museum photograph shows four young Shoshone women and a Shoshone child wearing cloth tunic dresses. Another photograph shows a Shoshone woman with a baby in a baby carrier. The sources do not provide a systematic account of everyday or ceremonial dress, nor do they document named Shoshone garment terms, belts, head coverings, or distinctions between men’s and women’s clothing beyond these catalogue descriptions.

### Architecture

The sources mention traditional homes made from *sosoni*, high-growing grasses. Some neighboring tribes called the Shoshone “Grass House People” on this basis. The sources used do not describe the structure, roof, decoration, or construction of these homes, and they do not document a named Shoshone house type such as a willow wickiup.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

A catalogue records a horn bow from the United States, dated 1854, made from the horn of a bighorn sheep and covered on the inside with sinew. The record states that horn bows were less common among Plains Indians and occurred chiefly among the Shoshone, Crow, Blackfeet, and others. A Metropolitan Museum of Art record lists a 19th-century frame drum made of wood, possibly ash, and sheepskin. The sources do not document Shoshone ceramics or metalwork.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The museum photographs record ear-rings and a large round disc worn on the neck by Chief Washakie, a shell neck ornament worn by one Shoshone man, and a circular metal fastening on a neck tie worn by another portrait subject. The sources do not describe the meanings or ritual functions of these objects, nor do they document tattoos, henna, named jewelry types, or hair practices beyond a photograph showing hair in two bunches wrapped in decorated fabric.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not describe an annual Shoshone festival calendar, life-cycle rites, weddings, funerals, or coming-of-age ceremonies. They do record that the Northwestern Band of the Shoshone Nation acquired the site of the Bear River Massacre in 2008 in order to protect the holy land and build a memorial to the massacre. The sources do not provide further details about Shoshone ritual practice.

## Foodways

The sources do not document Shoshone dishes, cooking methods, dietary rules, or ceremonial foods. They do record traditional bands whose names refer to food sources, including the Salmon Eaters, Jack Rabbit Eaters, Porcupine Grass Seed Eaters, Wild Wheat Eaters, Groundhog Eaters, Fish Eaters, Pinenut Eaters, Tule Eaters, Ricegrass Eaters, Ryegrass Seed Eaters, and Buffalo Berry Eaters. The source material does not describe how these foods were prepared or consumed.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Shoshone call themselves *Newe*, meaning “People,” and the name “Shoshone” comes from *Sosoni*, a Shoshone word for high-growing grasses. Meriwether Lewis recorded the tribe as the “Sosonees or snake Indians” in 1805. The sources used do not document Shoshone folktales, epic poetry, proverbs, riddles, or named narrative cycles. They do document contemporary language-preservation work, including Shoshoni classes, digital language archiving, dictionaries, recordings, and a Shoshone language video game released in August 2013.

## Language & religion

Shoshoni is a Uto-Aztecan language of the Numic branch, spoken primarily in Wyoming, Utah, Nevada, and Idaho. Its principal dialects are Western Shoshoni in Nevada, Gosiute in western Utah, Northern Shoshoni in southern Idaho and northern Utah, and Eastern Shoshoni in Wyoming. The language is classified as threatened, while UNESCO has classified it as severely endangered in Idaho, Utah, and Wyoming. Shoshoni has no single agreed-upon writing system; the Crum-Miller system and the Idaho State system are the two main systems in use. The sources mention holy land connected with the Bear River Massacre but do not describe a religious system, denomination, or detailed spiritual practices.
