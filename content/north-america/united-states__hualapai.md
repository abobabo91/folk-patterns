## Overview

The Hualapai are a federally recognized Native American tribe in Arizona, with about 2300 enrolled citizens; approximately 1353 live on the Hualapai Reservation. The reservation covers 1,142 square miles in Northern Arizona, in Coconino and Mohave counties, while traditional territory extends along a 108-mile stretch of the southern side of the Grand Canyon and the Colorado River. Peach Springs is the tribal capital, headquarters, and most important community; other reservation communities include Valentine and Grand Canyon West. The Hualapai language belongs to the Pai branch of the Yuman–Cochimí language family. Hualapai culture documented in the sources includes regional bands and villages, ceremonies, distinctive deerskin and rabbit-skin dress, cedar-bough houses, bilingual education, and a history shaped by the Hualapai War.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no documented textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional Hualapai dress consists of full suits of deerskin and rabbit-skin robes. The sources do not distinguish men’s and women’s clothing, everyday dress, ceremonial garments, head coverings, belts, footwear, or other named garment types.

### Architecture

Traditional Hualapai houses were conical structures formed from cedar boughs. The single-slope form was called a *Wikiup*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

## Festivals & rituals

Major traditional Hualapai ceremonies include the “Maturity” ceremony and the “Mourning” ceremony. The modern Sobriety Festival is celebrated in June.

The sources describe a belief concerning the dead: souls were believed to go northwestward to a beautiful land where plentiful harvest grew. This land was believed to be visible only to Hualapai spirits.

The Hualapai War lasted from 1865 to 1870. It began amid increased traffic through the area on the Fort Mojave-Prescott Toll Road and involved armed conflict between the Hualapai and European Americans. The sources state that Anasa was killed in May 1865, that surrender began on August 20, 1868, and that the warrior Sherum’s surrender marked the end of the Hualapai Wars in 1870. These are historical events rather than annual ritual observances.

## Foodways

They state that the dead were believed to travel to a beautiful land where plentiful harvest grew, but they provide no further account of Hualapai foodways.

The village *Hê'l* or Milkweed Springs was used particularly for the irrigation of tobacco.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record Hualapai names and translations connected with people, bands, villages, mountains, springs, and rivers.

The Hualapai language article describes preservation efforts through bilingual education programs. Hualapai’s orthography was developed in the 1970s partly to preserve the language for pedagogical and historical purposes. The Peach Springs School District runs a bilingual program for local students and immersion camps.

## Language & religion

Havasupai–Hualapai is a Native American language spoken by the Hualapai and Havasupai peoples of northwestern Arizona. It belongs to the Pai branch of the Yuman–Cochimí language family. The Hualapai and Havasupai dialects are spoken in different areas: the Havasupai dialect is spoken at the bottom of the Grand Canyon, while the Hualapai dialect is spoken along the southern rim. As of 2010, approximately 1500 people spoke Havasupai–Hualapai; UNESCO classifies the Havasupai dialect as endangered and the Hualapai dialect as vulnerable.

The language has separate Havasupai and Hualapai orthographies adapted from the Latin Script. Hualapai’s orthography was developed in the 1970s. The sources describe traditional Hualapai beliefs about the destination of the souls of the dead.
