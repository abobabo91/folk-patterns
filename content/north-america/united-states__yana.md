## Overview

The Yana are a group of Native Americans indigenous to Northern California in the central Sierra Nevada, on the western side of the range. Before encroachment by white settlers, their lands bordered the Pit and Feather Rivers. Their territory was approximately 2,400 square miles, or more than 6,000 km2, and included mountain streams, gorges, boulder-strewn hills, and lush meadows. The Yana-speaking people comprised four groups: the North Yana, Central Yana, Southern Yana, and Yahi. The Yana were nearly destroyed during the California genocide in the latter half of the 19th century. Descendants of the Central and Southern Yana continue to live in California as members of Redding Rancheria.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Yana language includes a body of linguistic work containing texts and stories. Edward Sapir recorded Northern Yana, Central Yana, and Yahi through work with Betty Brown, Sam Batwi, and Ishi respectively. Only a small collection of words and phrases of Southern Yana was recorded by Sapir in his work with Sam Batwi, who spoke that dialect only in his childhood. The sources describe Yahi stories as relying on direction, location, repetition, and a high level of presumed knowledge among their native audience.

## Language & religion

Yana, also called Yanan, was formerly spoken by the Yana people in north-central California between the Feather and Pit rivers, in what is now the Shasta and Tehama counties. The language is often classified in the Hokan superstock, although contemporary linguists generally consider it a language isolate. There were four known dialects: Northern Yana, Central Yana, Southern Yana, and Southern Yahi. Yana has 22 consonants and 5 vowels; it is polysynthetic and agglutinative, with subject-verb-object word order. Verbs contain meaning through affixation, and verbs of motion must contain a different directional affix. Different word forms were used by male and female speakers, though this distinction was not used in the Yahi dialect.
