## Overview

The Piscataway, also called Piscatawa, are an Indigenous people of the Northeastern Woodlands whose historic homeland lay along the north bank of the Potomac River, especially in what is now Charles, southern Prince George’s, and western St. Mary’s counties in southern Maryland. Their descendants are primarily located in Southern Maryland, although some northern migrants live among the Six Nations of the Grand River First Nation in Ontario, Canada. The Piscataway language was an Algonquian regional dialect similar to Nanticoke and is now extinct. In 2012, Maryland recognized the Piscataway Indian Nation and Piscataway Conoy Tribe as Native American tribes; none of the Piscataway groups are federally recognized. Their folk-culture significance in the sources lies in agriculture, village life, hunting, fishing, houses, language-revival work, and historical continuity.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They mention copper fish headdresses as an indication of hereditary elites during the contact period, but provide no further description of their construction, use, or symbolism.

### Architecture

Piscataway villages consisted of individual houses protected by defensive log palisades. Traditional houses were rectangular longhouses, typically 3 metres (10 feet) high and 6 metres (20 feet) long. They had barrel-shaped roofs covered with bark or woven mats, and a central hearth with a smoke hole overhead. The sources also describe fortified towns and settlements near navigable waterways, where canoes could be used. Moyaone was the administrative center of the Piscataway Werowance and later tayac; Captain John Smith recorded it as the “king’s house,” or the capital village and residence of the Tayac.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention bows and arrows used by men for hunting and copper fish headdresses associated with hereditary elites. The sources also refer to bark and woven mats as roofing materials and to canoes as part of the settlement environment.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Copper fish headdresses are the only named form of adornment, and the source identifies them as indicators of hereditary elite status during the contact period without describing their materials beyond copper or explaining how they were worn.

## Music & performance

They do record singing as part of a twentieth-century dispute over the burial of Phillip Sheridan Proctor, known as Turkey Tayac: some tribal citizens celebrated the death by spitting, dancing, and singing over his gravesite.

The sources mention the American Indian Movement, Native American identity, and cultural revitalization movements in connection with Turkey Tayac. Proctor/Tayac was described as a prominent figure in early and mid-twentieth-century cultural revitalization, and his leadership inspired other groups and revival movements among Southeastern mixed-race communities. The Piscataway community’s current language-revival efforts include learning the foundation of the Algonquin language while conducting linguistic studies.

## Dance & theatre

They mention dancing only in the account of the burial of Turkey Tayac, when some tribal citizens danced and sang over his gravesite.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document several historical religious and life-cycle events. The Tayac Kittamaquund and his wife converted to Christianity in 1640 through their friendship with the English Jesuit missionary Father Andrew White, who also performed their marriage. Their daughter Mary Kittamaquund was given the name Mary after her family converted to Christianity. At the age of 10, she married the 38 year old English colonist Giles Brent.

The sources also describe political and funerary practices. Piscataway authority was organized under hereditary chiefs, including werowances and the Tayac, known to the English as “Emperor of Piscataway.” Tribal tension surrounded the official burial of Turkey Tayac in 1978, and the event contributed to a division among Piscataway groups.

## Foodways

Piscataway subsistence relied more heavily on agriculture than that of many neighboring peoples. Crops included maize, several varieties of beans, melons, pumpkins, squash, and ceremonial tobacco. Women mainly bred and cultivated these crops, processed numerous varieties of maize and other plants, and gathered berries, nuts, and tubers in season. Men cleared new fields, hunted, and fished.

The Piscataway also hunted bears, elk, deer, wolves, beavers, squirrels, partridges, and wild turkeys. Fishing and oyster and clam harvesting supplemented cultivated foods, while wild plants from nearby freshwater marshes remained part of the diet. Around AD 800, people living along the Potomac began cultivating maize as a supplement to hunting, gathering, fishing, game, and wild plants. By 1300, maize, beans, and squash created a calorie-rich agricultural surplus that supported larger communities.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do mention oral history among related communities: the Patawomecks’ oral history stated that they had been in allied union with the Piscataways some generations earlier. The sources also preserve historical statements attributed to Piscataway leaders, including Wannas’s response to English Lord Calvert that he would neither tell him to go nor tell him to stay, but that he might use his own discretion.

Language documentation began during the colonial period. Jesuit missionary Father Andrew White translated the Catholic catechism into Piscataway in 1640, and other English missionaries compiled Piscataway-language materials. Current community efforts include learning the foundation of the Algonquin language and conducting linguistic studies to revive the dialect for later generations. A small number of speakers remain, but none are fully fluent in the Piscataway dialect.

## Language & religion

Piscataway belongs to the Algonquian language family and was a regional dialect similar to Nanticoke. The language is now extinct, while the dialect is largely dissipating among tribal members. The sources report a small number of language speakers, none fully fluent in the Piscataway dialect, and identify institutional barriers and lack of funding for linguistic studies as major challenges to revitalization.

The sources describe historical religious change rather than a complete contemporary religious landscape. Piscataway religious development during the contact period exalted the hierarchy of hereditary rulers. Kittamaquund and his wife converted to Christianity in 1640, and Father Andrew White translated the Catholic catechism into Piscataway in the same year.
