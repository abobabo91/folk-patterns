## Overview

The Bannock were originally Northern Paiute but are more culturally affiliated with the Northern Shoshone. Their traditional lands included northern Nevada, southeastern Oregon, southern Idaho, and western Wyoming. Today they are enrolled in the federally recognized Shoshone-Bannock Tribes of the Fort Hall Reservation of Idaho, located on the Fort Hall Indian Reservation. The Shoshone-Bannock live on the Fort Hall Indian Reservation in Southeastern Idaho, where Lemhi and Northern Shoshone also live. In the 2010 U.S. census, 89 people identified as having “Bannock” ancestry, including 38 “full-blooded” people; 5,315 people were enrolled in the Shoshone-Bannock Tribes. The sources present Bannock material culture, beadwork, dwellings, ceremonies, hunting, fishing, and food gathering, but do not identify a language family.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Beadwork** — After the introduction of glass beads, Bannock people transferred geometric designs from their petroglyphs to beadwork. A museum needle housing made by Lizzie Edmo in 1960 at Fort Hall, Idaho, is made of leather embroidered with light blue, darker blue, yellow, and red glass beads.

**Cornhusk bag** — A museum catalogue records a Bannock plant-fiber bag, possibly made from corn, with a square, flat form and leather-strap handles. Both sides carry geometric patterns including green, pink, yellow, and red, with varying motifs.

**Salmon-skin carrying bags** — The Bannock traditionally made carrying bags from salmon skin.

**Moccasin** — A Bannock moccasin from Fort Hall, Idaho, was given to the Museum of Ethnography in 1958 by Bessie Judson.

**Rawhide cradle** — A museum object identified as a Bannock rawhide cradle contains a doll lying in a partly untreated leather cradle.

**Baby carrier** — British Museum catalogue records show a Shoshone or Bannock baby in a baby carrier, with a beaded bag in front of the baby.

**Blanket** — A British Museum photographic record shows a Bannock man seated with a blanket over his legs.

**Motif vocabulary.** Geometric designs; geometric patterns; varying motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources do not provide a complete account of everyday or ceremonial Bannock dress. Museum records show Bannock people wearing cloth tunics in photographic portraits, while one portrait shows a man with a blanket over his legs. The sources also record moccasins and a baby carrier, but do not identify Bannock names for garments, footwear, belts, head coverings, or ceremonial clothing.

### Architecture

Before the late nineteenth century, Bannock people lived primarily in tipis made from buffalo hide during winter. During summer they built conical dwellings of brush and grass. The sources also identify sweat lodges and menstrual huts as Bannock building types. A British Museum photograph records a family group of six Bannock people sitting in a tipi at Medicine Lodge Creek, United States of America. The sources do not describe roof construction, interior organization, decoration, or urban architecture.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Bannock traditionally made pottery, utensils from bighorn sheep horns, and tule reed rafts for water transport. The sources do not document Bannock metalwork. Museum records additionally identify a leather needle housing embroidered with glass beads, a plant-fiber bag with leather handles, and a rawhide cradle.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources do not document Bannock jewelry, tattooing, henna, hair practices, or named forms of body adornment. They do record glass beads used in beadwork and a beaded bag shown with a Shoshone or Bannock baby in a baby carrier.

## Music & performance

The sources do not identify Bannock musical instruments, song genres, musical scales, or performance settings. They name several Bannock ceremonies: the Scalp Dance, Grass Dance, Circle Dance, and Bear Dance. After acclimation to reservation life, the Bannock also practiced the Sun Dance. The sources describe religious practices involving worship and appeals to spirits, healing arts involving medicine men, and beliefs concerning the soul’s journey to the land of the dead after death, but do not explain the music associated with these practices.

## Dance & theatre

The Bannock’s named dances include the Scalp Dance, Grass Dance, Circle Dance, and Bear Dance. The Sun Dance was also practiced after acclimation to reservation life. The sources do not describe choreography, costumes, instruments, dramatic performance, theatre, or distinctions between ceremonial and entertainment contexts.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do not provide an annual festival calendar or name festivals with dates. Bannock religious practices included worship and appeals to spirits and healing arts involving medicine men. The sources state that the soul was believed to undertake a journey to the land of the dead after death. The dead were wrapped in blankets and placed in rock crevices. Mourners practiced minor self-mutilation, sacrificed the deceased person’s horse, and destroyed the person’s tipi. The named ceremonies were the Scalp Dance, Grass Dance, Circle Dance, Bear Dance, and, later, the Sun Dance.

The sources also document historical events rather than ritual calendars. The Bannock War took place in 1878. A treaty signed at Soda Springs in 1863 established territorial boundaries and guaranteed annuities, but was never ratified. In 1869 Ulysses S. Grant ordered the Bannock to report to the Fort Hall Indian Reservation, and most complied.

## Foodways

Before the late nineteenth century, Bannock people fished for salmon on the Snake River in Idaho and hunted buffalo herds in the fall. In the 1870s they continued to hunt and gather food outside the reservation because provisions supplied by the U.S. government were not reliable enough to support them. The sources do not name dishes, staple grains, cooking methods, beverages, dietary rules, or ceremonial foods. They also do not document camas harvesting, root digging, or a named Bannock food tradition beyond salmon fishing, buffalo hunting, and general food gathering.

## Oral tradition & literature

They mention a Bannock Trail used to travel from Oregon, northern Nevada, southern Idaho, and western Wyoming to Montana and Canada to hunt buffalo, but do not describe oral traditions associated with it.

## Language & religion

The Bannock were originally Northern Paiute and are more culturally affiliated with the Northern Shoshone. The Wikipedia source gives the Northern Paiute terms *panaki* and *kutsutɨkaˀa* for the Bannock, but does not provide a language-family classification, dialect description, historical script, or current language situation.
