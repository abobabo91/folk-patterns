## Overview

The Missouria, also called Missouri, are a Native American people whose own name is *Nutachi*, also spelled *Niutachi*, meaning “People of the River Mouth.” They originated in the Great Lakes region before European contact and later lived near the mouth of the Grand River, at the confluence of the Missouri and Mississippi rivers, and in what is now Saline County, Missouri. Since Indian removal, they have lived primarily in Oklahoma and are federally recognized as the Otoe-Missouria Tribe of Indians, headquartered in Red Rock, Oklahoma. The tribe belongs to the Chiwere division of the Siouan-Cawtaban language family. Historical population figures recorded by James Mooney range from about 200 families in 1702 to 13 people in 1910, after which Missouria population numbers were combined with those of the Otoe. Today, Missouri are part of the Otoe-Missouria Tribe of Indians.

## Music & performance

The sources identify Chiwere words and songs as subjects of preservation work. The Iowa Tribe of Oklahoma provided tribal elders with recording devices to collect Chiwere words and songs, and a 2012 NSF grant provided digital access to existing audio recordings of fluent speakers. The Otoe–Missouria Tribe of Indians also established a language program in conjunction with the University of Oklahoma Native American Studies Department.

The Missouria hold the Otoe-Missouria encampment each July and host social dances and ceremonies at the Otoe-Missouria Cultural Center in Red Rock, Oklahoma. The language article mentions “Otoe-Missouria Hymns,” translated from English by Earl Plumley, but gives no further description.

## Festivals & rituals

The Otoe-Missouria hold the Otoe-Missouria encampment each July and host social dances and ceremonies at the Otoe-Missouria Cultural Center in Red Rock, Oklahoma.

The historical sources record oral traditions concerning Missouria origins and migration. One account says that the people once lived north of the Great Lakes and began migrating south in the 16th century. Another oral history says that the Missouria split from the Otoe because of a love affair between the children of two tribal chiefs.

The sources also mention historical religious and social divisions rather than religious rituals. In 1880, the tribes divided into the Coyote, described as traditionalists, and the Quakers, described as assimilationists. By 1890, most of the Coyote band had rejoined the Quakers on their reservation.

## Foodways

The sources state that the Missouria acquired horses and hunted bison after migrating west of the Missouri River into Osage territory. They also state that the Missouria were primarily farmers in the early 20th century. No source gives the names of crops, dishes, cooking methods, beverages, ceremonial foods, dietary rules, or food-related festivals.

The name *Nutachi* or *Niutachi* means “People of the River Mouth,” while the French-derived name *Wimihsoorita* translates as “One who has dugout canoes.” These names are explained in the sources.

## Oral tradition & literature

Missouria oral history records an origin north of the Great Lakes, participation in a larger group that included the Ho-Chunk, Iowa, and Otoe, and migration south in the 16th century. It also records a division from the Otoe associated with a love affair between the children of two tribal chiefs. These accounts are presented as oral history rather than as a named epic or written literary genre. They do record contemporary preservation efforts for Chiwere, including language workshops, recordings made with tribal elders, digital access to audio recordings, and the establishment of an Otoe–Missouria language program.

## Language & religion

Chiwere is a Siouan language originally spoken by the Missouria, Otoe, and Iowa peoples. It is also called Iowa–Otoe–Missouria or *Báxoje-Jíwere-Nyútʼach*. The Otoe–Missouria dialect is called *Jíwere ich'é*, while the Iowa tribe refers to its language as *Báxoje ich'é* or *Bah Kho Je*. Chiwere is closely related to Ho-Chunk, also known as Winnebago.

Non-Native Christian missionaries first documented Chiwere in the 1830s. The language declined after extended European American contact in the 1850s and had almost totally ceased to be spoken by 1940. The last two fluent speakers died in the winter of 1996; as of 2006, an estimated four members of the Otoe–Missouria Tribe of Indians still spoke the language. Chiwere is critically endangered. Its grammar is agglutinative, its word order is subject-object-verb, and it has separate male and female registers.
