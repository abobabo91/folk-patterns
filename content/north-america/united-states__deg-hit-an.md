## Overview

The Deg Hit'an are a group of Athabaskan peoples in Alaska. They live along the Anvik River in Anvik, the Innoko River in Shageluk, and the lower Yukon River at Holy Cross. The GASH region also includes Grayling, Anvik, Shageluk, and Holy Cross in Interior Alaska. Their native language is Deg Xinag, a Northern Athabaskan language. The ethnic population is approximately 250 people, while only two people still speak the language. The Deg Hit'an are members of the federally recognized Alaska Native tribes of Anvik Village, Shageluk Native Village, and Holy Cross Village. Their cultural documentation includes traditional folk tales, oral traditions, language-learning materials, and a vocabulary record.

## Material culture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Deg Xinag vocabulary includes words for “iron, metal,” “door,” “house,” “mountain,” “snow,” and “earthquake,”.

## Foodways

The Deg Xinag vocabulary includes *vanhgiq*, glossed as “Indian ice cream,” but the sources provide no recipe, ingredients, preparation method, or social context. The vocabulary also includes words for “fish,” “bear,” “fox,” “animal,” and “mammoth,” without describing their roles in Deg Hit'an foodways.

## Oral tradition & literature

Deg Xinag oral tradition is represented by *Engithidong Xugixudhoy* (“Their Stories of Long Ago”), a collection of traditional folk tales in Deg Xinag by the elder Belle Deacon. It was published in 1987 by the Alaska Native Language Center. The sources also identify Deenaalee Chase-Hodgdon as a storyteller and mention Deg Xinag oral traditions as a field of documentation. A literacy manual with accompanying audiotapes was published in 1993.

## Language & religion

Deg Xinag, also called Deg Hit'an, is a Northern Athabaskan language spoken by the Deg Xit'an peoples of the GASH region. Its two main dialects are Yukon and Kuskokwim. Yukon Deg Xinag is the traditional language of Anvik, Shageluk, and Holy Cross, while Kuskokwim Deg Xinag is the traditional language of settlements of Middle Kuskokwim. As of 2009, no speakers were living in Anvik and Holy Cross. The language is severely endangered, with only two speakers among an ethnic population of approximately 250 people.
