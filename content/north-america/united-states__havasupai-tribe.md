## Overview

The Havasupai people (*Havsuw' Baaja*) are a Native American people and tribe who have lived in and around the Grand Canyon for at least 800 years. They live primarily in Havasu Canyon, where Havasu Creek runs through Supai, the capital of the Havasupai Indian Reservation in Coconino County. As of 2019, the tribe consisted of about 730 members, of whom about 400 lived on the reservation. Havasupai is a dialect of the Upland Yuman language spoken by about 450 people on the reservation and in the Grand Canyon. The name Havasupai means “people of the blue-green water,” referring to Havasu Creek, a tributary of the Colorado. Agriculture, hunting, gathering, irrigation, land recovery, and tourism are central themes in the sources’ account of Havasupai folk culture.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Before 1882, the Havasupai used homes on the plateau of the canyon during winter and lived inside the canyon during the summer gardening season. The sources state that the National Park Service later razed residents’ traditional homes and replaced them with cabins. Supai now contains 136 houses, a café, a general store, a tourist office, a lodge, a post office, and a school.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do document agricultural and hunting equipment, including hand-dug irrigation canals, small headgates, bows, arrows, and rifles. The Havasupai irrigation system diverted water from Havasu Creek through open earthen channels to crop fields, while families regulated the flow to distribute water equitably and limit erosion and over-saturation.

## Music & performance

 The sources do mention a traditional Havasupai belief that the tribe originated in the canyon. This belief was contrasted with a genetic research study conducted without the consent or permission of the Havasupai, which indicated that their ancestors migrated from Asia to North America. The sources also mention a ceremonial return of blood samples after a legal controversy involving Arizona State University, but they provide no description of music or performance connected with that ceremony.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do document the importance of seasonal movement and agricultural work: in winter, tribal members stationed themselves on the plateau of the canyon, while in summer, irrigation gardening brought them back inside the canyon walls. The sources describe a ceremonial return of blood samples to the Havasupai after a legal dispute concerning unauthorized genetic research. They also record that President Gerald Ford signed the legislation returning a large share of Havasupai land on January 4, 1975. No birth, coming-of-age, wedding, or funeral rites are described.

## Foodways

Havasupai subsistence traditionally depended heavily on agriculture, hunting, and gathering. The principal crops were corn, beans, squash, sunflowers, gourds, and some cotton. Corn was the main crop and was generally harvested in the later summer months. A farming technique called *cepukaka* was used to protect corn from being blown over: the farmer loosened soil around the plant and pulled it into a hill around the stalk base.

The Havasupai also cultivated melons, watermelons, and orchard trees introduced with the arrival of the Spanish. By the 1940s, these had become staples of the Havasupai diet. The Hopi introduced crops such as the gourd and sunflower, which also became dietary staples.

Hunting provided sheep, deer, rabbits, and squirrels. Historically, Havasupai hunters worked in large groups and generally distributed game fairly among the hunters. Havasupai women gathered walnuts, wild candytuft, barrel cacti, and other plants and seeds during the spring and summer months. Lighter foods that could be moved to the plateau in winter and dry foods that could be stored for long periods were preferred.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They do record a traditional Havasupai belief that the tribe originated in the canyon, a belief that was contradicted by a genetic research study conducted without the tribe’s consent.

The sources mention oral and contemporary accounts concerning the effectiveness of the traditional irrigation system. This knowledge was passed down from generation to generation and included practical understanding of water management, seasonal rainfall, sediment build-up, canal orientation, and the sustainable cultivation of crops on the canyon floor. Havasupai language documentation includes grammatical descriptions, vocabularies, and texts. The sources also list *Gwe gnaavja*, *Baahj muhm hatm hwag gyu*, and *Tim: Tñuda Hobaja* among publications prepared by or for the Havasupai Bilingual Education Program.

## Language & religion

Havasupai is a dialect of the Upland Yuman language spoken by about 450 people on the Havasupai Indian Reservation and in the Grand Canyon. The Havasupai variety is nearly identical to the variety of Hualapai, although the groups are socially and politically distinct and use different orthographies. Speakers of Havasupai and Hualapai consider their languages separate. Havasupai is more distantly related to Yavapai.

The name *Havasupai* means “people of the blue-green water,” while *Havasuuw* is the Havasupai name for Supai. They do document a traditional belief concerning the tribe’s origin in the canyon.
