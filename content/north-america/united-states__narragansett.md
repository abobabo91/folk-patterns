## Overview

The Narragansett are an Indigenous people of the Northeastern Woodlands whose historic territory extended across western Narragansett Bay in Rhode Island and portions of Connecticut and eastern Massachusetts, from the Providence River to the Pawcatuck River. Today, Narragansett people are enrolled in the federally recognized Narragansett Indian Tribe, headquartered in Rhode Island. The tribe governs 1,800 acres of trust lands in Charlestown and owns several hundred acres in Westerly. Sources report approximately 2,400 members according to tribal rolls, while another account gives a tribal enrollment of 4,000 and says that the rolls are closed. Their language belongs to the Algonquian family and historically had a “Y-dialect.” Their cultural history includes language revival, a longhouse, an annual powwow, and continued efforts to maintain tribal identity.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The Narragansett tribe incorporated in 1900 and built a longhouse in 1940 as a traditional place for gatherings and ceremonies. The longhouse was constructed along RI Route 2, also called South County Trail, and later fell into disrepair. Plans described in the source propose upgrading it as a place for American Indian cuisine and a cultural meeting house. The Narragansett Indian Church, founded by colonists in the 1740s during the First Great Awakening, and its surrounding 3 acres remained under tribal control and ownership.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention arts and crafts as part of the 342nd powwow in August 2017.

## Music & performance

The annual meeting powwow is described as a gathering of thanksgiving and honor to the Narragansett people. The source says that the powwow includes a traditional grand entry, a procession of military veterans, dancers, and honored tribal representatives, as well as the ceremonial lighting of a sacred fire. It also includes a church service, food vendors, and arts and crafts. The gathering is held on the second weekend of August on the reservation in Charlestown, Rhode Island, and is described as the oldest recorded powwow in North America, with colonial documentation dating the gathering to 1675.

## Festivals & rituals

The Narragansett host their annual meeting powwow on the second weekend of August on their reservation in Charlestown, Rhode Island. It is described as a gathering of thanksgiving and honor to the Narragansett people. Colonial documentation dates the gathering to 1675, although the source says it had been held long before European contact. In August 2017, the tribe held the 342nd powwow, which included a traditional grand entry, a procession of military veterans, dancers, honored tribal representatives, and the ceremonial lighting of a sacred fire. A church service, food vendors, and arts and crafts were also included.

The sources describe the tribe’s longhouse as a traditional place for gatherings and ceremonies. They also describe the Narragansett Indian Church, founded in the 1740s to convert Indians to Christianity, and note that the tribe retained control and ownership of the church and its surrounding 3 acres.

## Foodways

The sources used provide very little information about Narragansett foodways. The tribe’s plans for its longhouse include using it as a place for American Indian cuisine and as a cultural meeting house. Food vendors were present at the 342nd powwow in August 2017.

## Oral tradition & literature

Roger Williams learned the Narragansett language and wrote *A Key into the Language of America* in 1643. In that work, he gave the tribe’s name as *Nanhigganeuck*, while later using the spelling *Nahigonset*. The source also records Williams’s account of hearing that *Nahigonsset* was named after a little island between *Puttaquomscut* and *Mishquomacuk*, near *Sugar Loaf Hill*. The precise location could not be determined according to William Simmons, although the account is used to discuss the geography of the Narragansett homeland.

They do document contemporary language-revival efforts based on early twentieth-century books and manuscripts and new teaching programs.

## Language & religion

Narragansett is an Algonquian language formerly spoken in most of what is now Rhode Island by the Narragansett people. It was closely related to Massachusett and Mohegan-Pequot. The Narragansett language became almost entirely extinct during the twentieth century and during the centuries of European colonization through cultural assimilation. Narragansett speech had a “Y-dialect,” similar enough to the “N-dialects” of Massachusett and Wampanoag to be mutually intelligible. Shinnecock and Pequot are also identified as Y-dialects.

Roger Williams’s 1643 work was the earliest study of the language in English. Ezra Stiles collected a list of 45 Narragansett words, eight of which were transcribed in Hebrew characters. American English absorbed loan words from Narragansett and related languages, including *quahog*, *moose*, *papoose*, *powwow*, *squash*, and *succotash*. The tribe has begun language-revival efforts using early twentieth-century books and manuscripts and new teaching programs.

Christian missionaries began converting tribal members during the colonial period. In the 1740s, colonists founded the Narragansett Indian Church to convert Indians to Christianity.
