## Overview

The Puyallup are a federally recognized Indigenous people from western Washington state in the United States. The Puyallup Tribe is primarily located on the Puyallup Indian Reservation, which lies in parts of Pierce and King counties and includes territory in and around Tacoma, Fife, Milton, Edgewood, Puyallup, Waller, and Federal Way. The tribe has approximately 4,000 citizens, including about 2,500 residents of the reservation. The reservation has a land area of 73.935 km2 and a total population of 41,341. The Puyallup speak Southern Lushootseed, also known as Twulshootseed, a dialect of the Coast Salish language Lushootseed. Fishing remains central to Puyallup culture and is described as surrounded by ritual and spirituality.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Puyallup Tribe’s language revitalization work includes the creation of “language nests,” locations where only Twulshootseed, rather than English, is spoken, as well as support for language learning in the school, home, workplace, and community.

## Language & religion

The language of the Puyallup Tribe is Lushootseed, a Coast Salish language spoken by different peoples in the Puget Sound region. Lushootseed is divided into Northern Lushootseed and Southern Lushootseed. The Puyallup speak Southern Lushootseed, also known as Twulshootseed, from *txʷəlšucid*, the Puyallup name for Lushootseed. The Puyallup name for the people is *spuyaləpabš*, translated in the source as “people of the bend.” The Puyallup name for the Tribal Council is *sk̓ʷapad ʔə tiiɫ siʔiʔab*, translated as “council of leaders.”

Lushootseed was historically oral, and its alphabet was created by Lushootseed-speaking elders working with linguist Thom Hess in the 1960s. The last fluent speaker, Vi Hilbert, died in 2008. The Puyallup Language Program was created to revitalize Twulshootseed and emphasizes using it for communication.
