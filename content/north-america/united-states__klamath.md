## Overview

The Klamath lived in Southern Oregon and Northern California. They returned each year to permanent winter settlements along rivers. They relied mainly on fish, gathered roots, and seeds. They were closely linked with the Modoc and spoke the northern dialect of Klamath–Modoc.

## Material culture

Klamath material culture is best represented by basketry, shell adornment, earth-lodge settlements, and a small group of recorded clothing and household objects.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Basketry was the main named textile tradition, using grass, bark, pine, and cedar.

- **Twined basket** — A basket used twined technique and had a flint or obsidian blade design.
- **Winnowing basket and mat** — Museum records list a tray, winnowing basket, and mat made of basketry.
- **Basket-covered bottle** — Some bottles were covered with basketry.
- **Klamath bowl** — A bowl-shaped basket from Oregon had a triangle pattern.
- **Grass basketry** — Several recorded baskets were made of grass.

### Clothing & dress

The sources name little Klamath clothing, but they record moccasins, regalia, and dentalium worn for adornment.

- **Woman’s moccasin** — A museum record identifies a woman’s moccasin from the Klamath Reservation in Oregon.
- **Brush-dance regalia** — A postcard shows Klamath men and women wearing regalia for a brush dance in California.
- **Dentalium nose and ear adornment** (*dentalium*) — Dentalium shells were worn through the nose septum and as ear pendants.

### Architecture

Klamath winter settlements used earth-lodges in permanent places that families reoccupied each year.

- **Earth-lodge winter settlement** — Settlements stretched along riverbanks, and houses could form a nearly continuous line for five or six miles.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources record several basketry and everyday objects.

- **Basketry tray** — A basketry tray is listed in a museum catalogue.
- **Winnowing basket** — A winnowing basket is listed among the recorded objects.
- **Basket-covered bottle** — Museum records describe bottles covered with basketry.
- **Cradle** — Several Klamath cradles were also catalogued as cots or cribs.
- **Basketry rattle** — A Museum of World Culture record identifies a rattle made of basketry.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Dentalium shells were important personal adornments, worn through the nose and as ear pendants and necklaces.

- **Septum dentalium** (*dentalium*) — Both sexes inserted dentalium shells horizontally through the nose septum.
- **Four-shell ear pendant** (*dentalium*) — An ear pendant consisted of four dentalia hung together by their tips.
- **Female nose ornament** — Museum records identify a female nose ornament made of discoidal shell beads.
- **Child’s dentalium necklace** (*dentalium*) — A museum record identifies a child’s necklace made with dentalium.
- **Bear-claw and blue-glass necklace** — One necklace was made of bear claws and large blue glass beads.

## Music & performance

The sources give little detail about Klamath music but record a brush dance and a basketry rattle.

- **Brush-dance performance** — A postcard shows Klamath men and women in regalia performing a brush dance in a grassy field.
- **Basketry rattle** — A basketry rattle is recorded, but its use is not known.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name one dance.

- **Brush dance** — A postcard depicts Klamath men and women in regalia performing a brush dance.

## Festivals & rituals

The profile does not describe named festivals or ceremonies, but it records family practices and marriage customs.

- **Septum piercing for dentalium** (*dentalium*) — Klamath families commonly gave younger members septum piercings so dentalium could be inserted.
- **Marriage without bride-price talks** — Klamath families did not hold formal talks about a bride price, and wives could leave their husbands.

## Foodways

Klamath foodways centered on fish and gathered roots, seeds, and parts of plants.

- **Fish gathering** — Fish were a primary part of Klamath subsistence.
- **Lomatium roots** (*Lomatium canbyi*) — The roots of Lomatium canbyi were eaten.
- **Arrowhead rootstocks** (*Sagittaria cuneata*) — The rootstocks of Sagittaria cuneata were eaten.
- **Carex stems and bases** (*Carex*) — Klamath ate the fresh stems and tuberous stem bases of Carex.
- **Carex pith juice** (*Carex*) — They used juice from the pith of Carex as a beverage.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources do not provide named Klamath stories or literary traditions, but language revitalization work was underway by 2019.

- **Klamath language revitalization** — Revitalization efforts aimed to create new speakers of the Klamath language.

## Language & religion

- **Klamath–Modoc language** (*Klamath–Modoc*) — Klamath–Modoc was spoken around Klamath Lake in southern Oregon and northern California.
- **Lutuamian** — Klamath–Modoc was historically also called Lutuamian.
- **Northern Klamath dialect** (*fi-ukshikni*) — The Klamath spoke the northern, or fi-ukshikni, dialect.
- **People** (*maqlaqs*) — Klamath and Modoc people called themselves maqlaqs, maqlags, or Maklaks, meaning “people.”

## Glossary

- *dentalium* — Shell used for adornment
- *Lomatium canbyi* — Plant whose roots were eaten
- *Sagittaria cuneata* — Plant whose rootstocks were eaten
- *Carex* — Plant used for food, drink, and mats
- *Klamath–Modoc* — Plateau Penutian language
- *Lutuamian* — Historical name for Klamath–Modoc
- *fi-ukshikni* — Northern Klamath dialect
- *maqlaqs* — People
- *maqlags* — People
- *Maklaks* — People
