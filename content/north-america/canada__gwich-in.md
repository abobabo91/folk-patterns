## Overview

The Gwichʼin, also called Kutchin or Loucheux, are an Athabaskan-speaking First Nations people of Canada and an Alaska Native people. They live in the northwestern part of North America, mostly north of the Arctic Circle. Over 6,000 Gwichʼin live in 15 small communities in northern parts of the Northwest Territories and the Yukon Territory of Canada and in northern Alaska. Their communities include Aklavik, Fort McPherson, Inuvik, Tsiigehtchic, Old Crow, Arctic Village, Fort Yukon, Venetie, and other settlements. Gwichʼin culture is closely connected with caribou, hunting, fishing, clothing production, beadwork, quillwork, snowshoes, birchbark canoes, sleds, oral storytelling, and knowledge of the northern environment. Their language belongs to the Athabaskan language family and has Eastern and Western varieties.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Caribou-skin clothing* is made from caribou skin and has traditionally been central to Gwichʼin clothing. The sources state that most clothing was made from caribou skin and that overalls were made from “really good white tanned caribou skin.”

**Porcupine quillwork embroidery** is a highly regarded Gwichʼin practice. The sources identify it as embroidery associated with traditional caribou-skin clothing.

**Beadwork** is described as intricate and ornate. The sources identify it as a renowned Gwichʼin craft.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional Gwichʼin clothing was largely made from caribou skin. The sources specifically mention caribou-skin clothing, traditionally made caribou-skin garments, and overalls made from white tanned caribou skin. Porcupine quillwork embroidery was applied to traditional caribou-skin clothing, and beadwork is described as intricate and ornate. The sources also state that Gwichʼin tents and most clothing were made from caribou skin. They do not distinguish men’s and women’s everyday clothing, name specific garment terms beyond “overalls,” or describe head coverings, belts, or footwear. A ceremonial costume of the Kutchin tribe was represented in a painting used as the basis for a Canada Post stamp issued on 4 April 1975.

### Architecture

They state only that Gwichʼin tents were traditionally made from caribou skin.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do identify several everyday objects and technologies: snowshoes, birchbark canoes, the two-way sled, drums made from caribou hide, and bedding and flooring made by placing caribou fur skins over spruce branches. Soap was made from boiled poplar tree ashes mixed with caribou fat. The sources also describe traditional tools among the wider body of knowledge associated with caribou anatomy.

### Jewelry & body adornment

*Yidįįłtoo* are identified as the traditional face tattoos of the Hän Gwich’in. Beadwork and porcupine quillwork are discussed as crafts and embroidery, but no jewelry types or named adornment forms are provided.

## Music & performance

They do state that caribou anatomy is associated with an “encyclopedia of stories, songs, games, toys, ceremonies, traditional tools, skin clothing, personal names and surnames, and a highly developed ethnic cuisine.” The sources also mention drums made of caribou hide. No specific drum name, playing technique, song title, or musical performance tradition is identified.

The sources describe Canada Post’s 1975 recognition of Gwichʼin-related imagery through two stamps in the Indians of Canada, Indians of the Subarctic series. One stamp was titled Ceremonial Dress and represented a ceremonial costume of the Kutchin tribe; the other, Dance of the Kutcha-Kutchin, was based on a painting by Alexander Hunter Murray.

## Festivals & rituals

Ceremonies are mentioned as part of the knowledge associated with caribou anatomy, and the sources state that caribou are integral to Gwichʼin oral histories, legends, and survival.

Traditional belief structures are represented in the sources by Johnny and Sarah Frank, Sahneuti, and Ch’eegwalti’. The Gwichʼin creation story tells that the Gwichʼin people and the caribou separated from a single entity. Caribou therefore have a central place in Gwichʼin cultural understanding, subsistence, stories, songs, and material life.

## Foodways

Caribou is traditionally a major component of the Gwichʼin diet. The Gwichʼin prefer to hunt Porcupine caribou or the barren-ground Blue Nose herd when these are available, while Gwichʼin living in Inuvik, Aklavik, Fort McPherson, and Tsiigehtchic harvest woodland caribou but not as much as other caribou. The sources state that Gwichʼin traditionally lived “mostly on caribou and all other wild meats.”

The sources also describe hunting and fishing as major parts of the contemporary Gwichʼin economy. Caribou fat was used with boiled poplar tree ashes to make soap, while caribou remains supported clothing, bedding, drums, and other material practices. The sources refer to a highly developed ethnic cuisine associated with caribou anatomy and knowledge. They also mention plants used for food, medicine, shelter, fuel, and tools, including Ts’iivii.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Gwichʼin have a strong oral tradition of storytelling that has only recently begun to be written in modern orthography. Their folk stories include the “Vazaagiitsak cycle,” literally “His Younger Brother Became Snagged,” which focuses on the comical adventures of a Gwichʼin misfit. Other named characters include Googhwaii, Ool Ti’, Tł’oo Thal, K’aiheenjik, K’iizhazhal, and Shaanyaati’.

Folk tales about prehistoric times begin with *Deenaadai’*, translated roughly as “In the ancient days.” These stories describe a time when people could talk to animals and animals could speak with people. They often function as parables concerning proper behavior, with themes including equality, generosity, hard work, kindness, mercy, cooperation for mutual success, and just revenge. Named stories include “Tsyaa Too Oozhrii Gwizhit,” “Zhoh Ts’à Nahtryaa,” and “Vadzaih Luk Hàa.”

Language revitalization projects document traditional knowledge and strengthen the writing and translation skills of younger Gwichʼin speakers.

## Language & religion

Gwichʼin is an Athabaskan language spoken in Canada and the United States. It has Eastern and Western varieties, roughly divided at the Canada–United States border, as well as several village dialects. The language is severely endangered. The sources give about 260 Gwichʼin speakers in Canada and about 300 in Alaska in one account, while another account states that fewer than 150 fluent speakers are in Alaska and another 250 in northwest Canada. Gwichʼin was named an official language of the Northwest Territories in 1988, and Gwichʼin was declared a recognized language in Alaska in 2014.

Christianity was introduced throughout Gwichʼin territory in the 1840s. Anglican and Catholic missionaries led widespread conversion, and these became the two dominant Christian sects among the Gwichʼin. The Takudh Bible is a translation of the entire King James Bible into Gwichʼin. The sources also describe traditional belief structures centered partly on caribou and creation narratives.
