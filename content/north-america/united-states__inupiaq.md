## Overview

The Iñupiat are Alaska Natives whose territory spans the Arctic coast and interior. They descend from the Thule culture and live in 34 villages across three regional corporations. Their material arts—baleen, walrus ivory, sea-mammal skin and gut—come almost entirely from the animals they hunt and eat. They maintain strong subsistence practices and a values system called the Iñupiat Ilitqusiat that guides sharing, respect and community life.

## Material culture

Iñupiat material culture is made from the animals they hunt: baleen, walrus ivory, sea-mammal intestine, hide and fur are crafted into baskets, clothing, boats, tools and art with no pottery or metalwork.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Iñupiat work fibre and sheet materials from animals by coiling, sewing and stitching rather than weaving.

- **Baleen basketry** — Round coiled baskets built from dark-grey baleen strips and bird quills, set on walrus-ivory base plates, often with carved ivory lids.
- **Gut sewing** — Sea-mammal intestine panels sewn together with sinew to make waterproof, windproof sails and surfaces.
- **Qiviut** — Muskox shed fur gathered and spun into wool for scarves, hats and gloves.
- **Fur and hide sewing** — Sealskin, moose, caribou and polar-bear hide made into garments, trimmed with rabbit, beaver, marten, otter and squirrel fur.

### Clothing & dress

Iñupiat clothing is made from hides and furs chosen by season and function—many materials are naturally waterproof or windproof.

- **Parkas, mukluks and hats** — Made from sealskin, moose hide, caribou hide or polar-bear hide, trimmed with warm fur.
- **Qiviut scarves, hats and gloves** (*qiviut*) — Muskox shed fur spun into wool for these items.
- **Labret** — A lip ornament worn in a pierced lip, made of walrus ivory or stone.

### Architecture

The sources give little detail on traditional house forms, though historically some Iñupiat were sedentary and others nomadic.

- **Nikaitchuat Iḷisaġviat** — An Iñupiaq language immersion school in Kotzebue, founded 1998.
- **Iḷisaġvik College** — The one Iñupiaq culture-oriented institution of higher education, located in Utqiaġvik.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Iñupiat everyday objects are carved from bone, ivory, baleen, stone, wood and sinew—no ceramics or metalwork.

- **Atlatl** — A wood and bone spear-thrower with an ivory spur, carved to fit the hand and set with glass beads.
- **Club** — A walrus-skull-bone head carved as a polar-bear head, lashed to a pine shaft with baleen strips.
- **Bow drill** — Walrus-ivory engraved form showing animals, hunters, dwellings, people paddling or dancing in umiaqs.
- **Umiaq and qayaq** (*umiaq, qayaq*) — Large and small skin boats made from walrus intestines and hides.
- **Dance drum** — Made from walrus intestine.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The labret, a plug worn in a pierced lip, is the best-documented ornament, made of walrus ivory or stone.

- **Labret** — A lip plug of walrus ivory or stone (slate or syenite), roughly 1.75–2.8 cm, sometimes with a hollow to hold a glass bead.

## Music & performance

Iñupiat dance drums are made from walrus intestine and are played at the Nalukataq whaling festival.

- **Nalukataq** — The spring whaling festival where dance groups from across the North perform songs and dances honouring the whale's spirit.
- **Katherine Paul** — An Iñupiaq and Swinomish heritage singer-songwriter.

## Dance & theatre

Dance at Nalukataq is performed by visiting groups from across the North to honour the whale.

- **Nalukataq dances** (*Nalukataq*) — Spring whaling festival dances performed by groups to celebrate the hunt and honour the whale's spirit.

## Festivals & rituals

Nalukataq, the spring whaling festival, is a ritualized celebration of whale hunting where the catch is distributed according to traditional formula.

- **Nalukataq** — Spring whaling festival celebrating traditional whale hunting; dance groups honour the whale's spirit as it fed entire villages.
- **Iñupiat Ilitqusiat** — A list of values created by elders: respect for elders, hard work, hunter's success, family roles, humour, respect for nature, knowledge of family tree, respect for others, sharing, love for children, cooperation, avoiding conflict, responsibility to tribe, humility and spirituality.
- **Whole-catch utilization** — An ethical practice of using all of a killed animal: hide to clothing, intestine to drums and boats, ivory and baleen to tools and art.

## Foodways

The Iñupiat are hunter-gatherers who rely heavily on subsistence hunting and fishing for walrus, seal, whale, caribou and fish.

- **Maktak** — Skin and blubber of bowhead and other whales, rich in vitamins A and C, eaten raw to preserve vitamin content.
- **Akutaq** — A traditional dessert of berries mixed with tallow.
- **Seasonal staples** — Ducks, geese, rabbits, berries, roots and shoots; combined with rosehips and highbush cranberries boiled into syrup.
- **Coastal and inland hunting** (*Tikiġaġmiut, Nunamiut*) — Coastal Iñupiat hunt walrus, seal, beluga and bowhead whale; inland Iñupiat hunt caribou, Dall sheep, grizzly bear and moose.

## Oral tradition & literature

Iñupiat oral tradition is recorded in drill-bow engravings and drawings showing hunting, travel and village life; some twentieth-century pictographic writing systems developed and died with their creators.

- **Alaskan Picture Writing** — Pictographic writing systems developed by several Iñupiat in the early twentieth century; the systems died with their creators.
- **Simon Paneak** — A Nunamiut oral historian and namesake of an ethnographic museum.
- **Contemporary writers** — Include poets Joan Kane and dg nanouk okpik, educator and poet Ticasuk Brown (1904–1982), and Howard Rock (1911–1976), founder of Tundra Times.

## Language & religion

Iñupiaq is a polysynthetic Inuit language with four major dialects; roughly 2,000 of 24,500 Iñupiat speak it, mostly elderly, after English-only boarding schools.

- **Iñupiaq language** (*Iñupiaq*) — An Inuit language of the Inuit–Yupik–Unangan family, polysynthetic and ergative–absolutive, with base-20 numerals subdivided by five.
- **Four major dialects** — North Slope, Malimiut, Bering Straits and Qawiaraq, grouped into North Slope Iñupiaq and Seward Peninsula Iñupiaq.
- **Roy Ahmaogak alphabet** — Developed in 1946 by Roy Ahmaogak, an Iñupiaq Presbyterian minister, with Eugene Nida, establishing the current Latin-script alphabet.

## Glossary

- *Iñupiaq* — A real person; singular of Iñupiat
- *qiviut* — Muskox underlayer fur spun into wool; also sea-mammal membrane
- *maktak* — Whale skin and blubber
- *akutaq* — Traditional dessert of berries and tallow
- *atlatl* — Spear-thrower
- *umiaq* — Large skin boat
- *qayaq* — Small skin boat
- *Nalukataq* — Spring whaling festival
- *labret* — Lip ornament worn in a pierced lip
- *Iñupiat Ilitqusiat* — The ethical values and guidelines created by Iñupiat elders
- *Tikiġaġmiut* — Coastal Iñupiat
- *Nunamiut* — Inland Iñupiat
- *Alaskan Picture Writing* — Pictographic writing systems developed by Iñupiat in early 20th century
