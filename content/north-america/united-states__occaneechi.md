## Overview

The Occaneechi are Indigenous peoples of the Northeastern Woodlands whose historical territory was in the Piedmont region of present-day North Carolina and Virginia. In the 17th century, they primarily lived on Occoneechee Island and east of the confluence of the Dan and Roanoke rivers, near current-day Clarksville, Virginia. They later lived near the Eno River, including Occaneechi Town near present-day Hillsborough, North Carolina, and moved to Fort Christanna in southeast Virginia. Their population was drastically reduced to approximately 600 people after warfare, settlement pressure, and newly introduced infectious diseases. Their language was possibly Siouan-Catawban or Algonquian and became a commonly used trade language. The sources describe their role in long-distance trade, archaeology, language, and continuing community identity.

## Material culture

### Architecture

They do document settlements and forts, including Occaneechi Town, Fort Christanna, and an Occaneechi village on the Eno River.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Archaeological studies found finely chipped projectile points on the former Occoneechee Island, Field's Island, Lewis Island, and Tottero, or Nelson's, Island in the Roanoke River. Researchers and lay people also discovered thousands of artifacts from Occoneechee Town, Saponi Town, and Tutelo Town on islands in the Roanoke River near Clarksville, Virginia.

## Music & performance

They state that priests and conjurers of other Virginia Indian tribes performed adorations and conjurations in the Occaneechi language, which was described as a general language and lingua franca.

## Festivals & rituals

In 1995, a community centered around Pleasant Grove, North Carolina, began hosting an annual powwow and organized under the name Occaneechi Band of Saponi.

## Foodways

The historical sources mention attempts to buy powder from the Occaneechi and describe losses of cattle during Bacon's expedition, but they do not present these references as an account of Occaneechi foodways.

## Oral tradition & literature

The historical record includes writings about the Occaneechi by Edward Bland, John Lederer, John Lawson, Robert Beverley, Jr., William Byrd II, and others. Archaeological research at Occaneechi Town and related sites provides evidence for settlement and cultural change.

## Language & religion

The meaning and origin of the name Occaneechi is unknown. The Occaneechi language was described as a Siouan-Catawban language related to the Saponi and Tutelo languages, while another account compared it with what Lahontan called “the Algonkine” and raised the possibility of an Algonquian classification. Its linguistic affiliation cannot be defined because it has no attestation. It became a commonly used trade language by the early 18th century and was widely used as a lingua franca. Linguistic scholars believe that the Occaneechi spoke a dialect of Tutelo. The sources mention priests and conjurers among other Virginia Indian tribes who used the general language for adorations and conjurations.
