## Overview

The Alutiiq, also called Sugpiaq, Pacific Eskimo, or Pacific Yupik, are a Yupik people and one of eight groups of Alaska Natives. They inhabit the southern-central coast of Alaska, including Prince William Sound, the outer Kenai Peninsula, the Kodiak Archipelago, and the Alaska Peninsula. Their traditional homelands date back to over 7,500 years ago. In the early 1800s, more than 60 Alutiiq villages existed in the Kodiak archipelago, with an estimated population of 13,000 people; today, more than 4,000 Alutiiq live in Alaska. The Alutiiq language belongs to the Eskaleut family and the Yup'ik branch. Their folk-culture significance in these sources lies in coastal subsistence, seasonal knowledge, beadwork, woodcarving, sewn skin clothing, grass weaving, storytelling, singing, and dancing.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Grass weaving** — Grass weaving served both artistic expression and functional application.

**Pinguat** — *Pinguat* are beads made from shell, bone, ivory, amber, coal, shale, slate, and fish vertebrae.

**Skin-sewn articles** — Skin-sewn articles of clothing were made for celebratory and traditional use.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no Alutiiq motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources identify skin-sewn articles of clothing used for celebratory and traditional purposes.

### Architecture

Before contact with Russian fur traders, the Alutiiq lived in semi-subterranean homes called *ciqlluaq*. In the 21st century, Alutiiq people live in coastal fishing communities in more modern housing.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that woodcarvings were associated with Alutiiq religion and that trees and shrubs were harvested for medical and nutritional value. Trade brought materials such as antler, ivory, caribou pelts, and glassy stone to Kodiak.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources identify beads called *pinguat*, made from shell, bone, ivory, amber, coal, shale, slate, and fish vertebrae, as a physical medium of Alutiiq artistic expression. They also identify Denise Wallace as a jeweler.

## Music & performance

During festivals, storytelling, singing, and dancing were important means of passing down history through generations. The sources explain that Alutiiq people lacked a formal system of writing, making these performances significant for transmitting history.

## Dance & theatre

Dancing was practiced during festivals and, together with storytelling and singing, helped pass down history through generations.

## Festivals & rituals

During winter, celebrations and festivals were held in honor of the harvest and ancestors. Seasonal activities were organized around four named periods: *Ugnerkaq*, or spring; *Kiak*, or summer; *Uksuaq*, or fall; and *Uksuq*, or winter. In spring, people collected shellfish, hunted octopuses, picked greens during low tide, hooked cod and halibut, collected herring eggs, and hunted seals. Summer brought increased activity on the open ocean, including fishing, hunting, harvesting at seal and sea lion haulouts, and activity near bird rookeries and humpback whale feeding grounds. In fall, people collected berries, harvested salmon, hunted bears, and shot migrating ducks, preserving much of the harvest for winter. During winter storms, people stayed indoors, with occasional hunting, trapping, and ice fishing.

## Foodways

Alutiiq people traditionally followed a coastal lifestyle based primarily on ocean resources, supplemented by berries and land mammals. Seasonal foods and activities included shellfish, octopuses, greens, cod, halibut, herring eggs, seals, sea lions, salmon, ducks, bears, and berries. Trees and shrubs, including cedar, Kenai birch, and Sitka spruce, were harvested for medical and nutritional value. The sources name *Qar’usiq* and *Qasrulek* for cedar and *Napaq* for Sitka spruce. Food was preserved by drying, smoking, storing in oil, or freezing. Trade supplied materials unavailable on Kodiak, including antler, ivory, caribou pelts, and glassy stone.

## Oral tradition & literature

Storytelling was an important means of passing down history through generations during festivals, alongside singing and dancing. The sources state that the Alutiiq lacked a formal system of writing. They identify Deenaalee Chase-Hodgdon as a storyteller and list the book *Qulianguat Kiputʹsluki = Bringing the Stories Back: Alutiiq Sugpiaq Remembrances of the Outer Coast of Kenai Peninsula, Alaska*. Contemporary preservation efforts include language teaching and revitalization: in 2010, the high school in Kodiak agreed to teach the Alutiiq language after requests from Alutiiq students.

## Language & religion

The Alutiiq language is also called Sugpiak, Sugpiaq, Sugcestun, Suk, Supik, Pacific Gulf Yupik, Gulf Yupik, and Koniag-Chugach. It is a distinct language closely related to Central Alaskan Yup'ik and belongs to the Eskaleut family, in the Yup'ik branch. Its two major dialects are Koniag Alutiiq and Chugach Alutiiq. About 400 of an Alutiiq population of 3,000 still speak the language, while the Kodiak dialect was spoken by only about 50 elderly persons and was in danger of being lost entirely. Alutiiq woodcarvings are associated with Alutiiq religion.
