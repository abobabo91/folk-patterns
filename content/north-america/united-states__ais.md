## Overview

The Ais or Ays were a Native American people of eastern Florida whose territory included coastal areas and islands from approximately Cape Canaveral to the Indian River. Their chiefdom consisted of towns led by chiefs subordinate to a paramount chief. The Indian River was known to the Spanish as the “River of Ais.” The Ais were hunter-gatherers who depended on fishing, shellfish collection, and gathered plants. Their language is unknown, although it has been linked to the Chitimacha language, and Spanish reports suggest that neighboring peoples spoke the same language. After 1703 the Ais were absorbed into the Costas tribe; their numbers had diminished to 137 individuals by 1711, and diseases brought by Europeans eradicated the remaining Ais/Costas by the mid-1740s. The Ais disappear from area records after 1760.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Woven palm-leaf loincloth** — Ais men wore a *loincloth* made from woven palm leaves. Jonathan Dickinson described it as colored straw-work in a triangular form, with a broad belt, strings, and a horsetail-like bunch of silkgrass fastening it behind.

**Palm-leaf platters** — Fish were served on small palmetto leaves. The sources describe this as a food-serving practice rather than a textile tradition.

### Clothing & dress

Ais men wore a *loincloth* of woven palm leaves. Dickinson described the garment as a piece of “platwork of straws” made in various colors and shaped as a triangle, with a belt four fingers broad, a section passing between the legs, strings, and a fastening of horsetail or silkgrass resembling it. This was described as the men’s entire apparel or covering. The sources say little about women’s clothing. Dickinson recorded that his wife and female slaves were given raw deer skins with which to cover themselves after their European clothing had been taken away.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name a triangular figure, colored straw-work, and a horsetail-like fastening.

### Architecture

Ais settlements included central villages that served as the seats of chiefs, scattered households, and temporary camps for seasonal resource gathering. Historic settlements were primarily located along the shores of the Indian River Lagoon. Álvaro Mexía reported in 1605 that Ais towns had dual locations, with a mainland site occupied during the summer and a barrier-island site occupied during the winter, although the sources state that little work had been done to validate this pattern. Some barrier-island sites may have been occupied year-round.

Dickinson described the town of Jece as standing about half a mile from the seashore, within land on the sound and surrounded by a swamp containing white mangrove trees. He described a cacique’s house in Santa Lucea as about forty feet long and twenty-five feet wide, covered on the top and sides with palmetto leaves. It had a range of cabins, or a barbecue, on one side and at both ends, with benches forming a passage toward the cabins.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources document pottery traditions archaeologically. The Indian River region had a ceramic tradition developing from the late Archaic Orange period through Malabar II, approximately 2000 BCE to after 1700 CE. The Malabar culture was divided into Malabar I and Malabar II on the basis of the relative abundance of pottery types.

Everyday objects included woven palm-leaf coverings and platters, baskets used to transport dried berries, fishing staffs, and European goods obtained through shipwrecks and trade. These goods included gold and silver, looking-glasses, axes, knives, tobacco, and ambergris.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They mention silkgrass and a horsetail-like fastening as part of the men’s palm-leaf clothing.

## Festivals & rituals

Archaeological evidence does document burial practices. Low sand or sand-and-shell mounds were common in the Malabar culture area and were generally located close to settlements. Mounds were typically used for burials, as were some middens. Burials were not accompanied by grave goods, and the sources report no indication of status differences in the burials. A few mounds contained circular or “wheel-spoke” burials, with up to 50 skeletons whose heads pointed toward the center of the mound.

A burial mound used by the Ais tribe for 500 to 1,000 years rises about twenty feet in Old Fort Park on Indian River Drive in Fort Pierce. The location later became an Army fort during the Second Seminole War (1838–1842), and it may have been the site of a Spanish settlement, mission, and military outpost dating to 1567.

## Foodways

The Ais did not practice agriculture. They depended on fishing, shellfish collection, and gathering wild plants. Their foods included fish, turtle, shellfish, cocoplums, sabal palm berries, palmetto, seagrape berries, and other gathered fruits. They dried some berries for future use, and Dickinson observed large baskets of dried berries being brought from different towns and delivered to the king or Young Caseekey of Jece. Archaeological sites contain charred acorns and palmetto seeds.

Fish formed the largest part of the animal diet documented for the Malabar culture. Bony fish made up about 80% of the vertebrate animals consumed, including Atlantic croaker, black drum, mullet, porcupine fish, redfish, sea catfish, and sea trout. Sharks and rays were also taken. Birds, mammals, and reptiles accounted for no more than 10% of the vertebrate animals consumed. A Spaniard shipwrecked on the Florida coast in 1595 reported that fish were caught by thrusting a thin stick “like a little harpoon” through the dorsal fin.

The Ais boiled fish and ate them from small palmetto-leaf platters, leaving the scales, head, and gills on the fish and removing the gut. Shellfish included coquina and quahog clams, oysters, cross-barred Venus clams, crown conchs, moon snails, and whelks. Dickinson recorded that clams were roasted.

## Oral tradition & literature

The principal written source for the Ais at the end of the seventeenth century is Jonathan Dickinson’s Journal, which records observations made after Dickinson and his party were shipwrecked and spent several weeks among the Ais in 1696. The journal includes descriptions of food, clothing, settlements, and encounters with chiefs. The sources also preserve accounts of Spanish contact, including Pedro Menéndez de Avilés’s report of being wrecked at Cape Canaveral in December 1571 and Álvaro Mexía’s diplomatic mission in 1605.

## Language & religion

The Ais language is unknown. It has been linked to the Chitimacha language by Julian Granberry, who noted that “Ais” means “the people” in the Chitimacha language, although the supplied source also states that Bernard Romans’s derivation of “Ais” from the Mvskokē word isi, meaning “deer,” has no basis according to Swanton. Spanish reports suggest that the Ais, Surruque, Mayaca, Jaega, and Guacata spoke the same language. Similarity of place names and other evidence has also been cited in suggesting a relationship with the unknown language of the Calusa and other peoples in southernmost Florida.
