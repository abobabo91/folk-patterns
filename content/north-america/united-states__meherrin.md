## Overview

The Meherrin were an Indigenous people of the Northeastern Woodlands who lived between the Piedmont and coastal plains at the border of Virginia and North Carolina. They spoke an Iroquian language and lived in dispersed villages where they farmed, hunted, and gathered wild foods. James Mooney estimated their population at 700 in 1600; a 1669 Virginia Indian census recorded two villages and 50 fighting men, for an estimated total of 180 Meherrin. Many later migrated north and joined the Haudenosaunee Confederacy, while the Meherrin Indian Tribe became state-recognized in North Carolina in 1986. Their documented history includes relations with the Tuscarora, Nottoway, Susquehanna, Saponi, and Machapunga peoples.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources do record that Meherrin villages included Cowinchahawkon and Meherrin Town, and that Thomas Pollock’s forces destroyed crops, homes, and belongings at Meherrin Town in August 1707.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record political and diplomatic events, including a peace treaty with the Susquehanna in 1720, and the delivery of two sons of a paramount chief as hostages in 1713 to ensure that the Meherrin would keep the peace.

## Foodways

The sources used state that the Meherrin farmed, hunted, and gathered wild foods, and that in 1707 the destruction of their crops contributed to the violence at Meherrin Town.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that Tuscarora oral history indicates common origins among the Tuscarora, Nottoway, and Meherrin, and that the Meherrin Indian Tribe says its autonym is *Kauwets'a:ka*, translated as “People of the Water.”

## Language & religion

The Meherrin spoke the Meherrin language, which is described as most likely Iroquian and as closely related to the languages of the Tuscarora and Nottoway. Linguistic evidence indicates that the three groups shared a common ancestry and likely spoke the same Iroquoian language or similar dialects. The related language article identifies Nottoway, also called Cheroenhaka and Nottoway-Meherrin, as an extinct language historically spoken by the Meherrin and Nottoway peoples; it describes Nottoway as closely related to Tuscarora within the Iroquoian language family.
