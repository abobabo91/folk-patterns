## Overview

The Cahto, also spelled Kato, are an Indigenous Californian group whose descendants are chiefly enrolled in the Cahto Indian Tribe of the Laytonville Rancheria; a smaller group is enrolled in the Round Valley Indian Tribes of the Round Valley Reservation. They traditionally occupied Cahto Valley and Long Valley, south of Blue Rock and between the headwaters of the two main branches of Eel River. Their country consisted of rolling hills, oak savannas, and streams that were nearly dry in summer and torrential in winter. The Kato language was one of four Athabaskan languages spoken in northwestern California and is now extinct. The Cahto are important to the study of folk culture because the sources document distinctive village organization, house construction, clothing, tattoos, funerary practices, mythology, shamanism, and singing traditions.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The primitive costume for both men and women consisted of a tanned deer-skin wrapped around the waist and a close-fitting knitted cap that held in place the knot of hair at the back of the head. At a later period, the Cahto garment included a shirt made from two deer-skins, laced down the front and reaching to the knees. The sources describe these garments for both men and women rather than distinguishing separate forms of everyday dress. Clothing was also associated with funerary preparation: a corpse was washed, dressed in good garments, and wrapped in deer skins.

### Architecture

A Cahto house was built in a circular excavation about two feet deep. Four forked posts were erected at the corners of a square within the excavation; the front pair were slightly taller, giving the roof a slight pitch toward the rear. The house’s final form was determined more by the circular base than by the small roof. Bunches of long grasses, slabs of wood, and bark were placed between the posts. An opening in the roof carried away smoke, while the doorway was a narrow opening extending from the ground to the roof. As many as three families could occupy one house and cook at the same fire. Summer camps used brush lean-tos. The sources also mention a ceremonial house where boys gathered during the winter for instruction in tribal folklore.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that the Cahto traditionally made articles of stone, bone, horn, wood, and skin. The sources mention bone objects in connection with singing: two people kept time by striking one bone against another.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The Cahto generally had tattoos on the face and chest. The designs consisted largely of upright lines, both broken and straight. Hair was tied in a knot at the back of the head and held in place by a close-fitting knitted cap. In mourning, women, and occasionally men, cut their hair short as a sign of grief.

## Music & performance

A favorite pastime for Cahto women was to assemble early in the evening for singing in chorus. One of the best singers led the performance, while two others kept time by striking one bone with another. The men did not participate in the singing but gathered around and listened.

Music and performance also formed part of instruction and ritual life. In midsummer, boys between 12 and perhaps 16 years old were led to a solitary place by two men, one of whom was the teacher. They received instruction in mythology and in the supposed origin of customs, including mortuary rites, shamanistic practices, and puberty observances. During the winter, the boys assembled again in the ceremonial house and remained there during the four winter months while receiving instruction in tribal folklore.

## Festivals & rituals

Annually in midsummer, a group of boys ranging from 12 to perhaps 16 years old was led to a solitary place for instruction in mythology and the origins of customs. In winter, the same boys gathered in the ceremonial house for the four winter months to learn tribal folklore.

Children of both sexes were required to observe rites at puberty. At puberty, a girl began a quiet and abstemious life lasting five months. She remained in or near the house, abstained from meat, drank little water, and was not permitted to work because she might catch a cold. Marriage was arranged between the two people concerned without consulting anyone else. After securing a girl’s consent, her lover slept with her clandestinely at night and left at dawn. The secret could last several days, and the girl’s parents learned of the marriage indirectly. The young man might then erect a house of his own.

Marriage could also be dissolved by either partner for any reason. The man retained male children and the woman female children. When adultery was discovered, the result was described as bickering and possibly an invitation for the offender to establish a permanent relationship with the new love.

Funerals involved washing and clothing the corpse, wrapping it in deer skins, and placing it in a pit on a dry hillside. The grave was prepared with poles, bark, and deer skins before the corpse was covered with bark and earth. The population accompanied the bearers, wailing loudly. A mourning ceremony for prominent persons was held in the year following death and marked the end of mourning.

## Foodways

They state that several families could cook at the same fire inside a house and that a girl undergoing puberty observances abstained from meat and drank little water.

## Oral tradition & literature

Cahto oral tradition included mythology, tribal folklore, and accounts of the origin of customs. The religious conceptions of the Kato were grouped around Chénĕśh or T'cenes, the creator associated with thunder and lightning, and Nághai-cho or Nagaicho, the Great Traveler. Nághai-cho was described as a mischievous companion who urged Chénĕśh toward acts of creation while pretending to possess the knowledge and power to perform them himself.

In the creation account, Chénĕśh dwelt in the sky above an expanse of water with a rim of land in the north. Together with Nághai-cho, he descended and turned a monstrous deer into land. Chénĕśh created the people, while Nághai-cho made the mountains and streams. Nághai-cho repeatedly attempted to outdo Chénĕśh and played the role of buffoon and trickster.

The sources also describe a preceding story about animal-people who were swept from the earth by a deluge. Cahto people belonged to the Kuksu religion. The sources name Kato texts and an experimental Cahto lexical database among materials connected with documentation.

## Language & religion

Cahto, also spelled Kato, was an Athabaskan language formerly spoken by the Kato people of the Laytonville and Branscomb area at the head of the South Fork of the Eel River. It belonged to the California Athabaskan cluster of Pacific Coast Athabaskan languages. The other northwestern California Athabaskan languages named in the sources are Eel River Athabaskan, Mattole-Bear River, and Hupa-Chilula. Most Kato speakers were bilingual in Northern Pomo, and some also spoke Yuki. Cahto became extinct in the 1960s. The language had 26 consonant phonemes, 30 phones, 9 vowel phonemes including the diphthong, and 12 phones.

Cahto religious conceptions centered on Chénĕśh or T'cenes and Nághai-cho or Nagaicho. Cahto people also belonged to the Kuksu religion. Shamans were divided into three classes: *ŭtiyíņ'*, who removed the foreign object causing disease by sucking; *'náchǔlna'*, who cured illness caused by woodland creatures; and *'chģhályiśh'*, who were not healers but restored victims of the diminutive “outside people” and could foresee the future in dreams. The sources state that *ŭtiyíņ'* became medicine men through instruction, while the other two classes acquired their power through dreams.
