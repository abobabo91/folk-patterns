## Overview

The Wichita are a confederation of Southern Plains Native American tribes whose ancestral homelands extended through Oklahoma, Texas, and Kansas. Their settlements historically ranged from the Red River in Arkansas north to Nebraska, and from San Antonio, Texas, to Great Bend, Kansas. Today the Wichita and Affiliated Tribes are headquartered in Anadarko, Oklahoma, with their tribal jurisdictional area in Caddo County. In 2018, 2,953 people were enrolled in the Wichita and Affiliated Tribes. Wichita is a Caddoan language, historically related in language and culture to Pawnee, while Kichai is also identified among the affiliated peoples. Wichita cultural history includes settled villages, grass lodges, agriculture, bison hunting, trade, pottery, tattooing, and distinctive forms of social and political organization.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Tanned-hide clothing** — Wichita women prepared and sewed clothing from tanned hides. Dresses were often decorated with elk canine teeth.

**Wichita pottery** — Wichita people made ceramic pottery, which fascinated French and Spanish traders. The source describes Wichita pottery as difficult for an untrained observer to distinguish from Osage and Pawnee pottery.

**Grass-lodge models** — Museum records include models of grass lodges associated with the Wichita in Indian Territory. The catalogue identifies the Wichita as a Caddoan tribe formerly located in northern Texas.

**Museum photographic albums** — The museum records show Wichita and Tawakoni people wearing blankets, woven waistcoats, western-style shirts, and jackets. These records document clothing and adornment but do not identify named textile patterns or techniques.

**Motif vocabulary.** Solid and dotted lines and circles, tattoo marks around the eyes, elk canine teeth.

### Clothing & dress

Wichita people wore clothing made from tanned hides prepared and sewn by women. Dresses were often decorated with elk canine teeth. Men and women tattooed their faces and bodies with solid and dotted lines and circles. The museum records document Wichita men in western-style shirts, waistcoats, jackets, trousers, shoes, and moccasins. Blankets were wrapped around the waist, torso, or shoulders. Assadawa, identified as a Wichita chief, is shown wearing two-piece and three-piece suits, neck ties, metal ear ornaments, lapel pins, and a hat with a feather. Black Horse is shown with a western-style shirt, a blanket, bone ear ornaments, and a braid in his hair. Esquitzchew is shown wearing a western-style woven waistcoat, a blanket, and hooped ear ornaments. The sources do not identify named Wichita garments, belts, head coverings, or ceremonial dress.

### Architecture

The Wichita lived in settled villages with domed-shaped grass lodges, sometimes up to 30 feet (9.1 m) in diameter. For much of the year, they lived in huts made of forked cedar poles covered by dry grasses. In winter, they followed American bison in seasonal hunting camps. Museum collections include models and photographs of Wichita grass houses. Earlier Wichita communities also lived in rectangular, thatched-roof houses, while later descriptions record round, grass-thatched houses surrounded by large granaries for corn, beans, and squash. The source also describes villages with dispersed homesteads and, in Great Bend aspect settlements, structures called “council circles.” These consisted of a central patio surrounded by four semi-subterranean structures; their function remains unclear.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Wichita ceramic pottery was made locally and was traded in a region where French and Spanish observers encountered it. The source specifically compares it with Osage and Pawnee pottery. Wichita households used bison products for clothing, food, cooking fat, winter shelter, leather supplies, sinew, medicine, and armor. Agriculture produced maize, beans, squash, sunflowers, marsh elder, and tobacco, while people also gathered roots, fruits, walnuts, and hickory nuts. Historical accounts mention granaries for storing crops. The sources do not provide named Wichita forms of metalwork, wooden objects, baskets, or specialized household vessels.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Wichita men and women tattooed their faces and bodies with solid and dotted lines and circles. The Wichita name *kirikir?i:s* is explained in connection with tattoo marks around the eyes, and the source gives the gloss “raccoon-eyed people.” Pawnee, Arikara, and Kiowa names likewise refer to Wichita tattooing. Museum photographs record bone and metal ear ornaments, hooped ear ornaments, bear claw pendants, lapel pins, and a feather in a hat. The sources do not describe the ritual functions of these ornaments or identify named jewelry types.

## Music & performance

The Wichita Annual Dance is a powwow held at the Wichita Tribal Park on US-281, north of Anadarko, every August. The sources identify this as a contemporary cultural institution but do not name instruments, song genres, musical forms, or performance repertories. They also do not describe musical practice at weddings, funerals, courts, or other gatherings. The available material therefore covers the annual powwow as a public performance context but does not provide further information about Wichita music.

## Festivals & rituals

The Wichita Annual Dance, a powwow, is held every August at the Wichita Tribal Park on US-281, north of Anadarko. The Wichita Tribal History Center in Anadarko, opened in 2018, shares Wichita history, archaeology, visual arts, and culture with the public. The sources describe religious references to a Great Spirit worshipped by the Wichita and identify tobacco as important for religious purposes in earlier agricultural communities. They also preserve a creation account in which a man and woman dreamed that things were made for them, and a woman received an ear of corn as food for future generations. The sources do not describe a complete annual ritual calendar, weddings, funerals, coming-of-age ceremonies, or other life-cycle rites.

## Foodways

Wichita subsistence combined agriculture, hunting, gathering, and fishing. Crops included maize, beans, squash, sunflowers, marsh elder, and tobacco. These crops are identified in the source as the Three Sisters in the case of maize, beans, and squash. Gathered foods included walnut and hickory nuts and the fruits of plum, hackberry, and grape. Wichita people hunted deer, rabbits, turkey, bison, elk, pronghorn, and dog in different historical settings, and they caught fish and harvested mussels from rivers. Bison was especially important: Wichita people used all parts for food, cooking fat, clothing, shelter, leather, sinew, medicine, and armor. Historical accounts also mention maize, melons, pumpkins, squash, and tobacco grown by the Taovaya. The sources do not name specific dishes, beverages, cooking recipes, or dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

The source preserves a Wichita creation account attributed to Tawakoni Jim in *The Mythology of the Wichita*, published in 1904. In it, a man and woman dreamed that things were made for them; the woman received an ear of corn that was to become food for future generations. Historical descriptions also record statements attributed to Francisco Vásquez de Coronado about the people of Quivira and accounts by Juan de Oñate concerning Etzanoa. The Wichita language is being documented through the Wichita Documentation Project of the University of Colorado, Boulder. David S. Rood collaborated with Wichita speakers to create a dictionary and language CDs. The sources do not identify a Wichita epic, proverb tradition, riddle tradition, or named literary genre.

## Language & religion

Wichita is a Caddoan language, historically spoken by the Wichita and Affiliated Tribes in Anadarko, Oklahoma. The language had dialects identified as Waco, Tawakoni, and Kirikirʔi꞉s, also called Wichita Proper. The last fluent heritage speaker, Doris Lamar-McLemore, died in 2016, and the language is described as functionally extinct or dormant. The tribe offers classes and has created an immersion class for children and a class for adults. Wichita is agglutinative and polysynthetic, with information commonly expressed through bound morphemes in the verb complex. The language has no gender distinction and distinguishes singular, dual, and plural number. Historically, tobacco was important for religious purposes, and an account records belief in a Great Spirit. The sources do not provide a fuller description of Wichita religious institutions or current religious affiliations.
