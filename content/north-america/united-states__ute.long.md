## Overview

The Utes are an Indigenous people of the Great Basin and Colorado Plateau in present-day Utah, western Colorado, and northern New Mexico; their historical territory also included parts of Wyoming, Nevada, and Arizona. They had settled in the Four Corners region by 1500 CE and traditionally lived as nomadic hunting and gathering bands. Today, three federally recognized tribes are identified: the Southern Ute Indian Tribe of Colorado, the Ute Indian Tribe of the Uintah and Ouray Reservation in Utah, and the Ute Mountain Ute Tribe of Colorado. Ute is a Colorado River Numic dialect in the Uto-Aztecan language family. Ute culture includes hunting, trade, beadwork, basketry, pottery, music, dance, oral tradition, and sacred relationships with places such as Pikes Peak, the mineral springs at Manitou Springs, and Ute Mountain.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Beadwork** — The sources document seed beadwork on a small man's or child's fringed hide shirt, with decoration at the cuffs, neck, and shoulders in black, white, blue, and green. Shoulder fringes carried haliotis shell pendants, textile ribbons, strips of tin, and deer-hoof tinklers. Beaded chokers, sashes, neck ornaments, leggings, moccasins, and other garments also appear in museum portraits.

**Hide garments** — Museum records describe shirts, smocks, leggings, dresses, ponchos, and tunics made of hide, often with fringe, beads, or other decoration. The sources do not identify named Ute stitch techniques or pattern systems.

**Moccasins** — A British Museum record describes a pair of woman's moccasins with attached leggings, made of hide and beads. Other portraits show Ute men and women wearing moccasins with decorated leggings and hide clothing.

**Quillwork sashes** — Museum portraits show men wearing quillwork sashes, sometimes together with beaded chokers, bead neck ornaments, belts, or peace medals.

**Embroidered clothing** — A portrait of Suriap, an Ute chief, shows an embroidered shirt with tassels, an embroidered sash across the chest, embroidered leggings with long flaps, and moccasins.

**Fringed clothing** — The museum records repeatedly describe fringed hide shirts, skin dresses, leggings, tunics, smocks, and sashes. Fringe is also present on a painted skin quiver.

**Blankets and robes** — Museum portraits show chiefs and other Ute people with blankets, while one portrait depicts an Ute girl surrounded by a fur robe.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name triangular motifs on a woman's dress, tassels, fringe, painted decoration, quillwork, beadwork, and embroidered decoration; they do not provide a broader Ute motif vocabulary.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe Ute clothing through both historical accounts and museum portraits. Men wore decorated hide shirts, leggings, moccasins, smocks, sashes, belts, and sometimes western-style jackets, trousers, shoes, or military jackets. Some men wore hats, bowler hats, trailer warbonnets, or feathers. Women were shown wearing ponchos, skin dresses with fringes, dresses with triangular motifs, cloth dresses, moccasins, and stripe garments. A photograph depicts Ta-vah-puts wearing a cloth dress and blanket while seated in front of a tipi. Beaded chokers, bead and shell neck ornaments, metal ear ornaments, dentalium ear ornaments, shell gorgets, rings, and belts were part of documented dress. The sources also describe long hair in braids or bunches, and one photograph shows a woman wearing an ear-ring and neck-ornament.

### Architecture

The sources describe eastern Utes as living in tepees after the 17th century and show a museum photograph of an Ute woman seated in front of a tipi. They also mention winter camps established along rivers and mountain valleys, and a bear living in a wickiup in a Bear Dance origin story. The sources do not provide a fuller account of house construction, roof forms, or architectural decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum records document a coiled basket and a possible water-bottle made of vegetal material and partly covered in pitch, with cloth, skin, and possible horse-hair handles. They also describe a painted fringed quiver made of skin and beads. A photograph shows two pots in front of a tipi. Museum portraits record metal ear ornaments, strips of tin, peace medals, shell ornaments, and guns. The sources mention grinding stones at Garden of the Gods and describe the processing of meat and tanning of hides there. They do not identify named ceramic forms or a distinct metalworking tradition.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Documented adornment includes beaded chokers, bead neck ornaments, shell neck ornaments, a shell gorget, dentalium ear ornaments, metal ear ornaments, rings, and an ear-ring. The decorated hide shirt in the British Museum record includes haliotis shell pendants, textile ribbons, strips of tin, and deer-hoof tinklers among the shoulder fringes. Museum portraits also show quillwork sashes, embroidered sashes, breast ornaments, feathers, and belts. The sources describe men's hair in braids or two bunches but do not document tattoos, painting practices outside warfare, or named jewelry forms.

## Music & performance

Ute music includes social, religious, and emotional songs. Named song types include war songs, social dance songs, parade songs, medicine songs, love songs, game songs, and story songs. Most songs are chants with few lyrics; their histories and meanings rely heavily on the singer's emotions. The sources describe a guttural, tight singing tone, often compared by a Ute singer to singing while riding a galloping horse. Songs commonly remain within an octave and often descend from higher to lower registers. Ute music does not use rhythmic groups such as measures.

The sources name the **morache**, hand drum, large drum, and flageolet as instruments. The morache is a notched-stick rattle with a resonator, played by rubbing a short stick or bone across a longer notched stick. The hand drum is about 12 inches in diameter and has a skin head, cloth handle, and drumstick. The large drum is used for dances such as the Turkey dance and Women's dance, with 8 to 10 singers seated around it and as many as 14 sometimes present. The flageolet is a wooden wind instrument approximately 11 inches long, with 6 sound holes.

Frances Densmore recorded and studied Ute music as part of her work for the Bureau of American Ethnology. Ute folklore songs were handed down by rote, and the sources note that some Native American people opposed outside documentation of indigenous music.

## Dance & theatre

The **Bear Dance** is an iconic ceremonial dance of the Ute tribe and is also described as a social dance of sociability and general good feeling. It may be performed at festivals lasting multiple days, up to a week, including the Tam-Nam Nacup Springtime Festival. The Bear Dance uses singing, drums, and the morache, whose growling sound imitates a bear. Downward glissandos imitate bear sounds, and the dance typically is sung only by men.

The dance is named *Mamaqui Mawats*, meaning “to move forward and back.” Men and women participate while facing different directions: women face toward the band, while men face east. The dance is led by a Bear Dance leader, and a member of the band may request permission to give the dance. The sources also describe the Lame Dance, performed by women after war parties to symbolize pulling or carrying heavy loads of loot. No theatrical tradition is described.

## Festivals & rituals

The Utes traditionally came together for ceremonies and trade, and the sources describe annual trade fairs or rescates with Rio Grande River Pueblo people at Taos, Santa Clara, Pecos, and other pueblos. These fairs were ceremonial and social events that could last up to ten days or more. The sources do not provide a complete annual festival calendar or descriptions of Ute birth, marriage, or funeral rites.

The Bear Dance takes place in spring and is not danced in winter according to the story attributed to Snake John. It is connected with a bear emerging from its winter shelter and moving forward and backward near a tree. Ute belief holds that the people descended from bears and that the dance creates a bridge between people and their ancestors. The sources also mention annual performances of the Bear and Sun dances. Men preparing for warfare fasted, took part in sweat lodge ceremonies, and painted their faces and horses for special symbolic meanings.

## Foodways

The sources describe a subsistence economy based on hunting and gathering, including hunting buffalo, deer, elk, and other game. Meat was processed after hunts, and grinding stones found at Garden of the Gods suggest gatherings for tanning hides and processing meat. Utes traded meat, buffalo robes, soft tanned deerskins, maize, flour, and other goods. They also hunted in areas extending through Utah, Colorado, Wyoming, Oklahoma, Texas, and New Mexico.

The sources do not identify named Ute dishes, staple recipes, cooking methods, beverages, dietary rules, or ceremonial foods. They mention foods and trade goods such as meat, maize, and flour but do not provide enough information for a fuller foodways account.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ute mythology consists of stories and beliefs expressing cultural heritage and values. The sources describe a Ute creation narrative in which the people have always occupied their mountainous ancestral region. They also provide a Bear Dance origin story in which a bear emerges from its winter shelter, dances forward and backward, and is observed by a human hunter.

Named mythological figures include Wolf, described as the creator and culture hero, and Coyote, Wolf's younger brother and a trickster. Traditional Ute linguistic material includes words for Wolf, Little Bird, Mountain Lion, Fox, Mountain Sheep, Turtle, Bear, Coyote, and Weasle. Listed folk tales include Pokoh, the Old Man; Blood Clot; Porcupine Hunts Buffalo; Hunt deer; Hunt elk; Hunt grasshoppers; Puma and the Bear; Two Grandsons; and Coyote & Duck. The sources state that Ute folklore songs were learned by rote and were not written down.

## Language & religion

Ute is a dialect of Colorado River Numic in the Southern subdivision of the Numic branch of the Uto-Aztecan language family. It is closely related to Shoshone, and other dialects in the dialect chain include Chemehuevi and Southern Paiute. The Ute language is spoken on the Uintah-Ouray, Southern Ute, and Ute Mountain reservations. The language is classified as threatened, although tribally sponsored revitalization programs exist. The Ute people refer to their language as *núu-'apaghapi̱* or *núuchi*, meaning “the people's speech” and “of the people.”

The sources describe sacred relationships with nature, animals, and particular places. Pikes Peak was a sacred ceremonial area, and the mineral springs at Manitou Springs were sacred. Ute mythology holds that the people descended from bears and that the Bear Dance connects them with their ancestors. The sources do not describe a current religious denomination or formal religious institution.
