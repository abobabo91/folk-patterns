## Overview

The Appalousa, also called Opelousa, were an Indigenous people of the Southeastern Woodlands in Louisiana. In the 18th century they lived near present-day Opelousas, west of the lower Mississippi River, and their territory was associated with lakes, bayous, and the westernmost channel of the Mississippi River. Their 18th-century village had about 40 men, while historical estimates give roughly 130 men in 1715, about 40 people in 1805, and about 20 members in 1814. Their language is completely undocumented. John Sibley described it as different from all others, although many Appalousa understood Atakapa and French. In the early 20th century, John R. Swanton and Frederick W. Hodge tentatively classified it as Atakapa.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state only that the Appalousa may have painted or stained their lower legs a dark color, and that one interpretation of their name connects it with “black leg,” “black hair,” or “black skull.” The sources also report a debated explanation that the people painted their legs black to contrast with their lighter skin, while another explanation attributes the dark appearance to staining from the black waters of the lakes where they hunted and fished.

### Architecture

They state that an 18th-century Appalousa village existed and that, in 1814, Appalousa and Atakapa villages stood on the north and south parts of the bayou. The sources also connect Appalousa territorial history with the establishment of the city of Opelousas by French traders in 1740.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They report that, around the 1920s, Appalousas camped at Ringrose plantation, sold palmetto stems and cane split baskets to the plantation owners, and traded fish for flint with the Avoyel. The sources also mention that they raised corn, cattle, and pigs.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They record the debated reports that Appalousa people painted or stained their lower legs black, either because of the dark lake waters or as a deliberate contrast with lighter skin.

## Music & performance

They do record games of stickball involving Appalousas and the Tunica around the 1920s, when Appalousas camped at Ringrose plantation. This activity is described in connection with Claude Medford, a Choctaw craftsman, and with the sale of palmetto stems and cane split baskets.

## Dance & theatre

They mention stickball as a game played by Appalousas with the Tunica around the 1920s, but provide no further description of its rules, ceremonial meaning, or performance context.

## Festivals & rituals

Religious information is limited to genealogical records in St. Landry Parish, Louisiana, which list Appalousa Indians who were buried and baptized. These records also include Indians from other tribes across Louisiana. Joseph Willis, an African American Baptist preacher, was among the tribe prior to 1812.

The sources do describe an Atakapa origin story shared with neighboring tribes. It concerns two forbidden lovers from different tribes: an elite woman and a warrior. After the woman’s father killed the warrior, she stabbed herself. The great spirit then hung her hair in an oak tree, where it turned gray and spread through the trees. The story is presented as an example of the importance of Appalousa territorial history.

## Foodways

The sources used provide only limited information about Appalousa foodways. They state that the Appalousa raised corn, cattle, and pigs, and that the only documented diet was fish, especially flounder, described as a favorite of the region. Fish was also used in trade, including exchange with the Avoyel for flint.

The sources report that the Appalousa and Atakapa received fish from the Chitimacha and Atakapa and traded it with the Avoyel for flint. They also state that the Appalousa went to war with the Avoyel after the latter refused to trade flints they possessed in abundance. According to some accounts, captured Avoyels were eaten, although whether cannibalism occurred among the Appalousa or Atakapa is unknown and debated.

## Oral tradition & literature

The principal oral tradition recorded in the sources is the Atakapa origin story shared with neighboring tribes. It tells of forbidden lovers, the killing of a warrior, the woman’s self-inflicted death, and the great spirit’s transformation of her hair into the gray growth of an oak tree and surrounding trees. The story served as an example of the importance of Appalousa territorial history and was connected with the profitability and economics of the region, which led French traders to establish the city of Opelousas in 1740.

The sources also preserve competing explanations for the name Appalousa or Opelousa. Antoine-Simon Le Page du Pratz referred to the people as the Lopelousas and Oqué-Loussas, although it remains debated whether these names identified the same tribe. Proposed Choctaw translations include “black above,” “black legs,” “black head,” and “black hair.” The origin of the name remains unknown.

## Language & religion

The Appalousa language is completely undocumented. John Sibley reported in an 1805 letter to Thomas Jefferson that the Opelousa spoke a language different from all others, while many understood Atakapa and French. The region had been colonized by the French since the mid-18th century. In the early 20th century, John R. Swanton and Frederick W. Hodge tentatively classified the Opelousa language as Atakapa. The sources state that Opelousa and Atakapa are Choctaw words.

The sources say very little about Appalousa religious practices. Genealogical records in St. Landry Parish list Appalousas who were buried and baptized, but the same list includes members of other tribes. The Atakapa origin story refers to a great spirit.
