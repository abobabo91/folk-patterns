## Overview

The Luiseño, or *Payómkawichum*, are an Indigenous people of California whose traditional territory extended along the coast of southern California from the present-day southern part of Los Angeles County to the northern part of San Diego County, and inland from the coast. Their name for themselves means “People of the West.” Their communities included villages near the Santa Margarita River, San Luis Rey River, Temecula, Pala, Palomar Mountain, and other places in Riverside County and San Diego County. Six federally recognized Luiseño bands have reservations, while another organized band is not federally recognized. The Luiseño language belongs to the Cupan group of Takic languages within the Uto-Aztecan family. Luiseño culture is documented through its relationships with native plants, animals, rivers, missions, villages, oral traditions, songs, and continuing language-preservation work.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state that the Payómkawichum used native plants and hunted animals for food and clothing, and that men hunted animals for game and skins.

### Architecture

They do identify settlements and villages located near rivers, lakes, springs, valleys, mountains, and the coast, including *Páala* at Pala, *Pichaang* at Pechanga, *Táaxanashpa* at La Jolla, and *Wáșxa* at Rincon.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They record the use of native plants, seeds, berries, nuts, fruits, vegetables, animal skins, and toxins leached from California buckeye nuts to stupefy fish in mountain creeks.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They do record the extraction of pink tourmaline, pegmatite, and morganite beryl around the Pala Reservation in the 1890s. Pink tourmaline became the tribe’s main export until 1911.

## Music & performance

The sources describe singing as part of contemporary Luiseño language-preservation work. In some independent bands, individuals study the language, preservation materials are compiled, and singers sing traditional songs in Luiseño.

The Luiseño language article records sound documentation by linguist John Peabody Harrington. In the 1930s, Harrington made recordings of Luiseño speakers on aluminum disks. The recordings were deposited in the United States National Archives and later digitized and made available on the internet by the Smithsonian Institution.

The sources also identify Pablo Tac as an important historical figure in Luiseño language documentation. Born at San Luis Rey in 1822, Tac devised a written form of the language through his study of Latin grammar and Spanish while working among international scholars in Rome.

## Dance & theatre

They mention traditional songs and the preservation of the Luiseño language.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe major historical events affecting Payómkawichum communities.

Mission San Luis Rey de Francia was established entirely within Payómkawichum territory in 1798 and was founded on June 13, 1798, by Father Fermín Francisco de Lasuén. After the Mexican War of Independence in 1821, the Mexican Empire assumed ownership of Payómkawichum lands. In 1822, Mexican troops confiscated coastal lands and granted much of them to Mexican settlers.

Following the Mexican-American War, Payómkawichum leaders entered negotiations connected with the Treaty of Temecula and Treaty of San Luis Rey, but the treaties remained unratified. In 1875, reservations were permitted to be established in the area, while the state of California evicted the Payómkawichum from the Temecula Valley. In 1882, another round of reservations was permitted under President Chester A. Arthur. These events are historical and political contexts rather than documented ritual practices.

## Foodways

The Payómkawichum used many native plants and harvested seeds, berries, nuts, fruits, and vegetables for a varied diet. The men hunted many animal species for game and skins, including antelopes, bobcats, deer, elk, foxes, mice, mountain lions, rabbits, wood rats, river otters, and ground squirrels. The sources also mention a wide variety of insects.

In mountain creeks, the Luiseño used toxins leached from the nuts of California buckeye to stupefy fish and harvest them.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do describe an extensive oral tradition in connection with the Luiseño language.

Pablo Tac recorded that approximately five thousand people were living in Payómkawichum territory before the arrival of the Spanish, stating that this information came perhaps from oral history and official records. Tac was born in 1820 according to the Luiseño article and at San Luis Rey in 1822 according to the language article. He developed a written form of Luiseño while studying in Rome and created an Indigenous framework for understanding the language through his word choice, narrative form, and continual translation between Luiseño and Spanish.

The Luiseño language article also records language-revitalization efforts. The Pechanga Band of Luiseño Indians offers classes for children, and in 2013 the tribe began funding a graduate-level Luiseño class at Cal State San Bernardino. A Luiseño video game for the Nintendo DS was being used to teach the language to young people in 2012.

## Language & religion

Luiseño, also called *Chamtéela*, is a Uto-Aztecan language of California. It belongs to the Cupan group of Takic languages within the larger Uto-Aztecan family. The language article states that Luiseño went extinct in the early 2010s, while an active revitalization project assisted by linguists from the University of California, Riverside, is underway. The Luiseño article states that about 30 to 40 people speak the language.

Luiseño has no standardized spelling, although a commonly accepted orthography is used in reservation classrooms and college campuses in San Diego. Its written tradition extends back to the Spanish settlement of San Diego. Pablo Tac developed an orthography while studying in Rome to become a Catholic priest, and his system was strongly influenced by Spanish.

The sources mention Catholic missions, including Mission San Luis Rey de Francia.
