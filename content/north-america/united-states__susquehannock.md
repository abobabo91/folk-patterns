## Overview

The Susquehannock, also known as the Conestoga, Minquas, and Andaste, were an Iroquoian people who lived in the lower Susquehanna River watershed in what is now Pennsylvania. Their name means “people of the muddy river,” although what they called themselves is not known. Their settlements extended through parts of present-day Pennsylvania, Maryland, and West Virginia, including the lower Susquehanna River valley and the Conestoga River area of Lancaster County, Pennsylvania. The Susquehannock language was a Northern Iroquoian language closely related to the languages of the Haudenosaunee, especially Onondaga. Their population declined through disease, war, displacement, and massacre. The last members of the Conestoga community were killed in 1763, although people of Susquehannock ancestry remain among recognized Haudenosaunee nations and other communities.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name geometric patterns on Schultz Incised pottery, including incised lines forming geometric patterns.

### Clothing & dress

In the fur trade, Susquehannock exchanged furs for cloth, glass beads, brass kettles, hawk bells, axes, hoes, and knives. The Pennsylvania government occasionally provided clothing and provisions to the impoverished inhabitants of Conestoga Town. Burial deposits included bead or shell necklaces, pendants, tobacco pipes, combs, knives, clay pots, brass kettles, and occasionally gun parts.

### Architecture

Susquehannock communities lived in semi-permanent palisaded villages built on river terraces and surrounded by agricultural fields. Villages contained numerous longhouses surrounded by a double palisade. Each bark-covered shelter could be up to 80 feet (24 m) in length and house as many as 60 individuals. Multiple families related through the matriline lived in one longhouse; sons remained in the extended household until marriage and then moved to their wife’s family’s longhouse.

The Susquehannock moved their villages roughly every 25 years when soil fertility and nearby resources became depleted. The Schultz Site in present-day Lancaster County contained at least 26 longhouses before it was largely abandoned c. 1600. A larger fortified town near what is today Washington Boro was estimated at 250,000 square feet and had a population of about 1,700 people. In 1675, the Susquehannock erected a palisaded fort on Piscataway Creek.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Susquehannock women made shell-tempered pottery of various sizes, primarily for cooking. Three pottery types are identified in what is now Lancaster County: Schultz Incised, produced until c. 1600; Washington Boro Incised, produced between 1600 and 1635; and Strickler Cordmarked, produced between 1635 and 1680.

Schultz Incised had a high collar marked with incised geometric patterns. Washington Boro Incised had a narrower collar and was known for “face pots,” whose collars carried two to four expressionless human faces. Strickler Cordmarked lacked the collars, geometric designs, and face effigies of the earlier types. As European goods became more common, many cooking pots were replaced by brass kettles.

Dugout canoes carried meat, pelts, and people across the Susquehanna River. Fishing weirs were used to catch American shad and eels. Dried crops were stored in circular or bell-shaped subterranean storage pits lined with bark and dried grasses.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Burial goods included bead or shell necklaces and pendants. Susquehannock graves also contained tobacco pipes, combs, knives, clay pots, brass kettles, and occasionally gun parts. Pipes were formed from clay or carved from soapstone, and their bowls were frequently decorated with geometric designs or human or animal effigies.

## Music & performance

The vocabulary compiled by Johannes Campanius includes words specifically meaning “smoking tobacco” and a word for “pipe for smoking tobacco,”.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe funerary practices. The Susquehannock typically buried their dead in individual graves in cemeteries outside the palisade walls. Multiple burials were also found, especially at the Strickler site, occupied from c. 1645 to c. 1665. These burials generally contained an adult and one or more children. Bodies were flexed and were usually accompanied by grave goods such as bead or shell necklaces, pendants, tobacco pipes, combs, knives, clay pots, brass kettles, and occasionally gun parts.

Tobacco was an important aspect of Susquehannock culture. Almost all graves dating from the relevant period, including those of women and older children, contained pipes among the grave goods.

## Foodways

Maize, beans, and squash were staple foods. Iroquoian people called them the Three Sisters. In companion planting, maize and climbing beans were planted together in mounds, while squash was planted between the mounds. Maize-based meals, usually in the form of soup, provided nearly half of the Susquehannock caloric intake.

The Susquehannock had a varied and seasonal diet. Deer was the most common animal protein, while elk, black bear, fish, freshwater mussels, wild turkey, and waterfowl were also eaten. Wild plants, fruits, and nuts supplemented the diet. Dried crops were stored in subterranean pits lined with bark and dried grasses.

## Oral tradition & literature

A vocabulary of just over 100 words and phrases was recorded by the Swedish missionary Johannes Campanius during the 1640s in the *Vocabula Mahakuassica*. The vocabulary was published by his grandson Thomas Campanius Holm in 1696 and 1702; Peter Stephen Du Ponceau translated the 1702 work from Swedish to English in 1834.

The sources also mention a historical novel, *Jacob My Friend: His 17th Century Account of the Susquehannock Indians*, by Barry Kent. It concerns the Dutch fur-trader and interpreter Jacob Young, who married a Susquehannock woman and had several children.

## Language & religion

Susquehannock was an extinct Iroquoian language spoken by the people variously known as the Susquehannock or Conestoga. It was a Northern Iroquoian language closely related to the languages of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy, particularly Onondaga. Little of the language has been preserved. The chief source is the vocabulary compiled by Johannes Campanius during the 1640s; it contains about 100 words in one account and just over 100 words and phrases in another.

Examples of Susquehannock-language place names include Conestoga, Juniata, and Swatara. The language is considered extinct as of 1763, when the last remnant community was massacred at Lancaster, Pennsylvania. After 1763, some Conestoga remnant peoples joined nations of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy, and the Conestoga language survived for a time.
