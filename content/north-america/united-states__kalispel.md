## Overview

The Kalispel, also known as the Pend d’Oreilles, are Indigenous peoples of the Northwest Plateau. Today many live in Montana and eastern Washington in the United States. They referred to their primary tribal range as Kaniksu, a territory associated with the drainage systems of the Flathead River, Clark Fork, and Pend Oreille rivers. Their historic lands extended from roughly present-day Plains, Montana, westward along the Clark Fork River to Lake Pend Oreille and Priest Lake in Idaho, and along the Pend Oreille River into eastern Washington and British Columbia. The people are divided geographically and culturally into Upper Kalispel and Lower Kalispel groupings. Their language, Kalispel-Pend d’Oreille, is a Southern Interior Salish language and is also known as Flathead-Kalispel.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Tule mats** — The Pend d’Oreille made mats by weaving cattails, which were abundant where they lived. These mats were attached to a tree-branch frame to form a winter hut.

No additional pattern-bearing textile traditions or named motifs are covered by the sources used here.

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that Pend d’Oreille clothing was traditionally made from rabbit pelts and deer hides. These hides were embellished with dyes, paints, beads, and porcupine quills. The sources used do not distinguish everyday from ceremonial dress, identify separate men’s and women’s clothing, or describe head coverings, belts, footwear, or named garments.

### Architecture

In summer, the Pend d’Oreille lived in tipis, while in winter they lived in lodges made from large cattails. The cattails were woven into tule mats and attached to a tree-branch frame to form a hut. A large community building on the Kalispel reservation bears the name “Tule Hut” in reference to this traditional housing.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Pend d’Oreille made weapons and tools from flint, and shaped many other things with rocks.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The name Pend d’Oreille, meaning “hangs from ear,” was attributed to the people by French-speaking colonists and traders in reference to the large shell earrings they wore.

## Music & performance

The sources describe annual pow wows held by the Kalispel. Long after the Pend d’Oreille band of Kalispel had been dispossessed of hereditary lands around Lake Pend Oreille, it continued to gather for an annual pow wow on traditional grounds just east of what is now Sandpoint City Beach. The three-day event included ceremonies, dancing, and traditional stick games. The pow wows in Sandpoint were discontinued in the early 1950s.

Since 1975, the Kalispel Tribe has held an annual pow wow at its Usk, Washington, reservation in July or August. These events are open to the public and include a dance contest, traditional foods, stick games, and other activities.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is included in the annual Kalispel pow wow at Usk, Washington, where the event includes a dance contest. The earlier three-day pow wow held just east of what is now Sandpoint City Beach included ceremonies and dancing.

## Festivals & rituals

The Kalispel have held an annual pow wow since 1975 at their Usk, Washington, reservation in July or August. The event is open to the public and includes a dance contest, traditional foods, stick games, and other activities. An earlier annual pow wow at traditional grounds just east of what is now Sandpoint City Beach lasted three days and included ceremonies, dancing, and traditional stick games; these pow wows were discontinued in the early 1950s.

The sources also describe historical religious and communal events. Jesuits established a Roman Catholic mission in Kalispel territory in 1846.

## Foodways

Camas was a staple of the Kalispel diet. The name Kalispel is an anglicization of Ql̓ispé, meaning “Camas people,” referring to the flower bulb. The sources also state that the Kalispel traditionally traded bison hides for horses and other useful goods.

Traditional foods are included among the activities of the annual pow wow held by the Kalispel Tribe at its Usk, Washington, reservation.

## Oral tradition & literature

They identify *Kaniksu, Stories of the Northwest* by Thomas F. Lacy as a further-reading work and mention Pend d’Oreille tales by James A. Teit in Franz Boas’s *Folk-tales of Salishan and Sahaptin tribes*. The sources do not summarize the contents of these tales.

Language preservation is documented through teaching and educational materials. Salish Kootenai College offers Salish language courses and trains Salish language teachers at its Native American Language Teacher Training Institute. The organization Yoyoot Skʷkʷimlt, meaning “Strong Young People,” was teaching language classes in high schools as of May 2013. A dictionary, *Seliš nyoʔnuntn: Medicine for the Salish Language*, expanded from 186 to 816 pages in 2009, and children’s books and language CDs are also available.

## Language & religion

Kalispel-Pend d’Oreille is a Southern Interior Salish language also known as Flathead-Kalispel. The related Salish article identifies dialects spoken by the Spokane, Kalispel, Pend d’Oreilles, and Bitterroot Salish. Salish is polysynthetic and does not make a clear distinction between nouns and verbs. The sources state that Salish was spoken as of 2005 by about 64 elders of the Flathead Nation and the Kalispel Indian Reservation, and by another 50 elders as of 2000 at the Spokane Indian Reservation. As of 2012, UNESCO described Salish as “critically endangered” in Montana and Idaho.

The sources mention Roman Catholic mission activity beginning in 1846.
