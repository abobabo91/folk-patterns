## Overview

The Osage Nation is a federally recognized Native American tribe in Oklahoma and an Indigenous people of the Great Plains. Osage people call themselves *Wazhazhe*, meaning “Middle Waters,” in their Dhegihan Siouan language. Their ancestors lived in the Ohio and Mississippi river valleys before migrating west during the 17th century. By the early 19th century, the Osage controlled territory between the Missouri and Red rivers, the Ozarks, and the foothills of the Wichita Mountains. They depended on nomadic buffalo hunting and agriculture, while their villages participated in Great Plains trading networks. In 2025, the federally recognized Osage Nation had approximately 25,000 enrolled citizens, including 6,780 living in its jurisdictional area.

## Music & performance

Osage ceremonial songs preserved knowledge acquired by spiritual leaders in a society without a written language. These songs were taught and shared among Osages who were sincere and had demonstrated themselves to be trustworthy. The sources state that songs and ceremonies addressed many areas of life, including adoption, marriage, war, agriculture, and honoring the rising sun in the morning.

Osage ceremonies were highly ritualized and used bundles and ceremonial pipes containing tobacco offerings. Medicine people and spiritual leaders presided over them. The ceremonies sought Wakonda’s aid for continued tribal existence and for the blessing of a long life through children. The sources describe Wakonda, also called Wah'Kon-Tah or Wakonda, as a supreme life force and as the mysterious life-force associated with the sun, moon, earth, and stars.

## Dance & theatre

They state that Osage ceremonies could be elaborate and concerned adoption, marriage, war, agriculture, tribal existence, and long life.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe a ritual system organized around Wakonda, the life-force believed to exist within trees, plants, the sky, animals, and human beings. Osage cosmology divided life into sky and earth: life was created in the sky and descended to earth in material form, with the sky understood as masculine and the earth as feminine.

Clan organization reflected this cosmology. Children received ceremonial names introducing them to the community, and without such a name an Osage child could not participate in ceremonies. Marriage was regulated through the clans, whose members had to marry people from opposite clans or divisions. Clan representation also shaped the arrangement of villages.

Ceremonies involved bundles, ceremonial pipes, and tobacco offerings. They requested help from Wakonda and sought continued tribal existence and long life through children. The sources also describe ceremonial songs connected with adoption, marriage, war, agriculture, and the rising sun. At funerals, the faces of dead Osage were traditionally painted to indicate their tribe and clan.

The sources mention that the Osage participated in a mass partially conducted in Osage at St. Francis Xavier College Church of St. Louis University on April 2, 2014.

## Foodways

Osage subsistence combined nomadic buffalo hunting with agriculture. From traditional homes in the woodlands of present-day Missouri and Arkansas, Osage groups made semi-annual buffalo-hunting journeys into the Great Plains. They also hunted deer, rabbit, and other wild game. Near their villages, women cultivated varieties of corn, squash, and other vegetables, processing them for food. They harvested and processed nuts and wild berries as well.

The sources describe this economy as combining elements of Woodland Native American and Great Plains cultures. The Osage depended on both hunting and cultivation, while their villages functioned as hubs in a Great Plains trading network in which Kaw people served as intermediaries.

## Oral tradition & literature

Osage oral tradition included accounts of migration shared with other Dhegihan-Siouan peoples, including the Ponca, Omaha, Kaw, and Quapaw. According to these stories, the groups migrated west as a result of war with the Haudenosaunee and/or in search of more game.

Traditional spirituality was transmitted through ceremonial songs because the Osage had no written language. Spiritual leaders acquired knowledge through these songs, which were taught only to Osages considered sincere and proven. The sources state that many songs and ceremonies were created for adoption, marriage, war, agriculture, and honoring the rising sun.

## Language & religion

Osage belongs to the Dhegihan Siouan language family. The Osage name for themselves is *Wazhazhe*, “Middle Waters,” while *Wažaže Nikaši* means “People of the Middle Waters.” The sources also state that the term Osage is a French version, derived from *eau sage*, and can be roughly translated as “calm water.” The Osage called Europeans *I'n-Shta-Heh*, meaning “Heavy Eyebrows.”

Traditional Osage spirituality centers on Wah'Kon-Tah or Wakonda, a supreme life force understood as present throughout creation. Animals such as hawks, deer, and bears were respected for qualities including courage, while people were expected to learn from other forms of life. The sky and earth formed the two main divisions of existence, and clans were organized in a way that symbolically reflected this cosmos.

The sources also describe Catholic, Presbyterian, Dutch Reformed, Associate Reformed, and Jesuit missionary activity among the Osage. Catholic priests remained with the Osage during the 1837 Great Plains smallpox epidemic, creating what the Osage understood as a special covenant, although the sources state that they did not convert in great numbers.
