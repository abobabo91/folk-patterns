## Overview

The Akimel O'odham live in Arizona and northwestern Mexico. Their culture is closely tied to the Gila and Salt Rivers, irrigation, and farming. Basketry, woven cloth, thatched houses, oral tradition, and the way of life called himdagi are important themes. Their language is O'odham, with several dialects and writing systems.

## Material culture

Material culture includes intricate baskets, woven cloth, thatched houses, pottery, stone arrow-heads, game-sticks, gourds, and carrying containers.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Basketry and woven cloth are important crafts, with many baskets decorated by geometric black designs.

- **Basketry** — Akimel O'odham makers produced intricate baskets, platters, storage vessels, conical containers, and beaker-shaped baskets.
- **Basketry vessel** — Straight-sided coiled vessels were decorated with black geometric ornament.
- **Carrying-sling jar** — A museum jar has a sling made from basketry, gum, and cotton cloth.
- **Woven fabric cap** — A woven cap was worn by a Pima chief in studio portraits.
- **Woven cloth** — The Akimel O'odham produced woven cloth, but the sources give no further details.

### Clothing & dress

The sources give little information about everyday or ceremonial clothing.

- **Western-style suit** — Portraits show Pima chiefs wearing western-style suits.
- **Woven fabric cap** — One Pima chief wore a woven cap with plaits hanging from it.
- **Dreadlocks** — Another portrait shows a Pima chief with dreadlocks at the back of his head.

### Architecture

Traditional homes were thatched jacales, while villages also used shared central areas and seasonal field houses.

- **Jacal** (*jacales*) — These thatched wattle-and-daub huts used reed-grass mats over arched sticks and included a petate and gourds.
- **Central ramada and kitchen** (*ramada*) — Familial groups in earlier villages shared a central ramada and kitchen area.
- **Brush round house** (*Olas Ki:ki*) — Brush Olas Ki:ki were round houses built around the central area.
- **Ranchería village** (*ranchería*) — Earlier villages were loose groups of houses organized around shared spaces.
- **Field house** — People lived seasonally in temporary field houses while tending crops.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Everyday objects included pottery, baskets, stone arrow-heads, game-sticks, platters, and carrying vessels.

- **Stone arrow-head** — Arrow-heads were made from grey stone, red stone, and obsidian.
- **Game-stick** — Wooden game-sticks were made from four slats.
- **Pottery pot** — Museum records include pots made of pottery.
- **Basketry pot-like vessel** — The museum catalogue includes a basketry vessel shaped like a pot.
- **Platter** — Basketry platters are recorded among the everyday objects.

### Jewelry & body adornment

- **Cap plaits** — A portrait shows plaits hanging from a woven fabric cap, without explaining them as a cultural practice.
- **Dreadlocks** — A portrait shows dreadlocks at the back of the head, without explaining them as a cultural practice.

## Music & performance

Ceremonial speeches recite parts of a cosmic myth and are adapted for each occasion.

- **Ceremonial speech** — Speakers recite portions of the cosmic myth in set speeches during ceremonies.
- **Storyteller dialect song** — A song from O'odham Hoho'ok A'agida is identified as an example of the Storyteller dialect.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe ceremonies and rules about speaking personal and deceased people's names, but no annual festival calendar.

- **Cosmic-myth ceremony** — Ceremonies often include set speeches based on the cosmic myth.
- **Name silence for children** — From age ten until marriage, boys and girls did not say their own names aloud because this could bring bad luck.
- **Name silence for the dead** — People did not say deceased people's names aloud so the dead could move on and their spirits could return among the living.

## Foodways

The traditional economy combined farming, hunting, gathering, trading, and irrigation in an arid environment.

- **Irrigation system** — The people built extensive canals and changed them as community needs changed.
- **Farming** — Communities cultivated foods and used temporary field houses while tending crops.
- **Hunting and gathering** — Hunting and gathering were major parts of the ancient economy.
- **Trade** — Extensive trading was part of the ancient economy.
- **Introduced crops and livestock** — After European contact, wheat and livestock were introduced along with metal tools and trade goods.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral teaching, cosmic myths, songs, and named language works carry stories and moral instruction.

- **O'odham Legends and Lore** (*O'odham Hoho'ok A'agida*) — This work includes a song with an English translation.
- **Roadrunner** (*Taḏai*) — Taḏai is an excerpt presented in the Salt River dialect and Saxton orthography.
- **Cosmic myth** — Speakers recite portions of a cosmic myth during ceremonies.
- **Children's verbal instruction** — Children received careful verbal teaching about moral, religious, and other matters.

## Language & religion

O'odham is a Uto-Aztecan language, and himdagi is a river-centered way of life covering religion, morals, values, and worldview.

- **O'odham language** (*O'odham*) — O'odham is spoken in southern Arizona and northern Sonora, Mexico.
- **O'odham language names** (*O'odham ha-ñe'okĭ*) — The language also has names including O'ottham ha-neoki, O'odham ñiok, O'odham ñe'okĭ, O'odham ñi'okĭ, and O'otham ñiok.
- **Akimel O'odham dialect** — Akimel O'odham is one of the language's dialect groupings.
- **Alvarez–Hale orthography** — Alvarez–Hale is one of the common ways to write the language.
- **Saxton orthography** — Saxton is another common writing system for the language.
