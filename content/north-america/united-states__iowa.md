## Overview

The Iowa are a Native American people whose ancestral movements linked the Great Lakes, Iowa and the Great Plains. They historically spoke Chiwere and are now enrolled in Iowa tribes in Oklahoma or Kansas and Nebraska. Their material culture included lodges, tipis, hide clothing, moccasins, quillwork and bead decoration. Language recordings, workshops and songs remain important to cultural preservation.

## Material culture

Iowa material culture used hides, cloth, wool, quills, beads, bark and earth for clothing, adornment, buildings and everyday objects.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources document hide clothing, quillwork, bead decoration and cloth garments, but no named Iowa textile pattern tradition.

- **Hide clothing** — Bison and deer hides were used for clothing, and moccasin hides were made supple with a mixture containing bone marrow, ground entrails and cactus juice.
- **Quillwork** — Iowa chiefs wore leggings decorated with quillwork, but the sources do not describe its methods or meanings.
- **Beaded clothing** — Portraits show beaded leg bands, beaded leggings and decorated moccasins.
- **Wool blankets and cloth garments** — Portraits show chiefs wearing wool blankets, cloth shirts, cloth headdresses, tunics and wool leggings.
- **Moccasins** — A museum catalogue lists Iowa moccasins dated 1870.

### Clothing & dress

Iowa dress combined hide clothing with cloth, wool, feathers, beadwork and quillwork.

- **Hide garments** — Bison and deer hides were used for clothing.
- **Decorated moccasins** — Museum records describe moccasins with bead decoration.
- **Chiefs' dress** — Portraits show chiefs wearing headdresses, feathers, chokers, gorgets, ear ornaments, necklaces, peace medals, wrist decorations and blankets.
- **Shaved and decorated heads** — Iowa men traditionally shaved their heads and decorated them with deer hide.
- **Deer Ham's dress** (*Ta réke*) — Deer Ham is shown wearing combinations of cloth or fur clothing and holding a walking stick or tomahawk.

### Architecture

Iowa buildings included bark lodges, chakiruthan, tipis and earth lodges.

- **Bark lodge** — Bark lodges were among the historic Iowa house types.
- **Chakiruthan** — Chakiruthan was one of the historic Iowa house types.
- **Tipi** — Portable tipis were used during hunting or warfare.
- **Earth lodge** — Earth lodges were oven-shaped buildings covered with earth, with a central hearth, smoke hole and orientation to a cardinal direction.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources mention alum pipes and many personal or practical objects, but do not describe Iowa ceramics or craft techniques.

- **Alum pipes** — Iowa made alum pipes that were traded with furs to French colonizers.
- **Walking sticks** — Walking sticks appear in museum portraits of Iowa chiefs.
- **Weapons** — Portraits show rifles, bows and arrows, and tomahawks.
- **Peace medals** — Peace medals appear among the objects worn by chiefs.
- **Gorgets and necklaces** — Gorgets and necklaces appear in portraits of Iowa chiefs.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Chiefs' adornment included neck, ear, hair and wrist ornaments, feathers and several kinds of headdress.

- **Chokers** — Chokers appear in portraits of Iowa chiefs.
- **Gorgets** — Gorgets were worn by chiefs in museum portraits.
- **Ear ornaments** — Ear ornaments appear in portraits of Iowa chiefs.
- **Headdresses** — Headdresses included cloth forms, a decorated fur cap, a roach and wound cloth arrangements.
- **Hair and feather adornment** — Hair ornaments and feathers were part of chiefs' dress, while men also decorated shaved heads with deer hide.

## Music & performance

The sources document a recorded Iowa courtship song and efforts to preserve Chiwere words and songs.

- **Courtship song** — George Miller performed an Iowa courtship song in 1897, collected by Alice Cunningham Fletcher and Francis La Flesche.
- **Chiwere recordings** — Tribal elders used recording devices to collect Chiwere words and songs.
- **Digital audio archive** — A 2012 NSF grant provided digital access to existing recordings of fluent speakers.

## Dance & theatre

The supplied sources do not document named Iowa dances, theatre forms or dramatic traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources give no Iowa festival calendar or detailed life-cycle rites, but mention battle feats valued by Iowa men.

- **Battle feats** — The sources state that Iowa men traditionally valued three feats during a battle, without explaining them further.

## Foodways

Iowa foodways combined maize farming with hunting, especially the use of bison for food and materials.

- **Maize farming** — The Iowa planted maize and adopted an agricultural lifestyle similar to tribes of the Eastern woodlands.
- **Bison hunting** — Immense bison herds were important to Plains life, supplying food and many materials.
- **Bison hides** — Bison hides were used for tents, clothing, storage bags, horse gear, shields, knife sheaths and cases.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources do not record Iowa folktales or named storytelling genres, but they describe active Chiwere preservation.

- **Language workshops** — The Iowa Tribe of Oklahoma sponsored Chiwere language workshops.
- **Elder recordings** — Tribal elders made recordings to preserve Chiwere words and songs.
- **University language program** — The Otoe–Missouria Tribe established a language program with the University of Oklahoma Native American Studies Department.
- **Recorded courtship song** — A courtship song performed by George Miller in 1897 is identified in the Wikipedia source's external links.

## Language & religion

Chiwere is the Iowa language, but it declined sharply after European American contact and the sources do not describe Iowa religion.

- **Chiwere** (*Báxoje ich'é*) — Chiwere was historically spoken by the Missouria, Otoe and Iowa peoples, and the Iowa name for it is Báxoje ich'é.
- **Iowa–Otoe–Missouria** (*Báxoje-Jíwere-Nyútʼach*) — This is another name for the Chiwere language.
- **Otoe–Missouria dialect** (*Jíwere ich'é*) — Jíwere ich'é is the name for the Otoe–Missouria dialect.
- **Language relationship** — Chiwere is closely related to Ho-Chunk.
- **Language decline** — The language had almost totally ceased to be spoken by 1940, and the last two fluent speakers died in the winter of 1996.

## Glossary

- *Báxoje* — Iowa name for the people and language
- *Báxoje ich'é* — Iowa name for the language
- *Báxoje-Jíwere-Nyútʼach* — Another name for Chiwere
- *Jíwere ich'é* — Otoe–Missouria dialect name
- *Chiwere* — Siouan language of the Iowa, Otoe and Missouria
- *chakiruthan* — Historic Iowa house type
- *tipi* — Portable house used during hunting or warfare
- *earth lodge* — Earth-covered oven-shaped house
- *moccasin* — Hide shoe
- *choker* — Neck ornament
- *gorget* — Ornament worn by chiefs
- *roach* — Type of headdress
- *tomahawk* — Handheld weapon shown in portraits
- *peace medal* — Medal worn by chiefs
- *courtship song* — Song performed by George Miller in 1897
