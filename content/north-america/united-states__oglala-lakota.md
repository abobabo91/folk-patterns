## Overview

The Oglala are one of the seven subtribes of the Lakota people, who together with the Dakota make up the Očhéthi Šakówiŋ, or Seven Council Fires. A majority of the Oglala live on the Pine Ridge Indian Reservation in South Dakota, the eighth-largest Native American reservation in the United States. The Oglala are federally recognized as the Oglala Sioux Tribe of the Pine Ridge Reservation, South Dakota. Their history includes the fur trade, the Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868, the establishment of the Pine Ridge Reservation in 1878, and continued political and social changes. In 1890, the reservation included 5,010 people in approximately 30 communities.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Skinworking and clothing production** — Oglala women historically worked skins to make clothing and footwear, as well as storage bags, tipi coverings, and other household objects.

**Tipi coverings** — Women historically made the coverings of *tipis*.

**Beadwork** — Imogene Goodshot Arquero is identified as a beadwork artist.

**Ledger art** — Kicking Bear is identified as a ledger artist.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Oglala women historically made clothing and footwear by working skins. They also produced storage bags and other objects for family and tribal use. The sources state that women prepared game and fish, worked skins, and made clothing, footwear, and tipi coverings.

### Architecture

The sources mention tipis as a form of dwelling whose coverings were historically made by women. The Oglala flag depicts eight white tepees, representing the eight districts of the Pine Ridge Reservation: Porcupine, Wakpammi, Medicine Root, Pass Creek, Eagle Nest, White Clay, LaCreek, and Wounded Knee.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They state that women historically made storage bags, tipi coverings, clothing, footwear, and other objects, and that women controlled food, resources, movable property, and the family’s home.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 They identify beadwork as the field of the artist Imogene Goodshot Arquero but give no further catalogue of adornment types or materials.

## Music & performance

The sources identify the Sacred Pipe as an important element associated with the coming of the White Buffalo Calf Woman. According to the account cited, this revered spirit woman brought the Sacred Pipe to the tribe and taught the Lakota people lessons concerning family and community.

The Oglala flag was first displayed at Sun Dance ceremonies in 1961 and officially adopted on 9 March 1962. The flag is also described as a common sight at Native American powwows and as often flown as a generic Native American flag.

The sources also identify Albert Afraid of Hawk as a member of Buffalo Bill’s Wild West Show, who died and was buried in Danbury, Connecticut, while on tour in 1900. His remains were exhumed and re-interred on the Pine Ridge Reservation in 2012.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention Sun Dance ceremonies and Native American powwows. They also identify Albert Afraid of Hawk as a performer in Buffalo Bill’s Wild West Show.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources name Sun Dance ceremonies and describe the Sacred Pipe as brought to the Lakota by the White Buffalo Calf Woman. The account connects the Sacred Pipe with teachings about family, *tiwahe*, and community, *tiyospaye*.

The Oglala flag was first displayed at Sun Dance ceremonies in 1961 and officially adopted on 9 March 1962.

## Foodways

The sources state that Oglala women historically prepared food, game, and fish and controlled food and other resources within the family. They also describe the effects of the Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868, after which some Lakota bands turned to Indian agencies for rations of beef and subsistence foods from the United States government, while other bands maintained indigenous lifeways.

The sources also state that, during the early nineteenth-century fur trade, Europeans and Americans sought furs, first especially beaver fur and later bison fur. The fur trade changed the Oglala economy and way of life.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oglala elders relate stories about the origin of the name “Oglala” and the emergence of the Oglala as a distinct group, probably sometime in the eighteenth century. A respected Oglala elder, Left Heron, explained that before the coming of the White Buffalo Calf Woman, the people ran around the prairie like wild animals and did not understand the central importance of community.

The White Buffalo Calf Woman is associated in the sources with bringing the Sacred Pipe and teaching the Lakota people about family and community. The sources also identify John Fire Lame Deer as a storyteller and Medicine Man, Albert White Hat as a storyteller, and Amos Bad Heart Bull as an artist. No specific epic, proverb, riddle, or contemporary literary revival is described.

## Language & religion

The sources identify the Oglala as one of the Lakota subtribes and mention Lakota pronunciation for the name Oglala.

The sources describe the White Buffalo Calf Woman as a revered spirit woman who brought the Sacred Pipe and taught the Lakota people about family and community. They also describe a temporary suspension of Christian missions on the Pine Ridge Reservation in July 2022. The Oglala Sioux Tribal Council called for an investigation into the financial practices of the Dream Center Missionary, and the Jesus is King Mission was ejected from the reservation for spreading pamphlets regarded by the tribe as hateful.
