## Overview

The Odawa — also written Ottawa or *Odaawaa*, and syncoped in their own speech to *Daawaa* — are an Indigenous people of the Eastern Woodlands, living today in southeastern Canada and the northeastern United States across a border that was drawn across their territory only in the 18th and 19th centuries. Their remembered homeland is Manitoulin Island, on the northern side of Lake Huron, and the Bruce Peninsula in Ontario; after the 17th century Odawa communities also formed along the Ottawa River, in Michigan's Lower Peninsula, in Wisconsin, and in northwest Ohio along the Maumee, Auglaize and Blanchard rivers. About 15,000 Odawa live in Ontario, Michigan and Oklahoma. They are one of the Anishinaabeg, distinct from but closely related to the Ojibwe and Potawatomi, with whom they formed the Council of Three Fires, and they speak a divergent dialect of Ojibwe within the Algonquian family. In folk-culture terms their defining role is commercial: their name derives from *adaawe*, "to trade", and neighbours knew them as the traders who moved cornmeal, sunflower oil, furs and skins, rugs and mats, tobacco, and medicinal roots and herbs between nations.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The objects this atlas shows are dominated by two techniques — glass beadwork on skin and cloth, and porcupine quillwork on birchbark — together with plaited mats.

**Floral glass beadwork** — Beadwork in floral arrangements worked on tanned deer skin, as on a pouch finished with tassels, and on the cloth garments worn in the Harbor Springs photographs: a beaded vest, beaded leggings and beaded moccasins.

**Beaded skin pouches** — A chief's pouch keeps the whole beaver skin with head, legs and tail intact; the tail is embroidered on one side with white beads laid on blue serge, and the bag is lined with pink patterned white calico. It is recorded as having belonged to the "Heathen Chief of the Spanish River Indians".

**Birchbark-and-sweetgrass quillwork** — Mats, baskets and a wall pocket built from birchbark with coiled sweetgrass edging and decorated with porcupine quillwork. The sequence of near-identical mats, baskets and wall pockets in the collection is catalogued as souvenir ware, that is, work made for sale rather than for the household.

**Rush matting** — A mat woven of rush, the plainest of the recorded floor and wall coverings, and consistent with the "rugs and mats" named among Odawa trade goods.

**Black ash basketry and birchbark biting** — Continued as living practices; Kelly Church (born 1967) is recorded as a black ash basket weaver and birch bark biter.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name only one pattern class by name: *floral*, on beadwork. Champlain's 1615 account adds, of bodies rather than cloth, tattooing "in many fashions and designs".

### Clothing & dress

The earliest description is Samuel de Champlain's, who in 1615 met 300 men near the mouth of the French River whom the French called *cheveux relevés* — "hair lifted, raised, rolled up". He recorded that they wore no breech clouts, that their bodies were tattooed in many fashions and designs, their faces painted and their noses pierced, and that their equipment was a bow and arrows, a buckler of boiled leather and a club. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century photographs in the collection show two very different registers of dress. For display and ceremony, an Odawa man at Harbor Springs, Michigan, poses in a feather headdress with a beaded and decorated tunic; in another Harbor Springs postcard, Frank Leo wears a feather headdress, face paint, a patterned cloth shirt, a beaded vest, a loin covering, beaded leggings and beaded moccasins, while the woman beside him wears a headband, several necklaces, a fringed dress and shoes. For dealings with the United States government, a seated studio portrait of the Ottawa chief John Wilson (Partee), made in Washington, D.C., shows him in shirt, vest, suit, tie and pocket watch. Cloth and blankets were among the goods the Odawa took in exchange for furs.

### Architecture

The sources record one built feature of the homeland: the Saugeen complex people of the Bruce Peninsula, a Hopewell-influenced Middle Woodland group that may have developed into the Odawa, raised earthwork mounds for burials, a practice that ended about 250 CE; the Saugeen mounds have not been excavated.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Bark and wood dominate. A model canoe is made of birch bark, wood and gum, and belongs to a set with five paddles — the canoe being also the technology the Odawa held over inland partners who lacked it. Birchbark served equally for containers: baskets, mats and a wall pocket, all coiled with sweetgrass. Rush was woven into mats. Metal and manufactured goods entered the household through the fur trade rather than local workshops: hatchets, knives, kettles, traps, needles, fish hooks, and later firearms. The asymmetry of that trade is recorded bluntly — the Cree are said to have given the Odawa "all their beaver robes for old knives, blunted awls, wretched nets and kettles used until they were past service". Birchbark also carried writing, in the *Wiigwaasabak* scrolls that preserved the migration history.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Body ornament is documented mainly in early contact description and in later photographs. Champlain recorded tattooing in many designs, painted faces, pierced noses, and the raised, rolled hair that gave the people their French name. The twentieth-century photographs show a headband and multiple necklaces on an Odawa woman, and face paint on a man in a feather headdress. "Jewelry and decorative items" are listed among the European goods received for furs, and white glass beads are the medium of the embroidery on the beaver-skin chief's pouch. The sources name no individual ornament types.

## Festivals & rituals

Odawa religion is described as the traditional Native American religion of the Odawa, practised in communities in both Canada and the United States, with no formal leadership or organizational structure and much internal variation; it is particularly close to that of the Ojibwe, and like other Native American religions has adapted to environmental change and contact with other communities. The sources record one body of sacred narrative in detail rather than a festival calendar: directed by the *miigis* (luminescent) beings, the Anishinaabe peoples moved inland along the Saint Lawrence River, and at the "Third Stopping Place" near the present Straits of Mackinac the southern Anishinaabeg divided into Ojibwe, Odawa and Potawatomi. Burial under earthwork mounds is attested archaeologically for the Saugeen complex. Christian practice arrived unevenly: Catholic missionaries, among them Frederic Baraga, worked in Michigan, Manitoulin Island converts divided between Catholic, Methodist and Anglican affiliation, and Enmegahbowh (c. 1807–1902) became the first Native American ordained an Episcopal priest.

## Foodways

What the sources document is the food economy. Cornmeal and sunflower oil head the list of goods the Odawa traded, alongside tobacco and medicinal roots and herbs, which indicates both horticulture and the processing of seed oil. Hunting supplied the trade in beaver — the most highly prized fur in Europe — and in deer, marten, raccoon, fox, otter and muskrat. Among the European goods taken in exchange, kettles, traps, needles and fish hooks all bear on getting and cooking food, as does the alcohol that entered later. One episode of ritual cannibalism is recorded in the sources, at the 1752 raid on Pickawillany, where the Miami chief Memeskia was killed, boiled and eaten in front of his warriors by the allied Odawa, Potawatomi and Ojibwe war party assembled by Charles Langlade.

## Oral tradition & literature

Anishinaabeg tradition, recorded in *Wiigwaasabak* birch bark scrolls, holds that the Odawa came from the eastern side of North America — Turtle Island — and from a region called Dawnland on the East Coast. Odawa literacy in their own language began on Manitoulin Island in the 19th century, where speakers wrote official letters and petitions, personal documents, band regulations, a proclamation and census statements between 1823 and 1910, and contributed articles to *Anishinabe Enamiad* ("the Praying Indian"), an Ojibwe newspaper published at Harbor Springs, Michigan, from 1896 to 1902. Andrew Blackbird (c. 1814/1817–1908) wrote a history of his people in English with an appended grammar, vocabulary lists and translations of the Ten Commandments and the Lord's Prayer. The major modern collection comes from Jane Willetts Ettawageshik, who spent about two years recording stories in the Grand Traverse Band of Ottawa and Chippewa Indians community; Howard Webkamigada translated them into English as *Ottawa Stories from the Springs*. Basil Johnston is noted as a prominent Odawa author.

## Language & religion

Odawa is a dialect of Ojibwe within the Algonquian family, known to its speakers as *Nishnaabemwin*, "speaking the native language", or *Daawaamwin*, "speaking Ottawa". It is among the most changed of the Ojibwe dialects, above all through a pervasive vowel syncope that deletes short vowels and creates consonant clusters found nowhere else in Ojibwe; the third-person prefix /o-/ has been eliminated entirely, so that *jiimaan* means both "canoe" and "his/her canoe". Two subdialects, "Ottawa Ottawa" and "Chippewa Ottawa", reflect 19th-century in-migration by Southwestern Ojibwe and Potawatomi speakers who shifted to Odawa. Of roughly 5,000 ethnic Odawa and 10,000 more with Odawa ancestry, about 500 people in Ontario and Michigan speak the language, and the Ottawa Tribe of Oklahoma has three fluent speakers; revitalization runs through second-language teaching in primary and secondary schools. There is no official orthography, but the Double Vowel system, attributed to Charles Fiero, is used in the 1985 dictionary and 2001 reference grammar. Religious life combines traditional Odawa practice, closely allied to Ojibwe practice, with the Catholic, Methodist and Anglican affiliations taken up from the 19th century.
