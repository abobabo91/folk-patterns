## Overview

The Muscogee, also called Mvskoke, Mvskokvlke, Muscogee Creek, or Creek, are related Indigenous peoples of the Southeastern Woodlands in the United States. Their historical homelands included southern Tennessee, much of Alabama, western Georgia, and parts of northern Florida. Most were forcibly removed to Indian Territory, now Oklahoma, during the 1830s. Muscogee communities are also present in Alabama, Florida, and Oklahoma. Their languages belong to the Eastern Muskogean branch of the Muskogean language family. Muscogee society developed autonomous towns, confederated town networks, agriculture, craft specialization, hunting, trade, ceremonial grounds, and ballparks. Their history also includes Mississippian culture, moundbuilding sites, oral traditions, complex political organization, and continuing language programs.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Chief’s dress** — The British Museum catalogue records a complete dress of a Muscogie chief, including a hide jacket with fringe, a cloth belt, a chief’s bag made of worsted cloth and beads, fringed worsted-cloth garters with beads, and skin moccasins sewn with silk-thread decorations.

**Beaded accessories** — The catalogue identifies beads on the chief’s bag and garters, while the belt is made of worsted cloth. The available records do not describe particular patterns, motifs, or regional distinctions.

**Silk-thread moccasin decoration** — The recorded pair of chief’s moccasins is sewn of skin and decorated with silk thread.

**Fringe** — Fringe appears on the recorded hide jacket and garters.

**Cloth and hide clothing materials** — The museum records distinguish hide, skin, worsted cloth, beads, and silk thread in the chief’s dress.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no specific textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The museum catalogue records a chief’s dress consisting of a hide jacket with fringe, a worsted-cloth belt, a chief’s bag with a tassel, fringed garters made of worsted cloth and beads, and skin moccasins decorated with silk thread. A British Museum drawing depicts Hilis Hadjo, or Josiah Francis, wearing red head-gear, a military red coat with epaulettes, a waist belt with tassels, and shoes with spurs; he is also shown with a pistol and a long thin pipe. Other catalogue records depict Creek men in shirts, trousers, jackets, waistcoats, bow ties, watch chains, and suits.

### Architecture

The sources describe Muscogee towns, plazas, ballparks, and square ceremonial dance grounds, as well as complex cities with earthwork mounds and surrounding networks of satellite towns and farmsteads.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources mention pottery in connection with the Woodland period, when locals developed pottery and small-scale horticulture in the Eastern Agricultural Complex. They also record trade in firearms, gunpowder, axes, glass beads, cloth, rum, and European goods, and identify craft specialization among precontact Muscogee polities. The Metropolitan Museum of Art catalogue records a nineteenth-century drum made of wood, skin, and various materials.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The museum records mention beads on a chief’s bag and garters, silk-thread decoration on moccasins, and tassels on a bag and belt.

## Music & performance

Muscogee town life included square ceremonial dance grounds and ballparks. The sources also describe the *heles hayv*, or medicine maker, officiating at rituals and providing black drink for purification ceremonies. The *tustunnuggee* was a ranking warrior and principal military adviser, while micos and other leaders organized village life. Muscogee creation stories and legends include spoken narratives concerning human emergence, the Maker of Breath, the Sun, the Moon, Thunder, the Tie Snake, the Horned Serpent, and Corn Woman.

The Metropolitan Museum of Art catalogue records a nineteenth-century drum made of wood, skin, and various materials.

## Dance & theatre

Muscogee confederated town networks were organized around plazas, ballparks, and square ceremonial dance grounds. The sources identify ball-play as an important activity: in 1790, the Muscogee and Choctaw settled a land dispute by playing a game involving nearly 10,000 players and bystanders.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe ceremonial dance grounds and several ritual practices. The *heles hayv*, or medicine maker, officiated at rituals, including the provision of black drink for purification ceremonies. Muscogee cosmology divided the universe into the Upper World, Middle World, and Lower World, with the Middle World understood as a balance between order and chaos.

Muscogee mythology includes creation stories in which humans emerge from beneath the earth in the West and journey eastward. Legends of the Tie Snake and Horned Serpent concern prohibited food, transformation, and the maintenance of cosmic balance. Stories of Corn Woman describe corn coming from her body and her sacrifice allowing corn crops to grow.

## Foodways

The sources identify corn, or maize, as central to the development of Mississippian agriculture, agricultural surpluses, and population growth. They also describe Muscogee farming, hunting, deer-hide exchange, and the use of black drink in purification ceremonies. Corn Woman legends connect corn with sacrifice, fertility, and the growth of crops.

## Oral tradition & literature

Muscogee oral history describes a migration from places west of the Mississippi River, followed by settlement on the east bank of the Ocmulgee River. Creation stories from Kasihta, Kasihta-Chickasaw-Coweta, and Coweta origins describe humans emerging from beneath the earth in the West and travelling eastward. These stories refer to Hesaketvmesē, meaning “Maker of Breath” or “the One Above who holds life’s breath,” also called Epohfvnkv, “the Creator” or “the One Above us.”

Muscogee legends include the Tie Snake, the Horned Serpent, and Corn Woman. The sources also describe the Muscogee Nation’s language classes and immersion camps, including programs offered by the College of the Muscogee Nation and other educational institutions.

## Language & religion

Muscogee, also called Muskogee or Mvskoke, belongs to the Muskogean language family and the Eastern Muskogean branch. Related languages and varieties include Hitchiti-Mikasuki, Koasati, and Alabama. The three main dialects identified in the sources are Muscogee proper, Oklahoma Seminole Muscogee, and Florida Seminole Muscogee. Muscogee uses a traditional alphabet adopted by interpreters and chiefs as the National Alphabet in 1853.

Muscogee grammar includes synthetic verbs, person markers, tense, aspect, mood, tone, nasalization, and subject–object–verb order. Muscogee mythology describes the Upper, Middle, and Lower Worlds and figures including the Maker of Breath, the Sun, the Moon, Thunder, the Tie Snake, and the Horned Serpent.
