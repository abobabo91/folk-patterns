## Overview

The sources assembled for this entry are museum catalogue records alone — no encyclopaedic article and no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription accompany them — so they describe what Mi'kmaw makers produced rather than who the Mi'kmaw are as a people, where they live, or how many they number. What the records do establish is a nineteenth-century material world centred on birchbark, porcupine quill, spruce root and ash splint, held today by the British Museum and the Metropolitan Museum of Art, with catalogue entries dated between 1840 and about 1860. One record, a black-and-white photographic postcard, shows a Mi'kmaw family of two men, a woman and three children seated outdoors or in a studio in western-style clothing, tentatively located in Massachusetts, USA. Everything below is limited to those records; the older catalogue spelling "Micmac" appears in several of them.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The catalogue records document no woven or embroidered textile tradition. Cloth appears only as a component of other objects — velvet and cotton on a pair of moccasins, and silk, cotton and wool on a set of dolls — so the pattern-bearing traditions the records actually attest are in bark, quill and splint rather than in textile.

**Etched birchbark** — Bark containers whose surfaces carry incised decoration. One circular box and lid has sides of birchbark with a wooden lid and base, possibly cedar, its seam stitched with plant fibre and reinforced with wooden pegs and unevenly spaced metal nails; the sides bear repeating geometric designs resembling an eight-pointed asterisk and the lid a vertical zig-zag. A second etched container, the *mocock*, is wide and rectangular at the base, narrowing to a small circular opening at the top, with etched linear design.

**Quillwork on bark** — Porcupine quill worked onto birchbark grounds over wooden structure, recorded on a lidded trunk of 1840–50 (birchbark, wood, spruce root, quill), a mid-nineteenth-century chair seat, a dollhouse chair of about 1860, and a birchbark canoe model of about 1860.

**Coiled ash splint basketry** — A lidded basket made from coiled ash splints, decorated with bands of birchbark and sweetgrass.

**Glass beadwork** — Beaded decoration on leather and cloth, recorded on a pair of moccasins whose designs include crowns.

**Motif vocabulary.** Eight-pointed asterisk (repeating geometric band on box sides), vertical zig-zag (lid decoration), etched linear design (on the *mocock*), crowns (beaded on moccasins), and bands of birchbark and sweetgrass alternating on coiled splint basketry.

### Clothing & dress

The records name one garment: a pair of moccasins of leather, velvet, cotton and glass beadwork, the beaded designs including crowns. Beyond this, the only dress evidence is photographic — the postcard shows two men, a woman and three children all wearing western-style clothes. The sources describe no head covering, belt, ceremonial outfit, or distinction between men's and women's dress.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

No ceramics and no metalwork appear as Mi'kmaw products in these records; metal occurs only as repair or reinforcement, in the nails spaced unevenly around the circumference of a birchbark box. The containers are the substance of this group. The *mocock* is a birchbark box whose sides are joined by inverted Y seams stitched with plant fibre cord, possibly spruce root, the same cord wrapped around the rim of the opening; fragments at opposite sides of the top opening indicate a lost plant fibre carrying strap, and extensive interior discolouring records historic use, the cataloguer suggesting maple syrup. The circular etched box combines birchbark sides with a wooden lid and base fixed by wooden pegs. Alongside the containers stand furniture and models: a lidded trunk, a chair seat, a dollhouse chair, and a canoe model accompanied by dolls of silk, cotton, wool, glass and resin.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used do not cover jewelry, tattooing or hair practice; the only adornment they record is the glass beadwork applied to the moccasins.

## Foodways

The sources used carry one indirect trace of foodways: the heavy interior staining of the birchbark *mocock*, which the British Museum cataloguer attributes tentatively to maple syrup. No dish, staple, cooking method or ceremonial food is named.

## Language & religion

The only linguistic evidence they contain is a single object term, *mocock*, used by the British Museum for a birchbark container, and the older catalogue ethnonym "Micmac" applied across several records.
