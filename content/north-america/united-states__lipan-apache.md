## Overview

The Lipan Apache are an eastern band of Apache, a Southern Athabaskan Indigenous people who historically lived across the Southern Great Plains, Texas, the Gulf of Mexico region, New Mexico, Colorado, Oklahoma, Coahuila, and northern Mexico. Their descendants live primarily in Texas, New Mexico, Oklahoma, Arizona, and northern Mexico, including communities in Coahuila and Chihuahua. The Lipan Apache are not a federally recognized tribe, although descendants are enrolled in the Mescalero Apache Tribe, the Tonkawa Tribe of Indians of Oklahoma, and the Apache Tribe of Oklahoma. Their language, Lipan or *ndé miizaa*, belongs to the Eastern Southern Athabaskan branch of the Na-Dene language family and is closely related to Jicarilla Apache. Lipan cultural history includes mobile lifeways, horse use, agriculture, peyote ceremony, pictographs, oral narratives, and continuing language-revitalization efforts.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state that Lipan Apache adopted a nomadic lifestyle after obtaining horses from the Spanish by 1608, and that some Lipan Apache near the Gulf Coast adopted lifeways of the neighboring Karankawa in the 1860s.

### Architecture

They state that Lipan Apache lived in agricultural settlements by 1700 and that Spanish colonists built forts and missions near Lipan settlements, including a mission on the San Sabá River completed in 1757.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources do record agricultural settlements in which Lipan Apache farmed pumpkins, corn, beans, and watermelons, and they describe horses as important after their adoption from the Spanish by 1608.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The Spanish name *Pelones* is explained in the sources as probably referring to a Lipan custom of plucking facial hair.

## Music & performance

They do document sacred and narrative expressive traditions. Pictographs at Paint Rock, Texas, depict Lipan stories of emergence, sacred ceremonies, monsters, and mythic heroes. The sources also describe the peyote ceremony as a tradition taught to the Tonkawa by Jumano and Lipan, and subsequently shared with the Comanches, Mescalero Apaches, and Plains Apaches.

## Dance & theatre

They mention sacred ceremonies in the pictographs at Paint Rock, Texas.

## Festivals & rituals

They document the peyote ceremony as a significant religious practice associated with Lipan Apache history. Jumano and Lipan taught the ceremony to the Tonkawa, who in turn shared it with the Comanches, Mescalero Apaches, and Plains Apaches. The sources also state that pictographs at Paint Rock, Texas, depict sacred ceremonies, stories of emergence, monsters, and mythic heroes. No dates or detailed ritual sequences are provided for these ceremonies.

## Foodways

The sources used document agriculture. By 1700, Lipan Apache agricultural settlements produced pumpkins, corn, beans, and watermelons, the latter described as introduced from Africa. The sources also state that Lipan Apache hunted buffalo: the Uplander band migrated to the upper Nueces River in Texas to hunt buffalo. No named Lipan dishes, preparation methods, or food-related ceremonies are recorded.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used describe Lipan Apache stories and mythic subjects. Pictographs in Paint Rock, Texas, depict stories of emergence, sacred ceremonies, monsters, and mythic heroes. The Lipan Apache language article records the phrase *Ndé Bikéyaa*, meaning “Ndé land” in Lipan, and states that Lipan people preserve their own toponymic names for important places within their history and culture. The sources also list *Myths and legends of the Lipan Apache* by Morris E. Opler and a work on the history and customs of the Lipan as told by Augustina Zuazua. Contemporary preservation efforts include language revitalization, a community-based language planning council in Mexico, and an official alphabet validated in 2023.

## Language & religion

Lipan, also called *ndé miizaa*, is an Eastern Southern Athabaskan language in the Na-Dene family and is closely related to Jicarilla Apache. It has been spoken in Coahuila and Chihuahua, in parts of southern Texas, and on the Mescalero Reservation in New Mexico. In 1981, only two or three elderly speakers were reported in New Mexico; in 2023, the Lipan community in Mexico estimated one hundred and ten speakers there. Lipan has 30 consonants and 16 vowels, with high, low, falling, and rising tones. The sources associate Lipan Apache religious culture with the peyote ceremony and sacred ceremonies represented in Paint Rock pictographs.
