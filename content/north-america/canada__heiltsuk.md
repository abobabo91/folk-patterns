## Overview

The Heiltsuk are carvers, painters and ceremonial specialists of the BC central coast, renowned for their artistic expertise and the quality of their bentwood work. Their ancestors inhabited the region by at least 7190 BCE. A devastating smallpox epidemic in 1862 killed about 72% of the population, but the community has since recovered and revived its cultural practices, especially since the 1951 lifting of the potlatch ban. Their language belongs to the Northern Wakashan family and is spoken at Bella Bella and Klemtu.

## Material culture

Heiltsuk material culture centres on carved and painted wood, produced by skilled artisans renowned for bentwood boxes, chests, canoes and ceremonial objects.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Heiltsuk artists worked primarily in painted and carved wood rather than cloth, using the formline design tradition of the Northwest Coast.

- **Formline** — The two-dimensional design style also called Northwest Coast art, used for painting killer whale heads, faces and other motifs in black, green and red.
- **Bentwood boxes and chests** — Highly skilled artisans constructed and decorated bentwood boxes and chests, alongside canoes and horn spoons, which were famous well beyond their territory.
- **Cedar-bark rings** — Circular worked cedar bark head rings and headdresses worn by ceremonial figures and dancers as markers of rank and sacred status.

### Clothing & dress

Ceremonial dress featured carved frontlets, cedar-bark head rings and skull headdresses, with high-ranking women wearing the distinctive lip ornament.

- **Carved frontlet** — A head decoration worn as part of a ceremonial outfit, executed in Northwest Coast style and drawing on eagle, raven and hummingbird imagery.
- *Labret* — An elliptical lip ornament carved in wood, bone or horn and worn by high-ranking women; larger ornaments marked increasing rank.
- **Skull headdress** — Worn by the officiating medicine man in the Hamatsa ceremony, representing the death and rebirth of initiates.

### Architecture

Heiltsuk settlement centred on large permanent winter villages with traditional Northwest Coast houses featuring smoke holes and painted decoration.

- **Winter village house** — A traditional Northwest Coast house with a smoke hole, painted red, black and blue, with a large carved raven head at the entrance inlaid with abalone.
- **Chiefs' houses** — The setting for feasts, including Christmas feasts held when the potlatch was banned, where gift distribution continued.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Heiltsuk artisans excelled in woodcarving, producing bentwood boxes, chests, canoes, horn spoons, elaborate masks and ceremonial objects.

- **Bentwood boxes** — Finely constructed and decorated wooden boxes made by skilled artisans and sought as trade goods and prestige items.
- **Speaker's staffs** — Carved and painted staffs showing stacked killer whales, hatted figures, three-dimensional copper forms and human heads in high relief.
- **Face masks** — Large masks of alder wood with red cedar mandibles, moving eyes, bear-skin whiskers and baleen, showing transformation themes.
- **Canoes** — Traditional cedar canoes and later western-style trading schooners built by Heiltsuk canoe makers, used for harvesting and trade.
- **Horn spoons and ladles** — Decorated serving utensils made by master artisans, possibly used in ritual feeding during the Hamatsa ceremony.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The most distinctive Heiltsuk adornment is the labret, the lip ornament worn by high-ranking women, alongside abalone inlay and shell ornaments.

- *Labret* — An elliptical lip ornament of wood, bone or horn worn by high-ranking Heiltsuk women, exchanged for larger versions as rank increased.
- **Abalone ornaments** — Shell ornaments cut and filed from abalone, used as nose or lip ornaments and as inlay for eyes on carved figures.

## Music & performance

The Heiltsuk are renowned for ceremonial expertise; singing has grown in strength since the 1950s alongside carving and language revitalization.

- **'Qatuwas canoe gatherings** (*'Qatuwas*) — Major public occasions in 1993 and 2014 featuring cultural sharing including dancing, singing, stories and food, with up to 60 canoes participating.

## Dance & theatre

Masked performance belongs to the winter sacred season, with the Hamatsa secret society central to Heiltsuk ceremonial life.

- **Hamatsa ceremony** (*Tánis*) — A secret society ritual involving symbolic cannibalism and initiation of young men into humanity through a four-part ceremony, shared with the Kwakwakaʼwakw.
- **Transformation masks** — Bird's head masks with transformation flaps depicting bears, showing the ritual change from a wild to a civilized state.

## Festivals & rituals

The Heiltsuk year divides into winter sacred ceremonies and summer harvest; the potlatch is the culminating institution of their social and political order.

- **Potlatch** — A feast system of gift-giving central to Heiltsuk social organization, banned by the Canadian government from the 1870s until 1951, now regularly practised again.
- **Chiefs' Christmas feasts** — Feasts held during the potlatch ban where gift distribution continued, allowing the feast system to survive underground.
- **'Qatuwas** — Canoe gatherings in 1993 and 2014 that brought together Heiltsuk communities for dancing, singing, stories and feasting.
- **Peace treaty renewal with Haida** — A 2014 journey to Masset to renew the peace treaty ending nineteenth-century wars, finalized at a potlatch in Bella Bella in 2015.

## Foodways

The Heiltsuk have relied on the sea for thousands of years, with herring as the keystone resource and a foundation of their trade and culture.

- **Herring harvest** — The traditional harvest of herring and herring spawn, recognized by the 1997 Supreme Court of Canada as an Aboriginal right to commercial fishing.
- **Clam gardens** — Managed marine food systems rediscovered throughout the BC coast through collaboration between archaeologists and traditional knowledge-holders.
- **Ceremonial feasting** — Gift-giving feasts in Chiefs' houses, ceremonial meals for Hamatsa initiates, and food sharing at community gatherings.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral tradition carries Heiltsuk historical memory and links cultural expression to origin stories; speaker's staffs served as regalia for formal oratory.

- **Story** (*núyṃ́*) — The Heiltsuk word for story, central to their cultural expression and historical memory.
- **Speaker's staffs** — Carved and painted staffs used as regalia in formal oratory, now held in museum collections.
- **Bella Bella Texts and Tales** — Collections published by Franz Boas in 1928 and 1932, recording Heiltsuk oral traditions.
- **Testimony** — A documented Heiltsuk genre in which the community declared their sovereignty over their lands before the McKenna–McBride Royal Commission in 1913.

## Language & religion

Heiltsuk is a tonal dialect of Northern Wakashan with conversational and ceremonial forms; the community is reviving the language with only seven fluent speakers remaining.

- **Heiltsuk language** (*Híɫzaqvḷa*) — A tonal Northern Wakashan language with three vowel phonemes and syllabic resonants, spoken at Bella Bella and Klemtu with two subdialects.
- **Chinook Jargon** — A trade language used by the Heiltsuk during the fur trade, with terms like booston for Americans and King-George-Man for the British.
- **Potlatch** — A ceremonial and spiritual practice banned from the 1870s to 1951 that has since been publicly restored.
- **Hamatsa** (*Tánis*) — A sacred winter ceremony involving initiation and ritual that has been publicly restored to Heiltsuk practice.

## Glossary

- *Híɫzaqv* — Heiltsuk in the Heiltsuk language
- *Formline* — A two-dimensional design style of Northwest Coast art in black, green and red
- *Bentwood boxes* — Wooden boxes and chests finely constructed and decorated by Heiltsuk artisans
- *Potlatch* — A feast system of gift-giving central to Heiltsuk social and political organization
- *Tánis* — The Hamatsa, a secret society ceremony involving initiation and symbolic cannibalism
- *Hamatsa* — A winter ceremony involving the initiation of young men and transformation from a wild to a civilized state
- *'Qatuwas* — Canoe gatherings featuring cultural sharing, dancing, singing and feasting
- *Herring spawn* — Herring eggs, the focus of traditional Heiltsuk harvesting and trade
- *Clam gardens* — Managed marine food systems along the BC coast that enhance shellfish productivity
- *Chinook Jargon* — A trade language used during the fur trade with terms like booston (American) and King-George-Man (British)
- *Abalone* — A shell material used for ornaments and inlay, introduced from California
- *Frontlet* — A carved head decoration worn as part of ceremonial outfits
- *Cedar-bark rings* — Circular worked cedar bark headdresses worn as ceremonial markers
- *Transformation masks* — Masks with flaps that depict the change from a wild animal form to a human form
- *Skull headdress* — A headdress worn in Hamatsa ceremony symbolizing death and rebirth of initiates
- *Booston* — Chinook Jargon term for an American person
