## Overview

Hitchiti was a tribal town associated with the Apalachicola Province along the Chattahoochee River and later with towns on Ochese Creek, now called the Ocmulgee River. Its people were among several communities whose members primarily spoke Hitchiti, a Muskogean language that was spoken in Georgia and eastern Alabama and later became extinct among the Oklahoma Seminole. Hitchiti returned to the Chattahoochee River after 1715 and was later forcibly removed to Indian Territory as part of the Trail of Tears. They record an estimated population of 381 in 1832 and state that former members of Hichiti became associated with Kasihta near Okmulgee, Oklahoma.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources describe Hitchiti settlements, archaeological sites, platform mounds, village middens, satellite settlements, and square grounds. Tuttallosee had recently built its own square ground around 1800, possibly indicating that it was becoming a separate tribal town.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Archaeological evidence indicates that the material culture of the lower Chattahoochee region had developed in place over several centuries. The sources mention a variant of the Lamar regional culture, influences from the Fort Walton culture, a major change in ceramic types between 1550 and 1650, platform mounds, and an extensive village midden at archaeological site 9Sw50.

## Music & performance

They state that the language was spoken in several Native American villages and that the Hitchiti and Mikasuki tribes were part of the Creek confederacy, but they provide no musical description.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources mention sacred fires and square grounds. In 1937, the tribal town of “Hichiti,” located northeast of Henryetta, Oklahoma, was reported to no longer be maintaining a sacred fire. Former members of Hichiti became associated with Kasihta, which still maintained a square ground near Okmulgee, Oklahoma.

## Foodways

Benjamin Hawkins reported in 1799 that Hitchiti possessed “a narrow strip of good land” bordering the river.

## Oral tradition & literature

Folklore of the Lower Towns of the Muscogee Confederacy supports an interpretation of Muscogee-speaking immigrants joining a Hitchiti-speaking resident population. The sources also state that scholars believe the Yamasee may have spoken Hitchiti, although the evidence is not conclusive.

## Language & religion

Hitchiti belonged to the Muskogean language family and was often considered a dialect of Mikasuki with which it was mutually intelligible. It was spoken in Georgia and eastern Alabama during the early historic period, and speakers moved into Florida during the 18th and 19th centuries. The language was associated with tribal towns including Hitchiti, Chiaha, Oconee, Okmulgee, Sawokli, and Apalachicola. It had an ancient “female” dialect, also known as the “ancient language,” which was remembered and sometimes spoken by older people. Mikasuki remains spoken by Seminole and Miccosukee people in Florida, while Hitchiti became extinct among the Oklahoma Seminole. The sources mention sacred fires and square grounds but do not define Hitchiti religious beliefs or a religious system.
