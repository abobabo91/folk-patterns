## Overview

The Mohave or Mojave, called *Aha Makhav* in Mojave, are a Native American people from the Colorado River region of the Mojave Desert in Arizona, California, and Nevada. Their ancestral lands followed the Colorado River from Black Canyon, where the pillars of the First House of Mutavilya stood above the river, past Avi kwame or Spirit Mountain, to the Quechan Valley. In contemporary political life, Mojave people belong to the Fort Mojave Indian Tribe of Arizona, California & Nevada and the Colorado River Indian Tribes of the Colorado River Indian Reservation. Their language belongs to the River branch of the Yuman language family and is closely related to Quechan and Maricopa. The sources record approximately 70 speakers in one account, about 22 elders at Fort Mojave as of 2012, and approximately 988 people in 1963. Their folk-cultural significance lies especially in creation traditions, songs, river-based subsistence, and language revitalization.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Accounts of the Fort Mojave Indian School state that students were required to adopt European-American hairstyles, clothing, eating habits, sleeping habits, toiletry, manners, industry, and language. The sources also state that administrators assigned English names to children and registered them as citizens of one of two tribes, divisions that did not reflect the traditional Mojave clan and kinship system.

### Architecture

They identify the First House of Mutavilya as a place whose tall pillars loomed above the Colorado River, and they describe Fort Mojave as a military post that was later converted into a boarding school.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do mention traps and curved throwing sticks used to capture rabbits, but provide no further description of their manufacture or form.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They state only that students at Fort Mojave were compelled to adopt European-American hairstyles, including hair cutting.

## Music & performance

Mojave musical traditions include stories and songs transmitted orally and the traditional Mojave “bird songs.” The language sources describe Hubert McCord, one of the four tribal leaders of the Mojave tribe, as fluent in Mojave and state that he worked with poet Natalie Diaz to document Mojave stories and songs. In one video, McCord sings while taking a group of tribal youth on a short voyage around the Colorado River.

The Center for Indian Education at Arizona State University facilitated workshops for learners and speakers at the Fort Mojave Indian Reservation as of 2012. The project brought elders together with younger people to teach the traditional Mojave bird songs. The language-preservation work of Natalie Diaz on the reservation was featured on PBS NewsHour in March 2012. The sources also identify the annual Megathrow Traditional Bird Singing & Dancing social event, held on the third weekend of March, and the Colorado River Indian Tribes Native American Days Fair & Expo, held annually in Parker from Thursday through Sunday during the first week of October.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention traditional bird singing and dancing. The Megathrow Traditional Bird Singing & Dancing social event is held annually on the third weekend of March.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources identify two annual public events. The Colorado River Indian Tribes Native American Days Fair & Expo is held annually in Parker, from Thursday through Sunday during the first week of October. The Megathrow Traditional Bird Singing & Dancing social event is celebrated annually on the third weekend of March.

Mojave religious tradition centers on the creator Matavilya, who gave Mojave people their clans, and his little brother Mastamho, who gave them the Colorado River and taught them how to plant. Historically, Mojave people were agrarian and planted in the fertile floodplain of the untamed river, following the customs of the Aha cave.

The sources describe a coming-of-age rite involving Datura. A Mojave person coming of age traditionally consumed the plant as a religious sacrament and rite of passage in order to enter a new state of consciousness.

## Foodways

Mojave agriculture included corn, melons, beans, pumpkin, and seeds of wild grasses. The fertile floodplain of the Colorado River was central to planting, and Mastamho was said to have taught the Mojave how to plant. The Mojave Desert supplied cacti bearing edible fruits and mesquite.

Mesquite pods were pounded and crushed to extract their pulp. The pulp was dried and ground into flour, then combined with water to make cakes. The crushed pods could also be soaked in warm water to produce a sweet beverage. Meat was not a prevalent part of the diet because game was scarce, although hunters sometimes travelled to the mountains east of the river to pursue deer. Rabbits were captured with traps or curved throwing sticks.

Fish from the Colorado River more frequently formed a significant part of the diet. A stew made from fish and corn was particularly favored. Datura is described as a plant used in a religious sacrament rather than as food.

## Oral tradition & literature

Early Mojave history was primarily oral because the Mojave language was not written in precolonial times. Disease, outside cultures, and white encroachment disrupted social organization and interrupted the transmission of Mojave stories and songs to later generations. The sources describe the creation traditions of Matavilya and Mastamho, including Mastamho’s giving of the Colorado River and teaching of agriculture.

The Mojave name has appeared in more than 50 Spanish and English transliterations, including Hamock avi, Amacava, A-mac-ha ves, A-moc-ha-ve, Jamajabs, and Hamakhav. The sources also explain that the Mojave call the peaks known in English as The Needles *Huukyámpve*, meaning “where the battle took place,” referring to the battle in which Mastamho slew the sea serpent.

Contemporary preservation work includes the documentation of Mojave stories and songs by Hubert McCord and Natalie Diaz, the traditional bird-song workshops supported by the Center for Indian Education at Arizona State University, and the language materials published by the tribe.

## Language & religion

Mojave or Mohave belongs to the River branch of the Yuman language family, together with Quechan and Maricopa. The language is spoken along the Colorado River in northwestern Arizona, southeastern California, and southwestern Nevada. It became endangered during the nineteenth-century manifest destiny movement when children were taken to boarding schools and prohibited from speaking their language. Fort Mojave operated as an Indian school from 1890 until 1931.

The sources state that approximately 74 people on the Colorado River and Fort Mojave reservations spoke Mojave in 1994, while another account gives approximately 70% of speakers as residing in Arizona and approximately 30% in California. As of 2012, Fort Mojave had about 22 elders who spoke some Mojave. The language has five vowel qualities, length distinction, and the weak vowel /ə/; its basic sentence structure is described as subject-object-verb.

Mojave religious tradition names Matavilya as creator and Mastamho as his little brother. Datura was traditionally used as a deliriant hallucinogen in a religious sacrament and coming-of-age rite.
