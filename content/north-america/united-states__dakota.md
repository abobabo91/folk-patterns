## Overview

The Dakota are a Native American people divided into Eastern (Santee) and Western (Yankton, Yanktonai) bands living across the Upper Midwest and Great Plains. They speak Dakota, a Siouan language with distinct eastern and western dialects, and maintain strong traditions in beadwork, music, dance, and ceremonial life. Their culture centers on voice-led drumming, powwow gatherings, and the stewardship of sacred sites like the Pipestone Quarry.

## Material culture

Dakota material culture emphasizes geometric beadwork, sinew craftsmanship, and composite objects like pipe-tomahawks, reflecting both practical skills and artistic sophistication.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Glass-bead embroidery in strict geometric patterns on white grounds is the dominant decorative technique across Dakota textiles and objects.

- **Glass-bead embroidery** — Beaded diadems, bandoliers, shirts, pouches, and quiver panels worked in strict geometry on white ground.
- **Porcupine-quill mosaic** — Colored quill segments strung and applied to hide sheaths for weapons and ammunition containers in an old tradition.
- **Winter count** (*Wintercount*) — A painted buffalo hide with pictorial records that convey information through image rather than stitching.
- **Feather regalia** — Pale feathers set into red cloth casings with beaded diadems and browbands, compared to Dakota chief regalia.

### Clothing & dress

Nineteenth-century male leaders wore decorated cloth shirts, embroidered leggings, moccasins, blankets, and warrior jackets, topped with eagle-feather headdresses ranging from single feathers to full warbonnets.

- **Warrior jacket** — Tasselled and embroidered jackets worn by leaders like Smutty Bear and Two Lances.
- **Eagle-feather headdress** (*Warbonnet*) — Full feather headdresses worn by high-status men like Sacred Hand Hawk and Yellow Thunder.
- **Dentalium ornaments** — Shell ear and neck ornaments worn by both men and women as status markers.
- **Bone breastplate** — Bone armor worn across the chest as part of formal dress.

### Architecture

No house forms or building techniques are described in the sources, though the Dakota language preserves an architectural reference in the prefix wi- for terms relating to the tipi.

- **Tipi** (*thípi*) — The primary dwelling form, referenced linguistically through the Dakota prefix wi- that classifies related terms.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Dakota craftsmanship in stone, bone, wood, and iron produced functional and ceremonial objects, with the pipe-tomahawk as the signature composite form.

- **Pipe-tomahawk** (*piptomahawk*) — A composite object combining pipe and blade, inlaid and carried by leaders, appearing throughout portrait records.
- **Yankton bow** — A sinew-backed ash or white oak bow 45.25 inches long, coated in white chalky material for moisture protection and known for exceptional power.
- **Hickory arrows** — Split hickory shafts 23.75 to 26.25 inches long with iron broad-head points, two hawk feathers and turkey cock feather, able to kill bison one per quiver.
- **Bow case and quiver** — Brain-tanned buckskin containers (46⅜ and 26.5 inches) with beadwork and sinew-sewn fringe for carrying arrows and bow.
- **Frame drum** — Nineteenth-century wood and hide drum used for percussion in songs and dances.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Dakota adornment centers on bear-claw necklaces, shell ornaments, feathers, and braided hair, each element marking status and spiritual protection.

- **Bear-claw necklace** — Claws on otter skin with red cloth patches between them, claws linked by cotton thread and glass beads, finished with a fabric tassel.
- **Umbilical-cord amulet** — A beaded lizard-form pouch holding an infant girl's umbilical cord, hung on the cradle and later worn by the mother for protection.
- **Dentalium ear ornaments** — Shell ornaments worn as markers of wealth and status by men and women.
- **Eagle feathers** — Worn singly in the hair or assembled into full headdresses as marks of honor and leadership.

## Music & performance

Dakota music is voice-led over drumming, with songs opening high and cascading lower in phrases repeated by the group, often using only vocables.

- **Traditional dance song** — Opens high with a lead singer whose phrase the group repeats, cascades to lower pitch, echoes the first half in incomplete repetition.
- **Flag Song** (*Lakota Flag Song*) — Opens powwows and honors veterans and warriors, not danced to, absorbing imposed American symbols into Dakota tradition.
- **Honor beat** — Usually four drum beats representing cannon fire in battle, played in contest songs.
- **Sun dance songs** (*Sun dance*) — A major song genre associated with ceremony outside the powwow circuit.
- **Elk Soldier and Bad Nation** — Named Dakota singing groups that perform traditional and contemporary music.

## Dance & theatre

Dakota dance traditions center on powwow contest categories — men's traditional, chicken, and fancy — and the Sun dance, with no documented masked or dramatic traditions.

- **Men's traditional dance** — A powwow contest category with a beat beginning as an irregular ruffle that turns to swift regular pulse.
- **Chicken dance** — A powwow contest category with syncopated percussion and dynamic footwork.
- **Fancy dance** — A modern powwow contest category showcasing contemporary interpretations of traditional movement.

## Festivals & rituals

Powwows are the principal public gatherings, opened by the Flag Song and structured around contest dances, with separate ceremonial contexts for Sun dance, Yuwipi, and Inipi.

- **Powwow** — The main public gathering opened by the Flag Song, structured around men's traditional, chicken, and fancy dance contests and honor songs.
- **Sun dance** — A major ceremonial context and song genre performed outside the powwow circuit.
- **Yuwipi** — A ceremonial gathering with its own song tradition, performed outside powwow settings.
- **Inipi** — A sweat lodge ceremony with associated songs, part of traditional Dakota spiritual practice.
- **Pipestone Quarry** — The cultural center of the Sioux people, stewarded by Yankton and Yanktonai as a sacred site for quarrying pipestone for pipes and pipe-tomahawks.

## Foodways

The Santee hunted woodland animals and gathered wild rice near Lake Superior; the Yankton hunted deer, antelope, and bison on the prairie with exceptional bow skill.

- **Wild rice** — Gathered by the Santee Dakota around Lake Superior in what is now northern Minnesota and Wisconsin.
- **Bow hunting** — The Yankton hunted deer, antelope, and small game with the bow and were reported able to kill bison with each arrow in a quiver.
- **Woodland and prairie hunting** — Before the seventeenth century, Santee hunted woodland animals and fished from canoes; Yankton hunted on the prairie.

## Oral tradition & literature

Dakota narrative tradition is lexicalised in the language itself, with the painted winter-count hide as the material counterpart and written literature emerging from historical documents and Indigenous scholarship.

- **Winter-count hide** (*Wintercount*) — Painted buffalo hide records that preserve history in pictures, a pictorial form of storytelling.
- **Dakota Prisoner of War Letters** — Fluent Dakota-language letters written from Camp Kearney prison in Davenport, Iowa, 1863–1866, edited by Clifford Canku and Michael Simon.
- **Zitkala-Ša** (*Zitkala-Ša (Gertrude Simmons Bonnin)*) — A Yankton writer, educator, and activist (1876–1938) who documented Dakota culture and advocated for Indigenous rights.
- **Ella Cara Deloria** — An ethnographer and linguist (1889–1971) who recorded and preserved Dakota language and stories.
- **Dakota Language Audio Journal** — A joint project of the University of Minnesota and Sisseton Wahpeton College recording conversations and stories in Dakota.

## Language & religion

Dakota is a Mississippi Valley Siouan language with Eastern (Santee, Sisseton) and Western (Yankton, Yanktonai) dialects, polysynthetic and agglutinative, with ongoing revitalisation efforts across universities and community programs.

- **Dakota language** (*Dakhód'iapi, Dakȟótiyapi*) — A polysynthetic, agglutinative Siouan language with five oral and three nasal vowels, written in Latin script, mutually intelligible with Lakota.
- **Sun dance songs** (*Sun dance*) — A religious song genre reflecting Dakota spiritual practice and ceremonial tradition.
- **Yuwipi songs** (*Yuwipi*) — A ceremonial song genre tied to Dakota spiritual practice outside the powwow circuit.
- **Inipi songs** (*Inipi*) — Songs associated with sweat lodge ceremony, part of traditional Dakota spiritual life.
- **Dakota Language House** — A University of Minnesota program supporting Dakota language learning and cultural transmission.

- *thípi* — Tipi; traditional conical dwelling
- *Wintercount* — Pictorial hide record preserving history in images on bison skin
- *piptomahawk* — Pipe-tomahawk; composite object combining pipe and blade
- *Erethizon* — Porcupine; source of quills for mosaic decoration
- *Dakhód'iapi, Dakȟótiyapi* — The Dakota language
- *Yuwipi* — A ceremonial and song tradition in Dakota spiritual practice
- *Inipi* — Sweat lodge ceremony with associated songs
- *Pipestone Quarry* — Sacred site stewarded by Yankton and Yanktonai; cultural center of the Sioux
