## Overview

The Achomawi, also called Achumawi, Ajumawi, and Ahjumawi, are the northerly nine bands of the Pit River tribe of Palaihnihan Native Americans. They live in what is now northeastern California, on both sides of the Pit River from Goose Lake to Montgomery Creek. Their historical territories extended from Big Bend to Goose Lake and included the Fall River Valley, Big Valley, the Goose Valley, and the surrounding mountains and tributaries. The name “Achomawi” means “river people” and properly refers to the band historically associated with the Fall River Valley and Pit River. The Achomawi language belongs with Atsugewi to the Palaihnihan languages. Their folk culture includes distinctive river fishing technologies, basketry, dugout canoes, seasonal houses, puberty rites, tattooing, and named fishing nets.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Twined baskets** — Achomawi baskets were made primarily from willow rods used as warp and pine root used as weft. They were colored with vegetable dyes and had shapes adapted to particular uses, including cooking on hot stones, holding semi-liquid food or water, wearing as caps, and carrying burdens. Compared with Hupa and Yurok basketry, Achomawi baskets were described as softer and larger, with designs that did not focus on one horizontal band.

**Tule-mat objects** — Tule was used to make twine, mats, and shoes. In summer, cone-shaped houses were covered with tule mats, and tule fiber was used alone in basket caps.

**Mesh seed beaters** — A mesh beater made from willow or a mixture of willow and pine root was used to harvest seeds into burden baskets.

**Woven fishing equipment** — Fishing equipment included baskets, fish traps, dip nets, gill nets, seines, and weirs. A minnow trap was made from willow rods and pine-root weft, while rawhide was used for fishing weirs.

**Tule footwear** — Moccasins of twined tule, stuffed with grass, were the most common footwear.

**Xerophyllum tenax overlays** — Many baskets were covered with a light white overlay of xerophyllum tenax, which protected the basket’s artwork and materials when the basket was used for boiling or holding water.

### Clothing & dress

Achomawi men wore buckskin coats and shirts made from deerskin sewn into a form with armholes and belted at the waist. Buckskin leggings with fringe were occasionally worn. Men also wore an apron-like kilt similar to the breechcloth of Eastern communities. Moccasins of twined tule and stuffed with grass were common, while deerskin moccasins were worn in dry weather.

Women wore short gowns or tops similar to those of men, together with a deerskin skirt or fringed apron. Buckskin moccasins and a basket cap were standard among women. Clothing worn by both men and women could be decorated with porcupine-quill embroidery.

### Architecture

Achomawi bands had separate territories along the banks of the Pit River. Within each territory, villages consisted of extended family members and were organized around a central village with smaller satellite villages. Villages generally contained about 20–60 inhabitants.

In summer, the Achomawi and other upper Pit River bands usually lived in cone-shaped houses covered with tule mats. People also spent time under shade or behind windbreaks made from brush or mats. In winter, larger houses were built. These winter houses were partially underground, with wooden frames supporting coverings made from bark, grass, and tule. Housing varied between the more densely forested lower Pit River zone and the drier sagebrush and juniper zone of the upper Pit River.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do describe wooden canoes, fishing spears, fish hooks, fish traps, nets, weirs, baskets, and tools made from bone, horn, willow, pine root, rawhide, and wood.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Both men and women had tattoos. Women had three lines tattooed under the mouth and perhaps additional lines on the cheek. Men had septum piercings with dentalium shell or other jewelry. Women wore a basket cap as part of standard dress.

Widowhood involved distinctive bodily practices. A widow cropped her hair and rubbed pitch into the stubble and on her face. She also wore a necklace with lumps of pitch around her neck. These items were worn for about three years.

## Music & performance

The sources describe music in connection with puberty rites. During a girl’s puberty ritual, a deer-hoof rattle accompanied dancing around a fire. The girl built a fire in front of her house and danced around it throughout the night while relatives participated around the fire or inside the house. The ritual was repeated for five days and nights.

The sources mention a victory dance held in the community after warfare. It involved carrying the head of an enemy, with women participating in the celebration. They also describe a double ball game played by women for leisure and the use of sweat lodges. No other instruments, song genres, performance institutions, or musical contexts are identified in the sources.

## Dance & theatre

The sources describe dancing in puberty rites and in a victory dance. During the girl’s puberty ritual, she danced around a fire throughout the night, accompanied by a deer-hoof rattle. The ritual included participation by relatives and continued for five days and nights.

A victory dance involved carrying the head of an enemy, with women participating in the celebration.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do describe life-cycle rites, fishing-related observances, warfare purification, marriage practices, funerary customs, and widowhood.

When a child was born, the parents entered seclusion and observed food restrictions until the baby’s umbilical cord fell off. If twins were born, one was killed at birth. Marriage followed a patrilocal pattern: the bridegroom initially lived in the bride’s home, hunting and working for her relatives, and she eventually moved with him to his family. Society was patrilineal, and the traditional chiefdom passed to the eldest son.

Girls underwent a puberty ritual that began with ear piercing, followed by being picked up, dropped, and hit with an old basket. The girl ran away, returned in the evening with firewood, built a fire, danced around it, and later underwent sprinkling with fir leaves and bathing. Boys’ rites included ear piercing, being struck with a bowstring, fasting, bathing in a lake or spring, lighting fires, piling stones, and drinking through a reed. An animal seen or dreamed of during the rite could become the boy’s personal protector.

The dead were buried flexed on their side facing east, sometimes in woven baskets. Those who died outside the community were cremated, and their ashes were brought back for burial. Belongings and offerings were buried or burned with the body, and the dead person’s house was burned. The sources state that there were no special ceremonies or rituals.

## Foodways

Achomawi subsistence combined fishing, hunting, gathering, and exchange. Foods included deer, wildfowl, bass, pike, trout, catfish, wild plants, herbs, eggs, insects, larvae, acorns, tule, Oregon grape, Oregon plum, Pacific yew, whiteleaf manzanita, camas, seed-bearing grasses, Indian potatoes, and lilies. Acorns were a staple, although scarcity of oak trees in Achomawi territory meant that they were often obtained from neighboring cultures.

Fishing was a major food source. The Sacramento sucker was of “paramount importance.” Salmon was scarce for eastern groups but abundant in the lower Pit River. It was sun-dried, lightly roasted, or smoked, then stored in large bark-covered baskets in slabs or crumbled pieces. Trout, pike, suckers, minnows, and allis were caught with nets, traps, spears, and weirs.

The sources name several fishing nets: *taláka'yi*, a dip net suspended from a forked pole; *tamichi*, used only for suckers; *lipake*, a small dip net with an oval hoop; *tuwátifshi*, a gill net; and *talámámchi*, a seine. A weir called *tatápi* was used in shallow streams, while *tafsifschi* was used in larger streams to catch allis when they returned to the sea in fall. The only meat avoided was domestic dog, and salt was used in extreme moderation because too much salt was believed to cause sore eyes.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They do describe guardian spirits called *tinihowi*, the Deer Woman in boys’ puberty rites, and spiritual presences associated with mountain peaks, springs, and other sacred places.

The Achomawi language is extinct. The sources state that perhaps ten people spoke it in 1991, with only eight in 2000, and that the last speaker died in 2013. A mobile app was planned as of 2013, and Louise Davis used flashcards with her children and supported efforts to preserve the language. The sources also list the Achumawi Dictionary and other teaching and documentation materials.

## Language & religion

Achumawi, also called Achomawi or the Pit River language, was spoken by the Pit River people in northeastern California. The name Achumawi is an anglicization of the Fall River band’s name, *ajúmmááwí*, from *ajúmmá*, “river.” The language had nine traditional bands with dialect differences between upriver dialects—Atwamwi, Astariwawi, Kosalektawi, Hammawi, and Hewisedawi—and downriver dialects—Madesiwi, Itsatawi, Ilmawi, and Achumawi proper.

Achumawi and Atsugewi comprise the Palaihnihan language family. Achumawi had 29 consonants, a five-vowel system, contrastive vowel and consonant length, and contrastive high and low tones. It had no grammatical gender but distinguished animate and inanimate forms of the copula.

Adolescent boys sought guardian spirits called *tinihowi*, and both genders experienced puberty ceremonies. Achomawi shamans acted as doctors and treated physical and spiritual “pains,” believed to have been placed on people by hostile shamans. Quartz crystal, mountain peaks, springs, waterfalls, and other sacred places had spiritual significance.
