## Overview

The Tewa are a Pueblo people living mainly along the Rio Grande in New Mexico. Their culture includes farming, pottery, weaving, wood carving, jewelry, and religious traditions. Six Tewa-speaking pueblos are in New Mexico, and Hopi Tewa communities live in Arizona. Tewa language programs, dictionaries, recorded stories, and learning tools support language preservation.

## Material culture

Pottery is the most influential art form described, while woven goods, clothing, jewelry, and wood carving also support daily life and income.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Woven goods, cotton garments, and hide clothing are described.

- **Woven goods** — Woven goods provide an alternative source of income and are part of the modern economy.
- **Cotton garments** — Cotton was made into clothing and shoes.
- **Hide clothing** — Hides from deer, rabbits, and other hunted animals were made into clothing and shoes.

### Clothing & dress

Tewa clothing used cotton and animal hides, but the sources do not distinguish named everyday or ceremonial garments.

- **Cotton clothing** — Cotton was made into clothing and shoes.
- **Hide clothing and shoes** — Animal hides were made into clothing and shoes.

### Architecture

The sources mention homes, pueblos, villages, kivas, and agricultural settlements.

- **Pueblos** (*pueblo*) — Pueblos are mentioned as Tewa communities, but their building forms are not described.
- **Kivas** (*kiva*) — Kivas are mentioned.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery was used for storage, eating, cooking, and trade and was the most influential art form described.

- **Santa Clara black clay vessel** — A Santa Clara Pueblo vessel is black clay with a stylized animal motif and a signature by Madeleine M.
- **Earthenware bowls** — Some museum records describe bowls with brown and red ornament on a yellow-white ground.
- **Painted bowl** — Another record describes a painted bowl with a white ground and a handle.
- **Iron kettles and pots** — Iron kettles and pots were introduced as cooking objects, while pottery continued to be used for storage and dishes.
- **Tewa or Hano clay vessels** (*Hano*) — Museum records document clay vessels identified as Tewa or Hano.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Jewelry provided income before Spanish colonization and remains part of the modern economy, but named forms are not given.

- **Jewelry** — Jewelry was an alternative source of income and contributes to the modern-day economy.
- **Beadwork** — Museum records describe beadwork in white, blue, yellow, orange, maroon, red, and other colors on regalia components.

## Festivals & rituals

Tewa ritual life includes family ceremonies, marriage customs, childhood teaching, Catholic rites, and reverence for the natural world.

- **Marriage negotiation** — Marriage was usually arranged through negotiation between families and accompanied by an exchange of gifts.
- **Native marriage ritual** — A marriage ceremony could include a native ritual and might also include a nonnative ritual.
- **Childhood role groups** — At age ten, boys and girls were divided into two groups to learn community roles.
- **First Communion** — Children from primarily Catholic families also attended First Communion.

## Foodways

The Tewa cultivated maize, beans, and squash with irrigation and also hunted, gathered, and processed plants.

- **Irrigated crops** — The Tewa used irrigation to grow maize, beans, and squash.
- **Hunting** — They hunted deer, bison, and elk.
- **Gathered foods** — They gathered berries and nuts.
- **Herbal teas and potions** — Plants and other natural flora were processed into herbal teas and sometimes potions.
- **Introduced foods** — Spanish colonization brought cows, pigs, chickens, wheat, tomatoes, apples, pears, peaches, and chili.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tewa language preservation uses both oral tradition and literacy, supported by dictionaries, programs, recorded stories, and learning tools.

- **Tewa dictionary** — Ohkay Owingeh published a Tewa dictionary.
- **TewaTalk** — TewaTalk uses recorded speech and learning games to support language learning.
- **Digitized children’s stories** — The University of New Mexico digitized children’s stories in Tewa.

## Language & religion

Tewa is a Tanoan language with pueblo-level dialect differences, and Tewa religion combines reverence for nature, supernatural forces, and Catholicism.

- **Tewa language** (*Tewa*) — Tewa is spoken in New Mexico’s Rio Grande valley and in Arizona.
- **Tano** — Tano is another name for the Tewa language.
- **Tée-wah** — Tée-wah is another name for the Tewa language.
- **’Agojo so’jo** — ’Agojo so’jo is associated with the Morning star and warfare.
- **Ponu’chona** — Ponu’chona is a Hopi-Tewa deity associated with the morning star, animals, and hunting.
