## Overview

The Tlingit — *Lingít*, "People of the Tides" — are an Indigenous people of the Pacific Northwest Coast: Alaska Natives in the United States and First Nations in Canada. Their historical territory ran from the Portland Canal north to just southeast of the Copper River delta and covered almost all of the Alexander Archipelago; inland Tlingit live around Atlin, Teslin and Tagish Lakes in northwestern British Columbia and southern Yukon. The territory divides into Southern, Northern, Inland and Gulf Coast Tlingit, along ecological, linguistic and cultural lines. Tlingit society is matrilineal: children belong to the mother's clan, and property and hereditary roles pass through her line. The two moieties, Raven (*Yéil*) and Eagle, are subdivided into clans and house groups, each with heraldic crests displayed on totem poles, canoes, feast dishes, house posts, weavings and jewelry. In Alaska there are no reservations; the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act created Sealaska Corporation (1972) for the Tlingit, Haida and Tsimshian.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Two weaving traditions carry Tlingit crest art in wool. Chilkat weaving, named after the Chilkat region near Klukwan on the Chilkat River, is practised by the Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian and other Northwest Coast peoples, and is unusual in that curvilinear and circular forms are made within the weave itself. Chilkat robes (*naaxein*) are worn by high-ranking people on civic and ceremonial occasions, including dances, and chiefs wore them at potlatches. They are woven from mountain-goat wool, dog fur and yellow cedar bark, almost always in black, white, yellow and blue, with a long fringe that sways when the wearer dances. The loom has only a top frame and side supports, so the warp hangs free and the weaver works in vertical sections; designs are therefore often broken into bilaterally symmetrical columns. A robe can take a year to weave. Yellow dye came from wolf moss, which also deterred moths, and black from western hemlock, iron and copper. In the 1990s only about six people still practised true Chilkat weaving; the Chilkat weaver Jennie Thlunaut (c. 1891–1986) was among those who carried it on.

Ravenstail weaving (*yéil koowú*) is a finger-twined geometric style with sharp lines and few colours — black, white and sometimes yellow — and is thought to be the precursor of Chilkat weaving. Only about a dozen historical Ravenstail robes survive in North American and European museums; one at the British Museum is a fringed goat-wool cloak with a strip of sea-otter fur woven into the top edge. The style almost died out after the 1800s and was revived from the 1980s, with Cheryl Samuel's *The Raven's Tail* (1987) and a Ravenstail Weavers' Guild founded in Ketchikan in 1990.

The design language of both, and of Tlingit carving and painting, is Northwest Coast formline — ovoids, U-forms and S-forms combined into stylised but representational crests and figures from oral history.

### Clothing & dress

Museum records hold woven bark hats painted with formline crests. Faces were painted white, black and red; the paint protected against snow blindness in winter and gnats and mosquitoes in summer, and black marked death, anger, sorrow and war. Tattooing, which the Tlingit learned from the Haida, marked higher-status families; it was done almost always by a woman, paid in blankets and food, with a bone or metal needle drawing blue-black stained sinew under the skin, and slaves were forbidden it. Girls of wealthy households were often tattooed at great potlatches. Warriors wore dense wooden helmets with neck protectors and visors, and wooden slat armour over linen and a leather jacket, sometimes painted; some armour was covered in Qing-dynasty Chinese coins obtained through Russian traders.

### Architecture

Tlingit clan houses were cedar plank houses, square or rectangular, reportedly built without adhesive, nails or other fastenings, with storage beneath the floor and front designs and totem poles showing the owners' clan and moiety. Totem poles are carved from cedar with crests aligned in a column; they stand at the outside corners of dwellings, support interiors, or stand on shores. They tell stories that can only be read by someone who already knows them, commemorate the dead and a chief's achievements, and mortuary poles held a box of the cremated remains. Iron tools led to many more poles by the early 1800s.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Even everyday objects such as spoons and storage boxes are decorated and carry spiritual power. Museum records hold carved wooden halibut hooks with plant-fibre and skin lashings, a wood-and-horn ladle, a wooden comb carved as the measuring-worm crest, a wooden pipe bowl in the form of a human head, and carved and painted canoe and totem-pole models. Shield-shaped coppers were used as valuables. Before European trade copper was the main metal, much of it traded from the Ahtna; iron is said in oral tradition to have come first as drift iron from a shipwreck. Daggers were double-bladed or hafted-pommel, the pommels carved as ravens, bears and other crests, and reached their greatest elaboration in the early 19th century; they remained symbols of status and heirlooms after firearms replaced them in war.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adults and children over eight of the higher classes wore earlobe ornaments of abalone (*haliotis*) shell, copper, wood and bone, shaped in geometric forms; silver gradually replaced bone and wood. The dead were dressed and given a nose ring, *tunás*. Museum records hold a carved ivory amulet (1840–60).

## Music & performance

Songs, like dances, stories and names, are clan property. Lullabies are public, but any song written with serious intent — a love song or a song of mourning — belongs to its owner and may not be sung, recorded or performed without the owning clan's permission; before a dance or song, performers state whose permission they have and whose it is. Museum records hold carved and painted wooden rattles filled with pebbles, some in bird form, and two 19th-century lutes at the Metropolitan Museum attributed to the Yakutat Tlingit.

## Dance & theatre

Dances are clan property on the same terms as songs. Chilkat robes are dance regalia. Museum records hold carved and painted masks — a beaver mask of alder wood painted blue and red, a mosquito mask, a shaman's mask — and a transformation puppet of bone, sinew and shell inlay from the late 18th or early 19th century.

## Festivals & rituals

Potlatches (*koo.éex'*) were held for deaths, births, naming, marriages, sharing wealth, raising totem poles, special events, and honouring leaders or the dead. The memorial potlatch, a year or two after a death, restored the balance of the community and let the family stop mourning; members of the opposite moiety received gifts and heard and performed songs and stories, and the successor of an important person such as a chief or shaman was chosen. Burning food at potlatches was thought to feed new totem poles, and wealthy Tlingit commissioned large poles for great potlatches. July was the war season, when weather allowed disputes to be settled and potlatches held.

## Foodways

Salmon receives more emphasis than any other food, with seal and game close behind. Halibut, shellfish and seaweed are spring foods; seal and salmon come in late spring and summer, with salmonberries, soapberries and currants; sea otters are hunted in autumn. Herring and eulachon are staples eaten fresh or dried. Fish provide meat, oil and eggs; deer, bear and mountain goat are hunted in the forests. Living on beach food alone was considered contemptible and a sign of poverty, and shamans' families had to abstain from it entirely. Clans own salmon streams, herring spawning grounds, berry patches and fishing holes, though reciprocal relations let nearly anyone harvest in most places.

## Oral tradition & literature

Stories are clan property: some are shared freely, others may not be told without a clan member's permission, and many humorous tales of the Raven cycle are in the public domain. Raven is a central protagonist who stole the sun, the waters and the fish to provide for the Tlingit lands, and appears on totem poles with great regularity. Heirlooms (*at.oow*), such as blankets, are passed down and held in trust. Names are inherited from deceased clan relatives, and a name could be taken from another clan to settle an unpaid debt.

## Language & religion

The Tlingit language (*Lingít*) is an independent branch of the Na-Dené family, with a complex grammar and several rare sounds. It has an estimated 200 to 400 speakers in the United States and about 100 in Canada, all bilingual in English; Sealaska Heritage Institute, Goldbelt Heritage Institute and the University of Alaska Southeast run language programmes, and community classes are held in Klukwan and Angoon. Tlingit belief was animist, with the reincarnation of humans and animals as a central tenet; hunters purified themselves before hunting, and shamans (*íxt'*), mostly men, cured disease, influenced the weather, aided hunting, foretold the future, protected against witchcraft and directed war parties. Between 1886 and 1895, when shamans could not treat smallpox and other introduced diseases, many Tlingit converted to Russian Orthodoxy, whose missionaries had translated the liturgy into Tlingit. First contact with Europeans was with Russian explorers in 1741, followed by the Spanish in 1775. The 1862 smallpox epidemic killed about 60% of the Mainland Tlingit and 37% of the Island Tlingit. In 1802 a Tlingit coalition took the Russian garrison at Sitka; Russian forces drove them out again at the Battle of Sitka, and the Tlingit did not return until 1824. Hereditary slavery was practised extensively, with as many as a third of people enslaved, until the United States outlawed it.
