## Overview

The Machapunga were a small Native American tribe who spoke a Carolina Algonquian language and belonged to the Secotan people. They lived in coastal northeastern North Carolina, especially in what is now Hyde County, with lands possibly extending into Beaufort, Washington, Tyrrell, and Dare counties. In 1700 and 1701, their village of Mattamuskeet held 30 warriors and was likely located on the shore of Mattamuskeet Lake. In 1701, John Lawson described about 100 members living on the mainland. The Machapunga language became extinct, and the tribe ultimately became extinct as a tribe in the 18th century. An unrecognized organization now represents individuals who state that they are of Machapunga descent.

## Material culture

### Architecture

They state that the Machapunga maintained a village named Mattamuskeet and later lived in a single village with the Coree, but provide no architectural description.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources state that the Machapunga were excellent watermen, meaning boatsmen, in 1713.

## Music & performance

They state that the Machapunga spoke a Carolina Algonquian language that became extinct.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do record participation in the Tuscarora War against the colonists in 1711.

The sources mention Christian missionary activity in the 18th century. In 1761, Rev. Alexander Stewart founded a school for eight Native children and two African-American children. He wrote that he had baptized seven “Attamuskeet, Hatteras, and Roanoke” adults and children, and in 1763 baptized 21 more Native people from the region.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources do record historical writings about the Machapunga by John Lawson, Frank Speck, Patrick H. Garrow, and other observers, but do not preserve Machapunga oral narratives.

Anthropologist John Reed Swanton wrote that the name Machapunga meant “bad dust” or “much dirt” in their Algonquian language. This is the only translated vernacular expression supplied by the sources.

## Language & religion

The Machapunga spoke a Carolina Algonquian language, which became extinct. The sources describe them as an Algonquian language–speaking Native American tribe and connect them with the Secotan people and the Powhatan Confederacy. They also state that the Machapunga were a group from the Powhatan Confederacy who migrated from present-day Virginia.

The sources document Christian missionary activity in the 18th century, including baptisms conducted by Rev. Alexander Stewart.

The name Machapunga also referred to an early 16th-century village on the Potomac River and an 18th-century Powhatan Confederacy village in Northampton County, Virginia. The Machapunga village of Mattamuskeet was located in what is now Hyde County, North Carolina.
