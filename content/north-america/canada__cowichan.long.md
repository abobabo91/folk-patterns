## Overview

The Cowichan — *Quwʼutsun* in Halkomelem — are a group of Coast Salish peoples of the Cowichan Valley on Vancouver Island, British Columbia. Their territory covered the whole valley, the country around Cowichan Lake and Shawnigan Lake, and extended into the Gulf Islands and to the Fraser River; the most heavily settled part was the lower valley from present-day Duncan down to Cowichan Bay, including the lower Koksilah River. Cowichan Tribes, the band government created under the Indian Act by amalgamating seven neighbouring peoples, has over 3,800 registered members and is the single largest First Nations band in British Columbia. The language is Halkomelem, a Salishan language of the Central Salish branch. In folk-culture terms the Cowichan are known above all for a distinctive type of knitting, most especially the Cowichan sweater, and for carved spindle whorls, horn rattles and spoons held in museum collections.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Cowichan knitting** — The sources identify the Cowichan with a distinctive type of knitting, most especially the *Cowichan sweater*; they do not describe its materials, technique or patterning, so nothing further can be said here from them.

**Spun-wool preparation and the spindle whorl** — The largest single class of Cowichan objects in the collection shown is the spindle whorl, the weighted disc that steadies a hand spindle. Those catalogued are cut from wood, several specifically of maple: roughly disc-shaped, often slightly convex or with a raised centre, pierced by an axial hole, and worked on the face with carving, engraving, openwork or red paint. One is worn smooth and well patinated around the axial hole from use; another appears unused. Wool is also attested as a finishing material on ceremonial objects — two horn rattles carry tassels or strips of wool threaded through holes along their edges.

**Cedar-bark construction** — Cedar bark worked as a flexible sheet is documented in a canoe bailer: a broad flat piece of cedar bark gently curved, its two ends turned up and gathered or pleated into vertical uprights, shaved down and bound with cherry (*Prunus*) bark onto an oval-sectioned horizontal handle, perhaps of cedar wood.

**Motif vocabulary.** The museum catalogue describes rather than names the designs; they include a nine-lobed flower-like circular design set immediately around a whorl's axial hole; a herring-bone pattern cut with a (crooked?) knife on a whorl's underside; a bird with outstretched wings, carved on the front of two whorls; twelve triangles spaced in pairs radiating from the central hole; openwork rays around the central hole; two concentric engraved lines; eight smaller holes spaced evenly around a central hole; a crest design on a horn spoon's bowl, a diamond form with a "U" form at the centre partly filled with crossed hatching and flukes or tail turned towards the handle; and a face flanked by trigons on a horn rattle.

### Clothing & dress

The sources document dress chiefly through three photographs. Two nearly identical carte-de-visite albumen prints show a seated studio portrait of a Cowichan woman on Vancouver Island wearing a woven brimmed Haida hat, a Hudson's Bay and Co. blanket over her shoulders, and a cloth shirt and skirt — an assemblage combining an imported trade blanket, a hat made by a neighbouring people, and settler-made cloth garments. A black-and-white postcard printed at Duncan shows three Quamichan men dancing in a clearing as part of a potlatch, dressed in ceremonial costume, with other Quamichan people gathered around watching; the caption does not itemise the costume, so its elements cannot be named from this source. Knitted garments — the Cowichan sweater above all — are the one dress form the sources attribute distinctively to the Cowichan.

### Architecture

The sources give almost nothing on built form. The potlatch postcard from Duncan shows wooden houses in the background of the dance ground, and the Halkomelem word *léləmʼ* "house" is recorded (in the Downriver dialect material), but no house type, framing method, roof form or carved or painted decoration is described. What the sources do document is settlement geography: seven traditional villages — Quamichan (*Kwaʼmutsun*), Comiaken (*Qwumʼyiqunʼ*), Koksilah (*Xwulqwʼselu*), Somena (*Sʼamunu*), Clemclemluts (*Lhumlhumulutsʼ*), Khenipsen (*Xinupsum*) and Cowichan Bay (*Tlʼulpalus*) — and nine reserves totalling 24 square kilometres (5,900 acres) within a core traditional territory of about 3,750 square kilometres (900,000 acres). The reserves lie along the Cowichan River and Cowichan Bay and include Theik 2, Kil-pah-las 3, Est-Patrolas 4, Tzart-lam 5, Kakalatza 6, and Skutz 7 and 8 at Skutz Canyon.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Wood and horn dominate the documented object repertoire. Besides the spindle whorls and the cedar-bark canoe bailer, the collection holds a wooden comb carved with a *xwe-xwe* mask figure; a spoon of mountain goat horn with a simple pointed handle and a fine, highly unusual engraved crest design on the bowl; a wooden salmon fish-lure; and a smoking pipe of wood and brass — the one object combining a worked metal element. Two rattles are made from horn: one from a circular piece folded in half into a semicircle and filled with unidentified material, with a wooden handle fixed to the lower edge; the other a length of horn with a handle carved as a bird's head, the far end steamed flat, folded over and sewn shut, and accented with abalone shell. No ceramics appear among the objects shown. In the present day, Cowichan Tribes owns Khowutzun Millwork & Joinery, a woodworking enterprise within its Khowutzun Development Corporation group.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used do not cover Cowichan jewelry, tattooing or hair practices; the only adornment material they name is the abalone shell used as an accent on a horn rattle.

## Music & performance

The sound-producing instruments in the collection are two horn rattles, both of mountain-goat-type horn worked by folding: one a semicircle with a face and trigons carved on each outer side, a wooden handle at the base and wool tassels threaded through holes along its lower edge; the other a horn length with a bird's-head handle, steamed flat and sewn shut at the opposite end, hung with strips of wool and accented with abalone. Both are filled with unidentified material or objects to produce the sound. The single documented performance context is a potlatch at Duncan, photographed for a printed postcard, at which three Quamichan men danced in ceremonial costume before an audience of other Quamichan people. The sources name no song genres, no further instruments, and no singers or song-keepers, and Canada has no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions, so there is no inscription to reference here.

## Dance & theatre

Two items touch on dance. The potlatch postcard records three Quamichan men dancing in a clearing in ceremonial costume as part of the potlatch, watched by a gathered crowd — the only dance event described. A wooden comb carries a figure of a *xwe-xwe* mask; the catalogue names the mask type.

## Festivals & rituals

The potlatch is the one named ceremonial occasion in the sources, documented in the Duncan postcard as a gathering with dancing, ceremonial dress and a watching community. For life-cycle practice, the sources record that arranged marriages between children in different Halkomelem-speaking language areas were common, and that these marriages helped establish a regional social network across the Strait of Georgia–Puget Sound basin — part of the same pattern of movement by which Island people crossed the Strait of Georgia to camp along the Fraser River, in both the Downriver and Upriver areas, for the summer salmon runs. The sources also describe institutions that stood outside festival life: before and during early contact, Cowichan warriors travelled long distances in large war canoes to raid other villages, seize goods and take captives for enslavement or ransom, captives being incorporated into households, traded or used as labour, and slavery was a recognised social institution before European contact. Birth, coming-of-age and funeral rites, and any annual calendar of observances, are not covered by the sources used.

## Foodways

The sources do not name a single Cowichan dish, cooking method or ceremonial food, but they establish that the food economy turned on salmon. Island Halkomelem speakers crossed the Strait of Georgia each summer to camp along the Fraser River for the salmon runs; among the objects shown is a salmon fish-lure of wood, together with a cedar-bark canoe bailer for keeping a working canoe dry and a mountain goat horn spoon for eating or serving. The continuing legal weight of the fishery is recorded in the Cowichan Tribes v. Canada ruling of August 2025, in which the Supreme Court of British Columbia granted the Cowichan Tribes Aboriginal title over a portion of Lulu Island in Richmond, adjacent to the Fraser River, and fishing rights over a portion of the river itself.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used contain no Cowichan folktales, epics, proverbs or riddles, and name no storytellers or storytelling occasions; what they document instead is language documentation and archiving — the FirstVoices website holds 1,754 archived words and 690 archived phrases of Halkomelem, delivered through an iPhone app released in 2011 and an Android version in 2016.

## Language & religion

Cowichan speak Halkomelem, a member of the Central Salish branch of the Salishan family; the other branches are Tsamosan, Interior Salish, Bella Coola and Tillamook, and speakers of Central and Tsamosan languages are usually identified in ethnographic literature as Coast Salish. The Island dialect, *Hulʼqumiʼnumʼ*, is also called "Cowichan" and is shared with the Snuneymuxw, Snaw-naw-as, Stzʼuminus, Lake Cowichan, Halalt, Lyackson, Penelakut and Lamalchi; tense, sharply articulated glottalized resonants are a key feature of Island speech. The Cowichan write the language in a cased Latin alphabet with modifier apostrophes and doubled letters for vowel length, using *ou* for the long [u] of French loanwords, *u* for schwa, and including the tetragraph *tthʼ*. The language is near extinction: fewer than twenty-five fluent speakers were estimated in 2000 and 263 reported in 2014, with 21 Head Start programs including a language-nest immersion preschool, and a language program at Cowichan First Nation.
