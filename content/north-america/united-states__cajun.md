## Overview

The Cajuns, also known as Louisiana Acadians, are an American ethnic group mainly found in Louisiana and the surrounding Gulf Coast states. Their historical roots lie among Acadian exiles who settled in Louisiana after the Great Upheaval, although Cajun communities also include people of Irish, Spanish, German, Italian, Native American, African, Afro-Latin Creole, Filipino, and Cuban ancestry. Most Cajuns historically resided in Acadiana, especially in southwestern Louisiana; Cajun populations are also found southwest of New Orleans, in Southern Mississippi, and in parts of Southeast Texas. Their language is Louisiana French, also commonly called Cajun French, and their folk culture is especially associated with music, dance, cuisine, and regional foodways.

## Music & performance

Cajun music, or *Musique cadienne*, is a traditional Louisiana genre rooted in the ballads of French-speaking Acadians in Canada. It is associated especially with southwest Louisiana and is often discussed alongside the Creole-based zydeco music; both traditions share French and African origins.

The accordion is central to Cajun music. Other named instruments include the fiddle, metal triangle, clarinet, piano, steel guitar, bass guitar, drum kit, rhythm guitar, acoustic guitar, electric guitar, keyboard, and washboard. The diatonic accordion became important in the mid- to late-1800s because its volume, together with the fiddle, provided accompaniment for dancing. Early musicians and singers performed at house dance parties called *bals de maison*, at small gatherings on front porches, and at public dances in halls known as *fais do-dos*.

The earliest form was the unaccompanied ballad, with narrative themes including death, solitude, ill-fated love, exile, frontier hardship, celebrations of love, and humorous tales. Early songs combined *la la*, contra dances, reels, and jigs, and were originally sung entirely in Cajun French. Named historical styles include Traditional Cajun music before 1930, Country and Texas swing Cajun music, Fais do-dos music from 1946–1960, the Cajun “renaissance” from the 1970s onward, and Contemporary Cajun music from the 1980s onward.

## Dance & theatre

The sources describe several Cajun dances: a Cajun one-step, also called a Cajun jig; a Cajun two-step, also called a Cajun Jitterbug; and a Cajun Waltz. Cajun dancers cover the dance floor, while zydeco dancers primarily move within a smaller area. Cajun music is performed at Louisiana festivals, dance halls, and weddings in Acadiana.

Public Cajun dance halls are known as *fais do-dos*. The expression may have developed from bringing children to dance halls to lull them to sleep; in French, *fais do-do* means “go to sleep.”

## Festivals & rituals

Cajun music and dance are associated with Louisiana festivals, public dance halls, weddings in Acadiana, house parties, and funerals. Ballads were sung at weddings and funerals, while informal performances took place for small groups as food was prepared and children played.

The sources name the **Tribute to Cajun Music** festival, created by CODOFIL in 1974 and later renamed the **Festival de Musique Acadienne**. Its purpose was to revive interest in Cajun music and culture among younger generations. The sources also mention the **Congrès Mondial Acadien**, a large gathering of Acadians and Cajuns held every five years since 1994, and the 1955 commemoration in Nova Scotia of the 200th anniversary of the expulsion, attended by a group of Cajuns led by State Senator Dudley LeBlanc.

The **crawfish boil** is a celebratory food event involving crawfish, potatoes, onions, and corn cooked in large pots. A **family boucherie** is a traditional Cajun outdoor event in rural Acadiana where family and friends socialize, play games, dance, drink, and prepare a meal centered on a slaughtered hog. Men traditionally slaughter and butcher the hog and cook the main pork dishes, while women make boudin.

## Foodways

Cajun cuisine, or *cuisine cadienne*, is a Louisiana cooking tradition developed by Cajuns and incorporating Native American, West African, French, and Spanish elements. It is described as a rustic cuisine based on locally available ingredients and simple preparation. Cajuns historically cooked dishes such as gumbo in one pot.

Common ingredients include crawfish, shrimp, and andouille sausage. Green bell pepper, onion, and celery are known as the “trinity” or “holy trinity.” Other seasonings include parsley, bay leaf, thyme, green onions, cayenne pepper, and black pepper. A roux made from wheat flour and fat is a foundation for dishes including gumbo, étouffée, and sauce piquante. Cajun cooks also use rice, pork, sausage, seafood, wild game, freshwater fish, vegetables, and locally grown produce.

Named dishes include **boudin**, **gumbo**, **jambalaya**, and **rice and gravy**. Boudin is a sausage made with pork, rice, green onions, and seasonings; versions may contain crawfish. Gumbo reflects French, Spanish, African, and Native American influences and may use okra or filé as a thickener. Jambalaya contains rice, meat, seafood, and available vegetables; Cajun-style “brown” jambalaya draws color and flavor from browned meat and caramelized onions. Rice and gravy is prepared with meat and brown gravy served over rice.

Cooking methods include barbecuing, baking, grilling, braising, boiling, deep frying, smothering, pan-frying, injecting, and stewing. Seafood boils are large social events, and crawfish may be eaten by hand.

## Oral tradition & literature

Cajun oral tradition is represented in narrative ballads, songs, family memories, and spoken Louisiana French. The earliest Cajun songs were unaccompanied ballads concerning death, solitude, ill-fated love, exile, frontier hardship, love, and humorous tales. They were sung at weddings, funerals, house parties, and other small gatherings. Early lyrics were entirely in Cajun French, although some later Cajun music was sung in English.

The sources name the Cajun student protest declaration from 1968, “We’re slaves to a system. Throw away the shackles... and be free with your brother,” and describe the recovery of ethnic pride through French-language and cultural preservation efforts. CODOFIL, founded in 1968, promoted the preservation of French language and culture in Louisiana. The sources also mention French-language websites, podcasts, multimedia platforms, web gazettes, and poetry as contemporary forms of Louisiana French cultural expression.

## Language & religion

Louisiana French is the variety of French traditionally spoken by French Louisianians in former colonial Lower Louisiana. In the twenty-first century, its dialects are used primarily in Louisiana, especially in the southern parishes. It incorporates words of African, Spanish, Native American, and English origin and remains mutually intelligible with other French dialects. The terms Louisiana French and Louisiana Regional French are generally regarded by linguists and anthropologists as more inclusive than Cajun French, although Cajun French remains common in everyday speech.

Louisiana French is spoken across ethnic and racial lines by people who identify as Cajuns and Creoles as well as Chitimacha, Houma, Biloxi, Tunica, Choctaw, Acadians, and French Indians. The sources identify it as endangered and describe the decline caused by English-only schooling, punishment for speaking French, and broader Americanization. CODOFIL was established in 1968 to promote its preservation.

The Cajuns’ historical religion is Roman Catholicism. The sources state that Bernardo de Gálvez permitted Acadians to maintain their native religion, and that Roman Catholicism was also the official religion of Spain.
