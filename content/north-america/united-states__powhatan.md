## Overview

The Powhatan people are an Indigenous people of the Northeastern Woodlands whose historic territories were in central Tidewater Virginia along the James River. They were the leading member of the Powhatan Confederacy in Tsenacommacah. The Powhatan people spoke Powhatan, an Eastern Algonquian language within the Algonquian language family. Their society was governed locally by a Weroance or Weroansqua and was also subject to the leadership of the Confederacy’s Mamanatowick. At the time of English contact, the Powhatan were connected with a larger political organization that included more than 30 groups by 1607. Their history is closely associated with Jamestown, English colonization, warfare, territorial dispossession, assimilation, and efforts by descendants to revive the dormant Powhatan language.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources used mention that Powhatan women produced clothing and furnished homes. The sources also state that Powhatan women cut hair. No further description of everyday or ceremonial dress is provided.

### Architecture

Powhatan houses were called *yehakins*. They were constructed by bending saplings and covering them with woven mats or bark. The Powhatan capital was called Powhatan or *Paqwachowng*, literally “town at the rapids,” and was located near the waterfalls called Paqwachowng in the vicinity of Richmond, Virginia. The people also inhabited at least three other towns, including a village on Mayo Island in the James River that John Smith called Pawatahs Towre or Powhatan Town.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that houses were covered with woven mats or bark and that bedding materials could be stored during the daytime. The sources also describe the use of fire for heating sleeping rooms and clearing land.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They state only that Powhatan women cut hair and produced clothing.

## Music & performance

The sources state that Powhatan people celebrated after a long day by dancing and singing. These activities also allowed them to release tensions associated with work. No Powhatan musical instruments, song genres, performance texts, or named musical traditions are identified.

The sources describe a social economy in which men were soldiers and hunters, while women were farmers and gatherers. Women formed work parties to complete tasks efficiently, and the domestic economy depended on the labor of both sexes. Extended hunting required women to construct hunting camps ahead of the men.

Powhatan religious practice included prayer and offerings at sunrise. People also prayed and made offerings to particular spirits believed to ensure a good harvest. The sources identify Powhatan priests as having the major duty of controlling the weather.

## Dance & theatre

The sources state that Powhatan people danced and sang after work as a form of celebration and as a way of releasing tension. No distinction between ceremonial and entertainment dance is given beyond the association of dancing with communal celebration.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that Powhatan people made offerings and prayed at sunrise and also prayed to specific spirits associated with a good harvest. The sources describe religion as Native and state that Powhatan priests had responsibility for controlling the weather. The available material instead connects agricultural practice with maize cultivation, fishing, hunting, offerings, and prayers for the harvest.

## Foodways

The Powhatan primarily supported themselves by growing crops, especially maize. They also fished and hunted in the forests of their territory. Their agricultural system is described as Three Sisters agriculture. Fields were cleared by felling, girdling, or firing trees at the base, after which fire was used to reduce slash and stumps. When local soil, fish, and game resources declined, inhabitants moved on so that the area could recover. They mention that women pounded corn into meals and gathered nuts. The Powhatan had rich fishing grounds, and bison had migrated into the area by the early 15th century.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources identify Henry Spelman as the author of a rare firsthand account of Powhatan life. They also state that John Smith and William Strachey recorded Powhatan vocabulary. Smith recorded about 50 words, while Strachey recorded about 500 words. These records constitute the principal documentary evidence for the Powhatan language.

The Powhatan language did not have a writing system. Its surviving documentation consists of writings from the 17th century and reconstruction based partly on related Algonquian languages. Strachey’s material was collected between 1610 and 1611 and probably written up in 1612 and 1613. Smith’s material was collected between 1607 and 1609 and published in 1612 and again in 1624. They do describe modern language-revival efforts by descendants of the Powhatan Confederacy and work with younger generations to learn the language.

## Language & religion

Powhatan, also called Virginia Algonquian, was an Eastern Algonquian language within the Algonquian language family. It is also classified as an Algic language. The language was formerly spoken by the Powhatan people of Tidewater Virginia. Its use became dormant after widespread deaths and social disruption, and it had likely vanished by 1790. Much vocabulary was forgotten.

The documentary record consists of two short wordlists. William Strachey recorded about 500 words, and Captain John Smith recorded about 50 words. Powhatan had no writing system. Frank Thomas Siebert, Jr. reconstructed aspects of its phonology, and attempts have been made to revive the language using the wordlists and better-documented Algonquian languages. The sources state that tribes today are working with a newer generation on learning it.

Powhatan religious practice involved sunrise prayer, offerings, spirits associated with a good harvest, and priests whose major duty was controlling the weather.
