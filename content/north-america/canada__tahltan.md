## Overview

The Tahltan, also called the Nahani or Nahanni, are a First Nations people of the Athabaskan-speaking ethnolinguistic group living in northern British Columbia, Canada, around Telegraph Creek, Dease Lake, and Iskut. They constitute the fourth division of the Nahane, or “People of the West.” Contemporary Tahltan society constitutes itself as the Tahltan Nation, comprising the membership and governments of the Tahltan First Nation and Iskut First Nation. Tahltan cultural practices and lifeways varied widely because communities were separated and faced differing local conditions. Their cultural record includes matrilineal social organization, oral histories concerning ancestors and Raven, the use of Mount Edziza obsidian, and efforts to revitalize a critically endangered Northern Athabaskan language.

## Material culture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources identify obsidian as an important material object in Tahltan history. Ten thousand years ago, the Tahltan people used obsidian from Mount Edziza to make tools and weapons for trading material; this is described as the main source of obsidian found in northwestern British Columbia.

## Music & performance

The Tahltan language article does record a digital archive of Tahltan recordings, located at the Tahltan Language Revitalization Offices in Dease Lake, Iskut, and Telegraph Creek, and states that these recordings can be used on iPods.

## Festivals & rituals

Tahltan oral history is connected to the Sacred Headwaters, also called the Klappan Valley, where the headwaters of the Nass, Skeena, and Stikine Rivers are located. The source states that Tahltan oral history holds these headwaters to be the place where the earth was first created and where Tahltan culture began.

## Foodways

The Sacred Headwaters are described as providing a home to important salmon stocks.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tahltan cultural knowledge includes accounts of ancestors who lived before a great flood. According to the source, some ancestors possessed knowledge that others did not; some used it for the good of the people, while others used it for evil and to the disadvantage of others. Raven is considered the protagonist hero opposing these evil ancestors.

Tahltan oral history also identifies the Sacred Headwaters, or Klappan Valley, as the place where the earth was first created and where Tahltan culture began. Contemporary preservation efforts include language-immersion classes, a Mentor-Apprentice program, language nests for young children, scholarships for part-time language learners, a digital archive of recordings, and an English-Tahltan dictionary.

## Language & religion

Tahltan is a poorly documented and endangered Northern Athabaskan language spoken in northern British Columbia around Telegraph Creek, Dease Lake, and Iskut. It is also called *Tāłtān*, *Tałtan ẕāke* (“Tahltan people language”), *dah dẕāhge* (“our language”), and *didene keh* (“this people’s way”). The language article describes Tahltan as critically endangered and states that several linguists classify it as a dialect of the same language as Tagish and Kaska. Another account describes Tahltan as having three divergent but mutually intelligible dialects, while other linguists consider these separate languages.

The sources give differing speaker figures: one article states that Tahltan was spoken by 235 of the Tahltan people, while a May 2013 estimate gave 30 Tahltan speakers. The broader language discussion lists approximately 400 Kaska speakers, approximately 35 Tahltan speakers, and no fluent Tagish speakers as of 2008. Tahltan has 47 consonant phonemes and 9 vowels, with vowel flattening, consonant harmony, vowel nasalization, and vowel laxing described as phonological processes.
