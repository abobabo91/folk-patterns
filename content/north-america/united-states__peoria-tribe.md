## Overview

The Peoria are a Native American people enrolled in the federally recognized Peoria Tribe of Indians of Oklahoma, headquartered in Miami, Oklahoma. Their tribal jurisdictional area is in Ottawa County, in the northeast corner of the state. The Peoria are remnants of the nations that constituted the Illinois Confederation and traditionally lived in areas now within Illinois, Ohio, Michigan, and Missouri. They are Algonquian-speaking people whose ancestors experienced colonial trade, missionary activity, forced relocation, intertribal conflict, infectious disease, land cessions, allotment, termination, and restoration of federal recognition. Of 3,713 enrolled tribal citizens, approximately 777 live within Oklahoma. Their history is also connected with the Cahokia, Kaskaskia, Michigamea, Moingwena, Piankeshaw, Tamaroa, and Wea peoples.

## Music & performance

The Peoria are associated in the sources with French Jesuit missionary activity and conversion of tribal citizens to Roman Catholicism, but no Peoria musical instruments, song genres, performance contexts, or musical traditions are described. The sources mention Father Jacques Marquette, Louis Jolliet, and Father Jacques Gravier in connection with early French exploration and missionary work. Father Jacques Gravier compiled a bilingual dictionary of Kaskaskia Illinois and corresponding French terms, described as nearly 600 pages and 20,000 entries. This linguistic documentation is not presented as a record of Peoria music. The Peoria Tribe owns the Peoria Gaming Center, Buffalo Run Casino and Hotel, Joe’s Outback, one casino, and the Peoria Ridge Golf Course.

## Dance & theatre

The sources identify Moscelyne Larkin as a Peoria/Shawnee ballerina, but provide no further account of ballet or of a Peoria dance tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that French Jesuit missionaries converted tribal citizens to Roman Catholicism. The sources do describe major political and historical events, including the 1849 formation of a confederacy under the Peoria name, the 1854 treaty recognizing the Confederated Peoria, the 1867 Omnibus Treaty, the 1959 loss of federal recognition, and the 1978 restoration of federal recognition; these are historical developments rather than ritual descriptions.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record substantial linguistic documentation associated with the Illinois language: Father Jacques Gravier compiled a bilingual dictionary of Kaskaskia Illinois and French terms, with nearly 600 pages and 20,000 entries. The sources also state that revitalization efforts for the Peoria Language began in August 2022 through a 10-week online course offered by the tribe. No Peoria literary revival, oral-history project, or named narrative tradition is described.

## Language & religion

The Peoria speak a dialect of the Miami–Illinois language, a Central Algonquian language whose two dialects are mutually intelligible. The name Peoria, also recorded as Peouaroua, derives from their Illinois autonym *peewaareewa*, with the modern pronunciation *peewaalia*. The source explains that the original meaning was “Comes carrying a pack on his back.” No native speakers of the Peoria language survive. Revitalization efforts began in August 2022 with a 10-week online course offered by the tribe. Historic Peoria citizens also spoke related Algonquian languages of Cahokia, Moingwea, and Tamaroa. French Jesuit missionaries converted tribal citizens to Roman Catholicism.
