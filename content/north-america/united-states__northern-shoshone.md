## Overview

The Northern Shoshone are Shoshone of the Snake River Plain of southern Idaho and the northeastern Great Basin, where Idaho, Wyoming and Utah meet. They are culturally affiliated with the Bannock people and belong to the Great Basin classification of Indigenous People. Their communities and historic bands were associated with the Snake, Salmon, Bear, Logan, Boise, Payette, Bruneau, Weiser and Owyhee rivers, as well as the Sawtooth, Lemhi, Beaverhead and Wind River ranges. Northern Shoshone is a dialect of Shoshone, a Central Numic language in the Uto-Aztecan family. Their culture is documented through adaptive subsistence, trade, clothing, basketry, hide work, warfare, and oral traditions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Beadwork and embroidery** — Women used bow-shaped or rectangular looms for beadwork. Quillwork and beadwork included characteristic stitches, and some pieces had Dakota-like ridged bead bands made by stringing and sewing rows of beads in alternating directions.

**Basketry** — Baskets were made by coiling or twining. Forms included gathering baskets, water-bottles, roasting trays, hats, fans and gambling trays. Cordage techniques included specific warp twisting, coiled borders and tight pitching that made water-bottles watertight.

**Rush mats** — Mats made from rushes were used for sleeping and transport. Rolled rush mats also formed part of the interior equipment of houses.

**Hide preparation** — Buffalo, elk and deer hides were tanned through prolonged soaking, brain or boiling treatments, scalping with elk-horn or rib scrapers, wringing and smoothing on cords or frames. Smoking finished and protected the hides.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no specific textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Clothing commonly consisted of blanket robes made from buffalo, antelope, deer and bighorn hides; beaver and wolf were also used. Men wore shirts made from dressed deer, antelope or elk skins, with fringed sides and decoration in quillwork or beadwork. Women wore similarly ornamented chemises closed at the sides. Tippets and collars made from otter or ermine were elaborately constructed and decorated with shell. Men commonly wore two front braids decorated with skins and feathers. Fans were made from eagle feathers with red cloth and bells. Face and body paint were used for dances and war, while painted faces, feathered bonnets and standards formed part of prairie war customs.

### Architecture

By the time of Robert H. Lowie’s visit, dwellings consisted of log cabins. Summer shade-lodges were rectangular structures with brush roofs, used for daily life and organized around a central fireplace. Smaller shade enclosures, conical menstrual huts and small dome sweat-lodge-like structures were also commonly used. House interiors had foliage flooring, skins for seats and beds, rolled rush mats for sleeping and transport, rawhide bags and parfleches for storage. During warfare, people used simple semi-cylindrical shelters made from willow frames and covered with blankets for mobility during campaigns.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Material culture included stone, bone and shell tools. Knives and arrow points were made from obsidian; awls, salmon-gigs and some game implements were made from bone; antlers and ribs served as scrapers for hide work. Drinking cups and spoons were made from mountain-sheep or buffalo horn. Steatite, or soapstone, vessels and possibly lava cooking pots were used. Rawhide bags and parfleches provided storage, while mats, baskets and water-bottles supported sleeping, transport, gathering and food preparation.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Shells, including abalone obtained through trade from coastal tribes, were used for personal decoration. Bead necklaces were preceded by salmon vertebrae or shells. Women and children wore elk-tooth necklaces, while bear-claw necklaces marked that the wearer had killed a grizzly. Quillwork, beadwork and shell ornamentation decorated clothing and collars. Women practiced limited tattooing or puncturing, using soot or grease. Men commonly decorated two front braids with skins and feathers. Face and body paint were used for dances and war.

## Music & performance

They state that face and body paint were used for dances and war, and that fans made from eagle feathers, red cloth and bells formed part of material and display practices. The sources also describe painted faces, feathered bonnets and standards in warfare, together with ritual shield-making ceremonies intended to provide protective power. No further musical contexts, such as weddings, funerals, court performances or public festivals, are covered by the sources.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention dances only in connection with face and body paint. Feathered bonnets, painted faces, standards and fans with bells are documented as elements of display, especially in relation to war and dance.

## Festivals & rituals

They document ritual shield-making ceremonies associated with warfare, in which shields were prepared to provide protective power. The origin story also presents Coyote as the central culture-hero and originator of the Shoshone: after Coyote brings water, women wash babies, and the first Shoshone are set apart from the other peoples. They record that the Cache Valley Shoshone were practically wiped out at the Bear River Massacre on January 29, 1863, after which survivors settled on the Fort Hall Reservation.

## Foodways

The subsistence economy consisted of intensive hunting of bison, elk, deer and small game, together with fishing. Band names were associated with primary food sources, including *Agaideka* or *Agai-deka*, the Salmon Eaters; *Tukudeka* or *Tuku-deka*, the Sheep Eaters or Mountain Sheep Eaters; *Kammitikka*, *Kamu-deka* or *Kamodika*, the Jack Rabbit Eaters or Black Tailed Rabbit Eaters; *Painkwitikka* or *Pengwideka*, the Fish Eaters; and *Hukundüka* or *Hukan-deka*, the Porcupine Grass Seed Eaters or Wild Wheat Eaters. The sources mention roasting trays, cooking pots and water-bottles.

## Oral tradition & literature

The central origin story concerns Coyote, who is described as the culture-hero and originator of the Shoshone. After Coyote departs, babies begin to fall from women’s wombs in great numbers. Women wash most of them with water brought by Coyote, while Coyote himself washes the babies who become the first Shoshone. He tells them, “you are my children, I am going to stay with you.” The story explains the distinct position of the Shoshone and their conflicts with other tribes. An alternative episode says that Coyote married the daughter of an old woman, and their descendants became the Shoshone.

## Language & religion

Northern Shoshone is a dialect of the Shoshone language, a Central Numic language in the Uto-Aztecan language family. It is primarily spoken on the Fort Hall and Wind River reservations in Idaho and Wyoming. The sources record several Shoshone names and expressions, including *Seuhubeogoi*, “Willow Valley,” and *Shewoki*, *si.wo.kki?i* or *Su:woki*, “willow-striped” or “Row of Willows.” They document Coyote as a culture-hero and originator in the Shoshone origin story.
