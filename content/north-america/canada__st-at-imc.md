## Overview

The Stʼatʼimc, also known as the Lillooet, are an Interior Salish people of the southern Whale Mountains and Fraser Canyon region of British Columbia. Their communities are located around the Fraser River, Seton Lake, Anderson Lake, Lillooet, Mount Currie, the Pemberton Valley, and the Lillooet River. The population is given as about 6259. The ancestral language, Lillooet, belongs to the Interior Salish group and is also known as Stʼatʼimcets or Ucwalmícwts. Stʼatʼimc culture displays features associated with Northwest Coast peoples, including the potlatch, clan names, mythology, prestige attached to wealth and generosity, and totem poles in some communities. Salmon and other fish historically formed the basis of the economy, while hunting, trapping, and gathering supplemented them.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The Stʼatʼimc had several kinds of dwellings: long plank houses, winter earthlodges, and summer lodges covered with bark or mats. These forms are described as resembling those at the Keatley Creek Archaeological Site. The Upper Stʼatʼimc settled in communities on the banks above the Fraser River and beside Seton and Anderson lakes, while Lower Stʼatʼimc communities were located along the Lillooet River and around the Pemberton Valley.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that salmon and other fish were central to the economy, and that animals were hunted and trapped while berries and fruit were gathered.

## Music & performance

The Lillooet language article includes a story told by Rosie Joseph of Mount Currie, presented in Lillooet with an English translation. The passage concerns Máma going to a roothouse for food, taking a bucket, gathering potatoes, catching what she thinks is a mouse, and discovering that it is a rotten potato. No instrument or musical genre is identified.

## Dance & theatre

They mention mythology, the potlatch, and totem poles in some communities, especially the Lilʼwat First Nation.

## Festivals & rituals

They identify the potlatch as a feature of Stʼatʼimc culture and mention mythology.

The historical record describes warfare and peacemaking. The Tsilhqot’in raided all 11 bands of the Stʼatʼimc and took women and children as slaves. The two nations met at many roots, identified as Graveyard Valley, where the Stʼatʼimc were victorious. Chief In-Kick-Tee, also called Hunter Jack, was the warchief in that battle and made a peace treaty in 1845.

The Declaration of the Lillooet Tribe was made in 1911 in Spences Bridge. It expressed the nation’s ownership of lands seized by non-native settlers at Seton Portage at the beginning of the 20th century and was described as a general statement concerning ownership of the traditional territories of Stʼatʼimcets-speaking peoples.

## Foodways

Salmon and other fish were the basis of the Stʼatʼimc economy. Hunting and trapping provided bear, sheep, caribou, deer, and small mammals, while berries and fruit were gathered.

The Lillooet-language sample text describes a roothouse as a place from which Máma took food. She carried a bucket and gathered potatoes; after catching what she believed was a mouse, she discovered that the object was a rotten potato.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources document mythology and storytelling. A sample narrative in the Lillooet language was told by Rosie Joseph of Mount Currie and concerns Máma, a roothouse, potatoes, a bucket, and a mistaken mouse.

The bibliography lists Lillooet legends and stories by Martina Larochell, Jan P. van Eijk, and Lorna Williams, published by Ts’zil Publishing House in 1981. It also lists works concerning Lillooet language teaching and documentation, including curricula by Marie Joseph, Jan P. van Eijk, Lorna Williams, and Gordon Turner. These sources indicate language preservation and teaching efforts.

## Language & religion

Lillooet, also called Stʼatʼimcets, St̓át̓imcets, Sƛ̓áƛ̓imxəc, or Ucwalmícwts in the relevant communities, is an Interior Salish language within the Salishan family. Upper Lillooet is associated with Fountain, Pavilion, Lillooet, and neighboring areas; Lower Lillooet is associated with Mount Currie and neighboring areas. The language article identifies two main dialects, Upper/Northern Lillooet and Lower/Southern Lillooet, and also mentions a Skookumchuck subdialect.

Lillooet is described as critically endangered, with around 120 fluent speakers and 393 semi-speakers. In 2022, 1092 people were reported to be learning the language. The Clao7alcw, or “Raven’s Nest,” language nest program at Mount Currie is conducted in Lillooet.
