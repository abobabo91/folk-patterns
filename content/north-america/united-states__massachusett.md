## Overview

The Massachusett were an Indigenous tribe of the Northeastern Woodlands whose historic homeland encompassed much of present-day Greater Boston in eastern Massachusetts, especially the watersheds of the Charles River and Neponset River and the coast of Massachusetts Bay. Their communities lived in politically autonomous villages led by sachems. They combined maize, beans and squash cultivation with fishing, shellfishing, hunting and gathering wild plants. They spoke Massachusett, an Eastern Algonquian language also used by neighboring Indigenous communities. Epidemics, English settlement, warfare, colonial guardianship and the alienation of communal lands disrupted their society. Descendants continue to live in eastern Massachusetts, including the Massachusett Tribe at Ponkapoag, although contemporary organizations do not have federal or Massachusetts state recognition.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state that inhabitants of the Praying towns were forced to adopt Puritan habits of modesty, hairstyle, dress and other cultural norms.

### Architecture

Massachusett families lived in domed houses called *wétu* in Massachusett. Their curved wooden support beams were covered with woven mats in winter or chestnut bark in summer. Possessions were stored in hemp dogbane bags and baskets of all sizes. The sources also describe Massachusett communities as conditional sedentary villages built along rivers. Natick had a Christian-style church in which services were conducted in Massachusett, with Indian preachers and parishioners called by Native drumming.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do record clamshell hoes used by women in cultivation, carved wooden bowls and spoons made by men, hemp dogbane bags, baskets of all sizes, and carved dugout and birchbark canoes used for water transport.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They mention changes to hairstyle and dress imposed in the Praying towns.

## Music & performance

The sources provide only limited information about Massachusett music and performance. Services at Natick’s Christian-style church were conducted in Massachusett with Indian preachers, and parishioners were called by Native drumming. The Massachusett Psalter included sung versions of the Massachusett translations of the Psalms of David. The sources also state that traditional medicines and rituals conducted by healers known as *powwow* (*pawâwak*) failed to protect some people from settler encroachment and newly introduced pathogens.

## Festivals & rituals

They describe religious and political changes associated with Christian conversion, the Praying towns and colonial rule. John Eliot preached to Massachusett communities, and many people joined Christian communities at Natick and Ponkapoag. Natick was established in 1651 and Ponkapoag followed shortly afterward in 1654; these and other settlements became known as Praying towns, or *Peantamwe Otanash* (*Puyôhtamwee 8tânash*). Their inhabitants were required to observe the eight tenements of the “Leaf of Rules” distributed in the Bibles, which forbade certain Indian cultural norms and reinforced Puritan modesty and hairstyles.

## Foodways

Massachusett subsistence combined the cultivation of maize, beans and squash with fishing, shellfishing, hunting and gathering wild plants. Women cultivated northern flint corn, called *weachimineash* in Massachusett, together with beans, squashes and pumpkins. Corn was planted in mounds, beans grew up the cornstalks, and cucurbits protected roots and discouraged weeds; the sources call this companion planting method the Three Sisters. Other plant foods included grapes, strawberries, blackberries, currants, cherries, plums, raspberries, acorns, hickory nuts, chestnuts, butternuts, leafy greens and pseudocereals such as chenopods. *Nasaump* was a cornmeal porridge. Men and women cleared fields by burning trees and removing stumps, while women used clamshell hoes. The sources describe customary cuisine and foraging and hunting among the Praying Indians.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Massachusett-language translation of the Bible produced through John Eliot’s mission became an important early work of Indigenous-language printing in colonial North America. A 1663 translation using the Natick dialect, known as *Mamusse Wunneetupanatamwe Up-Biblum God*, was the first printed in the Americas and one of the earliest Bible translations into a previously unwritten language. Literacy was taught by Native American ministers and teachers, and written records included court petitions, church records, Praying town administrative records, notes on book margins, personal letters and religious tracts. Language revitalization began in 1993 when Jessie Little Doe Baird launched the Wôpanâak Language Reclamation Project, which reintroduced the revived Wampanoag dialect to the Mashpee, Aquinnah, Assonet and Herring Pond communities.

## Language & religion

Massachusett is an Algonquian language of the Algic language family and belongs to the Eastern branch of Algonquian languages. It was historically spoken by several peoples of eastern coastal and southeastern Massachusetts and functioned in simplified pidgin form as a regional language of trade and intertribal communication. The language was also known as Natick or Wôpanâak, and historically as Pokanoket, Indian or Nonantum. Its traditional name was *hettꝏonk* (*hutuwôk*), “that which they [can] speak to each other.” Massachusett ceased to be the predominant language of the community by the 1750s and likely went extinct as a community language by the nineteenth century. Christianity spread through the work of John Eliot, but the sources describe mixed responses, including continued traditional practices, *powwow* healing and secret crypto-traditional practices alongside Christian observance.
