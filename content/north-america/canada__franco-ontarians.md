## Overview

Franco-Ontarians are Francophone Canadians who reside in Ontario. The term can refer either to francophone residents of Ontario, regardless of ethnicity or birthplace, or to people of French Canadian ancestry born in Ontario, regardless of their primary language or current residence. In 2021, the Government of Ontario counted 652,540 Francophones in the province. Most Franco-Ontarians live in Eastern Ontario, Northeastern Ontario, and the Golden Horseshoe, while smaller francophone communities are found in Essex, Penetanguishene, Welland, and elsewhere. Eastern Ontario includes Cornwall, Hawkesbury, Ottawa, and Pembroke; Northeastern Ontario has the highest proportion of francophones. Franco-Ontarians constitute the largest French-speaking community in Canada outside Quebec and have developed a distinct cultural identity since the late 1960s and 1970s.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document Franco-Ontarian Day, designated by the Ontario government on April 26, 2010, and observed on September 25. The date represents the anniversary of the official raising of the Franco-Ontarian flag in 1975. The sources also describe public and political actions connected with language rights, including the C'est l'temps civil disobedience movement, which emerged in 1975, and the first French-language provincial court proceeding, authorized in 1976. These are described as political and linguistic developments rather than religious or life-cycle rites.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do mention Quebec writer Yves Beauchemin, who controversially described the Franco-Ontarian community as “warm corpses” (« cadavres encore chauds »), and former Quebec Premier René Lévesque, who referred to them as “dead ducks.” These statements belong to political and sociological commentary about the community’s survival rather than to a documented oral tradition.

## Language & religion

Franco-Ontarians are associated with the French language. The sources describe French as an official language of Ontario’s education system, judiciary, and legislature, while the French Language Services Act of 1986 recognized it as a “historic language of Ontario” and required French services in designated communities and regions with a significant Franco-Ontarian population. In June 2009, Ontario expanded its definition of a francophone to include a person whose mother tongue is French, or a person with another mother tongue who uses French as the primary language at home. The majority of Franco-Ontarians are bilingual in French and English; in 2016, 40,045 respondents reported proficiency only in French and limited or no knowledge of English.
