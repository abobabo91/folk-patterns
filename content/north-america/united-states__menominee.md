## Overview

The Menominee, officially the Menominee Indian Tribe of Wisconsin, are a federally recognized Native American tribe whose reservation is in northeastern Wisconsin, largely conterminous with Menominee County and the town of Menominee. Their historic territory extended across approximately 10 million acres in Wisconsin and the Upper Peninsula of Michigan. The tribe currently has about 8,700 members. The Menominee language belongs to the Algonquian language family and is critically endangered, with most fluent speakers elderly. Menominee oral history locates their Creation where the Menominee River enters Green Bay of Lake Michigan, near the present-day city of Marinette, Wisconsin. Their folk-culture significance in the sources lies especially in oral tradition, clan organization, wild-rice and sturgeon foodways, spiritual rites, language preservation, and sustainable forestry.

## Festivals & rituals

Traditional Menominee spiritual culture includes rites of passage for youth at puberty. These ceremonies involve fasting for multiple days and living in a small isolated wigwam. During the transition, youths meet individually with Elders, who interpret their dreams and provide information about adult responsibilities following the rites of passage.

The sources also state that feasts are still held annually at which wild rice and sturgeon are served. Wild rice has special importance as a staple grain, while sturgeon has mythological importance and is often called the “father” of the Menominee.

## Foodways

Menominee traditional subsistence included a wide variety of plants and animals. Wild rice, called *manōmen* in Menominee, was cultivated and gathered as one of the most important staple foods. Sturgeon, *namāēw*, was another major food and also possessed mythological importance. Annual feasts continue to serve both wild rice and sturgeon.

The sources record several other local foods and plant uses. *Pekuac-sekākūhsyah* refers to ramps or wild garlic, identified as *Allium tricoccum*. Potatoes of *Sagittaria cuneata*, called *wāhpesohpāēnyak*, were boiled, sliced, strung together, and dried for winter use. The inner bark of *Abies balsamea*, *ōkekāhtek*, was used as a seasoner for medicines; an infusion of the inner bark was taken for chest pain, while liquid balsam pressed from the trunk was used for colds and pulmonary troubles.

## Oral tradition & literature

Menominee oral history states that the people have always been present in their homeland and identifies them as *Kiash Matchitiwuk*, “Ancient Ones.” According to the tradition recorded in the sources, their Creation occurred where the Menominee River enters Green Bay of Lake Michigan, and their reservation lies 60 miles west of that site.

Sturgeon is described as having mythological importance and as being referred to as the “father” of the Menominee. The Menominee language uses a quotative mode for statements learned from another person or from a dream or vision; this is also the mode used in traditional narrative.

Language preservation is active. The Menominee Language & Culture Commission promotes continued use of the language, while residents of the Menominee reservation at Keshena have held intensive classes and worked with linguists from the University of Wisconsin–Madison to document the language and develop curriculum and learning materials. A dictionary project led by Monica Macaulay has produced modern dictionaries supporting revitalization.

## Language & religion

Menominee is an Algonquian language and is critically endangered. It is also called *omāēqnomenēweqnaesen*, *omāēqnomenēweqnaesewen*, or *omāēqnomenēw kīketwan*. The language is characterized in the sources by extensive use of the low front vowel /æ/, rich negation morphology, and a distinctive lexicon. Many people use English as their first language. A 1997 report recorded 39 first-language speakers, all elderly, 26 second-language speakers, and 65 others who had learned some of the language.

Traditional Menominee spiritual culture includes puberty rites, fasting, isolation in a wigwam, meetings with Elders, and dream interpretation.
