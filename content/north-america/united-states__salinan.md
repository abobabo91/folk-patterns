## Overview

The Salinan are an Indigenous people of California whose ancestral territory lies in the southern Salinas Valley and the Santa Lucia Range in the Central Coast of California. The Antoniano lived in the lower Salinas Valley near the future sites of Mission San Antonio de Padua and Mission San Miguel Arcángel; the Miguelino lived along the upper Salinas River and south toward Slates Hot Springs, Junipero Serra Peak, and Soledad; and the Playano lived on the Pacific Coast near what is now Lucia and San Simeon. Salinans were hunter-gatherers organized in small groups with little centralized political structure. Their language was a language isolate, possibly related to the Hokan family. The 2000 United States census reported 681 Salinan people, while estimates for earlier populations vary.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources describe archaeological occupation. The Wagon Caves rock formation, approximately 18 miles northwest of present-day Jolon, contains rock overhangs and caves with fire-scarred roofs showing occupation over hundreds of years. Researchers believe the site was used by the Salinan Antonianos subtribe, who occupied at least two villages in the area: an older site dating to approximately 450 AD and a later protohistoric site dating from about 1450 to 1650 AD.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Wagon Cave site has yielded a stemmed biface, lithic flakes, shell beads, non-human bones, shell, bone, flaked stone, fire-affected rock, charred seeds, and mortars.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used mention shell beads from the Wagon Cave archaeological site.

## Music & performance

The Salinan language has been recorded in field materials, including Antoniaño Salinan field recordings collected by William H. Jacobsen Jr. and spoken by Elario Quintana and Dave Mora.

## Foodways

Oak acorns were the staple food of the Salinans. The Salinan language source records several terms associated with oak species, while noting that Mason’s attempt to match Salinan terms with scientific species was unsuccessful. The source describes *cxa'uwat`* as a live oak with spined leaves, *t`io'i* as a large white tree with a white acorn growing on the coast, *paxa'kiL* as an oak with pointed leaves and a very large acorn growing on the hills, *p`a'pix* as an oak with serrated leaves, *p`a`t* as an oak with small serrated leaves and a large acorn, and *cmo'* as an oak with smooth, non-spined leaves. It states that acorns from live oaks seem to have been preferred for mush and those of deciduous oaks for bread. The sources also mention multiple Salinan words for acorn.

The Salinans’ summer diet consisted mainly of fish and shellfish, as indicated by shell middens and fine shell particles in the soil at former coastal camps.

## Language & religion

Salinan is the historical Indigenous language of the Salinan people of the central coast of California. It has been extinct since the death of the last speaker in 1958, although the Salinan article states that it was spoken until the 1950s. Two dialects are recognized: Antoniaño and Migueleño, associated respectively with the missions of San Antonio and San Miguel. Antoniaño was also sometimes called Sextapay and was associated with the area of the Franciscan Mission of San Antonio de Padua in Monterey County. A third Playano dialect may have existed, but the source states that little is known of it linguistically apart from a few proper names possibly recorded in mission records.

Salinan is a language isolate and may be part of the Hokan family. Edward Sapir included it in a Hokan subfamily together with Chumash and Seri, although the sources discuss uncertainty and disagreement surrounding that classification.
