## Overview

The Penobscot, who call themselves *Pαnawάhpskewi*, are an Indigenous people of North America’s Northeastern Woodlands and are organized as a federally recognized tribe in Maine. Their main settlement is the Penobscot Indian Island Reservation, located along the Penobscot River. The Penobscot Nation is part of the Wabanaki Confederacy, together with the Abenaki, Passamaquoddy, Wolastoqiyik, and Miꞌkmaq nations. Historically, the Penobscot spoke a dialect of Eastern Abenaki, an Algonquian language. Their folk culture includes seasonal hunting and gathering, basket-making, birchbark-canoe construction, folklore, and a spiritual relationship with the land and living world.

## Material culture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Penobscot material culture in the sources includes baskets and birchbark canoes. Baskets were traditionally made from sweet grass, brown ash, and birch bark, materials found in wetlands throughout Maine. They were originally made for practical use, while after European contact the Penobscot also produced “fancy baskets” for trade with Europeans. Basket-making is traditionally a women’s skill passed down in families, and members of the tribe have learned traditional forms while creating new variations.

The birch bark canoe was once an important means of transportation for the nations of the Wabanaki Confederacy. Each nation made a characteristic canoe shape. Penobscot canoes were made from one piece of bark from a white birch tree; when removed correctly, the bark could be taken without killing the tree. European trade also introduced metal axes, guns, and copper or iron cookware to Penobscot communities.

## Music & performance

 They do identify several Penobscot performers and entertainers. Molly Spotted Elk, also known as Mary Alice “Molly Dellis” Nelson Archambaud, was an internationally known dancer who starred in the film *The Silent Enemy*. Lucy Nicolar Poolaw was an entertainer billed as “Princess Watahwaso.” Horace Nelson was the father of Molly Spotted Elk.

The sources also mention the Penobscot High Stakes Bingo operation, opened on Indian Island in 1973. It was one of the first commercial gambling operations on a reservation in the United States, and bingo was held one weekend every six weeks. This is documented as a social and commercial activity rather than as a musical tradition.

## Dance & theatre

The sources document dance through Molly Spotted Elk, an internationally known Penobscot dancer who starred in *The Silent Enemy*.

The 1825 novel *Brother Jonathan* by the Maine native John Neal has its climax set on Indian Island during the American Revolutionary War. It features a protagonist of mixed Penobscot-English descent and describes the island as “the last encampment of the Penobscot Red men.”

## Festivals & rituals

 They describe a seasonal way of life in which bands moved according to the patterns of game and fish. Men hunted beaver, otters, moose, bears, caribou, fish, seafood, birds, and possibly seals, while women gathered and processed bird eggs, berries, nuts, and roots.

Penobscot spirituality is connected with the land and its resources in Maine. Their cosmology includes folklore, reverence for living things, and a particularly close relationship with the Penobscot River, which is cherished and personified. Their origin story centers on Klose-kur-beh, also called Gluskbe, who provides spiritual and practical knowledge, including knowledge of canoe construction, and teaches ethical precepts through twelve episodes.

## Foodways

 They do describe food acquisition before and during early contact. Penobscot men hunted game, fish, seafood, birds, and possibly marine mammals such as seals. Women gathered and processed bird eggs, berries, nuts, and roots, all of which were found locally.

People on the present-day Maine coast practiced some agriculture, though not to the same extent as Indigenous peoples in southern New England. Food was potentially scarce toward the end of winter, in February and March. During the rest of the year, the Penobscot and other Wabanaki likely had access to the bounty of the land and ocean waters. The sources also state that damming the Penobscot River reduced access to running fish, while clear-cutting forests reduced access to big game.

## Oral tradition & literature

Penobscot folklore teaches that plants and animals were helpers and companions of the people, while people were expected to act as kin and companions to the living world. The sources describe these stories as placing listeners within reciprocal relationships among people, plants, and animals.

The Penobscot origin story centers on Klose-kur-beh, or Gluskbe. He provides humans and animals with practical skills needed to live in the climate of the Northeast and punishes those who act outside his code. His teachings include “spiritual knowledge” and practical knowledge such as how to construct a canoe. The sources state that his teachings include twelve episodes emphasizing distinct values.

The sources also mention Joseph Nicolar, celebrated author of *The Life and Traditions of the Red Man* (1893), as well as contemporary writers, storytellers, and activists including Donna M. Loring, Sherri Mitchell, and ssipsis. The elementary school and the Boys and Girls Club on Indian Island are making an effort to reintroduce the Penobscot language by teaching it to children.

## Language & religion

The Penobscot historically spoke a dialect of Eastern Abenaki, an Algonquian language similar to the languages of other members of the Wabanaki Confederacy. There are no fluent speakers, and the last known Penobscot speaker of Eastern Abenaki, Madeline Tower Shay, died in the 1990s. Frank Siebert compiled a dictionary. The written language was developed with a modified Roman alphabet, including distinct characters for sounds not found in the Roman alphabet.

The French missionaries converted many Penobscot people to Christianity. In the 21st century, some members practice traditional spirituality, while others on Indian Island are Catholic or Protestant. Penobscot cosmology is deeply connected with the Maine landscape, the Penobscot River, animals, plants, and other living things. Klose-kur-beh, or Gluskbe, is the central figure in the origin story and provides both spiritual and practical knowledge.
