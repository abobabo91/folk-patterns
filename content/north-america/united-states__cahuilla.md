## Overview

The Cahuilla, also known as *’Ivilluwenetem*, are a Native American people of the various tribes of the Cahuilla Nation. They live in inland southern California, especially around the Coachella Valley, San Gorgonio Pass, San Jacinto Mountains, Palm Springs, and surrounding desert and mountain areas. Their traditional territory covered about 2,400 square miles and extended between the San Bernardino Mountains, Borrego Springs, the Chocolate Mountains, the Colorado Desert, the San Jacinto Plain, and the eastern slopes of the Palomar Mountains. Cahuilla belongs to the Uto-Aztecan language family and is critically endangered: a 1990 census recorded 35 speakers in an ethnic population of 800. The sources present Cahuilla culture through language, oral narratives, ritual, foodways, basketry, relations to desert plants, and continuing tribal institutions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Palm-leaf basketry* is documented as a Cahuilla use of California fan-palm leaves. The leaves were used for baskets of many shapes, sizes, and purposes, as well as for sandals and roofing thatch.

### Clothing & dress

They state that palm leaves were used to make sandals. The mythology source says that *Kutya’i*, a spirit of wind, steals clothing. The sources also mention that Cahuilla men hunted deer and rabbits.

### Architecture

The Cahuilla used palm leaves as roofing thatch for dwellings. In connection with ritual cremation, they name a large structure called the *kis-amnawut*, translated roughly as “house power-great.”

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They record the use of palm leaves for baskets, sandals, and roofing thatch, and they mention bows and arrows in the creation narrative: Mukat taught his people the art of fighting with a bow and arrow. No catalogue records of museum objects were supplied.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They mention clothing only in the context of the spirit Kutya’i, who steals it.

## Music & performance

The sources mention Cahuilla bird songs and state that traditional bird songs were sung in the Cahuilla language. Alvin Siva preserved these songs by teaching them to younger generations of Cahuilla people. Paul Apodaca and Luke Madrigal are associated in the cited material with “Cahuilla bird songs,” and the language article lists a preservation effort connected with the teaching of Cahuilla to younger generations.

## Dance & theatre

They mention mourning ceremonies, funerary ritual, initiatory rites during puberty, and bird songs.

## Festivals & rituals

Cahuilla mourning ritual was described as a six-night affair marking the passing of a community member and bringing grief to an end. The ritual was held annually during the winter months, with its date fluctuating according to supplies and internal factors. It commonly took place in the *kis-amnawut*, a large structure whose name is translated roughly as “house power-great.” The fourth day of the mourning ritual was associated with the public naming of infants.

The creation narrative attributes mourning ceremonies and a proper funerary ritual by cremation to Mukat, who taught them to the people after his death. Along with initiatory rites during puberty, these practices form the essential corpus of Cahuilla religious observance according to the source. They state that the ritual date fluctuated and do not give a fixed calendar date.

## Foodways

Cahuilla subsistence relied heavily on desert vegetation, while men engaged in hunting deer and rabbits. Mesquite was an important food plant because its deep roots accessed water. During summer, its green bean pods were processed into a beverage. The Cahuilla also used agave and yucca as food sources, and various desert cacti produced edible fruits. Palm fruit was eaten, and seeds were harvested from juniper and pine trees, as well as chia and other plants. Several types of berries were dried and ground into a meal.

Acorns were significant, although oak trees were not prevalent across much of the desert territory because of scarce water and the arid environment.

## Oral tradition & literature

Cahuilla oral legends describe the existence of Lake Cahuilla when the people first moved into the Coachella Valley. The mythology source presents a creation story in which Mukat and Temayuwat were born from twin balls of lightning, manifestations of Amnaa, or Power, and Tukmiut, or Night. Mukat and Temayuwat began a creative contest, and Temayuwat fled below the earth after being defeated.

The creation story also concerns the origin of the world, the death of Mukat, the need for death, social roles, supernatural power, and its proper use. At the beginning of creation, creatures called *nukatem* were larger than contemporary beings; they later shrank and became natural objects such as mirages, rainbows, and earth. Mukat’s death led to mourning ceremonies, funerary ritual, and the teaching of the bow and arrow.

The sources name several mythological figures, including Menill, Mukat, Muut, Pemtemweha, Sungrey, and Taqwush. Contemporary preservation includes the teaching of bird songs and Cahuilla language to younger generations.

## Language & religion

Cahuilla, or *’Ivillu’at*, is an endangered Uto-Aztecan language spoken by tribes of the Cahuilla Nation in the Coachella Valley, San Gorgonio Pass, and San Jacinto Mountains region. Three dialects are identified: Desert, Mountain, and Pass, along with other sub-dialects. The language forms the Cupan subdivision with Cupeño. Its indigenous designations include *’Ivillu’at*, *’Ivi’a*, and *Kawiiya*; the people may be called *’Ivilluqalet* in the singular and *’Ivilluwenetem* in the plural.

Cahuilla has been, and to an extent remains, an unwritten language. The sources describe writing through IPA and NAPA, with a modified NAPA orthography used in the *Cahuilla Dictionary*. Cahuilla mythology includes Mukat as creator, Menill as lunar deity, Muut as psychopomp, and other supernatural figures. Religious observance includes mourning ceremonies, cremation, and initiatory rites during puberty.
