## Overview

The Ahtna, also known as Ahtena, Atna, Ahtna-kohtaene, or Copper River, are an Alaska Native Athabaskan people of the Athabaskan-speaking ethnolinguistic group. Their homeland, Atna Nenn’, is in the Copper River area of southern Alaska, including the Copper River Basin and the Wrangell Mountains. Their neighbors include Dena’ina, Koyukon, Lower Tanana, Tanacross, Upper Tanana, Southern Tutchone, Tlingit, Eyak, and Chugach Sugpiaq peoples. The Ahtna population is estimated at around 1,427. Their culture is documented through regional bands and villages, subsistence practices centered on hunting, fishing, gathering, and trade, distinctive dwellings and transport technologies, and the Ahtna language, which has Upper, Central, Lower, and Western dialects.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do record a *tumpline*, made of animal skin or cloth and worn across the forehead or chest to support a heavy load carried on the back. The sources also mention snowshoes and load-bearing toboggans as winter equipment.

### Architecture

Ahtna summer dwellings were temporary rectangular structures made of spruce and cottonwood. Their sides were covered with bark, and their entrances were covered with skins. During winter, families lived in large semi-underground homes constructed from wood and covered with spruce bark. These houses could be as large as 10 feet wide by 36 feet long. A second room was sometimes attached for sweating rituals.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Copper was an important traded material, and the name “Copper Indians” was used in some documentation because the Ahtna homeland lies in the basin of the Copper River and its tributaries. The sources also describe copper-colored knives in connection with the name “Yellowknife,” while noting that the Yellowknives are another tribe. Moose-hide boats were used for water travel, and snowshoes, toboggans, and tumplines supported winter travel and carrying goods.

## Music & performance

They do mention that the Ahtna language has been recorded and preserved through linguistic work. In 1990, university linguist James Kari published a dictionary, and the Ahtna People later published *The Ahtna Noun Dictionary of Pronunciation Guide*. A digital archiving project was underway as of 2010, and external references listed in the source include Ahtna recordings in the PARADISEC archive.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record a sweating ritual associated with a second room attached to some winter dwellings. The Ahtna also had ceremonial meals connected with wolves: a killed wolf could be propped up and fed ceremonial meals. Trade meetings took place three times a year at Nuchek on the Prince William Sound, but the sources describe these as economic gatherings rather than festivals.

## Foodways

The Ahtna traditionally hunted moose, caribou, mountain sheep, and rabbits. Salmon was a staple and was caught with nets in rivers and streams. The Ahtna also gathered berries and roots. To support healthy prey populations, they monitored and reduced predator populations, including wolves, eagles, and bears. The sources state that wolf dens were tracked in traditional hunting areas and that cubs were killed. In Ahtna mythology, killed wolves could be propped up and given ceremonial meals.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources identify mythology as an important context for Ahtna relations with wolves and describe a central mythological figure associated with the practice of propping up killed wolves and feeding them ceremonial meals. Language preservation is documented through James Kari’s 1990 dictionary, *The Ahtna Noun Dictionary of Pronunciation Guide*, and a facing-bilingual collection of poetry in Ahtna and English, *The Indian Prophet*, published by John Smelcer in 2012. Katie John recorded a pronunciation guide of the Mentasta dialect and helped develop an Ahtna alphabet in the 1970s.

## Language & religion

Ahtna is a Na-Dené language classified within the Northern Athabaskan languages, itself a subgroup of the Athabaskan languages. It is also known as Copper River or Mednovskiy. The language has four dialects: Upper, Central, Lower, and Western; three of the four are still spoken. Dialectal differences occur according to location along the Copper River, and Ahtna is closely related to Dena’ina. About 80 Ahtnas are believed to still speak the language in one source, while another describes 15 elderly speakers out of a population of 500 and says the language is facing extinction. The sources describe mythology and ceremonial meals.
