## Overview

Paiute refers to three non-contiguous groups of Indigenous peoples of the Great Basin: Northern Paiute people of northeastern California, northwestern Nevada, eastern Oregon, and southern Idaho; Southern Paiute people of northern Arizona, southern Nevada, and southwestern Utah; and Mono people of east central California, divided into Owens Valley Paiute (Eastern Mono) and Western Mono (Monache). The term is a historical label rather than the name of one single, unified group. Their languages are related within the Numic group of Uto-Aztecan languages, although the three languages do not form a single subgroup. The museum records associate Paiute objects with the western United States and with people living near Pyramid Lake, in Nevada.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used do not document named Paiute textile or pattern traditions in sufficient detail for this section.

**Basketry** — The catalogue records include baskets made of basketry and string, a water-bottle made of twigs and pitch, and a completely watertight bottle made of plant fibers. One record describes a basket as made by the Pah-utes and identifies it as a so-called Navajo Wedding basket.

**Triangular foundation** — A museum record states that the Ute and Paiute both employ a triangular foundation consisting of three rods of sumac (*Rhus*). It contrasts this with Navajo basketry, which uses a triangular foundation of two rods of sumac, with the top formed by softer yucca leaf.

**Pah-Utes basketry** — The catalogue records state that Navajos originally made a certain type of basket for themselves but later traded textiles for baskets made by the Pah-utes.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no Paiute motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The photographic records document Paiute men wearing cloth shirts, blankets, leggings, moccasins, ear ornaments, hair ornaments, necklaces, embroidered garments, and beaded decoration. One man is described as wearing a warrior shirt with beaded decoration and tassels and holding a tomahawk. A Paiute woman identified as Young Antelope (Won-si-vu) is shown wearing a fringed tunic, loin covering, and cloth headband. Another Paiute woman, Ka-ni, is described as wearing a fibre loin covering. Other records show Paiute women wearing headbands, necklaces, blankets, and moccasins. The sources also describe children wearing cloth tops, jackets, necklaces, and blankets. The sources used do not distinguish everyday from ceremonial dress beyond these catalogue descriptions.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum records document water-bottles and baskets. One bottle is made of twigs and pitch; another is made of plant fibers and is completely watertight. The catalogue also includes a cradle or cot and a bear basket. The sources used do not document Paiute ceramics, metalwork, wooden tools, or other household forms in further detail.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The photographic records describe ear ornaments, hair ornaments, beads in the hair, beaded necklaces, and necklaces. One Paiute man has a section of his hair in a plait wrapped in cloth, while another is described with two sections of hair wrapped in cloth. The sources also document embroidered clothing, beaded decoration, and a cloth headband. The sources used do not document tattoos, ritual functions, or named Paiute adornment types.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not document an annual Paiute festival calendar, life-cycle rites, weddings, funerals, or other rituals. One museum record preserves a legend associated with Pyramid Lake: the sound of crying children is sometimes heard coming from the lake, and the sound tempts people into the water, where they drown because of strong underwater currents. The same record states that without these spirits there would be no water and that people must show the water proper respect.

## Language & religion

Paiute language may refer to any of the languages spoken by Paiute people. The sources name Northern Paiute language, also known as Numu and Paviotso; Mono language (California), specifically the Western dialect, also known as Owens Valley Paiute; and Colorado River Numic language, also known as Ute, Southern Paiute, Ute–Southern Paiute, or Ute-Chemehuevi. The Paiute languages are related within the Numic group of Uto-Aztecan languages, but the three languages do not form a single subgroup. The museum record concerning Pyramid Lake describes spirits associated with the lake and states that people must show the water proper respect. The sources used do not cover scripts or the current religious landscape.
