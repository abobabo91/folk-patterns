## Overview

The Ojibwe (also Ojibway, Ojibwa or Chippewa; plural *Ojibweg*) are an Anishinaabe people whose homeland, *Ojibwewaki*, covers much of the Great Lakes region and the northern plains and reaches into the subarctic and the northeastern woodlands. In Canada they live from western Quebec through Ontario, Manitoba and Saskatchewan to eastern British Columbia; in the United States from Michigan through Wisconsin and Minnesota, with communities in North Dakota and Montana. Their total population is roughly 320,000, about 160,000 of them in Canada registered with one of some 200 bands, making them Canada's second-largest First Nations population after the Cree. Their language, *Anishinaabemowin* or *Ojibwemowin*, belongs to the Algonquian family. In folk-culture terms they are known for birchbark canoes, birchbark scrolls, copper mining and trade, and the harvesting of wild rice and maple syrup, and for the Midewiwin society that keeps the scrolls of events, oral history, songs, maps, stories, geometry and mathematics.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Glass beadwork (*manidoominensikaan*)** — The dominant pattern-bearing craft in the museum collections shown here: woven and sewn glass seed beads worked onto cloth, velvet and tanned skin. Museum documentation records beadwork as traditionally women's work and glosses the Ojibwe term as compounded from the words for "spirit" and for "seed" or "bead."

**Beaded bands (*band*, woven strips)** — Long narrow strips of woven glass beads, several of them about half a metre long and one a full metre, collected in the Great Lakes region and northern Midwest. One strip appears to carry animal figures — otter or turtle, or both — worked into the beading as part of the pattern.

**Bandolier bags** — The flashiest and most important item of Woodlands formal attire in the 1800s, according to the Cleveland catalogue: a shoulder bag of plain-weave cotton and twill-weave wool with velvet, plaited wool binding, wool tassels and glass beads. The form evolved from smaller native bags, perhaps under the influence of British ammunition pouches.

**Beaded pouches and pipe bags** — Buckskin pouches worked with beads, cloth and stroud, including bags made to hold a smoking pipe, and beaded knife cases of native-tanned skin with glass, silk and wool.

**Medicine bags** — A composite bag of red velvet bound in black, elaborately beaded, with a long wide shoulder sling rising from the bottom of the bag and tassels ending in red wool.

**Beaded panels and horse trappings** — Flat panels of beads, cloth and wool, catalogued as possible horse trappings.

**Birchbark work (*wiigwaas*)** — Not textile, but the other great patterned surface: cylindrical bark boxes and oval bark dishes sewn to bent withe hoops with strips of bark, their rims reinforced and decorated with wavy cut bark strips laid inside-out for contrast, and bark trays recorded under Ojibwe names.

**Motif vocabulary.** Floral motifs, introduced by Europeans into Woodlands imagery but taken up enthusiastically because plants are held to be animate and powerful — one bandolier bag carries blueberries, described in the catalogue as "soul food" that refreshes the spirit; otter and turtle figures, both meaningful in Anishinaabe kinship and cosmology, on a beaded band; Thunderers, a sun and cattle painted on a drum; zoomorphic drawings on birchbark scrolls; *doodem* (clan) signs, carved on grave markers and shown beside portrait heads in a printed group portrait of nine men; and a flower carved into the head of a wooden club.

### Clothing & dress

Footwear is *makizinan* (moccasins); one of the explanations offered for the name Ojibwe is "those who cook/roast until it puckers," referring to the fire-curing of moccasin seams to make them waterproof, and another derivation points to the puckered seam itself. A pair of child's moccasins of skin, cloth and beads survives from northern Minnesota. Photographs of the later nineteenth century show the mix of Ojibwe and imported dress then worn: a woman in a cloth dress at the door of a birchbark wigwam; a studio portrait of a woman named Josephine in earrings, several necklaces, a dress and moccasins; a woman in a beaded vest, armbands and skirt beside a man in a brimmed felt hat, beaded shirt and beaded belt; men photographed in feathered headgear, in blankets wrapped over western suits, and in one case a necklace that appears to be made of human hair. Blankets draped or wrapped over the head recur in several photographs. For ceremonial and social dance, the jingle dress worn by women at powwows originated with the Ojibwe — dark cloth dresses hung with rows of tin cones, often cut from tobacco-can lids, that jingle as the dancer moves; both Plains and Woodlands Ojibwe claim the earliest form, and the style has since spread to all tribes as a distinctly Ojibwe contribution to Pan-Indianism.

### Architecture

The characteristic dwelling is the *wiigiwaam* (wigwam), built either as a *waginogaan*, a domed lodge, or as a *nasawa'ogaan*, a pointed lodge, framed on willow saplings and covered with birch bark and juniper bark. A stereoscope photograph in the British Museum shows a birchbark wigwam standing in a clearing with a woman seated at its entrance; another shows a campsite with a wigwam, a tipi behind it and an upturned canoe in front. A postcard photograph records a bark-built house with snowshoes leaning against it and birchbark baskets and model canoes hung on cords across its front. Most Ojibwe today live in modern housing, but the older structures are still raised for particular sites and events. Over burials, many families erect a *jiibegamig* or "spirit house" on the mound; historically such a mound carried a wooden marker inscribed with the dead person's *doodem*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Bark, wood and stone dominate the household objects here; the sources record no pottery. Birchbark is worked into cylindrical boxes (*ask*), oval dishes, trays and baskets, and into canoes — the collections include a miniature bark canoe with the bark sewn to its ribs. A cradle (*a'dikina'gun*) of mixed materials came from Nipigon, Ontario. Tools and implements include a stone-bladed adze or axe lashed to a wooden handle with twisted fibre cord; a wooden club with a squared-off handle, a circle carved with a flower for its head and a metal ring with a cotton strap at the butt; and two lacrosse sticks of wood and buckskin. Smoking pipes are carved in hard wood, inlaid with lead and crested, or cut in black stone inlaid with white metal and stars of catlinite. Copper, mined and traded across the continent as far back as the Hopewell tradition, is held to come from the body of the underwater serpent *Mishebeshu*; maple sugar was once granulated by boiling syrup down and working it in a trough with wooden paddles.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment in the collections is made of beads and shell. A necklace of plaited glass beads and several beaded bands came from the Great Lakes region; a beaded choker in red and white fields divided by strips of leather has at its centre a concave pink disc carrying two yellow-metal balls, with two short cords hanging from its back, each strung with a cylindrical bead and knotted around a shell at the end — the collector recorded such chokers as status symbols among the Ojibwe. Studio portraits show women wearing earrings and multiple necklaces, and one man a necklace apparently of human hair. The *miigis* (cowrie) shell, which occurs naturally only on distant coasts, is used in certain ceremonies and figures throughout the migration story; its presence inland is evidence of a trade network spanning the continent. Some Ojibwe have had the image of their guardian *manitou* tattooed on the body.

## Music & performance

The collections hold three instruments. A flute of two pieces of wood, about two feet long and coloured red, was made by the Serpent River on Lake Huron. A second wood-and-skin flute is catalogued under the names *Pib-be-gwun* and *Siyontanka*. And there is a drum: a military drum of European shape in wood, skin and cord, repainted with Thunderers across its upper half, what may be a sun at the middle, and cattle below — creatures the cataloguer connects with the underworld. Two birchbark scrolls bear zoomorphic drawings said to represent musical notes, which places song inside the same pictorial writing system used in the Midewiwin rites. Beyond that, the sources distinguish two summer gathering types: *jiingotamog*, spiritual, and *niimi'idimaa*, the social gathering or powwow, held at reservations across *Anishinaabe-Aki*, Anishinaabe Country. The Red Lake, White Earth and Leech Lake reservations are known for singing hymns in the Ojibwe language.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name only one dance tradition in any detail: the women's jingle-dress dancing of the powwow, for which the Ojibwe-origin tin-cone dress was made, and the social *niimi'idimaa* gatherings at which such dancing takes place. One further dance-like practice is domestic rather than ceremonial — "jigging" wild rice, dancing or walking on the parched grain to loosen its hulls.

## Festivals & rituals

The year turns on harvests. Wild rice, *manoomin*, is gathered in late summer and early autumn, two people to a canoe, one poling while the other knocks the ripe grain in with knocking sticks, on rice beds called *Manito Gitigaan*, the Great Spirit's Garden; tobacco and prayers are offered to secure a good harvest. Spring is the maple sugar harvest, taught in some schools by having elders and older students narrate the work in Ojibwemowin while the youngest gather wood, help drill the trees and haul sap. Summer brings the *jiingotamog* and the *niimi'idimaa*, and the season for picking berries, hunting and making medicines. The central life-cycle rite before the twentieth century was the vision quest, undertaken by almost every Ojibwe male at puberty to establish a lifelong relationship with a guardian *manitou*; the practice declined sharply under the reservation system and missionary pressure. Ritual specialists hold powers from the *manitouk*: the commonest is the *Jiisakiiwinini*, whose *Jiisakiiwin* or shaking-tent rite calls on spirits for healing. The Midewiwin, a healing society formed among southern Ojibwe by at least the eighteenth century, keeps its own birchbark scrolls and pictorial script. Hunting carries its own observances — a conciliatory mourning for the slain animal, an apology, an offering of tobacco, kin terms spoken over it, and the decorating of a killed bear's skull. The dead are buried in mounds under spirit houses; wild rice is often among the first and last foods an Ojibwe person eats.

## Foodways

*Manoomin*, wild rice, is the defining food: nutty in flavour, uneven in colour and size when grown wild, eaten by Ojibwe people as early as two thousand years before contact with white settlers, and named in the prophecy that sent the Anishinaabeg west to "the place where there is food upon the waters." After knocking, the grain is parched in an iron kettle over an open fire, jigged to strip the hulls, then winnowed by tossing it up so the wind carries the chaff off. Treaties of 1837 and 1854 guaranteed the right to harvest it, though the right was not always honoured, and in the 1930s Minnesota declared a state of emergency over the collapse of the rice beds and leaned on Native elders to write and enforce statutes protecting them. The other staples are fish, maple sugar, venison and corn, with beans, squash and potatoes grown alongside, and blueberries, blackberries, choke cherries, raspberries, gooseberries and huckleberries foraged; deer, beaver, moose, goose, duck, rabbit and bear were hunted in summer. Frybread and "Indian tacos" have displaced some older dishes, and communities have answered with gardens in food deserts and a mobile kitchen teaching food preparation.

## Oral tradition & literature

Storytelling is central to Anishinaabe life: stories are held to create worlds, to bind the generations through teaching and listening, and to connect people with the non-human world. Ojibwe tradition separates ordinary stories about living people from sacred stories about the *manitouk* — *tipacimowinak* and *kanatipacimowinak* in the Severn River area, *dbaajmowin* and *aadsookaan* among the southeast Ojibwe. Stories of the *manitouk* were told only in winter, when the water was frozen and *Mishebeshu* could not be drawn out by the telling. The recurring figures are *Nanabush*, the culture hero and trickster who taught people to hunt and fish, found wild rice and invented snowshoes but could also be greedy or cruel; *Amik*, the beaver, whose stories are about making shared worlds; and *Nokomis*, the grandmother. The Seven Grandfather Teachings — wisdom, respect, love, honesty, humility, bravery and truth, each with its animal — are still in use. Elders are the "knowledge keepers"; the historian Anton Treuer has recorded stories from about fifty elders and writes them out with his mentor, the Ponemah elder Eugene Stillday, in Ojibwe and in English translation. Narratives were also recorded in pictographs serving as mnemonics, and the *wiigwaasabak* birchbark writing is still used by traditional people and by young people on social media.

## Language & religion

*Anishinaabemowin* is an Algonquian language descended from Proto-Algonquian, with dialects running west to east: Western Ojibwe (Saulteaux), Southwestern Ojibwe (Chippewa), Northwestern Ojibwe, Severn Ojibwe (Oji-Cree), Ottawa (Odawa), Eastern Ojibwe and Algonquin. It is the second most spoken First Nations language in Canada after Cree and fourth in North America, and it served for centuries as a trade language around the Great Lakes. Boarding schools drove heavy loss; immersion schools such as Waadookodaading on the Lac Courte Oreilles Reservation, the Ojibwe People's Dictionary, and a Mille Lacs partnership with Rosetta Stone now work the other way, and Ojibwe has been the official language of Red Lake since 2011. Ojibwe religion is polytheistic, centred on the *manitouk*: the *animikeek* (thunderbirds), the underwater serpents *Mishebeshu*, the four winds, the owners of animal species, the *windigo*, and *Nanabush*, with the creator *Kitche Manitou* above them. Many Ojibwe practise it alongside Christianity, usually Catholicism, identifying *Kitche Manitou* with the Christian God.
