## Overview

The Miwok are four linguistically related Indigenous peoples of California. Their homelands are in Northern and Central California, including the Sacramento Valley, the foothills of the Sierra Nevada, and the San Francisco Bay Area. Anthropologists distinguish Plains and Sierra Miwok, Coast Miwok, Lake Miwok, and Bay Miwok, although these distinctions were not used among the Miwok before European contact. Historically, Miwok languages belonged to the Utian family. In 1770, the estimated population included 500 Lake Miwok, 1,500 Coast Miwok, and 9,000 Plains and Sierra Miwok, totaling about 11,000 people; California historians estimate that the Miwok population was at least 25,000 in 1769. Miwok culture is documented through subsistence practices, basketry, mythology, language, and mixed-gender games.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The mythology source describes Miwok villages in a creation narrative.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources document flat-bottomed baskets as objects used to store food for later consumption. They also describe the use of arrows, clubs, and snares for hunting, and an elk hide ball in a mixed-gender game. No Miwok ceramics, metalwork, wooden household forms, or named craft objects are described.

## Music & performance

The sources describe a prayer song in the creation story of Coyote and Silver Fox. In that narrative, Silver Fox feels lonely, mentions this in a prayer song, and proposes, “We will sing the world”; the world is then created through dancing and singing. The sources also refer to a radio episode, “Mouse Steals Fire,” from Coast Miwok lore in *Californian Indian Nights Entertainments*. No instruments, named song genres, musical scales, dance accompaniment, or performance institutions are described.

The mythology source presents storytelling as a medium for accounts of creation, the first spirits, the formation of the earth, the creation of humankind, death, and the afterlife. These narratives include Coyote, Silver Fox, Turtle, the Turkey Buzzard, Raven, Crow, Chicken Hawk, Lizard, and other animal figures.

## Dance & theatre

The mythology source says that Coyote and Silver Fox create the world together by dancing and singing. It also describes Coyote shaking his *walik*, something similar to a blanket of tule, toward the four directions in a Coast Miwok creation account. These actions occur within creation narratives rather than a separately named dance tradition.

The Miwok also played a mixed-gender game on a 110-yard (100 m) playing field called *poscoi a we'a*. The object was to kick or carry an elk hide ball to the opposing team’s goalpost. Women could control the ball with any part of their bodies, while men were only allowed to kick it; a man could also carry a woman holding the ball toward the goal.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe religious narratives and beliefs. Miwok creation stories concern the origins of the earth and humankind, while the Coast Miwok account of the dead describes an ocean path leading westward to the setting sun and an afterworld called *ute-yomigo* or *ute-yomi*, meaning “dead home.”

The sources also state that Miwok people had totem animals associated with one of two moieties, which were respectively associated with land and water. These animals were understood as predecessors rather than literal human ancestors. No ritual calendar, priesthood, shrine system, or ceremonial objects are documented.

## Foodways

The Sierra Miwok harvested acorns from the California Black Oak. The sources state that cultivation by Miwok tribes partially contributed to the modern-day extent of California Black Oak forests in some areas of Yosemite National Park. Miwok people burned understory vegetation to reduce the fraction of Ponderosa Pine.

Nearly every other kind of edible vegetable matter was used as food, including bulbs, seeds, and fungi. Animals were hunted with arrows, clubs, or snares, depending on the species and situation. Grasshoppers were a highly prized food source, and groups living adjacent to the Stanislaus River ate mussels. Coastal Miwok relied predominantly on food gathered from the inland side of the Marin peninsula, including modern San Pablo bay, lakes, and land-based foods, while also diving for abalone in the Pacific Ocean.

The Miwok ate meals according to appetite rather than at regular times. Food was stored for later consumption, primarily in flat-bottomed baskets.

## Oral tradition & literature

Miwok mythology consists of accounts of world order, creation, and how things came to be. Several creation stories describe Coyote as an ancestor and creator god, sometimes acting with other animals. Coyote creates or helps form the earth and human beings from materials including feathers, twigs, mud, or earth mixed with *Chanit* seeds and water.

The mythology source names several narrative cycles. In “The Diver,” Coyote sends Turtle into the ocean for earth. In “Coyote and Silver Fox,” the world is formed through singing and dancing. Coast Miwok stories include “Coyote and Walik,” “Coyote and Turkey Buzzard,” “Coyote and Chicken Hawk,” and “Ocean Path West.” Sierra Miwok narratives include “How Kah'-kah-loo The Ravens Became People” and “Coyote and Lizard.”

The first spirits named in one account include O-let'-te Coyote-man, the Creator; Mol'-luk the Condor; Wek'-wek the Falcon; Hul'-luk mi-yum'-ko, the two beautiful women chiefs of the Star-people; Os-so-so'-li, the Pleiades; Ke'-lok, the North Giant; Hoo-soo'-pe, the Mermaids or Water-maidens; Choo'-hoo, the Turkey Buzzard; Kok'-kol, the Raven; Ah-wet'-che, the Crow; and Koo-loo'-loo, the Humming-bird.

## Language & religion

Miwok or Miwokan languages, also known as Moquelumnan or Miwuk, are a group of endangered languages spoken in central California from the San Francisco Bay Area to the Sierra Nevada. The languages belong to the Utian family. The source identifies seven Miwok languages, four of which have distinct regional dialects: Plains Miwok, Bay Miwok, Northern Sierra Miwok, Central Sierra Miwok, Southern Sierra Miwok, Coast Miwok, and Lake Miwok. The Sierra Miwok languages have a few dozen speakers, and in 1994 there were two speakers of Lake Miwok. Southern Sierra Miwok is described as the best-attested language, and the name Yosemite originates from it. The Northern Sierra Miwok word *miw·yk* means “people.”

Miwok religious narratives describe animal and human spirits. Coyote appears as ancestor, creator god, and trickster god. Miwok totem animals were associated with land and water moieties and were regarded as predecessors rather than literal ancestors.
