## Overview

The Miccosukee Tribe of Indians is a federally recognized Native American tribe in Florida, associated with the Indigenous peoples of the Southeastern Woodlands and related to the Seminole Tribe of Florida, the Seminole Nation of Oklahoma, and the Muscogee Nation. Miccosukee communities are concentrated in southern Florida, including the Everglades, Big Cypress Swamp, and reservations near the Tamiami Trail and Miami. The tribe gained federal recognition in 1962. Its language, Mikasuki, belongs to the Muskogean language family and was spoken by around 290 people in southern Florida as of 2014. The Miccosukee are significant in folk-culture terms because the sources describe continuing relationships among language, clan organization, subsistence activities, camps, artworks, and self-determined tribal institutions.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state that Miccosukees may use wetlands for hunting, fishing, frogging, and subsistence agriculture, and that the Miccosukee Indian Village Museum displays native paintings, hand crafts, photographs, and cooking utensils.

### Architecture

Miccosukee communities historically occupied camps in Big Cypress and the Everglades, including camps situated on tree islands, or hammocks, in the swamp. In the 20th century, more than a dozen camps or villages moved closer to the Tamiami Trail between 1928 and 1938. Today, some Miccosukees live in modern housing along Old Tamiami Trail and Loop Road, while others live in traditional clan camps in Big Cypress, Fakahatchee Strand Preserve State Park, and Collier-Seminole State Park.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Miccosukee Indian Village Museum displays cooking utensils among its artifacts. The sources also mention Indigenous artworks, airboat tours, and goods and services offered to tourists traveling across Florida.

## Music & performance

They state that presentations in the Mikasuki language have been featured at the Florida Folk Festival and that the Miccosukee Indian Village Museum presents native paintings, hand crafts, photographs, and other artistic expressions. These records do not identify musical forms or instruments.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do describe political and historical gatherings: a Miccosukee delegation delivered the Buckskin Declaration in 1954, Buffalo Tiger visited Cuba in July 1959 during the celebration of the 26th of July Movement in Havana, and the Miccosukee tribe opened an embassy in Tallahassee in 2024. These are historical and political events rather than a documented ritual calendar.

The sources describe matrilineal kinship and inheritance. Children are considered to be born into their mother’s clan, and the mother’s older brother has an important role in raising children, especially boys. The uncle introduces boys to the men’s groups of the clan and tribe.

## Foodways

They state that Miccosukees may use certain wetlands for hunting, fishing, frogging, and subsistence agriculture, and that the Miccosukee Indian Village Museum displays cooking utensils.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Miccosukee language article records that Mikasuki has been taught at the Miccosukee Indian School, where an area was devoted to “Miccosukee Language Arts” as of 2002. It also records the Elling Eide Endowed Professorship in Miccosukee Language and Culture at the University of Florida Department of Anthropology as of 2011. These sources document language education and preservation-related activity.

## Language & religion

Mikasuki, also called Hitchiti-Mikasuki or Hitchiti, belongs to the Muskogean language family. It is spoken by members of the Miccosukee Tribe and the Seminole Tribe of Florida. The extinct Hitchiti was a mutually intelligible dialect of, or ancestor of, Mikasuki. Mikasuki is written with the Latin alphabet and has high, low, and falling tones; vowel length is distinctive. The language article describes an ancient “female” dialect associated with Hitchiti and states that Mikasuki was spoken by around 290 people in southern Florida as of 2014. They identify Ar-pi-uck-i, also known as Abiaki, Abiaka, or Sam Jones, as a Miccosukee war chief and medicine man, but provide no further account of medicine-man practices.
