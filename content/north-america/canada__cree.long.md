## Overview

The Cree are a North American Indigenous people numbering more than 350,000 in Canada, where they form one of the country's largest First Nations macro-communities. Their homelands cover most of eastern and central Canada — from *Eeyou Istchee* on the east coast of James Bay in northern Quebec, across northern Ontario and the Canadian Prairies, into British Columbia and the Northwest Territories — with smaller communities in Montana, chiefly on Rocky Boy's Indian Reservation. Eight dialect-and-region groupings are usually distinguished (Plains, Woodland, Rocky, Swampy, Moose and East Cree, with the closely related Atikamekw, Innu and Naskapi), and settlements such as Moose Factory Island at the mouth of the Moose River, Mistissini on Lake Mistassini, and Whapmagoostui at the mouth of the Great Whale River anchor them. The language belongs to the Algonquian family. In folk-culture terms the Cree are known internationally for floral beadwork and silk embroidery on smoked mooseskin, and for pictures made by biting folded birchbark.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Floral beadwork** — Glass seed beads worked in flowering sprays on dark blue velvet bags (the drawstrings of surviving examples have been replaced by non-Native hands) and on smoked mooseskin panels cut from coats; one such beaded hide ornament from Island Lake, Manitoba, entered a Swedish collection as a gift in 1931. A hood of wool and cotton dated 1840–50 and legging panels of about 1870 combine wool cloth with glass beads and wood.

**Silk floral embroidery on smoked mooseskin** — Mittens and skin boots embroidered with floral work in silk floss, with skin fringing and multicoloured plaited wool cord ending in pompoms; the boots are worked in cotton thread instead, with pompoms of blue and burgundy wool on a braided cord tied around the upper leg.

**Quillwork** — A panel bag of about 1820–40 is sewn of native-tanned skin and worked with quill alongside glass beads and metal; the catalogue record does not describe the technique further.

**Embroidery on felted wool** — Trade cloth itself becomes the ground: a shot pouch of grey felted wool embroidered with multicoloured flowers and fitted with a metal zipper, and a rifle-case of the same grey wool carrying three sets of embroidered flowers, closed with a moose-skin end-piece and plastic buttons.

**Birchbark biting** — A rectangle of birchbark bearing four birds with outstretched wings and heads turned inwards, the image produced by biting the folded bark.

**Plaited and finger-woven cordwork** — Multicoloured plaited wool cords with pompoms join mittens and tie boots; a shot pouch hangs from a red and blue wool strap that appears to be finger-woven.

**Cloth appliqué and rickrack borders** — A baby-carrier of red checked cotton lined in off-white, bordered in brown cotton printed with white flowers, trimmed with orange rickrack and edged with pierced moose skin threaded with red nylon string; a rifle-case of denim is likewise banded with rickrack and embroidery.

**Motif vocabulary.** Multicoloured flower sprays (the dominant device, on hide, velvet and felted wool alike); blue flowers on red cloth; white flowers on brown cotton ground; birds with outstretched wings, in bitten birchbark.

### Clothing & dress

The documented working garment is the smoked mooseskin jacket: pull-over type with a straight hem, two chest pockets, short fringing along the seams and hem, two buttons at the neck opening, a separate hood sewn on, and a partial cotton lining. With it go paired mittens joined by a strip so they hang from the neck, skin boots with embroidered uppers and leg fringe, and moccasins, which photographs show worn with decorated leggings. Accessories carried on the body include firebags (wool, silk, glass beads and metal, about 1820), shot pouches, purses and small oval skin pouches with beaded ornament beneath the opening, of the kind used to hang a watch. Infants were dressed in caps of red cloth printed with blue flowers, lined in white cotton, with a ruffled border framing the face and ties at each front corner. Ceremonial and formal dress recorded in late nineteenth-century photographs differs sharply: the Plains Cree chief Kah-mi-yo-ki-suck-way (Fine Day) was photographed in two-horned head-gear with fur; He Shows His Blood, at Maple Creek, wore an arm band of deer hoof and skin, a bead and shell gorget choker and two eagle feathers in his hair; another man appears in a warbonnet on horseback. Women are shown wrapped in blankets, men at The Pas in hats, coats, trousers and boots.

### Architecture

The sources describe mobile dwellings rather than permanent houses. The basic residential unit was the lodge — eight to a dozen people, usually two related married couples and their families, sharing either a *wigwam*, a domed tent, or a *tipi*, a conical one. Several lodges moving and hunting together formed a band, and because lodges could leave bands and bands could form and dissolve easily, the built environment was designed to be struck and reassembled; banishment from any band was regarded as a severe punishment. Photographs from Battleford, Saskatchewan, show tipis standing in encampments behind assembled riders, and a Sun Dance lodge whose long supporting beams and covering material are visible behind a screen of cut shrubs, with men seated on the ground inside and standing behind the shrubs. The other structures the records name are colonial: the Hudson's Bay Company posts around which many communities grew, including Charles Fort of 1668 at the mouth of the Rupert River (later Fort Rupert or Rupert House, now Waskaganish), Moose Factory founded in 1672–73, and Fort Albany, established between 1675 and 1679.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources record no Cree ceramics or metalworking. What they do document is birchbark and skin work: the birchbark canoe, photographed upside down beside a tipi at a lakeshore, and bitten birchbark pictures. Household and hunting gear in the collections is overwhelmingly sewn — rifle-cases tailored from felted wool or denim and closed with moose skin and buttons, shot pouches, purses, and a cloth baby-carrier. Imported components are worked in without apology: metal zippers across a pouch and a purse, plastic buttons, canvas tape, orange rickrack and red nylon string. Pipes, flintlock muskets and rifles appear in the photographs as possessions rather than as products.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment documented in the photographic record is made from animal and trade materials: a bead and shell gorget choker, an arm band of deer hoof and skin, and eagle feathers worn in the hair, two at a time. Headdresses, feathers and two-horned head-gear with fur distinguish chiefs and Sun Dance participants, who also carry feather fans. Beadwork otherwise ornaments carried objects — the glass and iron beads below the opening of a small skin watch-pouch — rather than the body itself. The sources describe no tattooing, no cosmetic practice and no hair traditions.

## Music & performance

The sources name no Cree instruments, song genres or singers. The only performance context they document is the Sun Dance: a photograph taken at Battleford in June 1895 shows six men, five standing and one mounted, as participants following a two-day Sun Dance ceremony, three of them holding sticks and a feather fan, and a second photograph shows the men assembled inside the dance lodge, some in headdresses and feathers, several with feather fans, in a mixture of Cree and settler clothing.

## Dance & theatre

The Sun Dance is the one dance tradition the sources attest, and they attribute it to the Cree as its source: Ojibwe communities living near the Plains region also performed it, a practice likely adopted from the Cree. The 1895 Battleford photographs record it as a two-day ceremony held in a purpose-built lodge screened with cut shrubs. No theatrical or masked traditions are described.

## Festivals & rituals

There was no calendar of festivals in the sources' account; the rhythm was the hunting year. Bands maintained strong ties with their neighbours through intermarriage and assembled at different points in the year to hunt and socialize together, and apart from these regional gatherings there was no higher-level formal structure — questions of war and peace were settled by consensus in councils of allied bands, each clan sending a representative with a vote. War brought a temporary commander, the *okimahkan*, an office distinct from that of the peace chief, whose role was closer to a diplomat's; Big Bear led his band into the run-up to the 1885 North-West Rebellion, but once fighting began Wandering Spirit became war leader. The deepest ritual logic the sources record is that of the hunt. The Cree traditionally drew no cultural separation between the religious and the secular, and the anthropologist Colin Scott characterised their worldview as animistic, conceiving the world as a community of living entities and relationships. Success in a hunt requires the cooperation of the prey animal, and the kill is therefore understood as a gift: the animal should be killed respectfully, without waste, and with consideration for the well-being of the wider population of its species. The Sun Dance is the named ceremonial gathering. Birth, marriage and funeral rites are not covered by the sources used here.

## Foodways

The sources do not name a single Cree dish, cooking method or beverage tradition. They describe subsistence rather than cuisine: the Cree were hunter-gatherers whose bands moved and hunted together, those who moved onto the Great Plains adopting bison hunting and becoming known as the Plains Cree, while some of the Naskapi remain caribou hunters and more nomadic than many of the Innu. The hunt was governed by the ethic described above — the prey's cooperation, no waste, regard for the species' wider population. Animal pelts dominate the economic record: four Cree men were photographed at The Pas holding large assemblages of pelts, and after acquiring firearms from the Hudson's Bay Company the Cree moved as traders onto the plains, acting as middlemen for the company; with the Assiniboine they were important intermediaries in the First Nations trading networks of the northern plains, and the Iron Confederacy they formed with the Assiniboine, the Métis Nation and the Saulteaux was a major force in the fur trade from the 1730s to the 1870s.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources name no epic cycle, but they document a living narrative and oratorical tradition and its modern documentation. Among the Cree and the northern Ojibwe, the thunderbirds are sometimes called *pinesiwak*. Oratory survives in published form in *The Counselling Speeches of Jim Kâ-Nîpitêhtêw* (1998), a text collection notable also for using full punctuation in Cree. Preston's *Cree Narrative: Expressing the Personal Meaning of Events* (National Museum of Man, 1976) treats storytelling as a means of conveying the personal significance of events. Revitalization work is extensive: speakers of various dialects are compiling dictionaries for their communities, including the Cree Language Resource Project's online bilingual dictionary; free Cree-language electronic books for beginners became available to Alberta teachers in 2013; the Cree School Board publishes its annual report in Cree as well as English; and a movie has been dubbed into Cree in the Northwest Territories, where a Medicinal Plant Guide was produced in Cree and English with elders' input. Joshua Whitehead is among the writers using the Cree language in poetry.

## Language & religion

Cree — also called Cree–Montagnais–Naskapi — is a dialect continuum of Algonquian languages spoken by approximately 86,475 people across Canada in 2021, making it the Indigenous language with the most speakers in the country; the Northwest Territories is the only jurisdiction granting it official status, alongside eight other Aboriginal languages. Endonyms vary by dialect: *nêhiyawêwin* (Plains), *nīhithawīwin* (Woods), *ininîmowin* (Eastern Swampy), *ililîmowin* (Moose), *iyiyiu-Ayamiwin* (Northern East Cree), *nehirâmowin* (Atikamekw). Speakers of the western and central dialects write in Cree syllabics, a variant of Canadian Aboriginal syllabics with Western and Eastern forms, while the easternmost dialects use the Latin script exclusively; Standard Roman Orthography serves Plains and Swampy Cree. Cree also underlies the contact languages Michif, Northern Michif, Bungi, Oji-Cree and Nehipwat. Cree religion has no formal leadership or organizational structure and displays much internal variation, shares many elements with Ojibwe practice, and centres on an animistic understanding of the world and on the ethics of the hunt.
