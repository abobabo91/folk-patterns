## Overview

The Washo, also called Wašiw or waší:šiw, are a Great Basin Native American tribe living near Lake Tahoe on the California–Nevada border. Their territory historically included the eastern Sierra Nevada, the Pine Nut Mountains, the Virginia Range, the Carson, Truckee, and West Walker rivers, and the Sierra Valley. Washoe people have lived in the Great Basin and eastern Sierra Nevada for at least 6,000 years, with some estimates extending to 9,000 years or more. Their language is a linguistic isolate and is now endangered; only 20 elderly native speakers were reported in 2011. Washoe culture is documented through seasonal fishing, gathering, hunting, storytelling, ceremonies, land knowledge, and language-revitalization work.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources provide only limited information about clothing. During the spring fishing season, Washo people used loin clothes and blankets made from rabbit skin to protect themselves from the cold.

### Architecture

Historical Washo houses included conical bark slab houses. Earlier Martis pit houses gave way to these conical bark slab houses in the period associated with historic Washo culture. The sources also state that people used caves and natural shelters during the spring fishing season.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do describe rabbit-skin blankets, natural shelters, fishing grounds, and food-preservation practices in which fish were dried in the sun.

## Music & performance

The sources describe Washo storytelling as an important means of transmitting knowledge. Legends were handed down from one generation to another and told to younger people to teach gathering techniques, medicine preparation, respect for the land, and ways of living. Older tribe members conveyed knowledge to younger generations so that Washo culture could survive. The Pine Nut Dance is identified as an important ceremony.

The Washo language itself is central to cultural renewal. *wašiw wagayay maŋal*, translated in the source as “the house where Wašiw is spoken,” was the first attempt by Wašiw people to renew their language for future generations. A small immersion school had operated since 1994 and produced moderately fluent younger speakers; after its closure, language programming continued through the Cultural Resource Department of the Washoe Tribe.

## Dance & theatre

The Pine Nut Dance remains a very important Washo ceremony.

The sources also identify girls’ puberty rites as an important ceremony. No theatre, masked performance, shadow-puppet tradition, or dramatic genre is documented.

## Festivals & rituals

The Washo ceremonial life described in the sources includes the Pine Nut Dance and girls’ puberty rites. The Pine Nut Dance remains important, while girls’ puberty rites are identified as continuing ceremonies.

Lake Tahoe has been a center of Washo yearly cultural gatherings, where most traditional events took place. Seasonal life was nevertheless organized around fishing, gathering, and hunting. The “fishing year” began in early spring as mountain snow melted; the tribe was expected to return to the shores of Lake Tahoe by the beginning of June. The “gathering year” could take place throughout the year, while the “hunting year” began when the first animals appeared at the beginning of spring. Fall was the richest food season, and winter was a period of starvation when food stores quickly ran out. Girls’ puberty rites are the only specifically identified life-cycle practice.

## Foodways

Washo foodways were organized around fishing, gathering, and hunting. Piñon pine nuts gathered in the fall provided much of the food eaten during winter. Roots, seeds, berries, and game supplied food during the rest of the year. Washo people also relied heavily on fishing at Lake Tahoe and in surrounding streams; each family traditionally had its own fishing grounds.

During the spring fishing season, Washo people caught whitefish. Some fish were eaten immediately, while some were carried back to winter camps so that people could eat and regain strength before returning to the lake. Fish were preserved by drying them in the sun, creating reserves for the future.

Gathering was usually performed by women, while men practiced fishing or hunting. Hunting included larger animals such as deer, bears, and antelope, as well as rabbits, birds, and squirrels. Different techniques and hunting times were adapted to different animals.

## Oral tradition & literature

Washoe culture was based largely on legends explaining different areas of life. These legends were transmitted through storytelling from one generation to another and told to younger people as instruction in Washo ways of living. They taught gathering techniques, medicine preparation, appreciation of the land, and an understanding of Washo life.

Children were raised in an environment that recognized family as the most valued social unit. Storytelling supported the wider system of cooperation and unity in which family members had roles in fishing, gathering, and hunting. The sources state that older people conveyed knowledge to younger people so that Washo culture would survive.

The sources mention *Washo Tales: Three Original Washo Indian Legends* by Grace Dangberg and *Wa She Shu: A Washo Tribal History* by Jo Ann Nevers. Language-revitalization programs, including *wašiw wagayay maŋal*, represent a documented effort to renew Washo language and culture for future generations.

## Language & religion

Washo or Washoe, also called Wašiw or wá:šiw ʔítlu, is an endangered Native American language isolate spoken on the California–Nevada border, especially around Lake Tahoe and in the drainages of the Truckee and Carson rivers. It has no demonstrated relationship with neighboring Northern Paiute, Maidu, or Sierra Miwok, although it has sometimes been classified as Hokan. The language is written in the Latin script and shows little geographic variation.

The language has six distinct vowel qualities, occurring in long and short forms, and uses stress marking and colon notation for vowel length. Washo has a complex tense system, reduplication, prefixation, and suffixation. They do document former reliance on medicine men and knowledge of medicinal plants and ceremonies, much of which was lost through contact with the Western world.
