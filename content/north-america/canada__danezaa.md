## Overview

The Dane-zaa, also spelled Dunne-za or Tsattine, are an Athabaskan-speaking group of First Nations people whose traditional territory is around the Peace River in Alberta and British Columbia, Canada. Europeans historically referred to them as the Beaver. The name Dunne-za has been translated as “Those who live among the beaver,” while Dane-zaa is used for “the Real People.” About 1,600 Dane-zaa reside in British Columbia, including 270 speakers of the Dane-zaa language, and approximately 770 Dane-zaa live in Alberta. The Dane-zaa language is an Athabascan language of western Canada. Their cultural record includes oral histories, Dreamers’ teachings and songs, place-name knowledge, migration stories, language documentation, and continuing cultural and economic presence in the North Peace area.

## Music & performance

Dane-zaa has followed the teachings and songs of Dreamers, who first predicted the coming of the Europeans. The last Dreamer, Charlie Yahey, died in 1976. The sources describe these teachings and songs as part of the continuing cultural tradition of Dane-zaa communities.

A 2011 CD by Garry Oker features traditional Beaver language chanting with world beat and country music.

The language documentation associated with Dane Wajich: Dane-zaa Stories and Songs—Dreamers and the Land made Dane-zaa pronunciations and other resources on Dane-zaa culture available to the public. The DoBeS Beaver documentation project collected the language as spoken by elders of Beaver First Nations communities in Alberta and British Columbia from 2004–2011. These materials include stories connected with culturally relevant locations and personal migration stories.

## Festivals & rituals

The Peace River is described in Dane-zaa oral history as the site of a settlement of conflict between the Dane-zaa and the Cree. A peace treaty, negotiated in the late 1700s or early 1800s, stated that the Cree would live south of the Peace River and the Dane-zaa north. Before and after its new name, the Peace River marked a boundary zone where groups met for trade, celebration, and settling disputes.

The sources also identify Suu Na Chii Kʼchinge as the traditional meeting place for the Dane-zaa. Its loss, together with residential schools, resulted in language loss.

The Dane-zaa of Fort St John took an adhesion to Treaty 8 in 1900.

## Foodways

The historical sources identify bison meat and grease as important provisions supplied by the Dane-zaa to traders’ expeditions during hunting on the rich prairies of the upper Peace River area. By the time the Hudson’s Bay Company took over the North West Company in 1823, bison were scarce.

## Oral tradition & literature

Dane-zaa oral history explains the naming of the Peace River through the settlement of a conflict between the Dane-zaa and the Cree. Doig oral history confirms that the ancestors of present Dane-zaa families were in the upper Peace River area before the first contact by Alexander Mackenzie in 1793. A post journal of 1799–1800 mentions people trading at a post who can be identified as ancestors of members of the former Fort St John Band, now the Doig River and Blueberry River First Nations.

Teachings and songs of Dreamers are central to the historical and spiritual record described by the sources. The Dane-zaa Stories and Songs—Dreamers and the Land project created a virtual library of Dane-zaa pronunciations and other resources on Dane-zaa culture. Robin and Jillian Ridington’s *Where Happiness Dwells: A History of the Dane-zaa First Nations*, published by UBC Press in 2013, presents Dane-zaa oral history from pre-history to the present day.

## Language & religion

Dane-zaa, known in the language as Dane-ẕaa Ẕáágéʔ, is an Athabascan language of western Canada. The name means “people-regular language.” It is closely related to languages spoken by neighbouring Athabaskan groups, including Slavey, Sekani, Tsuu T’ina, Chipewyan, and Kaska.

The language has two main dialect groups: high-marked and low-marked. High-marked dialects include Boyer River, Child Lake, Prophet River, Blueberry River, and Doig River. Low-marked dialects include Halfway River and West Moberly Lake. Dane-zaa has 35 consonants and 10 phonemic vowels; two vowels contrast oral and nasal qualities. It has gender-neutral pronouns, including ma- for “His/Her/It” and da- for “His/Her own.”

English is now the first language of most Dane-zaa children and many adults. Dane-zaa was the primary language until children began to be sent to school in the 1950s, and English became dominant in the 1980s. The 1918 Spanish flu epidemic contributed to language loss, as did residential schools and the loss of Suu Na Chii Kʼchinge.

They do document the teachings and songs of Dreamers, including their predictions of the coming of Europeans.
