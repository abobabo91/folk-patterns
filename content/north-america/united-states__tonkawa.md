## Overview

The Tonkawa are a Native American tribe from Oklahoma and Texas. Their autonym is *Tickanwa•tic*, meaning “real people,” while the name Tonkawa derives from the Waco word *Tonkaweya*, meaning “they all stay together.” Historically, they were made up of related bands, lived as nomadic people, and practiced some horticulture. Their traditional movements included northwestern Oklahoma, Central Texas, northern Mexico, and the Red River region. Today, Tonkawa people are enrolled in the federally recognized Tonkawa Tribe of Indians of Oklahoma, headquartered in Tonkawa, Oklahoma. The tribe has more than 700 tribal citizens, and its numbers had increased to close to 950 as of 2023. Their language is a linguistic isolate and is now extinct as a first language.

## Festivals & rituals

The annual Tonkawa Powwow is held on the last weekend in June. It commemorates the end of the tribe’s own Trail of Tears, during which the Tonkawa were forcibly removed and relocated from their traditional lands to present-day Oklahoma.

The City of Austin and leadership from the Tonkawa Tribe celebrated Austin-Tonkawa Friendship Day on September 12, 2024.

The Tonkawa purchased Sugarloaf Mountain near Gause in Milam County on December 12, 2023. The mountain is known by the tribe as “Red Mountain,” forms part of its origin story, and is associated with plans for a historic park.

## Foodways

Historical accounts state that Tonkawa people practiced some horticulture. A 1826 account attributed to Stephen F. Austin refers to Tonkawa people taking hogs and corn from settlers, and a later account describes Tonkawa people trading with residents of Bastrop at a dry-goods store. These passages do not provide a systematic description of Tonkawa foodways or cuisine.

The sources also mention buffalo hunting, including an account of Chief Campos returning from a buffalo hunt in 1843, and a plan to hunt mustangs and deer.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Tonkawa language source presents the first four sentences of *Coyote and Jackrabbit* from Hoijer’s *Tonkawa Texts*. The passage describes Coyote travelling on the prairie, Jackrabbit lying down, Coyote speaking, and Coyote catching Jackrabbit. The supplied source gives the Tonkawa text and an English gloss.

The Tonkawa language is described as having few speakers, with revitalization underway.

## Language & religion

Tonkawa is a language isolate with no known related languages. It was spoken in Oklahoma, Texas, and New Mexico by the Tonkawa people, and it has had no first-language speakers since the mid 20th century. Few Tonkawa people speak the language, but revitalization is underway.

The language has 10 vowels and 15 consonants. Its writing systems include an orthography based on Americanist phonetic notation and a somewhat different phonemic orthography used in Hoijer’s *Tonkawa Texts*. The sources describe affixation, subject-object-verb word order, reduplication, enclitics, and syllable structures.
