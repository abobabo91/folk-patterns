## Overview

The Chiricahua are a band of Apache Native Americans historically based in the Southern Plains and Southwestern United States. Their territory extended across 15 million acres in Southwestern New Mexico, Southeastern Arizona, Northern Sonora, and Chihuahua. Today, Chiricahua live in Northern Mexico and the United States, where they are primarily enrolled in the Fort Sill Apache Tribe, the Mescalero Apache Tribe, and the San Carlos Apache Tribe. The Chiricahua historically shared language, customs, territory, and family relations with other Apache groups. Their language, Mescalero-Chiricahua, belongs to the Southern Athabaskan branch of the Na-Dene language family. Chiricahua culture includes distinct bands, ceremonial practices, dances, religious traditions, and efforts to preserve the language.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources identify one Chiricahua name associated with clothing: *Tã'aa'ji k'ee'déõkaa'õde*, glossed as “The Ones who are Covered [with breech cloths].” Chiricahua names also include references to ceremonial paint, such as *Chíhéõde*, “The People of Red Ceremonial Paint.”

### Architecture

 They mention hidden camps built by Chiricahua groups in the Sierra Madre mountains, but provide no architectural description.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention pinole, a ground corn flour, and hoddentin, ceremonially prepared cattail pollen.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 They refer to red ceremonial paint and breech cloths in names and descriptions.

## Music & performance

 They do document ceremonial performance in connection with religion. Hoddentin, ceremonially prepared cattail pollen, was used in many Chiricahuan rituals. John Gregory Bourke recorded that the Chiricahua offered hoddentin to the sun, threw it after snakes, and used it in medicine dances and around dying people.

The sources also describe traditional dances and other ceremonies performed by Chiricahuas from Mexico at the Fiesta de los Remedios in Comonfort, Guanajuato. No instruments, musical forms, song texts, performance ensembles, or distinctions between secular and ceremonial music are described. The language documentation associated with Mescalero-Chiricahua includes traditional religious and secular stories.

## Dance & theatre

Chiricahuas from Mexico participate every year in the Fiesta de los Remedios in Comonfort, Guanajuato, where they represent and perform their traditional dances and other ceremonies.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources name the Fiesta de los Remedios in Comonfort, Guanajuato as an annual setting in which Chiricahuas from Mexico perform traditional dances and other ceremonies. No date for the festival is given.

Chiricahuan religious traditions include rituals involving Ussen, an all-powerful creator figure; White Painted Woman, who offered herself in sacrifice to end a drought; and Child of the Waters. Hoddentin, ceremonially prepared cattail pollen, was offered to the sun, thrown after snakes, and used in medicine dances and around dying people. Other traditional practices include death rituals and puberty ceremonies for young women. Caves, waterways, and birthplaces hold special spiritual significance.

The sources also describe the Chiricahua as preserving their culture while forming new relationships with surrounding peoples.

## Foodways

The sources mention pinole, described as a ground corn flour. In 1837, John Johnson and his men placed pinole on a blanket during a meeting with Chihenne Apache near the mines at Santa Rita del Cobre, New Mexico.

Hoddentin, made from cattail pollen, is described as a ceremonial substance used in rituals, medicine dances, and around dying people. It is not described as a staple food.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources identify traditional religious and secular stories in the Mescalero-Chiricahua language documentation known as *Chiricahua and Mescalero Apache Texts*, which includes a grammatical sketch and traditional religious and secular stories.

Chiricahuan mythology includes Ussen, an all-powerful creator figure; White Painted Woman, who sacrificed herself to end a drought; and her son, Child of the Waters. The sources also preserve explanations of names and ethnonyms, including *Hák'ą́yé*, “Eastern Sunrise” or “People in the East,” and *Ha’ishu Na gukande*, “Sunrise People.” Virginia Klinekole was known for efforts to preserve the language, and at least one language-immersion school for children exists in Mescalero.

## Language & religion

Mescalero-Chiricahua, also called Chiricahua Apache, is a Southern Athabaskan language spoken by Chiricahua and Mescalero people in Chihuahua and Sonora, México, and in Oklahoma and New Mexico. It is related to Navajo and Western Apache. The Chiricahua language is also called *n'dee biyat'i* and belongs to the Na-Dene language family. It is considered a national language of Mexico and is regulated by the Instituto Nacional de Lenguas Indígenas.

The Chiricahua autonym is given in several dialect forms: *Nde*, *Ne*, *Néndé*, *Héndé*, *Hen-de*, and *õne*, meaning “The People, Men” or “the People of.” The major Chiricahuan deity is Ussen. Other religious figures include White Painted Woman and Child of the Waters. Hoddentin, death rituals, puberty ceremonies for young women, and the spiritual significance of caves, waterways, and birthplaces are documented in the sources.
