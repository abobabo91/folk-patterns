## Overview

The Snohomish people (*sduhubš*) are a Southern Coast Salish people who speak Lushootseed and are Indigenous to the Puget Sound region of Washington State. Their traditional territory centered on the lower Snohomish River and extended across Snohomish, King, and Island counties, including Whidbey Island, Camano Island, Hat Island, the eastern shore of Puget Sound, and parts of the Skykomish River. Most Snohomish are enrolled in the Tulalip Tribes and live on or near the Tulalip Reservation, while others belong to other tribes or the non-recognized Snohomish Tribe of Indians. At least 5,100 members belonged to the Tulalip Tribes as of 2023, most of them of Snohomish ancestry. Potlatching, canoe use, fishing, hunting, gathering, and cultural teachings remain important.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Cedar-plank and cattail-mat house furnishings** — Snohomish longhouses used long cedar planks for construction and cattail mats for insulation and storage.

**Salish Wool Dog blankets** — The Snohomish kept the native Salish Wool Dog, known in Lushootseed as *sqix̌aʔ*, and sheared it for valuable wool used in clothing and blankets. The Snohomish were described as the center of woolly-dog craft among the Coast Salish.

**Mountain-goat wool blankets** — High-quality mountain-goat wool caught in the Cascade Mountains was used to make blankets.

**Feather and fireweed blankets** — Blankets were also made from feathers and fireweed. No named designs or motifs are given.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe clothing and blankets made from Salish Wool Dog wool, mountain-goat wool, feathers, and fireweed. The Snohomish kept the Salish Wool Dog (*sqix̌aʔ*) for its wool, which was highly valuable. Fish was dried, while meat was smoked and dried.

### Architecture

The main Snohomish dwelling was the winter longhouse. These structures were commonly between one-hundred to two-hundred feet long and had either a slanted shed roof or a triangular gable roof. They were built from long cedar planks tied to vertical posts. Rooms were assigned to individual families, while carved and painted house posts were made by the oldest family member. Cattail mats covered the walls, and sleeping platforms and storage shelves ran around the interior. Fireplaces stood along the sides rather than in the center.

Larger villages could include a potlatch house, known as *sgʷigʷialʔtxʷ*, used especially for ceremonies. The largest Snohomish potlatch house stood at *dxʷlilap* and measured 115 feet by 43 feet, with ten carved house posts. Villages such as *hibulb* also had cedar palisades. Temporary summer houses were square, lean-to or gabled structures covered with overlapping mats.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do describe wooden and fiber objects, including carved house posts, cedar palisades, cattail mats, longhouse planks, and boards associated with the spirit *sgʷədilič*. Fishing equipment included river weirs called *stqalikʷ*, dip-nets called *luk̓ʷ*, traps, hooks, and spears. Pine chips were used as flares for fishing at night.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 They mention that shamans could demonstrate their power by wearing a stone or belt that could turn into a snake, but provide no further description of this object.

## Music & performance

Music was closely connected with spirit powers, religious ceremonies, and potlatches. A hunter might sing a spirit song during a hunt, and hearing the spirit sing back was understood as a sign of success. Children traditionally undertook a spirit power journey, or *ʔalacut*, to remote locations where they received a power and its associated song.

The winter spirit power ceremony, *spigʷəd*, was especially important. Spirit powers were believed to return during winter, when a person might become sick and hear the song of their spirit power. The person then hosted a ceremony that could last several days, with singing, dancing, gift-giving, and a concluding potlatch. Friends joined the singing and dancing rather than leaving the person to perform alone.

The *sgʷədilič* was a spirit in the shape of a painted board. Its boards were red and black, about one-and-a-half feet long, and pierced by a hole. During its winter ceremony, four men danced in pairs, one carrying a larger board and one a smaller one. They danced counter-clockwise, and the dances traditionally lasted four days and four nights. Songs were sung four times fast and four times slow.

## Dance & theatre

The sources describe ceremonial dancing connected with spirit powers. In the *sgʷədilič* ceremony, four men danced in pairs while holding painted boards, placing a hand through each board’s hole and moving counter-clockwise. The dance traditionally continued for four days and four nights. During the winter spirit power ceremony, friends helped the person whose spirit had returned by singing and dancing with them.

## Festivals & rituals

Winter was traditionally reserved for religious observances and ceremonies, while summer was a period of travel across the territory for hunting, gathering, and fishing. The winter spirit power ceremony, *spigʷəd*, took place when a person’s spirit power was believed to return. The ceremony could last several days and involved singing, dancing, gifts to friends and family, and usually a potlatch.

Spirit power journeys, called *ʔalacut*, generally took place in spring and were best undertaken during a storm. Children traveled to remote locations, including Stevens Pass and Lake Getchel in the Cascades, where they sought spirit powers through fasting, repeated bathing, diving, and other difficult acts. Both women and men could acquire spirits, although men tended to receive more powerful ones. They do describe potlatches as important concluding events for some religious ceremonies and as a continuing Snohomish tradition.

## Foodways

The Snohomish traditionally relied on the abundant resources of Puget Sound. They caught steelhead, dog, pink, sockeye, and king salmon, as well as trout, sturgeon, and flounder, in lakes, rivers, and saltwater. Clams, cockles, and mussels were gathered along the coast. Hunting provided bear, deer, beaver, elk, goat, duck, and goose. Fish was mainly dried, while meat was smoked and dried.

Fishing techniques included river weirs, called *stqalikʷ*, from which fish were taken with dip-nets called *luk̓ʷ*. Traps, hooks, and spears were also used. At night, fishers practiced flare fishing with pine-chip flares. Roots, berries, and native vegetables were gathered in prairies, forests, and marshes. Berries, especially blackberries, were dried into cakes for dessert or later use. Flour and potatoes entered the diet through trade with settlers.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They do describe storytelling as an activity associated with the replica traditional shed-roof longhouse at the Hibulb Cultural Center. The Snohomish also preserve religious teachings known in Lushootseed as *x̌əč̓usadad*. The Tulalip Tribes are working to revitalize the Lushootseed language and preserve and restore traditional cultural teachings.

## Language & religion

The Snohomish are a Lushootseed-speaking Southern Coast Salish people. Traditionally they spoke a subdialect of Northern Lushootseed, although English became more common in modern times. The name *sduhubš* is a Lushootseed term; its meaning has been interpreted as “many men,” “lots of people,” or “lowland people.” The Tulalip Tribes are engaged in language revitalization.

Traditional religion centers on spirit powers or guardian spirits, called *sqəlalitut*. These powers could assist with hunting, warfare, healing, wealth, and other activities. Important spirit powers included *tiyuɬbax̌*, associated with wealth and property, and *tubšədad*, associated with war and courage. The *ʔəyahus*, or Ayahos, was an elk-snake spirit believed to live in the forest. Shamans, called *dxʷdahəb*, possessed distinctive spirits used to heal or harm people. After colonization, many Snohomish converted to the Indian Shaker Church, and a Shaker church was built at Tulalip.
