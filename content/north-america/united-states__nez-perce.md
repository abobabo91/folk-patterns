## Overview

The Nez Perce are an Indigenous Plateau people whose ancestral lands covered parts of four present-day states. They moved seasonally between winter villages and temporary camps while fishing, hunting, and gathering. Salmon was an important cultural and economic resource, and the people also hunted buffalo in Montana. Their heritage includes woven bags, clothing, beadwork, oral tradition, and language preservation.

## Material culture

Nez Perce material culture includes fibre bags, baskets, corn-husk bags, decorated hide clothing, beadwork, shell chokers, and everyday objects such as baby-carriers and pouches.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Nez Perce textile traditions include twined fibre bags, corn-husk bags, baskets, grass bags, and geometric patterns.

- **Fibre bags** — Fibre bags were made with a twining technique.
- **Cornhusk wallets and bags** — These used corn husk, hop twine, wool yarn, and deerskin, with geometric designs, roses, leaves, and diamond shapes.
- **Cornhusk bag from Kamiah** — This flat plant-fibre bag had a twisted closing cord and purple, pink, and blue geometric patterns.
- **Basket** (*korg*) — A museum record identifies a korg, or basket, associated with the Nez Perce and the Rocky Mountains.
- **Beaded clothing and bags** — Museum photographs show a Nez Perce woman wearing a beaded dress and holding decorated bags.

### Clothing & dress

Portraits show decorated hide clothing, moccasins, chokers, headdresses, headbands, and embroidered pouches.

- **Jason’s decorated hide outfit** (*Kol-kol-shu-a-tash*) — Jason wore a decorated fringed hide tunic, decorated hide leggings, moccasins, a shell choker, and a fur headdress.
- **Timothy’s decorated hide outfit** (*Tomoson*) — Timothy wore a decorated fringed hide tunic, decorated hide leggings, moccasins, a shell and bear claw choker, and a feathered headband.
- **Beaded dress** — A Nez Perce woman was photographed wearing a beaded dress and ornament at Lapwai, Idaho.
- **Embroidered pouch** — Timothy wore an embroidered pouch across his torso.

### Architecture

The sources show a clapboard structure but do not describe Nez Perce house forms or named buildings.

- **Clapboard structure** — A Nez Perce woman was photographed posing before a clapboard structure described in the catalogue as “house?”.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Recorded everyday objects include a deer-skin baby-carrier, bags, wallets, baskets, pipes, pouches, and canes.

- **Baby-carrier** — A baby-carrier was made of deer skin, leather, and brass.
- **Pipes** — Portrait records show pipes.
- **Embroidered pouches** — Portrait records show embroidered pouches.
- **Canes** — Portrait records show canes.
- **Corn-husk wallets** — Corn-husk wallets were made with corn husk, hop twine, wool yarn, and deerskin.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment included shell and bear claw chokers, fur headdresses, feathered headbands, ornaments, and beadwork.

- **Shell choker** — Jason wore a shell choker.
- **Shell and bear claw choker** — Timothy wore a shell and bear claw choker.
- **Fur headdress** — Jason wore a fur headdress.
- **Feathered headband** — Timothy wore a headband with a feather.
- **Nose piercing name** (*Nez Percé*) — French Canadian fur traders applied the name Nez Percé, meaning “pierced nose,” although the tribe did not practice nose piercing.

## Music & performance

Nez Perce sources describe spirit-singing and the idea of singing one’s spirit song.

- **Spirit song** (*wéeyekweʔnipse*) — The term wéeyekweʔnipse is glossed as “to sing one’s spirit song.”
- **Spirit-singing** — Nez Perce spirit-singing is described as a subject of performance and metaphor.
- **Nez Perce Stories** (*Titwáatit*) — Titwáatit: (Nez Perce Stories) is listed among Nez Perce language-learning materials.

## Dance & theatre

The sources do not document named Nez Perce dances, theatre, masks, or other staged performances.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe seasonal movement and relationships with fishing, hunting, and gathering places but give no annual festival calendar or named life-cycle rites.

- **Seasonal movement** — People moved between permanent winter villages and temporary camps, usually returning to the same locations each year.
- **Celilo Falls salmon fishing** — Salmon fishing was important at Celilo Falls before The Dalles Dam was built in 1957.

## Foodways

Nez Perce foodways centered on foraging, salmon fishing, hunting, and gathering camas.

- **Salmon** — Salmon was an important cultural and economic resource, and hatching, harvesting, and eating salmon remain important.
- **Buffalo hunting** — The people traveled to the Plains of Montana to hunt buffalo.
- **Camas prairie sites** — The sources mention camas prairie sites as important places.
- **Fishing and hunting grounds** — Fishing and hunting grounds were important parts of seasonal food procurement.

## Oral tradition & literature

Nez Perce oral tradition and literature are represented by stories, language documentation, and language revitalization work.

- **Name interpretation** — Nez Perce oral tradition is mentioned in connection with interpreting the name cú·pŉitpeľu.
- **Nez Perce Stories** (*Titwáatit*) — Titwáatit: (Nez Perce Stories) is listed in the language sources.
- **Language revitalization** — The Nez Perce Tribe is undertaking a program to reintroduce the language into native usage.

## Language & religion

Nez Perce is a highly endangered Sahaptian language with complex grammar, while the sources give only limited information about religion.

- **Nez Perce language** (*nimipuutímt*) — Nez Perce, also called nimipuutímt, belongs to the Sahaptian branch of the Plateau Penutian family.
- **People’s name** (*nimíipuu*) — The Nez Perce call themselves nimíipuu, meaning “we, the people.”
- **Spirit-singing** (*wéeyekweʔnipse*) — The sources describe spirit-singing but do not provide a broader account of spiritual practices.
