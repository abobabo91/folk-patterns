## Overview

The Cherokee are Indigenous people of the Southeastern Woodlands. Their ancestral lands included river valleys and mountain ridges across several present-day states. Today, the three federally recognized Cherokee tribes are the Cherokee Nation, the United Keetoowah Band, and the Eastern Band. Cherokee culture includes basketry, dances, oral traditions, spiritual beliefs, and language preservation.

## Material culture

Cherokee material culture includes cane baskets, wooden objects, shell rattles, clothing, silver adornment, and objects linked to hunting and ceremony.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Cherokee basketry used cane and patterns such as diagonal lines, triangles, rectangles, and braided cogs.

- **Cane baskets** — Cherokee baskets were made from cane using plaiting and geometric patterns.
- **Basketry trays** — Cane was also used to make basketry trays with diagonal lines, triangles, and rectangles.
- **Braided cogs** — Cogs of basket braiding appear in the documented pattern vocabulary.

### Clothing & dress

A documented Cherokee outfit included cloth clothing, mockasons, a mantle, and possible silver and wampum ornaments.

- **Mockasons** — Mockasons were shoes peculiar to Americans and were ornamented with porcupine quills.
- **Large mantle** (*match-coat*) — A large mantle, or match-coat, could be thrown over the clothing.
- **Wampum collar** (*wampum*) — Those who could afford it wore a collar made from wampum beads cut from clam-shells.
- **Silver breast-plate** — Some people wore a silver breast-plate over their clothing.
- **Cloth-boots** — The recorded clothing included cloth-boots.

### Architecture

The Council House was a seven-sided meeting house and the center of government in Cherokee towns.

- **Council House** (*ga-tu-yi*) — The Council House had seven sides, seven clan sections, and a sacred fire.
- **River-valley towns** — Cherokee towns were autonomous communities along river valleys and mountain ridges.
- **Platform mound** — Some towns in the region had a platform mound as a political center for smaller villages.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Documented Cherokee objects include wooden tools, stone pipes, shell rattles, masks, baskets, arrows, and blower pipes.

- **Wooden spoon** — A wooden spoon from the Qualla Reservation was used in food preparation.
- **Stone tobacco pipe-bowl** — A stone tobacco pipe-bowl is recorded in British Museum collections.
- **Terrapin-shell rattle** — Terrapin-shell rattles were filled with stones and used as objects connected with dance.
- **Snake mask** — A wooden mask had a human face and a snake.
- **Blower pipe** — A blower pipe was associated with Cherokee arrow poison.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Cherokee adornment included wampum, silver, feathers, beads, stained deer hair, stretched ears, and skin markings.

- **Wampum beads** (*wampum*) — Wampum beads were cut from clam-shells and worn in collars and hair.
- **Silver pendants** — Silver pendants were worn on the ears.
- **Silver rings** — Silver rings could be worn on the ears and nose.
- **Decorated hair patch** — A patch left at the back of the head could be decorated with beads, feathers, wampum, and stained deer hair.
- **Skin figures** — People were painted or had gun-powder pricked into the skin in figures.

## Music & performance

The sources document dance rattles, sacred-fire dancing, and prayers, but not named Cherokee song styles or musical repertories.

- **Seven-shell dance rattle** — Seven terrapin shells filled with stones were tied to the upper part of a boot with hide strips.
- **Shaft rattle** — A terrapin shell filled with stones was socketed into a hide-wrapped shaft.
- **Sacred-fire dancing** — People danced around the sacred fire, which was said to unite them as one mind.
- **Prayers to the thunder beings** — Priests prayed to the thunder beings for rain and blessings from the South.

## Dance & theatre

Cherokee culture includes many named social and ceremonial dances, including the Eagle Tail Dance and the Uku Dance.

- **Booger Dance** — The Booger Dance is one of the named Cherokee dances.
- **Stomp Dance** — The Stomp Dance is one of the named Cherokee dances.
- **War Dance** — The War Dance is one of the named Cherokee dances.
- **Eagle Tail Dance** — The Eagle Tail Dance is described as a victory dance.
- **Uku Dance** (*Uku*) — The Uku Dance is also called the Ookah Dance.

## Festivals & rituals

Cherokee ceremonial life included seven primary ceremonies, purification in flowing water, sacred fire, weddings, and other meetings.

- **New Moon Festival** (*First Festival*) — The New Moon Festival was also called the First Festival.
- **Green Corn Ceremony** (*Second Festival*) — The Green Corn Ceremony was also called the Second Festival.
- **Friends Made Ceremony** (*Propitiation Festival*) — The Friends Made Ceremony was also called the Propitiation Festival and Fifth Festival.
- **Uku Dance ceremony** (*Uku*) — The Uku or Ookah Dance was performed every seven years.
- **Going to the water** — The going to the water rite involved prayers and new water from free-flowing streams.

## Foodways

Cherokee foodways centered on corn, beans, hunted meat, and other cultivated plants.

- **Eastern flint corn** — Eastern flint corn produced larger crops and supported agricultural surpluses.
- **Corn and beans** — Corn and beans were important foods and appeared in the story of Selu.
- **Hunted meat** — Meat from hunted animals was an important part of Cherokee foodways.
- **Cultivated plants** — Agriculture included native squash, marsh elder, lambsquarters, pigweed, and sunflowers.
- **Wooden food spoon** — A wooden spoon from the Qualla Reservation was used in food preparation.

## Oral tradition & literature

Cherokee oral traditions tell of creation, corn, medicine, animals, spiritual beings, and migration from the Great Lakes region.

- **Story of Corn and Medicine** — The story tells of Kanáti, Selu, hunting, corn, beans, animals, and plants.
- **Kanáti** (*The Lucky Hunter*) — Kanáti is called The Lucky Hunter in the Story of Corn and Medicine.
- **Selu** (*Corn*) — Selu is called Corn and explains the origins of corn and beans.
- **Great Thunder** — Traditions describe the Great Thunder and the two Thunder Boys.
- **Little People** — The Little People are among the spiritual beings named in Cherokee traditions.

## Language & religion

Cherokee is an endangered Iroquoian language with a syllabary, and Cherokee spirituality describes humans living with the natural world.

- **Cherokee language** (*Tsalagi Gawonihisdi*) — Cherokee, or Tsalagi Gawonihisdi, is the only Southern Iroquoian language spoken today.
- **Cherokee syllabary** — Sequoyah created an 85-character syllabary that the Cherokee Nation adopted in 1825.
- **Kituwah dialect** (*Kituwah*) — The Middle, or Kituwah, dialect is spoken by the Eastern Band on the Qualla Boundary.
- **Overhill dialect** (*Otali*) — The Western, Overhill, or Otali dialect predominates in eastern Oklahoma.
- **Three worlds** (*Upper World; Under World; This World*) — Traditional cosmology describes the Upper World, the Under World, and This World.
