## Overview

Karuk culture is rooted along the Klamath River, with Happy Camp at its heart. Cultural life includes hunting, gathering, fishing, basketmaking, plant knowledge, ceremonial dances, and language work. Several ceremonies last for days and aim to heal and “fix the world.” The Karuk language remains active through revitalization programs.

## Material culture

Karuk material culture is best documented through twined basketry made from plant materials and sometimes animal materials.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Karuk basketry uses many plant and animal materials and includes caps, hats, trays, baskets, and containers.

- **Karuk basketry** — Twined basketry uses bear grass, roots, sticks, ferns, and porcupine quill.
- **Ceremonial basket caps** — Basket caps use plant materials and may carry designs such as grizzly paw, friendship, house ladder, or snake nose.
- **Woman’s basketry hat** — A recorded woman’s hat is made of basketry and may use pine root.
- **Wooden and root trays** — Recorded trays combine wood, roots, ferns, and porcupine quill.
- **Lidded baskets** — Lidded baskets were made from wood, roots, woodwardia fern, and bear grass, including a possible seed-basket lid.

### Clothing & dress

The profile gives little information about clothing beyond basketry hats and ceremonial caps.

- **Woman’s basketry hat** — One catalogue record identifies a woman’s basketry hat, possibly made with pine root.
- **Ceremonial basket caps** — The sources identify ceremonial caps and basket hats but do not describe other garments.

## Music & performance

Ceremonial performances last several days and seek healing, good harvests, social goodwill, and good luck.

- **Brush Dance** — The Brush Dance is a several-day ceremony for healing and repairing the world.
- **Jump Dance** — The Jump Dance is a several-day ceremony that includes prayer for plentiful acorns, deer, and salmon.
- **Pikyavish** (*Pikyáv*) — Pikyavish is a ceremony concerned with healing, repairing the world, goodwill, and good luck.
- **Pikyáv film** (*Pikyáv (to fix it)*) — A 2008 film documents the subject of Pikyavish and world repair.

## Dance & theatre

The named ceremonial dances are the Brush Dance, Jump Dance, and Pikyavish, and the sources do not describe theatre.

- **Brush Dance** — The Brush Dance lasts several days and is practiced for healing and repairing the world.
- **Jump Dance** — The Jump Dance includes prayer for plentiful acorns, deer, and salmon.
- **Pikyavish** (*Pikyáv*) — Pikyavish is practiced to restore social goodwill and individual good luck.

## Festivals & rituals

The profile names several-day ceremonies but gives no annual calendar or dates.

- **Brush Dance** — The Brush Dance serves healing and world repair.
- **Jump Dance** — The Jump Dance includes prayers for plentiful acorns, deer, and salmon.
- **Pikyavish** (*Pikyáv*) — Pikyavish supports healing, prayer, social goodwill, and good luck.
- **Tobacco cultivation** — Karuk people traditionally sowed and harvested a form of tobacco.

## Foodways

Karuk foodways center on hunting, gathering, and fishing along the Klamath River, with detailed plant and animal knowledge.

- **Acorns** — Ceremonies include prayers for plentiful acorns.
- **Deer** — Ceremonies include prayers for plentiful deer.
- **Salmon** — Ceremonies include prayers for plentiful salmon.
- **Eels** — Coastal woodfern fronds were used as anti-microbial agents while preparing eels.
- **Tobacco** — Karuk people cultivated a form of tobacco.

## Language & religion

Karuk is a language isolate with three tones, and revitalization includes bilingual education and immersion.

- **Karuk language** (*Karuk*) — Karuk is also called Karok and has high, low, and falling tones.
- **Karuk Language Restoration Committee** — The committee created plans for recording fluent speakers, training new speakers, and supporting language programs.
- **Standardized writing system** — A standardized Karuk writing system was adopted in the 1980s.
- **Master–apprentice immersion** — A master–apprentice immersion program began in 1992.
- **Ceremonial world repair** (*Pikyáv*) — Ceremonial practices focus on healing, repairing the world, prayer, social goodwill, and good luck.
