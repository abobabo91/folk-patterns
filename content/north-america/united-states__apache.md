## Overview

The Apache are several peoples related to the Navajo. Their historic homeland, called Apacheria, included mountains, valleys, canyons, deserts and the southern Great Plains. Apache tribes are politically autonomous and are based mainly in Arizona, New Mexico, Texas and Oklahoma. Apache material culture includes baskets, water containers, clothing, rattles, masks and figures.

## Material culture

Apache material culture uses skin, willow, sumac, wood, beads, feathers, shell, yarn and plant materials for clothing, baskets, containers and ritual objects.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Apache basketry uses coiling, twining, plant materials and distinctive designs.

- **San Carlos coiled tray basket** — This basket has a conventionalized floral design, and its broken rim line was said to keep the maker’s spirit from being caught in the design.
- **White Mountain bowl basket** — This basket has a characteristic design and a close, regular Apache stitch.
- **San Carlos burden basket** — This small twined basket uses sumac ribs, a cottonwood border, black martynia decoration and buckskin.
- **Jicarilla water jar** — This coiled willow jar is lined with pitch, made watertight and fitted with a stopper.
- **Apache motif vocabulary** — Recorded patterns include floral designs, parallel lines, red and yellow strips, colored beadwork and black martynia decoration.

### Clothing & dress

Recorded Apache clothing uses skin, fringes, colored strips, beadwork, blankets and feathered ornaments.

- **Fringed skin shirt** — This light tanned skin shirt has open sleeves, long lower fringes and red and yellow strips hanging from the front and back.
- **Five-feather cap** — This cap has five upright eagle feathers, meaningful colored beadwork and a leather base.
- **Apache figures and photographed clothing** — Records show fringed shirts, cloth dresses, blankets, jackets, waistcoats, headscarves, ties and feathered hair ornaments.

### Architecture

The sources give only a limited picture of Apache dwellings and mobile shelters.

- **Cowhide tent** — Mobile plains groups used tents made from tanned and greased cowhides.
- **Dog-carried camp** — Dogs carried tents, poles and belongings for mobile groups on the plains.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Recorded everyday objects are mainly baskets, water containers, tobacco containers, medical figures, rattles and weapons.

- **Leather tobacco canteen** — A museum record lists a leather tobacco canteen among Apache objects.
- **Wooden medicine figure** — A wooden figure was wrapped with colored yarn and fitted with stone and blue and red feathers.
- **Hoof rattle** — Some Apache rattles were made with bound hooves.
- **Bow and arrow** — A photograph shows a Jicarilla Apache man with a bow and arrow.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Apache adornment records feature beads, shells, feathers, medals and embroidered objects.

- **Beaded doll adornment** — An Apache doll has glass-bead embroidery and long and round beads in the ears, plus a necklace of long red and white beads.
- **Shell choker** — Photographs record shell chokers as Apache adornment.
- **Shell ear ornaments** — Photographs record shell ear ornaments.
- **Feather ornaments** — Records show feather ornaments and a cap with eagle feathers.
- **Embroidered horn** — A museum photograph record includes an embroidered horn.

## Music & performance

The sources record flutes, rattles, masks and a medicine figure.

- **End-blown flute** — A Metropolitan Museum record identifies a flute made of wood, skin, shell and beads.
- **Polychrome flute** — Another flute is made of wood, polychrome material and sinew.
- **Medicine-man rattle** — One rattle is described as a rattle for a medicine man.
- **Mountain-spirit mask** — This skin-and-wood mask has black and blue symbolic decoration and a cluster of four rattles on its horn tips.
- **Fertility medicine figure** — This wooden figure is associated with fertility and is wrapped in differently colored yarn.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name an Apache crown dancer and describe a masked ritual object, but give no dance sequence or theatre form.

- **Crown dancer** — A British Museum photographic record names an Apache crown dancer.
- **Mountain-spirit mask** — The mask represents a mountain spirit and is identified as a head ornament for a medicine man.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do not give an annual festival calendar or detailed life-cycle rites, but they connect some objects with medicine and spiritual practice.

- **Apache Alliance Summits** — Leaders of nine Apache tribes meet at summits hosted by a different Apache tribe each time.
- **Medicine-man rattle** — A rattle is identified as an object for a medicine man.
- **Mountain-spirit mask** — A skin-and-wood mask represents a mountain spirit.
- **Fertility medicine figure** — A wooden medicine figure is intended for fertility.

## Foodways

Historical accounts describe bison hunting, raw meat and blood drinking, while trade and plant use also supported Apache life.

- **Bison hunting** — Mobile groups on the plains followed and hunted bison and other game.
- **Raw meat and blood** — A historical account says the people ate raw meat and drank the blood of cattle they killed.
- **Pueblo trade** — Pueblo people exchanged maize and woven cotton goods for bison meat, hides and stone-tool materials.
- **Plant use** — Hundreds of plants were used by the Mescalero, with recorded uses also listed for the Western Apache, White Mountain Apache and San Carlos Apache.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources provide historical accounts but no named Apache folktales, poems, proverbs or storytelling cycles.

- **Coronado account** — A description attributed to Francisco Coronado in April 1541 tells of mobile people following bison, living in cowhide tents and using dogs to carry their possessions.
- **Dime novels** — Dime novels helped spread European fame of Apache tenacity and fighting skills.

## Language & religion

Apache languages form a Southern Athabaskan group, while the sources give only limited information about Apache religion.

- **Southern Athabaskan languages** (*Apachean*) — The seven languages are Plains Apache, Western, Navajo, Mescalero, Chiricahua, Jicarilla and Lipan.
- **Self-name** (*Indé*) — Apache speakers use Indé, meaning “person” or “people,” for themselves and their people.
- **Western Apache self-designation** (*N'dee biyat'i*) — This is given as a self-designation for Western Apache.
- **Navajo language names** (*Diné bizaad; Naabeehó bizaad*) — These names are given for Navajo.
- **Endangered Apache languages** — Lipan and Plains Apache are nearly extinct, Chiricahua is severely endangered, and Mescalero, Jicarilla and Western Apache are endangered.
