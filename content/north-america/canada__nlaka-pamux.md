## Overview

The Nlakaʼpamux, also known as the Thompson people, are an Indigenous First Nations people of the Interior Salish language group in southern British Columbia. Their traditional territory includes parts of the North Cascades region of Washington. They live in the Fraser Canyon, Thompson Canyon, and Nicola Country; Lytton is one of their largest communities and is named Camchin or Kumsheen in the Nlakaʼpamux language. The Nlakaʼpamux speak the Interior Salishan language nɬeʔkepmxcín, usually transliterated as Nlakaʼpamuxtsn and known in English as the Thompson language. The sources document their historical names, language, communities, ethnobotanical practices, and contemporary tribal organizations.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources state that the Nlakaʼpamux were the object of Anglican and Roman Catholic missionary efforts in the nineteenth century. By the beginning of the twentieth century, the vast majority belonged to one of those two denominations.

## Foodways

Blueberries (*Vaccinium myrtilloides*) are traditionally used by the Nlakaʼpamux in pies. The leaves of sedge (*Carex*) have been used as brushes for cleaning and as forage for livestock.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources mention *Thompson River Salish Dictionary*, *They Write Their Dreams on the Rock Forever: Rock Writings in the Stein River Valley of British Columbia*, *Spuzzum: Fraser Canyon Histories*, *Shackan Stories*, and *My Name is Seepeetza*. The sources also identify Tara Beagan, G. A. Grisenthwaite, Kevin Loring, and Terese Marie Mailhot as writers.

## Language & religion

Thompson, whose endonym is nɬeʔkepmxcín, is an Interior Salishan language also known as Nlakaʼpamuctsin, Nlakaʼpamux, or Nthlakampx. It is spoken by the Nlakaʼpamux in the Fraser Canyon, Thompson Canyon, and Nicola Country of British Columbia. A distinctive dialect of the Nicola Valley is called Scwʼexmx, which is also the name of the Nlakaʼpamux subgroup living there. The language has a consonant-heavy phonology; its sonorants can be syllabic consonants, and stress is transcribed with an acute accent. One writing system used for Thompson is the North American Phonetic Alphabet. The sources describe Anglican and Roman Catholic missionary activity in the nineteenth century and the resulting denominational affiliation by the beginning of the twentieth century.
