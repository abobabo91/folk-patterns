## Overview

The Chilcotin, also called Tsilhqotʼin, are an Athabaskan-speaking ethnolinguistic group and a North American tribal government living in what is now British Columbia, Canada. They are the most southern of the Athabaskan-speaking Indigenous peoples in British Columbia. Their name refers to the Chilko River, described as the “red ochre river”; they also call themselves *Nenqayni*, and their country is called *Tŝilhqotʼin Nen*. Today, some 5,000 Tsilhqotʼin people live in Alexandria, north of Williams Lake, and in communities accessible from Williams Lake on Highway 20, including the Nemiah Valley and the Xeni-Gwetʼin. Their country includes the Chilcotin Plateau, with grasslands, forests, lakes, rivers, creeks, ponds, volcanic and glaciated landforms, and snow-covered peaks. Their history includes extensive trade in obsidian, contact with European trading goods, epidemic disease, the Chilcotin War, residential schools, and the 2014 landmark court case establishing Indigenous land title.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Architecture

They mention reserves, mission schools, offices, communities, and two small unincorporated towns, Alexis Creek and Anahim Lake, but provide no architectural description.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do state that, before contact with Europeans, the Tŝilhqotʼin were part of an extensive trade network centred on the control and distribution of obsidian, which was the material of choice for arrowheads and other stone tools.

## Music & performance

The sources do describe the Chilcotin language, or Tŝilhqotʼin, as a Northern Athabaskan language spoken in British Columbia by the Tsilhqotʼin people. It has 47 consonants, 6 vowels, and two tones: high tone and low tone. The language has vowel flattening and consonant harmony; vowel flattening is described as unique to Chilcotin, while consonant harmony is common in the Athabaskan language family. The sources also describe vowel nasalization and laxing, progressive and regressive flattening, and the grouping of consonants into neutral, sharp, and flat categories. No source connects these linguistic features to a documented musical tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do record several historical events affecting Chilcotin communities. European trading goods were first encountered in the 1780s and 1790s, and the North West Company established posts in Carrier territory by 1808. In 1821, the Hudson’s Bay Company established a fur-trade post at Fort Alexandria on the Fraser River, which became a major source of European goods. Epidemics affecting Tsilhqotʼin populations included whooping cough in 1845, measles in 1850, smallpox in 1855, smallpox in 1862–1863, and Spanish flu in 1919. Catholic missionaries sent First Nations children to receive a so-called “formal” education; by 1891, the first group of students had been sent, and the mission school closed circa 1981.

## Foodways

They do mention subsistence agriculture and the cultivation or production of grains, hay, and vegetables. During the 1870s, the loss of hunting territories and crashes of the salmon runs placed more dependence on agricultural produce. Activities included cutting hay, constructing irrigation ditches, and practicing animal husbandry. Settlers’ assumption of water rights made agriculture increasingly fragile, and starvation became a threat. The sources provide no further account of Chilcotin food preparation or cuisine.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that oral history of the bands has continued to recount the effects of deaths during epidemic disease outbreaks. They also describe the region as having produced a relatively large collection of literature mixing naturalism with Indigenous and settler cultures. The bibliography names *Nemiah: The Unconquered Country* by Terry Glavin, *Chilcotin Cowboy* and *Smith and Other Events* by Paul St. Pierre, *Crusoe of Lonesome Lake* by Ralph Edwards, *Chiwid* by Sage Birchwater, *The Chilcotin War* by Mel Rothenburger, and *High Slack: Waddington's Gold Road and the Bute Inlet Massacre of 1864* by Judith Williams.

## Language & religion

Chilcotin, or Tŝilhqotʼin, is a Northern Athabaskan language spoken in British Columbia by the Tsilhqotʼin people. The people call themselves *Nenqayni*, glossed in the sources as “people of the Earth,” “people of the world,” and “Native,” and they call the language *Nenqayni Chʼih*, “the Native way.” The name *Tŝilhqotʼin* is glossed as “people of the red ochre river.” The language has many spelling and pronunciation variations, including Tsilhqotʼin, Tsilhqútʼin, Tsinlhqut’in, and Tŝinlhqut’in. The sources describe no dialect system, historical script, contemporary religious landscape, denomination, or spiritual practice. They do state that Catholic missionaries were sent to convert First Nations children to Christianity and that residential-school education continued for six decades before Indigenous children were allowed into the public school system.
