## Overview

The Coquille are a Native American people historically associated with the Coquille River watershed and the nearby coast south of Coos Bay. Today, Coquille people live both in the traditional homeland, centered in southwest Oregon where the Coos River flows into Coos Bay, and on the Siletz Reservation in northwestern Oregon. They were signatories to the Oregon Coast Tribes Treaty of 1855 and were subsequently removed to the Siletz Reservation in 1856. The Coquille include Lower Coquille Miluk and Upper Coquille Tututni communities. Their historical languages belong to the Coosan and Pacific Coast Athabaskan language groups. Their folk-culture significance is documented through fishing technologies, shellfish gathering, cedar-plank and willow-frame houses, canoe routes, oral histories, and continuing language revitalization efforts.

## Foodways

The Coquille name is described as deriving from a native word for lamprey, which was a staple food for the tribe. The Coquille fished in tidewaters and estuaries along the Oregon coastline using fishing weirs and basket traps, and collected shellfish.

## Oral tradition & literature

Extensive oral histories of the Coquille have been collected and preserved at the Coquille Indian Tribe Library in Coos Bay, Oregon. The sources also identify published works titled *Oral traditions of the Coquille Indians* and *The Coquille Indians: yesterday, today and tomorrow*.

## Language & religion

The Coquille historically spoke two languages: Miluk, a Coosan language, and the Upper Coquille dialect of Lower Rogue River, a Pacific Coast Athabaskan language classified within the Oregon Athabaskan subgroup. Tututni, also known as Upper Coquille, (Lower) Rogue River, and Nuu-wee-ya, is described as an Athabaskan language spoken by the Tututni tribe, the Coquille tribe, and the Chasta Costa tribe. Its dialects include Coquille, Tututni, Euchre Creek, and Chasta Costa. After relocation to the Siletz Reservation, Coquille people used Chinuk Wawa, the reservation’s lingua franca. The Coquille Indian Tribe is involved in revitalizing Miluk and Upper Coquille Athabaskan, while the Confederated Tribes of Siletz have a language program focused on Siletz Dee-ni.
