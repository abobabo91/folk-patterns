## Overview

The Iñupiat (singular *Iñupiaq*) are Alaska Natives whose territory runs northeast from Norton Sound on the Bering Sea to the northernmost stretch of the Canada–United States border. Their communities number 34 villages across *Iñupiat Nunaat*, grouped under three regional corporations created by the 1971 Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act: seven North Slope Borough villages under the Arctic Slope Regional Corporation, eleven in the Northwest Arctic Borough, and sixteen affiliated with the Bering Straits Regional Corporation. The 2000 U.S. Census counted more than 19,000 Iñupiat. The name is built from *iñuk* "person" and *-piaq* "real" — "real people". Their language belongs to the Inuit–Yupik–Unangan family, and the people descend from the Thule culture, which migrated from islands in the Bering Sea to what is now Alaska circa 300 B.C. In folk-culture terms they matter as an Arctic hunting society whose material arts — baleen, walrus ivory, sea-mammal skin and gut — are made almost entirely from the animals they subsist on.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources describe no woven or loom textile tradition. What they document instead are fibre and sheet materials taken from animals and worked by coiling, sewing and stitching.

**Baleen basketry** — Round, coiled baskets built from dark-grey baleen strips and bird quills, set on a base plate of walrus ivory. One British Museum example carries a single decorative strip of white baleen just below the upper rim and a lid finished with a carved walrus-ivory finial in the form of a walrus head with tusks, its eyes, nostrils and whiskers incised and inked black. A closely comparable basket in the Finnish Heritage Agency collection is flattish and convex-sided, with a round walrus-ivory disc at the centre of the lid carrying a standing four-legged animal, probably a polar bear, and a second bone disc at the centre of the base; the coiling was begun from those discs outward. It was black when new and has lightened considerably with age.

**Gut sewing (*qiviut*-era sea-mammal membrane work)** — A sail in the British Museum is made of sea-mammal intestine, either walrus or bearded seal, the panels sewn together with sinew. The same membrane technology yields waterproof and windproof surfaces from an animal already taken for food.

**Qiviut** — Muskox shed their underlayer of fur, which is gathered and spun into wool for scarves, hats and gloves.

**Fur and hide sewing** — Sealskin, moose and caribou hide and polar-bear hide are turned into garments, with fur from rabbit, beaver, marten, otter and squirrel used to adorn clothing for warmth.

**Motif vocabulary.** Caribou and caribou calves; seal heads; walrus; polar bear; *umiaq* crews paddling; hunters; dwellings; incised circle-and-dot; a knotted rope attached to a whale.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe dress in terms of material rather than cut. Hides and furs are made into parkas, mukluks, hats, gloves and slippers, with the choice of skin following the season and the animal: sealskin, moose and caribou hide, and polar-bear hide for the garment body, and rabbit, beaver, marten, otter and squirrel fur added as trim for warmth. The stated reason is functional — many of these materials are naturally waterproof or windproof, and the assemblage is what has kept Iñupiat warm in the conditions of their homelands. Muskox *qiviut*, gathered as the animals shed, is spun and worked into scarves, hats and gloves. The one item of dress the museum records show directly is the labret, a lip ornament worn in a pierced lip (see Jewelry). The sources used do not distinguish men's from women's dress, describe ceremonial garments as separate from daily wear, or name particular garment types in Iñupiaq.

### Architecture

The museum drawings show *men constructing winter quarters* in the top row of two separate pencil drawings, and one drawing includes a small igloo-like construction with two people inside and a sled outside. Beyond this the sources give little: historically some Iñupiat lived in sedentary communities and others were nomadic, and some villages in the area have been occupied by Indigenous groups for more than 10,000 years. Engraved drill bows depict dwellings alongside people and caribou. Named modern institutions rather than house forms are what the sources supply: the Iñupiaq language immersion school *Nikaitchuat Iḷisaġviat* in Kotzebue, founded 1998, and *Iḷisaġvik College* in Utqiaġvik, the one Iñupiaq culture-oriented institution of higher education. The sources used do not describe house construction, roofing, materials or architectural ornament in any detail.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The catalogued objects are of bone, ivory, baleen, stone, wood and sinew — no ceramics or metalwork appear. Hunting gear includes an **atlatl** (spear-thrower) of wood and bone with glass beads: dagger-shaped in outline, the handle carved to fit the right hand, with a flat rectangular bone finger peg and a central index-finger socket, the spear-shaft groove terminated by an ivory spur, that side coloured red (now faded) and the opposite side rising to a surface set with two blue and white beads. A **club** has a head of walrus skull bone carved as a polar-bear head, lashed to a pine shaft with baleen strips. A lance- or spear-head is flaked from flint. A whale-shaped **box** of pine with a sinew-corded lid and an inset blue glass bead underneath held stone harpoon points for whale hunting. Other forms: a woman's paddle of pine or cedar; a net gauge carved knife-shaped from walrus ivory; a root-digging pick of antler or bone bound to a wooden handle with spruce root, the wood stained red with alder bark; a carved ivory bag or bucket handle with miniature caribou calves and seal heads and circle-and-dot inlay in black; and a rectangular feeding bowl or spoon of mammoth ivory, also used as a blubber scraper, with a human head and a seal's head carved side by side at one end. **Bow drills** of walrus ivory are the principal engraved form — one with turquoise beads and incised animals, hunters and dwellings, another with holes drilled at each end for a thong and engravings of people standing, paddling or dancing in *umiaqs* on the front and a group of caribou and umiaqs along the sides. Walrus intestines are made into dance drums and the skin boats *qayaq* and *umiaq*.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The labret — a plug worn in a pierced lip — is the best-documented ornament in the collection, with four examples from the Finnish Heritage Agency, all exchanged from the Peabody Museum at Harvard. One is carved from walrus ivory: a round plug widening into a flat rim at the other end, with a round hollow in its side that may have held a glass bead, and a hole in the end that possibly carried a further ornament. The other three are stone. One is of pale grey slate, described as resembling a top hat in profile. Two are of syenite — one grey with small black spots, the other black with greyish-white flecks and some mica, its wider end flaring into a flat triangular rather than round rim. Sizes run roughly 1.75–2.2 cm by 2.05–2.8 cm. Walrus ivory and bowhead baleen are used more broadly as materials for Native art and tools, with the harvest of walrus solely for ivory prohibited by federal law and socially condemned. The sources used do not cover tattooing, hair practice or beadwork headdresses.

## Music & performance

Dance drums are made from walrus intestine. The **Nalukataq**, a spring whaling festival, is the performance context the sources name: dance groups from across the North perform songs and dances honouring the whale's spirit, which gave its physical body to feed entire villages. Engraved drill bows show figures standing, paddling or dancing in *umiaqs*. Katherine Paul, of Iñupiaq and Swinomish heritage, is a singer-songwriter. Beyond this the sources used do not name instruments, song genres or musical repertoires.

## Dance & theatre

Dance at Nalukataq is performed by visiting groups from across the North, the songs and dances serving to honour the whale (see Festivals). The sources used name no individual dances, mask traditions or dramatic forms.

## Festivals & rituals

**Nalukataq** is the spring whaling festival, celebrating traditional whale hunting and honouring the whale's spirit as it gave its physical body to feed entire villages; dance groups from across the North perform songs and dances at it. The distribution of a whale is itself a ritualised act rather than a simple division of food: the animal is butchered and its meat and blubber allocated according to a traditional formula, and even city-dwelling relatives thousands of miles away are entitled to a share of each whale killed by the hunters of their ancestral village.

The ethical framework surrounding these practices is set out in the *Iñupiat Ilitqusiat*, a list of values created by elders in Kotzebue and articulated similarly by other Iñupiat communities: respect for elders, hard work, hunter's success, family roles, humor, respect for nature, knowledge of family tree, respect for others, sharing, love for children, cooperation, avoid conflict, responsibility to tribe, humility, and spirituality. These serve as guideposts for how Iñupiat are to live, and both inform and derive from subsistence practice. A related value governs the hunt itself — the utilisation of the whole catch, seen in the conversion of hides to clothing, intestine to drums and boat covers, and ivory and baleen to tools and art.

The sources used do not describe birth, coming-of-age, marriage or funeral rites, and there are no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions for the United States.

## Foodways

The Iñupiat are hunter-gatherers who continue to rely heavily on subsistence hunting and fishing, harvesting walrus, seal, whale, polar bear, caribou and fish according to location. Both the inland *Nunamiut* and the coastal *Tikiġaġmiut* depend greatly on fish. Inland Iñupiat hunt caribou, Dall sheep, grizzly bear and moose; coastal Iñupiat hunt walrus, seal, beluga whale and bowhead whale, with polar bear hunted cautiously. Seasonal staples include ducks, geese, rabbits, berries, roots and shoots.

*Maktak* — the skin and blubber of bowhead and other whales — is rich in vitamins A and C. Because cooking destroys the vitamin C content of meat, eating these foods raw contributes to health in a population with limited access to fruit and vegetables. *Akutaq* is a traditional dessert of berries mixed with tallow; berries are also combined with rosehips and highbush cranberries and boiled into a syrup.

The dietary picture has changed. Chronic diseases traditionally absent from circumpolar populations — diabetes and heart disease — now afflict the Iñupiat, and obesity rates are on par with American rates, with some 64% of adults having an at-risk BMI.

## Oral tradition & literature

The drill-bow engravings and the four pencil drawings in the British Museum are narrative in structure, each laid out in registers: men building winter quarters, sleds each pulled by a single animal dragging a seal, men hunting walrus from an *umiaq*, a kayaker with three seals, a whale with a long knotted rope attached, and whale hunting from an umiaq. Several Iñupiat developed pictographic writing systems in the early twentieth century, known as **Alaskan Picture Writing**; the Wikipedia language article records that this system "died with its creators". Simon Paneak was a Nunamiut oral historian and is the namesake of an ethnographic museum. Contemporary writers include the poets Joan Kane and dg nanouk okpik, the educator, poet and writer Ticasuk Brown (1904–1982), and Howard Rock (1911–1976), founder of the *Tundra Times*. The sources used name no epic or folktale cycle.

## Language & religion

*Iñupiaq* (also *Iñupiatun*) is an Inuit language of the Inuit–Yupik–Unangan family, closely related to and to varying degrees mutually intelligible with the Inuit languages of Canada and Greenland. Its dialects are the most conservative forms of the Inuit language. Four major dialects are recognised — North Slope, Malimiut, Bering Straits and Qawiaraq — grouped into two larger collections, North Slope Iñupiaq and Seward Peninsula Iñupiaq. It is polysynthetic, ergative–absolutive, with base-20 numerals subdivided by five (*tallimat* 5, from the word for hand/arm; *qulit* 10, from "top"; *akimiaq* 15, "it goes across"; *iñuiññaq* 20, "entire person"). Roughly 2,000 of some 24,500 Iñupiat speak it, most elderly, after decades of English-only boarding-school policy under Rev. Sheldon Jackson, appointed General Agent of Education in 1885. Explorers first wrote the language ad hoc; in 1946 Roy Ahmaogak, an Iñupiaq Presbyterian minister from Utqiaġvik, worked with Eugene Nida of the Summer Institute of Linguistics to develop the current Latin-script alphabet. Iñupiaq became an official language of Alaska in 2014. Spirituality appears in the sources as one of the *Iñupiat Ilitqusiat* values and in the honouring of the whale's spirit at Nalukataq; no denominational landscape is described.
