## Overview

The Ho-Chunk — *Hoocąk*, *Hoocągra*, also known as Winnebago — are a Siouan-speaking people whose historic territory covers parts of Wisconsin, Minnesota, Iowa and Illinois. At European contact their country reached from Green Bay on Lake Michigan beyond Lake Winnebago to the Wisconsin River and the Rock River in Illinois, with Red Banks on Green Bay remembered as the homeland. They are enrolled today in two federally recognized tribes: the Ho-Chunk Nation of Wisconsin, headquartered at Black River Falls and holding scattered parcels in more than a dozen Wisconsin counties rather than one contiguous reservation, and the Winnebago Tribe of Nebraska, with a reservation in Thurston and Dixon counties, Nebraska, and Woodbury County, Iowa. Current estimates put the two tribes together at about 12,000 people. Their name, from *Ho* "voice" and *cąk* "sacred", means "People of the Sacred Voice" — an oral-historical claim to have originated the branches of the Siouan language family.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here — the Wikipedia articles and the catalogue records of the objects shown — describe finished objects rather than named weaving or beading traditions, so only what the catalogue states can be listed.

**Bandolier bag** — A Metropolitan Museum example of the 1880s is built from wool and cotton trade cloth with wool yarn, glass beads and metal, the combination of European trade textiles with beadwork that characterizes Ho-Chunk work of the removal and post-removal decades.

**Tanned hide work** — Wikipedia records that Ho-Chunk women tanned hides for clothing and storage bags, and that most parts of hunted game were turned into tools, binding, clothing and dwelling coverings.

**Woven baskets** — A British Museum postcard shows Ho-Chunk women posing with woven baskets at a tent encampment at the Eaton County Fair in Charlotte, Michigan; the record does not name the materials or technique.

The sources name no motif vocabulary, so none is given.

### Clothing & dress

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century photographs in the British Museum are the most detailed dress evidence available here. Everyday wear in them is blankets wrapped around the body and over the head, cloth headscarves, shirts, leggings and boots or moccasins; one stereoscope shows three Winnebago people so dressed peering into a wigwam. A woman photographed in a studio in Iowa wears shell ear ornaments, a shell necklace, a patterned shirt and a skirt; another, photographed at Omaha, wears ear ornaments, a shirt and a blanket wrapped around her legs, with a child beside her in a tunic. Men's formal and leaderly dress is distinct: Winnesheick, head chief of the Winnebagos, was photographed at Fort Snelling in a fur or feather bonnet, a bear-claw necklace and skin trousers, holding a bow and arrows in one hand and a pipe in the other. Chief White Wing, photographed at Wisconsin Dells, wears a feather headdress, a bone choker with a gorget, a bone breast plate, beaded loin covering and beaded leggings. Another studio pair shows one man in a feathered bonnet with several beaded necklaces, a waist garment and moccasins, the other in skin leggings, blanket, moccasins and beaded necklaces.

### Architecture

The Ho-Chunk lived in permanent villages of *wigwams* — the British Museum describes the form as a domed single-room dwelling — and coverings for dwellings were among the uses women made of hunted game. Tipis also appear in the photographic record: Wak-ka-mas, a Winnebago chief, was photographed speaking to a seated group in front of a tipi, and another card shows a tent village set up in the woods for the Eaton County Fair. Far older than either is the built landscape the Ho-Chunk claim as ancestral work: in the Late Woodland period (650–1200 CE) thousands of effigy mounds were raised across Wisconsin and the surrounding states, a practice that ceased abruptly in the Terminal Late Woodland with the appearance of the Oneota culture. The Ho-Chunk claim descent from both the effigy-mound builders and their Oneota successors. Dugout canoes recovered from small lakes and rivers near Madison, Wisconsin, have prompted new archaeological work on ancient settlement.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Silver and copper working was a men's craft: some men learned to make jewelry and other body decorations from those metals, for men and women alike. Among the objects shown, a Metropolitan Museum **medicine bundle bowl and bag** of 1850–60 is made of wood, wool and native-tanned skin — the bundle itself being the material form of blessings received from spirits, holding objects such as feathers, bones, skins, flutes and paints. A nineteenth-century **whistle** at the same museum combines wood, sinew, ribbon, metal thimbles and buckskin, the thimbles showing how trade goods were absorbed into ceremonial equipment. Household and subsistence gear otherwise named in the sources is the canoe used for fishing, the spear and club used on fish, and the carved bow held by a man in a British Museum studio portrait.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The photographic record names bear-claw necklaces, shell ear ornaments and shell necklaces, several strands of beaded necklaces worn by men, bone chokers with gorgets and bone breast plates. Metal ornament was locally made: silver and copper body decorations were produced by Ho-Chunk men for both sexes. Headgear in the same portraits ranges from fur or feather bonnets and full feather headdresses to a hat with a single feather. Infants were carried in wooden baby-carriers wrapped in cloth, and one such carrier photographed in Iowa has charm ornaments hung around the baby's neck.

## Music & performance

The only instrument in the sources is the Metropolitan Museum's nineteenth-century whistle of wood, sinew, ribbon, metal thimbles and buckskin, and flutes are named among the objects that could be gathered into a war-bundle. The sources name no song genres, singers or musical occasions.

## Festivals & rituals

Ho-Chunk ritual life in these sources turns on the acquisition of spiritual protection and on the ceremonies attached to the war-bundle. Boys passed through a rite of passage at puberty: they fasted for a period during which they were expected to acquire a guardian spirit, and without one their lives would be miserable. Beyond that guardian, men sought protection and power from particular spirits by making offerings along with tobacco. A man would not go on the warpath before performing the **war-bundle feast**, a ceremony in two parts, the first honoring the night-spirits and the second the Thunderbird spirit; the blessings those spirits gave were embodied in the objects gathered into the bundle — feathers, bones, skins, flutes, paints. The seasonal round also had a fixed rhythm: families moved from area to area with the changing seasons to find food, and many returned to Black River Falls, Wisconsin, to pick berries in summer. Clan membership structured social and ceremonial obligation. There were twelve clans, each associated with an animal spirit and with a defined role in the survival of the people: chiefs were appointed from the Thunderbird Clan, and the Bear Clan enforced discipline and oversaw prisoners. Marriage was generally required outside one's own clan. The tribe is patrilineal today, but anthropologists hold that a matrilineal system may have preceded it in the seventeenth century, under which clan matriarchs named the chief and could reclaim the office if they disapproved of his conduct.

## Foodways

The Ho-Chunk cultivated corn, squash, beans and wild rice (*Zizania* spp.) in permanent villages, taking up maize at the end of the Late Woodland period while continuing to hunt, fish and gather. Women grew and processed the food, deliberately cultivating several varieties of corn and squash so that different types would come in across the growing season, and gathering nuts, berries, roots and edible leaves from forest and river's edge. They also tapped sugar maples: sap was boiled down into syrup and candy, and sugar from the maple was harvested alongside the wild rice. Preserved food for travel took the form of dried meats combined with berries, which women prepared to sustain families on the move. Men hunted muskrat, mink, otter, beaver and deer, and speared fish and clubbed them to death; women processed and cooked the game. Women's knowledge extended to a wide range of roots and leaves used medicinally and herbally. Tobacco appears in the sources not as food but as the substance offered to spirits when a man sought their blessing. The sources name no individual dishes and no tea or coffee tradition.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ho-Chunk oral tradition is unusually well recorded: the anthropologist Paul Radin took down Ho-Chunk ethnology between 1908 and 1912, and his Winnebago notebooks are held at the American Philosophical Library. The **migration myth** tells how a Menominee (*Kaǧi*) chief, acting on a vision, had supplies assembled on a white sand beach on Lake Michigan; a great flock of ravens — *kaǧi*, spirit birds with iridescent rainbow plumage — came out of the eastern sky, and each bird on landing became a kneeling man. These were the Hocąk nation, and so they came to Red Banks, on the bay they called *Te-rok*, "Within Lake"; Lake Michigan itself was *Te-šišik*, "Bad Lake". **Trickster tales**, passed down orally, taught lessons through animals, nature and trickery — in "The Trickster and Talking Bulb", the Old Man disregards a bulb's warnings, is punished for his defiance, and learns to respect nature rather than go against it. The **Red Horn Cycle** follows Red Horn, "He Who Wears (Human) Faces on His Ears", and his companions Turtle and the thunderbird Storms-as-He-Walks (*Mą'e-manįga*) against the *Wąge-rucge*, the "Man-Eaters", a race of giants killing the human beings Red Horn had pledged to help; he eventually married a red-haired giant woman. Archaeologists have proposed Red Horn as a mythic figure in Mississippian art, represented on Southeastern Ceremonial Complex artifacts, and Robert Hall has drawn analogies between the cycle of Red Horn and his sons and the Mesoamerican Hero Twins.

## Language & religion

Ho-Chunk (*Hoocąk*, *Hocąk*) belongs to the Siouan family and is closely related to the Chiwere Siouan dialects of the Iowa, Missouria and Otoe; the people hold that the language was given to them by their creator, *Mą'ųna*, Earthmaker. It is an agglutinating, pro-drop, Subject–Object–Verb language, distinguishing vowels by nasality and length (*pąą* 'bag' against *paa* 'nose'), and it carries a sound law of its own, **Dorsey's law**, by which the vowel after a resonant is copied back into a preceding voiceless-obstruent cluster (*/pra/* → *para* in *paras* 'wide, flat'). For a short period in the mid-to-late 1800s the language was written in an adapted "Ba-Be-Bi-Bo" syllabics; since 1994 the official alphabet of the Ho-Chunk Nation has been an Americanist Latin script with the ogonek for nasal vowels, whose diacritics have Ho-Chunk names — *sįįc* 'tail' for the ogonek, *wookąnąk* 'hat' for the haček. The Wisconsin and Nebraska nations spell long vowels differently, Wisconsin doubling the vowel and Nebraska using a macron. The language is highly endangered, with about 200 native speakers among Wisconsin elders, and is the object of sustained revitalization: the Hoocąk Waaziija Haci Language and Culture Division runs classes, an immersion daycare and a language apprentice program, the Ho-Chunk Renaissance program teaches in Nebraska schools, and an online dictionary and an iOS app extend the work. Religious life in these sources is the clan system, the guardian-spirit quest, the war-bundle ceremonies, and the spirit figures of the mythology — the Thunderbird, the night-spirits, and *Herešgúnina*, the solar deity born with his legs backwards, who created crawfish, grizzly bears and gambling and whose severed head formed the solar disk.
