## Overview

The Alabama, also known as the Alibamu, are an Indigenous people of the Southeastern Woodlands whose historical homelands were on the upper Alabama River. They formed part of the Muscogee Creek Confederacy and now live in Oklahoma and Texas. The Alabama and the closely allied Coushatta migrated from Alabama and Mississippi to present-day Texas in the late 18th century and early 19th century under pressure from American settlers. In Texas, the two peoples became the Alabama-Coushatta Tribe of Texas, whose 1,137 citizens have about 4,500 acres (18 km2) of reservation. The Alabama language belongs to the Muskogean family, and an estimated 100 speakers, primarily from Texas, still speak it. The Alabama–Quassarte Tribal Town is federally recognized and headquartered in Wetumka, Oklahoma.

## Festivals & rituals

The historical sources record that Alabama and Coushatta towns were organized as “red” and “white” towns. The “white” towns kept the peace and provided refuge, while the “red” towns conducted military campaigns. Although the towns had these responsibilities, the Alabama–Coushatta thought of themselves as peace-loving people.

The sources also record Alabama and Coushatta origin narratives. One oral history states that the two tribes sprouted from opposite sides of a cypress tree. Another account, shared by Se-ko-pe-chi in 1857, states that the tribes “sprang out of the ground between the Cohawba and Alabama Rivers.” These accounts concern the interconnectedness of the two tribes.

## Foodways

The Alabama people’s historical movement is described in relation to land, migration, roaming, and hunting. In the early 19th century, the Alabama and Coushatta developed a strong friendship as they roamed and hunted their new land together.

The only specifically documented plant practice concerns the obtusifolium subspecies of *Pseudognaphalium obtusifolium*. It was used in a compound decoction for nervousness and sleepiness and in a decoction as a face wash for nerves and insomnia. The sources describe these uses as ethnobotanical and medicinal rather than as foodways.

## Oral tradition & literature

Alabama oral tradition is represented in the sources by narratives about the relationship between the Alabama and Coushatta tribes. One narrative says that the two tribes sprouted from either side of a cypress tree. In 1857, Se-ko-pe-chi, described as one of the oldest Muscogee in Indian Territory, gave another account in which the tribes “sprang out of the ground between the Cohawba and Alabama Rivers.” The emblem of the Alabama-Coushatta Tribe of Texas also expresses this connection through two intertwined woodpeckers derived from precontact Mississippian culture. They do document a contemporary language revitalization and documentation project begun in January 2024 by the Alabama–Coushatta Tribe of Texas in partnership with the WOLF Lab at Harvard University. Its stated five-year goal includes documenting the language, studying its grammar and lexicon, and producing educational resources for the Alabama–Coushatta community.

## Language & religion

Alabama, also known as Alibamu, has the endonym *Albaamo innaaɬiilka*. It is a Muskogean language spoken by the Alabama–Coushatta Tribe of Texas. It was once spoken by the Alabama–Quassarte Tribal Town of Oklahoma, but there are no more Alabama speakers in Oklahoma. Alabama is closely related to Koasati and Apalachee and more distantly related to Hitchiti, Chickasaw, and Choctaw. It is believed to have been closely related to the Muklasa and Tuskegee languages, which are now extinct.

The language has fourteen consonant phonemes and three vowel qualities, /i o a/. Vowel length is distinctive, and vowels can be nasalized in certain morphological contexts. The final syllable generally carries primary stress, with a pitch-accent system containing high-level and high-falling tones.
