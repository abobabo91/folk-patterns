## Overview

The Makah are an Indigenous people of the Pacific Northwest Coast. They live around Neah Bay and on their reservation, which includes Tatoosh Island. Their culture is closely tied to the ocean, cedar, whaling, fishing, and oral history. The Makah Cultural and Research Center helps preserve and present their history and culture.

## Material culture

Makah material culture centers on western red cedar, canoes, baskets, longhouses, and hunting and fishing tools.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Cedar bark and roots were used for clothing, hats, and baskets, including baskets with a Birdcage pattern.

- **Twined basket** — A Makah basket from Washington was made by twining and had a Birdcage pattern.
- **Bird cage pattern** — The Bird cage pattern is associated with Makah Indians of Washington.
- **Coiled and woven basket** (*coiled basketry and woven basketry*) — One small square basket had coiled walls and a woven bottom.
- **Basket with lid** — Museum records list lidded baskets associated with Makah and Ozette Indians.
- **Small basket** — A small basket was associated with Makah and Ozette Indians near Cape Flattery.

### Clothing & dress

Western red cedar bark was used to make water-resistant clothing and hats.

- **Cedar-bark clothing** (*western red cedar bark*) — Makah traditionally used western red cedar bark to make water-resistant clothing.
- **Cedar-bark hats** (*western red cedar bark*) — Makah traditionally used western red cedar bark to make water-resistant hats.

### Architecture

Makah villages traditionally had large cedar longhouses with movable plank walls.

- **Cedar longhouse** (*longhouse*) — Makah villages traditionally consisted of large longhouses made from western red cedar.
- **Movable cedar-plank wall** (*cedar-plank walls*) — Longhouse walls could be tilted or removed to provide ventilation or light.
- **Ozette buried longhouses** (*buried longhouses*) — Archaeologists found several buried longhouses at Ozette village.
- **Longhouse replica** (*cedar longhouses*) — The Makah Cultural and Research Center displays replicas of cedar longhouses.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Makah made cedar canoes and used specialized wooden, shell, and horn hunting tools.

- **Cedar canoe** — Whole western red cedar trees were carved out to make canoes for hunting seals and whales.
- **Whaling harpoon** — A traditional harpoon had a yew-wood shaft, a mussel-shell tip, and elk-horn barbs.
- **Yankee-style harpoon head** (*steel “yankee style” head*) — Later hunters used a steel Yankee-style head with the traditional yew-wood shaft.
- **Lance** — Lances were among the hunting tools documented for the Makah.
- **Fishing and sealing equipment** — Makah used equipment for fishing and sealing.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The profile does not document Makah jewelry or named body adornment.

## Music & performance

Songs and ceremonies welcome the spirit of a hunted whale, and Makah Days includes public singing and performance.

- **Whale-welcoming songs** (*songs*) — After a whale reaches shore, traditional songs welcome the whale’s spirit.
- **Whale ceremony** (*traditional ceremonies*) — Traditional ceremonies are performed after a whale is brought to shore.
- **Makah Days** — Makah Days is an annual public gathering with singing, dancing, games, feasting, races, a parade, a street fair, and fireworks.
- **Dead Man film scene** (*Makah language*) — The final scene of Jim Jarmusch’s 1995 film Dead Man takes place in a reconstructed Makah village with Makah actors and dialogue in Makah.

## Dance & theatre

The profile names dancing at Makah Days and a film scene set in a reconstructed Makah village.

- **Makah Days dancing** (*dancing*) — Dancing is part of the annual Makah Days gathering.
- **Dead Man village scene** (*reconstructed Makah village*) — The final scene of Dead Man takes place in a reconstructed Makah village.

## Festivals & rituals

Makah Days is the main public gathering, while whaling includes ceremonies, songs, special ownership, and a potlatch.

- **Makah Days** — Held in late August, Makah Days includes a parade, street fair, canoe races, games, singing, dancing, feasting, and fireworks.
- **Whale-welcoming rite** — Ceremonies and songs welcome the whale’s spirit after it reaches shore.
- **Saddle-piece ceremony** (*“saddle piece”*) — The harpooner owns the saddle piece and takes it home for a special ceremony.
- **Whale potlatch** (*potlatch*) — Much of the whale meat and oil is consumed during a potlatch.
- **Childbirth plant practice** (*Viola adunca*) — Makah women chew the roots and leaves of Viola adunca while giving birth.

## Foodways

Makah foodways center on ocean foods, forest hunting, gathering, and sharing whale meat and oil.

- **Whale meat and oil** — Whale meat and oil were distributed among community members after a hunt.
- **Seal** — Seals were hunted and eaten.
- **Salmon and halibut** — Salmon and halibut were part of Makah fishing and diet.
- **Shellfish** — Makah gathered a wide variety of shellfish.
- **Forest foods** (*nuts, berries, edible plants, and roots*) — Women gathered nuts, berries, edible plants, and roots, while people hunted deer, elk, and bear.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral history preserves Makah accounts of Ozette, whaling, and the traditional way of life.

- **Great slide** (*“great slide”*) — Makah oral history describes a great slide that engulfed part of Ozette village long ago.
- **Ozette oral history** (*oral history*) — Makah oral history relates that a mudslide preserved Ozette houses and their contents until excavation in the 1970s.
- **Whaling history** (*aboriginal whaling*) — Makah oral history says aboriginal whaling was suspended and re-established several times.
- **The Sea Is My Country** (*The Sea Is My Country: The Maritime World of the Makahs*) — James Reid’s book concerns the maritime world of the Makahs.
- **Voices of a Thousand People** — This book concerns Makah history and life.

## Language & religion

Makah is a Southern Nootkan Wakashan language being revived as a second language, while no wider religious system is identified.

- **Makah language** (*Makah*) — Makah is the only Wakashan language in the United States.
- **Makah endonym** (*qʷi·qʷi·diččaq*) — The Makah endonym is qʷi·qʷi·diččaq.
- **Southern Nootkan branch** (*Southern Nootkan*) — Makah belongs to the Southern Nootkan branch of the Wakashan family.
- **Language revival** (*preschool classes*) — The Makah Tribe is reviving the language and has established preschool classes for children.
- **Whale-spirit belief** (*whale’s spirit*) — Traditional ceremonies and songs welcome the spirit of a hunted whale.
