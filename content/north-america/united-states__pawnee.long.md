## Overview

The Pawnee call themselves *Chatiks si chatiks*, "Men of Men," and speak a language of the Caddoan family. Historically they lived in earth-lodge villages along the Loup (*ickariʾ*), Republican and South Platte rivers in what is now Nebraska and northern Kansas; they reached Nebraska around 1750 and, after forced removal in the 1870s, settled in Indian Territory, today's Oklahoma. The federally recognized Pawnee Nation of Oklahoma is headquartered in Pawnee, Oklahoma, with a jurisdictional area covering parts of Noble, Payne and Pawnee counties; roughly 3,200 people were enrolled in 2011, down from more than 12,000 in the early eighteenth century. Their culture matters here for two linked reasons: a year divided between river-bottom horticulture and long-range bison hunting, and a cosmology in which the earth lodge itself was built as a scale model of the sky.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here — Wikipedia and the catalogue records of the objects shown — do not describe named Pawnee textile or weaving traditions. What the museum records document instead is decorative work applied to hide, leather and imported cloth:

**Quilled and flannel-trimmed leather gear** — a brown leather rein in the British Museum has one end slit into two straps, each ornamented with red flannel, quills and a brass bell; the other end carries a leather disc perforated at the centre with a star. Quillwork and traded red flannel appear together on the same working object.

**Painted hide shield** — a leather shield painted green on the front, bearing a large red disc on the upper part with further devices below.

**Fringed and tasselled shirts** — studio portraits record a decorated fringed shirt and a "warrior shirt with tassels," worn with embroidered leggings, also tasselled.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name only a few devices: the *red disc* on the shield's upper face; the perforated *star* at the centre of the rein's leather disc; a *square enclosing a circle*, carved on one end of a baby-carrier board. On corn — the material most fully described — colour itself is the classificatory system: black, spotted, white, yellow and red, the four unspotted colours corresponding to the four semi-cardinal directions.

### Clothing & dress

Dress is documented almost entirely through nineteenth-century studio photographs of men, so women's everyday clothing is barely covered: one portrait shows a Pawnee woman wearing a blanket and shell ear ornaments, with her hair in braids. Men in these portraits wear decorated fringed or tasselled shirts, sometimes a manufactured cloth shirt, with embroidered leggings and moccasins; a blanket or buffalo robe is wrapped around the waist, and one man wears a fur robe. Headgear varies: a fur turban with two shells and two eagle feathers attached, a fur hat with the same combination, a cloth turban, a cloth headscarf, or simply a single eagle feather worn in the hair. Around the neck are shell necklaces, a shell choker, beaded necklaces, and a bear-claw-and-fur necklace. Shell ear ornaments are near-universal in the portraits; finger rings, chains and pendants appear on some sitters. Chiefs are photographed with peace medals, holding tomahawks, tomahawk pipes, a rifle or a pipe, and in one case wearing a US military coat — the dress of men negotiating with the government, not of daily village life. Face paint appears in one record, queried by the cataloguer.

### Architecture

The Pawnee built *earth lodges* on the higher banks close to rivers. Rectangular at earlier stages, by historical times they tended toward an oval plan. The frame began with ten to fifteen posts set about ten feet apart outlining a central room, with four, eight or twelve centre poles depending on size; four of these were painted, standing for the four cardinal directions and the four major star gods. A second outer ring of posts marked the lodge's circumference, with horizontal beams linking them. Smaller poles tied with willow withes covered the frame, then thatch, then earth; a central hole served at once as chimney, smoke vent and skylight. The floor was recessed about three feet below ground, insulating the interior. The door always faced east and the rising sun, opening onto a long low passageway and an entry room closed by a hinged buffalo-skin door that could be wedged shut at night. Opposite it, on the west side, a buffalo skull with horns was displayed as great medicine. Mats screened small sleeping rooms in the outer ring. Roofs were strong enough for adults to sit on and children to play on. Thirty to fifty related people might share one lodge; a village held 300–500 people in ten to fifteen households. Earlier Caddoan villages also used grass lodges, unfortified until European encroachment pushed communities onto high ground behind ditch-and-wall defences.

The lodge doubled as an observatory. Its axis was oriented east–west so that the vernal-equinox sunrise fell on the altar, and the dimensions of the smoke hole and door were set to permit sky observation — the smoke hole aligned to allow sighting of the Pleiades. It was understood simultaneously as the universe and as the womb of a woman, household activity standing for her reproductive powers.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Pawnee objects in this atlas are hide, wood, metal and sinew rather than pottery. An arrow combines wood, metal, feather and sinew. The baby-carrier is a large flat board of brownish-yellow wood, carved at the larger end with a square enclosing a circle. Brass bells hang from the ornamented rein. Bows and arrows were the hunting weapons used from horseback, the rider guiding his pony with his knees; a single arrow into the flank between the lower ribs and the hip could incapacitate a bison. Women processed the carcass: flesh sliced into strips and dried on poles over slow fires kept for several months, while skins from bison, elk, bear, panther and skunk became clothing and accessories, storage bags, foot coverings, fastening ropes and ties. Tomahawks, tomahawk pipes and pipes appear in the portrait photographs. Sacred bundles held an ear of holy corn among other materials.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Shell dominates: shell necklaces, shell chokers and shell ear ornaments recur across the studio portraits, with shells also fixed to fur turbans and hats alongside eagle feathers. Bear claws were worn as a neck ornament, in one case combined with fur. Beaded and shell necklaces, chains, pendants, earrings and finger rings appear on individual sitters, as do peace medals — government gifts worn as insignia by chiefs. One photograph records a man's face as painted. Tattooing is named in Pawnee mythology as one of the arts Tirawa taught the people, but the sources give no description of its designs. Women's hair was worn in braids.

## Music & performance

The sources used here name no Pawnee instruments or song genres. They record only that the Spring Awakening ceremony proceeded through a priest's songs, and that in the Morning Star ceremony a five-day sequence of songs described the advancing stages of the rite.

## Dance & theatre

The only dance tradition the sources name is the **Ghost Dance** movement, in which the Pawnee participated in the 1890s, already in Oklahoma.

## Festivals & rituals

The ceremonial year tracked the sky and the crops. The most important was the **Spring Awakening ceremony**, also called the Thunder ceremony, meant to wake the earth and ready it for planting; the priest opened it when he first tracked two small twinkling stars known as the *Swimming Ducks* on the north-eastern horizon near the Milky Way, and then heard rolling thunder from the west. Sowing in spring and harvesting in autumn each began with ceremony, and regular rites preceded the semi-annual buffalo hunts — Kawaha, a god of good luck, was closely tied to these. The Chawi band held a **White Beaver ceremony**, serving much the purpose the Skidi's Spring Awakening did, but revitalizing hibernating animals rather than renewing the corn crop. Seeds were taken from the sacred bundles for the spring planting ritual, and Holy Corn was grown expressly to replenish the bundles for the winter and summer seasons.

The **Morning Star ceremony** was performed only by one Skidi village, Village Across a Hill, and stood outside the ceremonial calendar. Usually symbolic, it involved an actual sacrifice only when a man dreamed that the Morning Star had commanded it — typically in autumn. He consulted the Morning Star priest, and both wept, knowing the mission was wrong to carry out; volunteers then raided an enemy village for a girl, who was kept and well treated through the winter and the buffalo hunt. In spring, over five days, she was ritually transformed into the Evening Star, then killed at the star's rising on a scaffold of sacred woods and skins representing the Evening Star's garden in the west. Her body was laid face down on the prairie so her blood would fertilize the earth. Knife Chief and Petalesharo (Man Chief) worked to end the rite; Petalesharo cut a Comanche captive loose from the scaffold in 1817. The last known sacrifice was of Haxti, a fourteen-year-old Oglala Lakota girl, on 22 April 1838.

Life-cycle practice is documented mainly through descent and residence: the Pawnee are matrilineal, children belong to the mother's clan, and a young couple moved into the bride's parents' lodge.

## Foodways

Pawnee women cultivated ten varieties of corn, seven of pumpkins and squashes, and eight of beans, planted in the fertile river bottomlands — a combination making whole proteins. Beyond flint corn and flour corn for eating, they grew an archaic breed called **"Wonderful" or "Holy Corn"** solely for the sacred bundles, keeping the strains separate as they cultivated them. Corn set the agricultural calendar, first planted and first harvested, with priests and men taking part in the accompanying ceremonies; squash and beans carried no comparable theological weight. Crops were planted according to the position of the stars, and maize and other crops were sacrificed to them.

Bison was the preferred meat, hunted on summer marches from late June to about 1 September and winter hunts from late October to early April, sometimes 500 miles or more from the villages. Hunters favoured cows and young bulls, the taste of older bulls being disagreeable. Women sliced the flesh into strips and dried it on poles over slow fires, keeping it usable for months. Elk, bear, panther and skunk were also taken. **Eagle Corn soup**, unavailable for ceremonies for 125 years, returned in November 2010 after the last 25 remaining seeds of Pawnee Eagle Corn were sprouted in 2005; its taste is described as almonds with cream.

## Oral tradition & literature

Pawnee oral history and cosmology were recorded above all by the anthropologist **James Rolfe Murie** (Skiri Pawnee, 1862–1921), a fluent speaker. The creation narrative holds that the male Morning Star in the east was made first, a warrior; the female Evening Star in the west resisted the plan to create humankind, and Morning Star had to overcome a series of forces in the western sky with his fireball before mating with her — the first human being was a girl. The Sun (*Shakuru*) and Moon (*Pah*) produced a boy, likewise placed on Earth. *Atíʼas Tirawa*, "Our Father Above" (also *Tirawahat*), taught the people tattooing, fire-building, hunting, agriculture, speech and clothing, ritual and sacrifice; his wife *Atira*, Mother Corn, was the earth goddess. Six major stars served under Tirawahut, including the Northeast or Big Black Meteoric Star, described by some Skidi as a buffalo bull carrying the heaven on his back. Skidi and South Band traditions diverge: the Skidi were "the great star specialists," while the South Bands relied on animals as intermediaries — the *nahurac*, spirit animals with five named lodges at Pahuk, Lalawakohtito, Ahkawitakol, Kitzawitzuk (Pahowa) and Pahur.

## Language & religion

Pawnee, or *pâri pakûru'*, is a Caddoan language with two dialects, South Band and Skiri, differing in phonetic inventory and lexicon. It is ergative-absolutive and polysynthetic, with eight consonant phonemes (possibly nine, counting the glottal stop) and four short and four long vowels; the alphabet has nine consonants and eight vowels. The Skiri dialect became extinct in 2001 with the death of Lula Nora Pratt, and by 2007 fewer than ten elderly native speakers remained. The Pawnee Nation is producing teaching materials for the local high school and adult classes, posted language videos online in 2019 and 2020, and runs a language program with Indiana University; extensive documentation is archived at the American Indian Studies Research Institute. Religious life centred on the star gods, the four cardinal directions and priest-tended sacred bundles; the Ghost Dance reached the people in the 1890s.
