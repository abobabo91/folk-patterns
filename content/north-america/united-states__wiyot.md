## Overview

The Wiyot are an Indigenous people of California living near Humboldt Bay and a small surrounding area. Their traditional homeland extended from Mad River (*potawot*), through Humboldt Bay—including the present cities of Eureka and Arcata—to the lower Eel River basin. Inland, the territory was heavily forested in ancient redwood; the shoreland was mostly sandy, with dunes and tidal marshes. The Wiyot are culturally similar to the Yurok and called themselves the *Ku'wil*, meaning “the People.” Their language, Wiyot, is an Algic language distantly related to the Algonquian languages. The sources give present estimates of approximately 450 and about 500 Wiyot people. Their cultural history includes the World Renewal Ceremony, the village of Tuluwat, distinctive log canoes, and ongoing language and land revitalization.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state that ceremonial masks were worn during the World Renewal ceremony, but provide no further description of their materials, forms, or decoration.

### Architecture

The Wiyot language article gives examples involving a “house” and “boards of the house,”.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources describe long, carved, log canoes as an important Wiyot object. They also state that the people ate mostly clams and acorns.

## Music & performance

The principal performance tradition described in the sources is the annual World Renewal Ceremony, held at the Wiyot village of Tuluwat on Tuluwat Island, formerly called Indian Island. At the start of each year, a ceremonial dance was held there for seven to ten days. Ceremonial masks were worn, and all people were welcomed. Traditionally, men left the island and returned the next day with the day’s supplies, while elders, women, and children remained on the island with a few men.

World Renewal ceremonies were being held at Tuluwat for several days before the massacre of February 26, 1860. Up to 250 Wiyot participated, and Captain Jim organized and led the ceremony to start a new year.

The ceremony is central to Wiyot spirituality and cultural identity. After the massacre, the World Renewal Ceremony was interrupted, but it later returned to the island and was being revived by current tribal members. In 2013, Wiyot tribal members returned to Tuluwat Island by canoe and announced plans to hold another ceremony; the sources describe this as the first such ceremony to take place on the island since the massacre.

## Dance & theatre

The World Renewal Ceremony included a ceremonial dance held at Tuluwat village on the northern part of Tuluwat Island. It lasted seven to ten days and included ceremonial masks.

## Festivals & rituals

The central annual ritual described in the sources is the World Renewal Ceremony, held at Tuluwat at the start of each year. The ceremony lasted seven to ten days and was led in the nineteenth century by Captain Jim, the leader of the Humboldt Bay Wiyots. It was a communal event in which all people were welcomed and no one was turned away. The ceremony was held at the village site of Tuluwat, while men traditionally left the island and returned with supplies on the following day.

The World Renewal Ceremony is described as a central act in Wiyot spirituality. Tuluwat Island was and is the center of the Wiyot world. Ceremonies were being held there before the massacre of February 26, 1860, when most of the men had left the island after the final ceremony and women and children remained. Memorials have been held annually at Tuluwat village since 1992. The ceremony later returned to the island and has been in the process of revival by current tribal members.

## Foodways

The sources state that the Wiyot ate mostly clams and acorns.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Wiyot language article includes a short example of Wiyot narration, translated as: “She began to throw aside the boards of the house, thinking in vain, ‘I’ll take that man back.’ She never took him back.” It also states that Wiyot traditional narratives exist.

Contemporary preservation efforts focus especially on language revival. The Wiyot tribal government is fostering revival through videos, online dictionaries, an annual Wiyot language calendar, language courses, and Wiyot texts published for distribution. The sources also describe the revival of the World Renewal Ceremony and the restoration of Tuluwat as continuing cultural efforts.

## Language & religion

Wiyot is an Algic language spoken by the Wiyot people of Humboldt Bay, California. It is distantly related to the Algonquian languages, and Wiyot and Yurok are the westernmost languages in that family grouping. The language was also called Wishosk or *Soulatluk*, glossed as “your jaw.” The last documented native speaker, Della Prince, died in 1962, after which Wiyot became an extinct language as a first language. The Wiyot tribal government is now supporting revitalization.

The sources identify three divisions based on dialect and geography: Patawat Wiyot, Batawat Wiyot, and Mad River Wiyot near Mad River; Wiki Wiyot, Humboldt Bay Wiyot, or Wikigadakwi’, glossed as “poor folks,” near Humboldt Bay; and Wiyat Wiyot or Eel River Wiyot near the Eel River delta.

Wiyot religion shares much with that of the neighboring Yurok, with certain differences. Healers and ceremonial leaders were mostly women, who received their powers on mountaintops during the night. Tuluwat Island and the World Renewal Ceremony are central to Wiyot spirituality.
