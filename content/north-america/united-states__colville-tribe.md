## Overview

The Colville people (*sx̌ʷýʔłpx* in nselxcin) are an Interior Salish people and an Indigenous people of the Northwest Plateau. In the 18th century, they lived around the Kettle and Columbia rivers in northeastern Washington, between Kettle Falls and the town of Hunters. Their territory extended from Almond Creek and the Granby River to Marias creek, Timber Ridge, Thirteenmile Mountain, Twin Lakes, Hunters, and the Colville River. Today, most Colville people are enrolled in the Confederated Tribes of the Colville Reservation, while some descendants belong to the Columbia Rivers Indians. The Colville language, also called N̓x̌ʷʔiłpcən, is one of six dialects of Colville-Okanagan, one of four Southern Interior Salishan languages of the Plateau.

## Music & performance

Jim Boyd is identified as a Colville musician.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe Christian missionary activity and conversion. Christian missionaries entered Colville territory in 1838, and in 1846 the Jesuit St. Paul's Mission was established; through its influence, nearly all the upper Columbia tribes were converted to Christianity.

## Foodways

Kettle Falls was an important salmon fishing resource in Colville history.

## Oral tradition & literature

Mourning Dove is identified as a Colville author.

## Language & religion

The Colville language, or N̓x̌ʷʔiłpcən, is one of six dialects of Colville-Okanagan. Colville-Okanagan is spoken by many Indigenous nations in the Interior Plateau, including the Syilx, Methow, Sanpoil (Nesilextcl'n), and Nespelem. Together with Wenatchee-Columbian, Spokane-Kalispel-Bitterroot, and Coeur d'Alene, it belongs to the four Southern Interior Salishan languages of the Plateau.
