## Overview

The Plains Apache are a small Southern Athabaskan tribe living on the Southern Plains of North America in close association with the linguistically unrelated Kiowa Tribe. They are headquartered in Anadarko, Oklahoma, and mostly live in Comanche and Caddo County, Oklahoma. In 2011, the tribe had 2,263 citizens, of whom 1,814 lived in-state. Their language belongs to the Southern Athabaskan branch of the Na-Dene languages and became extinct in 2008, when Alfred Chalepah, Jr., the last native speaker, died. The Plains Apache are significant in folk-culture studies through their oral history, extended-family organization, hunting and gathering life, tipi ownership and maintenance, women’s societies, music and dance documentation, tales, peyote ritual, and distinctive language.

## Music & performance

The sources provide bibliographic references to *Kiowa-Apache Music and Dance*, *The Peyote Ritual of the Plains Apache*, and *Six Plains Apache Tales*. They state that skilled artists joined women’s societies and that the Plains Apache communicated with their allies using the Plains Indian Sign Language.

## Dance & theatre

The sources include a bibliographic reference to *Kiowa-Apache Music and Dance*.

## Festivals & rituals

 They identify *The Peyote Ritual of the Plains Apache* in the bibliography and state that Pacer kept the main group of Plains Apaches on the reservation during the Red River War of 1874–75, but they provide no ritual account beyond those references.

## Foodways

The sources state that the Plains Apache hunted bison and that extended families camped together for hunting and gathering.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Plains Apache oral history states that they broke away from the Tsuutʼina, an Athabascan people who moved onto the Great Plains in Alberta, Canada. The sources also list *Plains Apache Tales* and *Six Plains Apache Tales* in the bibliography, but do not summarize particular tales, epics, proverbs, riddles, storytelling settings, or contemporary literary preservation efforts.

## Language & religion

Plains Apache is a Southern Athabaskan language and the most divergent member of that subfamily. It has an extremely complex system of verbal morphology; its verbs show polypersonal agreement, rich aspect marking, and an Athabaskan classifier system. The language has four vowel qualities, short and long vowels, nasal and oral vowels, and a two-level register tone system. Its syntax has strongly head-final tendencies, predominantly subject-object-verb word order, and postpositions rather than prepositions. The sources state that the language became extinct in 2008, when Alfred Chalepah, Jr., died. They reference peyote ritual, medicine men, prayer, ceremonial payment, and the spiritual universe of the Plains Apache.
