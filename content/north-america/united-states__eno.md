## Overview

The Eno or Enoke, also called Stuckenock, were an American Indian tribe located in North Carolina during the 17th and 18th centuries. Their settlements included the Piedmont of North Carolina and places along the Eno River, near the territory of the Shakori and Adshusheer. By the early 18th century, the Eno, Shakori, Tutelo, Saponi, Keyauwee, and Occaneechi had been reduced to approximately 750 people. About 1715, the Eno merged with the Catawba in the North Carolina–South Carolina border area. The Eno were later absorbed into the Catawba, while some remnants may have re-migrated with the Saponi to northern North Carolina in the 1730s. Their language is known as the extinct and unclassified Shoccoree-Eno language.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The Eno built houses and other structures from interwoven saplings and sticks, which were covered with mud rather than the bark commonly used by other nearby tribes. John Lederer described their houses as generally round and noted that some were made of reed and bark, while others used watling and plaister. Each house had a small oven-shaped hovel used to store corn and mast and keep them dry. In summer, people also slept outside beneath thin arbours of wilde Palm. Their villages were built around a field used for sports and physical contests.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do record household and sporting objects, including granaries, oven-shaped storage hovels, staffs, stone bowls, and slinging stones. The Eno played *Chenco*, a sport carried out with a staff and a bowl made of stone, which was rolled across a smooth place resembling a Bowling-Green.

## Music & performance

They do describe sports and public recreation. At the Eno town visited by John Lederer, the settlement was built around a field where people engaged in strenuous sports in large numbers, including the slinging of stones. John Lawson described the Eno and Shakori as strongly associated with *Chenco*, a sport played with a staff and a stone bowl rolled across a prepared smooth surface. The Eno language article identifies *Chenco* as the only attested word of the Shoccoree-Eno language and connects it with the sport played by the Shakori and Eno. The term is also described as the source of the English word Chunkey.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do describe public entertainments and communal hospitality. People gathered for sports in a field at the center of the town, and at great entertainments they served cakes dipped in a mixture made from pressed nuts and acorns.

## Foodways

The Eno planted abundant grain and were said to reap three crops in a summer. Their granary supplied the surrounding parts. They parched nuts and acorns over a fire to remove their rank oiliness; after pressing, the nuts produced a milky liquor and the acorns an amber-coloured oil. These were mixed together and used for dipping cakes, which were served to guests at great entertainments as an unusual delicacy. The sources also mention corn and mast as materials stored in small oven-shaped hovels attached to houses.

## Oral tradition & literature

Historic descriptions are preserved through colonial records and travel accounts. William Strachey mentioned the “Anoeg” in *The Historie of Travaile Into Virginia Britannia*. Francis Yeardley recorded the “Haynokes” in a May 1654 letter, later published in *Narratives of Early Carolina (1650–1708)*. John Lederer described the Eno town visited in 1670, while John Lawson wrote about the Eno, Shakori, and Adshusheer in *A New Voyage to Carolina*. James Needham and Gabriel Arthur also recorded traveling through “Aeno” in 1673.

## Language & religion

The Shoccoree-Eno language, also known as Shoccoree, is described as an extinct and unclassified Indigenous language believed to have been spoken by the Shakori and Eno in North Carolina. It is attested by only one recorded word, *Chenco*. The Eno dialect was still spoken within the Catawba as late as 1743. The sources describe disagreement over the group’s linguistic affiliation: some scholars grouped the Eno and Shakori with Eastern Siouan peoples, while more recent discussions emphasize the absence of direct linguistic evidence and the possibility that political alliances do not establish original linguistic relationships.
