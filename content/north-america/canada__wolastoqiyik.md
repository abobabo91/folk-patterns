## Overview

The Wolastoqiyik, also known as the Maliseet or Malecite, are an Algonquian-speaking First Nation of the Wabanaki Confederacy. They are Indigenous people of the Wolastoq, or Saint John River, valley and its tributaries. Their territory extends across present-day New Brunswick and Quebec in Canada and parts of Maine in the United States. Their historical homeland covered forest, river and coastal areas within the Saint John River watershed. Today, Wolastoqey people also live in other parts of the world. Approximately 7,700 Wolastoqiyik with status live in several New Brunswick First Nations, with additional communities in Maine and Quebec. Their culture includes river-based subsistence, horticulture, fishing, hunting, basketry, birchbark canoe building, songs and a closely related language shared with the Passamaquoddy.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Birchbark canoes** — Wolastoqiyik canoes were made from birchbark. The sources identify canoe building as a traditional craft and note that European metal cutting tools and materials later influenced Wolastoqiyik production.

**Basketry items** — Wolastoqiyik made basketry items and containers. Potato farming in Maine and New Brunswick created a market and demand for Wolastoqey baskets and containers.

**Wigwams** — Building wigwams was identified as a traditional craft practised as late as the 19th century.

### Clothing & dress

They state that Wolastoqiyik acquired European clothing during the previous two centuries and that clothing, like wood, bark and basketry items, formed part of the material changes associated with colonial contact. No specific garments, head coverings, belts, footwear or ceremonial dress are named.

### Architecture

The sources describe Wolastoqiyik living in walled villages during the early 17th century and identify wigwam building as a traditional craft practised as late as the 19th century. The sources also mention Indian reserves established at The Brothers, Oromocto, Fredericton, Kingsclear, Woodstock, Tobique, Madawaska and Cacouna.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention European metal cutting tools and containers, muskets, alcohol, foods and clothing as materials acquired during the previous two centuries. Named Wolastoqiyik everyday objects include birchbark canoes, wigwams, baskets and containers. The sources state that the Wolastoqiyik used the roots of the balsam fir as thread and its pitch to waterproof canoe seams.

## Music & performance

Wolastoqiyik musical traditions include a dance song and a love song collected and published by Natalie Curtis in 1907. As transcribed by Curtis, the love song has a meter cycle of seven bars and changes between major and minor tonality. Anthropologist William H. Mechling recorded many other Wolastoqey songs on wax cylinders. These recordings are held by the Canadian Museum of History.

Many of the songs recorded by Mechling were lost to the community as pressures to assimilate into mainstream Canadian culture led the Wolastoqiyik to stop passing songs on to youth. In the 2010s, Wolastoqew musician Jeremy Dutcher undertook a project of listening to the wax cylinder recordings and reviving the songs. His album *Wolastoqiyik Lintuwakonawa* was released in 2018 and won the 2018 Polaris Music Prize. They identify the dance song, love song, wax-cylinder recordings and later revival work as the documented musical traditions.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name a Wolastoqiyik dance song.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that Wolastoqiyik men and women participated in hunting, fishing, gathering, horticulture, cooking and farming.

The sources mention Catholic missions established by French Jesuits and state that some Wolastoqiyik converted to Catholicism. They also describe traditional coastal and river locations used for hunting, fishing and gathering.

## Foodways

In the early 17th century, Wolastoqiyik women cultivated corn, beans, squash and tobacco. They also gathered and processed fruits, berries, nuts and natural produce. Men contributed by fishing and hunting, while women cooked these finds. The sources describe this economy as partly agrarian and distinguish it from the Mi'kmaq economy in this respect.

Wolastoqiyik seasonal, shifting agriculture on the river was later curtailed by the encroachment of European settlers. In the 18th century, the beaver supply was severely diminished and the traditional ways of life became difficult to maintain. During the colonial wars, Wolastoqey women took on a larger share of the economic burden and began farming crops that had previously been grown only south of Wolastoqey territory. They state that European goods, foods and clothing entered Wolastoqiyik life and that potato farming in Maine and New Brunswick created demand for Wolastoqey baskets and containers.

## Oral tradition & literature

Peter Lewis Paul was a Wolastoqew oral historian who lived on the Woodstock Reserve in New Brunswick on the Saint John River. He shared information with academic linguists, ethnohistorians and anthropologists.

The sources document language and song preservation efforts. Silas Tertius Rand translated Bible selections into Wolastoqey, published in 1863, and translated the Gospel of John, published in 1870. In the 2010s, Jeremy Dutcher listened to wax-cylinder recordings of Wolastoqey songs and worked to revive songs that had been lost to the community. His album *Wolastoqiyik Lintuwakonawa* appeared in 2018 and won the 2018 Polaris Music Prize.

## Language & religion

Wolastoqiyik speak an Algonquian language. The Wolastoqey and Passamaquoddy languages are similar enough that linguists consider them slightly different dialects of the same language, and they are typically not differentiated for study. The customs and language of the Wolastoqiyik are very similar to those of the neighbouring Passamaquoddy and are also close to those of the Algonquian-speaking Mi'kmaq and Penobscot peoples.

The people call themselves *Wəlastəkwewiyik* and *Wolastoqiyik*. *Wəlastəkw* means “bright river” or “shining river,” and *Wəlastəkwewiyik* means “People of the Bright River.” The sources state that French Jesuits established missions and that some Wolastoqiyik converted to Catholicism.

About 650 native speakers of Wolastoqey remain, along with about 500 Passamaquoddy speakers living on both sides of the border between New Brunswick and Maine. An active scholarship program at the Mi'kmaq-Wolastoqey Centre at the University of New Brunswick works with native speakers to support the language.
