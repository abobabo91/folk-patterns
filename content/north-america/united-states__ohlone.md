## Overview

The Ohlone, formerly known as Costanoans, are a Native American people of the Northern California coast. When Spanish explorers and missionaries arrived in the late eighteenth century, they inhabited the coast from San Francisco Bay through Monterey Bay to the lower Salinas Valley. Their territory extended from the northern tip of the San Francisco Peninsula to northern Big Sur, and from the Pacific Ocean to the Diablo Range, including the Santa Clara Valley, Santa Cruz Mountains, Monterey Bay area, Alameda County, Contra Costa County, and the Salinas Valley. Before Spanish contact, they lived in more than 50 distinct landholding groups rather than considering themselves one unified group. Their languages belong to the Utian family, with newer proposals grouping Ohlone, Miwok, and Yokuts within Yok-Utian. Hunting, fishing, gathering, basketry, ceremonies, oral narratives, and sacred sites remain important to cultural revitalization.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*No named pattern-bearing textile traditions are documented in the sources used.*

### Clothing & dress

Ohlone clothing varied with weather and gender. Men generally wore no clothing in warm weather. In cold weather they might wear animal-skin capes or feather capes. Women commonly wore deerskin aprons, tule skirts, or shredded-bark skirts, and also wore animal-skin capes on cool days.

Ceremonial clothing appears in descriptions of Kuksu ceremonies, which included elaborate acting and dancing in traditional costume. Body ornamentation, including necklaces, shell beads, abalone pendants, and bone or wood earrings with shells and beads, could indicate status within the community.

### Architecture

Ohlone villages occupied fixed locations, although people moved temporarily to gather seasonal foodstuffs. Along bayshores and valleys, they constructed dome-shaped houses from woven or bundled tule mats. These houses measured 6 to 20 feet in diameter. In hills where redwood trees were accessible, they built conical houses from redwood bark attached to a wooden frame. Residents of Monterey recalled redwood houses.

A main village building was the sweat lodge. It was built low into the ground, with earth walls and a roof made of earth and brush. Some Ohlone bands also built prayer houses, or sweat lodges, near stream banks for ceremonial and spiritual purification. At Indian Canyon in Hollister, a traditional sweat lodge called *Tupentak* was built for these purposes. An *upen-tah-ruk*, described as a round house or assembly house, was also constructed there for tribal meetings, traditional dances and ceremonies, and education activities.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do describe boats made from tule, propelled on the bays with double-bladed paddles. Nets and decoys were used to capture waterfowl. Stone and bone tools, shell beads, ornaments, and other everyday objects have been found in and around shellmounds.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ohlone ornamentation included necklaces, shell beads, abalone pendants, and bone or wood earrings decorated with shells and beads. Ornamentation often indicated status within the community. Cultural arts also included female tattoos, ear piercings, and nose piercings.

Ornaments and other valuables were placed with the dead after cremation. Artifacts from shellmounds include shell beads and ornaments as well as stone and bone tools. The sources describe these offerings as connected with good fortune in the afterlife.

## Music & performance

 They do document singing, dancing, acting, and ceremonial performance within Kuksu religious practice. Kuksu included elaborate acting and dancing ceremonies in traditional costume, an annual mourning ceremony, puberty rites of passage, intervention with the spirit world, and an all-male society that met in subterranean dance rooms.

Some shamans engaged in ritualistic healing through dancing, ceremony, and singing. Kuksu was shared with other Indigenous peoples of Central California, including the Miwok, Esselen, Maidu, Pomo, and northernmost Yokuts, although researchers caution against treating the spiritual beliefs of all Ohlone groups as a single unified worldview.

At Indian Canyon, traditional dances and ceremonies are held in the *upen-tah-ruk*, or round house/assembly house. The site also provides space for tribal meetings and education activities.

## Dance & theatre

Kuksu ceremonies combined elaborate acting and dancing with traditional costume, ceremonial activity, and intervention with the spirit world. They do state that contemporary Indian Canyon provides a place for traditional dances and ceremonies.

Sacred narratives have also been shared through live performances and storytelling. These performances help Ohlone people create public awareness that their cultural group survived Spanish colonization and continues to seek recognition.

## Festivals & rituals

Kuksu practice included an annual mourning ceremony and puberty rites of passage. Kuksu ceremonies also involved elaborate acting and dancing, traditional costume, intervention with the spirit world, and an all-male society that met in subterranean dance rooms.

Sweat lodges were used by men and women to “cleanse, purify, and empower themselves” for activities such as hunting and spirit dancing. These structures were often built near stream banks because water was believed to possess healing power. Indian Canyon continues to provide a place for sweat-lodge ceremonies, traditional dances, tribal meetings, and education.

Burial practices changed over time. Before the arrival of the Spanish, cremation was preferred. After cremation, loved ones and friends placed ornaments and other valuables as offerings to the dead, believing this would bring good fortune in the afterlife. Shellmounds were habitation and burial sites, and their construction resulted from village life over thousands of years. They do document puberty rites of passage and contemporary efforts to preserve sacred burial places.

## Foodways

Ohlone people lived mainly by hunting, fishing, gathering, and harvesting. Their staple foods included crushed acorns, nuts, grass seeds, and berries. Other important foods included vegetation, hunted and trapped game, fish, and seafood, including mussels and abalone from San Francisco Bay and the Pacific Ocean.

Waterfowl were the most important birds in the diet and were captured with nets and decoys. Chochenyo traditional narratives refer to ducks as food. Juan Crespí recorded that geese were stuffed and dried “to use as decoys in hunting others.” The streams contained salmon, trout, steelhead, perch, and stickleback. Animals in the region included elk, pronghorn, deer, and grizzly bear.

People practiced active management of natural resources. A rough husbandry of the land involved setting fires annually to burn old growth and obtain a better yield of seeds.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ohlone sacred narratives teach moral or spiritual lessons and express cultural, spiritual, and religious beliefs. The stories vary among Ohlone groups because they did not share a single unified identity. Only a small number survived Spanish colonization during the 1700s and 1800s, and many narratives are incomplete because of translation differences and the limited historical record.

Ohlone folklore centers on Coyote, Eagle, and Hummingbird. Coyote is described as a trickster spirit who was clever, wily, lustful, greedy, and irresponsible. In Ohlone creation stories, the world was covered with water except for Pico Blanco near Big Sur, or Mount Diablo in the northern Ohlone version. Coyote, Hummingbird, and Eagle stood on the remaining peak. Coyote’s descendants became humans.

Chochenyo narratives feature Kaknu, Coyote’s grandson, an anthropomorphic being resembling a peregrine falcon. Kaknu fought the Body of Stone, called Wiwe, who became the rocks scattered across the world. Contemporary Ohlone groups use anthropological records, cultural discussion, live performances, and storytelling to reconstruct and interpret these narratives.

## Language & religion

Ohlone languages, also called Costanoan, form a small Indigenous language family historically spoken in Northern California. They belong with the Miwok languages in the Utian family, while newer work groups Ohlone, Miwok, and Yokuts as branches of Yok-Utian. Eight attested varieties are listed: Awaswas, Chalon, Chochenyo, Karkin, Mutsun, Ramaytush, Rumsen, and Tamyen. The sources describe a debate over whether these were eight dialects or eight separate languages. The last native speakers died by the 1950s, while Chochenyo, Mutsun, and Rumsen are undergoing revival from saved records.

The sources describe Kuksu as a form of shamanism probably practiced by Ohlone groups before contact, while also emphasizing differences among bands. Medicine persons included herbal healers and shamans believed to contact the spirit world. They state that the first conversions to Catholicism occurred at Mission San Carlos Borromeo in 1771 and that first baptisms in the San Francisco Bay area occurred at Mission San Francisco in 1777.
