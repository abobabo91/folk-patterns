## Overview

The Palus, or Palouse, are a Sahaptin tribe and an Indigenous people of the Northwest Plateau, living in Washington, Oregon, and Idaho. Their territory extended from the confluence of the Snake and Clearwater Rivers to the confluence of the Snake and Columbia Rivers. It included the Palouse River Valley up to Rock Lake and remained north of the Touchet River Valley. The people lived in three main regional bands: the Upper Palouse, Middle Palouse, and Lower Palouse. Their ancestral way of life was nomadic and followed seasonal food sources. They gathered with neighboring peoples for food-gathering, hunting, fishing, feasting, trading, dancing, sports, gambling, and celebrations. Today, Palus people are primarily enrolled in the Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakama Nation.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record seasonal gatherings connected with food-gathering and hunting. The ancestral people followed food sources through the seasons, and in October 1805 many Palus were away from the area for fall food-gathering and hunting when Lewis and Clark met with the tribe.

The sources also document historical ceremonies and political encounters. In October 1805, Lewis and Clark presented one of the expedition’s silver peace medals to paramount Chief Kepowhan. The Palus were signatories of the Treaty of 1855 with the United States along with the Yakama; the treaty was negotiated at the 1855 Walla Walla Council. The Palus refused to take part directly but sent Kahlotus, Tilcoax, and Slyotze as observers.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do preserve several historical names and designations. The proper Palus autonym is given as *Naxiyamtama* or *Naha’ámpoo/Naha'u'umpu'u*, meaning “the river people.” The present tribal designation is connected with *Palúšpam*, meaning “people of Pa-luš-sa/Palus,” one of the important settlements of the Middle Palouse Band.

The sources also record names used by neighboring peoples and historical observers, including Pa-loots-poo/Pelú`cpu/Peluutspu and Palúuspam/Pelúuspem. The Palouse River and Palouse Falls are associated with the settlement Pa-luš-sa/Palus. Palouse Falls is also identified as *Aputaput*, “Falling Water.”

## Language & religion

The Palus are described as a Sahaptin-speaking group of Native Americans living on the Columbia Plateau. They state that the people lived near the Nez Perce, Wanapum, Walla Walla, Umatilla, and Yakama.

The sources describe several historical religious encounters without providing a full account of Palus religion. Hinmahtutekekaikt, also known as “James,” was among the first to be Christianized and was associated with Henry H. Spalding, Marcus Whitman, and the Nimiipu band of Hollolsotetote. Husishusis Kute is identified as a *tooat*, translated as “Medicine man or Shaman, or Prophet,” and as a leader of the Wawawai Band.
