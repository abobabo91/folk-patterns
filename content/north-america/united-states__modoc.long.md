## Overview

The Modoc are an Indigenous American people whose historical homeland lies in northeastern California and central Southern Oregon, including the Modoc Plateau, Tule Lake, Lower Klamath Lake, and the Lost River. Today, about 600 Modoc live in Klamath County, Oregon, while 200 live in Ottawa County, Oklahoma. They include the Klamath Tribes in Oregon and the Modoc Nation, formerly the Modoc Tribe of Oklahoma. The Modoc traditionally spoke a dialect of Klamath, also called Klamath–Modoc or Lutuamian, a Plateau Penutian language. Their material and intangible culture documented in the sources includes earthen dugout lodges, basketry, aquatic plant foods, fishing, seasonal hunting and gathering, ritual dance, sweat-lodge ceremonies, oral traditions concerning Kumush, and objects associated with Modoc people and the Modoc War.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Korgflätning** — The museum records identify Modoc caps, women’s caps, headgear, baskets, and a basket plate as made of basketry. The objects include an open, basket-like head covering, a basket used in games, and an unfinished basket.

**Wokas plants** — The Modoc built winter lodges near lake shores with reliable sources of seeds from aquatic wokas plants.

**Earthen dugout lodges** — Winter dwellings were shaped like beehives, covered with sticks, and plastered with mud.

### Clothing & dress

The museum records identify a Modoc open, basket-like headgear, a basketry cap, and a basketry women’s cap. One catalogue record describes a cap as belonging to Captain Jack and as made of basketry. Other records concern necklaces made of glass beads and mussel shells or fruits. The British Museum photographs show Bogus Charley wearing a hat, a shirt, a cloth tied around his neck, trousers, and boots; Burgess Charley wearing a hat and a three-piece suit; Scar Faced Charley wearing a three-piece suit and bow tie; and Boston Charley wearing a shirt and jacket. Another photograph shows three Modoc men, including Donald McKay, wearing shirts and trousers.

### Architecture

Until the 19th century, the Modoc built earthen dugout lodges for winter use. These structures were shaped like beehives, covered with sticks, and plastered with mud, and were located near lake shores with dependable sources of aquatic wokas seeds and fishing. The sources also mention a Modoc village near the Lost River built by Kintpuash’s band after leaving the reservation in 1870. No further architectural decoration, settlement plan, or Modoc vernacular building term is given.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Museum records identify a basket, an unfinished basket, a basketry plate used in games, a basketry headgear, and basketry caps. The records do not describe their manufacture in detail or provide additional Modoc names for these forms.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Museum records identify a necklace made of glass beads and mussel shells that belonged to Hi-ne-ma Riaala, described as a heroine in the war of 1873, and another necklace made of fruits.

## Music & performance

The sources record the *Shuyuhalsh*, a five-night dance rite of passage for adolescent girls. The number five figured heavily in Modoc ritual. A sweat lodge was used for purification and mourning ceremonies. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription concerns the Modoc or the United States in the supplied material.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources identify several ritual practices. The number five figured heavily in ritual, including the *Shuyuhalsh*, a five-night dance rite of passage for adolescent girls. A sweat lodge was used for purification and mourning ceremonies. The sources also describe the Modoc War, during which Kintpuash and his band resisted removal from their traditional homelands; Kintpuash was captured on June 4, 1873, and was executed with three warriors in October 1873. These events are historical rather than identified in the sources as annual commemorations.

## Foodways

Before the 19th century, the Modoc caught salmon during salmon runs and migrated seasonally to hunt and gather other food. Their winter lodges were built near lake shores with reliable sources of seeds from aquatic wokas plants and fishing.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kumush, also called Kemush, Kmukamtch, K’mukamtch, Gmok’am’c, Gmo’Kamc, or Kumukumts, is identified as the Modoc spirit of creation. The account describes Kumush as creating the Modoc tribe, giving things names, designating California as their home, and naming Mount Shasta and Tule Lake as the Modoc Garden of Eden. Recorded versions differ because the account was transmitted by different speakers in different circumstances. The sources identify Jeremiah Curtin’s *Myths of the Modocs*, published in 1912 from fieldwork conducted in the late 19th century, and Albert Gatschet’s *The Klamath Indians of Southwestern Oregon*, published in 1890. The Modoc Nation preserves a contemporary version, while H. L. Delaney’s *The Book of Spirals: Native Myths of the American West*, published in 2025, presents a twelve-part cycle in which Kemush appears as a culture hero and cosmological mediator.

## Language & religion

The Modoc traditionally spoke a dialect of Klamath, also called Klamath–Modoc or Lutuamian. Klamath is described as a Plateau Penutian language, itself a branch of the proposed Penutian language family. By 1998, only one native speaker remained; by 2003, the last fluent Klamath speaker living in Chiloquin, Oregon, was 92 years old. As of 2006, there were no fluent native speakers of either the Klamath or Modoc dialects, while revitalization efforts were underway as of 2019. The Modoc religion is not known in detail. Ritual practices documented in the sources include the five-night *Shuyuhalsh* and the use of a sweat lodge for purification and mourning. Kumush is identified as the Modoc spirit of creation.
