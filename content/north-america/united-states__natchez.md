## Overview

The Natchez are a Native American people whose historic homeland was the Natchez Bluffs along the eastern side of the Mississippi River, near present-day Natchez, Mississippi, in the Lower Mississippi Valley. Their ancestors are associated with the Plaquemine culture, whose communities built platform mounds and cultivated maize. The Natchez survived as a complex chiefdom into the period of European colonization, with strongly matrilineal descent, hereditary nobility classes, and exogamous marriage practices. After wars with the French, especially the Natchez revolt of 1729 and the conflict ending in 1731, survivors scattered among the Chickasaw, Muscogee, Cherokee, and other communities. Most descendants now live in Oklahoma, where Natchez citizens are found mainly within the Cherokee Nation and Muscogee (Creek) Nation. The language is generally considered a language isolate and is being revived.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do record that Natchez society included hereditary Sun chiefs and that ritual status was associated with the Great Sun, the Female Sun, the Tattooed Serpent, and members of the Sun royal family.

### Architecture

Plaquemine communities in the Natchez Bluffs region built complex earthworks and platform mound architecture. Emerald Mound was an important ceremonial center and was the second-largest pre-Columbian structure in North America north of Mexico. The Natchez used Emerald Mound but abandoned it before 1700, after which their center of power shifted to the Grand Village of the Natchez. The Grand Village had between three and five platform mounds.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention a ceremonial pipe used during a three-day peace ceremony in 1700 and a craftsman of war clubs who died with the Tattooed Serpent in 1725.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They state that a chief known to the French as the Tattooed Serpent held a hereditary political position.

## Music & performance

The sources document ceremonial and verbal performance. In 1700, the Natchez received Iberville with a three-day-long peace ceremony involving the smoking of a ceremonial pipe and a feast.

Natchez oral performance included specialized storytelling. Storytellers used a register called “cannibal speech” when impersonating cannibal characters in oral literature. This register substituted particular morphemes and words for those used in ordinary Natchez. The language sources provide an example in which the ordinary optative prefix is exchanged for a cannibal-register prefix.

Known linguistic documentation includes wax-cylinder recordings made by Victor Riste in 1931 of Watt Sam speaking Natchez. These are described as the only known recordings of spoken Natchez.

## Dance & theatre

They describe oral narratives in which protagonists encounter, trick, marry the daughters of, kill, or are eaten by cannibals, and they note that storytellers adopted “cannibal speech” when impersonating those characters. This is the only performance convention described in the supplied sources.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe ceremonies connected with diplomacy, chiefly succession, and death. In 1700, the Natchez held a three-day-long peace ceremony for Iberville that included smoking a ceremonial pipe and a feast.

Natchez political and ritual life centered on the hereditary Sun lineage. The paramount chief was the Great Sun, whose authority was associated with civil affairs, while the Tattooed Serpent oversaw war, peace, and diplomacy. The Great Sun was described as the son of the Female Sun, whose daughter would be the mother of the next Great Sun, preserving control within a single Sun lineage.

The death of a Sun could involve ritual human sacrifice. Wives of a deceased male Sun were expected to accompany him through ritual suicide, and other people could choose to follow him into death. At the death of the Tattooed Serpent in 1725, two wives, one sister, his first warrior, doctor, head servant, the servant’s wife, nurse, and a craftsman of war clubs chose to die with him. Mothers sometimes sacrificed infants in such ceremonies; relatives of adults who died were honored and rose in status.

## Foodways

The sources mention maize cultivation among the Plaquemine people who preceded the historic Natchez and describe feasts in connection with diplomacy. The 1700 peace ceremony for Iberville included a feast. They also state that the Natchez traded furs for firearms, blankets, alcohol, and other supplies.

## Oral tradition & literature

Natchez oral literature included stories centered on cannibalism. Traditionally, certain stories could be told only during winter. Their protagonists might encounter cannibals, trick them, marry their daughters, kill them, or be eaten by them. When storytellers impersonated cannibal characters, they used the specialized register called “cannibal speech,” which replaced several ordinary morphemes and words.

John R. Swanton and Mary R. Haas documented Natchez oral literature while working with Watt Sam. The language sources also identify “The Woman Who Was a Fox” as the title of a Natchez oral narrative discussed in a scholarly publication. The supplied material does not provide the narrative’s plot.

The Natchez nation is working to revive Natchez as a spoken language. As of 2011, field linguists from the community were being trained in documentation techniques, and six members of the Natchez tribe in Oklahoma spoke the language, out of about 10,000.

## Language & religion

Natchez is generally considered a language isolate, although it has also been proposed as distantly related to the Muskogean languages. Historically, Natchez speakers lived in Mississippi and Louisiana; after the destruction of the chiefdom, speakers took refuge among neighboring peoples and became frequently multilingual in Muscogee, Cherokee, Natchez, and English. The language was traditionally passed down matrilineally and gradually became endangered. It is now generally considered extinct despite revitalization efforts.

The language has complex verbal inflection, active-stative alignment, and subject-object-verb, or more precisely Agent-Object-Verb and Subject-Verb, ordering. Its last fluent speakers included Watt Sam and Nancy Raven, who worked with Mary R. Haas in the 1930s.

The sources describe Natchez ritual practices, chiefly sacrifice, ritual suicide, infant sacrifice, and ceremonial diplomacy.
