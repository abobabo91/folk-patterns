## Overview

The name Cowlitz covers two culturally and linguistically distinct Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest: the Lower Cowlitz or Cowlitz proper, and the Upper Cowlitz, also called Cowlitz Klickitat or Taitnapam. Their traditional homelands are in western Washington state in the United States, especially along the Cowlitz and Lewis Rivers. Lower Cowlitz communities belong to the Coast Salish cultural and linguistic sphere, while the Upper Cowlitz are Sahaptin-speaking and part of the Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakama Nation. Today, Lower Cowlitz people are enrolled principally in the Cowlitz Indian Tribe, Quinault Indian Nation, and Confederated Tribes of the Chehalis Reservation. The group is important to folk-culture study because its material life followed a yearly cycle of root gathering, berry harvesting, fishing, hunting, basketry, and movement between river and upland locations.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Imbricated coiled baskets** — The Cowlitz produced fully imbricated, coiled baskets with strong geometric designs. They were made from bear grass, cedar root, horsetail root, and cedar bark, and were used to gather berries and fruits. Pigments came from bright fruits and vegetables such as beets or blackberries; baskets were often repaired and kept through many generations.

**Mats, fishnets, and basketry** — During spring, Cowlitz families harvested barks and grasses to make mats, fishnets, and basketry.

### Architecture

Cowlitz families lived in cedar houses along rivers and streams during the cold season. In spring they left these houses by canoe and horseback to harvest camas bulbs, roots, barks, and grasses, later moving into higher country for seasonal berries, and returning in fall to harvest salmon.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do document canoes, wooden boards used in head flattening, leather bands, cedar bark, baskets, mats, and fishnets, but provide no further catalogue of these objects.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The Cowlitz practiced head flattening. Infants were placed on a wooden board, usually covered with moss or loose cedar-bark fibers, and a pad and smooth piece of bark were strapped over the forehead with leather bands.

## Music & performance

 They state that the Cowlitz peoples were fluent in Chinook Jargon, an intertribal trade language used to bridge language differences among groups speaking different Salish and Sahaptin dialects. The sources also identify Native spirituality as continuing among Cowlitz communities during the period of Catholic missionary activity.

## Festivals & rituals

 They describe a yearly subsistence cycle organized by season. In spring, people left their cedar houses to harvest camas bulbs, roots, barks, and grasses. In summer, they moved into higher country to pick seasonal berries. In fall, they returned to river houses to harvest salmon for the coming season. Hunting and fishing were practiced throughout the year, while roots and fruits were harvested seasonally.

The sources also describe head flattening as a practice carried out on newborn infants until they reached eight to twelve months old. The child was placed on a wooden board and pressure was applied to the forehead with bark, a pad, and leather bands. No Cowlitz wedding, funeral, coming-of-age, or other life-cycle rites are described. Catholic missionaries visited the Cowlitz beginning in December 1838, but the sources state that many people retained elements of Native spirituality rather than fully immersing themselves in Catholicism.

## Foodways

Salmon was important to the Cowlitz diet, although the sources state that it was less central than among some other tribes. The Cowlitz were accomplished hunters, harvested roots as dietary supplements, and gathered seasonal fruits and berries. Their yearly cycle included harvesting camas bulbs and other roots in spring, gathering berries in summer, and harvesting salmon in fall. Hunting and fishing took place throughout the year. They do state that the Cowlitz generally lacked access to saltwater and that the Columbia River’s resources were of little use to them. Horses were used for multiple purposes, including travel during seasonal movements.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They record a debate concerning Cowlitz history, territory, ancestry, ethnicity, and language, and preserve several accounts of historical figures and encounters, including Simon Plamondon’s relationship with the Lower Cowlitz and the missionary activities of Herbert Beaver, François N. Blanchet, and Modeste Demers.

Language documentation and revitalization are better represented. In the 1960s, Dale M. Kinkade conducted interviews and audio recordings with Emma Mesplie and Lucy Foster for the Cowlitz language. This material became much of the basis for the Lower Cowlitz dictionary used by the Cowlitz Indian Tribe. Cowlitz went dormant in the 1960s and, as of 2022, was being revitalized by the Cowlitz Tribe in collaboration with the Language Conservancy.

## Language & religion

Cowlitz, also known as Cowlitz Salish and written in the source as ƛʼpúlmixq or Sƛ̕púlmš, is a Tsamosan language of the Coast Salish branch of the Salishan language family. It was spoken by the Lower Cowlitz people and is spoken today by both Lower and Upper Cowlitz people. The language had two dialects, but they were poorly documented because of its extinction as a spoken language. The Upper Cowlitz spoke a Sahaptin language; the relationship between Upper Cowlitz identity, Sahaptin, and earlier languages remains a matter of scholarly debate.

Cowlitz communities were fluent in Chinook Jargon. The sources describe Native spirituality continuing alongside or despite Catholic missionary activity.
