## Overview

The Pequot are a Native American people of Connecticut, associated especially with the coastal area between the Niantic River of Connecticut and the Narragansett in western Rhode Island. Today, nearly all individuals identified as Pequot live in the communities of the Eastern Pequot Tribal Nation and the Mashantucket Pequot or Western Pequot. The Eastern Pequot Tribal Nation has a reservation called “Lantern Hill,” while the Mashantucket Pequot have a reservation in Ledyard. The Eastern Pequot Tribal Nation has 1130 members, and the Mashantucket Pequot or Western Pequot number 800+. Historically, the Pequot spoke Pequot, a dialect of the Mohegan-Pequot language, an Eastern Algonquian language. Their cultural history is marked by the Pequot War, language loss, political dispersal, federal recognition, and contemporary language-revival efforts.

## Material culture

### Architecture

They state that Fidelia Fielding was the last person to live in the traditional log dwelling, but give no further description of its construction, roof, decoration, or social use.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Pequot language article states that Gladys Tantaquidgeon collected thousands of tribal documents and artifacts, and that the Mashantucket Pequot Museum and Research Center collection includes a 1992 menu attempting to translate words such as hamburger and hot dog into Mohegan-Pequot, but it does not catalogue the objects’ forms or manufacture.

## Music & performance

They do identify Samson Occom as an 18th-century Mohegan minister and scholar who was fluent in Mohegan and is believed to have used the language in his preaching. The sources also state that prayers from the Baháʼí Faith have been translated into the Mohegan-Pequot language.

The sources describe historical and contemporary language work connected with cultural preservation. In 1717, Experience Mayhew translated the Lord’s Prayer into Mohegan-Pequot. Ezra Stiles collected Pequot linguistic data in Groton in 1762. The Mohegan Language Project later created lessons, a dictionary, and online learning materials, with the primary goal of enabling the next generation of Mohegan people to become fluent. These activities are documented as language and religious or educational practices rather than as a music tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do describe several historical and cultural events that shaped Pequot identity. The Pequot War took place from 1634–1638, and the Treaty of Hartford sought to prohibit the Pequots from returning to their lands, speaking their tribal language, or referring to themselves as Pequots. Connecticut established two reservations for the Pequots in 1683: the Eastern Pequot Reservation in North Stonington, Connecticut, and the Western Pequots, or Mashantucket Pequot Reservation, in Ledyard. The Mashantucket Pequot tribe received federal recognition in 1983 through a settlement of a land claim. The cultural museum on the Mashantucket Pequot Reservation opened on August 11, 1998. The Eastern Pequot Tribal Nation was recognized in 2002, although the Department of the Interior revoked that recognition in 2005.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe a historical oral language and later documentation. Mohegan-Pequot was traditionally an oral language with no standardized writing system, and the language was passed down entirely through spoken word. The significant historic writings were produced by European colonizers who interacted with Mohegan-Pequot speakers.

Language documentation began at least in the 17th century. A Pequot vocabulary list was compiled by Reverend James Noyes in Groton in 1690. Experience Mayhew translated the Lord’s Prayer into Mohegan-Pequot in 1717, and Ezra Stiles collected Pequot linguistic data in Groton in 1762. Fidelia Fielding left four diaries, which are being used in the process of restoring the language. The Mashantucket Pequot are also conducting analysis of historical documents containing Pequot words and comparing them with closely related languages. They have reclaimed more than 1,000 words and have begun offering language classes with help from the Mashpee Wampanoag.

## Language & religion

Pequot was historically a dialect of Mohegan-Pequot, an Eastern Algonquian language. The broader language is also known as Mohegan-Pequot-Montauk, Secatogue, and Shinnecock-Poosepatuck. Its dialects included Mohegan, Pequot, and Niantic in New England, and Montaukett and Shinnecock on Long Island. Pequot became largely extinct within a generation or so after the Treaty of Hartford, which made speaking the language a capital offense. Pequot communities now speak English as their first language, while the Mashantucket Pequot are undertaking language revival.

Mohegan-Pequot was historically oral and had no standardized writing system. A practical orthography using the Latin alphabet was created during the 21st-century revitalization effort. The sources mention Christian Pequot communities, the Lord’s Prayer, preaching by Samson Occom, and translations of Baháʼí Faith prayers.
