## Overview

The Northern Paiute are a Numic people of the Great Basin, traditionally living in what is now eastern California, western Nevada, and southeast Oregon. Their territories were generally centered on lakes, wetlands, rivers, and desert valleys that supplied fish, waterfowl, and other foods. Named groups included the Kuyui Dükadü around Pyramid Lake and the lower Truckee River, the Toi Dükadü in the Carson Sink, and the Kucadikadi of Mono County, California. Northern Paiute originally lived a seminomadic life, following animal migrations and seasonal foods, before many communities became more sedentary after the arrival of settlers. Northern Paiute is a Western Numic language of the Uto-Aztecan family. The sources estimate around 500 fluent speakers in 1994.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

 They state that women made clothing, while men hunted and protected their families.

### Architecture

 They do describe small Indian colonies established near farms, cities, and ranches after environmental degradation and settlement made earlier lifeways difficult to maintain.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They state that women made household tools, gathered fruit and seeds, cooked, cleaned, cared for children, and made clothing.

## Music & performance

Northern Paiute stories were often poems performed musically and called “song-poems.” Members of the tribe chanted and acted out the stories to the beat of a drum, with people dancing.

Storytelling took place among large groups gathered around a fire. The season for storytelling in the American West was during the winter months. Elderly members of the tribe told stories from memory, as they had received them from previous elders and family members. These performances transmitted accounts of animal people and human people, their origins and values, their spiritual and natural environment, and their culture and daily lives.

The sources also describe shamanic performances and practices. A Northern Paiute shaman is called a *puhagim*. Shamans visited sacred sites, including mountains, caves, waterways, and unique geological formations. They performed duties including curing, rainmaking, warfare, fighting, and sorcery. In healing, a shaman could use a rattle, smoke, and songs to draw upon the power of the universe.

## Dance & theatre

 They state that people danced while members of the tribe chanted and acted out story-poems to the beat of a drum. Storytelling and performance transmitted legends, history, values, and knowledge from tribal elders to younger members and from grandmothers and grandfathers to grandchildren.

## Festivals & rituals

 They state that the season for storytelling in the American West was during the winter months.

Northern Paiute origin stories were transmitted orally. One version tells of a sagehen, also known as Centrocercus, surviving a massive flood, caring for a fire, and witnessing the appearance of a man called Nűműzóho. Another creation story tells of a man and a woman who heard a voice from within a bottle, from which four beings emerged. The sources describe these narratives as stories with spiritual and cultural significance, rather than as named annual rituals.

Shamans performed curing, rainmaking, warfare, fighting, and sorcery at sacred sites. The Northern Paiute believe in *puha*, a force that gives life to the physical world and moves the elements, plants, and animals. Shamans were intermediaries between illness and the well-being of the community.

## Foodways

Northern Paiute foodways were adapted to the desert environment of the Great Basin. Bands generally occupied territories centered on lakes or wetlands that supplied fish and waterfowl. Communal hunt drives, sometimes involving neighboring bands, took rabbits and pronghorn from surrounding areas.

People gathered pinyon nuts in the mountains in the fall as a critical winter food source. Women gathered grass seeds and roots, which were important parts of the diet. Several band names derive from characteristic foods. The Kuyui Dükadü were “cui-ui eaters,” or trout eaters; the Küpa Dükadü were “ground-squirrel eaters”; the Toi Dükadü were “tule eaters”; and the Kucadikadi were “brine fly eaters.”

The sources also record names connected with other foods. The Wadadökadö were “Wada Root and Grass-seed Eaters,” the Kamodökadö were “Hare-Eaters,” the Moadökadö were “Wild onion Eaters,” and the Tabussi Dükadü were “Pine nut Eaters” or “Edible seed, sedge Eaters.” The Pogai Dükadü were “[Wild] Onion Eaters” or “Grass seed Eaters.” Other names referred to crayfish, salmon caught in traps, yellow-bellied marmots, groundhogs, sagebrush, cutthroat trout, chub carp, salt, alkali, and Lomatium dissectum root tubers.

## Oral tradition & literature

Northern Paiute oral tradition includes creation stories and origin narratives. One account describes a massive flood, a surviving sagehen, the cannibal Nűműzóho, a woman who escaped, and four children who became Paiute and Pit Rivers. Another account tells of four beings emerging from a bottle and being divided into good and bad pairs. The stories repeatedly include Nűműzóho the Cannibal, Coyote as “the one who fixed things,” a man and a woman who bear four children, and pairs of children who quarrel.

These narratives were told to large groups around a fire, particularly during the winter months. They were performed as musical “song-poems,” with chanting, acting, drumming, and dancing. The sources state that many stories and much of Northern Paiute history continue to be passed on orally.

Language preservation efforts include the 2005 partnership between the Northwest Indian Language Institute at the University of Oregon and schools on the Warm Springs Indian Reservation. In 2013, Washoe County, Nevada, became the first school district in Nevada to offer Northern Paiute classes. Elder Ralph Burns and linguist Catherine Fowler helped develop a spelling system, a language-learning book called “Numa Yadooape,” and computer disks of language lessons.

## Language & religion

Northern Paiute, whose endonym is Numu or nɨɨmɨ and which is also known as Paviotso, is a Western Numic language of the Uto-Aztecan family. It is closely related to the Mono language. The language had around 500 fluent speakers in 1994 according to Marianne Mithun. Northern Paiute is agglutinative, using suffix complexes with several morphemes for a variety of purposes. The sources describe highly variable phonology and an alphabet using 19 letters. They document beliefs concerning *puha*, the force that gives life to the physical world, and shamanism. A shaman, or *puhagim*, could heal physically or spiritually ill people by using power from the universe. Sacred places included mountains, caves, waterways, and unique geological formations. Parowan Gap is identified as sacred to the Paiute.
