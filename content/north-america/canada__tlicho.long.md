## Overview

The Tłı̨chǫ — also spelled Tlicho and long known in English as the Dogrib — are a Dene First Nations people of the Athabaskan-speaking ethnolinguistic group in the Northwest Territories of Canada. Their land, called *Ndè* (also *Dè*, *Dèe* or *Nèe*), lies east of the Mackenzie River (*Deh Cho*) between Great Slave Lake (*Tıdeè*) and Great Bear Lake (*Sahtu*). Six communities have a Tłı̨chǫ majority: Behchokǫ̀ (formerly Rae-Edzo), the largest; Whatì (Lac la Martre); Gamèti (Rae Lakes); Wekweètì (Snare Lake); and Dettah (*T'èɂehdaà*) and Ndilǫ, the two communities of the Yellowknives Dene First Nation. The population grew from roughly 800 in the mid-nineteenth century to about 1,700 by the 1970s and about 2,080 at the 2011 census. In folk-culture terms the sources used here document the Tłı̨chǫ chiefly through tailored skin clothing and beadwork, through a bush economy of hunting, fishing and trapping defended across two treaties, and through an oral language now under organised revitalisation.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The catalogue records behind this atlas name no vernacular Tłı̨chǫ terms for the pieces they describe, so the traditions below are listed by the museum's own descriptions.

**Beaded smoked-moosehide footwear** — Moccasins sewn from smoked moose skin, trimmed with beaver fur, and decorated with cloth appliqué together with glass beadwork worked in a floral pattern. Smoked hide, fur trim and beadwork appear on one object as a single combined technique rather than as separate decorative registers.

**Porcupine quillwork on moose skin** — A pair of moose-skin slippers decorated with porcupine quillwork and finished, like the beaded moccasins, with beaver fur trim. Quill and bead decoration are documented side by side in the same footwear repertoire.

**Bead-embroidered cloth parka** — A green cotton parka with leather and fur trim at the shoulders, wrists and bottom hem, embroidered with glass beads and closed with a metal zipper. The garment shows commercial cotton and metal hardware carrying the same bead embroidery that the hide pieces carry.

**Padded cotton tumpline** — A head-band or tumpline made of cotton: an elliptical padded panel with rope tied to either end, strapped across the head to pull weights. It is a load-carrying textile rather than a decorated one.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Floral* — the only pattern named in the sources, used in the glass beadwork of the moose-skin moccasins.

### Clothing & dress

The dress documented here is cold-country tailored work in skin and cloth. Footwear comes in two forms: moccasins of smoked moose skin with beaver-fur trim, cloth insets and floral glass beadwork, and slippers of moose skin with porcupine quillwork and beaver-fur trim. Outerwear is represented by a parka — in this instance not of skin but of green cotton, with leather and fur trim placed at the shoulders, wrists and bottom hem, bead-embroidered, and fastened with a metal zipper, showing the incorporation of manufactured cloth and findings into a northern garment form. A padded cotton head-band or tumpline, with rope at each end for hauling loads against the forehead, belongs to the same body-worn kit, though its purpose is transport rather than dress. The sources used do not distinguish men's from women's clothing, describe no head coverings or belts, and name no ceremonial dress distinct from daily wear.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Two documented objects belong to the working equipment of a trapping and hauling economy. A **pelt-stretcher** for lynx is carved from a length of wood tapered to a point at one end — a single-piece tool for dressing fur, the commodity at the centre of Tłı̨chǫ trade at Old Fort Rae (*Nıhshı̀ Kʼe*, *Ninhsin Kon*) and Fort Norman (*Tiłihtʼa*). A **head-band or tumpline**, an elliptical pad of cotton with rope tied at both ends, carries loads by pressure across the wearer's head. The sources used describe no Tłı̨chǫ ceramics and no metalwork; the only metal they name is the zipper on a beaded cotton parka, and the only glass is the beadwork on parka and moccasins.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used name no Tłı̨chǫ jewelry, tattooing, or hair practices; glass beads appear in them only as embroidery sewn onto footwear and a parka, not as worn ornaments.

## Music & performance

The one broadcast and spoken-word institution they name is a radio station set up as part of the Tłı̨chǫ Government's language work, alongside a weekly Elder Evening Story Telling.

## Festivals & rituals

What the sources do record are the political ceremonies that structure recent Tłı̨chǫ public life: the signing of Treaty 8 in 1900 at Fort Resolution (*Įndàà*, *Deninoo Kue*), where Drygeese signed as spokesman for the Tłı̨chǫ and Yellowknives with the headmen Benaiyah and Sek'eglinan; the signing of Treaty 11 in the summer of 1921 by the trading chief Monfwi (*EwaroʼA*, "Small Mouth", 1866–1936), who had been appointed by the Tłı̨chǫ leadership to represent all Tłı̨chǫ groups; and an earlier peace treaty at Mesa Lake in 1825 (or 1829) between the great Tłı̨chǫ *dǫnekʼàawi* ("people's trader", i.e. trading chief) Chief Edzo and the Yellowknife trading chief Akaitcho, which ended a long period of hostility and warfare between the two peoples. The Tłı̨chǫ Agreement, signed on 25 August 2003 and in full effect from 4 August 2005, was marked as "the first official day of the Tlicho Government and the Tlicho community governments."

## Foodways

The sources used name no Tłı̨chǫ dishes, staples or cooking methods. They do establish the subsistence base and its political weight: during the debate over Treaty 11, following the discovery of oil in the Mackenzie River Valley, the Dene did not want to lose their right to hunt, fish, gather and trap in the area, and opposed being confined to reserves; under the Tłı̨chǫ Agreement the community legislatures hold the power to control hunting and fishing. Fishing is embedded in the landscape names themselves: Dettah's Tłı̨chǫ name *T'èɂehdaà*, "Burnt Point," refers to a traditional Dene fishing camp. The language preserves a grammatical distinction built on subsistence work — *nàzèe-dǫǫ̀ ts'ı̨ı̨lı̨* and *nàzèe-dǫǫ̀ ats'ı̨ı̨t'e* both mean "we are hunters," the first for a party hunting at the moment, the second for hunting as a regular occupation.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tłı̨chǫ Yatıì was traditionally an oral language only, and the sources record both its oral stock and its recent written turn. The people's own name, *Tłı̨chǫ Done* (or *Thlingchadinne*), "Dog-Flank People," refers to a fabled descent from a supernatural dog-man; the English "Dogrib" and the Cree-derived form *Alimospikayak*, "Dog-Flanks," recorded on the 1682 Franquelin map, both translate it. Titles of leadership carry their own glosses — *dǫnekʼàawi* or *donekʼawi* ("people's trader"), *Kʼàowo* ("leader"), *Kʼaàwidaà* ("highest trader") — as do group names such as Dechı̨ Laotʼı̨ ("Edge of the Woods People"), Etʼaa gotʼį ("People Next to Another People") and Sahtı Kʼe Hotʼı̨ı̨̀ ("Great Bear Lake People"). The first edition of *Tłıchǫ Yatıì Enįhtł'è – A Dogrib Dictionary* appeared in 1992, giving the Tłı̨chǫ a database of words and spellings and sparking the first revitalisation work. A weekly Elder Evening Story Telling, transcription and translation of materials for classes, and community and K–7 immersion classes continue that work. Tłı̨chǫ authorship and subject matter also reach print and broadcast: the writer Richard Van Camp, author of *The Lesser Blessed*; the artist James Wedzin of Behchokǫ̀; and Val Wake's novel *White Bird Black Bird*, which tells the story of Dogrib input into the formation of the NWT Indian Brotherhood.

## Language & religion

Tłı̨chǫ Yatıì, also called Dogrib, is a Northern Athabaskan language within the Na-Dene family. Statistics Canada counted 2,080 speakers in 2011 and 1,735 in 2016, with roughly 1,350 speaking it at home; mother-tongue numbers are falling and the language is listed as endangered, while speakers are commonly fluent in English. The Yellowknives Dene speak the *Wıı̀lıı̀deh Yatıı̀* dialect (the Dettah–Ndilǫ dialect), which arose after 1829 when Chipewyan speakers took up Tłı̨chǫ and carried over some Chipewyan words and grammar. Writing marks nasal vowels with an ogonek, called *wı̨ghǫą*, "its little nose," and low tone with a grave accent, called *wets'aà*, "its hat"; high tone goes unmarked and *i* is written without its dot. The language is agglutinating, polysynthetic and head-marking, with SOV word order and mainly prefixing morphology. It is one of nine official Indigenous languages of the Northwest Territories and one of two official languages of the Tłı̨chǫ Government. The sources used do not describe Tłı̨chǫ religious practice; they record the Canadian Indian residential school system, mandatory for Indigenous children by the 1920s and finally closed in 1996, as the main force driving language shift to English.
