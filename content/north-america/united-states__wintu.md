## Overview

The Wintu, also known as Northern Wintun, are a Native American people of Northern California and part of the broader Wintun, or Wintuan, association. Their traditional territory lay primarily on the western side of the northern Sacramento Valley, from the Sacramento River to the Coast Range, and included the southern Upper Sacramento River, the southern McCloud River, and the upper Trinity River. Many Northern Wintu continue to live on or near their traditional homelands in Trinity and Shasta counties. The Wintu language belongs to the Wintuan language family. The Wintu-speaking peoples also include the Nomlaki and Patwin. Hunting, fishing, gathering, basket weaving, dance, spiritual beliefs, and close relationships with rivers and other natural resources remain central subjects in descriptions of Wintu culture.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Basket weaving** — Basketry was a major part of Wintu culture. Women gathered plants used both for food and for making implements, and baskets served cooking, storing, sifting, and carrying purposes.

**Woven hats** — Woven hats were worn by many Wintu women. The sources identify these hats as part of the basket-weaving tradition.

### Clothing & dress

 They state only that many Wintu women wore woven hats.

### Architecture

Wintu homes were small and semi-permanent and were built along waterways. Larger communities had an earth lodge used as a sweat lodge for spiritual renewal, purification, and connection to nature; unmarried men without families also slept there. The sources additionally describe River and Hill Patwin houses: River Patwin homes were dome-shaped and made with sticks, straw, and other local materials, while Hill Patwin homes were conical and covered with bark.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They describe baskets used for cooking, storing, sifting, and carrying, as well as traps used by men when hunting animals.

## Music & performance

The sources identify dance as an important part of Wintu social and cultural life. When villages had extra food, they sometimes invited neighboring tribes to feast, dance, and play games. Dance had purposes beyond entertainment. The *suneh*, or begging dance, was performed when one person transferred property to another. The sources also mention Wintu songs in the bibliography, including *Wintu Songs* by Dorothy Demetracopoulou. No information is provided about court, funeral, wedding, or tea-house performance contexts.

## Dance & theatre

The *suneh*, or begging dance, is the only Wintu dance named in the sources. It was performed when one person transferred property to another, showing that dance could mark social and economic relationships as well as provide entertainment.

## Festivals & rituals

 They state that villages with extra food sometimes invited neighboring tribes to feast, dance, and play games. An earth lodge in larger communities served as a sweat lodge for spiritual renewal, purification, and connection to nature; unmarried men without families also slept there. They do describe a 1850 “friendship feast” in the history of settler violence, when poisoned food was served to local Native people; 100 Nomsuu and 45 Wenemem Wintu died as a result.

## Foodways

Fishing was central to Wintu life. Salmon from the McCloud and Sacramento rivers was the primary food source, and Steelhead trout came from the upper Trinity River. The Wintu also hunted deer, brown bears, rabbits, gophers, squirrels, wood rats, quail, and other avian species. Brown bears were pursued during the fall season, when they were well-fed and lethargic. Grasshoppers were boiled in baskets and then dried in the sun. Salmon flour was valuable in trade.

Acorns were the primary plant-based food. Other edible plants included manzanita berries, soaproot, clover, miner’s lettuce, skunkbush berries, hazelnuts, pine nuts, wild grapes, sunflower seeds, and cotton flower seeds. Manzanita berries were used to prepare soup and cider. Women gathered plants both for food and for making implements.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They list *Wintu Myths* by Cora A. Du Bois and Dorothy Demetracopoulou, *A Bag of Bones: The Wintu Myths of a Trinity River Indian* by Grant Towendolly, and *Dream Songs and Ceremony: Reflections on Traditional California Indian Dance* by Frank R. LaPena. They also identify *Wintu Songs* and *Wintu Texts* in the bibliography. The sources state that Wintu communities seek to preserve, promote, and protect tribal culture and history, and that the Winnemem Wintu have worked with the Indigenous Language Institute on language revitalization.

## Language & religion

Wintu is a Wintuan language formerly spoken by the Wintu people of Northern California and is the northernmost member of the Wintun family. The Wintun family was spoken in Shasta County, Trinity County, the Sacramento River Valley, and adjacent areas up to the Carquinez Strait of San Francisco Bay. Wintu has a flexible basic word order and a complex morphology involving suffixation, prefixation, compounding, reduplication, and vowel and consonant ablaut. As of 2011, Headman Marc Franco of the Winnemem Wintu had been working with the Indigenous Language Institute on revitalization of the Winnemem Wintu language.

The sources describe spiritual beliefs connected with nature, rivers, purification, and renewal. The Winnemem Wintu call themselves “Middle Water people” in their language and say that they were born from water, are the water, and fight to protect it.
