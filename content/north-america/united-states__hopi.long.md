## Overview

The Hopi are a Native American people living principally in northeastern Arizona, in villages set atop mesas — the First, Second and Third Mesas — on the Hopi Reservation, which is entirely surrounded by the far larger Navajo Reservation. Most are enrolled in the Hopi Tribe of Arizona, whose headquarters is at Kykotsmovi; others belong to the Colorado River Indian Tribes on the Arizona–California border. The 2010 U.S. census recorded about 19,338 people self-identifying as Hopi. The Hopi language (*Hopílavayi*) belongs to the Uto-Aztecan family. In folk-culture terms the Hopi matter because an unbroken agricultural and ceremonial life — maize farming, a lunar ceremonial calendar, matrilineal clans and the katsina societies — is expressed directly in their pottery, basketry, weaving, carved figures and silverwork, much of it now held in museums on three continents.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Hopi weaving is a men's craft: the Museum of World Culture records a woman's dress as "woven by men."

**Woman's dress (manta)** — A dark blue woven garment sewn with red yarn, with twisted decorative cords in red and green stitched on at both ends. The Gothenburg example was bought in 1969 from a crafts shop in Tucson, Arizona.

**Woven belt** — A long narrow warp-patterned warp-rib band woven in green, red and white, the green appearing as a narrow stripe along each edge, finished with a long twisted fringe. The Gothenburg belt was bought at Hubbell Trading Post, Ganado, Arizona, in the summer of 1969; its record notes a warp density of 16 threads per centimetre against a weft density of 4.

**Woven sash of purchased yarn** — The Finnish Heritage Agency holds a wool sash woven with commercial "Germantown" yarn: black weft, a warp of black, green and red, a red centre field carrying a chain of flattened squares in black, black edges with a green line between edge and centre, and fringes at the ends. The pick-patterned centre line stands slightly proud of the surface, the pattern formed by carrying warps on the face.

**Wicker tray basket** — A plaited tray from Oraibi whose design its collector called "the whirlwind," after the dust whirls common in that part of the Southwest. Warp ribs of sumac or willow twigs, weft of rabbit brush, the rim wrapped with yucca, all dyes native. The catalogue remarks that the Hopi have perhaps the widest range of native basket dyes in the Southwest.

**Coiled plaque** — A coiled basket plaque from Second Mesa whose design represents a katsina mask; both coil foundation and wrapping are yucca, giving a softer surface than the other basket types. Such plaques are used primarily as decoration, hung on house walls, though they also serve as trays.

**Twilled yucca sifter** — A plaited winnowing and sifting basket of twilled yucca, made on Second Mesa; the Gothenburg example was obtained from Tom Pavatea at Polacca in August 1934.

**Motif vocabulary.** Whirlwind (dust-whirl) design on wicker trays; katsina mask on coiled plaques; on painted pottery, per a study quoted in the Gothenburg record, geometric elements of lines and bands, triangles and scrolls, commonly set within formal divided bands, alongside highly conventionalized life forms; bird wings framed by geometric pattern; red cloud terraces; rain symbols in black on red slip; the eyes-and-mouth face of the female being Ha-har-wug-ti, mother of the six differently coloured Natacka monsters, flanked by two lines showing how married women dress their hair.

### Clothing & dress

For everyday wear the sources show photographs rather than garments: a Hopi man hoeing a corn field, five men crossing desert country with sticks and sacks, a woman holding her small son on a mule in front of their adobe, a child climbing a ladder from one adobe level to another with a much younger child on her back. Museum-held dress items are a dark blue woven woman's dress sewn with red yarn and finished with twisted red and green cords, and long woven belts and sashes with twisted fringes, worn at the waist. Ceremonial dress is documented mainly at second hand, through the figures that model it and through photographs: British Museum postcards of Fred Kabotie and another Hopi man in the Desert View Watchtower note that both wear traditional clothing and ornament, and a Náprstek Museum photograph shows a man in dance costume with necklaces and rattles. Carved katsina figures wear a case mask with feathers, a scarf at the neck, a skirt with green and red striping along the hem, a sash at the waist and bands on the upper arms. At Keams Canyon boarding school from 1887 children were forced to give up their traditional clothing, and boys to cut their long hair.

### Architecture

Precontact architecture reflects early Hopi society and its ideas of home and family, and many Hopi houses share traits with those of neighbouring Pueblo peoples. Early communal structures, above all the Pueblo Great Houses, combined living rooms, storage rooms and religious sanctuaries called *kivas*, each room allotted to particular activities. Because the Hopi lived in villages — *pueblos* in Spanish — the Spanish classed them with the other Pueblo peoples from the sixteenth century onwards. Old Oraibi, one of the four original villages, was founded before A.D. 1100 and is among the oldest continuously inhabited settlements in the United States; in the 1540s it was recorded with 1,500 to 3,000 residents. The Hopi first settled at the foot of the mesas and moved to the mesa tops during the seventeenth century for protection from the Utes, Apaches and Spanish. Photographs in the British Museum show multi-level adobe with wooden ladders connecting one roof level to the next. Franciscan missionaries built a church at Awatovi from 1629; in the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 the churches and mission buildings were dismantled stone by stone, and the smaller church begun in 1700 fell with the village itself in the winter of 1700–1701.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Hopi earthenware in these collections is painted in browns, yellows, reds and black, sometimes over a red or burnished cream-orange slip. Forms include jars with rounded bodies and flaring rims, a bowl with a flat base and a ring of conical knobs below the rim, small shallow plates and a short-handled clay ladle. Painted schemes run from bands of triangles, scrolls and hooked or horned elements to bird wings in geometric surrounds, rain symbols, and figural subjects such as the face of Ha-har-wug-ti. Several pieces carry curatorial identifications from a 2009 research visit by Kelley Hays-Gilpin — "Polacca Polychrome," dated to the 1800s or 1850–1910, with notes suggesting one was children's work and another made for sale. One polychrome jar reached Stockholm as a Peabody Museum loan to the 1892–1893 Madrid exhibition and then as a gift. Metalwork is silver (below); household craft is otherwise basketry — winnowing sifters, wicker trays and coiled plaques.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Hopi silver in the Gothenburg collection was bought new in 1969 from an Indian crafts shop in Albuquerque, New Mexico, and the original dealer labels name the makers and their villages: a round brooch labelled "Maise Mesa Design" by Eldon James of Second Mesa, carrying a Bear Clan symbol; a brooch of wind and direction symbols by Wayne Seyesvapetewa of Second Mesa; a brooch in the form of a hand incised with a snake, labelled a pictograph symbol, by Mark Loma of Second Mesa; and a silver bracelet decorated with moon symbols. A further silver brooch in the form of a bird came as a gift from Stockholm. The design and production of fine jewelry, especially in sterling silver, is one of the crafts by which Hopi people earn a living. Dance ornament is documented photographically: a man in dance costume wearing necklaces and rattles, and men in the Desert View Watchtower wearing traditional ornament.

## Music & performance

Pueblo music — that of the Hopi, Zuni, Taos Pueblo, San Ildefonso, Santo Domingo and other Puebloan peoples — is, in Bruno Nettl's assessment, one of the most complex Native American musical styles on the continent, and Hopi and Zuni music the most complex within the Pueblo area, with Tanoan and Keresan music simpler and intermediate between the Plains and the western Pueblos. Its characteristics are hexatonic and heptatonic scales, variety of form and melodic contour, percussive accompaniment, a melodic range averaging between an octave and a twelfth, and rhythmic complexity equal to that of the Plains. Nettl singles out the Kachina dance songs as the most complex songs of the area. Work songs, otherwise largely unknown in Native American folk music, are found among the Pueblos. The instruments held by the Metropolitan Museum of Art are two drums — one of wood, probably aspen, with skin, the other of metal and skin — a bell of Rocky Mountain sheep horn with rawhide and stone, and a gourd-and-wood instrument catalogued as *Ah-Yah*, all nineteenth century. A Náprstek Museum photograph shows a dancer wearing rattles. The Hopi say they first heard singing and dancing coming from the San Francisco Peaks during a great drought, and on investigating met the katsinam, who returned with them to their villages.

## Dance & theatre

The most widely publicized of Hopi katsina rites is the **Snake Dance**, an annual event in which the performers danced while handling live snakes; a Náprstek Museum photograph shows a priest of the Antelope clan in dance costume before the ceremony. The nine-day **Niman** or Going Home ceremony closes the katsina season with an outdoor katsina dance in which the line of katsinam bring harvest gifts for the spectators and katsina dolls for the young girls; different sets of katsinam perform each year, the Hemis group being the most favoured, accompanied by a variety of katsina *manas*. Katsina masks and dancers appear in rock art from around 1325 CE. The watercolours signed "K. Tavahoema, Hopi" in Stockholm are a gallery of dancer types — a Red Lizard dancer, a Snake dancer, a Corn dancer, a Beard dancer and a Bug dancer. British Museum postcards record Hopi men performing a dance at the Grand Canyon with tourists looking on. Clowning is a recognised idiom: in 1656 a young Hopi man, Juan Suñi, impersonated the resident priest at Awatovi in what was believed to be the spirit of Hopi clowning, and carved figures of the Mud Head Clown are held in the British Museum.

## Festivals & rituals

The Hopi practise a complete cycle of traditional ceremonies, though not every village retains or ever had the full cycle; the ceremonies follow the lunar calendar and are observed in each village. The katsina season occupies half the year: the Hopi hold that for six months the katsina spirits live in the Hopi villages, and that after the Going Home Dance in late July or early August they return to the San Francisco Peaks, north of Flagstaff, for the other six. These dances are held to be vital to the continued harmony and balance of the world, and to bring rain to a parched homeland. Life-cycle ritual centres on naming. A child is born into its mother's clan and named by the women of the father's clan: on the twentieth day of a baby's life the paternal clanswomen gather, each bringing a name and a gift, so that a child with many relatives might receive over forty names, from which the parents — by some accounts the village members collectively — settle on the one used. The naming follows the child's introduction to the Sun, and it remains traditional for Hopi mothers to seek a blessing from the sun for a newborn. A person may take a new name again on initiation into a religious society such as the katsina society, or at a major life event. The Hopi observe their ceremonies for the benefit of the entire world, and the village leader, the *kikmongwi*, promoted civic virtue and proper behaviour.

## Foodways

Maize is the staple and the centre of Hopi life: "for traditional Hopis, corn is the central bond. Its essence, physically, spiritually, and symbolically, pervades their existence. For the people of the mesas corn is sustenance, ceremonial object, prayer offering, symbol, and sentient being unto itself. Corn is the Mother in the truest sense that people take in the corn and the corn becomes their flesh, as mother milk becomes the flesh of the child." The Hopi are traditionally micro- or subsistence farmers; agriculture is central to both their way of life and their economy, and they understand themselves as caretakers of land inherited from their ancestors. The katsinam, on first coming to the villages, taught them various forms of agriculture. Photographs show a man hoeing a corn field. Corn processing leaves its mark on the craft record: twilled yucca winnowing and sifting baskets from Second Mesa, and plaited trays. For drink, the Hopi collect and dry a native perennial plant, *Thelesperma megapotamicum*, known as Hopi tea, and use it to make a herbal tea, as a medicinal remedy and as a yellow dye. The tribally owned Hopi Cultural Center on Second Mesa includes a restaurant serving Hopi dishes; the sources do not name them.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Hopi maintain a complex religious and mythological tradition reaching back centuries, and it is not told consistently: each mesa, and sometimes each village, may have its own version of a story, though "in essence the variants of the Hopi myth bear marked similarity to one another." The folklorist Harold Courlander notes "a Hopi reticence about discussing matters that could be considered ritual secrets or religion-oriented traditions," and it is unclear whether what was told to anthropologists represents genuine belief or stories offered to the curious while the more sacred teachings were kept safe. The central narrative is the passage through four worlds. Tawa, the sun spirit, formed the First World out of *Tokpella*, endless space; Spider Grandmother led its creatures up to the Second and then the Third World, where they became people, and taught them weaving and pottery while a hummingbird brought them a fire drill. Two versions of the emergence into the present Fourth World circulate: one in which a hollow reed grew at the opening called *sipapu*, traditionally identified with the Grand Canyon, and those with good hearts passed through; and another, told mainly at Oraibi, in which Tawa destroyed the Third World by flood and Spider Grandmother sealed the righteous into reed boats, which sailed east by island stepping-stones to the mountainous coasts of the Fourth World. Courlander observes that at Oraibi small children hear the *sipapu* story and the ocean voyage only when older, and that the name of the Hopi Water Clan, *Patkinyamu*, means "a dwelling-on-water" or "houseboat." Then follow the migration narratives: clans named for an event or sign received on the journey, wandering to the farthest extremity of the land in every direction, leaving their symbols on the rocks, joining and splitting again in alternating periods of harmony and contention.

## Language & religion

*Hopílavayi* is a Uto-Aztecan language with four varieties — First Mesa, Mishongnovi, Shipaulovi and Third Mesa — distinguished by Benjamin Whorf, who judged Mishongnovi the most archaic and phonemically complex. It is written in the Latin alphabet, and a handwritten English–Hopi dictionary of 1860 in the Deseret alphabet was rediscovered in 2014. In 1990 more than 5,000 people were estimated to speak Hopi natively, only 40 of them monolingual; a 1998 survey of 200 people found all elders fluent but only 5% of children aged 2–19. Revitalisation runs through the radio station KUYI 88.1 FM (on air from December 2000), the Hopi Literacy Project, the dictionary edited by Emory Sekaquaptewa, Mesa Media (founded 2004), and immersion programmes. Religion centres on Tawa, on Masauwu — Skeleton Man, Spirit of Death, Earth God and Keeper of Fire, who gave the Hopi their land and four sacred tablets — and on the katsina society. Christianity has had missionary presence since the Franciscans of 1629 and a Latter-day Saint church on Hopi land from 1875, but few have converted far enough to drop traditional practice.
