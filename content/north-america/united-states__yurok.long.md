## Overview

The Yurok are an Indigenous people of California whose territory follows the Klamath River from Trinidad, south of the river’s mouth, almost to Crescent City along the north coast. They live primarily within the exterior boundaries of the Yurok Reservation and in surrounding communities in Humboldt, Del Norte and Trinity counties. The reservation comprises some 56,000 acres of contiguous land, although only about 5,000 acres of scattered plots are under partial tribal ownership. The Yurok language is an Algic language. The people call themselves *Oohl*, meaning “the people,” and also use names connected with villages, the coast and the river. Fishing, hunting, gathering, basket weaving and woodcarving remain important, while the Jump Dance, Brush Dance and World Renewal Ceremony continue as ceremonial practices.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Yurok basketry** — Museum records describe basketry made with a twined technique from bear grass, spruce or willow roots, willow or hazel sticks, and maidenhair fern. The catalogue records include bowl-shaped vessels, basket lids, cooking baskets, bottles, vases, trays and mats.

**Ceremonial cap** — A basketry ceremonial cap used plant materials including bear grass, spruce or willow roots, willow or hazel sticks, and maidenhair fern. The recorded technique is twining, and one cap has an obsidian-blade design with lightning.

**Dance hat** — Dance hats were made from wood, coniferous root, maidenhair fern and bear grass. The museum records do not provide a Yurok vernacular name for this form.

**Hide-fringed regalia** — A catalogue record describes hide fringed with grass in the manner of quillwork and identifies it as Hupa ceremonial dance regalia. The supplied sources do not establish a specifically Yurok name or use for the object.

**Basketry medallion** — Several medallions were made from willow, coniferous root, maidenhair fern, woodwardia fern, bear grass and dyed quill.

**Korgfat** — A Museum of World Culture record identifies a *Korgfat*, or basket bowl, associated with Hupa, Yurok and Karok people of northern California. The record states that the three groups share much in their cultures.

**Penningbörs** — A Museum of World Culture record describes a Yurok purse made of bone. No further construction or use is given.

**Motif vocabulary.** Obsidian blade with lightning, flying geese design, smoke house ladder/stacked wood designs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe ceremonial clothing rather than a complete account of everyday dress. At tribal ceremonies, dancers wore elaborate clothing, and the peyerk supplied special clothing for dancers. Museum records include hide-fringed regalia bound with grass in the manner of quillwork, although that particular catalogue entry identifies the object as Hupa ceremonial dance regalia. Sacred artifacts returned to the Yurok included headdresses and deerskins; the regalia was intended for Yurok ceremonies and display at the tribe’s cultural center. The supplied sources do not provide Yurok vernacular names for garments, footwear, belts, hairstyles or ordinary daily clothing.

### Architecture

Yurok villages, or *ʼoʼloolekw*, consisted of individual families living in separate, single-family homes. Villages also contained sweat houses and menstrual huts. Sweat houses were places where men of an extended patrilineal family gathered, while women stayed in separate underground huts for ten days during menstruation. A traditional hamlet of wooden plank buildings called Sumeg was built in 1990. Plans for ʼO Rew include traditional redwood plank houses, a sweat house, and a visitor and cultural center. The Yurok see redwoods as living beings and used only fallen trees to build homes and canoes. The sources do not describe roof forms or further architectural decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources do not describe Yurok ceramics or metalworking. They document wooden, root, fern and grass household objects, including bowls, cooking baskets, bottles, basket lids, vases, berry-washing trays, mats and scoops. A British Museum record describes a bowl-shaped basket with white and black step-pattern ornament. Other objects use willow, hazel, coniferous root, maidenhair fern, woodwardia fern and bear grass. The museum records also include a glass bottle with a wooden lid and cover, and a bone purse. A Museum of World Culture record lists eight stone-tipped spears associated with the Yurok and gives lengths of 75, 77 and 80 cm for several examples.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The major currency of the Yurok nations was the dentalium shell, called *terkwterm*. Museum records include a necklace with a medallion made from shell, skin, grass, beads, twine, seeds and basketry. They also list earrings made of basketry, skin and metal, including one unfinished basketry earring. Several wooden medallions incorporate coniferous root, maidenhair or woodwardia fern, bear grass and dyed quill. The sources do not document tattooing, henna, hair practices or a Yurok name for a particular jewelry type.

## Music & performance

Songs and dances are central to Yurok ceremonial life. Every year, the Yurok came together for the World Renewal Ceremony, when songs and dances passed through many generations were performed. Dancers wore elaborate clothing for the occasion. The Jump Dance and Brush Dance remain part of tribal ceremonies. The sources do not name musical instruments or provide separate genres for secular entertainment, court performance, weddings or funerals.

Singing also contributes to language transmission. The Yurok language is passed on through master-apprentice teams and through singing. Language classes have been offered through Cal Poly Humboldt and through annual language immersion camps. The Yurok Language Project at the University of California, Berkeley, created an online searchable dictionary, an audio dictionary and a database of compiled texts. The project began in 2001, and Professor Andrew Garrett and Dr. Juliette Blevins collaborated with tribal elders on a Yurok dictionary.

## Dance & theatre

The Jump Dance and Brush Dance remain Yurok tribal ceremonies. The World Renewal Ceremony brings together songs and dances that have been passed down through many generations. Peyerk from many villages also came together to host tribal ceremonies; at these events, the peyerk supplied food and shelter and provided special clothing for dancers. The sources do not describe dramatic theatre, mask traditions, shadow puppetry or dances intended specifically as entertainment.

## Festivals & rituals

The World Renewal Ceremony was held every year and included songs and dances performed by the Yurok. The sources do not give a date or season for it. The Jump Dance and Brush Dance remain parts of Yurok ceremonies, but the supplied sources do not explain their annual calendar, sequence or ritual purpose.

Yurok medicine people were usually women. Women became shamans after dreaming that they had been told to do so, and another shaman assisted them in a ritual dance. Shamans used plants, prayer and rituals to heal people and performed ceremonies intended to ensure successful hunting, fishing and gathering. Yurok cosmology has been described as a “polytheistic monism,” in which various gods and spirits are facets of the Great Spirit. “Evil” is described as resulting from imbalance rather than from evil spirits like Satan.

The sources also document the repatriation of 217 sacred artifacts to the Yurok tribe by the Smithsonian Institution in 2010. Condor feathers, headdresses and deerskins had been in the Smithsonian’s collection for almost 100 years. The regalia was to be used in Yurok ceremonies and displayed at the tribe’s cultural center. The sources do not cover Yurok birth, coming-of-age, wedding or funeral rites.

## Foodways

Salmon is described as the vital source of food and nutrients for the Yurok and as foundational to Yurok identity. Traditionally, the Yurok fished for salmon along rivers, gathered ocean fish and shellfish, hunted game and gathered plants. They ate varied berries and meats. Whale meat was prized, although Yuroks did not hunt whales; they waited for a drift whale to wash up on the beach or near the water and dried the flesh.

Fish censuses from the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries suggested that 650,000 to 1 million adult salmon made the run from the mouth of the river to Upper Klamath Lake and beyond. More than 100,000 spring-run Chinook also returned each year. By August 2023, however, the Yurok salmon festival lacked salmon because of scarcity. The Yurok Fisheries Department works to revive streams and salmon and steelhead trout runs, while reforestation helps stabilize waterway banks and reduce sediment.

The sources do not provide named dishes, bread or grain traditions, cooking recipes, tea or coffee practices, dietary rules, or a detailed ceremonial food calendar. Peyerk supplied food and shelter during tribal ceremonies.

## Oral tradition & literature

Yurok oral tradition and archaeological records are used to estimate that Yurok lands originally covered some half-million acres. The people’s traditions also describe dentalium shells as living at the downstream and upstream ends of the world, where strange but enviable peoples live who suck the flesh of univalves.

The sources do not identify a Yurok epic, folktale cycle, proverb or riddle tradition. They do document contemporary language preservation. The Yurok Language Project created a searchable dictionary, an audio dictionary and a database of compiled texts. Archie Thompson, the last known native and active speaker, died on March 26, 2013, after helping to record and teach the language. Some books have been written partly in Yurok, including the graphic novel *Soldiers Unknown*, written by Chag Lowry, translated by Yurok language teacher James Gensaw and illustrated by Rahsan Ekedal.

## Language & religion

Yurok, also called Chillula, Mita, Pekwan, Rikwa, Sugon, Weitspek and Weitspekan, is an Algic language traditionally spoken by the Yurok people of Del Norte County and Humboldt County on the far north coast of California. Yurok is related in the source material to Wiyot as one of two Algic languages spoken in California. The traditional name *Puliklah* is explained as deriving from *pulik*, “downstream,” and *-la*, “people of.” The language is written in the New Yurok Alphabet using Latin characters; previously, Yurok was written in the Yurok Unifon.

The language has undergone a successful revitalization effort. Classes have been taught in schools, and instruction uses master-apprentice teams. The sources give differing speaker counts: one account says between twenty and one hundred people speak Yurok today, while another records more than 300 basic speakers in February 2013. Yurok spirituality includes the Great Spirit, gods and spirits, shamanic healing, prayer, ritual dances and ceremonies for successful hunting, fishing and gathering.
