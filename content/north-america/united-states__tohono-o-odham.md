## Overview

The Tohono O'odham are a Native American people of the Sonoran Desert, living primarily in southern Arizona and the northern Mexican state of Sonora. Their historical territory extended across much of the Sonoran Desert, from the Gila River and Colorado River to the Gulf of California and the San Miguel River valley. The Tohono O'odham Nation governs a reservation in Pima, Pinal, and Maricopa Counties, and the reservation extends into Sonora. The people call themselves Tohono O'odham, meaning “desert people.” Their language, Oʼodham, is a Uto-Aztecan language shared in related forms with the Akimel Oʼodham. In 2000, approximately 9,750 speakers were estimated in the United States and Mexico. Their folk culture is closely connected with seasonal movement, rain-fed agriculture, desert foodways, basketry, pottery, music, dance, oral tradition, and communal life.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Basketry** — Women wove baskets.

**Pottery** — Women made pottery, including *ollas*.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile or decorative motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources give little information about everyday clothing. In musical and dance activities, O'odham people wore muted white clay, and dancing took place in bare feet on dry dirt. Women gathered food, wove baskets, and made pottery, while men hunted and performed many farming tasks, but these roles are not connected in the sources with distinctive clothing.

### Architecture

The Tohono O'odham moved between summer and winter homes, usually following water. Summer homes were built along alluvial plains near fields where summer rains were channeled for cultivation. Winter villages were built in the mountains, where water was more reliable and men could hunt. Some dikes and catchment basins were constructed, but most streams were not dependable enough for permanent canal systems. Mission San Xavier del Bac, near Tucson, is a major building associated with O'odham history: both its first and current church buildings were constructed by O'odham, while the second building was constructed under Franciscan direction between 1783 and 1797.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources identify pottery such as *ollas*, baskets, overturned baskets used as drums, hard wood rasps, and long harvesting poles made from paired saguaro ribs. A *kuibit* was made by bundling a pair of saguaro ribs, each about 6 m long, for harvesting saguaro fruit. They also mention blankets, mats, shells, beads, and trinkets as objects used in gambling and exchange.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Shells and beads appear as valuables wagered in games, but their use as personal ornaments is not described.

## Music & performance

O'odham musical activity does not use the “grand ritual paraphernalia” associated with pow-wows or other large ceremonies. Songs are accompanied by hard wood rasps and by drumming on overturned baskets. The sources emphasize that these sounds lack resonance and are “swallowed by the desert floor.” Dancing accompanies this music and consists of quiet skipping and shuffling in bare feet on dry dirt. The dust raised by dancing is believed to help form rain clouds.

Saguaro harvest celebrations included storytelling, dancing, and singing. After the harvest, each family contributed a small amount of saguaro syrup to a communal stock, which the medicine man fermented. The resulting ceremonial wine was consumed by the men, and the intoxicated state was regarded as holy; dreams experienced then were considered portentous. The sources state that this was the only time the O'odham drank alcohol.

The Oʼodham language article gives examples of songs and narrative performance. A song in *Oʼodham Hohoʼok Aʼagida* is presented in the “Storyteller dialect,” while the *O'odham Piipaash Language Program* includes a text called *Taḏai* (“Roadrunner”) in the Salt River dialect.

## Dance & theatre

O'odham dancing is described as quiet skipping and shuffling in bare feet on dry dirt. It forms part of musical activities and saguaro harvest celebrations, where dancing accompanied songs and storytelling. Raised dust was believed to assist in forming rain clouds. They also do not distinguish a separate entertainment dance from ceremonial dancing.

## Festivals & rituals

Saguaro harvest celebrations took place during the harvest, which began in June. Villages traveled to saguaro stands for the duration of the harvest. The fruit was boiled into thick syrup, and each family contributed a small quantity to a communal supply. The medicine man fermented this stock into ceremonial wine. Stories were told, songs were sung, and there was much dancing. The wine was consumed by the men, and the resulting intoxication was treated as holy.

Rain was also connected with agricultural observation and ritual. The method called *Ak cin*, meaning “mouth of the wash,” involved monitoring storm clouds. When clouds appeared, farmers quickly prepared their plantations for seeding as rain began. This form of agriculture was especially associated with summer monsoons. The sources also state that the dust raised during dance was believed to help form rain clouds.

The sources describe communal gatherings for races, trade, socialization, and gossip. Daylong races were popular, and courses were 10–15 mi (16–24 km). Gambling was common: men played a stick game similar to dice, while women played a game involving painted sticks. Women also played *toka*, a field hockey-like game that remains a school sport on the modern reservation.

## Foodways

The original O'odham diet included wild game, insects, and regional plants. Food plants included ironwood seed, honey mesquite, hog potato, organ-pipe cactus fruit, cholla cactus, and acorns. Cultivated foods included white tepary beans, peas, Spanish watermelons, corn, and squash. People hunted pronghorn antelope, gathered hornworm larvae, and trapped pack rats. Traditional preparation included steaming plants in pits and roasting meat over open fires.

Saguaro cactus fruit was especially important. Long sticks were used to harvest the fruit, which was made into jams, syrups, and ceremonial wine. Its edible seeds were used for porridge. Fresh fruit was boiled for several hours into a thick syrup because it did not keep for long. Four kilograms of fruit yielded about 1 liter of syrup. In 1929, one harvest produced 45,000 kg among 600 families.

Rain-fed farming was practiced in basins that held monsoon water. Traditional foods combined goods provided by nature with cultivated crops.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that O'odham belonging was shaped by shared traditions, ways of life, language, related legends, and experiences of surviving in the desert. They also describe O'odham oral tradition concerning relations with the Apache, including a different account of intermarriage from that found in European records. Stories were told during saguaro harvest celebrations.

The language article presents *Oʼodham Hohoʼok Aʼagida* (“Oʼodham Legends and Lore”) by Susanne Ignacio Enos, Dean Saxton, and Lucille Saxton. It includes a song in the “Storyteller dialect,” with an English translation about a little gray horned toad youth visiting a girl repeatedly. The *O'odham Piipaash Language Program* includes *Taḏai* (“Roadrunner”), a text exemplifying the Salt River dialect.

Materials collected by a Franciscan friar are held in the Santa Barbara Mission Archive-Library. The Office of Ethnohistorical Research at the Arizona State Museum has produced a documentary history containing translated colonial documents about Spanish relations with the O'odham in the 17th and 18th centuries.

## Language & religion

Oʼodham is a Uto-Aztecan language spoken in southern Arizona and northern Sonora, Mexico. Native names for the language include *Oʼodham ha-ñeʼokĭ*, *Oʼottham ha-neoki*, and *Oʼodham ñiok*. It has Tohono Oʼodham and Akimel Oʼodham dialect groupings, as well as northern and southern differences. The Hia C-eḍ dialect is described as especially distinct. Two commonly used orthographies are Alvarez–Hale and Saxton; Alvarez–Hale is officially used by the Tohono Oʼodham Nation.

The sources describe resistance to Spanish religious change and state that Tohono O'odham villages resisted Catholic conversion for hundreds of years. They also describe medicine men, rainmaking celebrations, ceremonial wine, and dreams regarded as portentous.
