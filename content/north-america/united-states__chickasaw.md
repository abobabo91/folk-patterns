## Overview

The Chickasaw are an Indigenous people of the Southeastern Woodlands of the United States. Their traditional territory included northern Mississippi, northwestern and northern Alabama, western Tennessee, and southwestern Kentucky. Today, most descendants live in Oklahoma, and the federally recognized Chickasaw Nation is headquartered in Ada, Oklahoma. The Chickasaw language belongs to the Muskogean language family and is centered on Southeast Oklahoma. Chickasaw history includes migration from west of the Mississippi River, interaction with French, English, and Spanish colonists, the Treaty of Pontotoc Creek, and removal to Indian Territory during the 1830s. Their folk culture is documented through kinship, oral history, language revitalization, the Green Corn Ceremony, village architecture, cooking, art, storytelling, stomp dances, and stickball.

### Architecture

Chickasaw villages included a Council House, winter and summer houses, a replica mound, a corn crib, and a stickball field at the Chikasha Inchokka' Traditional Village of the Chickasaw Cultural Center in Sulphur, Oklahoma. The sources also describe a main house and main meeting ground as places where Chickasaw communities gathered for ceremonies, celebratory affairs, and important social, cultural, and political matters. The Chickasaw Cultural Center opened in 2010 and also includes the Honor Garden and Sky and Water pavilion.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources identify pottery as part of Green Corn Ceremony observance: villages were cleaned, old pottery was broken, and most old fires were put out. The Chikasha Inchokka' Traditional Village includes a corn crib, while the Chickasaw Cultural Center presents exhibits concerning Chickasaw art and cooking.

## Music & performance

The sources describe Chickasaw performance through stomp dances and stickball demonstrations held at the Chickasaw Cultural Center. Cultural performers display traditional Chickasaw culture through art, cooking, language, and storytelling. The sources also identify the Green Corn Ceremony as an annual ceremony connected with the corn crop and the community of the tribe. It begins when the corn crops begin to develop, usually in late June or early July, and Chickasaw participants fast from the afternoon of the first day until the second sunrise.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name stomp dances as a Chickasaw cultural performance and mention stickball demonstrations at the Chickasaw Cultural Center. The Chikasha Inchokka' Traditional Village includes a stickball field, and cultural performers display traditional Chickasaw culture there.

## Festivals & rituals

The **Green Corn Ceremony** occurs annually and begins when corn crops begin to develop, usually in late June or early July. It connects corn with Chickasaw culture, celebrates the crop and the sense of community, and represents a time of starting from scratch. Villages were cleaned, old pottery was broken, and most old fires were put out. Fasting was practiced by many tribes for purity; Chickasaw participants fasted from the afternoon of the first day of the ceremony until the second sunrise.

The sources also describe Chickasaw creation traditions. One account says that the Chickasaw and Choctaw arose at Nanih Waiya, a great earthwork mound built about 300 CE by Woodland peoples. Another account, relayed by the Choctaws to Bernard Romans, describes emergence from a hole in the ground between the two nations.

## Foodways

The sources identify corn as one of the most important foods for many tribes in the region and connect it directly with Chickasaw culture through the Green Corn Ceremony. The ceremony begins as the corn crops develop and celebrates both the crop and the community. Chickasaw participants fast during part of the ceremony to obtain purity.

The sources also state that the Chickasaw traditionally hunted for food and that men prepared provisions for the defense of their communities, while women cared for crops, children, and estate matters. The Chickasaw Cultural Center presents Chickasaw cooking as part of its cultural interpretation, but the catalogue material supplied here does not identify particular foods.

## Oral tradition & literature

Chickasaw oral history records migration from a land west of the Mississippi River into present-day Mississippi and neighboring regions. The Chickasaw and Choctaw were once one people and separated during migration. The Mississippi River is called *Sakti Lhafa' Okhina* in Chikashanompaꞌ, meaning “scored bluff waterway,” and Chickasaw culture associates the river with the foundation of Chickasaw identity and a new beginning.

The sources describe a Chickasaw creation story involving Nanih Waiya and also record a creation account transmitted by the Choctaws. Storytelling is presented as part of contemporary cultural performance at the Chickasaw Cultural Center. The Chickasaw Press publishes Chickasaw language literature, and language revitalization includes master-apprentice programs, community programs, self-study programs, immersion camps, and language classes.

## Language & religion

Chickasaw, or *Chikashshanompaꞌ*, is a Native American language of the Muskogean family. It is closely related to Choctaw, follows subject–object–verb word order, and is agglutinative. The language is spoken by Chickasaw people in Southeast Oklahoma, centered on Ada. It became endangered after removal from the homeland and the effects of boarding schools. The sources state that it is currently spoken by around 50 people, mostly Chickasaw elders, and that UNESCO lists it as a “severely endangered” language.

Chickasaw has been taught through the Chickasaw Language Revitalization Program, founded in 2007, as well as community classes, master-apprentice programs, immersion camps, and self-study. The sources discuss Christianity as a practice adopted by some Chickasaw during the nineteenth century.
