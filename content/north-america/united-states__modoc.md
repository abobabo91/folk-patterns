## Overview

The Modoc homeland included parts of northeastern California and southern Oregon. Their traditional life included seasonal hunting and gathering, salmon fishing, basketry, and winter lodges near lakes. Ritual life included the five-night Shuyuhalsh dance rite and sweat-lodge ceremonies. Oral traditions describe Kumush, the spirit of creation.

## Material culture

Modoc material culture documented here centers on basketry, earthen winter lodges, fishing, and foods from aquatic plants.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Basketry is the main named Modoc textile tradition in the sources.

- **Basketry** (*Korgflätning*) — Modoc caps, women’s caps, headgear, baskets, and a basket plate were made of basketry.
- **Wokas plants** (*wokas*) — Winter lodges stood near lake shores where aquatic wokas plants provided reliable seed sources.
- **Earthen dugout lodges** — Winter lodges were beehive-shaped, covered with sticks, and plastered with mud.

### Clothing & dress

The sources show basketry headgear and later clothing such as hats, shirts, suits, trousers, and boots.

- **Captain Jack’s basketry cap** — One catalogue record identifies a basketry cap as belonging to Captain Jack.
- **Modoc men’s clothing** — Photographs show Modoc men wearing hats, shirts, jackets, trousers, boots, suits, and bow ties.

### Architecture

Modoc winter homes were earthen dugout lodges built near lakes.

- **Earthen dugout lodge** — Until the 19th century, the Modoc built beehive-shaped lodges covered with sticks and plastered with mud.
- **Lost River village** — Kintpuash’s band built a Modoc village near the Lost River after leaving the reservation in 1870.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The documented everyday objects are mainly baskets and basketry items.

- **Basket** — Museum records identify a Modoc basket.
- **Unfinished basket** — Museum records identify an unfinished basket.
- **Game basket plate** — A basketry plate was used in games.
- **Basketry headgear** — Museum records identify open, basket-like basketry headgear.
- **Basketry caps** — Museum records identify basketry caps, including a women’s cap.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources name necklaces made from glass beads, mussel shells, and fruits.

- **Glass-bead and mussel-shell necklace** — A necklace made of glass beads and mussel shells belonged to Hi-ne-ma Riaala.
- **Fruit necklace** — Museum records identify another necklace made of fruits.

## Music & performance

The sources record a five-night dance rite but do not describe Modoc instruments or songs.

- **Shuyuhalsh** — The Shuyuhalsh was a five-night dance rite of passage for adolescent girls.
- **Sweat-lodge ceremony** — A sweat lodge was used for purification and mourning ceremonies.

## Dance & theatre

The named Modoc dance tradition is the five-night Shuyuhalsh rite.

- **Shuyuhalsh dance rite** (*Shuyuhalsh*) — The Shuyuhalsh was a five-night rite of passage for adolescent girls.

## Festivals & rituals

Modoc ritual life included the Shuyuhalsh and sweat-lodge ceremonies, while the Modoc War is recorded as history rather than an annual festival.

- **Shuyuhalsh** — The Shuyuhalsh was a five-night dance rite of passage for adolescent girls.
- **Sweat-lodge purification** — A sweat lodge was used for purification and mourning.
- **Modoc War** — During the Modoc War, Kintpuash and his band resisted removal from their homelands.

## Foodways

Modoc foodways included salmon fishing, seasonal hunting and gathering, and seeds from aquatic wokas plants.

- **Salmon fishing** (*salmon*) — Before the 19th century, the Modoc caught salmon during salmon runs.
- **Wokas seeds** (*wokas*) — Winter lodges were built near reliable sources of seeds from aquatic wokas plants.
- **Seasonal hunting and gathering** — The Modoc migrated seasonally to hunt and gather other food.

## Oral tradition & literature

Modoc creation traditions center on Kumush, who created the people and named important places.

- **Kumush creation story** (*Kumush*) — Kumush created the Modoc tribe, gave things names, and designated California as their home.
- **Modoc Garden of Eden** — Kumush named Mount Shasta and Tule Lake as the Modoc Garden of Eden.
- **Myths of the Modocs** — Jeremiah Curtin’s 1912 book recorded Modoc myths from late-19th-century fieldwork.
- **The Klamath Indians of Southwestern Oregon** — Albert Gatschet’s 1890 book recorded material about the Klamath Indians.
- **The Book of Spirals** — H. L. Delaney’s 2025 book presents a twelve-part cycle in which Kemush is a culture hero and cosmological mediator.

## Language & religion

The Modoc traditionally spoke Klamath–Modoc, and their recorded religious traditions include Kumush, the Shuyuhalsh, and sweat-lodge ceremonies.

- **Klamath–Modoc language** (*Klamath–Modoc*) — The Modoc traditionally spoke a dialect of Klamath, also called Lutuamian.
- **Lutuamian** — Lutuamian is another name for the Klamath–Modoc language.
- **Kumush** — Kumush is identified as the Modoc spirit of creation.
- **Shuyuhalsh** — The Shuyuhalsh was a five-night ritual for adolescent girls.
- **Sweat lodge** — The sweat lodge was used for purification and mourning ceremonies.
