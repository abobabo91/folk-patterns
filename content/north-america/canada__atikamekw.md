## Overview

The Atikamekw are an Indigenous people of Quebec, Canada, and one of the eleven Indigenous nations recognized by the Government of Quebec. Their ancestral territory, *Nitaskinan*, meaning “our land” in the Atikamekw language, lies primarily in the Mauricie and Lanaudière regions and extends mainly along the Saint-Maurice River valley. According to the Atikamekw territorial claim, it covers approximately 80,000 km2 (31,000 sq mi). The Atikamekw population was 8,547 as of 31 December 2025. The nation consists of the three First Nations communities of Manawan, Opitciwan and Wemotaci. Most Atikamekw speak Atikamekw, an Algonquian language closely related to Innu-aimun and other languages of the region. Hunting, fishing, trapping, gathering, forest resources and birch-bark work remain important elements of the documented culture.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Clothing made from tanned animal hides** — Women made clothing from tanned animal hides.

**Netting for snowshoes** — Women processed moose hides into thin, flexible strips used to weave netting for snowshoes.

**Blankets** — Blankets were made as part of the clothing and equipment required for survival.

### Clothing & dress

Women made clothing from tanned animal hides. During the processing of moose hides, women removed the hair, soaked and defleshed the hides, tanned them, and cut them into thin, flexible strips for weaving snowshoe netting. The sources also describe ceremonial robes decorated with bells made of bones emptied of the marrow.

### Architecture

Traditionally, the Atikamekw lived in dome-shaped homes made from branches and covered with bark called *piskokan*. Spruce boughs covered the floor, while furs served as beds and blankets.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Atikamekw made tools from wood and animal parts, including bone and tendon. They made hunting equipment such as bows, snowshoes and sled dog equipment. After the French entered the trading network, the Atikamekw traded furs for European manufactured goods such as metal tools. They were also known for birch-bark baskets and canoes decorated with designs.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They do describe ceremonial robes covered with bells made of bones emptied of the marrow.

## Music & performance

They identify Laura Niquay as a singer-songwriter and Oshim Ottawa as an actor and musician, but provide no further description of their music.

## Festivals & rituals

The Atikamekw recognize six seasons, each associated with a principal activity. *Sîkon* begins in late winter and is a time for making bark baskets and gathering maple sap. *Mirôskamin* corresponds to spring, when the Atikamekw generally fished and hunted for partridge. These activities continued through *Nîpin*, or summer. During *Takwâkin*, autumn, people hunted moose; the hunt involved removing the skin, making offerings, and processing the meat by smoking and drying. During the onset of winter, *Pîtcipipôn*, men trapped beaver. During *Pipôn*, winter, men made nets for fishing under the ice and others produced snowshoes.

The year was also divided into twelve months, whose names referred to activities or observations of nature.

## Foodways

Traditionally, the Atikamekw relied on hunting, fishing, trapping, gathering and forest resources. They supplemented fish, hunted meat and trapped animals with agricultural products made and processed by women, including corn and maple syrup. Maple sap was tapped from maple trees and boiled down into syrup. Meat was preserved by smoking and drying, a process still practiced by some families. Women collected berries and processed them into a paste that could be preserved for several weeks.

During the autumn moose hunt, the meat was processed through smoking and drying into moose “jerky.” In winter, people made nets to fish under the ice.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources also identify Chloé Leriche’s films *Before the Streets* (*Avant les rues*) from 2016 and *Atikamekw Suns* (*Soleils Atikamekw*) from 2023, without further discussion of their themes or relation to oral tradition.

## Language & religion

Atikamekw, also known by the endonym *Atikamekw Nehiromowin*, meaning “Atikamekw native language,” is a variety of the Algonquian language Cree. It belongs to the Cree–Montagnais–Naskapi linguistic continuum, within the Central branch of the Algonquian languages of the Algic family. It is spoken by nearly all the Atikamekw and is sometimes classified as a dialect of Cree. The language has three dialects associated with the three prominent communities. Most Atikamekw generally speak the language, but the majority do not write it.
