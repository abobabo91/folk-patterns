## Overview

The Sadlermiut, also called Sagdlirmiut or Sallirmiut, were an Inuit group living in near isolation mainly on and around Coats Island, Walrus Island, and Southampton Island in Hudson Bay. Their name is connected with Sadlerk, now Salliq, the Inuktitut name for the settlement of Coral Harbour, Nunavut. They survived into the early 20th century and maintained a culture and dialect distinct from mainland Inuit communities. The Sadlermiut were hunter-gatherers whose subsistence relied primarily on fishing and caribou hunting, with additional hunting of seals, polar bears, and walruses. Their cultural history is important because it preserves evidence of an isolated Inuit society with distinctive traditions and stone technology associated by some researchers with Dorset culture.

## Material culture

### Architecture

In 1954 and 1955, Henry B. Collins of the Smithsonian Institution studied Inuit house ruins in the Canadian Arctic. He determined that the ruins found at Native Point were characteristic of Sadlermiut culture, which had once been quite extensive.

The sources record that the Sadlermiut maintained local traditions.

## Foodways

The Sadlermiut were hunter-gatherers whose subsistence relied primarily on fishing and caribou hunting. They also hunted seals, polar bears, and walruses. Unlike mainland Inuit, they were reported to show very little interest in hunting whales and trapping.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record that the group maintained a distinctive dialect and that neighbouring Inuit described their speech as “baby talk,”.

## Language & religion

The Sadlermiut language is unknown and appears to have differed significantly from the language of their mainland neighbours. They report that neighbouring Inuit called the Sadlermiut speech “baby talk,” but state that it is unclear whether this meant a distinct variety of Inuit language or pidgin Inuktitut as a contact language.
