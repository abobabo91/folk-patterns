## Overview

The Sekani, also called Tseʼkhene, are a First Nations people of the Athabaskan-speaking ethnolinguistic group in the northern interior of British Columbia. Their territory includes the Finlay and Parsnip River drainages of the Rocky Mountain Trench, with communities associated with Kwadacha, Tsay Keh Dene First Nation, and McLeod Lake. Their neighbours include the Babine, Dakelh, Dunneza, Kaska, Tahltan, and Cree communities. The Sekani language is indigenous to the British Columbia Interior and is considered critically endangered; most speakers are semispeakers, and English predominates in education, government, wage labor, and interactions with outsiders. Traditional life was based on hunting and gathering, with game more important than fish and berries, especially blueberries, forming much of the plant food. The total population was estimated at nearly 700 in the late 1980s.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe funerary practices: the Sekani traditionally cremated their deceased; after cremation was discontinued, they revived a custom of covering the dead person with the brush hut that had sheltered them during their last days and then leaving the locality for a period. Persons of influence were buried in coffins raised on platforms or trees. The sources also state that the Sekani were said to have practiced polyandry before large-scale conversion to Catholicism.

## Foodways

Sekani traditional subsistence was based on hunting and gathering. Fish formed part of the diet, but the Sekani relied more heavily on game than their Carrier and Babine neighbours. Plant food consisted largely of berries, especially blueberries.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record that the language is closely associated with cultural knowledge and traditional ways of life, while many younger Sekani understand some Sekani but have limited fluency because of residential schools, language shift, and changing economic and social structures.

## Language & religion

Sekani or Tse’khene is an indigenous language of the Sekani and belongs to the Athabaskan-speaking ethnolinguistic group. It is spoken primarily along the upper Peace, Parsnip, and Finlay river systems, including areas around Kwadacha, Tsay Keh Dene First Nation, and McLeod Lake. Sekani people call the language [tsekʼene] or [tθekʼene], depending on dialect; appended with Dene, meaning people, this means “people on the rocks.” The language has 33 consonants and two tones, low and high; vowel nasalization is phonemic. The sources state that English predominates in education, government, wage labor, and interactions with outsiders, while Sekani is more frequent among older generations and in family or ceremonial contexts. The sources mention large-scale conversion to Catholicism.
