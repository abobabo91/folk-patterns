## Overview

The Odawa are an Indigenous people whose name means "to trade", a role that defined them as merchants moving goods between nations across the Great Lakes region. One of the Anishinaabeg peoples closely related to the Ojibwe and Potawatomi, they speak a distinctive dialect of Ojibwe marked by vowel deletion and consonant clusters. Today about 15,000 Odawa live across Ontario, Michigan and Oklahoma, with revitalization efforts teaching the language in schools.

## Material culture

Odawa material culture centers on glass beadwork and porcupine quillwork on skin and birchbark, along with woven rush and birchbark containers and canoes.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Beadwork in floral patterns and porcupine quillwork on birchbark are the dominant techniques.

- **Floral glass beadwork** — Beads arranged in floral designs on tanned deer skin and cloth garments like vests, leggings and moccasins.
- **Beaded skin pouches** — Pouches made from whole beaver skins with head, legs and tail, embroidered with white beads on blue serge.
- **Birchbark quillwork** — Mats, baskets and wall pockets made from birchbark with sweetgrass edging, decorated with porcupine quills.
- **Black ash basketry** — Baskets woven from black ash, a practice that continues as a living tradition.
- **Rush matting** — Plain mats woven from rush for floor and wall coverings, part of Odawa trade goods.

### Clothing & dress

Odawa dress ranged from tattooed and painted bodies with raised hair in early times to beaded garments and feather headdresses in later periods.

- **Cheveux relevés** — Hair lifted, raised and rolled up, a distinctive style observed by Champlain in 1615.
- **Beaded tunic and leggings** — Decorated cloth tunics and leggings embroidered with glass beads, worn for display and ceremony.
- **Feather headdress** — Headdresses made of feathers worn with beaded garments for formal occasions.

### Architecture

No sources describe Odawa houses, but their Saugeen predecessors built earthwork mounds for burials.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Bark and wood dominate Odawa everyday objects, with metal and manufactured goods entering through the fur trade.

- **Birch bark canoe** — Model canoe made of birch bark, wood and gum, part of a set with five paddles.
- **Birchbark containers** — Baskets, mats and wall pockets made from birchbark and coiled with sweetgrass.
- **Wiigwaasabak scrolls** (*Wiigwaasabak*) — Birchbark scrolls that preserve the migration history of the Anishinaabeg peoples.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Odawa body ornament included tattooing, face paint, pierced noses and glass beads from European trade.

- **Tattooing** — Designs and fashions tattooed on the body, recorded by Champlain in 1615.
- **Face paint** — Paint applied to faces for body adornment and ceremony.
- **Glass beads** — White glass beads from European trade used to embroider ornaments and garments.

## Festivals & rituals

Odawa religion combines traditional practice with Catholic, Methodist and Anglican affiliation, centered on the sacred narrative of Anishinaabeg migration.

- **Miigis migration** (*miigis*) — Luminescent beings directed the Anishinaabeg peoples inland along the Saint Lawrence River to the Straits of Mackinac.
- **Third Stopping Place** — The location near the Straits of Mackinac where Ojibwe, Odawa and Potawatomi divided from the southern Anishinaabeg.
- **Burial mounds** — Earthwork mounds used for burials by the Saugeen complex, a Hopewell-influenced group that may have developed into the Odawa.

## Foodways

Odawa foodways centered on cornmeal and sunflower oil production, hunting for furs, and European metal tools for processing food.

- **Cornmeal trade** — Cornmeal was a major good traded by the Odawa between nations.
- **Sunflower oil** — Oil pressed from sunflower seeds and traded as a major Odawa commodity.
- **Beaver hunting** — Beaver furs were the most highly prized trade good with Europeans.

## Oral tradition & literature

Odawa narrative tradition was recorded on Wiigwaasabak birch bark scrolls, and later in written texts by Odawa speakers and authors.

- **Wiigwaasabak scrolls** (*Wiigwaasabak*) — Birch bark scrolls recording the migration history and sacred narrative of the Anishinaabeg peoples from the Dawnland.
- **Anishinabe Enamiad** — An Ojibwe newspaper published at Harbor Springs, Michigan, from 1896 to 1902, with contributions from Odawa writers.
- **Andrew Blackbird's history** — A history of the Odawa people written in English with grammar, vocabulary and translations of Christian texts.
- **Ottawa Stories from the Springs** — Modern stories recorded from the Grand Traverse Band of Ottawa and Chippewa Indians, translated by Howard Webkamigada.

## Language & religion

Odawa is a highly changed dialect of Ojibwe marked by vowel deletion, with about 500 speakers in Ontario and Michigan and three in Oklahoma.

- **Nishnaabemwin** — The Odawa name for their language, meaning "speaking the native language".
- **Daawaamwin** — An Odawa name for the language, meaning "speaking Ottawa".
- **Double Vowel orthography** (*Double Vowel*) — Writing system attributed to Charles Fiero, used in the 1985 dictionary and 2001 reference grammar.
- **Vowel syncope** — Pervasive deletion of short vowels and creation of consonant clusters unique to Odawa among Ojibwe dialects.

## Glossary

- *Wiigwaasabak* — Birch bark scrolls preserving migration history
- *miigis* — Luminescent beings who directed the Anishinaabeg migration
- *Nishnaabemwin* — Speaking the native language; Odawa name for their language
- *Daawaamwin* — Speaking Ottawa; another Odawa name for their language
- *cheveux relevés* — Hair lifted, raised, rolled up; French term for Odawa hairstyle
- *Third Stopping Place* — Location near Straits of Mackinac where Anishinaabeg peoples divided
- *Anishinaabeg* — Ojibwe, Odawa and Potawatomi peoples; also spelled Anishinaabe
- *Anishinabe Enamiad* — The Praying Indian; Ojibwe newspaper published at Harbor Springs 1896–1902
- *Dawnland* — Region on the East Coast from which Anishinaabeg peoples originated
- *Double Vowel* — Orthography system for writing Odawa, attributed to Charles Fiero
- *Saugeen complex* — Hopewell-influenced Middle Woodland group on Bruce Peninsula, possible Odawa ancestors
- *Manitoulin Island* — Remembered homeland of the Odawa on the northern side of Lake Huron
- *Bruce Peninsula* — Region in Ontario that was part of Odawa homeland
- *Algonquian* — Language family to which Ojibwe and Odawa belong
