## Overview

The Oneida are an Indigenous people of the Northeastern Woodlands of North America and one of the five founding nations of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy. Their traditional homeland was between the Mohawk people and the Onondaga people, particularly around Oneida Lake and Oneida County in what is now central New York State. Today, Oneida communities are located in New York, Wisconsin, and Ontario. The United States has the Oneida Indian Nation in New York and the Oneida Nation in and around Green Bay, Wisconsin; Canada has the Oneida First Nation at the Six Nations of the Grand River and the Oneida Nation of the Thames in Southwold. Oneida is an Iroquoian language, but only a small number of native speakers remain. Oneida culture includes matrilineal clans, oral narrative, formal oratory, ritual language, seasonal foodways, social dances, and ceremonial practices.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name woodland designs on women’s beaded tiaras.

### Clothing & dress

Before contact with Europeans, Oneida clothing was made from natural materials, including deer and other animal hides. After the arrival of European traders, calico cotton and broadcloth became common, and clothing remained predominantly cotton. Oneida men and women traditionally wore moccasins, called *ahtaˀu·wé*, on their feet. Men wore Haudenosaunee headdresses called *kastoweh*, or *kastówe*, made with feathers and insignia representing their nation. The Oneida insignia consists of three eagle feathers: two standing straight up and one falling downwards. Women wore beaded tiaras sewn with woodland designs.

### Architecture

 They state that sacred dances were performed privately in longhouses, but provide no architectural description of those buildings.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention wampum, strings of symbolic shell beads used in formal speech and diplomacy, and the Oneida Stone as a long-standing symbol of the Nation.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources describe women’s beaded tiaras sewn with woodland designs and men’s feathered *kastoweh* headdresses.

## Music & performance

The sources describe singing as part of ceremonial dancing and state that social dances are accompanied by chanting. Every song has an introduction; when the beat changes, dancing begins. Drums provide cues indicating when dancers should change partners. Ceremonial dances are sacred and are performed privately in longhouses rather than in public.

Oneida oral performance also includes formal oratory and ritual speech. Political and diplomatic processes are highly formalized, and spoken words are reinforced through wampum, strings of symbolic shell beads used in gesturing and as gifts indicating truth or seriousness. The condolence ceremony was a ritual of succession of chiefdom and played a central role in maintaining the Haudenosaunee Confederacy. The Three Bare Words ceremony, described as a metaphorical opening of the eyes, ears, and throat, preceded diplomacy. Religious rituals used prescribed language, including the Onnonhouaroia, or Midwinter, ceremony, and shamanistic medicine ceremonies.

## Dance & theatre

The sources identify two broad types of Oneida dancing: social and ceremonial. Social dancing is for the enjoyment of all people and includes the Round Dance, Rabbit Dance, Old Moccasin Dance, and Canoe or Fishing Dance. Ceremonial dancing is sacred and is not performed in public. The sources name dances associated with the Maple Syrup, Strawberry, Bean, Sun, and Moon ceremonies.

Singing accompanies ceremonial dancing, while social dances use chanting. An introduction precedes each song, and changes in the beat mark the beginning of dancing. Drums cue dancers to change partners. If someone invited to dance refused, etiquette required that person to offer tobacco as a settlement.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe seasonal ceremonies rather than a complete annual festival calendar. The Maple Syrup, Strawberry, Bean, Sun, and Moon ceremonies each include ceremonial dances. The Onnonhouaroia, or Midwinter ceremony, is described as a psychologically oriented celebration of dreams. Religious practice also included shamanistic medicine ceremonies.

The condolence ceremony was a ritual of succession of chiefdom and was important to the maintenance of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy. The Three Bare Words ceremony preceded diplomacy and was described as a metaphorical opening of the eyes, ears, and throat. The sources also state that individuals were identified through a spirit name, clan, and family unit within a clan. The people have a matrilineal kinship system: children belong to their mother’s clan, and descent and inheritance pass through that line. They state that the mother’s eldest brother supervised boys’ passage into adulthood as men.

## Foodways

Oneida foodways followed the seasonal availability of the land, described as “eating the seasons.” During autumn, fruits and vegetables were dried, while meat was preserved in brine or salt and hung to dry. Fall foods included deer, geese, duck, and raccoon. Nuts included hickory nuts, black walnuts, butternuts, and chestnuts. Wild rice was dried and provided complex carbohydrates.

In spring, wild onions, leeks, milkweeds, and dandelions were boiled and eaten. Fishing began in spring, with trout, bullhead, walleye, pike, bass, and salmon among the fish consumed. Maple sap was collected and boiled to make maple syrup and hard candy. The candy could be eaten immediately or saved to flavor foods.

During summer, the Oneidas consumed strawberries, blackberries, raspberries, blueberries, pears, plums, peaches, apples, and grapes. Sassafras was also used for tea.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral narrative has a central place in Oneida culture. Stories transmit social norms and cultural knowledge through myths, folktales, and legends. The Haudenosaunee distinguish narratives described as “things which truly happened,” stories in which “it is as if an animal walked,” and accounts in which “they went to the woods to hunt for meat.” These correspond to supernatural mythology, often anthropomorphic animal stories, and parables or fables of human deeds.

The Oneida have an extensive tradition of formal oratory and ritual speech. The Oneida language is described as a vital link to ancestors and national identity, even though it is no longer the primary language of communication for most Oneida. The Creation Story, compiled from narratives by Demus Elm and Harvey Antone, is identified as the only book written in Oneida in major publication. Elm and Antone’s Creation Story was published in 2000, and Anthony Wonderley’s collection of stories appeared in 2004.

Language documentation includes stories, hymns, prayers, videos, songs, dictionaries, grammar information, and texts. The Folklore Project, led by Morris Swadesh and later by Floyd Lounsbury, worked to preserve and expand the Oneida language. It began in 1936, was formally started in January 1939, and lasted nineteen months.

## Language & religion

Oneida is an Iroquoian language spoken primarily by the Oneida people in New York, Wisconsin, and Ontario. Its autonym is Ukwehuwehnéha. Oneida is related to Mohawk, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca within the Lake-Iroquois languages. The language has a small number of native speakers and is critically endangered according to the language-vitality assessment described in the source. Oneida is now generally a secondary language, while almost all Oneida are bilingual or monolingual English speakers.

Oneida writing was developed by French missionaries, but written records by Oneida individuals are relatively recent and are nearly exclusively in English. Oneida culture places particular importance on oral narrative, formal oratory, ritual language, wampum, and prescribed speech. The sources describe Christianity, traditional Oneida beliefs, religious rituals, the Onnonhouaroia ceremony, and shamanistic medicine ceremonies.
