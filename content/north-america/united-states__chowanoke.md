## Overview

The Chowanoke, also spelled Chowanoc, Chowanoke, Chawanook, Chowanock, and Chawwonock, were an Algonquian-speaking Native American tribe historically associated with the Chowan River in North Carolina. Their settlements extended from north of the confluence of the Chowan and Meherrin Rivers to the mouth of the Chowan River, with smaller towns along Bennett Creek and tributaries of the Meherrin and Wiccacon Rivers. At the time of first English contact in the 1580s, they were a large and influential tribe and the most populous tribe in their region. The name is Algonquian and translates as “they of the south” or “southerners.” The sources describe their history, territory, language, and political relations.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Archaeologists explored the primary town also called Chowanoc in the 1980s and learned that it was first settled in the 10th century CE.

## Music & performance

The sources state that in 1775 James Adair noted “Chowan” as a language still spoken within the Catawba Nation in South Carolina.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record political and military events involving the Chowanoc: a peace treaty with English colonists in 1663, the Chowanoc War of 1675 to 1677, and participation with the English against the Tuscarora in the Tuscarora War from 1711 to 1713.

## Foodways

The sources state that English colonists attempted to cultivate positive relationships with neighboring tribes and to ensure Chief Menatonon’s support of the English colonists.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record the names of historical leaders and places associated with the Chowanoc, including Chief Menatonon, his son Skiko, Chief Kiscutanewh, Maraton, Ramushonok, Obanoak, Metocaum, and Catoking.

## Language & religion

The Chowanoke were Algonquian-speaking, and their name is described as Algonquian, translating as “they of the south” or “southerners.” In 1775 James Adair noted “Chowan” as a language still spoken within communities comprising the Catawba Nation in South Carolina. By 1733 descendants of the Chowanoc had assimilated with the Tuscarora, and surviving Chowanoc and Tuscarora shared a 53,000-acre reservation around 1723.
