## Overview

The Kalapuya or Kʼalapʰuya are a Native American people whose traditional homelands were in the Willamette Valley of western Oregon, bounded by the Cascade Range, the Oregon Coast Range, the Columbia River, and the Calapooya Mountains of the Umpqua River. They consisted of eight autonomous subdivisions speaking three mutually intelligible dialects: Northern Kalapuyan, Central Kalapuyan, and Yoncalla, also called Southern Kalapuya. Before contact, their population is believed to have numbered around 15,000, although estimates range from 8,780 to 25,000. Most descendants are enrolled in the Confederated Tribes of the Grand Ronde Community of Oregon; others belong to the Confederated Tribes of the Siletz. Kalapuya culture is documented through seasonal food gathering, fishing, hunting, basketry, clothing, canoes, plank houses, ecological management, and language revitalization.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources state that women constructed baskets and other craft products, and that baskets, clothing, and household objects were made from rushes. They also describe a variety of mats made from Tule and Cattail.

### Clothing & dress

Kalapuya clothing included waterproof garments and hats made from Cedar bark. The sources also state that rushes were used for varieties of clothing. Men engaged in fishing, hunting, and warfare, while women gathered and prepared staple plant foods, constructed baskets, and made other craft products. The available material does not describe dress differences between men and women beyond these work divisions.

### Architecture

Kalapuya houses included plank houses made from Cedar tree wood. The sources also state that canoes and planks for plank houses were made from Cedar. Bands occupied year-round villages, where they lived during winter; in spring and summer, some members divided into smaller groups and travelled to gather seasonal food and raw materials.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They describe fishing baskets, dipnets, mesh net traps, fishing spears, snares, bows and arrows, carrying baskets, canoes, mats, rope, and household tools. Rope was made from dogbane, canoes from Cedar wood, and mats from Tule and Cattail. Obsidian obtained from the volcanic ranges to the east was used to make projectile points, including arrowheads and spear tips.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They mention beads as commodities obtained through the trading of slaves.

## Music & performance

They mention Chinook Jargon as a trade language spoken among the Kalapuya and state that the Confederated Tribes of the Grand Ronde Community renamed it Chinuk Wawa and developed a language immersion program for children. No musical tradition is described in the supplied material.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe a seasonal cycle of subsistence and land management. In summer, from May–August, the Atfalati-Kalapuya gathered berries, roots, plants, insects, fish, and freshwater animals. In fall, during September and October, they gathered Wapato, Bear Berries, Tobacco, Myrtlewood Nuts, Hazelnuts, and Acorns, and hunted animals including Raccoon, Timber Wolf, Bobcat, Cougar, Chipmonk, Grouse, and Quail. In winter, from November–February, they hunted Bear, Beaver, Geese, Roosevelt Elk, Black Tailed Deer, and WhiteTailed Deer. In spring, during March and April, they gathered Cats Ear, Bracken Fern, Cattails, and Camas.

Traditional fire setting was part of long-term land management. In Oregon, a yearly late Autumn burning of Wild Wheat grass prairies helped dry ripe wheat berries and was described as enriching soil, encouraging biodiversity, clearing brush, reducing disease and pests, and preventing unwanted large-scale fires.

## Foodways

Kalapuya subsistence combined fishing, hunting, and gathering. Men fished and hunted with baskets, dipnets, mesh net traps, fishing spears, snares, spears, bows, and arrows. Women gathered and prepared staple plant foods and processed food for winter storage. Wapato was collected by women using their feet to free its starchy bulb from the floor of a pond; because the bulb floated, it could be transferred by hand into a nearby canoe. Camas was freed from the soil with a handled digging stick and gathered into carrying baskets.

The sources name Wapato and Camas as important foods. Through the Columbia Trade Network, Kalapuyans mostly traded dried Wapato and Camas flour cakes for buffalo hides, shells, whale products, and dried salmon. Other documented foods and resources include nuts, berries, roots, Salmon, Sturgeon, Eulachon, Crawfish, Freshwater Mussels, Eels, Wild onions, Honey, Acorns, Hazelnuts, Bear Berries, and Myrtlewood Nuts.

## Oral tradition & literature

They identify language preservation and revitalization as contemporary concerns. The Kalapuyan languages are not currently under revitalization according to the supplied Kalapuya article, although revitalization is described as a future goal. The Confederated Tribes of the Grand Ronde Community have developed a Chinuk Wawa immersion program for children and have produced native speakers, expanding the program through the eighth grade.

The Kalapuyan language article states that descendants in the southernmost Kalapuya region of Yoncalla, Oregon, published 100 copies of a comprehensive dictionary, with plans to expand it. It also lists *Kalapuya Texts* by Melville Jacobs and *The Kalapuya Dictionary* by Paul Stephen McCartney Sr. among its further reading.

## Language & religion

Kalapuyan is a small extinct language family from the Willamette Valley of western Oregon and consists of three languages. The Kalapuya people spoke three mutually intelligible dialect groupings: Northern Kalapuyan, Central Kalapuyan, and Yoncalla, also called Southern Kalapuya. The language was categorized by John Wesley Powell as part of the Takelman language group and is known in the early 21st century within the Oregon Penutian languages. Kalapuyan has also been connected with various Penutian proposals, although a paper by Tarpent and Kendall found the proposed relationship with Takelma unfounded.

Chinook Jargon, renamed Chinuk Wawa by the Confederated Tribes of the Grand Ronde Community, served as a trade language and became popular on the Grand Ronde Reservation. They state that special religious leaders were recognized as a distinct social class and were believed to possess supernatural predictive or healing powers; these leaders could be male or female, free or enslaved.
