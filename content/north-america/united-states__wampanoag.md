## Overview

The Wampanoag, also rendered Wôpanâak, are a Native American people of the Northeastern Woodlands, currently based in southeastern Massachusetts and formerly parts of eastern Rhode Island. Their historical territory includes Martha’s Vineyard and Nantucket. Before English contact in the 17th century, as many as 40,000 people lived across 67 villages composing the Wampanoag Nation. Today, the Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe and the Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head (Aquinnah) are federally recognized. The Wampanoag language, also known as Massachusett, is a Southern New England Algonquian language. Wampanoag culture continues through oral traditions, ceremonies, song and dance, social gatherings, hunting, fishing, farming, and language-reclamation work.

## Music & performance

The sources identify song and dance as important traditional ways of life.

The sources describe literacy and religious translation in greater detail than performance. John Eliot produced a Wampanoag orthography and translated the Christian Bible into Wampanoag in 1663. Written records include church records, praying-town administrative records, personal letters, court petitions, and translations of religious tracts. The Massachusett Psalter is also mentioned, including sung versions of translations of the Psalms of David.

## Dance & theatre

The sources state that dance remains an important traditional way of life.

## Festivals & rituals

They describe a seasonal round of fishing, planting, harvesting, and hunting, with men undertaking seasonal fishing expeditions and women cultivating maize, climbing beans, and squash.

Ceremonies, mourning rituals, and social gatherings remain important traditional ways of life. Wampanoag converts on Martha’s Vineyard often maintained traditional mourning rituals, and Christian Indian settlements there were noted for sharing and mixing Wampanoag and colonial ways of life.

The sources discuss the 1621 celebration commonly depicted as the First Thanksgiving. One primary account states that Indians were present, but the source says nothing more about Wampanoag participation in the celebration. The sources also describe King Philip’s War, which began in 1675 and ended in 1676, as a major historical event involving Wampanoag political and military action.

## Foodways

Wampanoag foodways combined cultivation, fishing, hunting, gathering, and seasonal movement. Women cultivated the “three sisters”—maize, climbing beans, and squash—as staples of the diet. Fish and game caught by men supplemented these crops, while women gathered wild fruits, nuts, berries, and shellfish.

Men were mainly responsible for hunting and fishing, while women cared for farming and gathering. Women played an active role in many stages of food production and processing and were responsible for up to 75 percent of food production in Wampanoag societies. The sources describe food production as organized partly along gender lines and connected to a seasonal round of planting, harvesting, fishing, and hunting.

The sources also state that Tisquantum and other Wampanoag taught the Pilgrims how to cultivate corn, squash, and beans, catch and process fish, and collect seafood.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral traditions remain important to Wampanoag life.

The Wôpanâak Language Reclamation Project was founded in 1993 by Jessie Little Doe Baird, a member of the Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe. The project uses archival Wôpanâak documents and linguistic methods to reconstruct unattested words. Baird developed a 10,000-word Wôpanâak-English dictionary and produced a grammar, collections of stories, and other books. The project has reintroduced the revived dialect to the Mashpee, Aquinnah, Assonet, and Herring Pond communities, with children becoming the first native speakers in more than a century.

The sources also mention Mary Rowlandson’s *The Sovereignty and Goodness of God*, an account of her captivity by the Wampanoag during King Philip’s War.

## Language & religion

Wôpanâak, also called Massachusett, belongs to the Algonquian language family and is classified within the Southern New England Algonquian languages. It was historically spoken across eastern Massachusetts, eastern Rhode Island, southeastern New Hampshire, and the southernmost tip of Maine. The language went out of use in the late 19th century. A revival project began in 1993; as of 2014, there were five native speakers and over 500 second-language learners. In its revived form, it is spoken in four Wampanoag communities.

The first Bible published in America was a 1663 translation into Wampanoag by John Eliot. The language had a strong written tradition, including religious texts, church records, letters, petitions, and praying-town documents. The sources describe Christianity, Puritan missions, and 14 praying towns, while also recording the continued practice of traditional dress, hairstyle, governance, and mourning rituals among many converts.
