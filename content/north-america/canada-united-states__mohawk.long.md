## Overview

The Mohawk, who call themselves *Kanienʼkehá꞉ka* — "People of the Flint Place," from *Kanien꞉ke*, "Flint Stone Place" — are an Indigenous people of the Northeastern Woodlands and the easternmost nation of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy, known within it as the Keepers of the Eastern Door. Their traditional territory centred on the Mohawk Valley of present-day upstate New York, west of the Hudson, reaching north to the St. Lawrence, south into New Jersey and Pennsylvania, east to the Green Mountains of Vermont, and west to Oneida country. Today Mohawk communities include Akwesasne, Tyendinaga, Kahnawà꞉ke, Kanesatake, Wahta and Six Nations of the Grand River, alongside urban populations. Roughly 3,500 people spoke the Mohawk language as of 2011; it is the largest of the Northern Iroquoian languages. The sources used here document their basketry, dress and photographic self-representation, their clan system, and one of the most sustained language-revival efforts in North America.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used for this profile document one pattern-bearing craft tradition directly, through a museum object.

**Splint and sweetgrass basketry** — A lidded "fancy" basket in the British Museum is made from black ash splints dyed a fuchsia colour, worked together with sweetgrass. Its decorative language is three-dimensional rather than woven-in: the splints are looped into raised "curls" across the outside of both body and lid. The weft at the base of the basket and at the top of the lid is sweetgrass, a braided strip of sweetgrass runs around the lid rim, and the lid handle is a green-dyed splint. Against the plain-weave carrying baskets of neighbouring traditions, the effect depends on dyed colour, the aromatic sweetgrass element, and the projecting curl.

The catalogue records also show beadwork applied to garments — a beaded bandolier bag, a beaded collar, a beaded vest, a decorated headband —.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe both a historic hairstyle and a photographic record of nineteenth- and twentieth-century regalia. Mohawk men, like many men of the other Haudenosaunee nations, historically removed most of the hair from the head by plucking — not shaving — tuft by tuft, leaving a smaller section worn in styles that varied by community. Women wore their hair long, often dressed with bear grease, or tied back in a single braid. In traditional dress women often went topless in summer and wore a skirt of deerskin, with deerskin clothing in colder seasons.

The British Museum postcards photographed at Kahnawà꞉ke (Caughnawaga) show how Mohawk people presented themselves publicly in the era of tourist portraiture: Chief Poking Fire wears a feather headdress, beaded bandolier bag, tunic, fringed leggings and moccasins; Sio-he-ro-te and Sa-on-ta-ri-ia-kon wear feather headdresses and decorated hide shirts, Sio-he-ro-te with a beaded collar; Tha-nen-ri-shon (Thomas J. Williams) poses in regalia with a bow and arrow. A studio portrait of the Mohawk performer Go-Won-Go shows a decorated headband with a feather, a decorated jacket and a beaded vest over it.

### Architecture

The sources record the settlement pattern instead: three villages along the south side of the Mohawk River — Ossernenon, Andagaron and Tionontoguen, recorded by Isaac Jogues in 1642 and by the Dutch minister Johannes Megapolensis as Asserué, Banagiro and Thenondiogo — into which four earlier villages had been consolidated after the smallpox epidemic of 1635. In the eighteenth century most Mohawks in the Province of New York lived at Canajoharie, the "Upper Castle," with a second major settlement some thirty miles downstream at Tionondorage Castle, also called Fort Hunter, the "Lower Castle," a few families living at Schoharie.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

One object in this atlas speaks to everyday material culture beyond basketry: a Mohawk cradleboard of about 1860 in the Metropolitan Museum of Art, made of wood, rawhide and pigment — a carrying frame for an infant, its surface worked with applied colour. Flint itself was the group's defining economic material: the Mohawk became wealthy traders because other nations of the Confederacy needed their flint for tool-making, and the people's own name for themselves derives from the flint place. One further everyday word survives in the language sources as a noun class marker — *kanhoha*, "door," carries the prefix *ka-* that Mohawk generally attaches to human-made things, as against the *o-* of things found in the natural world.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources document adornment chiefly through the plucked male hairstyle and the bear-grease hairdressing described above, and through the beaded elements worn in the photographs: a beaded bandolier bag, a beaded collar, a beaded vest, and a decorated headband carrying a feather. They say nothing about metal jewellery, tattooing, or the materials and ritual functions of ornament.

## Music & performance

What the sources do record of the spoken-word tradition in performance is the Thanksgiving Address, *Ohén꞉ton Karihwatékwen* — "the words before all else" — recited every morning in the hallway by teachers and students together at the Ahkwesásne Freedom School, where the curriculum is built on a traditional cycle of fifteen seasonal ceremonies. Mohawk-language broadcasting is carried by CKON-FM, licensed by the Ahkwesáhsne Mohawk Nation, on air at 97.3 in Hogansburg, New York and Saint Regis, Quebec and streaming online; part of its programming is in *Kanienʼkéha*, and its call sign refers to the Mohawk word *she:kon*, "hello."

## Dance & theatre

The sources record Mohawk performance in other media: Go-Won-Go, photographed in a studio portrait held by the British Museum, was a Mohawk performer; and the language has carried a substantial screen presence, in the 2017 film *Mohawk*, the 1991 film *Black Robe*, the 1990 film *The Company of Strangers*, the 2020 series *Barkskins*, Ubisoft Montreal's 2012 game *Assassin's Creed III* — whose half-Mohawk protagonist Ratonhnhaké꞉ton speaks it — and the Marvel animated series *What If...?*, in the episode "What If... Kahhori Reshaped the World?"

## Festivals & rituals

Canada and the United States have no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions, so nothing in this profile rests on that list. Of the annual calendar the sources name the cycle of fifteen seasonal ceremonies on which the Ahkwesásne Freedom School's immersion curriculum has been based since about 1985, and the annual Strawberry Festival held at Kanaʼtsioharè:ke, the community west of Fonda, New York re-established in September 1993 under Thomas R. Porter (Sakokwenionkwas, "The One Who Wins"), which also holds cultural presentations, workshops and academic events. Nothing in the sources describes birth, coming-of-age, wedding or funeral rites.

Two ritual and political practices of a different kind are documented. Megapolensis, living near Fort Nassau for about six years and writing in 1644, recorded the Mohawk ritual torture of captives, while noting that their society had few other killings compared with the Netherlands of the period. And since 1971 the Mohawk Warrior Society, *Rotisken'rakéhte*, founded at Kahnawà꞉ke, has used roadblocks, evictions and occupations to assert rights and to protect the environment from pollution — the Oka Crisis blockades of 1990 and the 2006 occupation at Douglas Creek in Caledonia, Ontario among them. Related repossessions include the 1974 occupation of Moss Lake near Old Forge, New York and the 1993 purchase of 322 acres at Kanatsiohareke in Montgomery County.

## Foodways

The sources record one staple and its landscape. For centuries the Mohawk cultivated productive maize fields on the fertile floodplains along the Mohawk River, west of the Pine Bush, alongside hunting and fishing; they maintained the St. Lawrence River Valley as hunting grounds. Those floodplain fields were valuable enough that when the Dutch established Schenectady as a farming settlement further west in the valley, they took over some of the former Mohawk maize fields along the river. When French forces attacked the three Mohawk villages south of the river in 1666, they burned the stored food supply along with the villages. Maize appears in the language sources as *oʼnenste*, "corn," carrying the *o-* prefix that Mohawk gives to things of the natural environment, alongside *oienʼkwa*, "tobacco." The sources describe no cooking methods, named dishes, ceremonial foods or beverage traditions.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used do not record Mohawk folktales, epics, proverbs or riddles, but they document the verbal tradition that anchors daily practice and the revival apparatus built around the language. The Thanksgiving Address, *Ohén꞉ton Karihwatékwen*, "the words before all else," is recited each morning at the Ahkwesásne Freedom School, founded by Mohawk parents in 1979 out of concern at the lack of culture-based education in public and parochial schools; its immersion curriculum, adopted six years later, rests on that address and on the fifteen-ceremony seasonal cycle. Kanaʼtsioharè:ke — "place of the clean pot" — was conceived as a "Carlisle Indian Boarding School in Reverse," its stated mission to preserve traditional values, culture, language and lifestyles under the guidance of the *Kaienerekowa*, the Great Law of Peace. Documentation and teaching materials are substantial: Tom Porter and Dorothy Lazore's *Nobody Can Do It Better Than Wariso꞉se*, the FirstVoices archive, and the 2016 short documentary *Raising The Words*, on language revitalisation at Tyendinaga.

## Language & religion

*Kanienʼkéha* is an Iroquoian language, classified as threatened, with around 3,500 speakers in 2011 and 875 people reporting it as their only mother tongue in the 2016 Canadian census. It has three dialects, differing largely in phonology: Western (Ohswé:ken, Kenhté:ke), Central (Ahkwesáhsne) and Eastern (Kahnawà꞉ke, Kanehsatà꞉ke). It is polysynthetic, lacks labial consonants except in loans such as *mátsis* ("matches") and *aplám* ("Abraham"), and carries four tones on stressed vowels; its orthography was standardised in 1993. Society is organised by clans, *ken'tara'okòn꞉'a* — the Mohawk have three: Bear (*Ohkwa:ri*), Turtle (*A'nó꞉wara*) and Wolf (*Okwaho*) — descended nominally from a single female ancestor, with women holding the leadership role, children belonging to the mother's clan, and marriage within a clan forbidden. Traditional religion is largely animist, built around a primordial conflict between good and evil; many Mohawks follow the Longhouse Religion, while Catholicism took hold after the seventeenth-century Jesuit missions and produced Kateri Tekakwitha, born at Ossernenon, canonised in October 2012.
