## Overview

The Pueblo of Laguna, also called *Kawaika* in Western Keres, is a federally recognized Native American Pueblo people in west-central New Mexico, in the Rio San Jose basin about 45 miles west of Albuquerque. Their reservation occupies parts of Cibola, Sandoval, Valencia and Bernalillo Counties and includes Encinal, Laguna, Mesita, Paguate, Paraje and Seama. The population figures given by the sources include 6,758 people who reported being exclusively Laguna and 8,358 who reported being Laguna either exclusively or in combination with another group on the 2010 census; the State of New Mexico gives a population of 7,700. Laguna traditionally speak Western Keresan, part of the Keres language family. Their cultural history includes farming along the Rio San José, pottery, Pueblo religious traditions, a historic Spanish mission, and continued transmission of Keresan language and artistic knowledge.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used do not document a Laguna textile tradition sufficiently to describe its materials, techniques or distinctive patterns.

**Pottery** — Laguna people are described as skilled in pottery. Evelyn Cheromiah revived hand-coiled ceramic production in the 1970s by studying ancestral potsherds, identifying indigenous clay sources, restoring natural mineral and vegetal pigments, and reintroducing outdoor pit firing. Her vessels used stark white slips, precise geometric polychrome patterns, interlocking fine lines, and cloud or animal motifs.

**Museum vessels** — Museum records describe Laguna Pueblo vessels made from light or white clay, with red and black decoration. One vessel has a half-closed form with a strongly marked belly; another is small, closed, and has two openings separated by a handle. Other records describe a lerkärl with a broken handle and a pottery bowl with a white ground and dark-brown decoration.

**Motif vocabulary.** geometric patterns, interlocking fine lines, cloud motifs, animal motifs, red and black decoration, dark-brown decoration.

### Clothing & dress

The sources used do not describe Laguna everyday or ceremonial clothing, head coverings, belts, footwear, or gender-specific dress. A museum catalogue records a photograph of a Laguna woman nursing a child held in a carrier on her lap, but it does not provide a description of clothing or adornment. The sources also mention that Laguna residents in the Richmond boxcar community spoke Keresan, maintained community gardens, travelled to New Mexico for sacred ceremonies and annual Feast Days, and celebrated Grab Day, without documenting the clothing worn on those occasions.

### Architecture

The best-documented Laguna building is Mission San José de la Laguna, also called St. Joseph Church, constructed between 1699 and 1701. Its interior includes earthen-plaster murals with geometric and abstract Pueblo motifs made from hand-ground red, yellow and black native mineral pigments. The nave has exposed pine round-timber vigas covered by peeled-pole latillas arranged in a herringbone pattern. The sanctuary contains a hide-and-wood reredos, or altar screen, made between 1800 and 1808. The sources also refer to traditional Keresan kiva religious structures and to the old pueblo, now Old Laguna, but do not describe domestic houses in sufficient detail.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Laguna ceramic production uses hand-coiling, indigenous clay sources, natural mineral and vegetal pigments, and outdoor pit firing. During the mid-20th century, the craft had nearly reached extinction, and Laguna residents often relied on commercial goods or purchased utilitarian pottery from neighboring Acoma Pueblo. Museum records identify vessels and bowls from Laguna Pueblo, including light- and white-clay forms decorated in red, black or dark brown. The sources do not document Laguna wooden objects, tools, or household goods in detail. British Museum records do, however, identify a steel necklace and a steel bracelet associated with the material culture shown for this group.

### Jewelry & body adornment

A British Museum catalogue records a steel necklace consisting of a flexible band with hooks at both ends and 19 steel representations of squash blossoms. It also records a steel bracelet with two silver bands on the front, bisected by a zig-zag line representing mountains. The sources do not explain the social, ceremonial or ritual functions of these objects, and they do not document tattoos, henna, hair practices, or other forms of body adornment.

## Music & performance

The sources used do not document Laguna musical instruments, song genres, musical performance practices, or the contexts in which music is performed. They do state that Laguna residents travelled back to New Mexico to participate in sacred ceremonies and annual Feast Days, and that Keres is a sacred language traditionally understood as existing only in spoken form. These statements do not provide enough information to describe particular songs, instruments, dances, or performance structures.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used do not cover Laguna dance, theatre, dramatic traditions, masked performance, or named dances. Grab Day is described as a community celebration in which candies and gifts were thrown from boxcar roofs to village children, but the sources do not identify this as a dance or theatrical form.

## Festivals & rituals

Laguna residents of the Richmond boxcar community routinely returned to New Mexico for sacred ceremonies and annual Feast Days. They also celebrated Grab Day by throwing candies and gifts from boxcar roofs to village children. The sources do not give a calendar of Feast Days, dates for individual celebrations, or descriptions of the associated rites.

The historical sources describe continuing ties between Laguna religious life and the Roman Catholic Church, as well as traditional Keresan religious structures and altars. Mission San José de la Laguna combines Spanish colonial Catholicism with traditional Keresan iconography. Its altar screen depicts Saint Joseph at the centre, flanked by Saint Barbara and Saint John Nepomuk, while the sanctuary ceiling includes a rainbow, sun, moon and stars. The sources interpret this combination as a historical syncretism between Franciscan religious narratives and Pueblo cosmological oral histories. They do not describe Laguna birth, coming-of-age, wedding or funeral ceremonies.

## Foodways

The sources used do not document Laguna staple foods, named dishes, cooking methods, ceremonial foods, beverages, or dietary rules. They state that Laguna people historically farmed along the Rio San José and that the historic lake supported waterfowl including ducks, geese and swans, but they do not explain how these resources were prepared or consumed.

The Richmond boxcar community maintained community vegetable gardens, yet the sources provide no information about the crops grown, the foods prepared, or their ceremonial significance.

## Oral tradition & literature

Laguna oral history is mentioned as part of anthropological research demonstrating habitation of the area from 6500 B.C. to the present. The sources do not preserve named Laguna folktales, epics, proverbs, riddles or narrative genres. They also do not identify a Laguna literary canon.

Keresan religious beliefs are described as treating Keres as a sacred language that must exist only in spoken form. The sources connect the mission’s Pueblo celestial symbols with traditional Pueblo cosmological oral histories, but do not provide the content of those histories. The material and oral histories of the Richmond boxcar community are preserved in UC Berkeley’s Bancroft Library archives. Evelyn Cheromiah’s pottery teaching transmitted ancestral techniques to her daughters, Lee Ann Cheromiah, Mary Cheromiah-Victorino and Wendy Cheromiah, and to her grandchildren.

## Language & religion

Laguna traditionally speak Western Keresan, a variety of Keres. Keres is described as a Native American language spoken by Keres Pueblo people in New Mexico and as either a small language family or a language isolate, depending on analysis. The Western and Eastern groups differ significantly, while neighbouring Keres Pueblo varieties are mutually intelligible. Most Laguna elders do not speak English.

The language has a practical spelling system for Laguna, also called Kʼawaika in the language source, although Keresan traditionally has no unified orthographic convention. The sources describe Keresan as having tones, long and short vowels, and a complex consonant inventory. Keresan is generally verb-final, with flexible word order, and its verbs encode information including person, number, modality, polarity and aspect.

Laguna religious life historically includes Keresan kiva structures, community governance, traditional altars and ties to the Roman Catholic Church. Mission San José de la Laguna represents a combination of Spanish colonial Catholicism and traditional Keresan iconography. The sources do not identify a separate modern religious denomination or provide a complete account of present-day religious practice.
