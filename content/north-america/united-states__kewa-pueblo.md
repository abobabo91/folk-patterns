## Overview

Kewa Pueblo, also known as Santo Domingo Pueblo, is a federally recognized tribe of Keres Pueblo people in northern New Mexico. The community is located in Sandoval County, approximately 25 miles (40 km) southwest of Santa Fe and about 35 miles (56 km) northeast of Albuquerque, near Interstate 25 and the Rio Grande Valley. As of the 2020 census, the pueblo had a population of 2,311. Residents speak Keres, an eastern dialect of the Keresan languages. Kewa society has a matrilineal kinship system: children belong to their mother’s family and clan, while inheritance and property pass through the maternal line. The community is significant in folk culture for its pottery, shell and stone inlay jewelry, heishi beadwork, annual corn dances, and continuing religious and ceremonial practices.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Pottery decoration** — Kewa and Cochiti potters have made stylized pottery for centuries, developing forms for different purposes and expressing deep beliefs through their designs. Since the early decades of the 20th century, Kewa potters have continued to use traditional techniques while expanding their designs and repertoire for an international market.

**Heishi beadwork** — *Heishi* are flat disks or beads made from stone and shell. They are often assembled into necklaces and are identified in Eastern Keresan as meaning “shell bead.”

**Stone and shell inlay** — Kewa artists make inlay jewelry whose designs are inspired by prehistoric Anasazi and Hohokam inlay designs. The Reano family has continued developing shell and stone inlay jewelry.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name prehistoric Anasazi and Hohokam inlay designs.

### Architecture

Many residents live in structures, some of which date from circa 1700. A 48 acres (19 ha) area of the pueblo was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1973, including 80 contributing buildings. A church was built after a mission was established following Juan de Oñate’s visit in 1598. In 1807, Lieutenant Zebulon Pike described the church as decorated with elegantly ornamented paintings, including one depicting Saint Domingo.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery is an important art form and utilitarian craft at Kewa Pueblo. Large ollas and dough bowls are common forms. Many Kewa potters are women, although men also create ceramics. Tourism connected with a nearby railway stop in Wallace during the 1920s drew wider attention to Kewa pottery, which became a valuable export. The Aguilar Family, consisting of two sisters and one sister-in-law, made Kewa pottery from 1910 until approximately 1915 and became well known for their work. Robert Tenorio has continued his family legacy by making traditional Kewa pottery.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Kewa artists are known for stonework jewelry, especially flat disks or beads called *heishi*, which are often made into necklaces. These beads may be made from shell or stone. Angie Reano Owen, a Kewa inlay jeweler and lapidary artist, grew up in the Reano family of heishi beadmakers. Her designs draw inspiration from prehistoric Anasazi and Hohokam inlay designs. The Reano family has continued to develop shell and stone inlay jewelry.

## Music & performance

The principal performance tradition documented in the sources is the traditional corn dance. More than 2,000 Pueblo people participate in the dances held during the annual feast day on August 4, which honors the patron saint, Saint Dominic. ## Dance & theatre

Traditional corn dances are held annually at the pueblo on August 4, during the feast day honoring Saint Dominic. More than 2,000 Pueblo people participate.

## Festivals & rituals

Kewa Pueblo celebrates an annual feast day on August 4 to honor its patron saint, Saint Dominic. Traditional corn dances are held at this time, with participation by more than 2,000 Pueblo people. The sources also describe religious ceremonies conducted by the Cacique, the highest official in the pueblo. Although the Cacique does not serve as ruler, he serves as a priest and conducts a multitude of religious ceremonies.

Kewa members share a belief that their people emerged from the inner earth at a place in the north called *Shipap*. According to the legend, they migrated southward from Shipap and divided into smaller groups, which later founded other pueblos.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources document a Kewa origin legend centered on *Shipap*, the place in the north from which Kewa members believe their people emerged from the inner earth. The legend recounts a southward migration and the division of the people into smaller groups that founded other pueblos.

## Language & religion

Kewa Pueblo residents speak Keres, an eastern dialect of the Keresan languages. The sources give several names for the community: Kewa, also spelled Kiua; Eastern Keres [kʰewɑ]; Western Keres *Díiwʾi*; and Navajo *Tó Hájiiloh*. The earliest recorded name was Gipuy. According to Pueblo Council members, the local Keres name has always been Kewa, and in 2009 the pueblo officially changed its name to Kewa Pueblo. The Western Keresan name for the pueblo was *Díiwʾi*, while the name for its people was *Dîiwʾamʾé*.

The pueblo’s religious life includes the annual feast day honoring Saint Dominic, the priestly role of the Cacique, and the origin tradition associated with Shipap.
