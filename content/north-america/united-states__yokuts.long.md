## Overview

The Yokuts are an Indigenous people of California who live in the Central Valley, especially the San Joaquin Valley, from the Sacramento-San Joaquin River Delta south to Bakersfield and the adjacent foothills of the Sierra Nevada. They are often divided into Foothill Yokuts, Northern Valley Yokuts, and Southern Valley Yokuts. Before European contact, they consisted of up to 60 tribes speaking several related Yokuts languages. As of the 2010 census, 6,273 people identified as Yokuts; today, about 2,000 are enrolled in federally recognized tribes and an estimated 600 belong to unrecognized tribes. Yokuts material culture includes basketmaking, painting, tattoos, and piercings. Their baskets could carry woven designs and images, and museum records document coiled baskets, black geometric ornament, feathers, and ceremonial uses.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Basketmaking** — Basketmaking was an artistic practice in which Yokuts weavers incorporated designs and images into baskets. Museum records describe basin-shaped baskets made by coil technique, including examples with black geometrical ornament and black bands with double zigzag ornament.

**Feathered baskets** — A museum catalogue records a basket made of basketry and quail feathers. Another object, described as a bottle-neck form typical of Western Mono and Yokut, has black feathers tightly woven around the widest section.

**Ceremonial baskets** — The Museum of World Culture catalogue describes a so-called treasure basket used for storing gifts and ceremonial objects. Such ceremonial baskets could themselves be given as gifts, and the basket could be more significant than what it contained.

**Grass basketry** — A British Museum record describes a basket made of grass, possibly used as a food-serving bowl, and notes that it was much used.

**Basket tray** — A Museum of Ethnography record describes a tallriksformat basketry object identified as a “Dice Tray,” with patterns in black and dark brown and an association with “Yokut” and Tulare Indians.

Yokuts basketry styles are also discussed in connection with prehistoric central Nevada in the Yokuts language article.

**Motif vocabulary.** Black geometrical ornament, black bands, double zigzag ornament, patterns in black and dark brown.

## Music & performance

The sources identify music and singing as forms of Yokuts artistic expression. They do not name particular instruments or song genres. Two important religious ceremonies were the annual mourning rite and the first fruit rite. Shamans were believed to have supernatural powers, helped conduct ceremonies, and treated the sick. The sources do not describe the musical structures, instruments, dances, or performance settings associated with these ceremonies.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used do not name Yokuts dances, dramatic traditions, theatre forms, or mask performances. They state only that the crow and the eagle continued to be symbolic figures in Yokuts religious ceremonies.

## Festivals & rituals

Yokuts religious life included the annual mourning rite and the first fruit rite. The sources do not provide dates or detailed calendars for these ceremonies. Many stories explain how the Yokuts and their land came to be. In a common form of the creation story, the world was covered in water until an eagle and a crow encountered a duck; the duck brought mud from the water, and this mud became the land of the Yokuts, specifically the Sierra Nevada and the Coast Mountains.

Shamans held an important ceremonial role. They were believed to possess supernatural powers and could treat illness, but the sources state that they could use their power for good or evil and could be executed depending on how they used it. The sources do not describe birth, coming-of-age, wedding, or funeral rites beyond the annual mourning rite.

## Foodways

Acorn mash was the Yokuts staple food. They also made flour from tule roots and wild iris bulbs. Their diet included game birds, waterfowl, rabbits, turtles, fish, and mussels. Deer, elk, and pronghorn were hunted less frequently. Wasp grubs were harvested as a seasonal delicacy and source of protein.

Yokuts collected salt from salt grass and foraged manzanita berries, pine nuts, and other seeds. They gardened tobacco rather than food. Fishing and hunting used spears, basket traps, and other tools. The sources do not name additional dishes, cooking vessels, beverages, dietary rules, or ceremonial foods.

## Oral tradition & literature

Yokuts creation stories vary according to the tribe but commonly describe a world initially covered by water. An eagle and a crow asked a duck to bring mud from the water, and the mud became the land of the Yokuts. The eagle and crow remained symbolic figures in religious ceremonies.

The sources also preserve a first-person account attributed to Yoimut concerning white settlers introducing cattle to an island in the Tulare Lake archipelago. In the account, the Yokuts left after fearing that settlers would burn them out, and Yoimut’s mother and stepfather took the family to Téjon Ranch.

The Yokuts language article states that Chukchansi became a written language with its own alphabet developed through a federal grant. It also records a phrase book and dictionary that were partially completed, together with efforts to compile a Chukchansi dictionary and grammar texts, assemble native texts, and create a curriculum for teaching the language.

## Language & religion

Yokuts is an endangered language spoken in the interior of Northern and Central California, in and around the San Joaquin Valley. It was formerly known as Mariposa. The language consists of half a dozen primary dialects, while an estimated forty linguistically distinct groups existed before Euro-American contact. Glottolog places the dialects into four distinct languages: Palewyami Yokuts, Buena Vista Yokuts, Northern Yokuts, and Tule–Kaweah Yokuts.

The Yawelmani dialect of Valley Yokuts is described as the only surviving branch. Choynimni went extinct in 2017. Wikchamni, Chukchansi, Tachi, and Yawelmani were being taught to at least a few children during the first decade of the 21st century.

Yokuts languages have been placed in the proposed Penutian language stock, and a Yok-Utian language family has also been proposed. Yokuts religious practice included the annual mourning rite, the first fruit rite, creation narratives, eagle and crow symbolism, and the work of shamans.
