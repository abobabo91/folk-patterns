## Overview

The Erie, also known as the Eriehronon, Ehriehronnons, Eriechronon, or La Nation du Chat, were an Indigenous Iroquoian people of the Northeastern Woodlands who lived in the lower Great Lakes region until the mid-17th century. Their territory lay southeast of Lake Erie, along its southeastern shore between the Buffalo River and Presque Isle Bay, in what is now western New York and northwestern Pennsylvania. Their population before European diseases is estimated at roughly 12,000. They shared cultural traits with the Neutral, Wendat, and Seneca and lived in large palisaded villages. The Erie spoke a Northern Iroquoian language that was mutually intelligible with Wendat and Neutral, but their language became extinct after their dispersal by the Haudenosaunee in the mid-1650s. Their material culture included bark-covered longhouses, incised pottery, flared rims, panther effigies, pipes, and feline-motif bone carvings.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** Panther and feline motifs are documented in pottery, effigies, pipes, and bone carvings.

### Clothing & dress

A 1632 account describes seven “Hereckeenes” encountered at Little Falls on the Potomac River as wearing red fringe and beaver coats. The account also describes their manner as haughty and their appearance as strangely attired.

### Architecture

Erie village life centered on large, bark-covered longhouses arranged within palisaded enclosures. The longhouses served as multi-family dwellings for extended maternal kin groups and were characteristic of their Iroquoian settlement pattern. In the historical period, 2,000 Erie warriors, accompanied by women and children, rapidly constructed a palisaded fort during a major battle in the summer of 1654. The sources also distinguish Erie-style communal longhouses from the small oval or rectangular structures of the people associated with the Whittlesey tradition.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Erie pottery featured incised patterns and flared rims. The sources describe high-quality pottery as grave goods and identify panther effigies as prevalent in Erie material culture. Pipes and bone carvings featuring feline motifs were also recovered in archaeological research. The Erie economy used hunting, fishing, foraging, and the cultivation of maize, beans, and squash. The sources mention traditional weapons, poisoned arrows, canoes, and at least a few firearms, while Haudenosaunee attackers used flintlock muskets acquired from the Dutch.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They describe pipes, bone carvings, pottery, and grave goods.

## Music & performance

The sources mention pipes as specialized artifacts.

## Dance & theatre

No named dance or performance tradition is identified.

## Festivals & rituals

Archaeological evidence suggests beliefs concerning the afterlife: Erie burials included “flexed” positioning and the placement of high-quality pottery and grave goods. The sources also describe a high social value placed on kinship and communal care. During the 1654 conflict, the Haudenosaunee campaign included mourning-war practices involving the taking of captives for adoption to replace deceased kin, but this is described as a Haudenosaunee tradition rather than an Erie ritual.

## Foodways

The Erie economy relied on the Three Sisters—maize, beans, and squash—and was supplemented by hunting, fishing, and foraging. The available material therefore documents staple crops and subsistence activities.

## Oral tradition & literature

Most historical information about the Erie comes from French missionaries and their Wendat informants, especially the Jesuit Relations. The sources record descriptions of Erie speech, villages, warfare, captives, and names, but provide no substantial body of Erie oral literature. The Erie language article records *Chautauqua* as a word of uncertain definition or translation and as a surviving loanword believed to come from Erie.

## Language & religion

Erie was an extinct Northern Iroquoian language formerly spoken around Lake Erie. It was poorly documented, and the sources state that linguists are not certain that its Iroquoian classification is correct. Jesuit missionaries recorded that Erie was mutually intelligible with Wendat and Neutral; one account states that the Erie “till the soil and speak the same language as our Hurons.” The sources record names and terms including *Erie*, *Eriez*, *Erielhonan*, *Ronnongwetonwanca*, *Kahqua* or *Kahkwa*, *Gùkulëáwo*, *Chautauqua*, *Rigué* or *Riqué*, and *Gentaienton*.
