## Overview

The Nisgaʼa are an Indigenous people in British Columbia, Canada, who reside in the Nass River valley of northwestern British Columbia. Approximately 2,000 people live in the Nass Valley, while another 5,000 Nisgaʼa live elsewhere in Canada; the people number about 7,000 in total. The official languages are Nisg̱aʼa and English. Nisg̱aʼa is an endangered Indigenous language of northwestern British Columbia and belongs to the language family generally called Tsimshianic. It is closely related to Gitxsan, and many linguists regard the two as dialects of a single Nass–Gitksan language. Nisgaʼa society is organized into four tribes—Ganhada, Gisḵʼaast, Laxgibuu and Laxsgiik—and these are further divided into house groups and clans.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name family crests as decorations.

### Clothing & dress

Before European colonization, men wore nothing in summer, which was described as the usual season for hunting and fishing. Women wore skirts made from softened cedar bark and went topless. In colder weather, men wore cedar-bark skirts shaped more like loincloths, cedar-bark capes and basket hats outdoors in the rain; indoors, they wore nothing. Women wore basket hats and cedar blankets both indoors and outdoors. Both sexes made and wore necklaces of shell and bone. Seal blubber was rubbed into the hair, and men wore their hair long or in a top knot.

During warfare, men wore red cedar armour, a cedar helmet and a cedar loincloth. They used spears, clubs, harpoons, bows and slings, while wicker shields were common.

### Architecture

Traditional Nisgaʼa houses were large rectangles made from cedar planks, with cedar-shake roofs and doors oriented toward the water. The doors were usually decorated with the family crest. Inside, the floor was dug down to contain the hearth and conserve temperature; beds and boxes holding possessions were placed around the walls. Before the mid-twentieth century, approximately three or four extended families might live in one house, although this had become uncommon. Masks and blankets might decorate the walls.

The sources also record the Nisgaʼa Museum, which opened in Lax̱g̱altsʼap in 2011 as a project of the Nisgaʼa Lisims government. The Nisgaʼa Lisims Government Building is in Gitlaxtʼaamiks.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do record cedar-plank houses, cedar-shake roofs, beds, boxes, masks, blankets, basket hats, wicker shields, weapons and canoes.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Both men and women made and wore shell and bone necklaces. They rubbed seal blubber into their hair, and men kept their hair long or in a top knot.

## Festivals & rituals

The Nisgaʼa calendar revolves around the harvesting of foods and goods. The original year followed the various moons. Hoobiyee, also spelled Hobiyee, Hobiiyee or Hoobiiyee, is associated with “Like a Spoon” and occurs in February/March; it is the traditional time to celebrate the new year. X̱saak, “To Eat Oolichan,” occurs in March, when oolichan return to the Nass River at the end of February or beginning of March. Mmaal, “To Use Canoes Again,” occurs in April as river ice begins to break.

Yansaʼalt, “Leaves Are Blooming,” is associated with May. Misoʼo, “Sockeye Salmon,” occurs in June, and X̱maay, “To Eat Berries,” in July. Wii Hoon, “Great Salmon,” occurs in August. Genuugwiikw, “Trail of the Marmot,” is associated with September; X̱laaxw, “To Eat Trout,” with October; Gwilatkw, “To Blanket,” with November; Luutʼaa, “To Sit,” with December; Ḵʼaliiyee, “To Walk North,” with January; and Buxwlaks, “To Blow Around,” with February.

## Foodways

The Nisgaʼa traditionally harvest seafood throughout the year, including razor clams, mussels, oysters, limpets, scallops, abalone, fish and seaweed. They also harvest salmon, cod, char, pike, trout and other freshwater fish from streams, and hunt seals, fish and sea lion. In the forests they hunt mountain goat, marmot and game birds.

The grease of the oolichan fish, *Thaleichthys pacificus*, is sometimes traded with other tribes, although the source says this is now more usually a ceremonial practice. The family works together to cook and process meat and fish. Meat is roasted or boiled, while fish and sea mammals are eaten frozen, boiled, dried or roasted. The heads of a type of cod, often gathered half-eaten by sharks, are boiled into a soup that, according to folklore, helps prevent colds.

The Nisgaʼa also trade dried fish, seal oil, fish oil, blubber and cedar. The calendar includes food-related periods such as X̱saak, “To Eat Oolichan,” Misoʼo, “Sockeye Salmon,” X̱maay, “To Eat Berries,” Wii Hoon, “Great Salmon,” and X̱laaxw, “To Eat Trout.”

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record that the Nisgaʼa First Voices archive contains songs and stories, and that the Nisgaʼa app is a bilingual dictionary and phrase collection with audio recordings, images and videos.

Language documentation includes translations of parts of the Bible and Book of Common Prayer prepared by Anglican missionary James Benjamin McCullagh and published in 1890, as well as a Nisgaʼa primer published in 1897. In 1973, Bruce Rigsby and Lonnie Hindle compiled *A Short Practical Dictionary of the Gitksan Language*, and Rigsby created a simple alphabet for Nisgaʼa that is widely used today.

## Language & religion

Nisg̱aʼa is an Indigenous language of northwestern British Columbia and belongs to the Tsimshianic language family. It is very closely related to Gitxsan; many linguists regard Nisgaʼa and Gitksan as dialects of a single Nass–Gitksan language, although they are generally treated as distinct languages in recognition of the political separation of the two groups. The official languages of Nisgaʼa are Nisg̱aʼa and English.

The language is endangered. A 2018 report recorded 311 fluent speakers and 294 active language learners in a population of 6,113. The Wilp Wilx̱oʼoskwhl Nisg̱aʼa Institute was established in 1993 to provide post-secondary education and promote language and culture revitalization. The Nisgaʼa First Voices archive was created in 2003, and the Nisgaʼa app was released in January 2012.
