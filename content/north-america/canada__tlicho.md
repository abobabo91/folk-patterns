## Overview

The Tłı̨chǫ are a Dene people of northern Canada whose name comes from a fabled descent from a supernatural dog-man. Their documented material culture centers on tailored skin clothing decorated with beads and porcupine quills, made for life in harsh northern conditions. Their economy and identity rest on hunting, fishing, trapping and gathering rights hard-won through two major treaties and an agreement that gave them self-government in 2005. Their language, Tłı̨chǫ Yatıì, is endangered but undergoes active revitalization through schools, radio and community storytelling.

## Material culture

Tłı̨chǫ material culture is tailored for a northern trapping economy, built around skin clothing and working tools.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The main decorative technique documented is glass beadwork in a floral pattern on smoked hide, alongside porcupine quillwork.

- **Beaded smoked-moosehide footwear** — Moccasins of smoked moose skin with beaver fur trim, cloth appliqué and floral glass beadwork.
- **Porcupine quillwork on moose skin** — Moose-skin slippers decorated with porcupine quillwork and beaver fur trim.
- **Floral pattern** (*floral*) — The only named pattern in the sources, used in glass beadwork on moose-skin moccasins.

### Clothing & dress

Cold-country dress in skin and cloth combines smoked moose hide with beaver fur, glass beads and porcupine quills, now mixed with manufactured cotton and metal.

- **Beaded moose-skin moccasins** — Smoked moose-skin footwear with beaver-fur trim, cloth insets and floral glass beadwork.
- **Moose-skin slippers** — Slippers of moose skin with porcupine quillwork and beaver-fur trim.
- **Bead-embroidered cotton parka** — Green cotton parka with leather and fur trim at shoulders, wrists and hem, embroidered with glass beads and fastened with a metal zipper.
- **Tumpline** (*head-band or tumpline*) — A padded cotton head-band with rope at each end, worn across the forehead to carry loads.

### Architecture

No sources document Tłı̨chǫ house forms or vernacular architecture.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Working equipment for the trapping economy is represented by carved wood tools and load-carrying textiles.

- **Lynx pelt-stretcher** — A single-piece tool of tapered wood for dressing fur, the commodity at the centre of Tłı̨chǫ trade.
- **Tumpline** (*head-band or tumpline*) — An elliptical pad of cotton with rope at both ends, used to carry loads by pressure across the head.

### Jewelry & body adornment

No jewelry, tattooing or hair practices are documented in the sources.

## Music & performance

No instruments or musical performance is documented, but language revitalization includes a radio station and weekly Elder Evening Story Telling.

- **Elder Evening Story Telling** — A weekly broadcast of stories, part of the Tłı̨chǫ Government's language revitalization work.

## Dance & theatre

No dance or dramatic traditions are covered in the sources.

## Festivals & rituals

No annual festivals or life-cycle rites are described; the documented rituals are political treaties and agreements that shaped Tłı̨chǫ sovereignty.

- **Treaty 8** — Signed in 1900 at Fort Resolution, with Drygeese as Tłı̨chǫ spokesman alongside headmen Benaiyah and Sek'eglinan.
- **Treaty 11** — Signed in summer 1921 by trading chief Monfwi, who represented all Tłı̨chǫ groups.
- **Mesa Lake peace treaty** — Signed in 1825 or 1829 between Chief Edzo and Yellowknife trading chief Akaitcho, ending hostility between the peoples.
- **Tłı̨chǫ Agreement** — Signed 25 August 2003, in full effect from 4 August 2005, marking the first official day of the Tlicho Government.

## Foodways

No dishes or cooking methods are documented, but hunting, fishing, trapping and gathering rights are central to Tłı̨chǫ identity and were hard-won through treaties.

- **Dettah fishing camp** (*T'èɂehdaà*) — The community's name means 'Burnt Point' and refers to a traditional Dene fishing camp.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tłı̨chǫ Yatıì was historically oral only; the people have now created a dictionary and written literature while maintaining storytelling traditions.

- **Tłı̨chǫ Done** (*Tłı̨chǫ Done or Thlingchadinne*) — The people's own name means 'Dog-Flank People' and refers to a fabled descent from a supernatural dog-man.
- **Tłıchǫ Yatıì Enįhtł'è – A Dogrib Dictionary** — First published in 1992, giving the Tłı̨chǫ a database of words and spellings and sparking revitalization work.
- **Richard Van Camp** — Writer and author of The Lesser Blessed, a work of Tłı̨chǫ authorship.
- **Val Wake's White Bird Black Bird** — A novel telling the story of Dogrib input into the formation of the NWT Indian Brotherhood.

## Language & religion

Tłı̨chǫ Yatıì is a Northern Athabaskan language, endangered but now one of two official languages of the Tłı̨chǫ Government; religious practice is not documented.

- **Tłı̨chǫ Yatıì** (*Tłı̨chǫ Yatıì or Dogrib*) — A Northern Athabaskan language with about 1,350 speakers at home and endangered status, now an official language of the Tłı̨chǫ Government.
- **Wıı̀lıı̀deh Yatıì** — The Dettah–Ndilǫ dialect spoken by Yellowknives Dene, arising after 1829 when Chipewyan speakers adopted Tłı̨chǫ.

## Glossary

- *Tłı̨chǫ* — The people; also spelled Tlicho, long known as Dogrib
- *Tłı̨chǫ Done* — The Tłı̨chǫ people; means Dog-Flank People
- *Tłı̨chǫ Yatıì* — The Tłı̨chǫ language, also called Dogrib
- *Wıı̀lıı̀deh Yatıì* — The Dettah–Ndilǫ dialect of Tłı̨chǫ Yatıì
- *T'èɂehdaà* — Dettah; means Burnt Point, a traditional fishing camp
