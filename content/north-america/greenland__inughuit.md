## Overview

The Inughuit, also called Inuhuit, are an ethnic subgroup of the Greenlandic Inuit and the northernmost group of Inuit. They live on the west coast of Greenland, north of the Arctic Circle, between 75–80° N and 58–74° W. Their largest settlement is Qaanaaq, established in 1953, and other communities include Moriusaq, Siorapaluk, Qeqertat, Qeqertarsuaq, and Savissivik. Their population was estimated at 800 in 2010. The Inughuit speak Inuktun, an Eskimo–Aleut language and a dialect of Inuktitut, also known as North Greenlandic, Thule Inuit, or Polar Eskimo. Their distinctive language, hunting practices, sled technology, religious traditions, and historical isolation distinguish them within Greenlandic Inuit culture.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state that the Inughuit developed a distinctive fashion during the period when climate change and isolation restricted contact with other Inuit communities.

### Architecture

They identify Uummannaq, also known as “Dundas” or “Thule” to Europeans, as the chief settlement until 1953, and Qaanaaq as the largest Inughuit settlement.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Inughuit used and traded iron from meteorites such as the Cape York meteorite. The sources also record boats, leisters, bows and arrows, and sled technology as important objects and technologies. Inughuit knowledge of building kayaks or umiaks was lost during their period of isolation, while Inuit from Baffin later reintroduced boats, leisters, and bows and arrows; the Inughuit taught them a more advanced form of sled technology. No ceramics or household goods are described.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Amulets were popular among the Inughuit and were believed to protect their wearers from misfortune and danger.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that the Inughuit historically practiced their native Inuit religion. Shamans, called *angakkoq* by the Inughuit, acted as healers and were believed to possess spiritual powers that enabled them to counter supernatural hazards posed by nature. Amulets were also associated with protection from misfortune and danger.

## Foodways

The Inughuit traditionally engaged in hunting and fishing. Animals hunted included Caribou, Arctic hares, Arctic foxes, Little auks, Thick-billed murre, Polar bears, seagulls, Ringed seals, Beluga whales, Narwhals, Walruses, and various species of fish.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that the Inughuit developed a distinctive language and culture during a period when climate change and isolation cut them off from other Inuit and regions. The language was first described by the explorers Knud Rasmussen and Peter Freuchen, who travelled through northern Greenland in the early twentieth century.

## Language & religion

Inuktun, also known as North Greenlandic, Thule Inuit, Polar Eskimo, or *avanersuarmiutut*, is an Eskimo–Aleut language and a dialect of Inuktitut. It lies between the Greenlandic language, Kalaallisut, and Canadian Inuktitut, Inuvialuktun, or Inuinnaqtun. Inuktun is spoken in Qaanaaq and surrounding villages, including Moriusaq, Siorapaluk, Qeqertat, Qeqertarsuaq, and Savissivik. It has no official orthography and is not taught in schools, although most inhabitants of Qaanaaq and nearby villages use it in everyday communication. All speakers also speak Standard Greenlandic; many speak Danish, and some speak English. The Inughuit historically practiced their native Inuit religion, with *angakkoq* acting as healer and spiritual specialist.
