## Overview

The Arizona Tewa, also called Hopi-Tewa, Tano, Southern Tewa, Hano, Thano, or Arizona Tewa, are a Tewa Pueblo group living on or near First Mesa in the eastern part of the Hopi Reservation in northeastern Arizona. The name Tewa is the preferred autonym because the Tewa language refers to its people as “Tewas.” Arizona Tewa communities are related to Tewa communities in the Rio Grande Valley, including Santa Clara Pueblo and Ohkay Owingeh. Their long contact with Hopi peoples has produced similarities in social structure, kinship organization, and clans, while the Tewa dual moiety has been preserved. Many Hopi-Tewa are trilingual in Tewa, Hopi, and English; some also speak Spanish and/or Navajo. The sources present language, multilingualism, migration, and artistic biographies more fully than material culture.

### Textile & pattern traditions

### Architecture

They identify Tewa Village as one of the main Arizona Tewa settlements, but provide no architectural description.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources identify several Arizona Tewa artists associated with pottery and katsina figure carving. Nampeyo, Fannie Nampeyo, Elva Nampeyo, Priscilla Namingha Nampeyo, Joy Navasie, and Dextra Quotskuyva are identified as potters. Neil David Sr. is identified as an artist and katsina figure carver.

## Music & performance

The sources do describe multilingual communication and a historical language role. Traditionally, Hopi-Tewa people served as translators for Hopi leaders and consequently had command of Spanish and Navajo. Their language practices involve a strict separation of Tewa, Hopi, and English, even though many speakers are trilingual. Hopi-Tewa is a variety of the Tewa language, which belongs to the Tanoan family and has been influenced by Hopi, an unrelated Uto-Aztecan language.

## Dance & theatre

The sources identify hoop dancing through the biography of Nakotah LaRance.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that the Tewa dual moiety has been preserved and that Tewa organization into clans is almost identical with Hopi organization.

The sources also mention cultural activities connected with language revitalization. The Santa Clara Pueblo Tewa Language Revitalization Program sponsors cultural activities, including visiting Crow Canyon. This information concerns Tewa-language revitalization in Santa Clara Pueblo rather than a documented Arizona Tewa ritual calendar.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources mention the Hopi-Tewa migration legend as a subject of scholarship, specifically in a bibliography entry describing its role in reinforcing cultural patterns and prescribing social behavior. They do not recount the legend or provide its characters, places, episodes, performance settings, or ceremonial uses.

Language preservation is more fully documented. Esther Martinez, who lived to be 94 years old, was nationally known for preserving the Tewa language. Her San Juan Pueblo Tewa Dictionary was published in 1982, and the Esther Martinez Native American Languages Preservation Act was named for her. Children’s stories in Tewa have been digitized by the University of New Mexico and made available online. A 2012 documentary film, The Young Ancestors, follows teenagers from Santa Fe Preparatory School learning Tewa through a self-study program with a mentor.

## Language & religion

Tewa is a Tanoan language spoken by Tewa Puebloan nations in the Rio Grande Valley in New Mexico north of Santa Fe and in Arizona. It is also known as Tano or Tée-wah, an archaic form. Arizona Tewa is mutually intelligible with Rio Grande Tewa only with difficulty. Many Hopi-Tewa speakers use Tewa, Hopi, and English; some also speak Spanish and/or Navajo.

The language has been written only since the 1960s, and writing conventions developed independently across the pueblos. Tewa can be written with the Latin script, but some Pueblo elders have held that it should be preserved through oral tradition alone. The language has 45 distinct individual sounds and four tones according to the Arizona Tewa language source, while its description of Rio Grande Tewa discusses high, low, and glide tones.

The 1980 census counted 1,298 Tewa speakers. The Endangered Languages Project is reported as estimating 1,500 speakers worldwide, including 1,200 in the New Mexico pueblos and 300 in the Arizona village of Hano. The source states that, as of 2012, UNESCO defined Tewa as “severely endangered” in New Mexico.
