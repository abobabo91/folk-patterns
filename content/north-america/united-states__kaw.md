## Overview

The Kaw Nation, also known as the Kanza or Kansa, is a federally recognized Native American tribe in Oklahoma and parts of Kansas. The Kaw historically lived in the central Midwestern United States, particularly along the Missouri and Kansas Rivers. The Kaw Nation’s headquarters is in Kaw City, Oklahoma, and its tribal jurisdictional area is within Kay County, Oklahoma. The tribe has 3,126 enrolled members, of whom 1,428 live within Oklahoma. Their language, Kansa, is a Siouan-Catawban language of the Dhegihan group. The state of Kansas was named for the tribe, whose history includes migration, warfare, forced removal, language loss, federal recognition, and contemporary language revitalization.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe one historical ceremonial episode. On June 1, 1868, during a confrontation with Cheyenne warriors on the Kaw reservation, Kaw men painted their faces, donned their finery, and rode out on horseback.

### Architecture

They record that the Kaw lived in villages, including a single large village near the future site of Doniphan, Kansas, and that their primary village between about 1790 and 1828 stood along the Kansas River near its confluence with the Big Blue River of Kansas. The Kaw Council House and cemetery at Washunga were later moved to Newkirk, Oklahoma after Kaw Lake inundated the area.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do mention wagons, horses, buffalo meat preserved by jerking, and buffalo robes in accounts of the Kaw’s 1873 move to Indian Territory.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They record that Kaw men painted their faces and donned their finery during the 1868 confrontation with Cheyenne warriors, without identifying the pigments, designs, garments, or associated ritual meanings.

## Music & performance

They do record a military pageant during the 1868 confrontation between the Kaw and Cheyenne. The event featured horsemanship, fearsome howls and curses, and volleys of bullets and arrows. The sources present this as a historical encounter rather than describing it as a named musical tradition.

Kansa language documentation provides evidence of oral and linguistic materials rather than a musical repertory. James Owen Dorsey collected 604 Kansa words in the 1880s and made about 25,000 entries in a Kansa-English dictionary that was never published. He also collected 24 narratives, historical accounts, and personal letters from nine Kansa speakers. Robert L. Rankin later made extensive recordings of Walter Kekahbah, Ralph Pepper, and Maud McCauley Rowe, who were described as the last surviving native speakers of Kansa in 1974.

## Dance & theatre

The 1868 encounter with Cheyenne warriors is described as a military pageant featuring horsemanship, fearsome howls and curses, and volleys of bullets and arrows. The account does not identify a Kaw dance or theatre form within this event.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe historical communal activities connected with subsistence, warfare, migration, and political life. The Kaw hunted bison annually along the Kansas River and made their last successful buffalo hunt in the winter after their 1873 removal to Indian Territory, traveling on horseback to the Great Salt Plains. They preserved the buffalo meat by jerking it and sold the buffalo robes for five thousand dollars.

The sources also record several political and life-history events. In 1825, the Kaw ceded land to the United States in exchange for an annuity. In 1846, they sold most of their remaining land and received a reservation centered on Council Grove, Kansas. On June 4, 1873, they left Kansas for a new reservation in Indian Territory. In 1922, Lucy Tayiah Eads, the adopted daughter of Washunga, was elected principal chief with a council of eight members. The Kaw received federal recognition and reorganized in 1959, ratified a new tribal constitution in 1990, and created a tribal court in 1992.

## Foodways

They state that the Kaw moved their primary village toward the Kansas River valley partly to remain closer to the bison herds they hunted annually. Their village was also near the Pawnee, with whom they were often in conflict.

The sources describe farming as a contested economic issue. Government agents and missionaries urged the Kaw to take up farming as their sole livelihood, but the Kaw were indifferent to these appeals. A disastrous flood in 1844 destroyed most of the land they had planted and left the tribe destitute. During the last successful buffalo hunt after removal to Indian Territory, the Kaw preserved buffalo meat by jerking it and sold buffalo robes. The sources also mention coffee and sugar as a peace offering made by Council Grove merchants to the Cheyenne after the 1868 confrontation.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kaw oral history states that the ancestors of the five Dhegiha tribes migrated west from the east, possibly from somewhere around the Ohio Valley. According to this tradition, the Quapaw separated from the other Dhegiha at the mouth of the Ohio, while the other Dhegiha traveled up the Mississippi and Missouri Rivers. The Osage left the main group in central Missouri; the Kaw halted upstream on the Missouri River in northwestern Missouri and northeastern Kansas; and the Omaha and Ponca continued north.

The Kansa language sources record 24 narratives, historical accounts, and personal letters collected by James Owen Dorsey from nine Kansa speakers. Dorsey also collected 604 Kansa words in the 1880s and made about 25,000 entries in an unpublished Kansa-English dictionary. Robert L. Rankin’s recordings and later work documented Kansa and helped the Kaw Nation develop language-learning materials. The Kaw Nation offers online language learning for Kansa second-language speakers, and the 2nd Annual Dhegiha Gathering in 2012 brought Kansa, Quapaw, Osage, Omaha, and Ponca speakers together to share language-revitalization practices.

## Language & religion

Kansa, whose native name is Kaáⁿze Íe, is also known as Kaw or Kanza. It is a Siouan-Catawban language in the Dhegihan group, alongside Quapaw, Omaha–Ponca, and Osage. Kansa is part of the Mississippi Valley Siouan languages, which belong to the Siouan-Catawban languages. The language was mostly spoken in Kansas.

Kansa has no native speakers. Walter Kekahbah, described as the last mother-tongue speaker and the last fluent speaker of Kansa, died in 1983. Members of the tribe now use English, while some still understand certain Kansa phrases and words. Kansa has 29 consonants and 8 vowels. It is a subject-object-verb language, and its verbs are inflected according to the person and number of their subjects and objects.
