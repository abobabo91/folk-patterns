## Overview

The Seneca were the westernmost nation of the Haudenosaunee and were called the Keepers of the Western Door. Their traditional life included longhouse villages, farming, hunting, fishing, and gathering. Seneca stories describe spirits, monsters, witches, magical power, and named figures such as Djieien and Gaasyendietha. Today, Seneca communities remain in western New York, Oklahoma, and Ontario, while language revitalization continues.

## Material culture

Seneca material culture includes longhouses, moccasins, headgear, wooden bowls, clubs, masks, rattles, brooches, bows, and arrows.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources do not document named Seneca textile or pattern traditions in enough detail.

### Clothing & dress

Seneca clothing included moccasins, leggings, coats, headgear, and ear ornaments.

- **Moccasins** — Museum records show moccasins made from native-tanned skin and decorated with porcupine quill, silk, or glass.
- **Feathered cornhusk hat** — Seneca men wore Haudenosaunee hats topped with dried cornhusks and one feather standing straight up.
- **Deerfoot’s clothing** (*Lewis Bennett*) — A portrait shows Lewis Bennett, also called Deerfoot, wearing moccasins, leggings, a fringed frock coat, and ear ornaments.
- **Corn-husk doll** — Museum records include a mother-and-child doll made from corn husk, cotton, wool, native-tanned skin, glass, and silk.

### Architecture

Seneca communities traditionally lived in large shared longhouses protected by wooden palisades.

- **Longhouse** — A longhouse could be up to 100 feet long, hold up to 60 people, and contain shared hearths in a central aisle.
- **Ganondagan** — Ganondagan had 150 longhouses and was the largest Seneca village of the seventeenth century.
- **Chenussio** — Chenussio had 130 longhouses and was a major village of the eighteenth century.
- **Wooden palisade** — Major Seneca villages were protected by wooden palisades.
- **Log cabin** — During the nineteenth century, many Seneca adopted log cabins similar to those of their American neighbors.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Seneca everyday objects included wooden vessels, a decorated club, and tools linked to farming, hunting, and fishing.

- **Wooden bowl** — A museum record documents a Seneca wooden bowl or vessel from New York.
- **Gunstock club** — A wooden gunstock club was decorated with brass nails and fitted with a pointed, flat iron blade.
- **Corn, beans, and squash** (*työhe'hköh*) — Corn, beans, and squash were the staple crops of the Haudenosaunee and were called the three sisters.
- **Hickory bow** — Seneca bows were made from unbacked hickory.
- **Hickory and dogwood arrows** — Arrows could be made from split hickory, dogwood, or Viburnum.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Seneca adornment included brooches and ear ornaments, while the sources give little detail about other body practices.

- **Masonic-cross brooch** — One Seneca or Haudenosaunee brooch was shaped like a masonic cross and had a pattern on its front.
- **Square brooch** — Another brooch was a simple square with the pin attached at one corner.
- **Ear ornaments** — Lewis Bennett’s portrait shows him wearing ear ornaments.

## Music & performance

Turtle-shell rattles were used in Seneca and Iroquois ceremonial life, including dances and the mask society.

- **Turtle-shell rattle** (*Kanyáhte’ ká’nowa’*) — Kanyáhte’ ká’nowa’ is a turtle-shell rattle associated with Seneca and Iroquois ceremonial life.
- **Tortoise-shell rattle** (*ga no wa gusta we sa*) — A catalogue records a tortoise-shell rattle used in dances and by the mask society.
- **Seneca-language radio music** (*WGWE*) — The former Seneca-owned station WGWE broadcast a small amount of Seneca-language music and featured a daily Seneca Word of the Day.

## Dance & theatre

Seneca ceremonial performance included the Great Feather Dance, mask-society dances, and healing ceremonies with masks.

- **Great Feather Dance** — Turtle-shell rattles were used in the Great Feather Dance.
- **False face mask** — Wooden false face masks were used by shamans and medicine men in secret healing ceremonies.
- **Mask-society dance** (*masksällskapet Seneca*) — A wooden mask record says masks were used in dances and ceremonies by the Seneca mask society.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do not give an annual festival calendar but describe healing, mask-society, dance, and clan traditions.

- **Mask society** — The mask society used rattles and masks in dances, ceremonies, and healing contexts.
- **Healing ceremony** — Shamans and medicine men used false face masks in secret ceremonies to heal sickness.
- **Great Feather Dance** — The Great Feather Dance was a ceremony in which turtle-shell rattles were used.
- **Clan mother** — In the matrilineal clan system, the presiding elder was called the clan mother.
- **Seneca clans** (*Bear, Wolf, Turtle, Beaver, Deer, Hawk, Snipe, and Heron*) — Children belonged to their mother’s clan, and the clans were divided between an animal side and a bird side.

## Foodways

Seneca subsistence centered on farming corn, beans, and squash, along with hunting, fishing, and gathering.

- **Three sisters** (*työhe'hköh*) — Corn, beans, and squash were the staple foods and were called the three sisters.
- **Medicinal plants** — Seneca women gathered and processed medicinal plants, roots, berries, nuts, and fruit.
- **Managed hunting grounds** — Men hunted and fished in productive grounds that were altered and maintained to encourage game.
- **Wooden bowl** — A museum catalogue records a Seneca wooden bowl, vessel, and bowl-and-spoon object.

## Oral tradition & literature

Seneca oral tradition includes origin stories, mythology, and six groups of tales written down from the nineteenth century onward.

- **Nundawao** — Seneca oral history says the people originated in Nundawao near the south end of Canandaigua Lake.
- **Seneca Myths and Folk Tales** — Arthur C. Parker published Seneca Myths and Folk Tales in 1923.
- **Eagentci** — Eagentci is the Earth-mother or First Mother.
- **Djieien** — Djieien is a man-sized spider.
- **Gaasyendietha** — Gaasyendietha is a fire-breathing dragon said to inhabit Lake Ontario.

## Language & religion

Seneca is an Iroquoian language with about one hundred native speakers, and revitalization efforts connect language learning with Seneca tradition.

- **Seneca language** (*Onödowaʼga꞉ʼ*) — Seneca is an Iroquoian language most closely related to Cayuga, Onondaga, Oneida, and Mohawk.
- **Orenda** — Seneca mythology describes orenda as magical power.
- **Seneca Faithkeepers School** — Founded in 1998, the Seneca Faithkeepers School teaches Seneca language and tradition five days a week.
- **Seneca language revitalization** — Revitalization efforts include schools, language-learning tools, and programs.
- **Iroquois religious society** — Museum records connect turtle-shell rattles and masks with the Iroquois religious society.

- *työhe'hköh* — The three sisters: corn, beans, and squash
- *Kanyáhte’ ká’nowa’* — A turtle-shell rattle
- *ga no wa gusta we sa* — A phrase recorded for a rattle
- *masksällskapet Seneca* — The Seneca mask society
- *Great Feather Dance* — A dance using turtle-shell rattles
- *clan mother* — The presiding elder of a clan
- *orenda* — Magical power
- *Nundawao* — The village named as the Seneca origin place
- *Eagentci* — The Earth-mother or First Mother
- *Djieien* — A man-sized spider
- *Gaasyendietha* — A fire-breathing dragon of Lake Ontario
