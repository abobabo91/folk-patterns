## Overview

The Quapaw, officially the Quapaw Nation, are a federally recognized tribe of about 6,000 citizens. Also known as the *Ogáxpa*, meaning “Downstream” people, they trace their ancestral homelands from the Ohio River west to the Mississippi River and present-day St. Louis, then south across Arkansas and eastern and southern Oklahoma. The Quapaw reached the confluence of the Arkansas and Mississippi rivers by at least the mid-17th century and were removed from Arkansas Territory in 1834. Today the tribal capital and headquarters are in Quapaw, Oklahoma. Their traditional language belongs to the Dhegiha branch of the Siouan language family, and community classes, cultural gatherings, traditional arts instruction, and language revitalization continue through the Quapaw Nation.

## Music & performance

The sources identify traditional singing as a subject of Quapaw cultural classes and describe several performance forms associated with the annual Quapaw dance and gathering. The gathering includes a **gourd dance**, **war dance**, **stomp dance**, **49s**, **turkey dance**, **Quapaw Dance**, and dances from other area tribes.

The annual dance is held during the Fourth of July weekend. It began as a gathering of local tribes with the Quapaw shortly after the American Civil War, continued as a traditional Quapaw dance and gathering, and later became a large contest powwow attracting participants from other tribes. The event includes traditional camping and gathering. The sources state that 2022 was the 150th anniversary of the dance being held annually in the same general location.

## Dance & theatre

The principal documented Quapaw dance tradition is the annual dance held during the Fourth of July weekend. Its program includes gourd dance, war dance, stomp dance, 49s, turkey dance, Quapaw Dance, and dances from other area tribes. The event combines traditional camping, gathering, contests, food, games, and dancing.

## Festivals & rituals

The Quapaw people’s primary annual event is a dance and gathering held during the Fourth of July weekend. It developed shortly after the American Civil War and is now funded by the Quapaw Nation government. The event includes traditional camping, a contest powwow, gourd dance, war dance, stomp dance, 49s, traditional Quapaw games, footraces, handgame, traditional dinners, turkey dance, Indian Dice, Quapaw Dance, and dances from other area tribes.

The Quapaw Nation also hosts the Quapaw Fall Gathering. It began in 2020 after the July 4th event had been postponed because of the Covid-19 pandemic and became an annual social event featuring historical and cultural discussions, traditional games, and traditional foods. In 2024, the gathering included a display of the 1824 Quapaw Treaty with the United States on the 200th anniversary of its signing, as well as speeches, Quapaw Jeopardy, an art competition, a 5k and fun run, a traditional Quapaw foot race, handgame, food, and a dice game.

## Foodways

The sources mention traditional Quapaw dinners and traditional foods at Quapaw gatherings. The annual July event includes traditional dinners, while the Quapaw Fall Gathering includes traditional foods.

## Oral tradition & literature

Quapaw oral history describes the separation of the Quapaw from the Omaha, Ponca, Osage, and Kaw near the confluence of the Missouri and Mississippi rivers because of a lack of game. The sources also discuss oral histories among related Dhegiha tribes and distinguish them from scholarly migration scenarios. They do document preservation efforts involving historical recordings, an online audio lexicon, language classes, and cultural education.

## Language & religion

Quapaw, also called *Ogáxpa* or *Okáxpa*, is a Siouan-Catawban language belonging to the Dhegiha branch of the Siouan language family. It is similar to Kansa, Omaha, Osage, and Ponca. Quapaw is considered a dormant language because it is no longer spoken at home and children do not learn it as their primary language.

The language was documented in fieldnotes and publications by George Izard, Lewis F. Hadley, James Owen Dorsey, Frank T. Siebert, and Robert Rankin. A formal writing system has not been adopted, and existing materials use different writing systems. Revitalization includes an online audio lexicon, language classes, the Quapaw Nation Language Program at the Robert Whitebird Cultural Center, a youth language and cultural preservation camp, and a permanent Language Department created in 2024.

The sources describe Catholic missions among the Quapaw, including the Mission of “Saint Mary of the Quapaws,” St. Joseph’s Church in Pine Bluff, Lazarist missionaries, and Jesuit work.
