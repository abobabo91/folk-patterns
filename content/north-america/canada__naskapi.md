## Overview

The Naskapi, also called Nascapi, Naskapee, and Nascapee, are an Indigenous people of the Subarctic historically associated with St'aschinuw, “our [inclusive] land,” in present-day northern Quebec near Nunavik. They are closely related to the Innu and traditionally lived as nomadic peoples, in contrast with the territorial Montagnais. Today, most Naskapi of the Naskapi Nation of Kawawachikamach reside in Kawawachikamach, Quebec, approximately 16 kilometres northeast of Schefferville. The Nation has approximately 850 registered First Nations people, and Naskapi is the principal language of the community. Subsistence hunting, fishing, and trapping remain important sources of food and raw materials, while harvesting is central to Naskapi spirituality. Their history also includes repeated relocations, the Northeastern Quebec Agreement, and the construction of Kawawachikamach between 1980 and 1983.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do state that, in 1948, a biologist observed Naskapi men, women, and children near Fort Chimo, with the women and children picking berries on the barrens within a three-mile radius.

### Architecture

They do describe several modern housing situations: shacks built at Pearce Lake from scavenged and donated materials; 30 houses built by Indian and Northern Affairs by 1962; row-housing units constructed at Matimekosh by 1972; and the present community of Kawawachikamach, built largely by Naskapi between 1980 and 1983.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do mention tents, houses, ammunition used for hunting, and the transport of large vehicles, gasoline and fuel oil, and refrigerated goods in the regional rail system.

## Music & performance

They do describe a spiritual figure called “Moose-Fly,” or *Məsəna´kʷ*, who was often accompanied by actual moose-flies during the summer salmon-fishing season. People were required to obey the spirit’s commands, including a taboo against making fun of fish because they had extra-large eyes. Salmon was a vital resource, and the spirit’s commands therefore carried considerable weight.

## Festivals & rituals

They describe seasonal activities connected with subsistence and spirituality. Salmon-fishing season occurred in the summer, and the Moose-Fly spirit’s commands governed conduct during that period. The Naskapi continue to rely substantially on hunting, fishing, and trapping, and harvesting is described as being at the heart of Naskapi spirituality.

## Foodways

The sources used describe a subsistence economy based on hunting, fishing, and trapping, with harvesting supplying a large part of the food supply and many raw materials. Salmon was a vital resource, and the sources also mention caribou hunting and fishing operations, commercialisation of caribou, berries gathered near Fort Chimo, fish and game required for food, and the importance of the George River Caribou Herd.

## Oral tradition & literature

They record several historical names and descriptions applied to the people, including “Ounackkapiouek,” “Annes-carps,” “Nascopies,” “Nascopie,” and “Nascappe.” The sources also explain that the word “Naskapi” appeared in the 17th century and was subsequently applied to Innu groups living beyond the reach of missionary influence, especially in lands bordering Ungava Bay and the inland area between Hudson’s Bay and the Torngat Mountain range.

## Language & religion

Naskapi is an Algonquian language spoken in Quebec and Labrador, Canada. It is also known as *ᐃᔪᐤ ᐃᔨᒧᐅᓐ* or Iyuw Iyimuun in the Naskapi language. Naskapi is a “y-dialect” with linguistic features shared with the Northern dialect of East Cree and lexical items shared with Innu, but it remains distinct from other language varieties in the Quebec-Labrador peninsula. It is written in Eastern Cree syllabics, and both Latin and Cree syllabic writing systems are used. The sources state that the Naskapi came under the influence of Protestant missionaries and remain Protestant, while also describing Moose-Fly as a spiritual influence connected with salmon fishing and behavioural taboos.
