## Overview

The Miami, or *Myaamiaki*, are an Indigenous people of the Northeastern Woodlands and a Great Lakes tribe historically associated with present-day north-central Indiana, southwest Michigan, and western Ohio. Their traditional capital was Kekionga, at the confluence of the Saint Joseph, Saint Marys, and Maumee rivers. Today, the Miami Tribe of Oklahoma is the federally recognized Miami government, while the Miami Nation of Indiana lacks federal recognition. The Miami–Illinois language belongs to the Algonquian branch of the larger Algic family. The sources describe Miami history, migration, language, place names, and revitalization efforts, but provide little information about material and performance traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources mention traditional stories, mythological narratives, an origin story, and religious material recorded by missionaries.

## Foodways

The sources do mention maize-based agriculture among Mississippian societies and historical Miami hunting.

## Oral tradition & literature

Miami oral history states that migration to the Great Lakes region was a return to an area where the people had long lived before displacement during the Beaver Wars. The sources also record mythological narratives, stories, traditional stories from the Miami and Peoria tribes, and a Miami origin story. These materials were documented by missionaries, linguists, and ethnographers, including Albert Gatschet, Truman Michelson, Charles Trowbridge, and Charles Voegelin. The Myaamia Center develops language and culture resources, and published materials include a children’s book, an audio CD set with vocabulary and stories, and a compilation of traditional stories. Many Miami members describe the language as “sleeping” rather than “extinct,” and revitalization work is associated with Daryl Baldwin and the Myaamia Center.

## Language & religion

Miami–Illinois, also called Mihtohseenian, is an Indigenous Algonquian language within the Algic family. Its Miami varieties were historically spoken by the Miami, Wea, and Piankeshaw in Indiana and adjacent regions. The language record includes Illinois and Miami dialects, with a later three-way distinction among Peoria, Miami proper, and Wea. French Catholic missionaries produced early written materials, including prayers, catechisms, word lists, and dictionaries. The last native speaker died in the 1960s according to the source, while the language was probably no longer natively spoken by the 1970s. As of 2016, around 500 tribal members were estimated to use it regularly.
