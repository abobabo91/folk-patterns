## Overview

The Comanche, or *Nʉmʉnʉʉ*, are a Native American tribe from the Southern Plains of the present-day United States. Their historic domain, called Comanchería by Spanish and later Mexican colonists, included present-day northwestern Texas and adjacent areas of eastern New Mexico, southeastern Colorado, southwestern Kansas, and western Oklahoma. The Comanche Nation is headquartered in Lawton, Oklahoma, and its jurisdictional area includes Caddo, Comanche, Cotton, Greer, Jackson, Kiowa, Tillman, and Harmon Counties. Approximately 17,000 people are enrolled in the Comanche Nation, around 7,000 of whom reside in tribal jurisdictional areas around Lawton, Fort Sill, and the surrounding areas of southwestern Oklahoma. The Comanche language is a Numic language of the Uto-Aztecan family. Comanche culture was shaped by nomadic horse use, bison hunting, trade, warfare, and social organization through families, bands, and divisions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The museum catalogue records do document woven and manufactured clothing, including a woven cape, woven wrap, woven shawl, manufactured poncho, and patterned dress. These records describe objects shown in studio portraits rather than explaining their techniques, regional distinctions, or pattern systems.

### Clothing & dress

The museum records show Comanche men and women wearing a variety of cloth, woven, manufactured, and European-style garments. A Comanche boy identified as Buffalo Hump’s son is described wearing a cloth shirt, a bone and shell breast plate, and a blanket wrapped around his waist. Milky Way, identified as a Comanche chief, wears a cloth shirt, neck tie, dentalium ear ornaments, and fur sash, and holds a bow and quiver of arrows. Ten Bears is shown in a cloth shirt, waistcoat, blanket around his waist, glasses, and peace medal. Gray Leggings is recorded both in a cloth shirt, waistcoat, hat, ear ornaments, and peace medal, and in a three-piece suit with neck tie, ear ornaments, and peace medal.

The catalogue records describe Comanche women wearing a cloth shirt, woven cape secured with metal fastenings, printed waistband, metal bracelets, woven wrap, and woven shawl. Other portraits show women wearing a patterned dress with a blanket around the waist, or a manufactured poncho and skirt with an abstract motif.

### Architecture

The sources state that Comanche campaign tents were made from bison hide and that, as late as 1725, large dogs were used rather than horses to carry them.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The catalogue records do not describe ceramics or a distinct Comanche ceramic tradition. They do document everyday and personal objects appearing in portraits: bows, quivers of arrows, canes, hats, glasses, peace medals, metal bracelets, metal fastenings, bone and shell breast plates, dentalium ear ornaments, and fur sashes. Timber Bluff is shown holding a cane, while Gray Leggings is shown holding a hat.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The museum catalogue records document dentalium ear ornaments, other ear ornaments, metal bracelets, a bone and shell breast plate, and a peace medal. Milky Way is shown with dentalium ear ornaments, while Gray Leggings is shown with ear ornaments and a peace medal. The sources also describe some of the men’s hair as plaited in the portrait of Timber Bluff.

## Music & performance

The Comanche Homecoming Annual Dance takes place in mid-July in Walters, Oklahoma. Each July, Comanches gather from across the United States in Walters for the annual Comanche Homecoming powwow. The Comanche Little Ponies host two annual dances, one over New Year’s Eve and one in May.

The Comanche language had a prominent wartime use during World War II. A group of 17 young men known as the Comanche Code Talkers was trained by the U.S. Army to send messages conveying sensitive information in Comanche so that the messages could not be deciphered by the enemy.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name the Comanche Homecoming Annual Dance, the annual Comanche Homecoming powwow, and two annual dances hosted by the Comanche Little Ponies. The Homecoming events take place in Walters, Oklahoma; the annual dance is held in mid-July, while the Little Ponies dances occur over New Year’s Eve and in May.

## Festivals & rituals

The Comanche Homecoming Annual Dance takes place in mid-July in Walters, Oklahoma. The annual Comanche Homecoming powwow is held each July, when Comanches gather from across the United States to celebrate their heritage and culture. The Comanche Nation Fair takes place every September. The Comanche Little Ponies host annual dances over New Year’s Eve and in May.

The Annual General Council is held every year on the third Saturday in April. At this gathering, the annual budget is approved, new leadership is nominated for election, and tribal members may present resolutions for a vote. The Comanche Nation’s cultural institutions include the Comanche National Museum and Cultural Center in Lawton, which opened to the public in 2007 and has permanent and changing exhibitions on Comanche history and culture.

## Foodways

The Comanche practiced a nomadic horse culture and hunted, particularly bison. Comanche group names include *Kʉhtsʉtʉhka* or Kotsoteka, “Buffalo Eaters”; *Penatʉka Nʉʉ* or Penateka, “Honey Eaters”; *Kwaarʉ Nʉʉ* or Kwahadi, “Antelope Eaters”; and *Yaparʉhka* or Yamparika, “Root Eaters.” Other recorded names include *Tanimʉʉ* or Tanima, “Liver Eaters,” *Sʉhmʉhtʉhka*, “Eat Everything,” and *Pekwi Tʉhka*, “Fish Eaters.” These names are presented in the source as group names or descriptions and do not constitute recipes or a complete account of Comanche foodways.

The sources state that Comanches traded horses and mules, hunted bison, and supplied horses to Anglo-American traders and other Native American middlemen. They also mention surplus meat in the explanation of the nickname *Itehtah'o*, “Burnt Meat,” because the meat was thrown out in spring, where it dried and became black.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources record traditional explanations of several group names and nicknames, including *Nʉmʉnʉʉ*, “the people” or “the human beings”; *Nokoninʉʉ* or Nokoni, “Movers, Returners”; and *Kwaarʉ Nʉʉ*, “Antelope Eaters.”

The Comanche Language and Cultural Preservation Committee offers dictionaries and language-learning materials. The Comanche Nation Language Department has published learning materials on Memrise, and the former Comanche Nation College conducted a language-recording project. The Comanche language is described as “mostly oral,” and the preservation work emphasizes instruction and recording.

## Language & religion

Comanche is a Numic language of the Uto-Aztecan family. The Comanche were once part of the Shoshone people of the Great Basin, and Comanche later diverged from Shoshoni and became a separate language. The language’s own name is *nʉmʉ tekwapʉ*, meaning “language of the people.” The Comanche autonym *nʉmʉnʉʉ* means “the human beings” or “the people.” The language has a conventional writing system whose alphabet was developed by Dr. Alice Anderton and adopted as the official Comanche Alphabet by the Comanche Nation in 1994.

Most Comanche speakers are elderly. The source states that there were roughly 25–30 native speakers in July 2013 and fewer than nine fluent native speakers as of 2022. The sources describe boarding-school suppression of Comanche and current preservation efforts.
