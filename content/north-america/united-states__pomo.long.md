## Overview

The Pomo are a Native American people of Northern California. Their historical territory extended from the Pacific Coast inland to Clear Lake, mainly between Cleone and Duncans Point, with one small group, the Tceefoka, near Stonyford in Colusa County. Pomo communities were organized into small groups or bands linked by lineage and marriage rather than as a unified political group. Pomo is a language family of seven distinct and mutually unintelligible languages, including Northern Pomo, Northeastern Pomo, Eastern Pomo, Southeastern Pomo, Central Pomo, Southern Pomo, and Kashaya. The 2010 United States Census recorded 10,308 Pomo people in the United States, including 8,578 in California. Pomo culture is especially documented for basket weaving, shell-bead production, fishing, hunting, gathering, ceremonial dance, and religious traditions associated with Kuksu or Guksu.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Pomo basket weaving** — Pomo baskets were made chiefly by women, although men also made baskets for fishing weirs, bird traps, and baby baskets. The three techniques named in the sources are plaiting, coiling, and twining.

Materials included swamp canes, rye grass, black ash, willow shoots, sedge roots, redbud bark, bulrush root, gray pine root, maiden fern, bird feathers, abalone shell, magnesite beads, and glass beads. Materials were dried, cleaned, split, soaked, and dyed; some were boiled over a fire and dried in the sun. Baskets served for cooking, storing and serving food, harvesting acorns, cradling children, religious ceremonies, and carrying people across rivers.

**Feathered baskets** — Pomo women sometimes covered baskets with vivid red feathers of the pileated woodpecker. The sources describe 30–50 feathers to every inch, beads sewn to the border, and polished abalone-shell pendants. Gift baskets could take months or years to make.

**Coiled basketry** — Museum records describe circular, bowl-shaped coiled baskets made of vegetable fibre, including examples with floral designs, black zig-zag decoration, shell discs, red glass beads, and mauve glass beads.

**Lattice turning** — A Museum of Ethnography record states that Pomo basket makers were known for “lattice tumning,” glossed there as lattice or galler work. The same record also mentions “vicker work,” plaiting, and coiling.

**Dau** — *Dau* is a basket design made by a small change in stitching that creates an opening between two stitches. It is also called the Spirit Door.

**Basketry for acorns** — The sources describe large conical burden baskets for harvesting acorns and basket forms used for cooking and serving food. One museum record identifies a basket bowl as intended for acorn crushing.

**Basketry with shells and beads** — Shell discs, clamshell beads, red glass beads, mauve glass beads, abalone shell, and magnesite beads appear in the descriptions of Pomo baskets and related objects.

**Motif vocabulary.** Dau, or Spirit Door — an opening between stitches through which good spirits could circulate and good or bad spirits could be released; floral design; black zig-zag decoration.

### Clothing & dress

The sources document ceremonial costume. The Kuksu or Guksu impersonators painted their bodies black, or striped red, white, and black, and wore bulky, feathery headdresses or a large feather tuft with a yellow headband. The nose of the impersonation was made with feathers and painted red. A Calnis dancer was painted entirely black, carried a black staff without feathers, and wore a feather cape over the face. Museum records describe head-dresses, feather headdresses, dance regalia, and a rattle decorated with dark and dyed bird feathers. A 1906 postcard records men painted, costumed, and prepared for ceremonies associated with an Upper Lake roundhouse performance.

### Architecture

Pomo communities lived in villages and small groups. Religious ceremonies took place in subterranean dance rooms, sweat houses, dance-houses, and an Upper Lake roundhouse. The six supernatural beings in Pomo ceremony and tradition were imagined to live in sweat houses or dance-houses at the ends of the world. The sources also mention a ghost town identified as Amacha, built for 100 people and later burned after its inhabitants were taken to government lands.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources describe wooden smoking-pipes, staffs used by ceremonial impersonators, double bone whistles, rattles made from cocoons attached to a wooden stave, millstones, handstones, bowls, mortars, pestles, shell beads, pendants, and soapstone objects. Museum records include basket bowls, a basket plate, a basket vessel without a bottom, game sticks with burned ornamentation, a horn dagger, and a wooden smoking-pipe.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Pomo jewelry was made from abalone and clamshells and was worn during celebrations and rituals or given as gifts. The sources also describe beads and pendants made from shell, magnesite, soapstone, and glass. Clamshell beads served as a major currency among peoples of Central California. Museum records list 83 and 88 shell coins, shell discs sewn with red glass beads, and baskets with polished abalone-shell pendants. Ceremonial body adornment included black, red, and white body paint, feathered headdresses, feather tufts, yellow headbands, feather capes, and feather-made noses.

## Music & performance

The sources document ceremonial performance and instruments. The Guksu impersonator carried a staff 6 to 8 inches long with a feather tuft and used a double bone whistle; he whistled but did not speak. Museum records describe a rattle made from moth cocoons arranged around a wooden stave and decorated with dark and dyed bird feathers. The rattle formed part of dance equipment.

The Kuksu religion included elaborate acting and dancing ceremonies in traditional costume, an annual mourning ceremony, puberty rites of passage, shamanic intervention with the spirit world, and an all-male society that met in subterranean dance rooms. The person who played Guksu was often considered the medicine man and could dress as Guksu when treating the sick. A 1906 postcard records five men prepared for an Upper Lake roundhouse performance described as religious in nature; the record identifies Jim Rickabaugh, Johnny Bull, Dick Green, “Snap” Bucknell, and Charles Rickabaugh.

## Dance & theatre

Pomo ceremonial performance included the Guksu ceremony, Kuksu ceremonies, acting, dancing, costuming, impersonation, and physical and mental tests. The Guksu ceremony lasted 6 days, with the dancers appearing once a day. One part, called “The Scarifying Ceremony,” initiated children ages 5 to 10 through tests administered by the dressed-up dancers.

Guksu impersonators represented a healing spirit from the south. Calnis dancers were associated with Guksu and pursued people and “tripped them up.” Museum records describe Pomo men in dance regalia and an Upper Lake roundhouse performance.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources document an annual mourning ceremony, puberty rites of passage, shamanic intervention, the Guksu ceremony, and the “Scarifying Ceremony.” The Guksu ceremony lasted 6 days, and its dancers appeared once a day. During the Scarifying Ceremony, children ages 5 to 10 underwent physical and mental tests administered by dancers in ceremonial dress.

Pomo religious practice included an all-male society that met in subterranean dance rooms. The Pomo believed in Kuksu or Guksu, a supernatural being who lived in the south and came during ceremonies to heal illness, as well as spirits from six cardinal directions and Coyote as ancestor and creator god.

## Foodways

Fishing, hunting, and gathering supplied daily food. The sources name salmon, wild greens, gnats, mushrooms, berries, grasshoppers, rabbits, rats, and squirrels. Acorns were the most important staple. Women generally gathered and prepared plant foods, while men hunted and fished.

Acorns were processed with mortars and pestles and were harvested in large conical burden baskets. Archaeological sites contained bowls, mortars, pestles, millstones, and handstones for grinding seeds and nuts. Basketry was used to store, cook, and serve food, including in some watertight forms.

## Oral tradition & literature

The record of Pomo myths, legends, tales, and histories is described as extensive and is classed within the Central California cultural pattern. Creation stories tell of Coyote, also called Kunula, Cougar, Lizard or Hatanutal, the Sun, and the formation of human beings, light, the sun, the moon, fingers, and language.

In one story, the bodies of Coyote’s two sons became the physical sun and moon. In another, Coyote and Lizard wrestled over whether people should have fingers; Lizard won and people received fingers and language. The sources also describe Coyote as ancestor and creator god. Bole Maru continued to be practiced after other Ghost Dance movements declined, and Mabel McKay, described as the last Pomo dreamer, died in 1993. The Clear Lake Pomo Cultural Preservation Foundation is identified as working to revive xay tsnu.

## Language & religion

Pomo, also known as Pomoan or less commonly Kulanapan, is a language family with seven distinct and mutually unintelligible languages: Northern Pomo, Northeastern Pomo, Eastern Pomo, Southeastern Pomo, Central Pomo, Southern Pomo, and Kashaya. The languages became severely endangered after European-American colonization, and many declined through language shift to English. The sources state that about twelve Pomo language varieties are still in use. One variety, xay tsnu, spoken by Elem Pomo, is undergoing revival.

Pomo religious traditions included shamanism and the Kuksu religion. Guksu or Kuksu was a healing spirit from the south. The Pomo also recognized spirits associated with the four cardinal directions, the sky, and the earth, and regarded Coyote as ancestor and creator god.
