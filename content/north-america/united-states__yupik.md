## Overview

The Yupik are Indigenous peoples of Alaska and the Russian Far East who organized their lives around seasonal hunting and fishing. They developed a rich material culture of waterproof gut-skin clothing, woven grass items, and carved ivory and bone objects. Their ceremonial and social life centered on the qasgiq, a winter men's communal house where families gathered for singing, dancing, storytelling and instruction in survival skills.

## Material culture

Yupik objects were made from natural materials—seal gut, grass, ivory, bone and wood—shaped and decorated with engraved geometric designs, painted images and dyed accents.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Yupik wove and sewed natural fibers and seal gut, decorating them with geometric patterns and dyed accents.

- **Gut-skin coat** — A hooded coat sewn from translucent seal gut with red and black dyed strips marking the seams and a darker band running down from each shoulder.
- **Woven grass dance fans** — Women's dance fans woven of grass fibers in natural color with red-dyed accents, a triangular handle, caribou hair and red and green wool tassels.
- **Incised ring-and-dot ornament** — Geometric decoration on bone and ivory using small circles and rings in an engraved pattern.

### Clothing & dress

The kuspuk was the main traditional Yupik garment worn by both men and women; for water work, translucent waterproof gut-skin coats were worn.

- **Kuspuk** (*qaspeq*) — A traditional garment worn by both genders in casual and formal settings in Alaska.
- **Gut-skin hooded coat** — A waterproof coat sewn from seal gut with dyed strips marking seams, worn for work on water and in wet weather.
- **Women's dance fans** — Paired handheld fans woven of grass with caribou hair and wool tassels, used to accentuate upper-body gestures in group dances.
- **Men's dance fans** — Paired wooden hoops with white feathers spotted with brown, used to accentuate upper-body gestures in group dances.

### Architecture

Two communal houses—the men's qasgiq and the women's ena—stood side by side and organized village life and seasonal instruction.

- **Men's communal house** (*qasgiq*) — The community center for winter ceremonies, singing, dancing, storytelling and hunting instruction, where boys learned survival skills and toolmaking over the winter months.
- **Women's communal house** (*ena*) — Stood next to the qasgiq, sometimes connected by a tunnel; women taught girls hide-tanning, sewing, food processing and weaving.
- **Seasonal settlement pattern** — Families moved with food sources—spring and summer at fish camp, winter gathered at village sites with the communal houses.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Yupik made everyday objects from ivory, bone, wood and gut rather than clay or metal, decorating them with engraved geometric designs and painted images.

- **Story knife** — A walrus-ivory knife carved in one piece with engraved geometric designs and a carved salmon-head terminal, used by girls for storytelling.
- **Needle-case** — A carved bone cylinder open at both ends with incised ring-and-dot ornament, fitted with a wooden plug stopper and leather thong to hold needles.
- **Painted wooden paddle** — A paddle painted on both sides with images of whales, fish, birds and skeletonized human figures.
- **Seal-oil lamp** (*naniq*) — An important piece of household furniture that fueled light in the home.
- **Kayak** (*qayaq*) — A traditional boat built by men during winter months in the qasgiq.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used do not describe Yupik jewelry, tattooing or hair practices.

## Music & performance

Yupik music was built on frame drums, featured recitative-like singing with small melodic range and complex rhythm, and was used in spiritual ceremonies and hunting magic.

- **Frame drum** (*qilaut*) — A wooden circular drum made by boiling and bending wood strips into a frame with animal skin, usually caribou, stretched across and fastened with string; struck on the rim with a wooden beater.
- **Drum beater** (*qatuk*) — A wooden stick used to strike the rim of the qilaut frame drum.
- **One-string fiddle** (*kelutviaq*) — A lute-like instrument played specifically by Yupʼik of Nelson Island and southwest Alaska.
- **Accordion and country music** — European instruments and Scots-Irish-derived American country music brought by whalers became popular in the 20th century.

## Dance & theatre

Yupik group dances kept the body mostly still while moving the upper body and arms with rhythmic gestures accentuated by handheld fans.

- **Group dance** — Dancers stayed largely stationary while moving the upper body and arms rhythmically, with expressions ranging from flowing and graceful to energetic or humorous.
- **Dance fans** — Paired handheld instruments—woven grass with caribou hair for women, carved wooden hoops with spotted feathers for men—used to accentuate upper-body gestures.

## Festivals & rituals

The Yupik ceremonial year followed the subsistence year, with winter gatherings in the qasgiq featuring singing, dancing, storytelling and shamanic ceremonies.

- **Winter ceremonies and festivals** — Held in the qasgiq when families gathered at village sites, featuring singing, dancing, storytelling and spiritual guidance from a shaman.
- **Naming after the recently deceased** — A unique Yupik practice of naming children after the most recent person in the community to have died.
- **Three-to-six-week skill exchange** — A formal stage in growing up where boys learned women's skills and girls learned men's survival and hunting skills and toolmaking.

## Foodways

Yupik moved seasonally with food sources, harvesting Pacific salmon and seal that provided both meat and materials for clothing and light.

- **Seasonal movement** — Families moved in groups—spring and summer at fish camp, winter in villages—following food sources through the year.
- **Pacific salmon** — A traditional subsistence resource still harvested by many Yupik families, also depicted on carved story knives and painted paddles.
- **Seal** — Traditional subsistence resource that provided meat, gut for waterproof clothing and oil to fuel household lamps.

## Oral tradition & literature

Storytelling was a central winter activity in the qasgiq, and the story knife was a girl's tool for performing narratives.

- **Story knife** — A walrus-ivory implement engraved with geometric designs and a carved salmon-head terminal, used by girls to illustrate stories told in the communal house.
- **Winter storytelling** — One of three main activities—with singing and dancing—that filled winter gatherings in the qasgiq.
- **Religious translation** — Moravian missionaries in the late 19th century translated Christian scriptures into Yupik for use in church services.

## Language & religion

Five Yupik languages are still widely spoken; Yupʼik is the most spoken Native language in Alaska and the second most in the United States after Navajo.

- **Yupʼik language** (*Yugtun*) — The most spoken Native language in Alaska by both population and speakers, with around 20,000 speakers still speaking it at home in 2013; name means 'real people'.
- **Cupʼik dialect** (*Cupʼig*) — The Hooper Bay/Chevak and Nunivak Island dialect of Yupik; both the language and people are known by this name in those areas.
- **Moravian missionaries** — Brought Latin-alphabet writing to Yupik in the late 19th century and used the language in church services.
- **Eastern Orthodox Christianity** — Present in Yupik communities, represented by Saint Olga Michael (1916–1979), a matushka canonized in 2023 by the Orthodox Church in America.
- **Shamanism** — Traditional spiritual practice central to Yupik ceremonial life and winter gatherings.

## Glossary

- *qaspeq* — traditional Yupik garment worn by both men and women
- *qasgiq* — men's communal house, center for winter ceremonies and instruction
- *ena* — women's communal house, center for teaching girls domestic and craft skills
- *qayaq* — kayak; traditional boat built during winter
- *qilaut* — wooden frame drum used for dance music
- *qatuk* — wooden beater used to strike the qilaut drum
- *naniq* — seal-oil lamp, important household furniture
- *kelutviaq* — one-string fiddle or lute played in southwest Alaska
- *Yugtun* — General Central Yupʼik dialect, most widely spoken
- *Cupʼik* — Hooper Bay/Chevak and Nunivak Island dialect of Yupik
- *matushka* — Eastern Orthodox priest's wife
- *Yupʼik* — 'real people' (yuk = person, -pik = real/genuine)
- *story knife* — walrus-ivory implement used by girls for storytelling
- *ring-and-dot ornament* — geometric engraved pattern of circles and rings
