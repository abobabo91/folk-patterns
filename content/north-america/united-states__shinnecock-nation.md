## Overview

The Shinnecock Indian Nation is a federally recognized tribe of historically Algonquian-speaking Native Americans based at the eastern end of Long Island, New York. The tribe is headquartered in Suffolk County, on the southeastern shore, and its landbase is the Shinnecock Reservation within the geographic boundaries of the Town of Southampton. In 2012, the Shinnecock Nation numbered more than 1,400 people, with more than half residing on the reservation. Their name roughly translates as “people of the stony shore.” The Shinnecock are Indigenous to Long Island and have long been associated with fishing, sailing, whaling, wampum production, language renewal, traditional dancing, beadwork, crafts, music, ceremonies, and the annual Shinnecock Powwow.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Wampumpeag** — *Wampumpeag*, also called wampum, consists of shell beads strung onto threads. The beads served as currency, for record-keeping, for aesthetic purposes, and to symbolize a family. Long Island Indians were generally thought to be the largest producers of wampum in the colonial era, and the Indians of Long Island were reputed to have made the best wampumpeag among New England tribes.

The sources describe wampum as a material and exchange tradition rather than as a named pattern vocabulary. The beads were made from shells, and treaties reserved access to places where shells could be gathered for their manufacture.

### Clothing & dress

The Cultural Enrichment Program is described as involving the sharing of knowledge about clothing. The sources do state that the Shinnecock Native American Cultural Coalition studied traditional dancing, beadwork, Native American crafts, and music. They also describe a process through which food, clothing, arts, crafts, dance, ceremonies, and language are shared so that the ideals and traditions of ancestors are passed down through generations.

### Architecture

The Shinnecock Reservation is described as a self-governing reservation whose current borders of 800 acres were established by 1859. It contains a museum, shellfish hatchery, education center, cultural and community center, playground, and Presbyterian church. The reservation is three miles west of the village of Southampton, New York.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do identify several objects connected with Shinnecock material culture and working life. Wampumpeag was made from shell beads strung onto threads and used as currency, for record-keeping, aesthetic purposes, and family symbolism. A bull rake is a large clam-harvesting tool created in the mid-1800s; it is also called a “Shinnecock rake.” The sources additionally describe shellfish gathering as connected with the production of wampum.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They do mention beadwork as one of the subjects studied by the Shinnecock Native American Cultural Coalition, but provide no further description of bead types, ornaments, symbolic meanings, or techniques.

## Music & performance

Music is included in the Shinnecock Native American Cultural Coalition’s arts and crafts program, formed in 1972. The sources state that traditional dancing, beadwork, Native American crafts, and music are studied. A group called The Youngblood Singers was formed to learn traditional Algonquian songs, chants, and drum rituals. The group travels throughout the Northeast performing at powwows and drum contests.

The sources also describe the Shinnecock Powwow, which is held every Labor Day Weekend on the reservation. The event is based on ceremonies beginning in 1912. In 2008, the powwow attracted 50,000 visitors, and USA Today ranked it among the ten great powwows held in the United States.

## Dance & theatre

The Shinnecock Native American Cultural Coalition studies traditional dancing, and the Cultural Enrichment Program includes dance among the subjects shared within the community. The sources connect performance with powwows and drum contests, especially through The Youngblood Singers, who travel throughout the Northeast. The Shinnecock Powwow takes place every Labor Day Weekend and is based on ceremonies beginning in 1912.

## Festivals & rituals

The principal annual event described in the sources is the Shinnecock Powwow, held on the reservation every Labor Day Weekend since 1946. It is based on ceremonies beginning in 1912. The powwow includes traditional dancing, songs, chants, drum rituals, music, and other forms of Native American cultural expression. In 2008, it attracted 50,000 visitors and was ranked by USA Today among the ten great powwows held in the United States.

The sources describe the Cultural Enrichment Program as a sharing and learning process intended to pass ancestral ideals and traditions through the generations. It includes food, clothing, arts, crafts, dance, ceremonies, and language. The sources also state that The Youngblood Singers perform at powwows and drum contests throughout the Northeast.

## Foodways

They do state that the Shinnecock were historically connected with planting maize, fishing, shellfish gathering, and whaling. A Dutch official described Long Island Indians as supporting themselves by planting maize and making sewan, or wampum.

The Shinnecock were long fishermen and sailors around Long Island. Their history also includes shellfish harvesting, reflected in the bull rake, a large clam-harvesting tool created in the mid-1800s. The reservation has a shellfish hatchery.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do identify language renewal programs intended to secure the legacy of the Shinnecock language for future generations.

Cockenoe, a Montaukett/Shinnecock captured during the Pequot War in 1637, worked with John Eliot in Boston in the 1640s to translate the first parts of the Eliot Indian Bible before returning to Long Island. This is the only specific literary or translation activity described in the sources.

The Cultural Enrichment Program shares knowledge of language, ceremonies, arts, crafts, dance, food, and clothing across generations. The Youngblood Singers learn traditional Algonquian songs, chants, and drum rituals.

## Language & religion

The Shinnecock are historically Algonquian-speaking Native Americans. They are believed to have spoken a dialect of Mohegan-Pequot-Montauk, similar to that of the Montaukett on Long Island. The Shinnecock language was not allowed to be spoken in schools, and its use was highly frowned upon off the reservation. This contributed to a decline in the number of speakers, while the tribe is actively engaged in language renewal programs.

The sources place the Shinnecock within the wider Algonquian world of Long Island and southern New England. They describe political and historical relationships with the Pequot and Narragansett and cultural connections with neighboring Long Island peoples. The reservation has a Presbyterian church.
