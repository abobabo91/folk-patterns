## Overview

The Walla Walla, also called Walawalałáma or Walúulapam, are a Sahaptin Indigenous people of the Northwest Plateau. Their traditional territory lay along the Walla Walla River and near the confluence of the Snake and Columbia rivers, in areas now part of northeastern Oregon and southeastern Washington. Many live on the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation near Pendleton, Oregon, sharing land and governmental structure with the Cayuse and Umatilla; others are enrolled in the Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakama Nation. The name Walla Walla is most often translated as “many waters.” Their seasonal subsistence practices, Sahaptin language, historical riverine territory, and participation in the 1855 Walla Walla Council are the principal folk-cultural subjects covered by the sources.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state only that Lewis and Clark gave Yellepit a peace medal intended to be worn around the neck.

### Architecture

They mention Yellepit’s village on the Columbia River near the mouth of the Walla Walla River and the proposed establishment of a trading post nearby.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

In an 1805 transaction, Yellepit presented Clark with a white horse in return for a copper kettle; Clark instead gave Yellepit his own sword, gunpowder, and musket balls.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They mention a peace medal engraved with a portrait of President Thomas Jefferson, which Lewis and Clark gave Yellepit to wear around his neck.

## Music & performance

The Walla Walla language article states that Sahaptin has published grammars, a recent dictionary, and a corpus of published texts.

## Festivals & rituals

They record the 1855 Walla Walla Council, at which the Walla Walla were one of the tribal nations that signed the Treaty of Walla Walla alongside the Cayuse, Nez Perce, Umatilla, and Yakama.

## Foodways

They state that the Walla Walla followed seasonal subsistence practices similar to those of the Yakama, Palouse, Umatilla, and Wanapum, but provide no further foodways detail.

## Oral tradition & literature

The language article identifies published texts and language documentation, including Virginia Beavert’s *Wántwint Inmí Tiináwit: A Reflection of What I Have Learned*. The Yakama Nation tribal cultural resources program has promoted the traditional name Ichishkíin Sɨ́nwit for the language.

## Language & religion

The Walla Walla are described as a Sahaptin-speaking people. Sahaptin, also called Ichishkiin, belongs to the Sahaptian branch of the Plateau Penutian family and is spoken in parts of southern Washington, northern Oregon, and southwestern Idaho. In the Walla Walla and Umatilla dialects, the language is called Chishkín; the Umatilla forms include Čiškíin and Ičiškíin. The sources describe a writing system used for Umatilla Sahaptin and note that other works use the Yakima practical alphabet. Sahaptin has split ergative syntax, direct-inverse voicing, and applicative constructions.
