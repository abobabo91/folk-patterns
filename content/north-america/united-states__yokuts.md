## Overview

The Yokuts live in California's Central Valley, especially around the San Joaquin Valley. Before European contact, they included up to 60 tribes speaking related Yokuts languages. Their material culture is known for basketmaking, woven designs, feathers, tattoos, and piercings. Their religious life includes ceremonies, creation stories, symbolic birds, and shamans.

## Material culture

Yokuts material culture centers on baskets made for art, food serving, games, gifts, and ceremonies, along with fishing and hunting tools and body art.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Yokuts basketmakers used coil techniques, woven feathers, and dark geometric designs.

- **Coiled basket** — Basin-shaped baskets were made by the coil technique and could have black geometric designs or double zigzag bands.
- **Feathered basket** — Some baskets used quail feathers, while another bottle-neck basket had black feathers woven around its widest part.
- **Ceremonial treasure basket** — A treasure basket stored gifts and ceremonial objects and could itself be given as a gift.
- **Grass basket** — A much-used grass basket may have served as a bowl for food.
- **Basket tray** (*Dice Tray*) — A tall basketry tray had black and dark-brown patterns and was linked with the Yokut and Tulare Indians.

### Clothing & dress

The sources do not describe Yokuts clothing or named garments.

### Architecture

The sources do not describe Yokuts houses or other architecture.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources describe baskets, hunting and fishing tools, and marine shells used as money.

- **Food-serving basket** — Some baskets were used as bowls for serving food.
- **Dice tray** — A basket tray was identified as a dice tray.
- **Gift basket** (*treasure basket*) — A treasure basket held gifts and ceremonial objects.
- **Fishing spear** — Yokuts used spears for fishing and hunting.
- **Basket trap** — Basket traps were used in fishing and hunting.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Tattoos and piercings were forms of Yokuts artistic expression.

- **Tattoos** — Tattoos were a form of bodily artistic expression.
- **Piercings** — Piercings were a form of bodily artistic expression.

## Music & performance

Music and singing were Yokuts artistic expressions connected with religious life.

- **Music** — Music was a form of Yokuts artistic expression.
- **Singing** — Singing was a form of Yokuts artistic expression.
- **Shamanic ceremony** (*shaman*) — Shamans helped conduct ceremonies and were believed to have supernatural powers.

## Dance & theatre

The sources do not name Yokuts dances or theatre traditions, but eagle and crow remained symbolic religious figures.

- **Eagle symbol** — The eagle continued to be a symbolic figure in Yokuts religious ceremonies.
- **Crow symbol** — The crow continued to be a symbolic figure in Yokuts religious ceremonies.

## Festivals & rituals

Yokuts religious life included an annual mourning rite, a first-fruit rite, and creation stories.

- **Annual mourning rite** — The annual mourning rite was one of two important Yokuts religious ceremonies.
- **First-fruit rite** — The first-fruit rite was one of two important Yokuts religious ceremonies.
- **Creation story** — In a common creation story, an eagle and a crow asked a duck to bring mud from the water, and the mud became the land of the Yokuts.
- **Shamanic healing** (*shaman*) — Shamans were believed to treat illness and could use their powers for good or evil.

## Foodways

Acorn mash was the Yokuts staple food, supported by gathered plants, hunted animals, fishing, and seasonal foods.

- **Acorn mash** — Acorn mash was the Yokuts staple food.
- **Tule-root flour** — Yokuts made flour from tule roots.
- **Wild iris flour** — Yokuts made flour from wild iris bulbs.
- **Wasp grubs** — Wasp grubs were harvested as a seasonal delicacy and source of protein.
- **Manzanita berries** — Yokuts foraged manzanita berries along with pine nuts and other seeds.

## Oral tradition & literature

Yokuts oral tradition includes creation stories, a first-person account, and efforts to preserve Chukchansi in writing.

- **Creation story** — Yokuts creation stories commonly describe a world covered by water until mud brought by a duck became the land.
- **Yoimut account** (*Yoimut*) — A first-person account attributed to Yoimut describes Yokuts leaving after fearing that white settlers would burn them out.
- **Chukchansi alphabet** (*Chukchansi*) — Chukchansi became a written language with its own alphabet developed through a federal grant.
- **Chukchansi dictionary** (*Chukchansi*) — Efforts included compiling a Chukchansi dictionary and grammar texts and assembling native texts.
- **Chukchansi curriculum** (*Chukchansi*) — A curriculum was being created to teach the Chukchansi language.

## Language & religion

Yokuts is an endangered language group with several named languages and dialects, and religious practice includes rites, stories, symbols, and shamans.

- **Yawelmani** — Yawelmani is described as the only surviving branch of Valley Yokuts.
- **Choynimni** — Choynimni became extinct in 2017.
- **Wikchamni** — Wikchamni was being taught to at least a few children during the first decade of the 21st century.
- **Chukchansi** — Chukchansi was being taught to at least a few children during the first decade of the 21st century.
- **Tachi** — Tachi was being taught to at least a few children during the first decade of the 21st century.
