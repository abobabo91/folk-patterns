## Overview

The Esselen are an Indigenous people of the Santa Lucia Mountains and the Central California coast, in the region south of the Big Sur River. Their territory included the upper Carmel and Arroyo Seco rivers, the Big Sur coast from near Hurricane Point to the vicinity of Vicente Creek, and inland areas extending toward the Salinas Valley. Before Spanish imperialization, they lived seasonally between the coast and inland districts, relying on seafood, acorns, wildlife, and other locally available foods. Estimates of their population at the time of Spanish invasion range from 500 to 1,285 individuals. The Esselen language was probably a language isolate, although it has been included in the hypothetical Hokan family. The people are significant to folk-culture studies because archaeological evidence, historical records, surviving vocabulary, settlement remains, food practices, basketry, clothing descriptions, and rock imagery document a small California population whose culture was severely disrupted by Spanish missions.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Before European invasion, Esselen men were described as naked throughout the year. Women and girls may have worn a small apron. In cold weather, people could cover themselves with mud or with rabbit- or deerskin capes. Pedro Fages described a short apron made from red and white cords twisted and worked closely together, extending to the knee. Other women used interwoven green and dry tule, together with a partly tanned or untanned deerskin underskirt. No evidence of sandals or other footwear has been found.

### Architecture

The sources contain few contemporary records of Esselen houses and describe their dwellings partly through comparison with neighboring California peoples. Researchers state that the Esselen lived in a manner similar to the Ohlone and Costonoan. Dome-shaped dwellings covered with bundled tule mats were described as reaching 55 feet (17 m) across and housing three or four families. Such houses had a central fire pit and a roof vent or chimney. In mountainous areas where redwoods grew, the people may have built conical houses from redwood bark attached to wooden frames. A sweat lodge was built low into the ground, with earth walls and a roof of earth and brush. The sources also describe tule boats propelled with double-bladed paddles for travel on bays.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do document baskets, stone mortars and pestles, bone awls, antler flakers, projectile points, scrapers, bone tubes, gaming pieces, and tule boats. Baskets probably furnished households and included large conical burden baskets, hemispherical cooking bowls, flat trays, and small boat-shaped baskets that may have served as seed-beaters. Bedrock mortars were used to grind acorns and plant seeds into flour. At Apple Tree Camp, a boulder more than 9 feet (2.7 m) across contains a dozen or more deep mortar bowls worn into the rock over several generations.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They do record trade in shells and beads.

## Music & performance

Jean La Pérouse recorded 22 Esselen words in 1786, Dionisio Alcalá Galiano recorded 107 words and phrases in 1792, and Felipe Arroyo de la Cuesta recorded another 58 words and 14 phrases in 1832. These records concern language rather than music. The sources also preserve descriptions of Esselen and neighboring peoples.

## Dance & theatre

Archaeological sites contain pictographs in black, white, and red, and a rock shelter near Tassajara contains about 250 handprints.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe seasonal movement and several practices connected with settlement and food. The people lived near the coast in winter, when they harvested mussels, limpets, abalone, and other sea life, and moved inland in summer and fall to gather acorns. Certain areas around Slates Hot Springs were reserved as burial grounds. Esselen villages and districts were occupied according to the availability of food, water, and shelter.

Spanish mission records describe forced baptism and relocation rather than Indigenous ritual practice. Junípero Serra baptized Pach-hepas, identified as the 40-year-old chief of Excelen, on May 9, 1775. The priests gave baptized people new Christian names and required many to live under mission rules. The sources report that children were baptized during 1776 and later years, while additional baptisms occurred in the 1790s and from 1804 to 1808.

## Foodways

The Esselen remained hunter-gatherers and did not develop agriculture. Their seasonal food system followed the distribution of coastal and inland resources. In winter they lived near the coast and harvested mussels, limpets, abalone, and other sea life. In summer and fall they moved inland to gather acorns, a staple food abundant throughout Big Sur. They collected acorns from black oak, canyon live oak, and tanbark oak, especially on upper slopes above narrow canyons.

Acorns were soaked in running water to leach out bitter tannin, then ground with stone mortars into flour. The flour was cooked into a mush or baked as bread. Bedrock mortars were hollowed into granite outcrops over many generations, and other mortars have been found at campsites and near Clover Basin Camp in Miller Canyon. The Esselen also hunted rabbits and deer, probably with bows and arrows. Arrows were made from cane and pointed with hardwood foreshafts. Historical descriptions mention seeds, game, bear, cattle, bisons, and other animals. The people traded acorns, fish, salt, baskets, hides, pelts, shells, and beads with neighboring tribes.

## Oral tradition & literature

Historical observers recorded information about the people and their language, but the surviving linguistic material consists chiefly of word lists, phrases, a few complete sentences, and a short bilingual catechism. Jean La Pérouse recorded Esselen words during his visit to Monterey in 1786. Dionisio Alcalá Galiano recorded words and phrases in 1792, and Felipe Arroyo de la Cuesta recorded material at Mission Soledad in 1832.

The Esselen language was called *Huelel* by the people themselves. Examples recorded in the sources include *mamamanej* for “fire,” *koxlkoxl* for “fish,” and *ni-tsch-ekė* for “my husband.” Isabel Meadows, who also spoke Rumsen, was described as the last fluent Esselen speaker; she died in 1939.

## Language & religion

Esselen was probably a language isolate and has also been assigned to the hypothetical Hokan family. The language was spoken in the northern Santa Lucia Range and consisted of Western and Eastern dialects. Western Esselen was documented at Mission Carmel and associated with the Excelen local tribe and possibly the Ecgeajan local tribes. Eastern Esselen was documented at Mission Soledad and associated with the Eslenajan local tribe and probably the Aspasniajan and Imunajan local tribes. Each of five local tribes is described as having its own subdialect. Esselen word order was primarily subject-object-verb.

They record handprints on rock faces, including about 250 in one rock shelter, and identify certain areas as burial grounds. During the mission period, Spanish priests forcibly baptized Esselen people, imposed Christian names, and placed baptized people under mission authority.
