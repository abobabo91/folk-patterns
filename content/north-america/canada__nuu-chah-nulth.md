## Overview

The Nuu-chah-nulth are Indigenous peoples living on the west coast of Vancouver Island, their name meaning "all along the mountains and sea." Fifteen related tribes are described by this collective term, adopted in 1979. They are renowned as one of the few Pacific Coast peoples who hunted whales and as the origin of the word potlatch. Their material culture, language, and ceremonies reflect deep ties to the sea and forest.

## Material culture

The Nuu-chah-nulth made objects from cedar, bone, stone, whalebone and wood, with patterns and imagery centered on whaling and animal spirits.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Weaving here is fibre work in cedar, spruce root and grass rather than loom cloth, with patterns showing whaling scenes and formline designs.

- **Chief's whaling hat** — A conical twined hat of cedar bark with an onion-dome shape at the top, the upper half worked in imbricated stripes and the lower half carrying whaling scenes, with a hide strap headband tied inside.
- **Cedar-bark capes and robes** — Fringed capes and cloaks made of cedar bark and nettle fibre, some combined with mountain-goat wool, finished with borders of sea otter fur and painted designs.
- **Rain hats** — Brimless, dome-shaped hats twined from the bark fibre of giant cedar, the crown started in plain weave and the rest worked in tight twining, with a decorative band.
- **Basketry mats** — Oval and circular mats made from grass, using red, purple, green, orange and black dyed grass to show whaling scenes and geometric patterns.
- **Basketry-covered glass** — A discarded clear glass bottle sheathed in twined basketry with a cedar-bark warp and grass weft, banded with zones of dyed geometric lines and zigzags.

### Clothing & dress

Garments are woven of cedar bark and nettle fibre, with practical rain hats and elaborate ceremonial robes ornamented with sea otter fur and formline designs.

- **Fringed capes and cloaks** — Made of cedar bark and nettle fibre with fringes down the bottom and sides, marking working dress.
- **Ceremonial robe or blanket** — Cedar-bark thread and mountain-goat wool, fringed with sea otter fur and painted with designs, marking high-status pieces.
- **Conical whaling hats** — Twined of cedar bark or cedar root with an onion-dome or knobbed top, carrying whaling scenes and thunderbird imagery, with an interior hide headband.

### Architecture

Cedar wood served as the primary building material for houses and carved totem poles, with a specialized ritual structure for whaling preparation.

- **Totem poles** — Full logs of red and yellow cedar carved by artists and woodworkers, standing with houses in villages like Yuquot.
- **Ocean-going canoes** — Carved from full logs of red and yellow cedar by skilled woodworkers.
- **Yuquot Whalers' Shrine** — A house-like enclosure made up of memorial posts carved as spirit figures together with the bones of whaling ancestors, used in spiritual preparations for whale hunts.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Wood, bone, stone and whalebone carry the everyday repertoire, including carved bowls, fishing gear and tools with functional and spiritual elements.

- **Carved bowls** — Bowls carved from alder and pale brown wood, several in the form of a human figure lying on its back clutching its own knees and feet, some with necklaces of white china beads.
- **D-adze** — A tool with a yew handle pierced by an oval hole, its upper edge carved as a squatting singing figure and a repurposed steel axe blade lashed on with commercial string.
- **Halibut hook** — A fishing hook whose bone barb is lashed with spruce root and secured with tightly wound fabric thread.
- **Whalebone barkcloth beater** — A tool with a roughly carved head at the upper end.
- **Wooden comb** — A comb of fourteen teeth pierced through the centre.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ornament appears primarily on objects rather than directly on the body, using dentalium shells, talc, white china beads and human hair.

- **Dentalium shells** — Shells that serve as teeth on wolf frontlets and other masks.
- **Wolf frontlet** — A forehead piece studded with dentalium teeth, with talc for eyes.
- **White china beads** — Beads forming necklaces on carved bowl figures.

## Music & performance

Whaling is carried in song and story throughout Nuu-chah-nulth territories, reflected in songs, names, family lines and place names.

- **Bird-shaped rattles** — Rattles formed in two halves of painted alder secured with skin ties, or of wood painted white with a design in black.
- **Singing figure** — A squatting singing figure carved on a D-adze handle.

## Dance & theatre

Masked performance is evidenced by elaborate carved masks representing wolves, birds and humans, though specific dances and ceremonies are not named in the sources.

- **Wolf frontlet** — A forehead piece carved from two pieces of alder as upper and lower jaws, with a long muzzle, raised nostrils and sawtooth teeth.
- **Bird's-head mask** — A mask with moveable jaws, feather ornament, black-and-white painted body and a sleeping human face elaborately carved on the underside of the lower jaw.
- **Human-face mask** — Carved masks of cedar with hide teeth and eyebrows and a fringe of human hair, or with closed eyes and open mouth.

## Festivals & rituals

The potlatch is the central public ceremony, originating from the Nuu-chah-nulth, serving to redistribute wealth, maintain social status, cement alliances and solemnise important events.

- **Potlatch** — The host honours guests with generous gifts, redistributing wealth, maintaining social status, cementing alliances, celebrating marriage and commemorating important events.
- **Whaling ceremony** — Ceremonial bathing, abstinence, prayer and ceremony before and after the hunt, performed by the chief and his wife and understood as a key factor in the hunt's outcome.
- **Yuquot Whalers' Shrine** — A dedicated structure for spiritual preparation of whale hunts, made up of memorial posts carved as spirit figures together with the bones of whaling ancestors.

## Foodways

Whale supplied meat and blubber rendered into oil, with halibut, salmon and shellfish from the sea and camas root, berries and fern rhizomes from the land.

- **Whale hunting** — Grey and humpback whales were taken most often because they are more docile and come close to shore, while orca meat and blubber were rated higher in quality.
- **Halibut fishing** — Specialized fishing with hooks and gear including bone barbs lashed with spruce root.
- **Salmon streams** — Streams tended to keep the runs strong.
- **Gathered foods** — Clams, sea urchins and mussels gathered on the shoreline at low tide, and camas root, fern rhizomes and berries including blueberry and huckleberry from the land.

## Oral tradition & literature

Nuu-chah-nulth oral history is populated by animal spirits including trickster figures and stories of transformation and rescue.

- **Chulyen** — Crow, a trickster figure in Nuu-chah-nulth oral history.
- **Guguyni** — Raven, a trickster figure appearing in stories about bothering Octopus and other tales.
- **Tihtipihin and Kwatyat** — Brothers who let a monster swallow them in order to rescue their mother, then cut through its stomach and escaped with her.
- **Andaokut** — Born from the mucus or tears of a woman whose children have been stolen by Malahas, he recovers the children and kills the malicious forest goddess.
- **Matlose** — A hobgoblin whose head is not quite a man's, whose bulk bristles with black hair, and whose teeth and curved claws tear prey apart at a single stroke.

## Language & religion

Nuu-chah-nulth is a Southern Wakashan language with 35 consonants, phonemic vowels and polysynthetic morphology, with spiritual life centered on animal spirits and whaling ritual.

- **Nuu-chah-nulth language** (*Nuu-chah-nulth*) — Formerly called Nootka and earlier recorded as Tahkaht, spoken from Barkley Sound to Quatsino Sound with 35 consonants, phonemic short and long vowels and VSO word order.
- **Dialects** — Twelve dialects are recorded, among them Ahousaht, Hesquiat, Kyuquot, Mowachaht, Clayoquot and Ucluelet.
- **Chinook Jargon contribution** — The language supplied much of the vocabulary of Chinook Jargon, including hiyu meaning many and siah meaning far.

## Glossary

- *potlatch* — Central public ceremony of wealth redistribution, status recognition and alliance-making
- *Chulyen* — Crow, a trickster figure in oral tradition
- *Guguyni* — Raven, a trickster figure in oral tradition
- *Andaokut* — Hero born from mucus or tears who rescues stolen children
- *Malahas* — Malicious forest goddess who steals children
- *Matlose* — Hobgoblin with bristling hair and tearing claws
- *Tahkaht* — Earlier recorded name for Nuu-chah-nulth language, root aht meaning people
- *formline* — Distinctive black and red design style
- *imbricated* — Overlapping stripe pattern in weaving
- *twined* — Weaving technique creating textured fabric
- *D-adze* — Carving tool with curved blade and hole-pierced handle
- *frontlet* — Forehead mask piece worn in performance
- *dentalium* — Small shell used as ornamental teeth on masks and frontlets
- *Yuquot* — Village on west coast of Vancouver Island, site of Whalers' Shrine
- *Yuquot Whalers' Shrine* — House-like structure for spiritual whale-hunt preparation with carved posts and whaling ancestor bones
- *Wakashan* — Language family to which Nuu-chah-nulth belongs
- *VSO* — Verb-subject-object word order
- *hiyu* — Many, Nuu-chah-nulth word adopted in Chinook Jargon from word for ten
- *siah* — Far, Nuu-chah-nulth word adopted in Chinook Jargon from word for sky
