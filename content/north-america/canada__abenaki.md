## Overview

The Abenaki are an Indigenous people of the Northeastern Woodlands of Canada and the United States, part of the Wabanaki Confederacy and speakers of an Algonquian language. Their homeland, *Ndakinna*, previously extended across northern New England, southern Quebec, and the southern Canadian Maritimes. Eastern Abenaki communities were concentrated in New Brunswick and Maine, while Western Abenaki communities lived in the Connecticut River valley in Vermont, New Hampshire, and Massachusetts. The Abenaki also lived along the St. Francis, Bécancour, Connecticut, Kennebec, Penobscot, Merrimack, Androscoggin, Saco, St. Croix, and Wolastoq rivers. The communities of Odanak and Wôlinak remain in Canada. In 2011, the total Abenaki population on and off reserve was 2,101, with approximately 400 people living on the two reserves. The Abenaki are significant in folk culture through their language, storytelling, wigwams, basketry, subsistence practices, and plant knowledge.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Abenaki villages were generally small, with an average of 100 residents, and were located near fertile river floodplains, rivers, or the seacoast. During the spring and summer, dispersed bands of extended families came together at seasonal villages for planting and fishing. In winter, people lived in smaller groups farther inland. Villages could be fortified according to relationships with neighboring tribes or Europeans.

Most Abenaki made dome-shaped, bark-covered *wigwams*. A few preferred oval-shaped longhouses. During winter, the inside of conical wigwams was lined with bear and deer skins for warmth.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do document several everyday objects and materials. Abenaki people made baskets from ash and sweet grass for gathering wild berries, and basket weaving remains a traditional activity practiced by some tribal members. *Hierochloe odorata* (sweetgrass), *Apocynum* (dogbane), *Betula papyrifera* (paper birch), *Fraxinus americana* (white ash), *Fraxinus nigra* (black ash), *Laportea canadensis* (Canada nettle), willow, and *Tilia americana* (basswood, or American linden) were used for baskets, canoes, snowshoes, whistles, and containers. Paper birch was also used for moose calls and other utilitarian pieces.

## Music & performance

They do mention whistles made from plant materials, including sweetgrass, dogbane, paper birch, white ash, black ash, Canada nettle, willow, and basswood.

Storytelling is described as a major part of Abenaki culture. It served both as entertainment and as a method of teaching. Abenaki people viewed stories as having lives of their own and as being aware of how they were used. Stories taught children appropriate behavior; rather than punishing a child, adults could tell a story. The story of Azban the Raccoon concerns a proud raccoon who challenges a waterfall to a shouting contest. When the waterfall does not respond, Azban dives into it and is swept away because of his pride.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe seasonal patterns of community life. During spring and summer, bands gathered at villages near rivers or along the seacoast for planting and fishing. During winter, people lived in smaller inland groups.

Abenaki subsistence and seasonal practices included hunting, fishing, trapping, berry picking, and growing corn, beans, squash, potatoes, and tobacco. The three crops known as the “sisters” were grown together: corn supported beans, while squash or pumpkins provided ground cover and reduced weeds.

The Abenaki religious tradition is associated in the sources with the Midewiwin tradition, whose ceremonies were led by medicine keepers called *Medeoulin* or *Mdawinno*.

## Foodways

Abenaki subsistence included hunting, fishing, trapping, berry picking, gathering wild plants and fungi, and growing corn, beans, squash, potatoes, and tobacco. Men generally hunted, while women tended fields and grew crops. The animals hunted included bears, deer, fish, and birds.

Corn, beans, and squash were planted together as the “sisters.” The corn stalk supported the beans, and squash or pumpkins covered the ground and reduced weeds. The Abenaki also gathered wild berries and used plants in food and drink. The sources identify *Vaccinium myrtilloides* fruit as part of the traditional diet and record the use of *Viburnum nudum* var. *cassinoides* fruit and grains for food. Other plants were used as tea, soup, jelly, sweetener, condiment, snack, or meal, including red maple, sweet flag, *Caltha palustris*, *Fragaria virginiana*, *Gaultheria procumbens*, *Phaseolus vulgaris*, *Prunus virginiana*, *Rubus idaeus*, *Solanum tuberosum*, *Vaccinium angustifolium*, and *Zea mays*.

Abenaki people boiled maple sap to make syrup. In one myth, Gluskab diluted the syrup in maple trees with water so that humans had to collect and boil it.

## Oral tradition & literature

Abenaki storytelling was used for entertainment and instruction. Stories taught children behavior and were treated as living entities with awareness of how they were used. One named tale is the story of Azban the Raccoon, whose pride leads him to challenge a waterfall and be swept away.

Abenaki mythology describes Gici Niwaskw, also called Tabaldak or Dabaldak and Niwaskowôgan, as the highest deity and Great Spirit. In the creation story, Gici Niwaskw calls forth the giant turtle *Tolba*, places land on its shell, and dreams the creatures and plants into existence.

Gluskab, also known as Glooscap, Glooskap, Gluskabe, and Klooskomba, is responsible for caring for Gici Niwaskw’s creations and for the transition between three ages. He is described as a supernatural being who helps humanity while also using trickery. The mythology identifies an Ancient Age, a Golden Age, and a Present Age. The sources also name Oodzee-hozo, or Odzihózo, who created mountains, valleys, rivers, and Lake Champlain by dragging his body before the invention of legs.

Abenaki language revival work is taking place at Odanak and throughout New Hampshire, Vermont, and New York state.

## Language & religion

Abenaki is an endangered Eastern Algonquian language of Quebec and the northern states of New England. It has Eastern and Western forms that differ in vocabulary and phonology and are sometimes considered distinct languages. The Eastern Abenaki language was predominantly spoken in Maine, while Western Abenaki was spoken in Quebec, Vermont, and New Hampshire. The language is closely related to Panawahpskek, or Penobscot, and shares similarities with Pestomuhkati, Wolastoqiyik, Mi'kmaq, and other Eastern Algonquian languages.

The Abenaki language is polysynthetic: a phrase or an entire sentence can be expressed by a single word. The sources give *awanoch* for “white man,” formed from *awani*, meaning “who,” and *uji*, meaning “from.” Abenaki forms include Eastern *Alənαpαtəwéwαkan* and Western *Alnôbaôdwawôgan*. The English word “skunk” is probably borrowed from Abenaki *seganku*.

Abenaki religious beliefs are associated with the Midewiwin tradition and ceremonies led by Medeoulin or Mdawinno.
