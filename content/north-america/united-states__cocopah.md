## Overview

The Cocopah (*Xawiƚƚ Kwñchawaay*) are Native Americans who live in Baja California, Mexico, and Arizona, United States. They are also called the Cucapá, with the Cocopa names *Kwapa* and *Kwii Capáy*. Their self-designation translates as “Those Who Live on the Cloudy River.” Cocopah people in the United States belong to the federally recognized Cocopah Tribe of Arizona, whose reservation includes a Museum and Cultural Center. In 2010, the U.S. Census counted 1,009 Cocopah; the Cocopah Tribe of Arizona numbered 891 people in the 2000 United States Census. The Cocopah language belongs to the Delta–California branch of the Yuman family and is considered endangered.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources used mention customary ribbon shirts and ribbon dresses worn by Cocopah people on important occasions.

### Architecture

They state that, in the late 1970s and 1980s, the Cocopah Tribe of Arizona acquired additional land, constructed homes, installed utilities, developed infrastructure, and initiated economic development.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that many tribes along the Colorado River entered the ferry business after the establishment of Camp Independence, creating many jobs for the Cocopah.

## Music & performance

The Cocopah language article states that much of the language was passed down through speaking rather than writing. It also mentions Cocopah language audio Bible stories and lessons among its external links.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that Cocopah people wear customary ribbon shirts and ribbon dresses on important occasions, without identifying those occasions further.

## Foodways

The precontact ancestors of the Cocopah practiced floodplain agriculture where possible and relied heavily on hunting and gathering. During the conflict surrounding Garra’s Tax Revolt of 1851, disputes arose with the Quechan over the distribution of sheep confiscated from white sheepherders, but the sources do not present this as a Cocopah food tradition.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that much of the Cocopah language was transmitted through speaking because the language lacked an alphabet for most of its existence. A written language was developed in the 1970s, and the tribe developed a new alphabet in the early 2000s. As language revival progressed, Cocopah elders were given responsibility for developing words and phrases for modern objects that had not existed in the ancient language.

## Language & religion

Cocopah is a Delta language of the Yuman language family. It is related to the Native American languages of Mojave and Kumeyaay and is considered endangered, with fewer than 400 speakers at the turn of the 21st century. Yuma County’s Cocopah Museum began offering Cocopah classes to children in 1998. The language has 21 consonants and 4 vowels, including short and long vowels. The written language differs by location: Cocopah in Mexico use an orthography designed by INALI, while Cocopah in Arizona use another orthographic tradition.
