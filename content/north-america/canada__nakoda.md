## Overview

The Nakoda, also known as Stoney, Îyârhe Nakoda, or Stoney Nakoda, are an Indigenous people in Western Canada and the United States. Their former territory included large parts of what is now Alberta, Saskatchewan, and Montana. Their reserves are now in Alberta and Saskatchewan, where they are rarely differentiated from the Assiniboine. The Nakoda First Nation in Alberta comprises the Bearspaw, Chiniki, and Goodstoney bands. In their language, *Nakoda* means “friend, ally.” The name Stoney was given by Anglophone explorers because of the Nakoda technique of using fire-heated rocks to boil broth in rawhide bowls. Their language belongs to the Dakota subgroup of the Mississippi Valley grouping of the Siouan languages.

## Material culture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources describe rawhide bowls in connection with the origin of the name Stoney. Nakoda people used fire-heated rocks to boil broth in these bowls. No other ceramics, metalwork, wooden objects, tools, or household forms are described.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe historical and political events involving Nakoda communities. Members of the Nakoda nations of Paul and Alexis signed an adhesion to Treaty 6 in 1877. In the same year, representatives of the Nakoda nations of Bearspaw, Chiniki, and Goodstoney met with representatives of the British Crown to discuss Treaty 7. The Crown agreed to honour their right to self-government and an ancestral way of life and promised reserve lands of 279 km2 along the Bow River between the Kananaskis River and the Ghost River. These lands became the Big Horn, Stoney, and Eden Valley reserves, shared between the Bearspaw, Chiniki, and Goodstoney tribes.

The Stoney were excluded from Banff National Park between 1890 and 1920. In 2010 they were officially “welcomed back.”

## Foodways

The name Stoney refers to a documented cooking technique. Nakoda people used fire-heated rocks to boil broth in rawhide bowls, a practice observed by Anglophone explorers.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do identify John Snow’s book *These Mountains Are Our Sacred Places. The Story of the Stoney People* as further reading on the Stoney people.

The sources describe language-preservation and education efforts. Systematic work to establish a Stoney writing system began in 1965, when the Stoney Tribal Council collaborated with the Summer Institute of Linguistics and the elder J. R. Twoyoungmen. The book *The Stoney Alphabet*, with drawings by Jawn C. Poucette, was published in 1970. Stoney-language writing and learning materials were further developed during the 1970s by the Stoney Cultural Education Program and in the 1980s by the Stoney Indian Language Project.

## Language & religion

Stoney—also called Nakota or Nakoda, *Isga* in the Alexis dialect and *Îyethka* in the Mînî Thnî dialect, and formerly Alberta Assiniboine—is a member of the Dakota subgroup of the Mississippi Valley grouping of the Siouan languages. The Dakotan languages form a dialect continuum consisting of Santee-Sisseton, Yankton-Yanktonai, Teton, Assiniboine, and Stoney.

Stoney is spoken by five groups in Alberta. The 1996 Canadian census recorded 2,800 people as having Stoney as their first language. In 2016, the number of first-language Stoney-speakers was reported as 3,050. In 2024, Morley was renamed Mînî Thnî. In 2026, Deanna Two Young Men, a language teacher at Nakoda Elementary School in Mînî Thnî, reported that many students had minimal knowledge of Stoney and was engaged in revitalizing the language.

The usual word order is subject-object-verb, although this order is not obligatory. Stoney writing has used Latin letters and Cree syllabics.
