## Overview

The Tillamook are a Native American tribe of the Salish linguistic group from coastal Oregon, in the United States. Their traditional territory extended from Tillamook Head in the north to Cape Foulweather and the summit of the Coast Range mountains. The group included several divisions and dialects associated with the Siletz, Salmon, Nestucca, Tillamook Bay, and Nehalem areas. The Tillamook were estimated to number about 2200 at the beginning of the 18th century, but infectious diseases, conflicts, and murder caused severe population loss. In 1856 they were forced to live on the Siletz Reservation with other tribes and bands. Their culture is documented through basket-weaving, mythology, life-cycle practices, body adornment, foodways centered on salmon, and the Tillamook language, an extinct Salishan language.

### Clothing & dress

Tillamook adults distinguished themselves through hair arrangement, body painting, tattoos, and ear pendants. Both men and women painted their central hair part red. Men wore their hair in a single braid, while women wore two braids. Men and women had tattoos and wore ear pendants according to personal preference.

### Architecture

The sources used mention that Lewis and Clark described a Tillamook village of around 1000 people living in about 50 houses.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Children were named at an ear-piercing ceremony. Boys had their nasal septa pierced. Men and women had tattoos and wore ear pendants according to their preference.

## Festivals & rituals

Tillamook children were named during an ear-piercing ceremony. If an infant had older siblings, those siblings were required to stay away for at least a week because their presence was believed to cause the infant’s ear to swell and lead to death.

At puberty, girls were secluded and underwent ritual behaviors and food taboos. One ritual involved an all-night guardian-spirit vigil in the woods, during which the girl repeatedly bathed in a cold stream in an attempt to gain guardian spirits. Boys undertook fasting and guardian-spirit quests that included bathing. A boy’s power and adult occupation were equated with the spirit obtained through the quest. Boys and girls activated powers acquired from their guardian only at middle age.

Marriages were arranged, with services exchanged between the two families according to their status. Initial residence was in the groom’s parents’ village. Illegitimate births were a common result of the arranged-marriage process and led to a high occurrence of infanticide.

## Foodways

Salmon was the staple food source described for the Tillamook. The people caught salmon during the annual salmon run from April to October, when the fish went upstream in freshwater rivers from the ocean to spawn. They ate some salmon fresh and processed much of the catch for use throughout the year by drying it and grinding it into powder.

A whale washed ashore near the Tillamook village of Necost. The Tillamook quickly stripped it of flesh, saving the flesh and blubber as food and rendering oil for later use. Lewis and Clark traded for blubber and received 300 pounds and some oil in exchange for trade goods.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Tillamook had a detailed mythology connected with existing events. The **Story of the Thunderbird and Whale** reflects the large earthquake in the region in 1700. Tillamook mythology was divided into three categories: the Myth Age; the Age of Transformation, when the “South Wind” remade the land; and the “period of true happenings,” referring to events regarded as recent history. Stories from the third age were considered just as much myth as those from the first and second.

Between 1965 and 1972, researchers from the University of Hawaiʻi interviewed the few remaining Tillamook speakers and created a 120-page dictionary in an effort to revitalize the language.

## Language & religion

Tillamook is an extinct Salishan language formerly spoken in northwestern Oregon. The sources describe two dialects, Siletz and Tillamook proper. The last fluent speaker is identified in the sources as Minnie Scovell, who died in 1972. Another account states that the last fluent speaker died in 1970.

The language gradually gave way to English after European contact. Researchers from the University of Hawaiʻi interviewed remaining speakers between 1965 and 1972 and produced a 120-page dictionary. The sources describe guardian spirits, fasting, bathing, spirit quests, food taboos, and ritual seclusion.
