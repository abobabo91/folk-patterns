## Overview

The Kwakwakaʼwakw are twenty-five autonomous Indigenous nations on British Columbia's central coast, numbering about 5,500. Their culture centers on woodcarving and ceremonial performance, especially the potlatch and secret dancing societies that transmit inherited rank and supernatural privilege. Much knowledge comes from oral history, nineteenth-century records, inherited objects, and carvers trained in the tradition. Art reached a Golden Age in the late nineteenth century before Canadian laws suppressed ceremonies, but carving resumed as a livelihood afterward.

## Material culture

Almost all material culture is carved from cedar and other woods, painted with designs depicting ancestors, spirits and family crests.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Weaving is women's work producing ceremonial textiles with elaborate designs of ancestors and mythological figures.

- **Chilkat blanket** (*Chilkat*) — Woven from shredded cedar bark and mountain-goat wool with patterned designs of ancestors and myths, with wool fringes; takes almost a year to make.
- **Button blanket** — Brightly colored cloaks on navy blue ground with red wool appliqué, borders and family crests outlined in abalone or pearl buttons.
- **Mawihl** — Painted ceremonial curtain hung over the dancing house entrance, typically cotton, depicting the spirit of the dancing house.
- **Cedar-bark regalia** — Neck rings, arm bands and ankle bands of red cedar bark dyed red, plaited or cylindrical, finished with twisted bark tassels and red cotton ties.
- **Dance aprons and leggings** — Ceremonial garments hung with small coppers, puffin beaks and deer hoofs that sound as the dancer moves.

### Clothing & dress

Ceremonial dress includes carved headdresses and blankets displaying wealth; daily wear followed Western styles adopted from European contact.

- **Ceremonial headdresses** — Carved wooden objects worn by high-ranking people during ceremonies.
- **Copper ornaments** — High-ranking people wore coppers of varying sizes on their clothing as marks of wealth.

### Architecture

Carved cedar poles and posts stand outside family homes, displaying crests, history, rank and inheritance.

- **Totem poles** — Stand outside family houses displaying crests, history, wealth, rank and privilege; fifteen to fifty feet tall with protruding elements like stretched limbs and jutting beaks, painted in dark and light for contrast.
- **House poles** — Posts supporting important members' homes, simpler and bolder than totem poles, depicting ancestors, house symbols and animals like sea lions and grizzly bears.
- **Frontal entrance pole** — Carries a long opening at its base and supports no part of the roof.
- **Clan-house cross beams** — Sometimes carved with Sisiutl, the three-headed sea serpent.
- **Dancing house (Big House)** — Where initiates are brought to be ritually tamed during ceremonies.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Nearly everything is carved from cedar or yellow cedar, painted, and often functional for ceremony or feasting.

- **Kerfed boxes and storage boxes** — Carved wooden vessels for storage and holding goods.
- **Feast bowls** — Large serving dishes, sometimes carved in the shape of animals like seals.
- **Spoons** — Made of pine or alder with simple black designs inside.
- **Horn cooking utensils** — Horn softened by boiling, then bent and carved mostly for cooking.
- **Carved tools and implements** — Carvers made their own adzes, knives, axes, hammers and brushes, some ceremonial and carved themselves.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Jewelry is worked in abalone, stone, ivory and wood; contact with Europeans introduced gold and silver.

- **Copper** — A beaten copper plate engraved and shaped like a T or shield, the emblem of great wealth; can be broken into pieces and given away at potlatches.
- **Shell and stone jewelry** — Earrings, bracelets, necklaces, nose rings and lip piercings worked in abalone shell, stone, ivory and wood.
- **Wooden hairpins** — Carved wooden ornaments for the hair.
- **Hammered metal jewelry** — Gold and silver pieces hammered into shape and inscribed with patterns and mythological figures.
- **Mask adornments** — Masks finished with feathers, animal fur or cedar bark strips, sometimes with flattened copper applied to the face.

## Music & performance

Vocals carry the melody while percussion, rattles, whistles and clappers keep the beat; each dance has its own instruments designed by instrument makers.

- **Singer's baton (drum)** (*mEnāʼtsē*) — About a foot long; plain firewood for temporary batons given to guests to drum along, or elaborately carved hardwood and cedar sticks shaped as sea lions for song leaders.
- **Box drum** — Usually cedar, beaten collectively by a large group who sing the song they are drumming to.
- **Rattle** (*iaʼtEn*) — The most important instrument in ritual, an ancient icon meant to keep dancers calm and free of possession, reported to bring back the dead.
- **Whistles** — Each carries its own pitch; several chambers can be combined for up to three pitches at once, announcing supernatural presences and voicing spirits.
- **Clapper** — Made of leather and wood, combines rattle and baton in one hand and produces sharp, sudden sounds.

## Dance & theatre

Dance runs through every rite and is carefully staged and rehearsed; four main dancing societies each have their own house.

- **Hamatsa** — Cannibal society, the highest rank, open only after twelve years through three previous grades; novice is possessed by the Man-Eater spirit, disappears four days, returns crying and biting before being ritually tamed.
- **Winalagilis** — Making War All Over the Earth society; dances tell of violent, possessed warriors and ghost dancers revive dead warrior spirits.
- **Atlakim** — Taken Far Away Into the Woods society; songs introduce each dancer in turn.
- **Dluwalakha** — Klasila society restricted to spring; closes with a potlatch repaying mask makers and those the novice's dancing affected.
- **Transformation mask** — Built in sections and opened by strings mid-dance to split an animal or mythological exterior aside and reveal a human face within.

## Festivals & rituals

The ceremonial year turns on winter; the Tsetseka festival opens by singing for the dead and treats the whole season as supernatural.

- **Tsetseka** — A four-day winter festival and supernatural season greeting the new winter; opens by singing for everyone who died since the previous winter; the whole season is treated as supernatural so every ritual must reflect that.
- **Potlatch** — A ceremony that shares wealth and prestige to establish status and ensure witnesses remember the stories celebrated; often announces new initiates into secret dancing societies.
- **Hamatsa initiation** — The highest rank initiation open only to chiefs' sons after twelve years; novice is possessed and tamed, concluding by singing his new hamatsa song.
- **Tlugwe transmission** (*Tlugwe*) — Supernatural treasures in the form of masks, regalia, stories, songs, recitations and dances transferred and reaffirmed through ceremony.

## Foodways

Few details are documented beyond carved vessels and utensils for food service and preparation.

- **Cedar food vessels** — Carved containers for holding and serving food.
- **Feast bowls** — Large serving dishes used in ceremonial feasting.
- **Spoons and utensils** — Made of pine, alder or bone for serving and eating food.
- **Horn cooking utensils** — Softened horn bent and carved for cooking purposes.

## Oral tradition & literature

Much knowledge comes from oral history; narrative is inseparable from ceremony, with masks, songs, recitations and dances themselves serving as supernatural treasures.

- **Creation account** — A raven flies over water, drops pebbles to make islands, then makes trees, grass, and finally the first man and woman from wood and clay after several failed attempts.
- **Flood stories** — Ancestors transform into their natural form and vanish as waters rose, or lash oceangoing canoes to high mountain peaks.
- **Kwakiutl Legends** — Published collection of Kwakwakaʼwakw narratives by Chief James Wallas.
- **Kwakiutl Tales** — Published collection of Kwakwakaʼwakw stories by Franz Boas.
- **Spirit beings and deities** — Named beings include Tseiqami (man from the cedar tree), Thunderbird, Qaniqilak, Komokwa (Undersea Chief), Sisiutl, Dzunukwa, Bakwas, Uʼmelth the Raven, and Pugwis.

## Language & religion

Kwakʼwala belongs to the Wakashan family; spirituality is organized around four spirit realms contacted through masked trance.

- **Kwakʼwala language** (*Kwakʼwala*) — The language spoken by the Kwakwakaʼwakw; the name Kwakwakaʼwakw means Kwakʼwala-speaking tribes.
- **Summer song** (*baquyala*) — A song genre performed during summer season.
- **Winter song** (*tsʼēʼkꞏala*) — A song genre performed during the sacred winter ceremonial season.
- **Hamatsa society** (*Hamatsa*) — The secret society of particular importance; replaced an earlier feminine dance called hamshamtses.
- **Spirit realms** — Four realms — sky, sea, earth and otherworldly — that humans contact by channelling spirits while masked and in trance.

## Glossary

- *Kwakwakaʼwakw* — Kwakʼwala-speaking tribes; collective of twenty-five Indigenous nations
- *Kwakʼwala* — Language belonging to the Wakashan family
- *Tsetseka* — Supernatural season or winter ceremonial festival lasting four days
- *Potlatch* — Ceremony sharing wealth and prestige to establish status and witness stories
- *Hamatsa* — Cannibal society; secret dancing society and initiation rite
- *Tlugwe* — Supernatural treasures: masks, regalia, stories, songs, recitations and dances
- *Chilkat* — Woven blanket of cedar bark and mountain-goat wool with elaborate designs
- *Mawihl* — Painted ceremonial curtain for the dancing house entrance
- *Copper* — Beaten copper plate shaped like a T or shield; emblem of great wealth
- *Totem pole* — Carved cedar pole displaying family crests, history and rank
- *Sisiutl* — Three-headed sea serpent whose glance turns an adversary to stone
- *Transformation mask* — Mask built in sections, opened by strings to reveal a human face inside an animal
- *baquyala* — Summer song genre
- *tsʼēʼkꞏala* — Winter song genre
- *mEnāʼtsē* — Drum or singer's baton
- *iaʼtEn* — Rattle; most important instrument in ritual
- *Winalagilis* — Making War All Over the Earth; dancing society with warrior and ghost dancers
- *Atlakim* — Taken Far Away Into the Woods; dancing society
- *Dluwalakha* — Klasila; dancing society restricted to spring
- *Komokwa* — Undersea Chief; maker of coppers
- *Tseiqami* — Man from the cedar tree; Thunderbird who helps heroes build houses
- *Dzunukwa* — Cannibal giant who cries in grandmother's voice to lure children
- *Bakwas* — King of the ghosts; green and skeletal with long curving nose
