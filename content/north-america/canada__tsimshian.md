## Overview

The Tsimshian are an Indigenous people of coastal British Columbia and Alaska with 5,000 years of continuous habitation. Society is matrilineal, with identity and property passing through the mother's line, and a complex ranking system expressed in chiefly titles. The Tsimshian are celebrated for their mastery of cedar work, weaving, carving and the potlatch feast system, which governs law, knowledge transmission and social order.

## Material culture

Tsimshian material culture centres on the red cedar, worked into textiles, clothing, architecture and decorated objects that merge practical function with spiritual meaning.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Cedar bark and wool trade cloth are woven and twined into blankets, hats and baskets with distinctive formline designs and shell inlay.

- **Chilkat weaving** — A weaving tradition the Tsimshian are credited with inventing, worked with western red cedar and other materials.
- **Cedar-bark twining** — Twined cedar-bark work produces wide-brimmed hats and cylindrical lidded baskets.
- **Button blankets** — Dance capes cut from wool trade cloth and outlined in shell buttons, used in ceremonial performance.
- **Formline design** — Black and red painted designs on wood featuring dorsal fins, facial features, salmon heads, killer whales, ravens and clan emblems.
- **False embroidery basketry** — Twined cedar-bark baskets with overlay patterning in grass and maidenhair fern rather than dyed wool.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress uses cedar bark; ceremonial dress is sharply distinguished and includes carved frontlets, button blankets and animal skins.

- **Frontlet** — A carved wooden forehead ornament mounted on a headband, set with abalone shell and pigment, worn by chiefs.
- **Button blanket cape** — A dance cape of wool trade cloth outlined in shell buttons worn over the shoulders in ceremony.
- **Shaman's headdress** — Ritual headdress made of goat horns with leather and wire, or vegetable fibre, wood and horn.
- **Head-ring** — Worn by ritual officiants including a fire-eater's crown of vegetable fibre.
- **Carved bird's-head mask** — Masks carved in the shape of a bird's head worn on top of the head during ceremonial performance.

### Architecture

The Tsimshian built permanent cedar longhouses framed with house posts and panels, settling year-round in winter villages and moving to summer villages when salmon ran.

- **Cedar longhouse** — A large house framed and clad with cedar house posts and panels, sheltering an entire extended family.
- **Winter villages** — Permanent settlements in and around the islands of Prince Rupert Harbour and Venn Pass.
- **Summer villages** — Seasonal settlements along the lower Skeena where families returned when the salmon ran.
- **Totem pole** — Monumental carved cedar poles that accompanied houses, including the Kapshkoltsh pole.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Tsimshian produced no pottery; stone tools and cedar vessels carry the household repertoire.

- **Stone mauls and clubs** — Heads of granite or quartzite crowned with carved crests like salmon heads or whale blowholes, hafted with wood and sinew.
- **Feast trough** — An oblong cedar vessel rimmed with inlaid shell opercula, darkened by eulachon oil.
- **Bentwood box** — Carved and painted plank boxes used for burial and storage, bearing designs like sea-lion motifs.
- **Halibut hook** — A two-part V-shaped hook whose upper arm is carved as a spiritual helper like an otter or seal.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Engraved silver bracelets signed by named makers are the signature ornament, often inlaid with abalone shell.

- **Engraved silver bracelet** — Signed work by named makers bearing designs like Thunderbirds or frogs, such as those by Thunderbird Bill Hellin and D M Dennis.
- **Abalone inlay** (*haliotis*) — Shell inlay that runs through high-status objects including frontlets, clubs and feast bowls.
- **Shell opercula inlay** — Shell opercula inlaid along the rims of feast bowls.

## Music & performance

The wooden trumpet and rattles are the main instruments, sounded during secret society initiations and feasts.

- **Trätrumpet** — A large carved wooden trumpet used by the Tsimshian in Nakmnok performances and secret society initiations, often sounded by concealed performers.
- **Raven rattle** — A 19th-century cedar rattle with pebbles inside and polychrome painting.
- **Face rattle** — A mid-19th-century rattle of wood, fibre and leather shaped like a face.
- **Whale-shaped clapper** — A wooden percussion instrument painted in black and red formline with carved lips, nose and eyes.
- **Box drum** — A drum whose design contributed to identifying Tsimshian regalia in historical photographs.

## Dance & theatre

Masked performance within feasts and secret societies is documented through regalia, though named dance repertoires are not recorded.

- **Button blanket dance cape** — A wool cape outlined in shell buttons worn in masked performance.
- **Forehead frontlet** — A carved forehead ornament worn during dance and masked performance.
- **Crown mask** — A mask worn on the crown of the head during ceremonial dance.
- **Bird's-head mask** — A carved mask shaped like a bird's head worn during performance.
- **Fire-eater's crown** — A vegetable-fibre crown worn by a ritual performer in the feast and secret societies.

## Festivals & rituals

The potlatch feast system is the centre of ceremonial life, serving as the means of declaring law, transmitting knowledge and demonstrating chiefly stewardship.

- **Potlatch** (*yaawk*) — A feast held to honour deaths, mark succession to name-titles and express law and social obligation through formal payment distribution.
- **Four feast types** — The Tsimshian recognise four different types of feast, each with specific protocols binding guests and hosts.
- **Marriage ceremony** — An extremely formal affair of several prolonged and sequential ceremonies, often arranged to protect territorial rights.
- **Childbirth taboos** — Taboos prohibited women and men from eating improper foods during and after childbirth, many still actively practised.
- **Burial box ritual** — Carved and painted burial boxes are central to the ritual complex of death and succession.

## Foodways

Salmon is the centre of Tsimshian nutrition, with halibut and eulachon oil as staples; families moved seasonally to summer villages on the Skeena when salmon ran.

- **Salmon** — The centre of Tsimshian nutrition for 5,000 years, enabling permanent towns and remaining central despite commercial fishing.
- **Halibut** — Fished with a carved two-part hook set about a metre above the bottom, usually worked singly.
- **Eulachon oil** — A staple so pervasive that cedar troughs still seep oil from their wood centuries later.
- **Vaccinium berries** (*Vaccinium vitis-idaea ssp. minus*) — Wild berries eaten as part of the seasonal diet.
- **Fish traps** — At Annette Islands Reserve, the community held the only right in Alaska to use fish traps after statehood in 1959.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tsimshian narrative is divided into two genres: adaawx (true tellings about animal spirits and origins) and malesk (adventure and history tales).

- **Adaawx** — True tellings concerning animal spirits in human guise, generally tied to the origin of the earth and peoples.
- **Malesk** — Adventure or history tales meant to entertain rather than explain.
- **Raven Tales** (*Txamsem, We-gyet, or Giant*) — Stories of the central figure Raven, understood as creator of the universe and intermediary between physical and spiritual forms.
- **Raven creation narrative** — A story in which a pampered chief's son dies and is replaced by a youth who becomes Raven after learning generosity through hosting a potlatch.
- **Logobola** — Raven's brother, held responsible for the scarcity of fresh clear water and the fog.

## Language & religion

Sm'algyax is one of four Tsimshianic lects; traditional religion centred on the Lord of Heaven and supernatural servants, later influenced by Anglican, Methodist and Presbyterian missionaries.

- **Sm'algyax** — Real or true tongue, the main Tsimshian language spoken on the lower Skeena and at New Metlakatla.
- **Tsimshianic lects** — Four related languages: Coast Tsimshian, Southern Tsimshian (extinct 2013), Nisg̱a'a and Gitksan.
- **Lord of Heaven** — The central figure of traditional Tsimshian religion who sent supernatural servants to aid people in need.
- **Spiritual purification** — Charity and purification of the body through cleanliness or fasting was the route to the afterlife.
- **Language revitalization** — In 2016 only 160 people in Canada spoke Tsimshian; university courses have been offered at the University of Alaska Southeast since 2023.

## Glossary

- *Sm'algyax* — Real or true tongue; the Tsimshian language
- *adaawx* — True tellings; sacred narratives about animal spirits and origins
- *malesk* — Adventure or history tale meant to entertain
- *yaawk* — A specific type of potlatch feast
- *potlatch* — A ceremonial feast for honouring deaths, succession and demonstrating chiefly stewardship
- *trätrumpet* — A large carved wooden trumpet used in secret society initiations
- *frontlet* — A carved forehead ornament worn by chiefs
- *formline* — A style of black and red painted design on wood with carved facial features and fins
- *Txamsem* — Raven, the central creator figure in Tsimshian mythology
- *We-gyet* — Another name for Raven in Tsimshian tradition
- *Logobola* — Raven's brother, responsible for water scarcity and fog
- *haliotis* — Abalone shell, used for inlay in high-status objects
- *Nakmnok* — A performance in which wooden trumpets were used
