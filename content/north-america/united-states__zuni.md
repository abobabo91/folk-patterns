## Overview

The Zuni live mainly in Pueblo of Zuni in the Zuni River valley. Their culture includes farming, pottery, jewelry, ceremonies, dances, music, oral traditions, and religious societies. Around 9,500 people speak Zuni, which is a language isolate. The Zuni homeland is called Halona Idiwan’a, or Middle Place.

## Material culture

Zuni material culture is especially known for pottery, turquoise and silver jewelry, ceremonial clothing, and adobe homes.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The profile gives little detail about textiles, but it records weaving and many pottery and jewelry motifs.

- **Belt weaving** — A museum postcard shows We Wha, a Zuni Ihamana, weaving a belt, but gives no details about its materials or pattern.
- **Pottery decoration** — Zuni pottery uses clan symbols, spiral scrolls edged with triangles, animals, and signs linked to rain and water.
- **Needlepoint** — Needlepoint sets small, slightly oval stones with pointed ends close together in silver bezels.
- **Petit point** — Petit point uses stones with one pointed end and one rounded end in silver settings.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday clothing is not described in detail, but ceremonial clothing is important in dances and religious life.

- **Zuni man’s dress** — A museum postcard shows a Zuni man wearing a cloth shirt, trousers, boots, a metal disc belt, a beaded neck ornament, and a feathered cloth headband.
- **Zuni women’s dance dress** — A museum postcard shows Zuni women wearing Zuni-style clothing while performing a dance in regalia.
- **Shalako outfits** (*Shalako*) — Shalako dancers wear outfits that can be as high as eight feet and represent couriers of the rain deities.
- **Kachina costumes and headdresses** (*Kachina*) — The Lost Children instructed the Zuni to copy Kachina costumes, headdresses, and dances.
- **Awehuyan–hielashukti mask** (*Awehuyan–hielashukti Kachina*) — A museum photograph records a painted skin mask of Awehuyan–hielashukti Kachina.

### Architecture

Traditional Zuni homes are multi-level adobe houses, and villages were also built on mesas with kivas and defensive sites.

- **Multi-level adobe homes** — Traditional Zuni homes in the Pueblo are multi-level adobe houses.
- **Kivas** — Kivas are cultural and religious institutions, and the Village of the Great Kivas had nine of them.
- **Atsinna** — Atsinna was a historical Zuni village built on Inscription Rock.
- **Dowa Yalanne** — Dowa Yalanne was a steep, defensible mesa where the Zuni took refuge after the Pueblo Revolt of 1680.
- **Village of the Great Kivas** — The Zuni settlement called Village of the Great Kivas had nine kivas.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Zuni women traditionally made pottery from local clay through a careful process of shaping, polishing, painting, and firing.

- **Painted pottery** — Women made bowls, pots, krukas, and other vessels from local clay and painted them with homemade organic dyes using a yucca brush.
- **Traditional pottery making** — Potters ground and sifted clay, mixed it with water, shaped it with coils, smoothed it with slip, polished it with a stone, and fired it with animal dung.
- **Old Zuni and New Zuni vessels** — Museum records include painted bowls, krukas, vessels, and pots labeled “Old Zuni” and “New Zuni.”
- **Wooden and gourd rattles** — The sources record wooden and gourd rattles as Zuni objects.
- **Ceramic figurine** — A museum catalogue records a Zuni ceramic figurine.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Zuni jewelers are known for hand-cut stones set in silver, especially needlepoint, petit point, mosaic, and channel inlay.

- **Turquoise and silver jewelry** — Zuni jewelers set hand-cut turquoise and other stones in silver for earrings, bracelets, rings, brooches, and necklaces.
- **Mosaic inlay** — Mosaic inlay creates intricate designs and unique patterns from pieces of stone.
- **Channel inlay** — Channel inlay is a named Zuni technique for making intricate jewelry designs.
- **Fetishes** — Zuni make fetishes for ritual, trade, and sale to collectors.
- **Sunface bolo-tie piece** (*sunface*) — A trial bolo-tie piece used a sunface design made from mother-of-pearl, gold-lipped mother-of-pearl, acoma jet, and coral.

## Music & performance

Zuni music includes complex Kachina dance songs, work songs, peyote songs, flute melodies, and drum playing.

- **Kachina dance songs** (*Kachina*) — Bruno Nettl identifies Kachina dance songs as the most complex songs in Pueblo music.
- **Zuni Sunrise** — Zuni Sunrise, also called The Sunrise Call, is a well-known melody often played on Native American flute.
- **The Sunrise Call** — The Sunrise Call is another name for the Zuni melody Zuni Sunrise.
- **Work songs** — Work songs are found in Pueblo music.
- **Peyote songs** — Peyote songs share characteristics with Apache music and Plains-Pueblo music.

## Dance & theatre

Zuni ceremonial dance centers on Kachina rites and the night-long Shalako ceremony.

- **Kachina rites** (*Kachina Society*) — The Kachina Society performs the rites of the Kachina.
- **Shalako dances** (*Shalako*) — Shalako is a series of ceremonial dances in which six dancers move from house to house throughout the night.
- **Women’s regalia dance** — A museum postcard records Zuni women performing a dance in regalia.
- **Awehuyan–hielashukti Kachina mask** (*Awehuyan–hielashukti Kachina*) — A painted skin mask of Awehuyan–hielashukti Kachina is recorded in a museum catalogue.

## Festivals & rituals

Religion is central to Zuni life, with seasonal ceremonies, kivas, priesthoods, religious societies, and rites for homes and people.

- **Shalako ceremony** (*Shalako*) — The Shalako ceremony takes place around the winter solstice and blesses houses built during the year.
- **Kachina Society initiation** (*Kachina Society*) — Zuni males are initiated into the Kachina Society by the age of 12.
- **Kiva society initiation** (*kivas*) — Each boy is initiated into one of the six kiva societies by his ceremonial father at about age five or six.
- **Seasonal ceremonies** — Ceremonies occur during winter solstice, summer, harvest, and again in winter.
- **Marriage gift exchange** — Marriage includes gifts exchanged between female relatives and a meal shared by the husband with his wife’s family.

## Foodways

Zuni foodways are based on irrigated farming, livestock, trade, and the central role of corn.

- **Irrigated maize farming** — Ancestral Zuni used irrigation to grow maize on household-sized plots.
- **Wheat and maize fields** — Historical descriptions mention fields of wheat and maize, along with gourds and melons.
- **Corn porridge** — The people learned to eat corn by grinding, pounding, and molding it into porridge.
- **Corncakes** — Corn was also molded into corncakes.
- **Corn fetishes** (*fetishes*) — Corn fetishes were planted in fields, and after the harvest the corn was given to men to take home to their wives.

## Oral tradition & literature

Zuni mythology is an oral tradition that joins creation stories, religion, cosmology, customs, and ideas about the spirit world.

- **Middle Place** (*itiwana*) — The Twin Children of the Sun searched for the Middle, called itiwana.
- **Twin Children of the Sun** — Ko’wituma and Wats’usi were the Twin Children of the Sun in one creation account.
- **Four worlds** — One account tells how people emerged through four worlds before reaching the daylight world.
- **Prayer sticks** — Bow priests used prayer sticks made from pine, spruce, silver spruce, and aspen.
- **Storytelling** (*Delapna꞉we*) — Storytelling in the Zuni language is called Delapna꞉we.

## Language & religion

Zuni is an endangered language isolate, while Zuni religion combines traditional beliefs, religious societies, and some Roman Catholic elements.

- **Zuni way** (*Shiwiʼma*) — Shiwiʼma means “Zuni way” and is the endonym for the Zuni language.
- **Zuni people** (*ʼA꞉shiwi*) — Speakers are collectively known as ʼA꞉shiwi.
- **Language isolate** — Zuni is considered a language isolate, though it has borrowed religious words from neighboring languages.
- **Twenty-letter alphabet** — The Zuni language has twenty letters in its alphabet.
- **Religious societies** — Priests, clans, kivas, healing societies, and ceremonial societies provide religious instruction and cultural stability.
