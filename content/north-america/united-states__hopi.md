## Overview

The Hopi are a Native American people living on mesas in northeastern Arizona, with a population of about 19,338. Their unbroken agricultural and ceremonial life—maize farming, a lunar ceremonial calendar, matrilineal clans, and katsina societies—shapes their pottery, basketry, weaving, and silverwork. The Hopi language belongs to the Uto-Aztecan family, and traditional knowledge is preserved through oral tradition, though details vary by mesa and village.

## Material culture

Hopi material culture directly expresses their ceremonial life and agricultural tradition through weaving, pottery, basketry, and metalwork, with men as weavers and symbols drawn from katsina masks, rain, corn, and clan identity.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Weaving is a men's craft among the Hopi, producing garments and ceremonial pieces with warp-patterned designs and natural dyes.

- **Woman's dress** — A dark blue woven garment sewn with red yarn, finished with twisted red and green decorative cords.
- **Woven belt** — A narrow warp-patterned band in green, red and white with a twisted fringe, worn at the waist.
- **Wicker tray basket** — A plaited tray with sumac or willow warps and rabbit brush weft, often decorated with whirlwind designs using native dyes.
- **Coiled plaque** — A coiled basket plaque of yucca with designs representing katsina masks, hung on house walls as decoration.
- **Twilled yucca sifter** — A plaited winnowing and sifting basket of twilled yucca used in corn processing.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress includes dark woven garments and long belts, while ceremonial dress features masks, feathered ornaments, and striped skirts.

- **Woman's dress** — A dark blue woven dress sewn with red yarn and finished with twisted red and green cords.
- **Ceremonial katsina dress** — Includes a carved case mask with feathers, a neck scarf, a skirt with green and red striping, a sash, and upper arm bands.
- **Long woven belt** — A narrow warp-patterned belt with twisted fringe worn at the waist by both men and women.

### Architecture

Hopi villages feature multi-level adobe structures with wooden ladders and shared religious spaces, reflecting early communal and family-centered living.

- **Pueblo village structure** (*pueblo*) — Multi-level adobe buildings connected by wooden ladders, combining living rooms, storage, and religious spaces.
- **Religious chamber** (*kiva*) — A communal underground or semi-subterranean room used for ceremonies and religious practices.
- **Old Oraibi** — One of the four original villages, founded before A.D. 1100 and among the oldest continuously inhabited settlements in the United States.
- **Awatovi church** — Built by Franciscan missionaries from 1629, dismantled during the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, and the village fell in 1700–1701.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Hopi pottery is painted in earth tones with geometric designs and ceremonial motifs, while metalwork and household items are primarily silver jewelry and baskets.

- **Polychrome jar** (*Polacca Polychrome*) — Earthenware painted with browns, yellows, reds, and black over a red or burnished slip, dated to the 1800s or 1850–1910.
- **Painted pottery bowl** — A flat-based bowl with conical knobs below the rim, painted with bands of triangles, scrolls, or bird wing designs.
- **Pottery vessel motif** (*Ha-har-wug-ti*) — The face of the female mother of six differently coloured Natacka monsters, a common ceremonial design on pots and plaques.
- **Clay ladle** — A short-handled utilitarian ceramic vessel for household use.
- **Winnowing sifter** — Plaited yucca or wicker baskets used for corn processing and cleaning grain.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Hopi silversmiths create fine sterling jewelry with clan symbols, directional signs, and natural forms like snakes, hands, and moon symbols.

- **Bear clan brooch** — A round silver brooch labeled 'Maise Mesa Design' by Eldon James of Second Mesa, carrying a Bear Clan symbol.
- **Directional brooch** — A silver brooch with wind and direction symbols by Wayne Seyesvapetewa of Second Mesa.
- **Pictograph brooch** — A hand-shaped silver brooch incised with a snake symbol by Mark Loma of Second Mesa.
- **Moon symbol bracelet** — A silver bracelet decorated with moon symbols, reflecting celestial and ceremonial significance.
- **Dance ornament** — Necklaces and rattles worn by men during katsina dances and ceremonial performances.

## Music & performance

Hopi music is among the most complex Native American musical styles, featuring hexatonic and heptatonic scales, complex rhythms, and songs tied to katsina dances.

- **Kachina dance songs** — The most complex songs of the Pueblo area, tied to ceremonial performances and the katsina season.
- **Drum** — Percussion instrument of wood with skin, likely aspen, used to accompany ceremonial songs and dances.
- **Bell** — A Rocky Mountain sheep horn bell with rawhide and stone, used in nineteenth-century performances.
- **Gourd-and-wood instrument** (*Ah-Yah*) — A percussion instrument combining gourd and wood elements, recorded in nineteenth-century collections.
- **Work songs** — Songs sung during labor, a practice found among Pueblo peoples but uncommon in other Native American traditions.

## Dance & theatre

Hopi dances center on katsina ceremonies, with masked performers representing spirits and including dramatic elements like snake handling and clowning.

- **Snake Dance** — An annual ceremony in which performers danced while handling live snakes, performed by members of the Antelope clan.
- **Niman or Going Home ceremony** (*Niman*) — A nine-day ceremony closing the katsina season with an outdoor katsina dance in which katsinam bring harvest gifts and dolls for young girls.
- **Hemis group** — The most favoured set of katsinam performers in the Niman ceremony, accompanied by katsina manas.
- **Mud Head Clown** — A carved katsina figure representing a recognized idiom of clowning in Hopi ceremonial tradition.
- **Katsina dancer types** — Various performers including Red Lizard, Snake, Corn, Beard, and Bug dancers, each with distinct costumes and roles.

## Festivals & rituals

The Hopi observe a complete lunar ceremonial cycle with katsina dances occupying half the year, life-cycle naming rituals, and ceremonies meant to maintain world harmony and bring rain.

- **Katsina season** — A six-month period when katsina spirits are believed to live in Hopi villages, followed by their return to the San Francisco Peaks.
- **Naming ceremony** — A life-cycle ritual on the twentieth day of a child's life in which paternal clanswomen each bring a name and gift, often resulting in forty or more names.
- **Katsina society initiation** — A religious society into which individuals are initiated, marking a major life event and opportunity to take a new name.
- **San Francisco Peaks** — A sacred location north of Flagstaff where katsina spirits are believed to reside during the six months they are not in the villages.
- **Village leader role** (*kikmongwi*) — The village leader who promotes civic virtue and proper behaviour and oversees the ceremonial cycle.

## Foodways

Maize is the spiritual and material center of Hopi life, with traditional subsistence farming and corn processing deeply woven into craft and ceremony.

- **Maize cultivation** — The staple crop and central bond of Hopi existence, taught to them by the katsinam and understood as sacred sustenance and prayer offering.
- **Corn processing baskets** — Twilled yucca winnowing and sifting baskets from Second Mesa used to clean and process harvested grain.
- **Hopi tea** (*Thelesperma megapotamicum*) — A native perennial plant collected and dried to make herbal tea, used as a medicinal remedy and yellow dye.
- **Hopi dishes** — Traditional foods served at the tribally owned Hopi Cultural Center restaurant on Second Mesa.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Hopi maintain a complex mythological tradition reaching back centuries, with regional variations and a deliberate reticence about revealing sacred teachings.

- **Passage through four worlds** — The central Hopi creation narrative in which Tawa forms the First World, Spider Grandmother leads creatures upward, and humans eventually emerge into the Fourth World.
- **Emergence point** (*sipapu*) — A hollow reed growing at the opening between worlds, traditionally identified with the Grand Canyon, through which the righteous passed.
- **Spider Grandmother** — A figure in Hopi mythology who leads creatures between worlds and teaches weaving and pottery.
- **Water Clan** (*Patkinyamu*) — Named after the ocean voyage between worlds, with a name meaning 'a dwelling-on-water' or 'houseboat.'
- **Migration narratives** — Stories of clans wandering to the farthest extremity of the land, named for events received on the journey, leaving symbols on rocks.

## Language & religion

Hopílavayi, a Uto-Aztecan language with four regional varieties, is central to Hopi identity, while religion centers on Tawa, Masauwu, and the katsina society.

- **Hopi language** (*Hopílavayi*) — A Uto-Aztecan language with four varieties distinguished by mesa and village, with Mishongnovi judged the most archaic and phonemically complex.
- **Sun spirit** (*Tawa*) — The deity who formed the First World out of endless space and remains central to Hopi religious practice.
- **Earth god and spirit of death** (*Masauwu*) — Skeleton Man, Spirit of Death, Earth God and Keeper of Fire, who gave the Hopi their land and four sacred tablets.
- **Katsina society** — A religious society central to Hopi spirituality, with members initiate young people and perform ceremonial dances.
- **Hopi radio station** (*KUYI 88.1 FM*) — On air since December 2000, part of language revitalization efforts alongside the Hopi Literacy Project and immersion programs.

## Glossary

- *Hopílavayi* — The Hopi language, a Uto-Aztecan family language spoken in four regional varieties.
- *pueblo* — A Hopi village or multi-level adobe settlement with communal spaces and connected dwellings.
- *kiva* — A communal underground or semi-subterranean chamber used for ceremonies and religious rituals.
- *katsina* — Spirits believed to live in Hopi villages during half the year, represented by masked dancers in ceremonies.
- *Polacca Polychrome* — A style of Hopi pottery painted with multiple earth-tone colors, dating to the 1800s–1910.
- *Ha-har-wug-ti* — The female mother of six differently colored Natacka monsters, a design motif on Hopi pottery and plaques.
- *Niman* — The Going Home ceremony closing the katsina season with an outdoor dance in late July or early August.
- *kikmongwi* — The village leader who promotes civic virtue and oversees the ceremonial cycle.
- *Tawa* — The sun spirit who formed the First World in Hopi creation mythology.
- *Masauwu* — Skeleton Man, Spirit of Death, Earth God and Keeper of Fire who gave the Hopi their land.
- *sipapu* — The emergence point or hollow reed between worlds, traditionally identified with the Grand Canyon.
- *Patkinyamu* — The Hopi Water Clan, named for the ocean voyage, meaning 'dwelling-on-water' or 'houseboat.'
- *Thelesperma megapotamicum* — A native perennial plant known as Hopi tea, used for beverage, medicine, and yellow dye.
- *Ah-Yah* — A percussion instrument combining gourd and wood elements used in nineteenth-century Hopi performances.
- *mana* — A female katsina figure who accompanies male katsinam in ceremonial dances.
- *Hemis* — The most favored set of katsina performers in the Niman ceremony.
- *Mud Head Clown* — A carved katsina figure representing the idiom of clowning in Hopi ceremonial tradition.
- *Oraibi* — One of the four original Hopi villages, founded before A.D. 1100 and among the oldest continuously inhabited U.S. settlements.
- *San Francisco Peaks* — A sacred location north of Flagstaff where katsina spirits are believed to reside during the off-season.
