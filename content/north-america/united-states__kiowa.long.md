## Overview

The Kiowa, also called *Ǥáuigú*, are a Native American tribe and Indigenous people of the Great Plains of the United States. They migrated southward from western Montana into the Rocky Mountains in Colorado during the 17th and 18th centuries and reached the Southern Plains by the early 19th century. Their homeland included areas adjacent to the Arkansas River in southeastern Colorado and western Kansas, together with the Red River drainage of the Texas Panhandle and western Oklahoma. In 1867, they were forced onto a reservation in Southwestern Oklahoma. Today the federally recognized Kiowa Tribe is headquartered in Carnegie, Oklahoma; there were 12,000 Kiowa tribal citizens as of 2011. Kiowa belongs to the Tanoan language family and is severely endangered. Kiowa culture is documented through seasonal mobility, horse culture, tipi life, warrior societies, Sun Dance practices, calendars, music, clothing, foodways, and ceremonial objects.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Parfleche** — Kiowa women painted geometric designs on parfleche. The sources identify parfleche as rawhide containing camping goods.

**Hide painting** — Hide paintings adorned the outside and inside of tipis, with special meanings attached to certain designs. Kiowa women also painted geometric designs on hides.

**Beadwork** — Museum records describe multi-coloured beadwork on skin moccasins, glass-bead decoration on black ceremonial moccasins, a black beaded sash, and a black beaded belt. The catalogue records do not identify a named Kiowa stitch or compare the work with neighboring traditions.

**Quillwork** — The sources state that Kiowa women later beaded and quilled hides, but provide no further technical description.

**Black Leggings regalia** — A museum catalogue records a replica of Gus Palmer Sr.’s ceremonial Black Leggings regalia, including an eagle feather headdress, a Warrior costume, a black beaded sash, fringed leggings, black beaded moccasins, and a black beaded belt.

**Blankets** — A museum record describes a rectangular red cotton blanket with fringing and repeated geometric patterns sewn in black, red, and white, connected by white cotton triangles.

**Garlands and wreaths** — Kiowa use *Plantago virginica* to make garlands or wreaths for old men to wear around their heads during ceremonial dances as a symbol of health.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name geometric designs and geometric patterns; they do not provide a fuller motif vocabulary.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe clothing in both Kiowa life and museum records. Kiowa women tanned and skin-sewed hides, painted geometric designs on parfleche, and later beaded and quilled hides. Kiowa horses could be decorated with beaded masks, sometimes with bison horns attached to the sides, and feathers in their manes. During ceremonial dances, old men wore *Plantago virginica* garlands or wreaths around their heads.

The museum records include skin moccasins with multi-coloured beadwork, skin moccasin-boots with glass beads and brass buttons, and replicas of black beaded moccasins made from black leather or hide, glass beads, and metal tinkling cones. The Black Leggings regalia replica includes two fringed black textile leggings, a black beaded sash, a black beaded belt with metal buckle, and a Kiowa eagle feather headdress made from imitation eagle feathers, imitation otter fur, felt, and beadwork. A replica Kiowa Warrior costume includes a backdrop dragger made from fake black otter fur on red textile and decorated with medal buttons. The photographic records show chiefs and women wearing shirts, vests, blankets, shawls, bracelets, rings, scarves, peace medals, and woven hats.

### Architecture

The principal Kiowa dwelling was the *tipi*, also called a skin lodge. Tipis were conical structures made from bison hides shaped and sewn together. Wooden lodge poles, generally 12–25 feet (3.7–7.6 m) long, supported the structure; these poles were harvested from red juniper and lodgepole pine. Tipis had at least one entrance flap and smoke flaps at the top, allowing smoke to escape from the fire pit. Animal pelts and skins lined the floor for warmth and comfort.

The tipi was designed to remain warm during cold winter months and cool during warm summer months. It could be collapsed easily and raised in minutes, making it suitable for nomadic life. The poles could also be used to construct a travois. Hide paintings decorated the outside and inside of some tipis, and the sources state that particular designs had special meanings. Before horses were introduced, domestic dogs pulled travois carrying tipis, belongings, and small children; horses later performed this work.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources document wooden bows, lodge poles, long lances, bows and arrows, tomahawks, knives, war clubs, rifles, shotguns, revolvers, and cavalry swords. Shields were made from tough bison hide stretched over a wooden frame or from bison skulls. Weapons and shields could be decorated with feathers, furs, and animal parts such as eagle claws for ceremonial purposes.

Museum records include a dark-wood bow with a thin sinew cord and two lines of green paint on the shaft. They also record a commercially tanned leather belt decorated with connected pressed chromed-steel medallions bearing engraved and repoussé floral and geometric designs. A woman’s *skärppåse med nålhus* is listed in the Museum of World Culture catalogue.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources record hair and bodily adornment. The ancient Kiowa hairstyle was cut horizontally from the lower outside edge of the eyes to the back of the ears; the corresponding sign for Kiowa in Plains Indian Sign Language refers to this hairstyle. The cut helped prevent hair from becoming tangled while shooting an arrow from a bowstring.

Museum photographs show Kiowa chiefs and women wearing ear ornaments, bracelets, rings, pocket watches suspended from chains, peace medals, scarves, cloth ties, and woven shawls. Kiowa horses were decorated with body paint applied by a medicine man for ritual and spiritual purposes, including good fortune and protection during battle.

## Music & performance

Kiowa music is the music of the Kiowa Tribe of Oklahoma and forms part of the larger Southern Plains Indian music genre. Historically, it was strongly focused on dancing, including the gourd dance. The modern gourd dance follows Kiowa protocol and social mores, and most songs were composed or handed down by Kiowa song makers.

The sources name several song traditions: Brush Dance songs and Buffalo Dance songs, which commemorate the outlawed Kiowa Sun Dance; flag songs honoring warriors of the past, the present, and the people; memorial songs; Peyote songs; War Journey songs, also called 49 songs; war mother songs; victory or scalp dance songs; handgame songs; and hymns. Kiowa music is closely connected with warrior societies, including the Black Leggings Society, the Oh-Ho-Mah Lodge, and the Kiowa Gourd Clan.

Ceremonial elements of the four-day *k'aw-tow* included mock sham battles, purifying sweat baths, erecting the center cottonwood pole, building the arbor, bringing in brush, spreading sand, building the sacred Taimé altar, unveiling the Taimé by the Taimé keeper, distributing shields, ritual body painting, prayers, speeches, dancing, eagle-whistle blowing, visions, and feasting by pledge makers’ and dancers’ families.

The Smithsonian Institution made recordings during the 1930s, 1940s, and 1950s. Early 20th-century Kiowa recordings are held by the Library of Congress’s Archive of Folk Culture, Indiana University American Indian Studies Research Institute, the Oklahoma Historical Society’s Oklahoma History Center, and the Kiowa Tribal Museum. Named singers and musicians include Spencer Asah, Jack Hokeah, Stephen Mopope, Cornel Pewewardy, Tom Mauchahty-Ware, Terry Tsotigh, and the Cozad Singers, Bad Medicine Singers, Zotigh Singers, and Thunder Hill Singers.

## Dance & theatre

The sources describe dances and ceremonial performance. The gourd dance is a major named performance tradition, and the four-day *k'aw-tow* included dancing, ceremonial preparation, ritual body painting, prayers, speeches, eagle-whistle blowing, visions, and feasting. Modern Brush Dance songs and Buffalo Dance songs commemorate the Kiowa Sun Dance, which the source describes as outlawed.

During the annual Sun Dance, bands had distinct responsibilities. The Kogui conducted war ceremonies; the Kaigwu guarded the Sacred or Medicine bundle and the holy lance; the Kinep or Khe-ate performed police duties and ensured security; and the Kâtá supplied bison meat and other foods. The Semat participated equally but had no specific duties.

## Festivals & rituals

The Kiowa held a yearly Sun Dance gathering. The Kiowa name given for the Sun Dance is *K’áu:d̶ó*. The ceremony involved a tipi ring, band obligations, war ceremonies, the Sacred or Medicine bundle called *Tai-mé* or *Taimay*, a holy lance, shields, ritual body painting, prayers, speeches, dancing, eagle-whistle blowing, visions, and feasting. The Kâtá supplied bison meat and other foods; the Kogui conducted war ceremonies; the Kaigwu guarded the Sacred or Medicine bundle and holy lance; the Kinep or Khe-ate ensured security; and the Semat had no specific duties.

The sources also describe funeral practices. Death was associated with dark spirits and negative forces, and spirits were believed to remain around corpses, burial places, former living spaces, and possessions. Families demonstrated grief through wailing, ripping off clothes, shaving the head, and, in some accounts, body lacerations and cutting finger joints. Women and the widowed spouse were expected to express mourning more openly.

Before burial, the body was washed; historically, a woman combed the hair and painted the face. Burial occurred promptly, preferably on the same day, or the following morning if death occurred at night. After burial, most belongings were burned with the tipi. If the tipi or house was shared, surviving relatives moved into a new house.

## Foodways

The Kiowa historically had a nomadic hunter-gatherer society. The American bison or buffalo was their most important food source. Before horses, bison were hunted on foot with arrows and long lances; after horses were acquired, bison were hunted on horseback with bows, arrows, and lances. Women prepared bison meat roasted, boiled, and dried.

Dried meat was made into *pemmican* by grinding lean meat into powder and mixing it with a near-equal weight of melted fat or tallow, sometimes with berries. The mixture was shaped into bars and kept in pouches for use while travelling. The Kiowa also sometimes ate parts of the bison raw. Other hunted animals included deer, elk, pronghorn, wild mustang, wild turkey, bears, lizards, waterfowl, skunks, snakes, and armadillos. During difficult times, they raided ranches for Longhorn cattle and horses.

Women gathered berries, tubers, seeds, nuts, vegetables, and wild fruit. Plants important to Kiowa cuisine included pecans, prickly pear, mulberries, persimmons, acorns, plums, and wild onions. Squash, maize, and pumpkin were acquired through trade and raids. Before metal pots were obtained from Europeans, meat and vegetables were boiled in an earth-lined pit filled with water and heated with fire-heated rocks.

## Oral tradition & literature

One Kiowa origin story says that the people came into the world through a hollow log, but a pregnant woman became stuck, preventing further people from emerging. Ancient names, including *Kútd̶àu* or Kwu-da, meaning “emerging” or “coming out rapidly,” and *Tépdàu* or Tep-da, relate to this origin narrative.

Kiowa calendars recorded two events each year, creating twice as many entries for a given period as the systems of other Plains tribes that kept pictorial “winter counts.” Little Bluff, or Tohausan, was identified as the first Kiowa calendar keeper; Settan, or Little Bear, and Ankopaaingyadete, commonly called Anko, were later calendar keepers. Silver Horn, or Haungooah, kept a calendar and was a respected religious leader.

Kiowa language preservation includes teaching grammars, children’s stories, language classes, and a Kiowa Language Department.

## Language & religion

Kiowa is a member of the Kiowa-Tanoan, or Tanoan, language family. The language is spoken primarily in Caddo, Kiowa, and Comanche counties, Oklahoma, and the Kiowa tribal center is located in Carnegie. UNESCO classifies Kiowa as severely endangered; the source states that it had 20 mother-tongue speakers and 80 second-language speakers in 2007. As of 2024, the Kiowa Tribe had a Kiowa Language Department.

Kiowa has been written in several Latin-alphabet systems. Parker McKenzie developed one Kiowa alphabet and worked with John P. Harrington and other linguists. The current alphabet uses barred letters including B̶, D̶, G̶, and n̶, although Unicode support has been problematic. The sources describe Kiowa spiritual beliefs concerning dark spirits, ghosts, the afterlife, the Sacred or Medicine bundle, medicine men, ritual horse painting, Sun Dance practices, Peyote songs, and hymns.
