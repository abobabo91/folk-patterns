## Overview

The Makah, who refer to themselves as the *qʷidiččaʔa·tx̌*, are an Indigenous people of the Pacific Northwest Coast living in Washington, in the northwestern part of the continental United States. They live in and around Neah Bay, a small fishing village on the northwest tip of the Olympic Peninsula, and their reservation includes Tatoosh Island. Tribal census data from 1999 recorded 1,214 enrolled members, with some 1,079 living on the reservation. The Makah language belongs to the Southern Nootkan branch of the Wakashan family and is closely related to Nuu-chah-nulth and Ditidaht. Makah cultural life is documented through oral traditions, archaeological evidence from Ozette village, whaling, fishing, longhouses, canoes, baskets, and the preservation work of the Makah Cultural and Research Center.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources do not document a full textile tradition in sufficient detail for this section. They state that western red cedar bark was used to make water-resistant clothing and hats, and that cedar roots were used in basket making.

**Twined basket** — Museum of Ethnography records a basket from Washington identified as Makah, with a “Birdcage” pattern. The record describes it as a twined basket.

**Bird cage pattern** — A Museum of Ethnography catalogue record identifies this as a pattern associated with Makah Indians of Washington.

**Coiled basketry and woven basketry** — One catalogue record describes a small, square basket whose walls were made in coiled basketry and whose bottom was made in woven basketry. The same record associates the “Bird cage pattern” with Makah Indians of Washington.

**Basket with lid** — Museum of World Culture records several baskets associated with Makah and the Ozette Indians, including baskets with lids from Cape Flattery, Washington.

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that Makah traditionally used western red cedar bark to make water-resistant clothing and hats. They do not specify garment names, styles, footwear, belts, gender distinctions in dress, or ceremonial clothing. One ethnobotanical statement records that Makah women chew the roots and leaves of *Viola adunca* while giving birth, but the sources do not describe this as clothing or ornament.

### Architecture

Traditionally, the Makah lived in villages consisting of large longhouses made from western red cedar. These structures had cedar-plank walls that could be tilted or removed to provide ventilation or light. Archaeological work at Ozette recovered several buried longhouses, and the excavation produced evidence of Makah activities including whale and seal hunting, salmon and halibut fishing, toys, games, and bows and arrows. The Makah Cultural and Research Center displays replicas of cedar longhouses. The sources do not describe roof forms or architectural decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources do not document Makah ceramics or metalwork. They do document cedar canoes, baskets, bows and arrows, harpoons, lances, and fishing and sealing equipment. Whole western red cedar trees were carved out to make canoes used to hunt seals, gray whales, and humpback whales. The Makah whaling harpoon was traditionally made from two pieces of yew wood spliced together, with a mussel-shell tip and barbs from elk horns; later hunters used a steel “yankee style” head while retaining the yew-wood shaft. Museum records document baskets from Washington, Cape Flattery, Neah Bay, and the Ozette Indians.

## Music & performance

Makah traditional performance is described through songs and ceremonies connected with whaling. After a whale is brought to shore, traditional ceremonies and songs are performed to welcome the whale’s spirit. The whale is then divided in a precise and traditional fashion, with particular families having ownership of particular cuts. The meat and oil are distributed to community members, and much is consumed during a potlatch.

Makah Days, the Makah Tribe’s annual major public gathering, takes place in late August and includes singing, dancing, traditional games, feasting, canoe races, a grand parade, a street fair, and fireworks. The sources do not name instruments, song genres, musical forms, or dance names. The final scene of Jim Jarmusch’s 1995 film *Dead Man* takes place in a reconstructed Makah village; many actors were Makah tribal members, and the dialogue is in the Makah language.

## Festivals & rituals

Makah Days is the Makah Tribe’s annual major public gathering and is held in late August. It features a grand parade, street fair, canoe races, traditional games, singing, dancing, feasting, and fireworks.

Whaling includes ceremonies and songs performed after the whale reaches shore, welcoming the whale’s spirit before the carcass is divided. The “saddle piece,” located midway between the center of the back and the tail, belongs to the harpooner and is taken to his home, where a special ceremony is performed. The sources also state that much of the whale meat and oil is consumed during a potlatch.

The sources do not describe Makah wedding, funeral, coming-of-age, or other life-cycle ceremonies. They state that Makah women chew the roots and leaves of *Viola adunca* while giving birth.

## Foodways

Makah people acquired much of their food from the ocean. Their recorded diet included whale, seal, fish, and a wide variety of shellfish. They also hunted deer, elk, and bear in the surrounding forests, while women gathered nuts, berries, edible plants, and roots.

Makah fishing includes salmon, halibut, Pacific whiting, and other marine fish. The sources state that the Makah traditionally hunted seals, gray whales, and humpback whales, and that whale meat and oil were distributed among community members after a hunt. A great deal was consumed during a potlatch. The catalogue records and Wikipedia sources do not name dishes, cooking methods, staple grains, beverages, or dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

Much of what is known about the traditional way of life of the Makah comes from oral traditions. Makah oral history describes a “great slide” that engulfed part of Ozette village long ago; a mudslide in the early 17th century preserved houses and their contents until excavation in the 1970s. Makah oral history also relates that aboriginal whaling was suspended and re-established several times.

The Makah Cultural and Research Center preserves and presents Makah history and culture. The sources also mention books concerning Makah history and life, including *The Sea Is My Country: The Maritime World of the Makahs* by James Reid, *Voices of a Thousand People* by Patricia Pierce Erikson with Helma Ward and Kirk Wachendorf, and literary works by John Pappas, Will Hobbs, James G. McCurdy, Rosanne Parry, Arlyn Conly, and Frédéric Roux.

## Language & religion

Makah is a Wakashan language belonging to the Southern Nootkan branch. Its endonym is *qʷi·qʷi·diččaq*. It is closely related to Nuu-chah-nulth and Ditidaht and is the only Wakashan language in the United States. Makah has been extinct as a first language since 2002, when its last fluent native speaker died, but it survives as a second language. The Makah Tribe is working to revive it and has established preschool classes for children.

The sources describe traditional ceremonies and songs welcoming the spirit of a hunted whale, but they do not identify a religious system, denomination, historical script, or broader spiritual landscape.
