## Overview

The Greenlandic Inuit are an ethnic group native to Greenland, where they constitute the largest ethnic population. Approximately 89 percent of Greenland’s population of 57,695—51,349 people in 2012—were Greenlandic Inuit. They share ancestry, culture, and history and speak one of the Greenlandic languages. Ethnographically, they consist of the Kalaallit of west Greenland, the Tunumiit of Tunu or east Greenland, and the Inughuit of north Greenland. Inuit live in Polar, Eastern, and Western regions, while 84 percent of Greenland’s land mass is covered by the Greenland ice sheet. Their folk-culture significance is documented through hunting, foodways, skin-sewing, masks, tupilait, ivory carving, wooden maps, and a distinctive Inuit language tradition.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Skin-sewing* is a documented Greenlandic Inuit artistic practice involving the sewing of animal skins. The source identifies the practice.

**Wooden maps of Ammassalik** are carved maps of the Greenlandic coastline associated with customary art-making practices on Ammassalik Island. They were used in the late 19th century.

### Clothing & dress

Historically, women prepared meat and skins, while men hunted. The sources mention animal skins as part of Greenlandic Inuit artistic practice and state that women prepared them.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Greenlandic Inuit art includes carved maps of the Greenlandic coastline, known as Ammassalik wooden maps, which were used in the late 19th century. Sperm whale ivory, including teeth, remains a valued medium for carving.

### Jewelry & body adornment

## Foodways

The primary method of survival for the Thule, ancestors of the Inuit, was hunting seal, narwhal, and walrus and gathering local plant material. Greenland Inuit foodways combine local or traditional dishes with imported foods. Whale skin and dried cod are identified as customary local foods, while sausage and chicken are identified as imported foods. The kinds of whale historically hunted and consumed include Minke and Fin whales. Greenland Home Rule implemented International Whaling Commission quotas that reduced hunting of Minke whales to a maximum of 115 per year and Fin whales to 21 per year.

The sources report that Inuit aged 18 to 25 and 60 and older preferred customary local foods such as whale skin and dried cod over imported foods. People who grew up in villages consumed local Inuit cuisine 31 times a month, while those living in Danish areas consumed it 17 times per month. Reasons given for limited traditional-food consumption included wanting variety, difficulty obtaining traditional foods, and expense.

## Language & religion

Greenlandic, also known by its endonym *Kalaallisut*, is an Inuit language belonging to the Eskimoan branch of the Eskaleut language family. It is primarily spoken by the Greenlandic people and had about 57,000 native speakers as of 2025. Greenlandic is written in the Latin script and is the sole official language of Greenland. Its main variety is Kalaallisut or West Greenlandic; other varieties include *Tunumiit oraasiat*, or East Greenlandic, and *Inuktun*, or Polar Inuit.

Greenlandic was brought to Greenland by the Thule people in the 1200s. Danish missionaries began compiling dictionaries and describing its grammar in the early 1700s. Paul Egede wrote the first Greenlandic dictionary in 1750 and the first grammar in 1760. Samuel Kleinschmidt developed an orthography in 1851, and a new orthography was introduced in 1973. Greenlandic became the sole official language in June 2009. The sources describe a belief that spirits inhabited every human joint.
