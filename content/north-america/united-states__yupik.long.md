## Overview

The Yupik are a group of Indigenous peoples of western, southwestern and southcentral Alaska and the Russian Far East, closely related to the Inuit and Iñupiat. They comprise the Alutiiq or Sugpiaq of the Alaska Peninsula and the coasts and islands of southcentral Alaska; the Yupʼik or Central Alaskan Yupʼik of the Yukon–Kuskokwim Delta, the Kuskokwim River and the northern coast of Bristol Bay as far east as Nushagak Bay, and the northern Alaska Peninsula at Naknek River and Egegik Bay; and the Siberian Yupik, including Naukan, Chaplino and, linguistically, the Sirenik, of the Russian Far East and St. Lawrence Island. The Yupʼik are by far the most numerous Alaska Native group: the 2002 United States Census counted more than 24,000 Yupik in the country, over 22,000 of them in Alaska, most in the roughly 70 communities of traditional Yupʼik territory, with 1,700 more in Russia. Their languages belong to the Eskaleut family. In folk-culture terms they matter for a winter ceremonial life centred on the men's communal house, for gut-skin and grass-fibre making, and for engraved ivory and bone work.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Gut-skin sewing** — The British Museum coat shown here is sewn of pieces of seal gut with a hood, its seams decorated with strips of red- and black-dyed seal gut and a band of darker gut running down from each shoulder. The decoration is structural: the pattern follows the seams and the shoulder line rather than being applied to a surface.

**Coiled and woven grass** — A woman's pair of dance fans in the collection is woven of grass: two coils of fibre in the natural colour with red-dyed accents, with a triangle worked at one side to serve as the handle, finished with caribou hair and a red and green wool tassel opposite the handle. The Wikipedia sources record that women taught girls to tan hides, sew, and weave.

**Qaspeq** — The *kuspuk* (*qaspeq*) is a Yupʼik garment worn by both genders, in Alaska in casual and formal settings alike.

**Motif vocabulary.** Incised ring-and-dot (also given as dot-and-ring) ornament on bone; engraved geometric designs on ivory; painted representational images of whales, fish, birds and skeletonised humans; a carved salmon head terminal.

### Clothing & dress

The sources name few garments. The *kuspuk* (*qaspeq*) is the traditional Yupʼik garment, worn by men and women and still worn in both casual and formal settings in Alaska. For work on water and in wet weather the gut-skin hooded coat described above was sewn from seal gut, with dyed gut strips marking its seams — a translucent, waterproof material rather than a woven cloth. Dance costume is the one context the catalogue records document in detail: paired dance fans, distinct for women and for men. The woman's pair is of woven grass with caribou hair and wool tassels; the man's pair is carved of wood as two concentric circles, one inside the other, wrapped on one side with synthetic string, with five white feathers painted with brown spots inserted into holes around the circle. The sources used here do not describe belts, footwear or head coverings apart from the hood of the gut coat.

### Architecture

Two communal houses organised village life. The *qasgiq*, the men's house, was the community centre for ceremonies and festivals of singing, dancing and storytelling, used mainly in the winter months because families travelled in groups after food sources through spring, summer and autumn. Beyond ceremony it was the school: men taught boys survival and hunting skills and other life lessons there, and taught them to make tools and *qayaq* (kayaks) over the winter. The women's house, the *ena*, stood traditionally right next door, and in some areas the two were connected by a tunnel; there women taught girls to tan hides and sew, to process and cook game and fish, and to weave. Boys lived with their mothers until about five years old, then joined the men in the qasgiq. For a period of between three and six weeks the children switched, the men teaching the girls survival, hunting and toolmaking and the women teaching the boys what they taught the girls. The settlement pattern behind both houses was seasonal: spring and summer at fish camp, winter gathered at village sites. The sources give no description of wall construction, roofing or exterior decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The objects here are of ivory, bone, wood and gut, not clay or metal. A girl's **story knife** is carved in one piece of walrus ivory with engraved geometric designs, four protruding lobes at the grip and a carved representation of a salmon head at the end of the handle; its old ink inscription misidentifies it as a "Chilcat hide scraper". A cylindrical **needle-case** of carved bone is open at both ends so that a leather thong, probably sealskin, can pass through; it carries incised ring-and-dot ornament and a small hole near the upper edge, and its stopper has a wooden plug in the end and a leather thong to hold the needles, decorated in the same dot-and-ring manner. A wooden **paddle** is painted on both sides with whales, fish, birds and skeletonised human figures. In the house, the seal-oil lamp (*naniq*) was an important piece of furniture, and *qayaq* were built during the winter in the qasgiq.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used for this profile do not cover Yupik jewelry, tattooing or hair practices.

## Music & performance

The sources treat Yupik music as part of a wider Inuit, Yupik and Iñupiat tradition based, as far back as can be known, on drums used for dance music. Its described characteristics are recitative-like singing, complex rhythmic organisation, a small melodic range averaging about a sixth, prominent major thirds and minor seconds, and undulating melodic movement. Traditionally the languages had no word matching a European listener's idea of music; the nearest, *nipi*, covers music, the sound of speech and noise alike, and songs seem to have been heard as parts of a whole series of auditory experiences. Before commercial recording, music was used in spiritual ceremonies to ask the spirits for luck in hunting or gambling, and for lullabies; work songs and love songs are notably absent. The main percussion instrument is the wooden frame drum, the *qilaut*, made by boiling and bending strips of wood two to three inches wide into a circular frame with a protruding handle, with animal skin, usually caribou, stretched across and fastened with a string; it can reach a metre across but is usually nearer a yard, and is struck on the rim with a wooden beater, the *qatuk*. Smaller drums and rattles are also used. One instrument is specifically Yupʼik: the *kelutviaq*, a one-string fiddle or lute played by the Yupʼik of Nelson Island and southwest Alaska. European whalers, especially Scots and Irish, brought the accordion and the jig and reel, and Scots-Irish-derived American country music has been popular in the 20th century.

## Dance & theatre

In Yupʼik group dances individuals often stay stationary while moving the upper body and arms rhythmically, the gestures accentuated by handheld dance fans very similar in design to Cherokee dance fans; the restricted motion does not limit the expression, which can be gracefully flowing, bursting with energy, or wryly humorous. The paired fans in this gallery are the instruments of that gesture — grass-woven with caribou hair and wool tassels for women, carved wooden hoops with spotted white feathers for men. Dancing, with singing and storytelling, belonged to the winter ceremonies and festivals held in the qasgiq, and the ceremonies involve a shaman. The sources used here describe no masked or dramatic theatre repertoire.

## Festivals & rituals

The ceremonial year followed the subsistence year. Families spent spring and summer at fish camp and gathered with others at village sites for the winter, and it was in winter that the qasgiq served as the setting for ceremonies and festivals of singing, dancing and storytelling, conducted with a shaman. Many families still harvest the traditional subsistence resources, especially Pacific salmon and seal. The sources name one distinctive life-cycle practice: the Yupʼik are unique among native peoples of the Americas in naming children after the most recent person in the community to have died. The three-to-six-week exchange in which boys were taught the women's skills and girls the men's stands out as a formal stage in growing up. Christian observance enters the record through the Moravian missionaries of the late 19th century, who used Yupik in church services, and through Saint Olga Michael (1916–1979), an Eastern Orthodox priest's wife (*matushka*) canonised in 2023 by the Orthodox Church in America. No named annual festival with a fixed date appears in the sources used here.

## Foodways

The sources describe the food economy rather than a dish repertoire. Families moved with the food supply: spring and summer at fish camp, winter in the villages, travelling in family groups through spring, summer and autumn to follow food sources. Pacific salmon and seal are named as the traditional subsistence resources many families still harvest. Processing and cooking game and fish was among the skills women taught girls in the *ena*, and taught boys during the weeks-long exchange of instruction. Seal supplied more than meat: seal gut was sewn into waterproof clothing, and seal oil fuelled the *naniq* lamp that was an important piece of household furniture. Salmon is also the motif on the ivory story knife, whose handle ends in a carved salmon head, and fish and whales are painted on the wooden paddle. The sources used here name no prepared dishes, no ceremonial food and no beverage tradition.

## Oral tradition & literature

Storytelling was one of the three activities — with singing and dancing — that filled the winter gatherings in the *qasgiq*, and men used the same house to pass on survival knowledge and "other life lessons" to boys. The material trace of girls' storytelling is in this gallery: the walrus-ivory story knife, engraved with geometric designs and ending in a carved salmon head, a traditional Yupʼik girl's implement that a later owner mislabelled a "Chilcat hide scraper". Written literature begins with translation: Moravian missionaries in southwestern Alaska in the late 19th century used Yupik in church services and translated the scriptures into the language, and earlier efforts by Saint Innocent of Alaska, Reverend John Hinz and Uyaquq aimed at religious texts. Language documentation and revival are the current preservation work the sources name, including the Alaska Native Language Center at the University of Alaska Fairbanks and a Yupik immersion school.

## Language & religion

Five Yupik languages, in the Eskaleut family, are still widely spoken; Yupʼik is the most spoken Native language in Alaska by both population and speakers, and the second most spoken indigenous language in the United States after Navajo. Around 20,000 of about 21,000 Central Alaskan Yupʼik still spoke it at home in 2013. Its dialects are General Central Yupʼik (Yugtun), Norton Sound, Hooper Bay/Chevak, and Nunivak Island, called *Cupʼik* or *Cupʼig* — in the Hooper Bay–Chevak and Nunivak dialects both language and people are known as Cupʼik. The name *Yupʼik* (plural *Yupiit*) combines *yuk*, "person", with the post-base *-pik*, "real" or "genuine": "real people". The apostrophe marks gemination of the p. Aleut and Eskimoan diverged around 2000 BCE, the Yupik languages from the Inuit languages around 1000 CE; Sirenik has been extinct since 1997. Writing began with a Latin alphabet developed by Moravian missionaries in Greenland from the 1760s, replaced in the 1960s by a script devised at the University of Alaska with Yupik speakers. Ceremonial life involved a shaman; Moravian and Eastern Orthodox Christianity are both present in the record.
