## Overview

The Akimel Oʼodham, also called Akimel Oʼotham, are an Indigenous people living in central and southern Arizona and northwestern Mexico, in Sonora and Chihuahua. Their communities are associated especially with the Gila and Salt Rivers, including the Gila River Indian Community and the Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community. The Akimel Oʼodham are closely related to the Ak-Chin Oʼodham, Sobaipuri, Tohono Oʼodham, and Hia C-ed Oʼodham. Their language, Oʼodham, is a Uto-Aztecan language spoken in southern Arizona and northern Sonora, Mexico. The Gila River Indian Community has more than 19,000 enrolled citizens. Their folk-culture significance in the sources centers on river-based life, irrigation, farming, basketry, textiles, dwellings, oral tradition, and the religious and ethical worldview called *himdagĭ*.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Basketry** — The Akimel Oʼodham were experts in textiles and produced intricate baskets as well as woven cloth. The museum records include coiled baskets, basketwork platters made with coil technique and geometric ornament, storage vessels with conventionalised designs in black, conical containers with ornamented rims, beaker-shaped baskets with black ornament, and a basketry basket with lid made of vegetable fibre.

**Basketry vessel** — Museum records describe straight-sided, coiled basketry vessels with black geometrical ornament.

**Carrying-sling jar** — A museum object is a jar with sling made of basketry, gum, and cotton cloth.

**Woven fabric cap** — Museum records show a woven fabric cap worn by a Pima chief in studio portraits.

**Woven cloth** — The Akimel Oʼodham produced woven cloth.

**Thatched lodge** — A museum postcard depicts an Akimel Oʼodham woman weaving a large basket in front of her thatched lodge.

**Geometric ornament** — The museum catalogue names geometric ornament, conventionalised black designs, black geometrical ornament, and an ornamented rim; it does not identify named motifs or explain their meanings.

### Clothing & dress

The sources provide little information about everyday or ceremonial clothing. Museum records describe a Pima chief wearing a western-style suit and a woven fabric cap with plaits hanging from it. Another portrait shows a Pima chief in a western-style suit with dreadlocks at the back of his head.

### Architecture

Traditionally, the Akimel Oʼodham lived in thatched wattle-and-daub houses called jacales. The source describes these as huts made from reed-grass mats cut in half and built in the form of a vault on arched sticks; the top was covered with mats thick enough to resist the weather. Inside were a petate for sleeping and gourds for carrying and storing water. Earlier villages were ranchería-style settlements consisting of a loose group of houses, with familial groups sharing a central ramada and kitchen area. Brush *Olas Ki:ki*, described as round houses, were built around this central area. The people also lived seasonally in temporary field houses while tending their crops.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources describe pottery pots and a basketry pot-like vessel in the museum catalogue. The Akimel Oʼodham historically used metal tools and trade goods after European contact. Museum objects include arrow-heads made of grey stone, red stone, and obsidian; wooden game-sticks consisting of four slats; pots made of pottery; baskets; platters; a jar with carrying-sling; and basketry containers.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention plaits hanging from a woven fabric cap in a museum portrait and dreadlocks at the back of the head in another portrait.

## Music & performance

The sources describe ceremonies that often include set speeches. In these speeches, the speaker recites portions of the people’s cosmic myth; the speeches are adapted for each occasion while retaining the same general context. Before American rule, such recitation was especially important in preparation for war. The language source includes a song from *Oʼodham Hohoʼok Aʼagida* and identifies it as an example of the “Storyteller dialect,”. The material supplied does not cover dance accompaniment, instrumental practice, or musical performance at weddings, funerals, or annual festivals.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe Akimel Oʼodham ceremonies involving set speeches and cosmic myth, and state that personal names have particular importance in society. From age ten until marriage, boys and girls were not allowed to speak their own names aloud because doing so could invoke bad luck for the children and their future. People also did not say aloud the names of deceased people, in order to allow them to move on and to call their spirits back among the living.

## Foodways

The ancient Akimel Oʼodham economy was based primarily on farming, hunting, gathering, and extensive trading. They built an extensive irrigation system to compensate for arid conditions, and communities altered canal systems according to changing needs. The people cultivated foods and lived seasonally in temporary field houses while tending their crops. After European contact, new crops such as wheat, livestock, metal tools, and trade goods were introduced. The sources state that the diversion of river water and the introduction of a non-native diet contributed to a high rate of diabetes among the Akimel Oʼodham.

## Oral tradition & literature

Akimel Oʼodham ceremonies often include set speeches in which a speaker recites portions of a cosmic myth. The people also gave children careful verbal instruction in moral, religious, and other matters. The language source reproduces a song from *Oʼodham Hohoʼok Aʼagida* (*Oʼodham Legends and Lore*) by Susanne Ignacio Enos, Dean Saxton, and Lucille Saxton, with an English translation. It also identifies *Taḏai* (“Roadrunner”) as an excerpt from the Oʼodham Piipaash Language Program and presents it in the Salt River dialect and in Saxton orthography.

## Language & religion

Oʼodham is a Uto-Aztecan language of southern Arizona and northern Sonora, Mexico. The language is also called Oʼodham ha-ñeʼokĭ, Oʼottham ha-neoki, Oʼodham ñiok, Oʼodham ñeʼokĭ, Oʼodham ñiʼokĭ, or Oʼotham ñiok, depending on dialect and orthography. The language has Akimel Oʼodham and Tohono Oʼodham dialect groupings, among others. Two common orthographies are Alvarez–Hale and Saxton. The Akimel Oʼodham way of life, *himdagĭ*, is centered on the holy river and encompasses religion, morals, values, philosophy, and worldview. Their religious beliefs and worldview focus on the natural world.
