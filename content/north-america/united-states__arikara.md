## Overview

The Arikara, also known as Sahnish, Arikaree, Ree, or Hundi, are Native Americans whose communities are associated with North Dakota and South Dakota. They are enrolled with the Mandan and the Hidatsa as the federally recognized Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara Nation, and the three tribes are settled on the Fort Berthold Indian Reservation in North Dakota. The Arikara lived as a semi-nomadic people on the Great Plains, combining seasonal movement and bison hunting with agriculture in settled villages. Their women cultivated varieties of corn, an important staple called “Mother Corn,” while agricultural surplus, tobacco, and craft goods supported trading networks on the Upper Missouri. Their language belongs to the Caddoan language family and is close to Pawnee, though the two languages are not mutually intelligible.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state that Arikara people wore two upright bones in their hair in an ancient custom, a practice associated with an interpretation of the name Arikara as meaning “horns.”

### Architecture

Arikara settlements included fortified villages and villages of earth lodges. The Arzberger site near present-day Pierre, South Dakota, contains the remains of a fortified village with more than 44 lodges. During sedentary seasons, Arikara people lived primarily in earth lodges; while traveling or during seasonal bison hunts, they erected portable tipis as temporary shelter. The Arikara rebuilt the Mandan village Mitutanka after taking it over following the smallpox epidemic, and later built earth lodges and log houses near Like-a-Fishhook Village.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They describe the travois, a lightweight transportation device pulled by dogs, consisting of two long poles attached by a harness at the dog’s shoulders, with a ladder-like frame or a hoop made of plaited thongs between the poles. It carried firewood, infants, harvested meat, and loads that might exceed 60 pounds; a single dog could pull a quarter of a bison.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They describe an ancient hair practice involving two upright bones and state that the Arikara traditionally owned 30–40 dogs, which were used for hunting, as sentries, and for transportation.

## Festivals & rituals

They describe seasonal bison hunts, agricultural cycles, village life, warfare, and the Arikara creation myth. In that myth, Nishanu, the great sky chief, created giants; the good giants who survived a great flood became corn kernels under the earth. Nishanu planted corn in the heavens, yielding Mother Corn, who went to the earth to lead the people from the East into the West. She later returned with a leader who taught the people to fight their enemies rather than one another.

## Foodways

Corn was central to Arikara foodways and society. Arikara women cultivated varieties of corn, or maize, and the crop was referred to as “Mother Corn.” The Arikara produced surplus corn and other crops, as well as tobacco, and traded them to the Lakota, the Cheyenne, and more southern plains tribes during short-lived truces. Their villages became a “trading center on the Upper Missouri.” The sources also connect food production with the creation myth, in which the good giants saved from a flood became corn kernels under the earth and Mother Corn led the people.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Arikara creation myth resembles the creation myth of the neighboring Mandan people. It begins with Nishanu creating giants, most of whom were destroyed by a great flood. The surviving good giants became corn kernels, and the corn planted in the heavens produced Mother Corn. She led the people from the East into the West and later returned with a leader who taught them how to fight their enemies rather than one another. The myth is described as an “emergence” style creation myth and is said to reflect Arikara migrations from East to West. Arikara stories and language are documented in several volumes of interlinear texts, a learner’s introductory text, linguistic studies, and an online collection titled Arikara Traditional Narratives.

## Language & religion

Arikara is a Caddoan language, known natively as Sáhniš, and is close to Pawnee although the two languages are not mutually intelligible. The language is now spoken in North Dakota by very few elders. As of 2014, speakers were centered on White Shield, North Dakota, and the language was taught at Fort Berthold Community College, White Shield School, and the Arikara Cultural Center. Language revitalization efforts are continuing, and Arikara is extensively documented. The sources used describe the Arikara creation myth.
