## Overview

The Timbisha are a Native American tribe federally recognized as the Death Valley Timbisha Shoshone Band of California. They are located in south central California, near the Nevada border, especially in and around Death Valley, Furnace Creek, the Great Basin, Saline Valley, Panamint Valley, the northern Mojave Desert, and the southern Owens Valley. The tribe consists of around 300 members, usually 50 of whom live at the Death Valley Indian Community at Furnace Creek within Death Valley National Park; many members spend the summers at Lone Pine in the Owens Valley. The Timbisha language is a Central Numic language of the Numic branch of Uto-Aztecan, closely related to Shoshoni and Comanche. Their cultural history includes long-term relationships with the Death Valley region, named local districts and family groups, ancestral language, sacred places, subsistence practices, baskets, and rock art.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The Timbisha lived in an Indian village near park headquarters at Furnace Creek, constructed by the Civilian Conservation Corps in 1938. The adobe houses built in the 1930s were later described as dilapidated; by the mid-century they lacked electricity, air conditioning, indoor plumbing, and running water. The buildings were called adobe casitas in the account of the Park Service’s destruction of village houses. The sources also mention Indian Ranch, a small rancheria created in 1928 to the east of Death Valley for Panamint Bill and his extended family, and a reservation called the Death Valley Indian Community, established in 1982 at Furnace Creek.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention baskets made by tribal members, including a large collection held at the Eastern California Museum in Independence, California. The sources also describe adobe casitas, houses, springs, water supplies, firewood, plants, and hunting as elements of the community’s material and subsistence history.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They state that the name Timbisha refers to “rock paint” and to rich sources of red ochre used for paint, including the term *tümpisa*.

## Music & performance

The Timbisha language article states that the language is spoken by a few elderly individuals in California and Nevada, that none are monolingual, and that all use English regularly in daily life. The sources describe sacred ceremonies as traditional practices that were conducted at sacred places in the park.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that the National Park Service prohibited Shoshones from using sacred places in the park for traditional sacred ceremonies during the 1950s. The sources also identify Coso Hot Springs as a traditional cultural and healing ritual site associated with the local group called *Koosotsi* or *Muattantsi*. In 2014, the Ridgecrest Petroglyph Festival was created in honor of the petroglyphs and included an intertribal powwow, street fair, and tours to Big and Little Petroglyph Canyons.

## Foodways

They state that the National Park Service prohibited traditional subsistence practices including gathering firewood, gathering plants, and hunting within monument boundaries. The sources mention the self-designation *Tsakwatan Tükkatün*, glossed as “Chuckwalla Eaters,” but describe it as a pejorative term and a loan translation from the Mono people for the Timbisha Shoshone. No food preparation associated with this term is described.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Timbisha language article states that study of the language was carried on by Jon Dayley and John McLaughlin, who wrote grammatical descriptions, and that Dayley published a dictionary. The tribe’s website is described as containing photographs, history, and historical documents beginning with its 1863 treaty. The sources therefore document linguistic and historical preservation efforts, but not a specific oral-literary tradition.

## Language & religion

Timbisha, also called *Tümpisa* or Panamint, is the language of the Native American people who inhabited the region in and around Death Valley, California, and the southern Owens Valley since late prehistoric times. It is one of the Central Numic languages of the Numic branch of Uto-Aztecan and is most closely related to Shoshoni and Comanche. The language was formerly spoken from the Sierra Nevada mountains of eastern California to the region just east of Death Valley in Nevada, including Owens Valley, Indian Wells Valley, Saline Valley, Panamint Valley, and Death Valley, as well as villages along the southern slopes of the Kawich Range in Nevada. Each valley had its own variety, with mostly lexical differences. Timbisha spelling is based on Dayley and uses the Roman alphabet; Ü is used for ɨ and ng for ŋ. The sources describe traditional sacred ceremonies and sacred places.
