## Overview

The Otoe (*Jiwére* in Chiwere) are a Native American people of the Midwestern United States. Historically, they lived as a semi-nomadic people on the Central Plains along the Missouri River in Nebraska, Kansas, Iowa, and Missouri. The Otoe language, Chiwere, belongs to the Siouan family and is closely related to the languages of the Iowa, Missouria, and Ho-Chunk tribes. The Otoe lived in elm-bark lodges while farming and used tipis when traveling. They often left their villages to hunt buffalo, and the American bison became central to their diet and culture. Today, Otoe people belong to the federally recognized Otoe-Missouria Tribe of Indians, headquartered in Red Rock, Oklahoma.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Wool blankets** — Wool blankets appear in the museum records as clothing worn around the waist by Otoe chiefs. They are shown together with leggings, moccasins, necklaces, and other dress items.

**Beadwork** — Beadwork is documented on wool leggings worn by Medicine Horse and Buffalo Chief. The records describe beadwork decoration at the bottom of Medicine Horse’s leggings and beadwork decoration and fringe on Buffalo Chief’s leggings.

**Hide leggings** — Little Pipe is recorded wearing hide leggings with fringe in one studio portrait.

### Clothing & dress

The museum records describe chiefly dress in studio portraits. Little Pipe, an Otoe Chief, wears a decorated fur turban with an eagle feather, ear ornaments, a beaded choker, necklaces, a checked cloth shirt, rings, and a wool blanket around his waist. In another portrait he wears hide leggings with fringe, while a wool blanket lies on the floor beside him.

Medicine Horse, an Otoe Chief, wears a decorated fur turban with an eagle feather, ear ornaments, beaded necklaces, a peace medal, wool leggings with beadwork decoration at the bottom, leg bands, and moccasins. Buffalo Chief wears a decorated fur turban with two eagle feathers, ear ornaments, a beaded choker, necklaces, a peace medal, wool leggings with beadwork decoration and fringe, a wool blanket around his waist, and moccasins.

The sources also record non-Otoe interpreters wearing brimmed felt hats, ties, shirts, vests, suit jackets, trousers, and leather shoes.

### Architecture

The Otoe lived in elm-bark lodges while farming and used tipis while traveling. The sources identify these two building types and connect them with a semi-nomadic life on the Central Plains.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum records do show chiefs holding pipe tomahawks or tomahawks, and Medicine Horse and Buffalo Chief wear peace medals.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The museum records document ear ornaments, beaded chokers, beaded necklaces, rings, and peace medals in Otoe chiefly dress. The records also describe decorated fur turbans with eagle feathers: one feather for Little Pipe and Medicine Horse, and two feathers for Buffalo Chief.

## Music & performance

The sources mention a pipe tomahawk and tomahawks in museum portraits.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources state that the Otoe adopted the horse culture and semi-nomadic lifestyle of the Great Plains and that the American bison became central to their diet and culture.

## Foodways

The Otoe farmed, lived in villages, and often left their settlements to hunt buffalo. After adopting the semi-nomadic lifestyle of the Great Plains, the American bison became central to their diet and culture.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources identify Annette Arkeketa as a poet and playwright and Anna Lee Walters as an author. They also mention the Ioway-Otoe Language project of the Ioway Cultural Institute and the Baxoje, the Ioway Nation, on NativeWeb, but provide no further description of Otoe oral literature or language-preservation practices.

## Language & religion

The Otoe language is Chiwere, or *Jiwére*, and belongs to the Siouan family. It is closely related to the languages of the Iowa, Missouria, and Ho-Chunk tribes. The Otoe were once part of the Ho-Chunk and Siouan-speaking tribes of the Western Great Lakes and Upper Midwest. Around the 16th century, successive groups split off and migrated west and south, becoming distinct tribes including the Otoe, the Missouria, and the Ioway.

The sources mention Christian missionaries and a mission in connection with the Otoe during the 19th century.
