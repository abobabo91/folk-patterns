## Overview

The Hoh, or Chalá·at, are a Native American tribe living near the Pacific Coast of Washington on the Olympic Peninsula, at the mouth of the Hoh River in Jefferson County. Their reservation lies between Forks and Queets and has a land area of 1.929 square kilometres (477 acres). In the 2000 census, 102 people lived there, 81 of whom were Native Americans. The river is central to Hoh culture, providing salmon, other fish, vegetation, and access to fishing, hunting, trapping, and foraging grounds. The sources identify the original Hoh language as Quinault and describe later bilingual use of Quileute and Quinault, followed by widespread adoption of American English.

## Festivals & rituals

The Hoh watershed contained burial sites of ancestors, hidden locations of guardian spirits, family campgrounds, upstream summer-homesites, named landmarks, and sites associated with ritual and mythic occurrences. The Hoh’s annual cycle included salmon and other fish harvested from the river, while hunting, trapping, foraging, and gathering took place in the watershed and along riverside trails.

## Foodways

Salmon and other fish were harvested as part of the Hoh annual cycle, and salmon fishing was central to their lifestyle. The Hoh also hunted seals, sea lions, and whales off the coast for food and other uses. Their main resources included cedar trees, salmon, and nearby vegetation. The sources record the use of *Vaccinium myrtilloides*: its fruit was eaten raw, stewed into berries and made into a sauce, and canned for consumption in winter.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do mention sites associated with ritual and mythic occurrences and hidden locations of guardian spirits, but give no further account of these traditions.

## Language & religion

The name Hoh derives from the Quinault language name for the river, húxʷ. The Hoh call themselves Chalá·at or Chalat', glossed as “People of the southern river,” after their name for the Hoh River, Cha’lak’at’sit or Chalak'ac'it, meaning “southern river.” Their traditional lands and reservation are called ChalAt’i’lo t’sikAti, glossed as “the land belonging to the people who live at the Hoh River.” The original Hoh language was Quinault; after intermarriage with the Quileute, the Hoh became bilingual in Quileute and Quinault, until Quileute was favored. Today, all three tribes have overwhelmingly adopted American English as their home language.
