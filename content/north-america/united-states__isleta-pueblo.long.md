## Overview

Pueblo of Isleta, also known in Southern Tiwa as *Shiewhibak*, is a federally recognized tribe and an unincorporated community in Bernalillo County, New Mexico, United States. The pueblo lies in the Middle Rio Grande Valley, 13 miles (21 km) south of Albuquerque, on a knife-shaped lava flow crossing an ancient Rio Grande channel. It was originally established in the c. 14th century. The population was approximately 4,000 people, and the tribe owns 211,000 acres of land. The inhabitants traditionally speak Isletan Tiwa, one of the two varieties of Southern Tiwa, within the Tanoan branch of the Kiowa-Tanoan language family. Isleta is classified as an Eastern Pueblo because of its traditional dependence on irrigation and subsistence farming. Its documented cultural life includes matrilineal organization, kiva rituals, Kachina traditions, Catholic mission history, and distinctive pottery and clothing represented in museum collections.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used do not document a named Isleta textile tradition, textile technique, or locally specific pattern system.

**Patterned blankets** — Museum catalogue records describe patterned blankets worn around the waist, upper torso, or body in studio portraits of Alejandro Padilla and Ambrosio Abeita. The sources do not identify their material, technique, or local production.

**Cloth shirts and jackets** — British Museum records describe cloth shirts and jackets worn by Isleta men in photographic portraits. The sources do not provide vernacular names or details of manufacture.

**Leg ornaments** — Alejandro Padilla is described as wearing leg ornaments in several photographic records. The sources do not state their material or construction.

**Motif vocabulary.** The museum records name patterned blankets and, on pottery, red diagonal diamonds and red and brown ornaments.

### Clothing & dress

The museum catalogue records document clothing worn by Isleta men in studio portraits. Alejandro Padilla, identified as an Isleta governor, appears in a cloth shirt, jacket, leggings, leg ornaments, boots or moccasins, and a patterned blanket around his waist; in one record he also holds a piece of cloth. Ambrosio Abeita is described in cloth shirts, jackets, leggings, boots or moccasins, and a patterned blanket around his waist. Hats appear on the knee or on a table in several portraits. A British Museum postcard depicts an Isleta woman seated in front of painted pottery, but it does not describe her clothing. The sources do not explain everyday and ceremonial distinctions, women’s dress, belts, head coverings beyond hats, or the production of the garments.

### Architecture

Pueblo of Isleta was built on a knife-shaped lava flow running across an ancient Rio Grande channel. The Isleta Pueblo Historic District is listed on the National Register of Historic Places. The tribe maintains and operates a kiva as a sacred space for particular rituals and ceremonies. The Spanish Mission of San Agustín de la Isleta was built in the pueblo in 1613 by the Spanish Franciscan friar Juan de Salas, while the historic St. Agustin Mission Church is described as having been built by the Tiwa under the direction of a Spanish missionary in 1612. The sources do not describe the pueblo’s house forms, roof construction, building materials, decoration, or settlement layout in detail.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Museum of World Culture records document a small vessel with a cross handle and several pottery bowls from Isleta, New Mexico. One bowl is described as earthenware with exterior painting, a white ground, and red diagonal diamonds along the rim. Another is red on the exterior, with an interior white ground decorated in red and brown and having a wavy edge. The catalogue records state that the objects were obtained through an exchange with the Museum of Anthropology, University of California. A British Museum postcard shows an Isleta woman seated before painted pottery, with pottery placed on the ground in front of her. The sources also describe a turquoise cross mounted in silver, made by Isletans and given by Pablo Abeita to the King of Belgium in 1919.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources document a turquoise cross mounted in silver and made by Isletans. Pablo Abeita gave it to the King of Belgium during the royal visit to Isleta on October 26, 1919. The museum records also describe leg ornaments worn by Alejandro Padilla in studio portraits. No further jewelry types, ritual functions, body adornment practices, tattooing, henna, or hair customs are covered by the sources used.

## Music & performance

The sources used do not identify Isleta musical instruments, song genres, or musical performance traditions. They do document dances and celebrations in historical descriptions. Father Anton Docher wrote that a war captain and other officials had charge of various celebrations and dances, including the “dance of the kings” in January and the “tortoise dance” in February. The sources do not name instruments, describe musical structures, or explain who performs these dances, although the broader religious context includes masked Kachina dancers representing Kachinas at religious ceremonies. The inscription records supplied for this atlas contain no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage element concerning Isleta Pueblo.

## Dance & theatre

Father Anton Docher’s 1913 description names the “dance of the kings” in January and the “tortoise dance” in February among the celebrations and dances overseen by a war captain and other officials. The sources do not describe their choreography, costumes, music, or participants. Religious practice includes Kachina dancers, who are masked members of the community representing Kachinas at religious ceremonies. The Kachina concept is described as having three aspects: supernatural beings, dancers, and small dolls carved in their likeness and given as gifts to children. The sources do not document theatrical traditions, masks outside the Kachina context, or performances intended solely as entertainment.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources name several ritual and seasonal contexts. Father Anton Docher described a “dance of the kings” in January and a “tortoise dance” in February. The tribe maintains and operates a kiva as a sacred space for particular rituals and ceremonies. Traditional religion involves the cult of the Kachinas, spiritual beings personifying aspects of the immaterial and natural worlds. Masked Kachina dancers represent these beings at religious ceremonies, while Kachina dolls are given as gifts to children.

Isleta social organization includes a moiety system in which one moiety is connected with winter practices and the other with summer practices and traditions. The Pueblo also has a Catholic mission history: the Spanish Mission of San Agustín de la Isleta was built in 1613, and the St. Agustin Mission Church remains part of the documented history of the community. The sources do not provide a complete annual festival calendar or descriptions of birth, marriage, coming-of-age, or funeral rites.

## Foodways

The sources describe traditional Eastern Pueblo subsistence farming as relying more heavily on irrigation than the dry farming practices more common in Western Pueblos. Maize, also called corn, was cultivated, while squash and beans were staple Pueblo foods throughout the region. The sources do not name Isleta dishes, cooking methods, ceremonial foods, beverages, dietary rules, or food-preparation utensils. The tribe’s later economic history includes cattle ranching: the Pueblo’s Comanche Ranch is described as running 1,000 head of cattle. No source used for this profile identifies a distinctive Isleta food or beverage tradition.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources do not document Isleta folktales, epic poetry, proverbs, riddles, or a named oral-literary cycle. They do record the Southern Tiwa name *Shiewhibak*, meaning “a knife laid on the ground to play whib,” a traditional footrace. The sources also preserve historical descriptions by Father Anton Docher and museum catalogue identifications of Isleta people and objects. In August 2015, the tribe announced that the Tiwa language would be taught to children at Isleta Elementary School. In 2016, the Kellogg Foundation made grants totaling $148,000 for development of a dual-language Tiwa-English program for young children at the school. These educational efforts are the only documented language-preservation initiative in the supplied sources.

## Language & religion

The inhabitants traditionally speak Isletan Tiwa, one of the two varieties of the Southern Tiwa language. Southern Tiwa belongs to the Tanoan branch of the Kiowa-Tanoan language family. The other Southern Tiwa variety is spoken at Sandia Pueblo, while Northern Tiwa is spoken at Taos and Picuris Pueblos. The Southern Tiwa name for the pueblo is *Shiewhibak*, also written [ʃiexʷibʔàg]. The sources also give Western Keres *Dîiw'a'ane* and Navajo *Naatoohó* as names for the pueblo.

Traditional religion includes the cult of the Kachinas and the use of a kiva as sacred space for rituals and ceremonies. Kachinas are described as spiritual beings associated with immaterial and natural worlds, and Kachina dancers represent them during religious ceremonies. The sources also document Catholicism and the Spanish mission established at Isleta. They do not describe a historical script, current religious affiliation proportions, or detailed forms of religious syncretism.
