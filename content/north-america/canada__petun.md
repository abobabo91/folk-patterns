## Overview

The Petun, also known as the Tobacco people or Tionontati, were an Indigenous Iroquoian people of the woodlands of eastern North America. Their traditional homeland lay south of Lake Huron’s Georgian Bay, in what is now the Canadian province of Ontario. They were closely related to the Wendat, or Huron, and were organized as a confederacy. The Petun had eight or nine villages in the early 17th century and are estimated to have numbered around 8,000 before European contact. Epidemics between 1634 and 1640 reduced their population, and conflict with the Haudenosaunee in 1648–1649 virtually destroyed their society. Surviving Petun joined refugee Wendat and Wenrohronon and became the Wyandot.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources describe the Late Ceramic period as characterized by longhouse-based fortified settlements, often enclosed by palisades.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources mention a disc pipe found at a Petun site dated to 1630–47. The pipe was of a type similar to objects found at Odawa sites, indicating an element of material culture shared with the Odawa.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The Jesuit Relations in 1652 described tattooing among the Petun and the Neutrals. The account states that in the nation called the Tobacco, the practice was so common that it was not known whether a single individual lacked painted decoration on some part of the body.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do, however, document funerary practices in the broader Ontario Iroquoian archaeological tradition: the Early Ceramic period saw the appearance of more elaborate funerary practices, including secondary burials and the use of ossuaries.

## Foodways

The sources identify agriculture as increasingly important during the Late Ceramic period, while the importance of hunting decreased. Corn was introduced to the area during the Early Ceramic period, dated to 3000–1100 BP. Historical sources connect the name “Petun” with tobacco cultivation and trade, but the article notes that no contemporary French accounts mention tobacco cultivation at Petun settlements. A 1619 text by Samuel de Champlain records that the Petun grew corn, while a 1632 table accompanying a map attributed to Champlain explicitly mentions the cultivation and trade of tobacco.

## Oral tradition & literature

The main contemporary written source for the Petun before their dispersal was the Jesuit Relations, written from the perspective of predominantly French Jesuit missionaries in Huronia. Information about the Petun was often second-hand, and early sources did not clearly distinguish between the Wendat, Petun, and Neutral confederacies.

## Language & religion

The Petun spoke an Iroquoian language and shared a similar dialect with the Wendat Nation. They were independent of the Five Nations of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy. The sources describe the Petun as politically separate from the Wendat, although French observers considered their customs similar. The Petun were composed of two sub-groups that Jesuits called the “Nation of the Wolves” and the “Nation of the Deer.” The article also records alliances with the Neutral Nation and the Odawa. The sources mention that some Wendat converted to Christianity.
