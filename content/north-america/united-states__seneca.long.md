## Overview

The Seneca, whose own name is *Onödowaʼga꞉ʼ*, are an Iroquoian-speaking Indigenous people historically associated with the southern shore of Lake Ontario and the territory between the Genesee River and Canandaigua Lake. They were the westernmost nation of the Haudenosaunee and were called “The Keepers of the Western Door.” In the 21st century, more than 10,000 Seneca live in the United States, with communities in western New York and Oklahoma, while approximately 1,000 live in Canada near Brantford, Ontario. Seneca communities are also documented in the Allegheny River region, the Niagara River area, Pennsylvania, and Ontario. Their folk-culture record includes longhouses, agricultural practices involving corn, beans, and squash, oral mythology, language revitalization, masks, rattles, moccasins, clubs, bowls, and other museum-held objects.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Seneca men and women wore clothing that included moccasins; museum records document pairs made from native-tanned skin, with porcupine quill, silk, or glass, and dating to ca. 1830 and ca. 1808. The British Museum records a photographic portrait of Lewis Bennett (Deerfoot), a Seneca man wearing moccasins, leggings, a fringed frock coat, and ear ornaments while holding a hat.

Men of both Seneca branches wore the same headgear as other Haudenosaunee people: hats with dried cornhusks on top. Seneca men wore theirs with one feather standing straight up. A museum catalogue records a Seneca headgear object from the United States, associated with Levi Cook, Seneca, Cattarangue. The sources do not describe women’s everyday garments in detail. Museum records also document brooches and a mother-and-child doll made of corn husk, cotton, wool, native-tanned skin, glass, and silk.

### Architecture

Seneca communities traditionally lived in longhouses, large buildings up to 100 feet long and approximately 20 feet wide. Related families shared them, and a longhouse could hold up to 60 people. Hearths stood in the central aisle, with two families sharing a hearth. Major villages were protected by wooden palisades. Ganondagan had 150 longhouses and was the largest Seneca village of the 17th century; Chenussio had 130 longhouses and was a major village of the 18th century.

Seneca towns were relocated every ten to twenty years as soil, game, and other resources were depleted. During the nineteenth century, many Seneca adopted log cabins similar to those of their American neighbors. The sources also describe villages near rivers and agricultural fields, with settlements extending through the Genesee River valley, around Seneca Lake, and along the Allegheny River.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Museum records document a wooden bowl from New York, United States, identified with the Seneca and the League of the Iroquois. The catalogue describes it as a wooden bowl or vessel and records that it was obtained through exchange with the Denver Art Museum. A British Museum record describes a wooden gunstock club ornamented with brass nails and fitted with a pointed, flat iron blade.

The Seneca economy included hunting and gathering, fishing, and the cultivation of corn, beans, and squash. Women processed medicinal plants, roots, berries, nuts, and fruit, while men cleared forests for fields and maintained hunting and fishing grounds. The sources do not provide a fuller account of Seneca ceramics or metalworking.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Museum records document Seneca and Haudenosaunee brooches. One is described as being shaped like a masonic cross, with a pattern on its front; another is a simple square with the pin attached at one corner. The catalogues associate these objects with Seneca individuals in Cattarangus, New York, and with Oneida and Seneca provenance.

The British Museum’s portrait record states that Lewis Bennett (Deerfoot) wore ear ornaments. The Seneca archery description records that bows were made of unbacked hickory and that arrows could be made from split hickory, dogwood, or Viburnum. The sources do not describe tattoos, hair practices, or named forms of ritual body adornment.

## Music & performance

The museum records document turtle-shell rattles associated with Seneca and Iroquois ceremonial life. One object is named *Kanyáhte’ ká’nowa’* and is described as a turtle-shell rattle. Another catalogue record identifies a rattle of tortoise shell from the eastern woodland area and states that it was used in the Iroquois religious society, where participants often used masks.

The rattles were used by the mask society and in the Great Feather Dance. One catalogue gives the phrase “ga no wa gusta we sa” for the object and states that it was used in dances and by the mask society. The sources also mention Seneca-language music broadcasts by the former Seneca-owned radio station WGWE, although the station broadcast primarily in English. It featured a daily “Seneca Word of the Day,” broadcast a limited amount of Seneca-language music, and occasionally used Seneca in its broadcasts.

The sources do not name Seneca instruments other than turtle-shell or tortoise-shell rattles, and they do not document named song genres or a detailed musical system.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name the Great Feather Dance and state that turtle-shell rattles were used in it. Museum records also associate rattles with dances and ceremonies of the mask society. A wooden mask catalogue record states that masks were used in dances and ceremonies by the “masksällskapet Seneca” in Cattarangua.

Another museum record describes two wooden false face masks. They were used by shamans and medicine men in secret ceremonies in which a person with poor health was healed from sickness. The catalogue identifies the objects with the eastern woodland area, Iroquois, and Seneca. The sources do not provide a fuller description of theatrical plots, named dramatic genres, choreography, or distinctions between entertainment and ceremonial performance.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not document an annual Seneca festival calendar or provide dates for recurring seasonal festivals. They do describe ritual and ceremonial contexts associated with the mask society, turtle-shell rattles, the Great Feather Dance, and wooden false face masks.

Seneca mythology records stories transmitted orally and later written down in the nineteenth century. It describes spirits, dreams, magical power, monsters, witches, and sorcerers. The museum catalogue states that false face masks were used by shamans and medicine men in secret ceremonies connected with healing. Another mask was used in dances and ceremonies by the Seneca mask society.

The sources also describe Haudenosaunee social organization through matrilineal clans. Seneca clans include Bear, Wolf, Turtle, Beaver, Deer, Hawk, Snipe, and Heron, divided between an animal side and a bird side. Children belonged to their mother’s clan, and the presiding elder was called the “clan mother.” The sources do not describe Seneca birth, coming-of-age, wedding, or funeral rites.

## Foodways

Seneca subsistence traditionally depended on hunting and gathering, fishing, and cultivating varieties of corn, beans, and squash. These vegetables were the staple of the Haudenosaunee diet and were called “the three sisters,” *työhe'hköh*. Seneca women generally grew and harvested them and also gathered and processed medicinal plants, roots, berries, nuts, and fruit.

Men hunted and fished in productive grounds away from towns and villages. These locations were altered and maintained to encourage game. The sources do not name prepared dishes, cooking methods, beverages, ceremonial foods, dietary rules, or food-related festivals. They also do not provide a detailed account of serving vessels, although a museum catalogue records a Seneca wooden bowl and describes it as a bowl, vessel, and a bowl-and-spoon object.

## Oral tradition & literature

Seneca oral history states that the people originated in a village called Nundawao near the south end of Canandaigua Lake, at South Hill. Seneca mythology was transmitted orally, and stories began to be written down in the nineteenth century. Jeremiah Curtin began transcribing stories in 1883, and Arthur C. Parker published *Seneca Myths and Folk Tales* in 1923.

Parker classified Seneca stories into six groups: “When the World was New,” “Boys who Defied Magic and Overcame it,” “Tales of Love and Marriage,” “Horror Tales of Cannibals and Sorcerers,” “Tales of Talking Animals,” and “Tales of Giants, Pygmies, and Monster Bears.”

Named figures include Eagentci, the Earth-mother or First Mother; Djieien, a man-sized spider; Djodiʼkwadoʼ, a horned serpent; Dagwanoenyent, a northern witch depicted as a whirlwind; Gaasyendietha, a fire-breathing dragon inhabiting Lake Ontario; Hagondes, a cannibalistic trickster and clown spirit; and Nyáʼkwaehe꞉h, a giant hairless bear. The sources also describe Seneca language revitalization efforts and the publication of language-learning tools.

## Language & religion

Seneca is an Iroquoian language of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy and is most closely related to Cayuga, Onondaga, Oneida, and Mohawk. The language is spoken primarily in western New York, on the Allegany, Cattaraugus, and Tonawanda reservations, and in Ontario on the Grand River Six Nations Reserve. The speech community has dwindled to approximately one hundred native speakers, while revitalization efforts are underway.

The Seneca Faithkeepers School was founded in 1998 as a five-day-a-week school teaching Seneca language and tradition. In 2013, middle school students served as announcers for a lacrosse match in Seneca. The sources state that many Seneca adopted Christianity during the nineteenth century. Seneca mythology describes spirits throughout nature, orenda or magical power, souls of living creatures, a heaven world, dreams as experiences of the soul, and monsters, wizards, witches, and sorcerers. Museum records connect masks and rattles with the mask society, healing ceremonies, and the Iroquois religious society.
