## Overview

The Innu are an Indigenous people of northeastern Labrador and Quebec who have lived as hunter-gatherers for thousands of years, following seasonal migrations to hunt caribou, moose and other game. They developed a distinctive hide-working tradition centered on caribou skin, creating decorated clothing, moccasins and the famous tea doll. Since the 1980s, Innu musicians like Kashtin have created the most widely heard Indigenous-language popular music in Canada. Their language, Innu-aimun, belongs to the Algonquian family and has four regional dialects spoken by over 10,000 people.

## Material culture

Buckskin from caribou and deer hide was the foundation of Innu material culture, used for clothing, shelter, storage and decorated with beads, paint and quills.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Innu weavers and craftspeople decorated caribou and deer hide with painted designs, quillwork, seed beads and embroidery, creating distinctive patterned textiles and garments.

- **Buckskin** — Softened leather made by scraping hides, freezing them, stretching on frames, rubbing with animal brain and pine needle tea, smoking and tanning — used for clothing, boots, moccasins, house covers and storage.
- **Tea doll** (*Innu tea doll*) — A cloth doll stuffed with tea made by women from caribou hides and cloth scraps, carried by nomadic families as both plaything and essential freight, with hide face, black yarn hair, embroidered features and seed bead decorations.
- **Beadwork on tanned hide** — Borders of seed beads in green, red, blue, yellow, orange and white run around moccasin apertures and vamps, with smaller beaded patches carrying single motifs and hanging panels attached to smoking pipes.
- **Cotton thread embroidery and appliqué** — Tanned deer-hide moccasins are stitched with brown cotton thread and finished with red cotton bands and drawstrings; moccasins and children's slippers feature embroidered red flowers and cross-hatched borders.
- **Painted and quilled caribou skin** — Caribou skins decorated with painted or quill designs, then made into clothing and other garments by craft makers.

### Clothing & dress

Men wore caribou pants, boots and long buckskin shirts; women wore long buckskin dresses, with both wearing bone and bead necklaces and keeping their hair long.

- **Moccasins** — Tanned deer hide sewn with brown cotton thread and made for both adults and children, decorated with beadwork, embroidery and floral designs.
- **Caribou pants and boots** — Made by women from hide and worn by men alongside buckskin long shirts, continuing to be made from caribou hide even after trade cloth arrived.
- **Long buckskin dresses** — Traditional dress for women, often replaced in modern times with manufactured pants and jackets.
- **Bear-claw necklace** — Worn by a man who had killed a bear, marking joy and his initiation into adulthood.
- **Bone and bead necklaces** — Worn by both women and men as everyday adornment.

### Architecture

The Innu traditionally lived in portable wigwams covered with birchbark in the south and caribou hide in the north, supporting seasonal hunting migrations.

- **Wigwam** — A portable dwelling covered with birchbark in the forested south or caribou hide in the north, used during seasonal hunting migrations.
- **Portable skin tent** — Animal skin structures that supported the Innu's seasonal hunting migrations and nomadic way of life.
- **Modern house with central metal stove** — The metal stove has replaced the traditional hearth in modern Innu houses.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Buckskin was the principal material for Innu household equipment; smoking pipes had carved soapstone bowls, wooden stems and beadwork panels.

- **Smoking pipe** — Used by both women and men, with women's pipes shorter; carved soapstone bowl, short wooden stem and hanging beadwork panel.
- **Canoe** — Traditional summer travel vessel still in use in some communities.
- **Snowshoes** — Winter travel equipment still used where hunting and trapping require them.
- **Toboggan** — Winter transport equipment used for carrying goods across snow.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The Innu wore bone and bead necklaces, with bear-claw necklaces marking a man's initiation to adulthood, and maintained gender distinctions through their hair.

- **Bone and bead necklaces** — Worn by both women and men as everyday adornment.
- **Bear-claw necklace** — Made and worn by a man who had killed a bear, marking joy and his initiation into adulthood.
- **Hair coils** — Women traditionally wore their hair long or in two coils, while men wore theirs long, marking gender distinction.
- **Seed bead hair ties** — Blue, red and white seed beads strung for hair ties, worn by women and reproduced on tea dolls.

## Music & performance

Innu folk music is dance and percussion-centred, built around the teueikan frame drum, and has become the most widely heard Indigenous-language popular music in Canada.

- **Teueikan** — A Montagnais frame drum with snares, the core instrument of Innu folk music.
- **Philip Mackenzie** — Created a singer-songwriter tradition in Innu-aimun, working at first with guitar and teueikan.
- **Kashtin** — A folk-rock band that began its career at Innu Nikamu festival in the 1980s and brought Innu-aimun broad exposure in Canada and France.
- **Akua tuta** — An Innu song meaning 'Take care of yourself' by Florent Vollant, one of the most accessible texts in any Indigenous North American language, appearing on over fifty websites.
- **Innu Nikamu** — An annual festival in Quebec meaning 'The Innu Sings', a major gathering for Native American music where Kashtin began its career.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is central to Innu folk music, and Innu communities have been documented in films showing Indigenous people speaking their own languages.

- **Carcajou et le péril blanc** — A documentary series by Arthur Lamothe, among the first films in cinema history to show Indigenous people speaking their own languages.
- **Kuessipan** — A feature film set in Innu communities.
- **Ntapueu … i am telling the truth** — A 1997 film by Marjorie Beaucage documenting the Innu Nation's campaign over the environmental impact of the Voisey's Bay mining project.

## Festivals & rituals

Pre-colonial ritual life centred on respect for animals, especially the caribou; shamans performed shaking tent rites and hunters made offerings of bones and skulls to animal spirits.

- **Shaking tent** — A shamanic rite performed by Innu spiritual leaders.
- **Innu Nikamu** — An annual music festival held in Quebec as a gathering for Native American music.
- **Bear hunting initiation** — Killing a bear marked a man's coming of age, bringing joy and initiation into adulthood, after which the hunter wore a necklace of the bear's claws.
- **Caribou Master** — A spirit revered by the Innu, reflecting the caribou's central importance to their life and subsistence.
- **Animal spirit offerings** — Hunters appeased animal spirits with offerings of bones and skulls placed in trees or on raised platforms.

## Foodways

Caribou and moose were the staples, hunted alongside beaver, bear, fish and game birds; gathered plant foods included berries, nuts and maple sap.

- **Pemmican** — Preserved meat made with moose or caribou.
- **Bannock** — Oat bread introduced by the French in the sixteenth century that became a staple and is still eaten.
- **Apon** — A cornbread made from cornmeal traded from the Iroquois, Algonquin and Abenaki, later sometimes including oat or wheat flour.
- **Pine needle tea** — An infusion drunk to prevent infections and colds brought on by harsh weather, also used to soften hides.
- **Balsam fir inner bark** — The inner bark of Abies balsamea is grated and eaten to benefit the diet.

## Oral tradition & literature

Innu oral traditions feature Tshakapesh, a lunar folk hero, and carry two versions of first contact with French explorers at Uepishtikueiau.

- **Tshakapesh** — A lunar folk hero of particular importance in Innu oral tradition.
- **First contact narrative** — Two oral history versions circulate about the encounter with Samuel de Champlain's French explorers at Uepishtikueiau: one describes French using gifts to create dependence, the other that the French resented the Innu once they no longer needed teaching.
- **Pan-Innu dictionary** — A comprehensive dictionary covering all dialects spoken in Quebec and Labrador, published in 2013 in Innu, English and French.

## Language & religion

Innu-aimun is an Algonquian language with four dialects and over 10,000 speakers; pre-colonial religion centred on animal respect, now joined by Catholic, Moravian and Anglican Christianity.

- **Innu-aimun** — An Algonquian language also called Montagnais, polysynthetic and head-marking with four verb classes by transitivity, spoken by over 10,000 people.
- **Southern Montagnais** — One of four Innu-aimun dialects, spoken in Mashteuiatsh and Betsiamites.
- **Eastern Montagnais** — One of four Innu-aimun dialects, spoken in Ekuanitshit, Nutashkuan, Unamen Shipu and Pakuashipi.
- **Central Montagnais** — One of four Innu-aimun dialects, spoken in Uashat, Maliotenam and Matimekosh.
- **Labrador-Montagnais** — One of four Innu-aimun dialects, spoken in Sheshatshit, marked by the shift from y to n.

## Glossary

- *Innu-aimun* — The Algonquian language spoken by the Innu people
- *Nitassinan* — The Innu homeland, meaning 'our land'
- *Innu-assi* — Another name for the Innu homeland, meaning 'Innu land'
- *Buckskin* — Softened caribou or deer leather used for clothing and household goods
- *Teueikan* — A Montagnais frame drum with snares, core instrument of Innu music
- *Innu Nikamu* — An annual music festival in Quebec, meaning 'The Innu Sings'
- *Pemmican* — Preserved meat made from moose or caribou
- *Bannock* — Oat bread introduced by the French that became an Innu staple
- *Apon* — Cornbread made from traded cornmeal, later with oat or wheat flour
- *Shaking tent* — A shamanic rite performed by Innu spiritual leaders
- *Tshakapesh* — A lunar folk hero in Innu oral tradition
- *Caribou Master* — A spirit revered by the Innu, reflecting the caribou's importance
- *Uepishtikueiau* — The coastal land that became Quebec City, site of first contact with French explorers
- *Montagnais* — Another name for Innu-aimun language
