## Overview

The Chumash lived in over 150 independent villages along the California coast and Channel Islands, speaking variations of one Chumashan language. They are named for their craft of bead making and known for creating shell disc beads that served as currency in wide trade networks. Their material culture centered on basketry, stone and bone objects, and the distinctive sewn-plank canoe that enabled deepwater fishing and trade between settlements. Today about 5,000 Chumash people continue their traditions, with language revitalisation efforts underway.

## Material culture

Chumash material culture was made from natural local materials—stone, bone, shell, wood and plants—with no pottery or metalwork produced locally.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Coiled basketry is the principal pattern-bearing craft of the Chumash, named alongside bead manufacture and rock art as defining parts of their culture.

- **Coiled bowl basketry** (*'ayakuy*) — A coiled bowl or basket used in the preparation of acorn meal, made of deergrass, rush and sumac.
- **Coiled basketry hats** (*somelelu*) — A woven sombrero or Padre's hat likely made from bulrush and sedge, woven to the right by right-handed weavers.
- **Trinket baskets** — Small coiled baskets collected as a distinct named form.
- **Rock art with water creatures** — Water creatures often depicted in rock art, thought to depict celestial bodies and bring water or appease underworld spirits.
- **Scorpion tree arborglyph** — A six-legged creature wearing a headdress with a crown and two spheres, carved as an arborglyph and used in observing stars and the Chumash calendar.

### Clothing & dress

Few details survive about everyday Chumash dress, but headgear, hair ornaments and shell bead necklaces and ear-rings are recorded.

- **Coiled basketry hat** (*somelelu*) — A woven sombrero or Padre's hat worn by adults, likely made from bulrush and sedge.
- **Child's coiled basketry hat** (*'epsu' ho' l-čičiwun*) — A smaller coiled basketry hat made for a child.
- **Bone hair-pins** — Hair was dressed with bone pins, including one made from a pelican wing bone.
- **Third gender clothing** (*'aqi*) — Biological males who performed women's work and wore clothing traditionally of women, closely tied to non-procreative sexual activity.

### Architecture

The Chumash lived in over 150 independent villages spread across three environments: interior plains and mountains, the coast, and the Northern Channel Islands.

- **Ceremonial and residential mounds** — Large piles of shellfish waste were expanded over time to create ceremonial and residential mounds.
- **Coastal trade villages** — Villages along the mainland coast emerged as intermediaries in trade between island, coastal and mainland groups.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Chumash craftspeople worked stone, bone, shell and wood into everyday and ceremonial objects, but produced no pottery or metalwork locally.

- **Stone mortars and grinding tools** — Stone mortars including a round-based form survive in quantity; the Millingstone Horizon shows seed processing with metates and manos.
- **Monterey chert tools** — Monterey chert was worked into leaf-shaped points or knives, finely made lance points, and drills for making shell fish-hooks.
- **Bone and wood spear-heads and harpoon foreshafts** — Bone and wood served for spear-heads and for harpoon foreshafts with a bone barb lashed on with fibre cord.
- **Bone spoons** — Bone, possibly elk antler, was carved into spoons with a wide oval bowl.
- **Sewn-plank canoe** (*tomol*) — A sewn-plank canoe whose design allowed deepwater fishing and carried trade between villages.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Bead making is embedded in the Chumash people's own name, with shell beads serving as currency in exchange networks reaching to Arizona.

- **Shell disc beads** — Shell beads were made from the marine olive snail from the Millingstone period onward and served as currency in an exchange system.
- **Necklaces** — Surviving necklaces combine shell discs, single and multicoloured glass beads, stone pendants and cylindrical ivory or bone beads.
- **Ear-rings** — Ear-rings alternate shell discs with blue-green glass beads.
- **Abalone ornaments and fishhooks** — Red abalone shell was used for beads and ornaments as well as fishhooks.

## Festivals & rituals

Chumash cosmology centers on a multilayered universe where all things are alive, intelligent, dangerous and sacred, with shamans and astronomers tracking celestial movements.

- **Three-layered universe** (*'antap, C'oyinahsup, Alapay*) — The Middle Region where humans live rests on two giant snakes; the lower world is home to snakes, frogs and salamanders; the upper world is where the sky people live.
- **Sky figures** (*Shnilemun, Slo'w*) — Sky Coyote is a protector who looks out for the welfare of all, and Eagle, ruler of Polaris, keeps the other stars in order.
- **Scorpion tree observation** — Shamans took part in carving the scorpion tree, used in observing the stars and in the Chumash calendar; the position of Ursa Major around Polaris marked the time of year.
- **Annual tomol crossing** (*Elye'wun*) — The annual crossing to the Channel Islands, first made on 9 September 2001, when the Elye'wun ('swordfish') was paddled to Limuw (Santa Cruz Island).

## Foodways

Acorns were the staple food despite the work of removing their toxins, supplemented by deer, marine foods and gathered plants.

- **Acorn meal preparation** — Acorns of the coast live oak were best, ground into a paste easy to eat and to store for years, the mush usually served unseasoned with meat and fish.
- **Deer hunting** — Deer was the most important land mammal, hunted by dressing as deer and grazing alongside the animals until within arrow range.
- **Coastal seafood** — Abalone and clam are named as the basis of coastal cuisine, with mussels and a wide array of clams gathered from tidal zones.
- **Deepwater fishing** — Coastal and island villages took larger fish, swordfish among them, from the tomol.

## Oral tradition & literature

Chumash oral narratives were collected and published in the twentieth century, with extensive unpublished fieldnotes and a modern dictionary documenting the languages.

- **December's Child** — Chumash oral narratives collected and published by Thomas Blackburn in 1980.
- **Samala-English Dictionary** — The first Chumash dictionary, appearing in April 2008 with 4,000 entries and more than 2,000 illustrations.
- **John Peabody Harrington's fieldnotes** — Unpublished fieldnotes of the linguist who worked on all attested Chumashan languages, the primary documentation for all six languages.

## Language & religion

Six Chumashan languages are attested, known for consonant and low-vowel harmony, with revitalisation programmes underway for four languages; Catholicism arrived with the missions.

- **Six attested Chumashan languages** (*Obispeño, Purisimeño, Ineseño, Barbareño, Ventureño, Island Chumash*) — The divergent Obispeño, the Central group of Purisimeño, Ineseño, Barbareño and Ventureño, and Island Chumash, which had separate dialects on Santa Cruz, San Miguel and Santa Rosa.
- **Chumash Revolt** — The Chumash Revolt of 1824, beginning at Mission Santa Inés, was one of the largest and most successful revolts of Native Catholic neophytes in the Spanish West.
