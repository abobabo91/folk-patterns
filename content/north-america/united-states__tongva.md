## Overview

The Tongva are an Indigenous people of California whose traditional territory includes the Los Angeles Basin, the Southern Channel Islands, the coastal region of Los Angeles County, the northwest portion of Orange County, and off-lying islands. Their lands covered approximately 4,000 square miles (10,000 km2), and before colonization they lived in as many as 100 villages, identifying primarily by village. The people are also known as Gabrieleño, Gabrielino, Fernandeño, and Kizh, although the Spanish names were not their autonyms. In 2008, more than 1,700 people identified as Tongva or claimed partial ancestry; in 2013, the four groups seeking federal recognition reported more than 3,900 members in total. Tongva belongs to the Takic subgroup of the Uto-Aztecan language family. Its cultural history includes village-based social life, extensive trade through *te'aats*, creation stories, resistance to missionization, and ongoing language revitalization.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Tongva villages were the centers of social life, and one or two clans usually constituted a village. The sources describe the construction of Mission San Gabriel and the first town of Los Angeles at the Gabrieleño settlement of Yaanga, where missionaries and Indian neophytes built the town in 1781.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do record lithic and shell bead production and use during the mission period, as well as Tongva ancestral remains and artifacts whose control has been affected by the lack of federal recognition.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They mention shell bead production and use but provide no further description of bead forms, materials, or ceremonial functions.

## Music & performance

Tongva communities maintained dances, celebrations, and rituals despite mission efforts to suppress them. Nicolás José publicly participated in Catholic sacraments while privately remaining committed to traditional dances, celebrations, and rituals. In 1782, missionaries and the governor of California instituted a ban on dances and ceremonies, which Nicolás José described as intolerable because it prevented mourning ceremonies. The sources also state that many converts retained traditional practices in domestic and spiritual contexts. They mention *te'aats*, plank-built boats used in an extensive trade network. The Tongva language article states that there are no audio recordings of people speaking the language, although a few scratched wax cylinder recordings of Tongva songs exist.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used describe Tongva dances and ceremonies as important traditional practices, including mourning ceremonies and late-eighteenth-century dances, celebrations, and rituals. Mission authorities attempted to prohibit these activities, and resistance included the continued performance of traditional practices by converts.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe a religious worldview in which humans were one strand in a web of life rather than the apex of creation. Humans, plants, animals, and land were understood to participate in a reciprocal relationship of mutual respect and care, expressed in creation stories. The Tongva understand time as nonlinear, and the sources state that there is constant communication with ancestors.

Mourning ceremonies were significant enough that the mission prohibition against dances and ceremonies was cited as a reason for Nicolás José’s participation in the 1785 rebellion. The sources also state that many people returned to their village at the time of death and that many converts retained traditional practices in domestic and spiritual contexts.

## Foodways

Tongva food and material culture were based on an Indigenous worldview that placed humans within a web of life. The majority of Tongva territory lay in the Sonoran life zone, which provided acorns, pine nuts, small game, and deer. Coastal resources included shellfish, sea mammals, and fish. Indigenous peoples smoked fish to preserve it, a practice associated in the sources with the smoke fires observed by Juan Rodríguez Cabrillo in 1542.

During the mission period, traditional foods were incorporated into the mission diet. Later, there was little land available to Tongva people for food outside the ranches, although crops such as corn and beans were planted on ranchos to sustain workers.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tongva creation stories express the idea that humans are one strand in a web of life and that humans, plants, animals, and land exist in reciprocal relationships of mutual respect and care. The sources state that the Tongva understand time as nonlinear and maintain constant communication with ancestors. The quoted statement attributed to Toypurina concerning Spanish occupation of her land is presented as the subject of scholarly debate, because it may differ from the testimony recorded by Spanish authorities.

The Tongva language was documented primarily in the unpublished field notes of John Peabody Harrington. The Smithsonian, through the University of California, Davis, developed the “J.P. Harrington Project,” in which approximately 6,000 pages of notes on the Tongva language were coded for documentation by a Tongva member. Pamela Munro compiled a Tongva dictionary of over 1,000 words and maintains a Tongva language Facebook page containing words, phrases, and songs.

## Language & religion

Tongva, also known as Gabrielino, Gabrieleño, or Kizh, is a dormant and reawakening Uto-Aztecan language in the Takic subgroup. It is closely related to Serrano. The language has not been used for everyday conversation since the 1940s, although Tongva people are attempting to revive it in everyday conversation, ceremonial contexts, language revitalization classes, and public discussion of religious and environmental issues. In 1811, priests at Mission San Gabriel recorded Kokomcar, Guiguitamcar, Corbonamga, and Sibanga; three languages were recorded at Mission San Fernando.

Before Christianization, Tongva religious thought placed humans within a web of life and emphasized communication with ancestors. Missionization brought baptism, Catholic instruction, forced transformations, and attempts to suppress traditional religious practitioners, dances, celebrations, and rituals. Contemporary revitalization efforts include classes taught by Pamela Munro, in which adults and children practice pronunciation, grammatical particles, songs, and word games.
