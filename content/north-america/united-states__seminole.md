## Overview

The Seminole are a Native American people who developed in Florida in the 18th century through ethnogenesis among Native American groups, most significantly northern Muscogee Creeks from what are now Georgia and Alabama. Today they live in Oklahoma and Florida and comprise the Seminole Nation of Oklahoma, the Seminole Tribe of Florida, and the Miccosukee Tribe of Indians of Florida, as well as independent groups. Historically, Seminole communities formed in northern and peninsular Florida, later dividing between Florida and Indian Territory, now Oklahoma. Their culture includes Creek-derived ceremonies, local Florida adaptations such as *chickees*, Muskogean languages, ritual music and dance, and traditions associated with the Everglades.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

 They do record that Seminoles historically sold alligator hides, bird plumes, and other items sourced from the Everglades, and that the decline of the plume trade contributed to reduced demand for Seminole goods in the early 20th century.

### Architecture

The best-documented Seminole building form is the open-air, thatched-roof house known as a *chickee*. The sources describe chickees as a local tradition developed as Seminoles adapted to the Florida environment. No further information is provided about their structural design, materials beyond the thatched roof, decoration, settlement arrangement, or urban architecture.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention alligator hides, bird plumes, and other items sourced from the Everglades as commodities sold to white merchants during the early 20th century.

## Music & performance

Seminole traditional music includes rattles, hand drums, water drums, and flutes. Folk songs include songs used to treat the sick and injured and songs intended to encourage animals to be easily hunted. Hunting songs are performed a cappella and use call-and-response.

The two major ritual dances connected with the musical tradition are the **Green Corn Dance** and the **Hunting Dance**. The Green Corn Dance is held in June, while the Hunting Dance is held in October. Other informal dances take place throughout the year, with some performed only in summer or winter. Many dances are connected with animal spirits, including the Snake, the Crawfish, and the Alligator.

Muskogee-language hymns were developed by Western Presbyterian Christian missionaries and Creek translators in the 1830s. They continue to be sung in Creek and Seminole churches in Oklahoma. The hymns combine Muskogee and Western meanings through their texts and performance practices. In Christian Seminole communities, hymns may be led by a song leader, a practice also associated with traditional Indigenous song.

## Dance & theatre

The sources identify the **Green Corn Dance** and **Hunting Dance** as the major ritual dances. The Green Corn Dance is associated with traditional Native religion and is held at ceremonial grounds; the Hunting Dance is associated with hunting and takes place in October. Other dances occur informally during the year, and some are performed specifically in summer or winter.

## Festivals & rituals

The principal named seasonal ceremony is the **Green Corn Ceremony**, also called the **Green Corn Dance**. It is a traditional Native religious practice held at ceremonial grounds, and the sources state that Indigenous peoples have practiced Green Corn rituals for centuries. The Green Corn Dance is held in June. Contemporary southeastern Native American tribes, including the Seminole and Muscogee Creek, continue to practice these ceremonies.

The sources also identify the **Hunting Dance**, held in October. Other dances occur throughout the year, including dances associated with summer or winter and dances connected with animal spirits such as the Snake, the Crawfish, and the Alligator.

Seminole religious life has included both traditional Native religion and Christianity, including Protestantism and Catholicism. In the 20th century, Green Corn Ceremony attendance decreased as Christian pastors preached on reservations, creating tension between religiously traditional Seminoles and those adopting Christianity. By the 1980s, some tribal members revived traditional Green Corn Dance ceremonies, and by 2000 religious tension between Green Corn Dance attendees and Christians had decreased. Some Seminole families participate in both religions.

## Foodways

The sources identify the black drink and ritual tobacco as notable Seminole traditions.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They do describe oral and language-related cultural practices: Muskogee-language hymns continue to be sung in Creek and Seminole churches in Oklahoma, and traditional song practices include a song leader and call-and-response hunting songs.

The Seminole Nation of Oklahoma is working to revive the use of Creek, which had been the dominant language of politics and social discourse. More generally, Seminole communities became increasingly concerned about the loss of language and tradition by the 1980s, contributing to the revival of traditional Green Corn Dance ceremonies.

## Language & religion

Historically, Seminole groups spoke two mutually unintelligible Muskogean languages: Mikasuki, with its dialect Hitchiti, and Muscogee, also called Creek in the sources. Mikasuki is restricted to Florida and was the native language of 1,600 people as of 2000, primarily among the Miccosukee Tribe of Indians of Florida. Muscogee is spoken by some Oklahoma Seminoles and about 200 older Florida Seminoles; the youngest native speaker was born in 1960. English is now predominant among Oklahoma and Florida Seminoles, especially younger generations, and most Mikasuki speakers are bilingual. Afro-Seminole Creole is spoken by Mascogos in Coahuila, Mexico, and by some Black Seminoles in Texas and Oklahoma.

Seminole tribes generally follow Christianity, including Protestantism and Catholicism, while also observing traditional Native religion. Traditional practices include the stomp dance and Green Corn Ceremony. Christian Seminoles developed syncretic Indigenous-Western practices that incorporated tribal traditions, including Muskogee-language hymns and traditional song leadership.
