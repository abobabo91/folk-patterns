## Overview

The Kwakwakaʼwakw are a collective of Indigenous nations — counted in the sources as twenty-five nations, numbering about 5,500 — who live on the central coast of British Columbia, on northern Vancouver Island and the adjacent mainland. The name translates as "Kwakʼwala-speaking tribes," and the language belongs to the Wakashan family; the individual tribes are autonomous nations and do not regard themselves as a single group. Fort Rupert was the largest of their villages at the time of European contact. In folk-culture terms they matter above all for woodcarving and ceremonial performance: the transformation mask was carried to its highest form here, and dance, song and the potlatch organise the transmission of inherited rank and supernatural privilege. Much of what is documented comes from oral history, nineteenth-century archaeology, inherited objects, and carvers trained inside the tradition.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Weaving is most often women's work, and the documented textiles are ceremonial rather than everyday.

**Chilkat blanket** — adopted by the Kwakwakaʼwakw from the Tlingit and Tsimshian to the north, woven on a loom from shredded cedar bark and mountain-goat wool. Designs are patterned and elaborate, a mosaic of ancestors and mythological figures, with flowing wool threads left at the fringes; each blanket takes almost a year, and the design is traditionally painted first by a man and then woven by a woman in accordance with it. Mary Ebbets Hunt, a Tlingit woman married to an Englishman working in the Fort Rupert area, brought the northern technique into Kwakwakaʼwakw hands.

**Button blanket** — spread with the blanket trade: brightly coloured cloaks, usually on a navy blue ground with red Melton wool appliqué, the borders and a central family crest outlined in abalone or mother-of-pearl buttons.

**Mawihl** — the painted ceremonial curtain hung over the entrance to the dressing room in the dancing house, typically cotton, painted with the spirit of the dancing house. Dancers vanish behind it as the scene demands or to change costume.

**Cedar-bark regalia** — neck rings, arm bands and ankle bands of red cedar bark dyed red, plaited in strips or as a cylindrical tube stuffed with the outer bark, finished with three-ply twisted bark tassels and red cotton fabric ties. The British Museum examples are identified as Hamatʼsa ceremonial regalia.

**Dance aprons and leggings** — worn in ceremony and hung with small coppers, puffin beaks and deer hoofs, which sound as the dancer moves.

**Motif vocabulary.** Family crests; ancestors and mythological figures; Thunderbird; Sisiutl, the three-headed sea serpent carved on clan-house cross beams; the shield- or T-shaped copper, shown on poles; the hawk face with beak curved round to the upper lip; sun rays around a face; and "punning" or "kenning," the filling of visual voids with independent figures — a face painted inside a whale fin.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe ceremonial dress in detail and daily dress hardly at all. High-ranking people wore copper, in varying sizes, on their clothing. Chilkat and button blankets were worn as cloaks and displayed as wealth; dancers added aprons and leggings hung with sounding trinkets, and Hamatʼsa participants wore red-dyed cedar-bark neck rings, arm bands and ankle bands. Headdresses and headbands were among the carved wooden objects made. For everyday wear, the only evidence here is photographic: British Museum postcards from Vancouver Island and Quatsino show a woman in a shirt and long skirt and a man in hat, jacket and trousers with a pipe and walking stick, and another woman wrapped in a blanket and wearing a hat while her husband paddles a canoe — western-style clothing of hats, shirts, braces, and blankets wrapped around the body, as a Hope Island photograph of four men also records. Footwear and belts are not described, except that Sisiutl could transform himself into a magic belt with which to gird oneself against all dangers.

### Architecture

The sources treat the built environment mainly through its carved components. **Totem poles** stand outside family houses and display the family's crests, history, wealth, social rank, inheritance and privilege; the sequence of figures records past family events, ancestors, myths and heraldic crests, with the bottom figure usually the most prominent, and its meaning is recited in semi-legendary form when the pole is first dedicated to a house. They are built of red cedar, stand between fifteen and fifty feet, and in the Kwakwakaʼwakw manner use more protruding elements than other Northwest Coast poles — stretching limbs, beaks jutting out, wings thrust away from the shaft — painted in alternating dark and light for contrast. **House poles** are the posts supporting the homes of important members, commissioned by the future homeowner and carved by the tribe's carvers: simpler, bolder, thicker and well rounded, depicting ancestors, house symbols and animals such as the sea lion and grizzly bear. A frontal entrance pole carries a long opening at its base and supports no part of the roof. Clan-house cross beams are sometimes carved with Sisiutl. Thunderbird, in the person of Tseiqami, sometimes helps heroic ancestors build houses by placing giant cedar beams. A British Museum photograph shows a Kwakwakaʼwakw longhouse with totem poles and a canoe beneath a timber structure; a postcard shows three poles beside a timber building. The dancing house, or Big House, is where initiates are brought to be tamed.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Almost everything is wood, and virtually all of it is painted to some extent. Western red cedar (*Thuja plicata*) is preferred for large projects because it grows in abundance along the coast; yellow cedar (*Callitropsis nootkatensis*) serves for smaller objects, which are sometimes oiled, and wood was steamed to make it pliable. Carvers made their own adzes, carving knives, stone axes, stone hammers and brushes, some of porcupine hair, and some tools were ceremonial and carved. The documented forms include kerfed boxes, storage boxes, food vessels, feast bowls — one British Museum photograph shows a seal-shaped feast bowl and a large grease dish — spoons of pine or alder with simple black designs inside, whistles, rattles, effigies, staffs, utensils, ceremonial knives, headdresses and headbands. Horn is softened by boiling, then bent and carved, mostly into cooking utensils. Pigments include red ochre and a white paste from burnt clamshells. One British Museum bow is of red wood, its grip wrapped in leather, engraved at the back, with a twisted gut cord.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Kwakwakaʼwakw jewelry comprised earrings, bracelets, necklaces, nose rings and lip piercings, worked in abalone shell, stone, ivory and wood; wooden hairpins are also attested. Contact with Europeans brought gold and silver, hammered into shape and often inscribed with patterns and mythological figures — the British Museum holds an open silver bracelet worked in repoussé and engraved with an eagle, and a plain copper bracelet. **Copper**, in the sense of a beaten copper plate engraved and shaped like a "T" or a shield, is the emblem of great wealth; coppers can be broken into pieces and given away at potlatches, and shield-shaped coppers are represented on poles. Komokwa, the Undersea Chief, is the maker of coppers. Masks are finished with feathers and "hair" of animal fur or strips of cedar bark, and flattened copper is sometimes applied to the face of a sun mask. Tattoo and henna practices are not covered by these sources.

## Music & performance

Sacred and secular music has been practised for thousands of years, with vocals the only melodic voice in the ensemble; percussion, rattles, whistles and the clapper carry the beat while vocal expression establishes the rhythm, and instrument makers design new instruments for each dance. The singer's baton (*mEnāʼtsē* is given as the word for drum) is the main percussion: about a foot long, plain firewood for the temporary batons handed to every guest so they can drum along, and elaborately carved hardwood or cedar sticks — usually carved as a sea lion, whose shape the baton resembles — for the song leaders or baton masters. The **box drum**, usually cedar, is beaten collectively by a large group who sing the song they are drumming to; a British Museum hoop drum of glued carved wood segments, skin-covered and sinew-tied, is painted with a rayed face and inscribed "William Wasden Jr Hilamas 2005 Namgis Sun." The wooden **rattle** (*iaʼtEn*) is the most important instrument in ritual — Bill Holm, in *Crooked Beak of Heaven*, calls its sound a "direct contact with the supernatural" — an ancient icon meant to keep the dancer calm and free of possession, and reported to bring back the dead. **Whistles** each carry their own pitch, and several chambers may be combined for up to three pitches at once; they announce supernatural presences and voice the spirits in a story. The **clapper**, of leather and wood, combines rattle and baton in one hand and produces sharp, sudden sounds. Song genres are named in Kwakʼwala: *baquyala*, "summer song," and *tsʼēʼkꞏala*, "winter song." Love songs are important, and certain traditions involve ghosts who bring back the dead with their song. Each dancing society appoints a song master — uniquely, a non-inherited position, chosen for talent and paid for his work, composing and memorising one to four songs per novice; a song master's children are placed inside a drum while he sings and beats it in sequences of four, to pass the talent on.

## Dance & theatre

Dance runs through every rite, and each variety is carefully staged and rehearsed; designated members judge whether a dance was performed satisfactorily, and a botched dance can cost social position or require hosting a potlatch to regain prestige. Franz Boas counted fifty-three different characters in Kwakwakaʼwakw dance. There are four main dancing societies, each with its own house that only members may enter: **Hamatsa** ("Cannibal"), **Winalagilis** ("Making War All Over the Earth"), **Atlakim** ("Taken Far Away Into the Woods"), and **Dluwalakha** or Klasila. In the **hamatsa** initiation — the highest rank, open only after twelve years through three previous grades, and in the music sources only to chiefs' sons — the novice is possessed by the Man-Eater spirit (Baxbakualanuxsiwae / Baxbaxwalanuksiwe, "Man-Eater at the North End of the World"), disappears into the woods for four days, returns crying and biting the audience while the musicians match the possessing spirit's tempo, and is ritually tamed until human again, concluding by singing his new hamatsa song. Gwaxwgwakwalanuksiweʼ, the man-eating raven, is the most prestigious role, with Galuxwadzuwus ("Crooked-Beak of Heaven") and Huxhukw also participating. Winalagilis dances tell of violent, possessed warriors, and its ghost dancers revive dead warrior spirits. Atlakim songs introduce each dancer in turn. Dluwalakha is restricted to spring. **Transformation masks** are the theatre's central device: built in sections, opened by strings mid-dance to split an animal or mythological exterior aside and reveal a human face within.

## Festivals & rituals

The ceremonial year turns on the winter season. ***Tsetseka*** (*tsetseqa*, "Supernatural season" or Winter Ceremonial) is a four-day festival of song and dance greeting the new winter; the word comes from the Heiltsuk for "shaman." It opens by singing the songs of everyone who has died since the previous winter, and the whole season counts as supernatural, so every ritual within it must reflect that. Officials with specialised duties stage it, novices are drilled in dramatic acting, dancing and the proper cries, and the illusions are elaborate — hidden strings and tunnels, pounding on the roof to simulate mythical birds. In one recorded instance a novice appeared to arrive by canoe and drown, and was later "revived" amid celebration; the drowned figure was a wooden carving standing in for him. The living space became a live theatre of terror, drama, illusion and comedy. The **potlatch** (from Chinook "to give") shares wealth and prestige to establish status and to ensure witnesses remember the stories celebrated; potlatches often fall within tsetseqa to announce a new initiate into a secret dancing society, and Dluwalakha closes with a potlatch repaying the mask makers and everyone else the novice's dancing affected. Hamatsas are fed and given gifts before any other guest. These ceremonies transfer, justify and reaffirm family and spiritual status inherited from ancestors who contacted the spirit world, and convey *Tlugwe* — supernatural treasures in the form of masks and regalia, but also stories, songs, recitations and dances. Wealth from trade produced a Golden Age of potlatch art in the late nineteenth century; the Canadian Indian Act of 1884 outlawed the potlatch and other ceremonies, which contributed to a decline in artistic production. After the shock of the 1921 Cranmer potlatch wore off, art as a livelihood was encouraged again.

## Foodways

The sources say little about diet, naming only cedar food vessels, feast bowls (one carved as a seal) and a large grease dish, spoons of pine or alder, and horn cooking utensils; Uʼmelth the Raven brought the people salmon, and Thunderbird hunts whales at sea. No dishes, staples or preparation methods are described.

## Oral tradition & literature

Much of what is known of Kwakwakaʼwakw art comes from oral history, and narrative is inseparable from ceremony: masks, songs, recitations and dances are themselves the *Tlugwe*, the supernatural treasures handed down. The creation account has a raven flying over water with nowhere to land, dropping pebbles to make islands, then making trees and grass and, after several failed attempts, the first man and woman from wood and clay. Like all Pacific Northwest Coast peoples, most of the tribes hold flood stories: ancestors transforming into their natural form and vanishing as the waters rose, or lashing their oceangoing canoes to high mountain peaks — and the figures with supernatural powers in these accounts tend to be the founding clans. The spirit world divides into sky, sea, earth and otherworldly realms, all interacting. Its named beings include Tseiqami, the man from the cedar tree and Thunderbird, lord of the winter dance season, whose wingbeats make thunder and whose eyes flash lightning, with his younger brother Kolus; his adversary Qaniqilak, spirit of summer, identified with the sea god Kumugwe or Komokwa; Sisiutl, whose glance turns an adversary to stone and who can become an invincible war canoe; Dzunukwa, the cannibal giant who cries "hu-hu!" in the grandmother's voice to lure children and is frequently outwitted; Bakwas, king of the ghosts, green and skeletal with a long curving nose, who haunts forests and draws the living toward the dead, and who in some myths is Dzunukwa's husband; Uʼmelth the Raven, bringer of moon, fire, salmon, sun and tides; and Pugwis, an aquatic creature with a fish-like face and large incisors. Chief James Wallas's *Kwakiutl Legends* and Boas's *Kwakiutl Tales* are the published collections.

## Language & religion

Kwakʼwala belongs to the Wakashan language family, and *Kwakwakaʼwakw* means "Kwakʼwala-speaking tribes"; *baquyala* and *tsʼēʼkꞏala* — summer song and winter song — are among its recorded terms. Dialects and scripts are not covered by these sources. Spirituality is transmitted at ceremony, mostly in winter, and is organised around four spirit realms — sky, sea, earth and otherworldly — that humans contact by channelling spirits while masked and in trance. Neighbouring nations share many of the same spirits and deities despite speaking different languages, while some spirits belong to only one or two cultures; each tribe keeps its own history, practices and origin stories. The secret society Hamatsa is of particular importance, and it replaced an earlier feminine dance, *hamshamtses*. The Fort Rupert Kwakwakaʼwakw raided a Heiltsuk canoe in 1835 and took Hamatsa whistles, after which the nation adopted the hamatsa dance traditions. Contemporary historians hold that hamatsa initiates were not cannibals, using fake or real flesh as props without consuming it.
