## Overview

The Okanagan, also known as the Syilx, Suknaqinx, Swknaqínx, or Okinagan people, are a First Nations and Native American people whose traditional territory spans the Canada–US boundary in Washington state and unceded British Columbia. Their territory includes the basin of Okanagan Lake and the Okanagan River, the Similkameen River basin west of the Okanagan valley, and part of the upper Nicola River valley. They belong to the Interior Salish ethnological and linguistic grouping and are closely related to the Spokan, Sinixt, Nez Perce, Pend Oreille, Secwepemc, and Nlaka'pamux peoples. The Okanagan Nation Alliance includes communities in British Columbia and Washington. At the height of Okanagan Syilx culture, about 3,000 years ago, an estimated 12,000 people lived in the valley and surrounding areas.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The Syilx moved through their traditional areas during the year to fish, hunt, or collect food. During the winter months, they lived in semi-permanent villages of *kekulis*, a type of pithouse. In Nsyilxcn, “pit house” is *q̓ʷc̓iʔ*.

## Foodways

The Syilx used an adaptive seasonal strategy that involved moving within their traditional areas to fish, hunt, or collect food.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources identify Okanagon tales by James A. Teit and Marian K. Gould in Franz Boas’s *Folk-tales of Salishan and Sahaptin tribes*. The listed further reading includes *Nature Power: In the Spirit of an Okanagan Storyteller* by Harry Robinson and Wendy C. Wickwire. The external links also name “The bear woman,” an Okanagan legend about a woman kidnapped by a grizzly bear, and “Dirty boy,” an Okanagan legend about a woman who married the sun.

## Language & religion

The language of the Syilx people is Nsyilxcn, an Interior Salish language spoken across the Canada–United States border in southern British Columbia and northern Washington. It is also called Okanagan, Colville-Okanagan, or Nsyilxcən, with the forms n̓səl̓xcin̓ and n̓syilxčn̓ also recorded. Before European contact, it had developed into the Colville, Okanagan, and Lakes dialects. The language is extremely endangered: approximately 50 fluent first-language speakers remain, and it is classified as moribund, with no first-language speakers under the age of 50. Historically, it remained exclusively oral until the late 19th century, when missionaries and linguists began producing word lists, dictionaries, and grammars. It is now written in Latin script, and words are traditionally not capitalized.
