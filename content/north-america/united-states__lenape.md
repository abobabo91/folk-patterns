## Overview

The Lenape, also called the Lenni Lenape and Delaware people, are an Indigenous people of the Northeastern Woodlands who live in the United States and Canada. Their historical territory included northeastern Delaware, New Jersey, eastern Pennsylvania, New York Bay, western Long Island, and the lower Hudson Valley. Today, communities are based in Oklahoma, Wisconsin, and Ontario. Federally recognized Lenape tribes include the Delaware Nation and Delaware Tribe of Indians in Oklahoma and the Stockbridge–Munsee Community in Wisconsin; Lenape communities in Canada include the Munsee-Delaware Nation, Moravian of the Thames First Nation, and the Delaware First Nation of the Six Nations of the Grand River. Munsee and Unami belong to the Eastern Algonquian language group. The sources document Lenape foodways, clothing, adornment, agriculture, hunting, games, mythology, and language preservation.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

They do describe clothing made from natural materials, including buckskin, beaver pelts, bear skins, dyed deer hair, feathers, wampum, and porcupine quills.

**Painted and quilled skirts** — Women painted their skin skirts or decorated them with porcupine quills. Dutch settlers considered the elaborate decoration reminiscent of fine European lace.

**Turkey-feather cloaks** — Women’s winter cloaks were made from the iridescent body feathers of wild turkeys.

**Wampum ornaments** — Wampum consisted of shell beads traditionally used for ritual purposes and as ornaments. The Lenape also exchanged wampum for beaver furs after Dutch arrival.

### Clothing & dress

In hot weather, Lenape men wore loin cloths and women wore skirts. In winter, beaver pelts or bear skins served as mantles. Both sexes could wear buckskin leggings and moccasins in cold weather. Women wore their hair long, usually below the hip, while men kept a small “round crest” of about 2 inches in diameter. Men decorated headdresses and breast ornaments with deer hair dyed deep scarlet and with feathers. Women wore headbands of dyed deer hair or wampum. Their skirts could be painted or decorated with porcupine quills, and their winter cloaks could be made from wild-turkey feathers.

### Architecture

They state that Lenape villages were organized on a democratic and egalitarian basis and that, in the 18th century, Moravian missionaries established structured, European-style mission villages.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They record the use of metal tools acquired through trade and later adaptation to European methods of hunting and farming. Other documented objects include wampum shell beads, moccasins, a football-like game ball used in *pahsaheman*, wooden dolls called *ohta*, bows, pipes made with sumac branches and soapstone bowls, and agricultural products such as maize, beans, and squash.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Lenape adornment used stone, shell, animal teeth, claws, deer hair, feathers, wampum, and porcupine quills. Men wore headdresses and breast ornaments incorporating scarlet-dyed deer hair and feathers. Women wore headbands made from dyed deer hair or wampum. The sources also describe painted skin skirts and the use of wild-turkey feathers in women’s winter cloaks.

## Music & performance

Lenape mythology describes Nanapush using a bow as an instrument: he played it and sang a song that stopped the flood, and later played it and sang again so that the turtle grew. The sources also describe ritual and ceremonial contexts in which the life-spirits called *manitowak* were venerated, including ceremonies, rituals, dreams, visions, games, and *ohtas*. Metinuwak were healers, spiritual and emotional guides, and religious leaders who could communicate with the *manitowak*.

## Dance & theatre

Lenni Lenape play *pahsaheman*, a football-like game organized into separate male and female teams. More than a hundred players could be grouped into gendered teams. Men could use only their feet to carry or pass the ball, while women could carry, pass, or kick it; women could tackle men, while men could not tackle women carrying the ball. Dance was another common activity. Men danced and leaped loudly, often with bear-claw accessories, while women danced more modestly, wearing small thimbles or bells and stepping slightly forward and backward.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe ceremonial practices connected with spiritual life. The *manitowak*, understood as life-spirits of living things, were venerated in ceremonies, rituals, dreams, visions, games, and through *ohtas*, wooden dolls carved annually and said to possess healing and luck-giving powers. *Nianque* refers to an animal guide such as one seen in a vision quest. Metinuwak served as healers, spiritual and emotional guides, and religious leaders.

Lenape mythology describes Nanapush creating the first Sacred Fire after people had separated into different tribes and languages. He made a pipe from a sumac branch and a soapstone bowl, received tobacco from the creator, and instructed people to smoke together when they fought so that they could make decisions beneficial to everyone.

## Foodways

Lenape women practiced companion planting and cultivated the Three Sisters: maize, beans, and squash. Men hunted, fished, and harvested seafood. In the 17th century, they practiced slash-and-burn agriculture and used controlled fire to manage land and extend the productivity of farmlands. Maize was planted in March according to Dutch settler Isaac de Rasieres.

Men hunted deer mainly from September to January and from June to July. From January to the spring planting in May, they hunted bears, beavers, raccoons, and foxes. Lenape people harvested fish and shellfish from the bays and gathered clams year-round in southern New Jersey. One fishing technique involved adding ground chestnuts to stream water to make fish dizzy and easier to catch. The sources also state that Lenape traded agricultural products, mainly maize, with European settlers and traders.

Lenape herbalists used plants medicinally. Black walnut was used to cure ringworm, persimmons to cure ear problems, and Ohio buckeye nuts were carried for rheumatism. Ground buckeye nuts could be mixed with sweet oil or mutton tallow for earaches, or used to poison fish in streams.

## Oral tradition & literature

Lenape mythology describes Kishelamàkânk, or Kishelemukong, as the creator god. Before creation there was an empty darkness, and Kishelamàkânk dreamed of the Earth, mountains, forests, animals, people, and ceremonies before creating the world. Helper spirits called the Grandfathers of the North, East, and West and the Grandmother of the South assisted in creating the Earth. A special tree produced the first man from its roots, while woman sprang from it when the tree bent down and touched the ground.

The mythology also recounts the Sacred Fire, Nanapush, a giant bear tooth, a flood, and the turtle that carried the animals and branches on its back. A muskrat brought up the Earth that Nanapush placed on the turtle’s back, and the turtle grew into the land called Turtle Island. The story explains the wolf’s howl as a call for its ancestor to return home. The sources mention The Lenape Talking Dictionary, which preserves and digitizes the Southern Unami dialect, and describe language revitalization work by the Delaware Tribe of Indians.

## Language & religion

Munsee and Unami are closely related languages of the Eastern Algonquian subgroup of the Algonquian language family. The languages were spoken in the vicinity of the modern New York City area, including western Long Island, Manhattan Island, Staten Island, southeastern New York State, eastern Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Connecticut, Maryland, and Delaware. Three Unami dialects are identified: Northern Unami, Southern Unami, and Unalachtigo.

There is no standard writing system for either Munsee or Unami, although practical orthographies and community spelling systems have been developed. The Unami Lenape Talking Dictionary uses a practical writing system, while recent Munsee dictionaries and local word books use a Munsee orthography. As of 2023, only a few Delaware First Nation elders in Moraviantown, Ontario, fluently spoke Munsee according to the source. Lenape mythology centers on Kishelamàkânk, the *manitowak*, Metinuwak, *ohtas*, vision quests, ceremonies, rituals, dreams, and the Sacred Fire.
