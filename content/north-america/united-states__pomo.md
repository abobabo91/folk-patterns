## Overview

The Pomo lived in Northern California in small groups linked by family lines and marriage. Their culture is known for fine basketry, shell beads, fishing, hunting, gathering, ceremony, and religious traditions. Acorns were their most important food. Pomo includes seven distinct languages, many of which became endangered after colonization.

## Material culture

Pomo material culture centered on detailed basketry, shell and bead work, fishing and hunting tools, food-processing tools, and ceremonial objects.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Pomo basketry used plant fibers, feathers, shells, and beads in plaited, coiled, and twined forms.

- **Pomo basket weaving** — Women mainly made baskets for cooking, storing food, gathering acorns, carrying people, ceremonies, and caring for children.
- **Feathered baskets** — Some baskets were covered with vivid red pileated woodpecker feathers, beads, and polished abalone-shell pendants.
- **Coiled basketry** — Circular coiled baskets included floral designs, black zig-zag decoration, shell discs, and colored glass beads.
- **Lattice turning** (*lattice tumning*) — Pomo basket makers were known for lattice or gallery work, as well as plaiting and coiling.
- **Spirit Door** (*Dau*) — Dau is a small opening between stitches that was believed to let good spirits circulate and spirits be released.

### Clothing & dress

The profile mainly documents ceremonial clothing rather than everyday dress.

- **Guksu costume** (*Guksu*) — Guksu impersonators painted their bodies and wore feathered headdresses or feather tufts with yellow headbands.
- **Calnis costume** (*Calnis*) — A Calnis dancer wore black body paint, a black staff, and a feather cape over the face.
- **Ceremonial regalia** — Museum records describe head-dresses, feather headdresses, dance regalia, and feather-decorated rattles.

### Architecture

Religious life centered on underground dance rooms, sweat houses, dance-houses, and roundhouses.

- **Subterranean dance rooms** — An all-male society met in underground dance rooms for Kuksu ceremonies.
- **Sweat houses** — Pomo tradition placed supernatural beings in sweat houses or dance-houses at the ends of the world.
- **Upper Lake roundhouse** — An Upper Lake roundhouse hosted a religious performance recorded in 1906.
- **Amacha ghost town** (*Amacha*) — Amacha was a town built for 100 people and later burned after its inhabitants were taken to government lands.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The profile names many wooden, bone, stone, shell, and basket objects but no Pomo ceramic or metalworking tradition.

- **Smoking pipe** — Wooden smoking-pipes are among the objects described in the sources.
- **Double bone whistle** — A double bone whistle was used by a Guksu impersonator.
- **Cocoon rattle** — A rattle was made from moth cocoons arranged around a wooden stave and decorated with bird feathers.
- **Acorn grinding tools** — Mortars, pestles, millstones, handstones, and bowls were used to process acorns and seeds.
- **Game sticks** — Museum records include game sticks with burned ornamentation.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Pomo adornment used shell, magnesite, soapstone, glass beads, feathers, and body paint.

- **Clamshell beads** — Clamshell beads were worn, given as gifts, and used as major currency in Central California.
- **Abalone pendants** — Polished abalone-shell pendants decorated baskets and were used as adornment.
- **Shell coins** — Museum records list shell coins and shell discs sewn with red glass beads.
- **Ceremonial body paint** — Ceremonial performers used black, red, and white body paint.

## Music & performance

Pomo performance was mainly ceremonial and used whistles, rattles, acting, and dance.

- **Guksu whistle performance** (*Guksu*) — The Guksu impersonator carried a short feathered staff and whistled through a double bone whistle without speaking.
- **Cocoon rattle** — A feather-decorated rattle made from moth cocoons was part of dance equipment.
- **Upper Lake roundhouse performance** — A 1906 record shows five men prepared for a religious performance at an Upper Lake roundhouse.

## Dance & theatre

Pomo ceremonial theatre used impersonation, costuming, acting, dancing, and physical and mental tests.

- **Guksu ceremony** (*Guksu*) — The Guksu ceremony lasted six days, with dancers appearing once each day.
- **Healing-spirit impersonation** (*Guksu*) — Guksu impersonators represented a healing spirit from the south.
- **Calnis dance** (*Calnis*) — Calnis dancers were linked with Guksu and chased people and tripped them up.
- **Scarifying Ceremony** — Dressed dancers tested children ages five to ten in a ceremony called the Scarifying Ceremony.

## Festivals & rituals

Pomo ritual life included mourning, puberty rites, healing, shamanic work, and Kuksu ceremonies.

- **Annual mourning ceremony** — The Kuksu religion included an annual ceremony for mourning.
- **Puberty rites** — Puberty rites marked passage into a new stage of life.
- **Shamanic intervention** — Shamans intervened with the spirit world, including to treat illness.
- **Scarifying Ceremony** — Children ages five to ten underwent physical and mental tests led by ceremonial dancers.
- **All-male society** — An all-male society met in subterranean dance rooms as part of Pomo religious practice.

## Foodways

Acorns were the main staple, supported by fishing, hunting, and gathering.

- **Acorns** — Acorns were harvested in large burden baskets and processed with mortars and pestles.
- **Fishing** — Fishing supplied foods including salmon.
- **Hunting** — Men hunted animals such as rabbits, rats, and squirrels.
- **Gathering** — Women generally gathered and prepared foods such as greens, mushrooms, berries, and grasshoppers.
- **Watertight food baskets** — Basketry was used to store, cook, and serve food, including in some watertight forms.

## Oral tradition & literature

Pomo oral tradition includes creation stories about Coyote, other beings, the world, and human language.

- **Coyote creation stories** (*Kunula*) — Stories describe Coyote, also called Kunula, as ancestor and creator god.
- **Coyote and Lizard story** (*Hatanutal*) — In one story, Coyote and Lizard wrestled over whether people should have fingers, and Lizard won.
- **Sun and moon story** — One story says the bodies of Coyote’s two sons became the sun and moon.
- **Bole Maru** — Bole Maru continued after other Ghost Dance movements declined.
- **Xay tsnu revival** (*xay tsnu*) — The Clear Lake Pomo Cultural Preservation Foundation is working to revive xay tsnu.

## Language & religion

Pomo includes seven distinct languages, while Pomo religion centers on Kuksu or Guksu, spirits, shamanism, and Coyote.

- **Pomo language family** (*Pomoan*) — Pomoan includes Northern, Northeastern, Eastern, Southeastern, Central, Southern, and Kashaya.
- **Kashaya** — Kashaya is one of the seven distinct Pomo languages.
- **Elem Pomo language** (*xay tsnu*) — xay tsnu is spoken by Elem Pomo and is undergoing revival.
- **Healing spirit** (*Kuksu*) — Kuksu or Guksu was a healing spirit from the south.
- **Coyote creator** (*Coyote*) — Coyote was regarded as an ancestor and creator god.
