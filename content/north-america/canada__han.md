## Overview

The Hän, also called Han or Hwëch'in / Han Hwech’in, are a First Nations people of Canada and an Alaska Native Athabascan people of the United States. Their name means “People of the River,” referring to the Yukon River. Their traditional lands centered on the forested area around the Upper Yukon River (Chu Kon'Dëk), Klondike River (Tr'on'Dëk), Bonanza Creek (Gàh Dëk), and Sixtymile River (Khel Dëk), across what is now the Alaska–Yukon Territory border. In later times, their population became centered in Dawson City, Yukon, and Eagle, Alaska. The Hän belong to the Athabascan-speaking ethnolinguistic group. Their culture is documented through fishing, hunting, seasonal movement, moss-house construction, and the endangered Hän language.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

 They record that Hän communities became increasingly reliant on European goods such as clothing and canvas between 1887 and 1895.

### Architecture

The principal Hän dwelling was a square half-recessed house known as a moss house. It was made from wooden poles and insulated with moss. This served as the main type of housing. When traveling, the people erected temporary domed houses made of skin stretched over tied branches.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do record fishing tools including weirs, traps, gill nets, dip nets, spears, and harpoons, as well as woven spruce-root baskets used in cooking.

## Festivals & rituals

 They record seasonal movements connected with subsistence: salmon was caught along the Yukon River in June and August, river camps were abandoned between the salmon runs from June–September, and hunting later took place after the salmon run and for caribou in February and March.

The sources describe a historical religious transition rather than a detailed ritual calendar. Bishop William Bompas established the first Anglican Church mission in Hän territory, after which the people gradually shifted away from traditional religion while combining it with Christianity in a syncretic fashion.

## Foodways

Historically, fish, especially salmon, formed the main part of the Hän diet. King salmon was caught along the Yukon River in June, while chum salmon was caught in August. Salmon was dried and stored for winter consumption. Between the salmon runs from June–September, men sought other fish, moose, caribou, birds, bears, and small game. Men hunted game once after the salmon run and later hunted caribou in February and March, while women fished for fish other than salmon.

Women traditionally cooked by boiling food with water heated by stones placed in a fire and then dropped into woven spruce-root baskets.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They mention that members of the group appear in James A. Michener’s 1989 novel *Journey*, in which Europeans try to reach Dawson overland from Athabasca Landing in Alberta in 1897–99.

Language preservation efforts include the Hän Language program, hosted by the Robert Service School in Dawson City since 1991. The Trʼondëk Hwëchʼin supports adult language classes and bi-annual cultural gatherings, while FirstVoices and Yukon Native Learning Centre provide online resources for learning Hän and its tradition, culture, and history.

## Language & religion

Hän is a Northern Athabaskan language in the Na-Dené language family. It is most closely related to Gwich'in and Upper Tanana and is more distantly related to Northern Tutchone. The language has two dialects: the Eagle Dialect, associated with Eagle Village, and the Moosehide dialect, associated with Moosehide, a traditional village of the Trʼondëk Hwëchʼin First Nation in the Yukon.

The language served as a lingua franca among Gwich’in, Tutchone, Tagish, and Upper Tanana peoples toward the end of the 19th century during the Gold Rush in the Yukon. It is now the most endangered language of Alaska, with only a few speakers, all over 60 years of age; there are fewer than a dozen first-language speakers. The sources state that the last fluent speaker of Hän in Yukon, elder Percy Henry, died on April 6, 2024.

Bishop William Bompas established the first Anglican Church mission in Hän territory. The people gradually shifted away from traditional religion and combined it with Christianity in a syncretic fashion.
