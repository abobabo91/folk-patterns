## Overview

The Cheyenne are an Indigenous people of the Great Plains comprising the Tsétsėhéstȧhese, also called Tsitsistas, and the Só'taeo'o, also called Suhtai or Sutaio; the two tribes merged in the early 19th century. Today they are divided between the Southern Cheyenne, enrolled with the Southern Arapaho in the Cheyenne and Arapaho Tribes in western Oklahoma, and the Northern Cheyenne, enrolled in the Northern Cheyenne Tribe on the Northern Cheyenne Indian Reservation in southeastern Montana. Their combined population is recorded as 12,130 in 2008, while late-2014 enrollment figures for the Northern Cheyenne indicate approximately 10,840 members. The Cheyenne language belongs to the Algonquian language family. Their material culture includes quillwork, beadwork, painted buffalo-hide storage bags, pipe bags, clothing, horse equipment, and sacred bundles.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Porcupine-quill decoration** — Porcupine quills were commonly sorted by size, dyed, moistened in the mouth, flattened with the teeth, and attached to clothing, moccasins, bags, or horse equipment. Museum records describe quillwork on shirts, pipe bags, blankets, leggings, and other objects associated with Cheyenne people.

**Beadwork** — Museum records describe beadwork on leather tobacco bags, pipe bags, leggings, moccasins, shirts, and other clothing. Glass beads were sewn onto leather, sometimes in narrow or broad decorative bands, and beadwork traditions are described as especially developed on the central and northern prairies.

**Hide painting** — A museum catalogue records a storage bag made from untanned buffalo hide with red and green paintings on one side. The bag was used for storing dried meat and is identified in the record as certainly Cheyenne.

**Parfleche** — A *parfleche* is described as a storage bag for dried meat, made from untanned buffalo hide. One catalogue record identifies a painted buffalo-hide meat bag as certainly Cheyenne, while another records a similar object as possibly Blackfeet.

**Leather pipe bags** — Museum records describe hide pipe bags decorated with beadwork, glass beads, leather fringe, and quillwork. One pipe bag has beadwork arranged around the opening, along its length, and around the bottom point.

**Painted buffalo-hide storage bags** — These bags were made from untanned buffalo hide and used for dried meat. Catalogue descriptions emphasize their stiffness, folded form, painted surfaces, and association with North American prairie collections.

**Saddle equipment** — A museum record identifies a saddle with stirrups as Northern Cheyenne and places it in the context of horse equipment. The Cheyenne adopted horse culture after moving onto the Plains.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name red and green painted decoration, quillwork bands, beadwork bands, leather fringe, and painted buffalo hide; they do not identify named Cheyenne motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe shirts decorated with quillwork, embroidery, beadwork, tassels, and fringe; leggings decorated with beadwork or embroidery; moccasins; wool and hide blankets; and military jackets. Museum photographs and catalogue records show Cheyenne men wearing decorated shirts, leggings, moccasins, blankets, ear ornaments, feather ornaments, and headbands. Lean Bear is described wearing a shirt decorated with quillwork and fringing, hide leggings with beadwork, moccasins, metal ear ornaments, and an eagle feather in his hair. High Toe is described with a warrior shirt, quillwork leggings, moccasins, a dentalium choker, metal ear ornaments, and an eagle feather. Little Robe wore a wool blanket with quillwork decoration around his waist. The sources do not distinguish a complete everyday dress system from ceremonial dress, nor do they provide Cheyenne vernacular names for garments.

### Architecture

Cheyenne settlements formerly included earth lodges, but the sources state that these were replaced by portable tipis when the people adopted nomadic Plains horse culture.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum records describe storage bags, pipe bags, smoking pipes, a medicine rattle, saddles with stirrups, and horse equipment. Materials include untanned buffalo hide, leather, cloth, glass beads, pigments, feathers, animal tails, and metal fittings.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The catalogue records mention dentalium shell necklaces and chokers, abalone shell ear ornaments, metal ear ornaments, gorgets, peace medals, and feather ornaments. Hair was sometimes arranged in two sections or bunches wrapped in cloth, fabric, or fur; other records describe several braids.

## Music & performance

The sources describe sacred objects and ceremonies. They state that the Sacred Arrows and Sacred Buffalo Hat formed the two great covenants of the Cheyenne Nation and that the prophet Tomȯsévėséhe, “Erect Horns,” gave the people ceremonies and the Sun Dance. The sources also mention a ceremonial pipe associated with the Sacred Hat Bundle. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription concerns this ethnic group.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name the Sun Dance as a ceremony associated with Tomȯsévėséhe and the Ésevone, or Sacred Buffalo Hat.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe the Sacred Arrows and the Sacred Buffalo Hat as the two great covenants of the Cheyenne Nation. The Maahótse, or Sacred Arrows, were received by the prophet Motsé'eóeve at Nóávóse, identified in the source as Bear Butte, and were kept in the maahéome, or Arrow Lodge or Arrow Tepee. The Ésevone, also called Hóhkėha'e, was received by Tomȯsévėséhe at Tȯhóonévose and kept in the vonȧhéome or hóhkėha'éome, the Sacred Hat Lodge or Sacred Hat Tepee. The Tséá'enōvȧhtse was the Sacred Buffalo Hat Keeper and had to belong to the Só'taeo'o. The Sacred Arrows symbolized male power, while the Sacred Buffalo Hat symbolized female power. The sources mention the Sun Dance and ceremonies connected with the Sacred Hat.

## Foodways

The sources state that the Cheyenne formerly collected wild rice and hunted bison while living between the Mississippi River and Mille Lacs Lake. After adopting nomadic Plains horse culture, they replaced a diet of fish and agricultural produce with mainly bison and wild fruits and vegetables. Museum records describe buffalo-hide bags used for storing dried meat.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Cheyenne oral history describes two related tribal peoples represented by two cultural heroes or prophets who received divine articles from Ma'heo'o, whom the Só'taeo'o called He'emo. Motsé'eóeve received the Sacred Arrows and organized Cheyenne society, including military societies, legal justice, and the Council of Forty-four peace chiefs. Tomȯsévėséhe received the Sacred Buffalo Hat and gave the people accompanying ceremonies and the Sun Dance. The sources also state that Sweet Medicine predicted the coming of the horse, the cow, the white man, and other new things. No Cheyenne epic, proverb, riddle, written literary tradition, or contemporary literary preservation project is described.

## Language & religion

The Cheyenne language, Tsėhésenėstsestȯtse, belongs to the Algonquian language family. It is spoken in Montana and Oklahoma, with approximately 800 speakers in Oklahoma, and the two locations have only a handful of vocabulary differences. The alphabet contains 14 letters. The former Só'taeo'o or Suhtai bands spoke Só'taéka'ėškóne or Só'taenėstsestȯtse, described as a language so close to Cheyenne that it is sometimes called a Cheyenne dialect. Cheyenne spiritual life in the sources centers on Ma'heo'o, the Sacred Arrows, the Sacred Buffalo Hat, their keepers, sacred lodges, prophets, and the Sun Dance.
