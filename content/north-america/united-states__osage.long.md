## Overview

The Osage Nation — *Wažaže Nikaši*, "People of the Middle Waters" — is a federally recognized Native American tribe in Oklahoma, and an Indigenous people of the Great Plains whose earlier home lay in the Ohio and Mississippi river valleys. Their language belongs to the Dhegihan branch of the Siouan family, shared with the Kansa, Ponca, Omaha and Quapaw, from whom they separated in the course of a westward migration begun in the seventeenth century under pressure from Haudenosaunee incursions. By the early nineteenth century they controlled the country between the Missouri and Red rivers, from the Ozarks to the foothills of the Wichita Mountains, living by semi-annual buffalo hunts and by farming corn and squash near their villages. In 2025 the Nation counts roughly 25,000 enrolled citizens, 6,780 of them resident in its jurisdictional area in northern Oklahoma. For folk culture they matter above all for a cosmology of sky and earth expressed in clan organization, ceremonial naming and dress, and for a beadwork and fingerweaving tradition documented in museum collections.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The museum objects shown here are the clearest evidence the sources give of Osage pattern work. They divide into two techniques: beadwork applied to woven or cloth grounds, and fingerweaving in wool and vegetable fibre.

**Beaded bands, sashes and garters** — Narrow bands worn at the waist and below the knee, made of beads worked with thread onto wool or cloth backings. One sash carries black and blue floral motifs on a yellow ground with a series of triangles at each end; a textile band sets black and white beads against a red wool backing; a set of garters uses geometrical patterns in blue, white, green and yellow. A woman's sash in the same group is brightly coloured with a long fringe at either end composed of many plaited lengths of yarn, the woven body decorated with beads in a geometric pattern.

**Fingerwoven bags** — Carrying bags built up from dyed wool and vegetable fibre. One arranges its composition in bands of light-brown chevrons alternating with a hatched design in green, red and yellow, its mouth edged with coarsely woven green wool. A second is finger woven from sheep's wool fibre and decorated with a crossed diagonal design in white beadwork; the excess strands are braided and then left to form a fringe along the bottom, with individual strands occasionally beaded. Coarse red-dyed cotton edges its mouth, brown cotton lines the interior, and its shoulder strap is likewise finger woven, predominantly brown with black dyed fibre.

**Motif vocabulary.** Floral motifs in black and blue; triangles ranged at the ends of a sash; chevrons in graded shades of light brown; a hatched design; crossed diagonals; and undifferentiated geometric patterning in blue, white, green, yellow, black and red.

The sources do not name any of these designs in Osage, and say nothing about ribbonwork, wedding coats, broadcloth blankets or the other traditions in our index terms.

### Clothing & dress

Nineteenth-century studio portraits of Osage men, made at Fort Smith and elsewhere, record dress in some detail. The recurring element is a blanket, worn wrapped around the waist and legs or laid over the knees. Over it men wore either a manufactured cloth shirt or a decorated hide shirt — one is fringed, another decorated with porcupine quills. Embroidered skin leggings appear in one portrait. Headgear varies: a cloth turban, a cloth headband, a fur cap with a sheath, a bonnet, or a single feather worn in the hair. Ornament is heavy at the neck and ears — bear-claw necklaces, shell necklaces, a shell choker, shell ear ornaments, earrings, a peace medal, and a chain with a pendant shaped like a rounded shield. Several sitters have their faces painted with stripes and motifs; one has designs painted onto his torso. Men hold a tomahawk, a pipe, or a bow and arrows. A group portrait shows the same vocabulary distributed across eight men, mixing cloth shirts, hats and turbans with fringed hide shirts and leggings. Women's dress is represented only by the brightly coloured beaded sash described above. Painting also had a funerary use: the faces of the dead were painted to signal the person's tribe and clan.

### Architecture

The sources describe Osage settlement pattern rather than house form. Villages were laid out to express the tribe's two divisions: the sky people lived on the side opposite the earth people, with the lodges of the spiritual leaders placed between the two sides. Villages were also important hubs in the Great Plains trading network, with the Kaw acting as intermediaries. In the seventeenth century many Osage had settled near the Osage River in what is now western Missouri; Lewis and Clark in 1804 recorded the Great Osage on the Osage River, the Little Osage upstream and the Arkansas band on the Verdigris. On the built environment raised by outsiders in their territory the record is fuller — Fort Orleans, the first European colonial fort on the Missouri; Fort Carondelet, built by the Chouteau brothers in the village of the Great Osage; and the Union, Harmony, Neosho, Boudinot and three Hopefield missions in Kansas. The sources used do not describe Osage house construction, materials or roofing.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The objects here are ceremonial and personal rather than domestic. A nineteenth-century **dew claw strung rattle** combines dewclaws, leather, rawhide, brass, an eagle's claw and feathers, and a deer's tail. Ceremonial pipes are central: they are named among the ritual apparatus, used with tobacco as offerings in seeking Wakonda's aid, and two of the photographed men hold one. Bundles are the other named class of ceremonial object used in these rites. Tomahawks and the bow and arrows appear in the portraits. The other everyday gear the sources name is textile — the fingerwoven and beaded carrying bags described above, one with a woven shoulder sling. Ceramics are not mentioned, and metalwork appears only as brass in the rattle and as the peace medal and pendant worn in the portraits.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Shell and claw dominate. Bear-claw necklaces are worn by three of the men photographed, shell necklaces and a shell choker by another, and shell ear ornaments recur across the group; earrings and a neck ornament are noted elsewhere. Two metal ornaments enter from trade and diplomacy: a peace medal worn at the chest, and a chain carrying a pendant in the shape of a rounded shield. Face painting is the most consistent body practice in the photographs — stripes and motifs applied to the face, and in one case designs painted onto the torso. These designs had a formal function at death, when the face of the deceased was painted to signal tribe and clan. Porcupine quills were used to decorate a shirt. Hair is dressed with a feather in at least one portrait. The sources say nothing about tattooing or about named jewelry types.

## Music & performance

Song, not instrument, is what the sources document. Osage life was highly ritualized, and ceremonial songs served as the medium in which spiritual leaders recorded what they had learned, since there was no written language. Such songs were taught and shared only among Osage who were sincere and had proven themselves — a restriction that makes the repertoire a form of held knowledge rather than public performance. Songs and ceremonies existed for every facet of life: adoption, marriage, war, agriculture, and the honouring of the sun as it rose in the morning. The ceremonies in which they were sung were presided over by Osage medicine people and spiritual leaders, and used bundles and ceremonial pipes, with tobacco offered in seeking Wakonda's aid. Elaborate as they were, their functions were basic: to ask Wakonda's help in the tribe's continued existence, and to seek the blessing of a long life through children. The only instrument in the objects shown is the dew claw strung rattle. On a modern note, Osage language and the Osage script were used in Martin Scorsese's *Killers of the Flower Moon* (2023), for which Chief Geoffrey Standing Bear and the Nation's language department consulted; Osage and non-Osage actors spoke lines in the language. The sources used do not name any Osage song genre or dance-song cycle.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used do not describe Osage dance or dramatic traditions; the In-Lon-Schka is not mentioned in them.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual year the sources describe is organized around clan and cosmology rather than a festival calendar. Osage who keep traditional custom understand themselves as part of a broader universe created by a supreme life force, *Wah'Kon-Tah* or *Wakonda*, whose spirit is in everything from trees and plants and the sky to animals and human beings. Life divides in two: the sky, masculine, where life is created, and the earth, feminine, to which it descends in material form. Clans were named for elements of that world — animals, plants and weather phenomena such as storms — among them **Red Cedar** (*Hon-tse-shu-tsy*), **Travelers in the Mist** (*Moh-sho-tsa-moie*), **Deer Lungs** (*Tah-lah-he*) and **Elk** (*O-pon*), each with its own responsibilities in the tribe. The central life-cycle rite the sources name is the **ceremonial naming** of a child born into a clan, which introduced the child to the community; without a ceremonial name an Osage child could not take part in ceremonies at all, which made naming a foundation of Osage identity. Marriage was regulated through the clans: members married people of the opposite clan or division. Funerals involved painting the face of the deceased to signal tribe and clan. Ceremonies more generally were conducted with bundles and pipes by medicine people and spiritual leaders.

## Foodways

The sources describe subsistence rather than cooking. The Osage depended on nomadic buffalo hunting and on agriculture. From their woodland homes in present-day Missouri and Arkansas they made semi-annual buffalo hunting forays west into the Great Plains, and hunted deer, rabbit and other wild game in the central and eastern parts of their territory. Near the villages, women cultivated varieties of corn, squash and other vegetables, which they processed for food; they also harvested and processed nuts and wild berries. This mixed economy is what the sources mean in saying that the Osage, in their years of transition, held cultural practices drawn from both Woodland and Great Plains peoples. Tobacco appears not as food but as ceremonial offering, used with the pipe in seeking Wakonda's aid. The sources used name no Osage dishes, cooking methods or drink traditions.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Osage had no written language until the modern period, and the sources treat song as the vehicle of recorded knowledge: ceremonial songs documented what spiritual leaders had learned, and were transmitted only to Osage who had proven themselves. Origin and migration accounts are the other named body of tradition — the Osage tell, as do the Ponca, Omaha, Kaw and Quapaw, of a westward migration from the Ohio valley driven by war with the Haudenosaunee and by the search for game. Contemporary preservation centres on the language. The Osage Language Program, created in 2003, provides audio and video learning materials online; the 2nd Annual Dhegiha Gathering in 2012 brought Osage, Kaw, Quapaw, Ponca and Omaha speakers together to share practice in revitalization; and in early 2015 Chief Geoffrey Standing Bear made immersion a priority. The Nation's immersion school, **Daposka Ahnkodapi**, closed in 2026 for budget reasons. Carolyn Quintero's grammar and dictionary, and Francis La Flesche's 1932 dictionary, are the standing documentary works.

## Language & religion

Osage (*Wažáže ie*) is a Dhegihan language of the Siouan family, spoken in northern Oklahoma, with a sound inventory close to that of Dakota but adding vowel length, preaspirated obstruents and an interdental fricative; the high back vowel *u* has fronted to [y]. A Hominy dialect is distinguished by its treatment of /ð/ and by pronouncing *šc* as *šč*. As of 2009 about 15–20 elders were second-language speakers. The language is written in two systems: the Latin script with diacritics, and the Osage script created in 2006 — one of the few writing systems developed for an Indigenous language in the United States. Religiously, traditional practice centres on *Wakonda* and the sky–earth division. Presbyterian, Dutch Reformed and Associate Reformed missionaries worked among the Osage from the Kansas reservation period; Catholic clergy who stayed through the 1837 smallpox epidemic were held to have created a special covenant with the tribe, though the Osage did not convert in great numbers, feeling the Catholics did not fully embrace the Osage sense of the spiritual as incarnate in nature.
