## Overview

The Cayuga, or *Gayogo̱hó꞉nǫˀ*, are one of the five original constituent nations of the Haudenosaunee, an Iroquoian Indigenous confederacy. Their homeland lies in the Finger Lakes region along Cayuga Lake, between the Onondaga to the east and the Seneca to the west. Today, Cayuga people belong principally to the Lower Cayuga and Upper Cayuga First Nations of the Six Nations of the Grand River in Ontario, the Cayuga Nation of New York, and the Seneca-Cayuga Nation of Oklahoma. In 1995, nearly 450 Cayuga members lived in New York; in the 21st century, the Cayuga-Seneca Nation in Oklahoma had about 4,892 combined members. Their language, Cayuga, is a critically endangered Northern Iroquoian language.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe a dedication held on August 2, 1997, after the Cayuga Nation purchased land in Seneca Falls. Members of all the Haudenosaunee Confederacy nations were present, and elder women of the Cayuga Nation broke the ground and planted a pine tree as a symbol that the Cayuga people were still alive and wished to return to their home territory.

## Language & religion

Cayuga, or *Gayogo̱honǫˀnéha꞉ˀ*, is a Northern Iroquoian language of the Iroquois Proper, also called the “Five Nations Iroquois,” subfamily. It is closely related to Onondaga and especially Seneca. Lower Cayuga, *Ganedagehonǫ́ˀne̱ha꞉ˀ*, and Upper Cayuga, *Dagęhyatgehonǫ́ˀne̱ha꞉ˀ*, are spoken at Six Nations of the Grand River in southern Ontario; a Seneca-Cayuga variety was spoken in Oklahoma until its extinction in the 1980s. Cayuga is polysynthetic and has a verbal template containing pronominal, nominal, verbal, and aspectual elements. The language is critically endangered: 115 Indigenous people reported Cayuga as their mother tongue in the 2021 Canadian census, while around 240 Cayuga people speak it at Six Nations and fewer than 10 at the Cattaraugus Reservation.
