## Overview

The Pawnee, also known by their endonym *Chatiks si chatiks*, are an Indigenous people of the Great Plains. Historically, they lived in Nebraska and northern Kansas, especially in villages near the Loup, Republican, and South Platte rivers; today they are based in Oklahoma, where the federally recognized Pawnee Nation of Oklahoma is headquartered in Pawnee, Oklahoma. Their language belongs to the Caddoan language family. In 2011, approximately 3,200 people were enrolled Pawnee, and nearly all resided in Oklahoma. Pawnee life historically combined earth-lodge village residence, farming, seasonal buffalo hunting, political organization through autonomous bands, and religious ceremonies connected with the four cardinal directions, sacred bundles, crops, stars, and the renewal of life.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name painted poles representing the four cardinal directions and the four major star gods.

### Clothing & dress

 They state that buffalo skins were processed for clothing and accessories, storage bags, foot coverings, fastening ropes, and ties. During the Morning Star ritual, a scaffold was constructed from sacred woods and leathers from different animals. The sources also describe an earlier account in which Quivirans were “nearly naked,” but this is not presented as a general description of Pawnee dress.

### Architecture

Pawnee villages were established near rivers, with lodges placed on higher banks. The principal house form was the earth lodge, which in historical times tended to be oval, while earlier forms were rectangular. Its frame consisted of 10–15 posts set approximately 10 feet (3.0 m) apart, with most lodges having 4, 8, or 12 center-poles. Smaller poles were tied with willow withes, and the frame was covered with thatch and earth.

A hole in the center served as both chimney and smoke vent and as a skylight. The entrance faced east toward the rising sun and led through a long, low passageway into an entry room with an interior buffalo-skin door. A buffalo skull with horns was displayed on the west side of the central room and was considered great medicine. Mats divided the outer ring into sleeping and private spaces. Lodges were semi-subterranean, with their bases recessed approximately three feet (one meter) below ground. A lodge could house 30–50 people, while a village could contain as many as 300–500 people and 10–15 households.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They describe household and hunting materials including buffalo meat, skins, and bones, which women processed for various uses. Buffalo skins became clothing, accessories, storage bags, and foot coverings; they were also used for fastening ropes and ties. Lodge construction used posts, horizontal beams, willow withes, thatch, earth, mats, and buffalo-skin doors. Sacred bundles included materials such as an ear of sacred corn.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 In the account of Coronado’s visit to Quivira, a copper pendant is described as the only evidence of wealth discovered there.

## Music & performance

 They describe ceremonies conducted by Pawnee priests using sacred bundles and materials with symbolic value. Religious ceremonies were connected with maintaining the balance of nature and the Pawnee relationship with gods and spirits. The sources also state that Pawnee cosmology gave a central role to the stars and that crops were planted according to their positions.

The Morning Star ritual involved a sequence of prescribed actions, prayers, a procession, and the participation of men and boys from the village. The preparations took four days. Most of the ceremony took place in the earth lodge of the visionary, while the wider village participated in actions surrounding the scaffold. These materials do not identify songs, instruments, melodies, or dance forms associated with the ceremony.

## Dance & theatre

 They describe processions and other public actions connected with the Morning Star ritual, including the accompaniment of a captive girl from the village to the scaffold by men and boys. The account does not identify this as a dance or theatrical performance.

## Festivals & rituals

Pawnee religious life was organized around ceremonies connected with the four cardinal directions, sacred bundles, crops, stars, gods, and spirits. The sacred bundles included materials such as an ear of sacred corn and were used in ceremonies intended to maintain the balance of nature and the Pawnee relationship with the gods and spirits. In the 1890s, already in Oklahoma, Pawnee people participated in the Ghost Dance movement.

The agricultural cycle followed the cycle of corn. Sacred corn was cultivated and harvested to replace corn in sacred bundles prepared for the major seasons of winter and summer. Seeds were taken from sacred bundles for the spring planting ritual. Corn was the first crop planted and the first harvested, with accompanying ceremonies involving priests and men of the tribe. The Pawnee classified corn by color as black, spotted, white, yellow, and red; these colors were associated, excluding spotted, with the four semi-cardinal directions.

The Skidi Pawnees practiced the Morning Star ritual in Village Across a Hill. The ceremony involved the sacrifice of captive girls and was connected with fertility of the soil, success of the crops, renewal of life in spring, and triumphs on battlefields. It was not necessarily annual. A warrior who dreamed of the Morning Star, usually in autumn, could be commanded to sponsor it. A captive was kept and cared for over the winter, and the sacrifice was arranged in spring in relation to the rising of the Morning Star. The last known sacrifice was of Haxti, a 14-year-old Oglala Lakota girl, on 22 April 1838.

## Foodways

Pawnee women were skilled horticulturalists and cooks. They cultivated and processed ten varieties of corn, seven varieties of pumpkins and squashes, and eight varieties of beans along fertile river bottomlands. Corn, beans, and squash supplied varied nutrients and complemented one another in making whole proteins. The women grew flint corn and flour corn for consumption and also cultivated a sacred variety called “Wonderful” or “Holy Corn” for inclusion in sacred bundles.

Corn was central to the agricultural and religious cycle. The different strains were kept separate during cultivation, and corn was classified according to color: black, spotted, white, yellow, and red. Squash and beans were important agricultural crops but did not have the same theological meaning as corn. In 2005, the last 25 remaining seeds of the Pawnee Eagle Corn variety were successfully sprouted. The source describes its taste as similar to almonds with cream. In November 2010, a traditional Pawnee ceremony with Eagle Corn soup was held in Oklahoma.

Buffalo hunting also shaped Pawnee foodways. After successful kills, women sliced bison meat into strips and dried it on poles over slow fires before storing it. Prepared in this way, the meat was usable for several months. The Pawnee also hunted elk, bear, panther, and skunk for meat and skins.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They do describe a creation account in which the Morning Star and Evening Star gave birth to the first Pawnee woman, while the first Pawnee man was the offspring of the Moon and the Sun. The Pawnee understood themselves as descendants of the stars, and this cosmology shaped daily and spiritual life.

The sources also describe the Morning Star ritual through accounts of visionary dreams, priests, sacred actions, prayers, and the belief that the ceremony renewed life. They name Knife Chief and Petalesharo as Skidi leaders who opposed the old rite; Petalesharo cut loose a Comanche captive from the scaffold in 1817 and carried her to safety.

## Language & religion

The Pawnee language belongs to the Caddoan language family. The sources record Pawnee terms including *ickariʾ* for the Loup, *kíckatuus* for the Platte, and names associated with Pawnee bands and villages. The Pawnee were divided into the Skidi or Skiri-Federation in the north and the South Bands, including Cawi, Kitkahaki, Pitahawirata, and Ckiri.

Pawnee religion included a cosmology in which nature, the four cardinal directions, stars, gods, and spirits were interconnected. The Morning Star and Evening Star were understood as the parents of the first Pawnee woman, and the Moon and Sun as the parents of the first Pawnee man. Priests conducted ceremonies involving sacred bundles and sacred corn. The sources also state that Pawnee religion included ritual cannibalism and human sacrifice and that, in the 1890s, Pawnee people in Oklahoma participated in the Ghost Dance movement.
