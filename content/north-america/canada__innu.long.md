## Overview

The Innu — singular of *Innut*, "people" — are an Indigenous people of northeastern Labrador and parts of Quebec, whose homeland they call *Nitassinan* ("our land") or *Innu-assi* ("Innu land"). About 28,960 people of Innu origin live in settlements and reserves across the two provinces, including Sheshatshiu and Natuashish in Labrador and Kawawachikamach in Quebec. Their ancestors lived on this land as hunter-gatherers for thousands of years, following seasonal hunting migrations with portable skin tents and subsisting on caribou, moose, deer and small game. *Innu-aimun* belongs to the Algonquian family, within the Cree–Montagnais–Naskapi dialect continuum, and is unrelated to the Inuit languages of neighbouring peoples. In folk-culture terms the Innu matter for a hide-working and clothing tradition built entirely around caribou, for the tea doll, and for one of the most visible Indigenous-language popular musics in Canada.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Buckskin** — the leather that underlay clothing, boots, moccasins, house covers and storage. Women prepared it: hides were scraped free of fur, left outside to freeze, stretched on a frame, then rubbed with a mixture of animal brain and pine needle tea to soften them. The dampened hide was balled and left overnight, stretched again the next morning, smoked and tanned over a smoker, and left overnight once more. Only then was it called buckskin.

**Painted and quilled caribou skin** — Innu craft makers decorated caribou skins with painted or quill designs, and made many kinds of clothing from the decorated hide.

**Beadwork on tanned hide** — the dominant decorative technique on the museum objects shown here. Borders of seed beads run around the aperture and across the vamp of moccasins in green, red, blue, yellow, orange and white; smaller beadwork patches carry a single motif. Hanging beadwork panels in pink, brown, white and green, or in red, yellow, blue and green, are attached to soapstone-bowled smoking pipes.

**Cotton thread embroidery and appliqué** — tanned deer-hide moccasins are stitched with brown cotton thread and finished with a red cotton band and drawstrings around the aperture; on one pair a cross-hatched green cotton thread border frames an embroidered red flower. The same floral idea is worked in sewn red wool on white felt children's slippers.

**Innu tea doll** — a cloth doll stuffed with tea, made by women from caribou hides and scraps of cloth. Nomadic families left nothing behind on their long journeys, believing that "Crow" would take it away, and everyone including young children carried something; girls were given tea dolls so they could play with a load that was also essential freight. A documented example has black yarn hair, a red and black felt hat, a floral patterned cloth dress and apron, a tanned hide face with black embroidered features, hide hair ties strung with blue, red and white seed beads, hide boot soles with cotton uppers, and a hide mitten harness; it carries a baby doll with a hide face wrapped in cloth blankets and tied with a tanned hide cradle board.

**Motif vocabulary.** Red flower and floral sprays on moccasin vamps; a three-pronged design at the vamp centre; a bird's-foot design in green and orange beads; an oval bead panel; cross-hatched border bands.

### Clothing & dress

Men wore caribou pants and boots with a buckskin long shirt, all of it made by women; women wore long buckskin dresses. After trade cloth arrived from the French and English, cloth shirts replaced the buckskin ones, while boots and pants continued to be made from caribou hide, and contemporary Innu women have often replaced the dress with manufactured pants and jackets. Footwear survives best in collections: moccasins of tanned deer hide sewn with brown cotton thread, made for adults and for children, and white felt slippers for children. Women traditionally wore their hair long or in two coils, men theirs long. Both wore necklaces of bone and bead. A British Museum photograph taken near Davis Inlet, Labrador shows the mixed twentieth-century dress of Innu women at a tent: patterned and blue check skirts, blue and red shirts, and red and blue headscarves. A man who killed a bear wore a necklace made from its claws, a sign of joy and of initiation into adulthood.

### Architecture

The Innu traditionally lived in wigwams, covered with birchbark in the forested south and with caribou hide in the north; the seasonal hunting migrations were supported by portable tents of animal skins. In modern houses a metal stove in the centre of the room has replaced the hearth. The Davis Inlet photograph records a later variant of the same form: a tent of white sheets stretched over a frame of rough pine logs, pitched with a pile of brushwood and hanging canvas beside it, on a bay backed by pine-forested hills. The sources do not describe decoration of the built structures themselves, nor any named permanent building type.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Buckskin, not clay or metal, was the principal material of Innu household equipment: house covers and storage as well as clothing and footwear were made from it. Smoking pipes were used by women and men alike, the women's marked as shorter; surviving pipes have a carved soapstone bowl, a short wooden stem, and a beadwork panel hanging from the stem. Travel gear defines the rest of the inventory: canoes in summer, snowshoes and toboggans in winter. Snowshoes are still used where hunting or trapping requires them, while many communities now depend on trucks, SUVs and cars, and northern communities on snowmobiles. The sources record no Innu ceramic or metalworking tradition.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Necklaces of bone and bead were worn by both women and men. A bear-claw necklace was made and worn by a man who had killed a bear, marking both joy and his initiation into adulthood. Hair itself carried the clearest gender distinction: women wore it long or in two coils, men long. Seed beads in blue, red and white are used for the hair ties of a tea doll, the doll reproducing in miniature what women wore. The sources do not describe tattooing, body painting or any other form of permanent adornment.

## Music & performance

Innu folk music is dance- and percussion-centred, and it has become the most widely heard Indigenous-language popular music in Canada. Its core instrument is the *teueikan*, a Montagnais frame drum with snares. **Philip Mackenzie** created a singer-songwriter tradition in *Innu-aimun*, working at first with only guitar and *teueikan*; later performers in his folk Innu style added acoustic and electronic instruments. The annual **Innu Nikamu** ("The Innu Sings") festival in Quebec is a major gathering for Native American music of every kind, and the folk-rock band **Kashtin** began its career there. Kashtin's success from the 1980s onward, and the later solo careers of its founders **Claude McKenzie** and **Florent Vollant**, gave Innu-aimun broad exposure in Canada and France: "Ish-kuess" ("Girl"), "E Uassiuian" ("My Childhood"), "Tipatshimun" ("Story") and above all "Akua tuta" ("Take care of yourself") were widely heard, the last appearing on soundtrack compilations for the television series *Due South* and the documentary *Music for The Native Americans*. The lyrics of "Akua tuta" are reproduced on over fifty websites, making the song one of the most broadly accessible texts in any Indigenous North American language. Vollant also recorded Innu-language versions of well-known Christmas carols on his 1999 album *Nipaiamianan*. Other notable Innu musicians include Natasha Kanapé Fontaine, Kanen, Matiu, Geneviève McKenzie-Sioui, Scott-Pien Picard and Shauit.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is central to Innu folk music, but the sources name no individual dance, mask tradition or dramatic form. What they do document is Innu presence in film: Arthur Lamothe's documentary series *Carcajou et le péril blanc* (*Kauapishit Miam Kuakuatshen Etentakuess*) was among the first films in the history of cinema to show Indigenous people speaking their own languages, and later work set in Innu communities includes the features *Le Dep*, *Mesnak* and *Kuessipan*, and the documentaries *Innu Nikamu: Resist and Sing* and *Call Me Human*. Marjorie Beaucage's 1997 film *Ntapueu … i am telling the truth* documented the Innu Nation's campaign over the environmental impact of the Voisey's Bay mining project.

## Festivals & rituals

Canada has no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions, so nothing in that register bears on the Innu. The one named annual gathering the sources record is **Innu Nikamu**, held each year in Quebec as a festival of Native American music. Ritual life before colonization was organized around respect for animals, and above all around the caribou, which supplied most of the necessities of life and was the most revered animal. Shamans performed **shaking tent** rites. Hunters appeased animal spirits with offerings of bones and skulls, placed in trees or on raised platforms. The spirits named in the sources are **Caribou Master**, **Atshen** and **Matshishkapeu**. Coming of age is marked for men by killing a bear: the event was a sign of joy and an initiation into adulthood, and the hunter afterwards wore a necklace of the bear's claws. The sources record no birth, marriage or funeral rite in detail. Since the turn of the twentieth century, the Catholic, Moravian and Anglican churches, together with the Canadian and provincial governments, pressed the Innu to settle in permanent communities; this coercive assimilation cost them some of the hunting, trapping and fishing that structured the ritual year.

## Foodways

Caribou in the east and north and moose in the west were the staples, hunted alongside beaver, bear, lake fish and salmon; the Innu also fished for eels, hunted seals and gathered roots, berries and maple sap. The recorded meat and fish list is long: moose, caribou, porcupine, rabbit, marten, woodchuck, squirrel; Canada goose, snow goose, brant, duck, teal, loon, spruce grouse, woodcock, snipe, passenger pigeon, ptarmigan; whitefish, lake trout, salmon, Arctic char, seal, pike, walleye, suckerfish, sturgeon, catfish, lamprey and smelt. Fish were roasted or smoke-dried; moose meat and several fish were also smoked. Meat was eaten frozen, raw or roasted, and caribou sometimes boiled in a stew. *Pemmican* was made with moose or caribou. Oat *bannock*, introduced by the French in the sixteenth century, became a staple and Indigenous bannock is still eaten. Cornmeal traded from the Iroquois, Algonquin and Abenaki was made into *apon*, a cornbread that later sometimes included oat or wheat flour. Gathered plant foods were raspberries, blueberries, strawberries, cherries, wild grapes, hazelnuts, crab apples, red martagon bulbs, Indian potato, and maple sap for sweetening. Pine needle tea — the same infusion used to soften hides — was drunk to keep off the infections and colds brought on by harsh weather. The Innu also grate and eat the inner bark of balsam fir, *Abies balsamea*, to benefit the diet.

## Oral tradition & literature

Innu oral traditions resemble those of other Cree-speaking cultures. The figure of particular importance is *Tshakapesh*, a lunar folk hero. Oral history also carries the record of first contact: two versions circulate of the encounter with Samuel de Champlain's French explorers at *Uepishtikueiau*, the coastal land that became Quebec City. In the first, the French used gifts of farmed food and manufactured goods to make the Innu dependent, then converted the relationship into fur trade and enlarged their settlement while the Innu wintered inland, displacing them. In the second and more widespread version, the Innu taught the French how to survive, the French resented them once they no longer needed teaching, and open fighting followed until the Innu — outnumbered and outgunned — withdrew from the area. Both versions begin from the same point: neither side understood the other's language, and the Innu distrusted the newcomers' motives. On the documentary side, a comprehensive pan-Innu dictionary covering all the dialects spoken in Quebec and Labrador was published in 2013 in Innu, English and French.

## Language & religion

*Innu-aimun*, also called Montagnais, is an Algonquian language with over 10,000 speakers in Labrador and Quebec, part of the Cree–Montagnais–Naskapi continuum. It has four dialects — Southern Montagnais (Mashteuiatsh, Betsiamites), Eastern Montagnais (Ekuanitshit, Nutashkuan, Unamen Shipu, Pakuashipi), Central Montagnais (Uashat and Maliotenam, Matimekosh) and Labrador-Montagnais (Sheshatshit) — whose speakers understand one another well; Naskapi language and culture differ more, marked by the shift from *y* to *n*, as in Iiyuu against Innu. The language is polysynthetic and head-marking with relatively free word order, two genders (animate and inanimate) and four verb classes by transitivity. Pre-colonial religion turned on respect for animals, the caribou above all, with shaking tent rites and offerings of bones and skulls; Catholic, Moravian and Anglican missions worked among the Innu from the eighteenth century, the Moravians founding their first Labrador mission at Nuneingoak in 1771.
