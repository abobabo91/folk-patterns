## Overview

The Coeur d'Alene Tribe, also known as *Skitswish* and *Schi̱tsu'umsh*, is one of five federally recognized tribes in Idaho. Historically, the Coeur d'Alene occupied 3.5 million acres in northern Idaho, eastern Washington, and western Montana, with villages along the Coeur d'Alene, St. Joe, Clark Fork, and Spokane rivers and beside Lake Coeur d'Alene, Lake Pend Oreille, and Hayden Lake. Their reservation includes the lower third of Lake Coeur d'Alene, the Saint Joe River, and submerged lands. In the early 21st century, the tribe had approximately 2,000 enrolled citizens. Their language, Snchitsu'umshtsn, is an endangered Interior Salishan language. Fishing, hunting, plant gathering, seasonal movement, environmental stewardship, and language preservation are central themes in the available descriptions.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Historically, Coeur d'Alene people retreated during winter to clustered semi-subterranean dwellings.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do record fishing equipment, including gaff hooks, spears, nets, and traps.

## Music & performance

They do identify musicians and recording artists associated with the community. Mildred Bailey, described as a popular jazz singer and recording artist of the 1930s and 1940s, performed with Paul Whiteman and Benny Goodman and became known as “Mrs. Swing.” Her younger brother Al Rinker grew up on the reservation and belonged to “The Rhythm Boys” with Bing Crosby and Harry Barris through 1931. Charles Rinker, another younger brother of Mildred Bailey, was a lyricist. The sources also mention the independent film *Smoke Signals* (1998), set on the Coeur d'Alene Reservation and produced through an all-Native American production.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that precontact Coeur d'Alene economy and lifeways involved fishing, hunting, plant gathering, seasonal migratory patterns, and winter residence in clustered semi-subterranean dwellings. The sources also record conversion of many tribal members to Roman Catholicism in 1842 by Fr. Pierre-Jean De Smet, a Belgian Jesuit missionary from St. Louis, Missouri. Coeur d'Alene traditions are described as involving respect and reverence for natural law and responsible environmental stewardship.

## Foodways

They describe a subsistence economy based on fishing, hunting, and plant gathering. Natural resources included trout, salmon, whitefish, beaver, deer, and wild-fowl. The tribe supplemented hunting and gathering by fishing the St. Joe and Spokane rivers, using gaff hooks, spears, nets, traps, and angled fishing. The territory also contained camas-prairie, grass-covered hills, forested mountains, lakes, marshes, and river habitat.

## Oral tradition & literature

They preserve historical descriptions recorded by Alexander Henry the younger and Ross Cox, including accounts of the Coeur d'Alene, their territory, relations with neighboring peoples, trade, horses, and conflict with the Spokane. The language program has worked with elders to record more than 2,000 hours of audio and video. The Coeur d'Alene Names-Places Project records elders describing geographic sites on the reservation in English and Coeur d'Alene. Sherman Alexie, identified as Spokane-Coeur d'Alene, wrote the screenplay for *Smoke Signals* (1998), based on the short story “This Is What It Means to Say Phoenix, Arizona.”

## Language & religion

Coeur d'Alene, known to its speakers as *Snchitsu’umshtsn*, is an Interior Salishan language within the Salishan language family. In 1999 it was spoken by only two of the 800 individuals in the Coeur d'Alene Tribe on the Coeur d'Alene Reservation, and it was considered endangered. As of 2014, two elders in their 90s remained who had grown up with Cœur d'Alène as their first language, while use of the language was spreading among all age groups. The Coeur d'Alene Tribal Language Program, elders, North Idaho College, and radio station KWIS FM 88.3 in Plummer, Idaho, support language teaching and preservation. The language has three orthographies discussed in the sources: LPO, Bitar, and the Reichard orthography. The sources also record conversion of many tribal members to Roman Catholicism in 1842.
