## Overview

Copper Inuit, also known as *Inuinnait* and *Kitlinermiut*, are a Canadian Inuit group living north of the tree line in what is now the Kitikmeot Region of Nunavut and the Inuvialuit Settlement Region in the Inuvik Region of the Northwest Territories. Historically, most lived around Coronation Gulf, on Victoria Island, and on southern Banks Island. Their territory extended from Wise Point near Dolphin and Union Strait to the eastern shore of Great Bear Lake, Contwoyto Lake, and Lake Beechey on the Back River, and eastward to Perry River in Queen Maud Gulf. They traditionally spoke Inuinnaqtun and Inuvialuktun, sometimes called Western Canadian Inuktitut. For about 3,000 years they were hunter-gatherer nomads, known especially for gathering native copper and transforming it into tools and weapons.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

They do, however, describe ceremonial clothing whose designs referred to the integration of the human and animal realm and the natural and supernatural.

**Ceremonial clothing** — Historically, many Inuit had clothing made from short-haired summer skins for dancing and other ceremonial occasions. Copper Inuit dance clothing was studied and preserved in museums worldwide. Its design was linked with Copper Inuit religious practices. By the 1930s this ceremonial clothing had died out, although it was intentionally revived in the 1990s.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no specific textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Copper Inuit everyday clothing included short-waisted inner parkas with long, narrow back tails and sleeves ending short of the wrist. In severe weather, a heavy outer parka was added. Women’s parkas had elongated hoods and exaggerated, pointed shoulders. Boots extended up the leg and buttoned at the waistline; their soles were made from feathers or bird skins.

The sources name several materials used in contemporary clothing and boots. Dance caps could use caribou, ermine, and the bill of a yellow-billed loon. Parkas could be made from Arctic hare, otter, rabbit, or wild mink. Mitts could use beaver, polar bear, or skunk. Boots could use caribou, dog, polar bear, seal, wolf, or wolverine skins. *Kamiit* were made from caribou or moose. Copper Inuit also used different napkins for different meals: ptarmigan skins when eating caribou and gull skins when eating seal.

Ceremonial clothing consisted of short-haired summer skins worn for dancing and other ceremonial occasions.

### Architecture

Copper Inuit lived in communal snowhouses during winter. In summer, they dispersed into smaller family groups for inland caribou hunting and fishing. The sources also describe later settlement in Holman, Coppermine, Cambridge Bay, and outposts along Bathurst Inlet, Contwoyto Lake, Coronation Gulf, and Victoria Island. In 1960, the federal government shipped three housing units to Holman, followed by another four in 1961. Some families subsequently lived there permanently, while others remained seasonally.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Copper was central to Copper Inuit material culture. The people made copper arrows, spear heads, *ulu* blades, chisels, harpoons, and knives for personal use and for trade with other Inuit. Copper was gathered along the lower Coppermine River and the Coronation Gulf. Copper Inuit soapstone products were highly regarded in the Bering Strait trade network.

The sources also record that wood, copper, and iron recovered from HMS Investigator at Mercy Bay were used for years. The people used and cooked food and products from the sea but kept them separate from those of the land.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They mention dance caps, elongated hoods, exaggerated pointed shoulders, and clothing made from animal skins.

## Music & performance

They record that ceremonial clothing was worn for dancing and other ceremonial occasions, and that Copper Inuit dance clothing was connected with religious practices. No specific music or dance performance is described.

## Dance & theatre

Copper Inuit ceremonial clothing was worn for dancing and other ceremonial occasions. The sources state that this dance clothing was extensively studied and preserved in museums worldwide.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe seasonal activities. During winter, Copper Inuit lived in communal snowhouses and hunted seals at breathing holes, or *mauliqtoq*. In summer, they spread into smaller family groups for inland caribou hunting and fishing. Many gathered in the Cambridge Bay area in summertime because of plentiful game.

Copper Inuit religion included taboos and beliefs that animal spirits could be offended through taboo violations. The *angakkuit*, or shamans, could be male or female. They warded off evil spirits, acted as intermediaries between people and the spirit world, healed illness or taboo violations, and controlled weather.

## Foodways

Historically, Copper Inuit foodways combined hunting, fishing, and gathering. They hunted Arctic ground squirrel, Arctic hare, caribou, grizzly bear, mink, moose, muskox, muskrat, polar bear, wolf, and wolverine. Their freshwater fisheries included the Coppermine, Rae, and Richardson Rivers, where Arctic char, grayling, lake trout, and whitefish were found. Marine waters supported codfish, bearded seal, and ringed seal. Ducks, geese, guillemots, gulls, hawks, longspurs, loons, plovers, ptarmigans, and snow buntings also formed part of the diet.

They liked raw eggs but not boiled eggs. The sources distinguish foods and products from the sea from those of the land: Copper Inuit used and cooked both, but kept them separate. Ptarmigan skins served as napkins when eating caribou, while gull skins were used when eating seal.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources mention Inuit stories describing a history of conflict between Inuit and the Dene, and possibly involving Europeans. These stories are discussed in connection with trade disputes and raids for women. One battle was recorded by European explorer Samuel Hearne: in 1771, Matonabbee, leader of Hearne’s Chipewyan Dene guides, and his companions massacred a Copper Inuit group at Bloody Falls.

Copper Inuit religious belief included dwarfs, giants, “caribou people,” and the sea-goddess *Arnapkapfaaluk*, or “big bad woman.” Their conception of the *tupilaq* was similar to the Christian concept of the devil.

## Language & religion

Copper Inuit traditionally speak Inuinnaqtun and Inuvialuktun, sometimes referred to as Western Canadian Inuktitut. Many young people now speak English rather than Inuinnaqtun.

Copper Inuit had an animistic spiritual system. They believed that animal spirits could be offended through taboo violations, and that dwarfs, giants, “caribou people,” and *Arnapkapfaaluk* inhabited the world. The *angakkuit* could be male or female and served as intermediaries between people and the spirit world. They also healed illness or taboo violations, warded off evil spirits, and controlled weather. The sources state that Copper Inuit have gradually adopted Christian churches.
