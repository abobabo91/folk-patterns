## Overview

The Tunica are a group of linguistically and culturally related Native American tribes in the Mississippi River Valley. The group includes the Tunica, also spelled Tonica, Tonnica, and Thonnica; the Yazoo; the Koroa, also called Akoroa and Courouais; and possibly the Tioux. They first encountered Europeans in 1541 through members of the Hernando de Soto expedition. Under pressure from hostile neighbors, they moved from the Central Mississippi Valley to the Lower Mississippi Valley, eventually settling around present-day Marksville, Louisiana. The modern Tunica-Biloxi Indian Tribe includes Tunica, Biloxi, Ofo, Avoyel, Mississippi Choctaw, European, and African ancestry. Many members live on the Tunica-Biloxi Indian Reservation in central Avoyelles Parish, just south of Marksville. The 2000 census recorded 648 people who self-identified as Tunica.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Pottery in the Tunica tribal style** — Locally produced pottery in the Tunica tribal style was found at the Trudeau site together with European trade goods. Earlier archaeological descriptions also identify mussel shell-tempered pottery and differences in pottery styles among Central Mississippi Valley archaeological phases.

**Grave goods** — Pottery, European trade goods, beads, porcelain, muskets, kettles, and other objects were deposited as grave goods at the Trudeau site from 1731 to 1764.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no Tunica textile motifs or pattern vocabulary.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe clothing in several historical contexts. In 1541, Spanish observers reported taking skins and shawls from houses in the town of Quizquiz. In the 18th century, a Tunica chief was described as dressed in the French fashion. The sources also state that Tunica people continued to tattoo themselves during the late 18th century, after becoming acculturated to European ways. Tunica language records include *e'htiwa'hkuni*, glossed as “breechcloth,”. No specific head coverings, belts, footwear, or gender-differentiated clothing are documented.

### Architecture

The sources describe settlement forms, fortified towns, temples, and dwellings. During the Mississippian period, settlements included dispersed settlements, farmsteads, and villages; later settlements became more centralized towns with defensive palisades and ditches. The Parkin site was a 17-acre palisaded village at the confluence of the St. Francis and Tyronza rivers. Spanish accounts describe towns with stockades, towers, and ditches, including a canal carrying river water into a ditch.

At Quizquiz, the Curaca’s dwelling stood on a high mound that served as a fortress and was reached by two stairways. Around 1700, the Tunica and neighboring groups maintained temples, cult images, and a priest class. At Angola, the Tunica established a loose collection of hamlets and villages. Later, the Tunica lived in villages at Trudeau Landing, Pointe Coupée, and Marksville.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Tunica material culture included pottery, locally produced in a tribal style, as well as pottery associated with earlier Mississippian societies. Archaeological evidence identifies mussel shell-tempered pottery and differences in pottery styles between competing polities. At the Trudeau site, grave goods included beads, porcelain, muskets, kettles, and other European trade goods. The Tunica were also associated with salt production: at Tanico, people scooped sand into baskets, ran water through it to make brine, strained the brine, and dried it in shallow bowls before scraping off the salt. Salt became an important trade commodity between the Tunica, French traders, and Caddoan groups.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 They do state that Tunica people continued to tattoo themselves in the late 18th century, after becoming acculturated to European ways. Beads appear among the European trade goods deposited as grave goods at the Trudeau site.

## Music & performance

 They do document ceremonial and social practices that involved performance. A celebratory dance was held during the 1730 confrontation involving Tunica, Natchez, Chickasaw, and Koroa people. The dance continued until after midnight, when the Tunica retired to their cabins. The sources also mention the Corn Feast as an ancient tribal ceremony revived in the 1870s under the leadership of Volsin Chiki.

The Tunica-Biloxi Language & Culture Revitalization Program uses webinars to teach the Tunica language. In 2010, tribal members read from a new children’s book in Tunica at a pow wow. These activities connect language preservation with public cultural events.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name no Tunica dramatic tradition, masked performance, theatrical form, or named dance apart from a celebratory dance. During the 1730 attack at the Tunica village, Natchez refugees and their allies held a celebratory dance before the fighting began. The sources also identify the Corn Feast as a tribal ceremony revived in the 1870s.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do identify several ritual and ceremonial practices. During the period around the mouth of the Yazoo River, the Tunica, Taensa, and Natchez were described as having complex religions, temples, cult images, and priest classes. Regional tribes maintained eternal flames in their villages and visited the Tunica to replenish a flame when it went out.

The Corn Feast was identified as an ancient tribal ceremony revived in the 1870s by the Tunica chief Volsin Chiki. The sources also describe ritual punishment after the 1730 attack by Natchez, Chickasaw, and Koroa forces: the Tunica killed 33 Natchez warriors, took three prisoners, and later burned the prisoners. They do document the placement of pottery, European trade goods, and other objects as grave goods at the Trudeau site from 1731 to 1764.

## Foodways

Agriculture, salt production, trade, hunting, and fishing all appear in the sources. During the Mississippian period, communities in the Central Mississippi Valley practiced intensive maize agriculture. In the late 17th century, the Tunica were described as living entirely on Indian corn and working in their fields. Unlike some other Indigenous groups, they were said not to hunt at that time. The sources also connect Tunica culture with agricultural emphasis and cultivation by men rather than women.

Salt was a major Tunica occupation and trade commodity. At Tanico, people made brine by running water through sand collected in baskets, then dried the brine in shallow bowls. The Tunica acted as middlemen in the movement of salt from Caddoan areas to French traders. Their settlement near the confluence of the Mississippi and Red rivers allowed them to remain connected with salt sources in the Caddoan areas.

At Pointe Coupée, the Tunica relied more on hunting than farming and worked for Europeans as hunters or guides. In the 19th century, they became subsistence farmers, with hunting and fishing supporting them; some also became sharecroppers.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They do document an extensive effort to preserve and describe the Tunica language. William Ely Johnson worked with Swiss ethnologist Albert Gatschet in 1886 to document the language. Linguist John R. Swanton continued the work in the early 1900s, and Mary Haas worked with Sesostrie Youchigant in the 1930s. Haas’s description was published as *A Grammar of the Tunica Language* in 1941, followed by *Tunica Texts* in 1950 and *Tunica Dictionary* in 1953.

In 2010, the Tunica-Biloxi tribe formed the Tunica Language Project with the Linguistics Program at Tulane University. Tribal members read from a new children’s book in Tunica at a pow wow, and the Tunica-Biloxi Language & Culture Revitalization Program used webinars to teach people who did not live near the reservation.

## Language & religion

Tunica is a language isolate spoken historically in the Central and Lower Mississippi Valley. The language is also called Luhchi Yoroni, Tonica, or, less commonly, Yuron. There are no native speakers of Tunica. The last known native speaker, Sesostrie Youchigant, died in 1948. The sources report 32 second-language speakers in 2017 and 60 second-language speakers as of 2023.

Tunica has been documented through the work of William Ely Johnson, Albert Gatschet, John R. Swanton, and Mary Haas. By the 17th century, Tunica people communicated by Mobilian Jargon or French as the population declined through infectious diseases, warfare, and social disruption.

The sources describe an earlier complex religion involving temples, cult images, priests, and eternal flames. They also identify the Corn Feast and other native religious customs.
