## Overview

The Quileute are a Native American people indigenous to western Washington state in the United States. Their traditional territory lies on the Olympic Peninsula, including the mouth of the Quillayute River on the Pacific coast; the community of La Push is the main population center of the Quileute Indian Reservation. The Quileute Tribe is federally recognized and was formally established in 1936. In 2020, 404 Quileutes lived on the Quileute Tribe Reservation, while the tribe had 808 enrolled citizens in 2018. The Quileute language, *kʷòʔlí·yot̓ísk̓ʷa*, belongs to the Chimakuan language family and is considered dormant after the death of its last native speaker in 1999. Fishing, canoe making, woven baskets, dog-hair blankets, oral traditions, and ceremonial societies are central subjects in the sources.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Woven baskets** — The Quileute are known for finely woven baskets made from resources available in their environment. Some baskets were woven so tightly that they could hold water, and water could be boiled in them.

**Dog-hair blankets** — The Quileute raised specially bred, woolly dogs for their hair, which they spun and wove into blankets.

**Cedar-bark clothing** — Cedar bark was used to make waterproof skirts and hats that protected the body from the heavy rainfall of the region.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe waterproof skirts and hats made from cedar bark, which protected Quileute bodies from heavy rainfall. They also document dog-hair blankets made by raising specially bred, woolly dogs, spinning their hair, and weaving it into blankets. They do record that a settler’s fire in 1889 destroyed Quileute cultural property, including sacred regalia, hunting equipment, baskets, and carved masks, except for objects already held in private collections.

### Architecture

In precontact times, the Quileute built plank houses, also called longhouses, to protect themselves from the harsh, wet winters west of the Cascade Mountains. James Island, *alak̓ista*, functioned as both a fortified refuge and a village after a mainland village was relocated there. In 1889, a settler burned all twenty-six houses at La Push, destroying Quileute cultural property from precontact days.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do document wooden tools and household objects, reflecting the use of local resources: utensils, weapons, paints, canoes, and other necessities were made from wood. Cedar canoes ranged from small boats holding two people to giant vessels up to 58 metres (190 ft) long and capable of carrying up to 6,000 pounds. Canoes were also made for whaling and could hold tons of cargo and many men.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They do mention sacred regalia among the cultural property destroyed at La Push in 1889.

## Music & performance

Quileute ceremonial societies functioned as dance fraternities identified with occupational groups, and each society had a particular prestige. The five societies were the Black Face Society for warriors, the fishermen’s ritual, the hunters’ society, the whalers’ society, and the weathermen’s society. The warrior society was also known as the Wolf ritual. The hunters’ society was the oldest and of Quileute origin. The weathermen’s society was adopted from the Quinault, and its songs were sung in Quinault. The Black Face Society, fishermen’s ritual, and whalers’ society were adopted from the Makah.

Membership could be gained by demonstrating possession of a spirit power relevant to a society or by buying membership through a potlatch. Initiations took place during winter and typically included potlatches. Leadership within a society was a source of prestige.

## Dance & theatre

The Quileute held ceremonial societies whose activities included dances connected with occupational groups. These included the Black Face Society for warriors, the fishermen’s ritual, the hunters’ society, the whalers’ society, and the weathermen’s society. The warrior society was also called the Wolf ritual. They do mention carved masks among the Quileute cultural property destroyed in 1889.

## Festivals & rituals

Quileute potlatches were held for many purposes, including birth, naming ceremonies, coming of age, inheritance, marriages, memorials to the dead, and the maintenance of property rights. A typical potlatch lasted four to six days. Guests were reimbursed by the host according to their status, and the family holding the potlatch gained status corresponding to the amount of wealth it gave away.

Initiations into ceremonial societies took place during winter and typically included potlatches. Social prestige gave people rights to names, dances, songs, designs, and membership in secret societies. Traditional beliefs included spirit questing and adoption by a spirit power.

## Foodways

The Pacific Ocean was the main source of subsistence for the Quileute in historic times, and fishing was the principal subsistence activity. The waters of Quileute territory contained five species of salmon, steelheads, halibuts, smelts, trout, flounders, dogfish, and other fish. The Quileute also collected octopodes and more than 50 species of shellfish. They hunted whales and seals, and also hunted deer and elk with bow and arrow, traps, and snares, as well as birds and eggs.

The sources describe the use of velvetleaf huckleberries, *Vaccinium myrtilloides*. The berries were eaten uncooked, stewed into a sauce, and canned for use as food.

## Oral tradition & literature

According to Quileute oral history, the Quileutes were created from wolves by Qwaeti the Transformer, known in Quileute as *Ḳ̓ʷá̱·ti*. They were separated from their kin, the Chemakum, when a great flood washed the Chemakum away and deposited them at what is today Port Townsend.

Traditional Quileute beliefs center on the essence of the universe, *t̕siḳ̓áti*, the Creator-Transformer Qwaeti, spirit powers, *taxí·lit*, and the ghosts of the dead, *yalá·*. Raven, *bá·yaḳ*, is an important figure in Quileute stories, serving as trickster and culture hero. Other figures include Thunderbird, *t̕íst̕ilal*, the kelp-haired child-snatcher *dásk̓iya*, and other monsters who affected the world.

The Quileute Nation has pursued language and cultural preservation through books written for students by tribal elders, downloadable alphabet sheets, audiobooks read in Quileute, and a language and culture app released in 2021.

## Language & religion

Quileute, *kʷòʔlí·yot̓ísk̓ʷa*, is one of two Chimakuan languages; the only related language is the extinct Chemakum language. Quileute was spoken by the Quileute people and also by the Hoh people, who are sometimes considered a Quileute band. The language went extinct in 1999, although several second-language speakers retain knowledge of it. The 2010 version of the Atlas of the World’s Languages in Danger listed it as extinct.

The Quileute alphabet was developed in 1969 by the Quileute Culture Committee. Stress marks and a dot indicating a long vowel are used in its orthography. Traditional beliefs involved Qwaeti, spirit powers, the essence of the universe, and the ghosts of the dead. Around 1895, the Indian Shaker Church came to Quileute country and was quickly adopted.
