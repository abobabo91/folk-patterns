## Overview

The Gitxsan, also spelled Gitksan and Kitksan, are an Indigenous people in Canada whose home territory comprises most of the area known in English as the Skeena Country. Their territory covers approximately 35,000 km2 (14,000 sq mi), extending along the upper Skeena River from about Legate Creek to the river’s headwaters and surrounding tributaries. Approximately 5,000 people live across British Columbia, with many others elsewhere in the province and around the world. Gitxsan society belongs to the Tsimshianic language group and is associated culturally with the Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast, although the territory lies inland. The society is matrilineal and organized into Frog, Eagle, Wolf, and Fireweed Clans, each containing independent Houses known as *Wilp*. Their traditional language is Gitxsanimaax, also called Gitxsan or Gitksan.

## Festivals & rituals

Gitxsan society is matrilineal and consists of Frog, Eagle, Wolf, and Fireweed Clans. Each clan contains independent Houses, or *Wilp*, with their own High Chief, traditional territories, and fishing sites. Marriage within a clan is forbidden.

The sources identify the Gitksan Potlatch in the bibliography of the Gitxsan article, including John W. Adams’s *The Gitksan Potlatch: Population Flux, Resource Ownership and Reciprocity*, published in 1973. They also list William Beynon’s *Potlatch at Gitsegukla: William Beynon’s 1945 Field Notebooks*, edited by Margaret Anderson and Marjorie Halpin and published in 2000. The supplied sources do not themselves describe the potlatch’s ceremonies, objects, participants, or schedule.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources identify several works concerning Gitksan and Gitxsan history and oral tradition. *We-Gyet Wanders On: Legends of the Northwest*, published by Bookbuildes of *'Ksan* in 1977, is listed in the bibliography. Marius Barbeau’s *The Downfall of Temlaham*, published in 1928, and Kenneth B. Harris’s *Visitors Who Never Left: The Origin of the People of Damelahamid*, published in 1974, are also listed. Terry Glavin’s *A Death Feast in Dimlahamid* appeared in 1990, and Roy Russell’s *Feast: A Gitksan Story* in 2015. They also state that the online Gitxsan dictionary app includes flashcards, stories, and histories, alongside audio from different villages and several Gitxsan dialects. The app was released in the spring of 2018 through collaboration involving members of Gitksan Nation and researchers at the University of British Columbia.

## Language & religion

Gitxsan, also called Gitxsanimaax, Gitksan, Giatikshan, Gityskyan, Giklsan, and Sim Algyax, is an endangered Tsimshianic language of northwestern British Columbia. It is closely related to Nisga’a, although the two groups are politically separate and prefer to treat Gitxsan and Nisga'a as distinct languages. Gitxsan is divided primarily into Geenix, or Eastern Gitxsan, and Gyeets, or Western Gitxsan; each village has its own dialect. Eastern villages include Kispiox, Glen Vowell, and Hazelton, while Western villages include Kitwanga, Gitanyow, and Kitseguecla.

The sources report 255 fluent speakers, 339 people who understand or somewhat speak the language, and 648 learning speakers. A language revitalization program was established through the University of Northern British Columbia and Siiwiixo'osxwim Wilnataahl Gitksan Society, with courses offered through Northwest Community College.
