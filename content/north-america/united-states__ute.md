## Overview

The Utes traditionally lived as nomadic hunting and gathering bands. Their culture includes hunting, trade, beadwork, basketry, pottery, music, dance, oral tradition, and sacred places. Today, three federally recognized Ute tribes are identified. Ute is spoken on three reservations and has tribal revitalization programs.

## Material culture

Ute material culture centers on decorated hide clothing, beadwork, baskets, pottery, drums, rattles, quillwork, and trade objects.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Ute textile traditions feature decorated hide clothing, beadwork, fringe, quillwork, and embroidery.

- **Seed beadwork** — Seed beads decorated hide shirts, cuffs, necks, shoulders, chokers, sashes, leggings, moccasins, and other garments.
- **Hide garments** — Shirts, smocks, leggings, dresses, ponchos, and tunics were made from hide and often had fringe, beads, or other decoration.
- **Quillwork sashes** — Men wore quillwork sashes with beaded chokers, bead ornaments, belts, or peace medals.
- **Embroidered clothing** — A portrait shows an embroidered shirt, sash, leggings, and moccasins worn by the Ute chief Suriap.
- **Fringed clothing** (*fringe*) — Fringe appeared on hide shirts, dresses, leggings, tunics, smocks, sashes, and a painted skin quiver.

### Clothing & dress

Ute dress combined decorated hide clothing with beads, shells, metal ornaments, blankets, and some Western clothes.

- **Men's dress** — Men wore hide shirts, leggings, moccasins, smocks, sashes, belts, hats, feathers, and sometimes Western jackets and trousers.
- **Women's dress** — Women wore ponchos, fringed skin dresses, dresses with triangular motifs, cloth dresses, moccasins, and stripe garments.
- **Tipi portrait dress** — Ta-vah-puts was shown wearing a cloth dress and blanket while seated in front of a tipi.
- **Braided hair** — Men and women were shown with long hair worn in braids or bunches.

### Architecture

The sources describe tipis, winter camps, river and mountain settlements, and a wickiup in a Bear Dance story.

- **Tipi** — Eastern Utes lived in tepees after the 17th century, and a photograph shows a woman seated in front of one.
- **Winter camps** — Winter camps were established along rivers and in mountain valleys.
- **Wickiup** — A Bear Dance origin story describes a bear living in a wickiup.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Ute everyday objects included baskets, water containers, quivers, pots, ornaments, guns, and tools for processing food and hides.

- **Coiled basket** — Museum records document a coiled basket.
- **Pitch-covered water bottle** (*water-bottle*) — A possible water bottle was made of plant material, partly covered in pitch, and fitted with cloth, skin, and possible horse-hair handles.
- **Painted quiver** (*quiver*) — A painted fringed quiver was made of skin and beads.
- **Pots** — A photograph shows two pots in front of a tipi.
- **Grinding stones** — Grinding stones at Garden of the Gods suggest gatherings for tanning hides and processing meat.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ute adornment included bead, shell, metal, dentalium, quillwork, feathers, belts, and decorated hair.

- **Beaded chokers** — Beaded chokers were part of documented Ute dress.
- **Haliotis shell pendants** — Haliotis shell pendants hung from the shoulder fringe of a decorated hide shirt.
- **Shell gorget** — Shell gorgets were worn as neck ornaments.
- **Dentalium ear ornaments** — Dentalium ear ornaments were part of documented adornment.
- **Deer-hoof tinklers** — Deer-hoof tinklers hung among the shoulder fringes of a decorated hide shirt.

## Music & performance

Ute music uses emotional songs, chants, drums, rattles, and a wooden flute-like instrument.

- **Morache** — The morache is a notched-stick rattle with a resonator, played by rubbing a short stick or bone across a longer notched stick.
- **Hand drum** — The hand drum is about 12 inches wide and has a skin head, cloth handle, and drumstick.
- **Large drum** — The large drum is used for dances, with eight to ten singers seated around it and sometimes as many as fourteen.
- **Flageolet** — The flageolet is a wooden wind instrument about 11 inches long with six sound holes.
- **Song types** (*war songs*) — Named Ute song types include war, social dance, parade, medicine, love, game, and story songs.

## Dance & theatre

The Bear Dance is the central named Ute dance, while the Lame Dance represents carrying war loot.

- **Bear Dance** (*Mamaqui Mawats*) — Mamaqui Mawats means “to move forward and back” and is danced by men and women facing different directions.
- **Bear Dance sounds** (*morache*) — The Bear Dance uses singing, drums, the morache, and downward glissandos that imitate a bear.
- **Lame Dance** — Women performed the Lame Dance after war parties to symbolize pulling or carrying heavy loads of loot.

## Festivals & rituals

Ute ceremonies brought people together for dance, trade, seasonal gatherings, warfare preparation, and sacred relationships with bears.

- **Springtime Bear Dance festival** (*Tam-Nam Nacup*) — The Bear Dance could be performed at festivals lasting several days or up to a week, including the Tam-Nam Nacup Springtime Festival.
- **Trade fairs** (*rescates*) — Annual rescates with Rio Grande Pueblo people were ceremonial and social events that could last ten days or more.
- **Bear Dance ritual** (*Bear Dance*) — The spring Bear Dance links people with bear ancestors and is not danced in winter according to Snake John's story.
- **War preparation** — Men preparing for warfare fasted, joined sweat lodge ceremonies, and painted their faces and horses for symbolic meanings.
- **Sun dance** — The sources mention annual performances of the Bear and Sun dances.

## Foodways

Ute foodways centered on hunting, gathering, meat processing, hide tanning, and trade.

- **Game hunting** — Utes hunted buffalo, deer, elk, and other game across a wide area.
- **Meat processing** — Meat was processed after hunts, with grinding stones suggesting places where people gathered for this work.
- **Hide tanning** — The sources describe tanning hides at gatherings such as those suggested by the grinding stones at Garden of the Gods.
- **Trade goods** — Utes traded meat, buffalo robes, soft tanned deerskins, maize, flour, and other goods.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ute oral tradition includes creation stories, Bear Dance stories, animal figures, folk tales, and songs learned by rote.

- **Creation narrative** — A Ute creation narrative says the people have always occupied their mountainous ancestral region.
- **Bear Dance origin story** — A story tells of a bear emerging from winter shelter and moving forward and backward while a human hunter watched.
- **Wolf** — Wolf is described as the creator and culture hero.
- **Coyote** — Coyote is Wolf's younger brother and a trickster.
- **Folk tales** (*Pokoh*) — Listed folk tales include Pokoh the Old Man, Blood Clot, Porcupine Hunts Buffalo, Hunt deer, Hunt elk, Hunt grasshoppers, Puma and the Bear, Two Grandsons, and Coyote & Duck.

## Language & religion

Ute is a threatened language, and Ute beliefs connect people with nature, bears, ancestors, and sacred places.

- **Ute language** (*núu-'apaghapi̱*) — Ute speakers call their language núu-'apaghapi̱, meaning “the people's speech.”
- **Of the people** (*núuchi*) — Núuchi means “of the people” and is another name for the Ute language.
- **Colorado River Numic** — Ute is a Colorado River Numic dialect in the Southern subdivision of the Numic branch of the Uto-Aztecan family.
- **Sacred Pikes Peak** (*Pikes Peak*) — Pikes Peak was a sacred ceremonial area.
- **Sacred Manitou Springs** (*Manitou Springs*) — The mineral springs at Manitou Springs were sacred.
