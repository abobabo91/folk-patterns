## Overview

The Western Apache are an Indigenous people of North America and a subgroup of Apache peoples. They live primarily in east central Arizona, in the United States, and north of Mexico in Sonora and Chihuahua. Most live within reservations in Arizona, including the Fort Apache Indian Reservation, San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation, Yavapai-Apache Nation, Tonto Apache Reservation, and Fort McDowell Yavapai Nation. The Western Apache bands call themselves *Ndee* or *Indé*, meaning “The People.” Their language is a Southern Athabaskan language within the Apachean branch, and approximately 14,000 Western Apache speak it. Western Apache cultural life documented in the sources includes place-centered narratives, language preservation, basket weaving, bow and arrow making, buckskin regalia, silver jewelry, and mixed Apache-Yavapai social histories.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Basket weaving** — Tribal schools offer classes in basket weaving.

**Buckskin regalia** — Girls and young women are taught to make native regalia from buckskin.

**Silas John prayer-texts** — A Silas John prayer-text is made from graphic symbols written on buckskin or paper. The symbols are arranged in horizontal lines read from left to right in descending order; each symbol corresponds to a line of prayer that may consist of a word, phrase, or one or more sentences.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources document native regalia made from buckskin and identify silver jewelry as an element of Western Apache material culture. Girls and young women at elementary and secondary level are taught to make buckskin regalia, while young men often become jewelry makers and are taught skills in this area.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do record silver jewelry, which is taught and made within the community. They also mention bows, arrows, spears, shields, and cradles for infants as native handicrafts taught in tribal schools. The Western Apache writing system of Silas John used symbols written on buckskin or paper, and the symbols could represent verbal speech as well as nonverbal actions.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver jewelry is a documented Western Apache craft. Tribal schools teach girls and young women to make native regalia from buckskin as well as silver jewelry, and young men often become jewelry makers.

## Music & performance

 They do record a writing system created by Silas John in 1904 to record 62 prayers that he believed came to him from heaven. The prayer-texts were written on buckskin or paper using graphic symbols arranged in horizontal lines.

The sources also mention dances in connection with the Cibecue Apache medicine man Nakąįdotł’ini, who held dances and claimed to bring two dead chiefs, Diablo and Es-ki-ol-e, to life. The account does not name the dances, describe their movements, identify accompanying music, or explain their broader ceremonial form.

## Dance & theatre

 They state that Nakąįdotł’ini, a respected medicine man and chief of the Cañon Creek band, held dances and claimed to bring two dead chiefs to life. The account concerns the events that led to the Battle of Cibecue Creek on Aug. 30, 1881.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do document ritual and religious activity associated with prayers, dances, medicine men, and Apache Scouts. Silas John created a system of symbols in 1904 to record 62 prayers that he believed came to him from heaven. A written Silas John prayer-text could include a word, phrase, or one or more sentences, and its symbols could represent nonverbal actions as well as verbal speech. Nakąįdotł’ini held dances and claimed to bring two dead chiefs, Diablo and Es-ki-ol-e, to life. The resulting confrontation with the Army led to the Battle of Cibecue Creek on Aug. 30, 1881.

## Foodways

 They do state that several Western Apache groups lived near waterways used for crops, including the East Fork and North Fork of the White River, Willow Creek, Black River, and the Gila River. The Fossil Creek band had a few tiny farms on Fossil Creek, Clear Creek, and a site on the Verde River below the mouth of Deer Creek. The Mormon Lake band depended entirely on hunting and gathering wild plant foods for sustenance. The Bald Mountain band lived entirely by hunting and gathering plant foods.

## Oral tradition & literature

Western Apache narratives are spatially anchored to points upon the land, with precise depictions of particular locations. The Western Apache practice of “speaking with names” uses place descriptions to produce a mental image, evoke historical tales and sagas, affirm traditional moral precepts, convey concern and support, offer practical advice, transform distressing thoughts, and heal wounded spirits. The practice depends on shared knowledge of traditional narratives.

Place names may be descriptive, commemorative, or used to identify clans. Social groups use them to communicate what happened to them, relate a story connected with a location, or issue a warning. This use of place names is known as “shooting with stories.” The sources state that younger generations of Western Apache are increasingly ignorant of place names and traditional narratives, making “speaking with names” more difficult.

The sources also list *Ndee biyáti’ bígoch’il’aah* (“Learning Apache”), *Nnee biyati’ yánlti’go* (“Speak Apache”), *Nnee baa nadaagoni’* (“Apache stories”), and *Nnee Díí Kehgo Daagolii’ ni’* (“Apaches used to live this way”) among works connected with Western Apache language teaching and storytelling.

## Language & religion

Western Apache is a Southern Athabaskan language in the Apachean branch of the Southern Athabaskan language family. It is spoken in east-central Arizona and in Sonora and Chihuahua. The sources state that approximately 14,000 Western Apache speak the language, including approximately 6,000 speakers living on the San Carlos Reservation and 7,000 living on the Fort Apache Reservation. In 2011, the San Carlos Apache Tribe’s Language Preservation Program in Peridot, Arizona, began outreach to tribal members residing within the districts of Bylas, Gilson Wash, Peridot, and Seven Mile Wash; 20% still spoke the language fluently.

Researchers disagree about whether Western Apache has five dialect groupings—Cibecue, Northern Tonto, Southern Tonto, San Carlos, and White Mountain—or three main varieties: San Carlos, White Mountain, and Tonto. Western Apache uses a modified version of the Latin alphabet. Its only writing system native to Western Apache is the system of symbols created by Silas John in 1904. They document prayers, medicine men, dances, and traditional narratives, including Silas John’s belief that his 62 prayers came to him from heaven.
