## Overview

The Denaʼina are an Alaska Native Athabaskan people and the original inhabitants of south-central Alaska, extending from Seldovia in the south to Chickaloon in the northeast, Talkeetna in the north, Lime Village in the northwest, and Pedro Bay in the southwest. Their homeland, *Denaʼina Ełnena*, covers more than 41,000 mi² (110,000 km²). They arrived in south-central Alaska sometime between 1,000 and 1,500 years ago and are the only Alaskan Athabaskan group to live on the coast. Their culture is described as hunter-gatherer, with a matrilineal system and a comparatively sedentary lifestyle associated with saltwater access. Denaʼina society was organized into regional bands, villages, clans, and moieties.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Denaʼina villages contained multi-family dwellings called *Nichił*. Each village belonged to a regional band or *Ht’ana*, and local bands were organized into villages or *qayeh*. A *Nichił* was led by a *qeshqa*, described as a “rich man” or leader.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe Denaʼina social organization through matrilineal clans and moieties. Men and women belonged to their mother’s clan, and villagers could marry only outside their own clan and moiety. The language article mentions a yearly Denaʼina festival followed by a three-week intensive course led by elders, but gives no name, date, or description of the festival’s ceremonial content.

## Foodways

They describe the Denaʼina as having a hunter-gatherer culture and note that residence near saltwater supported the most sedentary lifestyle among Northern Athabascans.

## Oral tradition & literature

Denaʼina oral and literary materials include the collected writings of Peter Kalifornsky and published collections associated with Denaʼina-speaking communities. The bibliography names *Kʼtlʼeghʼi Sukdu, A Denaʼina Legacy: The Collected Writings of Peter Kalifornsky*, *Nuvendaltin Quhtʼana: The People of Nondalton*, *Nuvendaltun Htʼana Sukduʼa: Nondalton People’s Stories*, and *Denaʼina Sukduʼa*. The sources also list stories from Tyonek and Iliamna Lake, and a work titled *I’ll Tell You a Story: Stories I Recall From Growing Up On Iliamna Lake*.

## Language & religion

Denaʼina is an Alaska Athabascan language of the region surrounding Cook Inlet and is geographically distinctive because it includes territory bordering salt water. Four dialects are distinguished: Upper Inlet, Outer Inlet, Iliamna, and Inland. The language is spoken in places including Eklutna, Knik, Susitna, Tyonek, Kenai, Kustatan, Seldovia, Pedro Bay, Old Iliamna, the Lake Iliamna area, Nondalton, and Lime Village. The sources give different population and speaker estimates: one article describes about 70–75 fluent speakers out of a total population of about 1,400, while the language article gives about 75–95 speakers and describes Denaʼina as moribund. Denaʼina uses a variant of the Latin alphabet.
