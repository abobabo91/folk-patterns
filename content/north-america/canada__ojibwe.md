## Overview

The Ojibwe are Canada's second-largest First Nations population, spread across the Great Lakes and northern regions. They speak Anishinaabemowin, an Algonquian language that served as a trade language for centuries. Their culture centers on harvests, storytelling, and relationships with spirit beings. They are known for distinctive crafts, traditional foods, and the Midewiwin society that preserves knowledge through birchbark scrolls.

## Material culture

Ojibwe material culture emphasizes patterned surfaces through beadwork and birchbark work, with practical objects made from bark, wood, and stone.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Glass beadwork is the dominant craft, sewn onto cloth and skin in geometric and animal designs.

- **Glass beadwork** — Woven and sewn seed beads on cloth, velvet and tanned skin, traditionally women's work.
- **Bandolier bags** — Shoulder bags of cotton and wool with velvet, beads and tassels, the flashiest formal attire in the 1800s.
- **Birchbark boxes and dishes** — Cylindrical boxes and oval dishes sewn to bent hoops with decorative cut bark strips for contrast.
- **Beaded bands** (*band*) — Long narrow strips of woven glass beads, sometimes featuring animal figures like otter or turtle.
- **Medicine bags** — Red velvet bags elaborately beaded with long shoulder slings and wool tassels.

### Clothing & dress

Ojibwe dress mixed traditional moccasins and beaded garments with imported cloth and blankets.

- **Moccasins** — Skin and cloth footwear with beads, waterproofed by fire-curing the seams.
- **Jingle dress** — Dark cloth dress hung with rows of tin cones cut from tobacco-can lids, originating with Ojibwe women and spreading to all tribes.
- **Beaded shirts and belts** — Cloth garments decorated with glass beads worn for formal occasions.

### Architecture

The characteristic dwelling is the wigwam, framed on saplings and covered with birch and juniper bark.

- **Wigwam** (*wiigiwaam*) — Domed or pointed lodge covered in birch bark and juniper, still built for particular sites and events.
- **Domed wigwam** (*waginogaan*) — Rounded lodge structure framed on willow saplings.
- **Pointed wigwam** (*nasawa'ogaan*) — Cone-shaped lodge structure framed on willow saplings.
- **Spirit house** (*jiibegamig*) — Small structure erected over burials to house the spirit of the deceased.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Everyday objects are made from bark, wood and stone, with no pottery recorded in the collections.

- **Birchbark canoe** — Bark sewn to wooden ribs, used for traveling waterways and still made as miniatures.
- **Cylindrical bark box** (*ask*) — Storage container made from bent birchbark.
- **Wooden club** — Squared-off handle with a circle head carved with a flower and metal ring.
- **Lacrosse stick** — Wood and buckskin implement used in the traditional game.
- **Carved pipe** — Hard wood or black stone inlaid with lead or white metal, used for smoking.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment is made of beads and shell, with chokers serving as status symbols.

- **Beaded necklace** — Plaited glass beads strung together, worn with multiple necklaces in formal dress.
- **Beaded choker** — Red and white beaded band with concave disc center and shell-tipped cords, a status symbol.
- **Cowrie shell** (*miigis*) — Rare shell from distant coasts used in ceremonies and traded across the continent as evidence of wide networks.
- **Guardian spirit tattoo** (*manitou*) — Image of a person's spiritual guardian marked on the body.

## Music & performance

Musical instruments include flutes and drums; songs are recorded in birchbark scrolls using pictographic notation.

- **Wooden flute** — Two-piece red wooden flute about two feet long, made at Serpent River on Lake Huron.
- **Painted drum** — Military-style wooden drum repainted with Thunderers, sun and cattle representing the underworld.
- **Summer spiritual gathering** (*jiingotamog*) — Spiritual gathering held at reservations.
- **Powwow** (*niimi'idimaa*) — Social gathering held at reservations across Anishinaabe Country for singing, dancing and celebration.

## Dance & theatre

The main documented dance tradition is jingle-dress dancing performed at powwows.

- **Jingle-dress dancing** — Women's dance performed at powwows wearing the distinctive tin-cone dress.
- **Wild rice jigging** — Domestic practice of dancing or walking on parched grain to loosen its hulls.

## Festivals & rituals

The year revolves around harvests, with vision quests formerly central to male life and healing societies still active.

- **Wild rice harvest** (*manoomin*) — Late summer and early autumn gathering by canoe, with tobacco and prayers offered to the Great Spirit's Garden.
- **Maple sugar harvest** — Spring activity, taught in schools with elders narrating in Ojibwemowin while students gather wood and haul sap.
- **Vision quest** — Puberty rite undertaken by males to establish a lifelong relationship with a guardian spirit, now declined.
- **Shaking-tent rite** (*Jiisakiiwin*) — Healing ceremony performed by a ritual specialist calling on spirits for healing.
- **Midewiwin** — Ancient healing society that keeps birchbark scrolls and pictorial scripts recording sacred knowledge.

## Foodways

Wild rice is the defining food, with fish, maple sugar, venison and corn forming the staple diet.

- **Wild rice** (*manoomin*) — Nutty-flavored grain eaten for two thousand years, knocked from beds called the Great Spirit's Garden and parched, jigged and winnowed.
- **Maple sugar** — Spring harvest syrup granulated by boiling and working in a trough with wooden paddles.
- **Fish and venison** — Staple proteins hunted and caught throughout the year.
- **Foraged berries** — Blueberries, blackberries, choke cherries, raspberries, gooseberries and huckleberries gathered in summer.

## Oral tradition & literature

Storytelling is central to Ojibwe life, with sacred and ordinary stories kept separate and recorded in birchbark pictographs.

- **Nanabush** — Culture hero and trickster who taught people to hunt and fish, found wild rice and invented snowshoes.
- **Seven Grandfather Teachings** — Seven teachings of wisdom, respect, love, honesty, humility, bravery and truth, each paired with an animal.
- **Birchbark writing** (*wiigwaasabak*) — Pictographs serving as mnemonics for stories and knowledge, still used by traditional people and young people on social media.
- **Sacred stories** (*aadsookaan*) — Stories about spirit beings told only in winter when water was frozen to prevent drawing out the underwater serpent.

## Language & religion

Anishinaabemowin is an Algonquian language with seven dialects; religion centers on the manitouk, often practiced alongside Christianity.

- **Anishinaabemowin** — Algonquian language, the second most spoken First Nations language in Canada and fourth in North America.
- **Manitouk** — Spirit beings in Ojibwe religion, including thunderbirds, underwater serpents and animal owners.
- **Creator spirit** (*Kitche Manitou*) — Supreme creator spirit, often identified with the Christian God by Ojibwe practicing both religions.
- **Underwater serpent** (*Mishebeshu*) — Powerful spirit being from which copper is believed to come, whose presence must not be invoked in warm months.
- **Windigo** — Spirit being in Ojibwe cosmology representing a dangerous cannibal entity.
