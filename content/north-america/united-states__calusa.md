## Overview

The Calusa were a Native American people of Florida’s southwest coast whose historic society belonged to the Caloosahatchee culture. Their territory extended at least from Charlotte Harbor to Marco Island, with influence reaching other southern Florida peoples, including the Mayaimi, Tequesta, and Jaega. Their principal city of Calos was probably at Mound Key, near present-day Estero, Florida, and Mound Key was also the capital from which their rulers administered the kingdom. Calusa society developed around estuarine fisheries rather than agriculture and had high population densities. Estimates of the total population at European contact range from 10,000 to several times that number, although the figures are described as speculative. Their language is unclassified, and little is known of it.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no Calusa textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The Calusa wore minimal clothing. Men wore deerskin breechcloths, while the Spanish left less information about women’s clothing. The sources state that most Indigenous women of Florida at the time wore skirts made from Spanish moss. Calusa men wore their hair long. During Pedro Menéndez de Avilés’s visit in 1566, the chief’s wife was described as wearing pearls, precious stones, and gold beads around her neck. The chief’s heir wore gold in an ornament on his forehead and beads on his legs. These ornaments are recorded in connection with elite status rather than ordinary dress.

### Architecture

The Calusa lived in large communal houses that were two stories high. The chief’s house at the capital was large enough to hold 2,000 people without crowding and also served as a council house. It had two large windows, suggesting that it had walls, and the chief’s house, as well as possibly other houses at Calos, was built on an earthen mound. In 1697, Spanish observers reported that the roof allowed rain, sun, and dew to enter. They also recorded 16 houses in the Calusa capital of Calos, which had 1,000 residents. The chief’s house contained raised seats for the chief and his sister-wife, with principal men and women seated around them.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Calusa pottery traditions belonged to the wider archaeological sequence of the region. Undecorated pottery associated with the early Glades culture appeared around 500 BC, while pottery distinct from the Glades tradition developed around AD 500, marking the beginning of the Caloosahatchee culture. Between 500 and 1000, undecorated, sand-tempered pottery was replaced by Belle Glade Plain pottery, made with clay containing spicules from freshwater sponges. Calusa tools included bone and shell gauges for net weaving, mollusk-shell sinkers and net weights, fishhooks, spears, throat gorges, fish traps, weirs, and fish corrals. Mollusk shells and wood were used for hammering and pounding, while shell and shark teeth served for grating, cutting, carving, and engraving. Wooden objects included bowls, masks, plaques, ear ornaments, ornamental standards, and a carved deer head.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The Calusa painted their bodies regularly but did not tattoo themselves. Men wore their hair long, and missionaries considered cutting a Calusa man’s hair upon conversion to Christianity and European style a significant sacrifice. Little was recorded about jewelry among the population as a whole. The chief’s wife wore pearls, precious stones, and gold beads around her neck during Menéndez de Avilés’s visit in 1566. The chief’s heir wore gold in an ornament on his forehead and beads on his legs. Ceremonial or artistic masks have also been discovered. Some had moving parts operated by pull strings and hinges, allowing the wearer to alter the mask’s appearance.

## Music & performance

They do record ceremonies involving processions of priests and singing women. Priests wore carved masks, which at other times were hung on the walls inside a temple. Hernando de Escalante Fontaneda described “sorcerers in the shape of the devil, with some horns on their heads,” who ran through the town yelling like animals for four months at a time.

## Dance & theatre

They document processions of priests, singing women, carved masks, and masks with moving parts operated by pull strings and hinges. These objects allowed a wearer to alter the appearance of a mask while using it. Fontaneda also described figures wearing horned forms who ran through the town yelling like animals.

## Festivals & rituals

The Calusa believed that three supernatural beings ruled the world and that each person had three souls. One ruled the physical world, another ruled human governments, and the third helped in wars by choosing which side would win. The three souls were identified with the pupil of the eye, the shadow, and the reflection. The soul in the pupil remained with the body after death, while the other two entered animals.

The Calusa buried their dead in mounds. A body was placed in a charnel house so that the flesh could fall away naturally, or a medicine man scraped the flesh from the bones. The bones were then gathered into a basket and buried in a mound. Burial mounds were also used for religious ceremonies, where the Calusa gathered on “Holy Days” to sacrifice aromatic plants and honey. They do record that the chief was expected to take his sister as one of his wives, although contemporary archeologists suggested this may have reflected a requirement to marry a “clan-sister.”

## Foodways

Calusa settlements along the coast and estuaries depended primarily on fish. Recorded fish included pinfish, pigfish, hardhead catfish, larger bony fish, sharks, and rays. The diet also included mollusks, crustaceans, ducks, sea turtles, land turtles, and land animals. When Pedro Menéndez de Avilés visited in 1566, the Calusa served the Spanish only fish and oysters. At the Wightman site on Sanibel Island, more than 93% of the energy from animal foods came from fish and shellfish, less than 6% from mammals, and less than 1% from birds and reptiles. At Platt Island, an inland site, mammals, primarily deer, accounted for more than 60% of the energy from animal meat, while fish provided just under 20%.

The Calusa gathered wild berries, fruits, nuts, roots, and other plant parts. Archaeological evidence indicates consumption of cabbage palm, prickly pear, hog plum, acorns, wild papaya, and chili peppers. They cultivated a gourd of the species *Cucurbita pepo* and the bottle gourd, using them as net floats and dippers. The earliest known aquaculture in Florida was practiced by the Calusa kingdom.

## Oral tradition & literature

The principal early written accounts include Hernando de Escalante Fontaneda’s memoir and descriptions by Spanish missionaries and observers. Fontaneda was shipwrecked on the east coast of Florida, likely in the Florida Keys, about 1550, when he was thirteen years old, and lived with various tribes in southern Florida for 17 years before being found by the Menéndez de Avilés expedition. The Calusa language article notes that only a dozen translated words and 50 or 60 place names form the entire known corpus of the language.

## Language & religion

The Calusa language is an unclassified language of southern Florida, United States, spoken by the Calusa people. Circumstantial evidence suggests that peoples of southern Florida and the Tampa Bay area, including the Tequesta, Mayaimi, Tocobaga, and Calusa, spoke dialects of a common language. The language was probably related to Mayaca, which is described as closely related to Ais, while Julian Granberry suggested a relationship with Tunica of the lower Mississippi River Valley. The recorded corpus is very small. Examples listed in the sources include *tepe* “join,” *kuči* “destroy,” *ñoka* “war,” *ño* “village,” *śahka* “tree,” and *mayai* “on the other side.” *Sipi* was the name of a main idol in a Calusa temple according to a 1743 report.

Calusa religion included three supernatural rulers, three souls, temple ceremonies, priests, masks, and burial mounds. The Calusa resisted Spanish attempts to convert them to Catholicism for almost 200 years.
