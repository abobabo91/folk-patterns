## Overview

The Eyak are an Alaska Native people historically located on the Copper River Delta and near Cordova, Alaska, within the Indigenous peoples of the Northwest Coast. Historical territory extended from present-day Cordova east to the Martin River and north to Miles Glacier, with villages at Alaganik, Eyak, an unnamed settlement south of Eyak, and Orca. Eyak people today live in Cordova, Yakutat, across Alaska, and the United States. In 1900, the total Eyak population was estimated at 60; as of 1996, 120 partial Eyak descendants lived in Cordova. The Eyak language is related to the Athabaskan languages and belongs to the Eyak–Athabaskan group within the Na-Dené language family. Eyak culture is documented especially through its language, oral narratives, territorial history, and ongoing language-revival work.

## Music & performance

The Eyak corpus is described as consisting mainly of narrative material, with very little spontaneous conversation and with some conversation embedded in narratives. The sources state that Eyak stories were used in linguistic analysis, including traditional Eyak stories examined through morphemic analyses.

## Festivals & rituals

They do mention an annual Eyak Culture Camp held every August in Cordova as part of contemporary language-preservation work.

## Foodways

They state that the Eyak harvested salmon fishing grounds and seasonally occupied fish camps at Point Whitshed and Mountain Slough, but they provide no further foodways description.

## Oral tradition & literature

Eyak oral tradition is represented in the sources through narrative performance and traditional Eyak stories. The available description emphasizes that most of the Eyak corpus is narrative, while spontaneous conversation is rare and generally appears within narratives. Eyak syntax is therefore better documented in narrative style and performance than in natural speech. The sources also state that oral tradition preserved many Eyak etymologies in Tlingit place names along the Gulf Coast and that both Tlingit and Eyak oral histories describe migration throughout the region.

Contemporary preservation work includes the Eyak Language Project, which provides language resources, immersion workshops, an online dictionary with audio samples, and eLearning lessons. In June 2014, the Eyak Language Revitalization Project announced an online program called *dAXunhyuuga'*, meaning “the words of the people.” The Eyak Preservation Council also received an Alaska Humanities Forum Grant that enabled the creation of a website devoted to preservation of the Eyak language.

## Language & religion

Eyak is an extinct and revived Na-Dené language historically spoken near the mouth of the Copper River in south-central Alaska. Its closest relatives are the Athabaskan languages, and the Eyak–Athabaskan group forms a basic division of the Na-Dené language family alongside Tlingit. The last native speaker was Marie Smith Jones, who died on January 21, 2008; Guillaume Leduey learned Eyak to fluency and became a speaker, translator, and instructor. Eyak remains classified as “dormant” because there are no native speakers. The language is agglutinative and polysynthetic, has subject-object-verb as its basic word order, and is not tonal.
