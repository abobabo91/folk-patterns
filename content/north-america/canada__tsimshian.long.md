## Overview

The Tsimshian (*Tsʼmsyen*, "Inside the Skeena River") are an Indigenous people of the Pacific Northwest Coast, living mainly in coastal British Columbia around Terrace and Prince Rupert on the lower Skeena River, and at Metlakatla, Alaska, on Annette Island — the only reservation in that state. The Tsimshian estimate 45,000 Tsimshian people, with roughly 10,000 federally registered members in eight First Nations communities; about 1,450 Alaska Tsimshian are enrolled in the Metlakatla Indian Community. They speak *Sm'algyax*, "real or true tongue," of the Tsimshianic family. Society is matrilineal: identity, clans and property pass through the mother's line, and a stratified ranking system is reflected in some 27 distinct Tsimshian terms for "chief." Archaeology shows 5,000 years of continuous habitation around Prince Rupert Harbour. In folk-culture terms the Tsimshian are credited with inventing Chilkat weaving, and their carvers, weavers and feast-hosts sustain object traditions held in museums worldwide.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Chilkat weaving** — A weaving tradition the Tsimshian are credited with inventing, worked with western red cedar alongside other materials; cedar bark was the coast's universal fibre, made into clothing as well as tools, roofing, armour, building materials and canoe skins.

**Cedar-bark twining** — Twined cedar-bark work produced wide-brimmed hats, including one with a single weft of black-dyed bark run near the edge, and cylindrical lidded baskets. A surviving wooden weaving frame with its woven strap still attached documents the loom technology itself.

**Basketry with false embroidery** — A twined cylindrical cedar-bark basket made to sheathe a glass jar carries designs in "false embroidery" worked in grass and maidenhair fern on both body and lid — overlay patterning in contrasting plant fibres rather than dyed wool. Other baskets combine grass, straw and red cedar bark.

**Button blankets** — The dance cape known as a button blanket is cut from wool trade cloth and outlined in shell buttons; a mid-19th-century Tsimshian example dates to 1850–60. A 1903 photograph of chiefly regalia was attributed to Coast Tsimshian partly on the strength of the symbols worked on the blankets.

**Motif vocabulary.** Formline design (black and red formline painting on wood; dorsal fins and facial features carved in formline), salmon heads, killer whale and whale blowhole, raven, frog, wolf, eagle, Thunderbird, sea lion and seal, sturgeon-like fish, beaver, bird heads, and crest clan emblems — *Gispwudwada* (Killer Whale or Fireweed), *Ganhada* (Raven or Frog), *Laxgibuu* (Wolf), *Laxsgiik* (Eagle).

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress drew on western red cedar bark, which was fashioned into clothing as well as armour; twined cedar-bark hats with wide brims shed the coastal rain. Ceremonial dress is sharply distinguished and is what the museum record preserves best. Chiefs wore a *frontlet* — a carved forehead ornament mounted on a headband — of wood set with abalone shell and pigment, one dated 1820–40. Over the shoulders went the button blanket dance cape of wool trade cloth and shell buttons. A shaman's headdress survives made of goat horns with leather and wire; another, described as made for a dog feast, combines vegetable fibre, wood and horn. Ritual officiants also wore head-rings, including a fire-eater's crown of vegetable fibre. Masks include one carved in the shape of a bird's head. A 1903 photograph of a ruling chief and family in regalia shows a frontlet, a mask worn on top of the head, patterned blankets, and three children wearing wolf skins. Sources name no distinct footwear or belt traditions.

### Architecture

Because salmon gave a dependable food base, the Tsimshian built permanent towns rather than moving camps, and lived in large longhouses framed and clad with cedar house posts and panels chosen to withstand a wet climate; a single house was very large and usually sheltered an entire extended family. The settlement year was double: winter villages stood in and around the islands of Prince Rupert Harbour and Venn Pass (Metlakatla), and families returned to summer villages along the lower Skeena when the salmon ran. House form left its mark on tribal names — *Gitasts'uu* (Kitasoo) means "(People of) a large, tiered house-depression." Monumental carving accompanied the houses: a 19th-century Tsimshian totem pole is held by the Smithsonian, and a historic photograph records the original setting of the Kapshkoltsh pole. When the Hudson's Bay Company moved its fort to present-day Lax Kwʼalaams in 1834, nine Tsimshian tribal chiefs relocated their houses to the surrounding area for trading advantage.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources describe no pottery; stone and cedar carry the household repertoire. Stone mauls and clubs are the best-documented form: heads of granite, light grey igneous rock and whitish quartzite, grooved for hafting and crowned with crests — a salmon head, an animal face with eyes, lips and teeth, a carved head read as a whale's blowhole. Hafts of hard wood were lashed on with sinew, leather thongs, or windings of spruce or cedar root; one example from Fort Simpson, in Coast Tsimshian territory, is labelled simply "Stone hammer." Two types of stone hammer were in use along the coast historically, and some are still used today. Cedar vessels include an oblong feast trough rimmed with inlaid shell opercula, darkened almost black by the eulachon oil it served. Bentwood and plank boxes were carved and painted, including burial-box walls and a box bearing a sea-lion design. Halibut were taken with a two-part V-shaped hook whose upper arm was carved as a spiritual helper — otters, seals, halibut, wolves and sea monsters being the usual subjects.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Engraved silver bracelets are the signature ornament in the collection, and they are signed work by named makers: one bearing a Thunderbird is inscribed "Thunderbird Bill Hellin 83," another with a frog "D M Dennis. 83 Frog." Inlay with haliotis (abalone) shell runs through high-status objects — the 1820–40 frontlet, an elkhorn club surmounted by a bird's head, and a carved wood-and-horn ornament set with abalone and copper that was probably once the upper element of a headdress or mask. Shell opercula were inlaid along the rims of feast bowls.

## Music & performance

The wooden trumpet is the instrument most specifically tied to the Tsimshian: a large carved *trätrumpet* in the Stockholm collection is documented as used above all by the Tsimshian in "Nakmnok" performances and at the initiation of members into the secret societies, and similar trumpets were used by the northern and southern Kwakiutl groups and the Haida. These trumpets were often sounded in secret by concealed performers, to mark an arrival. Rattles are the other major class: a 19th-century Raven rattle of cedar with pebbles inside and polychrome painting, and a mid-19th-century face rattle of wood, fibre and leather. Percussion and sound-making also include a whale-shaped wooden clapper with carved lips, nose and eyes, painted in black and red formline, and a two-piece tapering hollowed wooden instrument catalogued ambiguously as a wind instrument or clapper, with a mouthpiece at the narrow end and vegetal-fibre bindings. A 1903 photograph of chiefly regalia includes a box drum whose design contributed to the Coast Tsimshian attribution. Music belongs to the feast: the Tsimshian feast system, or potlatch — called *yaawk* for one specific event — is the setting in which law is declared, knowledge transmitted, and chiefly stewardship demonstrated.

## Dance & theatre

The sources document the regalia and the occasions rather than named dance repertoires. Dance capes (button blankets), frontlets worn on the forehead, masks on the crown of the head, bird's-head masks and a fire-eater's crown all point to masked performance within the feast and within the secret societies, whose initiations the hidden wooden trumpets announced. David A. Boxley, *Laxsgiik*, was the first to host a potlatch and raise a totem pole in modern times in Metlakatla, Alaska.

## Festivals & rituals

The ceremonial year centres on the feast system rather than a calendar of seasonal festivals; the Tsimshian recognise four different types of feast. Today the potlatch is held to honour deaths and burials and to mark succession to name-titles, and it functions as the agency of social reproduction, the expression of law, the transmission of knowledge, and the demonstration of a chief's obligation to steward resources and attend to community needs. Planning and delivery follow very specific protocols that bind guests as well as hosts: it is untoward to hold out one's hand while payments — what outside observers call gifts — are being distributed. Life-cycle rites are equally formal. Marriage was an extremely formal affair of several prolonged and sequential ceremonies, and arranged marriages and births were common as a way of protecting rights of access to territories and resources. Taboos prohibited women and men from eating improper foods during and after childbirth; several such taboos are still actively practised. Hereditary chieftainship passes through the maternal line via the mother's brother, but the inheritor must be trained in proper behaviour and groomed for specific obligations, since no lineage should be sullied by the misconduct of a high-ranking member. Burial boxes, carved and painted, belong to this ritual complex.

## Foodways

Salmon is the centre of Tsimshian nutrition, as it has been since the abundance of sea life allowed permanent towns to be built; it remains central despite large-scale commercial fishing in the area, and families historically moved to summer villages on the lower Skeena when the salmon returned. Halibut was fished with the carved two-part hook, set about a metre above the bottom and usually worked singly rather than in gangs. Eulachon oil was a staple: an oblong cedar trough in the collection is so impregnated with it that oil still seeps from the wood. Berries of *Vaccinium vitis-idaea* ssp. *minus* were eaten. At Annette Islands Reserve the community held the only right in Alaska to keep using fish traps after statehood in 1959, taking fish as food for reserve residents; required to use them at least once every three years, they stopped early in the 2000s and lost the right. Feast foods are distributed under the protocols described above, and food taboos attach to childbirth. The sources name no individual dishes, and do not describe tea or other beverage traditions.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tsimshian narrative is transmitted orally and divided by genre. An *adaawx* — "true tellings" — concerns animal spirits in human guise and is generally tied to the origin of the earth and of the peoples on it; a *malesk*, by contrast, is an adventure or history tale meant to entertain rather than explain. The Raven, called *Txamsem*, *We-gyet* or Giant, is the central figure, part of the Raven Tales shared across the Pacific Northwest Coast, and is understood as creator of the universe and intermediary between its physical and spiritual forms. In the creation narrative a pampered chief's son sickens and dies, a youth "bright as fire" appears in his bed in a dark and still universe, is tricked by slave spirits into eating scabs, develops an insatiable appetite, and is sent away with a raven blanket to become Raven; as he learns generosity he hosts a potlatch and wishes his guests to stone, giving form to a previously immaterial world. *Txamsem* has a brother, *Logobola*, held responsible for the scarcity of fresh clear water and for the fog in which Txamsem became lost. Henry W. Tate and Walter Wright worked as oral historians, and William Beynon of the Gitlaan as an ethnographer; artists today continue storytelling in their own media.

## Language & religion

*Sm'algyax* — "real or true tongue" — is one of four Tsimshianic lects: Coast Tsimshian on the lower Skeena and at New Metlakatla, Southern Tsimshian at Klemtu (extinct in 2013 with its last speaker), Nisg̱a'a on the Nass, and Gitksan on the upper Skeena. Most linguists treat Tsimshianic as an independent family; its inclusion in Edward Sapir's Penutian hypothesis remains genuinely contested, with Marie-Lucie Tarpent finding the connection probable and others holding the family unrelated to any other North American language. In 2016 only 160 people in Canada were recorded as Tsimshian speakers, and about 50 of the 1,300 Tsimshian in Alaska still speak Coast Tsimshian; courses have been offered at the University of Alaska Southeast since 2023. Tsimshian religion centred on the "Lord of Heaven," who sent supernatural servants to earth to aid people in need, and held that charity and purification of the body — by cleanliness or fasting — was the route to the afterlife. Anglican, Methodist and Presbyterian missionaries, William Duncan foremost among them, worked among the Tsimshian from the 19th century.
