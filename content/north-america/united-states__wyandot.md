## Overview

The Wyandot are an Indigenous Iroquoian people who emerged in the Great Lakes region in the mid-17th century through the merging of Wendat refugees with the Petun, Wenro, and other displaced peoples. Their historical territories included Wendake between Georgian Bay and Lake Simcoe, the Détroit area, the Ohio Country, Kansas, and Indian Territory west of the Mississippi River. Today, the federally recognized Wyandotte Nation is headquartered in Wyandotte, Oklahoma; Wyandot organizations are also located in Kansas and Michigan, and more than 4,000 Wyandot are found in eastern Kansas and northeastern Oklahoma. The Wyandot language belongs to the Northern Iroquoian language family and is related to Wendat. Wyandot cultural history includes matrilineal clans, palisaded villages, bark-covered longhouses, agriculture, language revitalization, and the continuing re-establishment of Wyandot identity.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

 They mention the hairstyle of Wendat warriors only in explaining the French name “Huron,” which is believed to derive from *hure*, meaning “boar’s head.”

### Architecture

The Wendat and Etionnontateronnon lived in palisaded villages of bark-covered longhouses. The Wyandott Mission at Upper Sandusky was established in 1819.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They state that the Wyandat practised a “Three Sisters” agricultural economy based on maize, beans, and squash.

## Music & performance

 They describe Kondiaronk as a skilled diplomat and brilliant negotiator who was famed for his oratory. In 1701, despite being terminally ill, he gave a final speech during negotiations that helped unite 39 different Indigenous nations and contributed to the Great Peace of Montreal.

## Dance & theatre

 They provide no information about ceremonial or recreational dance.

## Festivals & rituals

 They record that Kondiaronk was accorded a grand funeral in Montreal after his death a few days before the signing of the Great Peace of Montreal on Aug 4, 1701, but provide no description of the funeral rites. The sources also state that the Wyandot were organized into matrilineal clans and lived in villages, without explaining associated ceremonies.

## Foodways

The sources identify a “Three Sisters” agricultural economy based on maize, beans, and squash. They also report that, after removal to Kansas, the Wyandot maintained agriculture that produced an annual surplus for the market. They state that the Etionnontateronnon were known to the French as the “Petun,” an obsolete French word meaning tobacco.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They emphasize Kondiaronk’s reputation for oratory and describe the importance of his final speech in the negotiations that led to the Great Peace of Montreal. Modern documentation and revitalization efforts include Marius Barbeau’s transcriptions of Wyandot in Wyandotte, Oklahoma, in 1911–1912, Craig Kopris’s grammar and dictionary, and language lessons and classes offered by the Wyandotte Nation and the Wendat Nation of Quebec.

## Language & religion

Wyandot is an Iroquoian language traditionally spoken by people descended from the Tionontati. It is related to Wendat but is no longer mutually intelligible with it. Wyandot essentially died out as a spoken language with the death of the last native speaker in 1972, although revitalization efforts include classes in Wyandotte Public Schools, the Wyandotte Nation’s “Turtle-Tots” preschool program, online lessons, and instruction by Richard Zane Smith. Wyandot is written in the Latin script, with ⟨ʔ⟩ representing a glottal stop.
