## Overview

The Coast Miwok are an Indigenous people of California and were the second-largest tribe of the Miwok people. They inhabited the general area of present-day Marin County and southern Sonoma County in Northern California, from the Golden Gate north to Duncans Point and eastward to Sonoma Creek. Their cultural subdivisions included the Bodega Bay Miwok, or *Olamentko*, the Marin Miwok, or *Hookooeko*, and Southern Sonoma Miwok, or *Lekahtewutko*. They spoke Coast Miwok, an Utian language, and lived by hunting and gathering in small bands without centralized political authority. Their material culture included tule and redwood-bark houses, tule reed canoes, basketry, plant-fiber technology, and extensive ecological knowledge. Their population was estimated at 1,500 in 1770, 300 in 1848, and 60 in 1880.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Basket weaving** — Coast Miwok people are described as skilled at basketry. The sources identify sedges, willows, and ferns as basket-making materials, including *Carex*, *Cyperus*, *Eleocharis*, *Salix*, and *Adiantum*. Maidenhair and Five Finger ferns were used for contrasting black stems.

**Plant-fiber work** — Milkweed, California Hemp, Ground Iris, Dogbane, and Ninebark supplied fibers for technological uses. The sources name *Asclepias*, *Hoita macrostachya*, *Iris macrosiphon*, *Apocynum*, and *Physocarpus capitatus*.

**Tule reed canoe making** — Sedges, including *Schoenoplectus acutus* and *S. californicus*, were used to make Tule Reed canoes for travel.

**Redwood-bark construction** — *Sequoia sempervirens* supplied redwood bark used as building material. Ferns were used for cross-thatching.

### Clothing & dress

They do describe elaborate acting and dancing ceremonies in traditional costume as part of Kuksu religious practice, but provide no further information about the costume.

### Architecture

Coast Miwok traditional houses were called *kotcha*. They were constructed from slabs of tule grass or redwood bark in a cone-shaped form. Redwood bark from *Sequoia sempervirens* was used as a building material, and ferns were used for cross-thatching. A recreated Coast Miwok village called Kule Loklo is located at the Point Reyes National Seashore. The sources also identify village locations including Olompali, Nicasio, and authenticated villages around Bodega Bay, Tomales Bay, Petaluma, San Rafael, Sonoma, Cotati, Nicasio, Olema, Sausalito, Bolinas, Freestone, Ignacio, Novato, Valley Ford, and Salmon Creek.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do document tools and other objects made from local plants. Incense cedar and Pacific Yew were used for bow making; plant fibers supplied cordage or other technological materials; California Buckeye was used in fishing technique stunning with poison; and sedges supplied materials for Tule Reed canoes. Over 1,000 prehistoric charmstones and numerous arrowheads were unearthed at Tolay Lake in Southern Sonoma County. The lake was regarded as a sacred site and ceremonial gathering and healing place for the Miwok and others in the region.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Tattooing was a traditional practice among Coast Miwok. Poison oak was burned to produce a pigment.

## Music & performance

They describe Kuksu as a religious system involving elaborate acting and dancing ceremonies in traditional costume, an annual mourning ceremony, puberty rites of passage, shamanic intervention with the spirit world, and an all-male society that met in subterranean dance rooms.

Kuksu was shared with other Indigenous ethnic groups of Central California, including the Pomo, Maidu, Ohlone, Esselen, and northernmost Yokuts. The sources characterize Coast Miwok religion as part of the wider Central and Northern California cultural pattern.

The sources also record that Coast Miwok people traveled and camped on the coast and bays during peak fishing seasons. No songs, dances, musical occasions, or performance locations connected specifically with those journeys are described.

## Dance & theatre

Coast Miwok religious practice included elaborate acting and dancing ceremonies associated with Kuksu. The sources connect these performances with traditional costume, annual mourning, puberty rites of passage, shamanic intervention with the spirit world, and an all-male society that met in subterranean dance rooms.

They also do not distinguish between ceremonial and entertainment performances beyond describing the Kuksu activities as religious and ceremonial.

## Festivals & rituals

The Coast Miwok worldview included animism, and Kuksu was one form of this religious practice. Kuksu included an annual mourning ceremony, puberty rites of passage, elaborate acting and dancing ceremonies in traditional costume, shamanic intervention with the spirit world, and an all-male society that met in subterranean dance rooms.

Plants were used in practices connected with success for hunting, rites of passage, fertility, protection, health, and mourning. Sacred Datura was associated with ritual use and an age rite of passage. California Mugwort was associated with headache relief and mourning, while Coastal Sagebrush was associated with a rite of passage and protection. Holy Herb California Yerba Santa was used for smudging.

The Coast Miwok believed in animal and human spirits and regarded animal spirits as their ancestors. Coyote was viewed as their ancestor and creator god. Their stories described the Earth as beginning with land formed out of the Pacific Ocean.

## Foodways

Coast Miwok people lived by hunting and gathering. In springtime they headed to the coasts to hunt salmon and other seafood, including seaweed. Their staple foods were primarily acorns, especially from black oak and tan oak, together with wild game such as deer and cottontail rabbits. Black-tailed deer, identified as *Odocoileus hemionus columbianus*, were a coastal subspecies of the California mule deer.

Coast Miwok foodways included active stewardship of ecological resources. Cultivation efforts involved seed collection, storage, propagation, and transplantation. Other methods included prescribed burns and generational management of oak groves or stands.

The sources list Golden Chinquapin, Black Oak, Coast Live Oak, Valley Oak, Tan Oak, Hazel Nut, and California Bay among their nut and acorn resources. Seeds included Clarkia, Tidy Tips, Mule’s ear, Tarweeds, *Hezmizonia*, and Checkerbloom. Berries included Thimbleberry, Currants and Gooseberry, Blackberry, Wild Strawberry, Huckleberry, Bilberry, and Salmon Berry. Tule shoots, wild onion, Mariposa lily, and Ithuriel's spear supplied bulbs and shoots. Clover and Miner’s lettuce were greens.

## Oral tradition & literature

Coast Miwok mythology and narratives were similar to those of other Indigenous peoples of Central and Northern California. The sources describe myths, legends, tales, and histories as participating in the general cultural pattern of Central California.

Their narratives included animal and human spirits, with animal spirits understood as ancestors. Coyote was regarded as ancestor and creator god, and the Earth was said to have begun with land formed out of the Pacific Ocean.

The Bodega dialect was documented in Catherine A. Callaghan’s *Bodega Miwok Dictionary*, and the Coast Miwok language is represented in the California Language Archive and other language documentation resources named in the source material.

## Language & religion

Coast Miwok was one of the Miwok languages spoken in California from San Francisco Bay to Bodega Bay. It belonged to the Utian linguistic group. The Marin and Bodega varieties may have been separate languages, and all of the population has shifted to English. The Bodega dialect was documented in Callaghan’s *Bodega Miwok Dictionary*. Its nouns had cases expressed through suffixes, including present subjective, possessive, allative, locative, ablative, instrumental, and comitative. Sentences were most commonly subject-verb-object, although syntax was described as relatively free.

Coast Miwok religion included animism and Kuksu. Kuksu involved ceremonies, mourning, puberty rites, shamanic intervention, and an all-male society meeting in subterranean dance rooms.
