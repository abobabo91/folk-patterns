## Overview

The Duwamish (*dxʷdəwʔabš*) are a Lushootseed-speaking Southern Coast Salish people in western Washington and the Indigenous people of metropolitan Seattle. Their historical homeland centered on the Black and Duwamish rivers, with related communities around Lake Washington and the Sammamish River. The name *dxʷdəwʔabš* means “people inside the bay,” referring to the Cedar River and to villages located inland rather than along Puget Sound. Before colonization, Duwamish society was organized through villages and extended-family longhouses. Today, Duwamish people are enrolled in several tribes, including the unrecognized Duwamish Tribe and federally recognized tribes such as the Muckleshoot Indian Tribe and Suquamish Indian Tribe. Their contemporary cultural work includes treaty-rights advocacy and the preservation and revitalization of culture, language, and land.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The Duwamish village was the central form of social organization before contact. Villages included one or more cedar plank longhouses, each housing one or more extended families. A longhouse could support tens or hundreds of people. Interiors were often divided by cattail or cedar partitions, with each family occupying its own section and maintaining a fire pit at the center.

Duwamish villages were primarily riverine and were commonly built along the *dxʷdəw*, now called the Duwamish, Black, and Cedar Rivers. The related *x̌ačuʔabš* were primarily lake-oriented, with villages along Lake Washington and Lake Union. Traditional longhouses continued to be built at Duwamish settlements into the mid-1800s. One settlement stood at the confluence of the Black and Cedar rivers, historically called the Lake Fork.

Longhouse architecture remains in use in cultural settings. The north face of the Burke Museum at the University of Washington is given as one example. On January 3, 2009, the Duwamish Tribe opened the Duwamish Longhouse and Cultural Center; its main hall closely echoes a traditional longhouse. The Center was built near the former village *həʔapus*.

## Music & performance

They do record a *sing gamble* ceremony held in 1894 by William, also known as Stoda, who brought together Duwamish from existing villages and from the Port Madison and Muckleshoot reservations.

The Duwamish historically participated in a broad trade and potlatch network that helped distribute resources between areas whose food supplies varied from year to year. The network was described as extensive, effective, and connected to the wider Pacific Northwest until European diseases arriving in the 1770s severely affected the region.

The Duwamish remain involved in Seattle’s urban Indian culture. They are represented in institutions including United Indians of All Tribes and the Seattle Indian Health Board, and federally recognized tribes and the Duwamish Tribe work with the city of Seattle to preserve and develop local Native culture and history.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document several historical and ceremonial contexts.

The Duwamish participated in a trade and potlatch network that distributed resources among communities facing different conditions from year to year. In 1894, William, also known as Stoda, brought Duwamish from villages and reservations to a *sing gamble* ceremony. The source names this ceremony.

Duwamish social life was organized through villages, extended families, marriage alliances, and relationships between communities. Intermarriage could provide access to distant resources, while good marriages brought prestige and could result in material wealth. Duwamish society was divided into upper, lower, and slave classes, and social status was associated with genealogy, intertribal kinship, resource use, and knowledge concerning spirits and the spirit world.

In the early 1900s, many Duwamish converted to the Indian Shaker Church, described as a local religion combining Christian and Indigenous beliefs. Since then, the primary religion of the Duwamish has been Christianity in several denominations.

## Foodways

Before colonization, the Duwamish depended on resources from rivers, lakes, tidelands, and surrounding territories. The tidelands at the mouth of Elliott Bay were abundantly rich in marine life and provided a plentiful source of food. The Duwamish were primarily riverine, while the *x̌ačuʔabš* were primarily oriented toward Lake Washington and Lake Union.

Food resources were not always sufficient to last through spring. This variation contributed to the development of an extensive trade and potlatch network through which resources were distributed to areas in need. The network extended across much of the Pacific Northwest, and the Puget Sound served as a principal waterway connecting Lushootseed-speaking peoples with other communities.

As of late 2022, ʔálʔal Cafe in Seattle was described as using local ingredients and sharing traditional Native American dishes from around North America.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Duwamish Hill Preserve in Tukwila is described as a culturally significant space in traditional stories and as a historical vantage point.

They do document preservation efforts involving culture, language, and land. The Duwamish Tribe works with nearby cities to preserve and protect Duwamish history, while the Renton History Museum maintains a small exhibit on the archaeological and cultural history of the Duwamish.

## Language & religion

The Duwamish historically spoke a subdialect of the southern dialect of Lushootseed, a Coast Salish language spoken throughout much of western Washington. The primary language used by the Duwamish today is English. The Duwamish name *dxʷdəwʔabš* is an anglicization of the Lushootseed name and means “people inside the bay.” It is composed of the prefix *dxʷ-*, meaning “toward, to,” the suffix *=abš*, meaning “people,” and the root *√dəw*, a variant form of *dəkʷ*, meaning “inside something relatively small.” The name has also been spelled *dxʷduʔabš*.

The Duwamish are classified as a Southern Coast Salish people. The Southern Coast Salish include Lushootseed-speaking peoples and the Twana, who speak the Twana language. In the early 1900s, many Duwamish converted to the Indian Shaker Church, a local religion combining Christian and Indigenous beliefs. Since then, Christianity, in several denominations, has been the primary religion.
