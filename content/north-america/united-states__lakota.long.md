## Overview

The Lakota, also known as the Teton Sioux, are one of the three prominent subcultures of the Sioux people, alongside the Eastern Dakota and Western Dakota. Their current lands are in North and South Dakota, and Lakota communities also occur in Nebraska, Montana, Manitoba, and Saskatchewan. The Lakota language, Lakȟótiyapi, is the westernmost of three closely related languages in the Siouan language family and has approximately 2,000 speakers. In 2010, the Lakota population exceeded 170,000. Lakota history is associated with the northern Great Plains, the Missouri River, the Black Hills, horse-mounted buffalo hunting, winter counts, treaty diplomacy, and religious traditions involving the sacred pipe, Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka, and White Buffalo Calf Woman.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Tobacco pouch** — Museum records identify a Lakota pipe bag dating approximately to 1870–1880, made from hide, glass beads, porcupine quills, and pigments. The record describes painted geometric designs and fringe.

**Warrior’s shirt** — A museum record describes a warrior’s shirt from the Yellowstone River area, identified as Sioux and associated with a chief’s garment during the war of 1867. The record lists mixed materials.

**Moccasins** — A museum record identifies a pair of Lakota/Sioux moccasins embroidered with beads. The record states that Plains peoples commonly used a hard, stronger rawhide sole.

**Man’s tunic-like shirt** — A Finnish Heritage Agency record describes a man’s shirt made from brain-tanned deer skin, following the poncho form of a traditional so-called war shirt. The shirt is decorated with porcupine quills and has long fringe decorations.

**Ceremonial bundles** — Lakota religion describes *wašicu* as ceremonial or medicine bundles containing objects regarded as material manifestations of sacred power. The bundles may be associated with particular prayers, rituals, and songs.

**Tipi circle** — The sacred hoop, or *cʽągléška wakʽą*, is described as a camp circle formed by a ring of tipis. It came to symbolize harmony among nations and the connectedness of life.

**Motif vocabulary.** Geometric designs, four horses moving at full gallop, eagle feathers, porcupine-quill decoration, fringe.

### Clothing & dress

Museum records document Lakota and Sioux clothing made from deer skin, hide, cloth, beads, porcupine quills, metal ornaments, and feathers. Recorded garments include a warrior’s shirt, a man’s tunic-like shirt, leggings, moccasins, blankets, and decorated shirts. A British Museum portrait records an Oglala man wearing a war bonnet, a decorated scalp shirt, leggings, and moccasins. Other records describe eagle-feather headdresses, bone breast plates, shell chokers, ear ornaments, dentalium and abalone ornaments, ribbons, and waistcoats. A photograph of an Oohenonpa chief records leggings with embroidered flaps and moccasins. The museum records also show women’s decorated clothing, including a shawl, necklace, bangles, and finger rings.

### Architecture

The sources describe winter camps, tipis, and tipi circles. Lakota seasonal life included sheltered winter camps, dispersed band life during spring and autumn, and a great summer gathering for ceremony and communal hunting. The sacred hoop, or *cʽągléška wakʽą*, was historically conceived as a ring of tipis.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Museum records document breast plates made from bone tube beads, glass and metal beads, brass beads, leather, eagle claws, and old United States one-cent copper coins. Other records mention weapons, pipes, tobacco pouches, herbal medicines, and war bundles. Lakota religion also describes stones regarded as animate and capable of possessing a *šicų* spirit; such stones could be kept in a *wašícų tʻųká* bundle.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Museum records describe dentalium and abalone shell ear ornaments, shell chokers, necklaces, bangles, finger rings, metal hair-plate ornaments, and feathers worn in the hair. A British Museum portrait records a person wearing an eagle feather in the hair, with ribbon, dentalium, and abalone ear ornaments. Lakota religion states that some afterlife traditions involve an old woman who admits only those with proper tattoos to the Sky-Road.

## Music & performance

Lakota and Dakota music prominently features the human voice accompanied by drumming. Traditional dance songs generally begin in a high pitch, led by a solo vocalist whose phrase is repeated by a group. The phrase then descends to a lower pitch, followed by a brief pause; the second half echoes the first and may contain “honor beats,” usually four beats representing cannon fire in battle. The entire song may be repeated several times at the discretion of the lead singer.

Many songs use vocables, syllabic utterances without lexical meaning. A Lakota lullaby is described as a calming hum. In some traditional songs, women sing one octave above men, although they do not sing the first time the song is sung or the lead line. Dakota percussion uses drums, sometimes with syncopation. In some contest songs, beats begin with an irregular ruffle and continue with a swift regular beat.

The Lakota Flag Song begins special events such as powwows and is not accompanied by a dance. Other songs honor veterans, warriors, or other people. Named non-Powwow song types include Sun dance, Yuwipi, Inipi, courtship, flute, lullaby, peyote, and Christian hymns. Singers and groups named in the source include Earl Bullhead, Porcupine Singers, Tianna Spotted Thunder, Lakota Thunder, James Iron Shell, Red Leaf Singers, Common Man Singers, Crazy Horse Singers, and White Bull.

## Dance & theatre

The sources describe the Ghost Dance movement, which spread rapidly across the reservations in 1890 and promised the resurrection of the old world and the disappearance of white settlers. Lakota religion also identifies the sun dance, vision quest, sweat lodge purification ceremony, and Yuwipi as important ritual traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

Lakota religious traditions include seven ceremonies said to have been given by White Buffalo Calf Woman. The sources specifically name the sweat lodge purification ceremony, the vision quest, and the sun dance. Prayers may be facilitated by smoking a sacred pipe or making offerings, usually cotton flags or tobacco. A ritual specialist is commonly called a *wičháša wakhá*, while a *yuwípi wičháša* performs the Yuwipi ritual, which typically invokes spirits for healing.

The Lakota seasonal cycle included spring and autumn dispersal, winter camps, and a great summer gathering for ceremony and communal hunting. The Ghost Dance movement spread during the drought and starvation conditions of 1890. The Lakota Flag Song begins powwows and other special events.

## Foodways

The sources associate Lakota life with buffalo hunting and describe buffalo as a source of meat, hide, clothing, and shelter. Hunting and butchery traditionally had ceremonial elements. Lakota horse-mounted buffalo hunting developed after horses were obtained through trade with the Cheyenne along the Missouri River in the early 18th century. The sources also mention tobacco as an offering and as an element of smoking rituals.

## Oral tradition & literature

Lakota history was recorded in winter counts, or *waníyetu wówapi*, pictorial calendars painted on hides or later recorded on paper. The Battiste Good winter count records Lakota history to 900 CE and associates that time with White Buffalo Calf Woman giving the Lakota the White Buffalo Calf Pipe. Lakota religious and mythological traditions include stories of Inyan, Maka, Skan, Anpo, Wi, Iktomi, Unhcegila, Wakinyan, and White Buffalo Calf Woman.

Lakota mythology describes Iktomi as a trickster spider god, Unhcegila as a serpent monster, and Wakinyan as the Thunderbird. Creation traditions state that the Lakota emerged from Wind Cave in the Black Hills, while another account describes Inyan, the Rock, creating Maka, the Earth. Lakota language teachers, immersion programs, written materials, and recordings are part of contemporary preservation efforts.

## Language & religion

Lakota, or Lakȟótiyapi, is a Siouan language and is mutually intelligible with the two dialects of Dakota, especially Western Dakota. It traditionally had no written form, although missionaries and linguists worked on written forms in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The language has approximately 2,000 speakers, and many communities have immersion programs. Several orthographies are used, including systems associated with Albert White Hat, the Lakota Language Consortium, Standing Rock, and Red Cloud Indian School.

Lakota religion has no formal leadership or centralized organizational structure and displays internal variation. *wakʽą* is described as an energy or power permeating the universe, while *Wakʽą Tʽąką* is its unified totality and is translated as the Great Mystery. Lakota traditions include sacred pipes, offerings, spirits called *wakʽąpi*, medicine bundles, vision quests, sweat lodges, and the sun dance. Many Lakota practice traditional religion alongside Christianity, including Catholicism, Episcopalianism, or the peyote religion of the Native American Church.
