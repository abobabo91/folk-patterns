## Overview

The Sauk are closely allied with the Meskwaki and have lived across a wide region of the central and eastern United States. Their cultural life includes village living, hunting, farming, trade, lacrosse, clan organization, and oral traditions. Museum portraits show men wearing decorated clothing, blankets, necklaces, feathers, and headdresses. Sauk is an endangered Algonquian language with a syllabic writing system.

## Material culture

The sources mainly document men’s clothing, blankets, necklaces, feathers, medals, weapons, and other objects in museum portraits.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Sauk textile records focus on decorated leggings, moccasins, blankets, necklaces, and headdresses.

- **Beaded necklaces and headdress** — Museum portraits show Sauk and Fox men wearing beaded necklaces and a beaded headdress.
- **Fur and bear-claw necklaces** — Some necklaces were made from fur and bear claws.
- **Embroidered moccasins** — Museum records show embroidered moccasins worn by Big Bear, Dead Indian, and White Fish.
- **Decorated leggings** — Records mention embroidered leggings, feather decoration, quill or bead decoration, fringe, and wool leggings with beadwork.
- **Wool blankets** — Portraits show blankets and wool blankets wrapped around the body, waist, shoulders, or legs.

### Clothing & dress

The clothing records show shirts, blankets, leggings, moccasins, headwear, and many forms of decoration worn by men.

- **Fringed shirts** — Men are shown wearing shirts, cloth shirts, and shirts with fringing.
- **Leggings and moccasins** — The records include leggings, decorated leggings, leg bands, and decorated or embroidered moccasins.
- **Feathered headwear** — Men wore feather hair ornaments, eagle feathers, fur headdresses, cloth head wraps, beaded headdresses, and fur hats with eagle feathers.
- **Ceremonial objects** — Portraits show men holding feather fans, feather pouches, walking sticks, ceremonial objects, and tomahawks.

### Architecture

The sources do not cover Sauk houses or named building traditions.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources record several personal and everyday objects but do not describe Sauk pottery or house goods.

- **Peace medals** — Museum records document peace medals worn or carried by Sauk men.
- **Tomahawks** — Portraits show men holding tomahawks.
- **Feather fans and pouches** — Portraits show feather fans and feather pouches.
- **Walking sticks** — Museum records include a walking stick.
- **Boats** — Sauk people used boats to cross a river.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Sauk adornment records include shell, bead, fur, feather, metal, and leather ornaments.

- **Dentalium shell necklaces** — The records include dentalium shell necklaces and chokers made of dentalium.
- **Cloth and shell necklaces** — Some necklaces combined cloth and shells.
- **Gorgets and conchos** — Men wore scarves with gorgets and leather straps with conchos.
- **Feather ornaments** — Adornment included eagle feathers, feather hair ornaments, decorated fur headdresses, and fur hats with eagle feathers.
- **Moiety paint symbols** (*Kîshkôha; Shkasha*) — The two moieties were symbolized by black charcoal and white clay.

## Music & performance

The sources do not identify Sauk music, instruments, or song traditions, but they describe lacrosse as an important practice.

- **Lacrosse** — Lacrosse was played for recreation, at festivals, to toughen young warriors, and to prepare for wars or raids.
- **Written language samples** — Sauk language materials include a greeting and a narrative about turtles, but do not call them songs or performance genres.

## Dance & theatre

The sources do not cover Sauk dances, theatre, masked performance, or named dance forms.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources mention festivals, moieties, chiefs, and war preparation but do not give an annual ritual calendar.

- **Festival lacrosse** — Lacrosse was part of festivals and was also used to prepare young warriors for combat.
- **Two moieties** (*Kîshkôha; Shkasha*) — The Sauk and Fox peoples were divided into the Kîshkôha and Shkasha moieties, linked with long hair and bravery.
- **Clan council** — The tribe was governed by a council of sacred clan chiefs, a war chief, family heads, and warriors.
- **Civil, war, and ceremonial chiefs** — Chiefs were grouped as civil, war, and ceremonial chiefs, but only civil chiefs were hereditary.

## Foodways

Sauk villages raised crops, hunted American bison, and traded with other tribes.

- **Crop raising** — The Sauk lived in villages and raised crops.
- **Bison hunting** — The Sauk hunted American bison.
- **Trade** — After adopting a semi-sedentary prairie lifestyle, the Sauk actively traded with other tribes.

## Oral tradition & literature

Sauk oral traditions explain place names, describe past movements, and preserve legends and written narratives.

- **Saginaw name tradition** (*O-Sauk-e-non*) — One tradition explains Saginaw through the Ojibwe expression O-Sauk-e-non, meaning “land of the Sauks” or “where the Sauks were.”
- **Battle of Skull Island** — A legend tells how Sauk people escaped by boat to an island before a river froze enough for attackers to cross.
- **Turtle narrative** — A written Sauk narrative tells of two turtles, a thunderstorm, rain, and a lake.
- **Saginaw River tradition** — Sauk traditions say the tribe occupied the area near the Saginaw River.

## Language & religion

Sauk is an endangered Algonquian language with several names, close ties to Meskwaki, and a syllabic writing system.

- **Sauk language** (*Thâkiwâtowêweni; Thâkîwaki*) — Sauk is also called Thâkiwâtowêweni or Thâkîwaki and may be a Fox dialect or a distinct language.
- **People of the outlet** (*asakiwaki*) — The Sauk once called themselves asakiwaki, meaning “people of the outlet.”
- **Syllabic writing** — Sauk has a syllabic orthography, including a 1975 Primer Book based on a syllabary from 1906.
- **New orthography** — A newer Sauk orthography was proposed around 1994.
- **Language preservation** — Five elders are described as fluent speakers, and Sauk is considered endangered.
