## Overview

The Biloxi, whose endonym is *Tanêks(a)*, are Native Americans of the Siouan-Catawban language family. When Europeans first encountered them in 1699, they lived near the Gulf of Mexico, near what is now Biloxi, Mississippi. Their descendants later merged with the Tunica and other remnant peoples. Together, they were federally recognized in 1981 as the Tunica-Biloxi Indian Tribe, which shares a small reservation in Avoyelles Parish, Louisiana. Biloxi descendants speak English or French, while the Biloxi language, *Tanêksąyaa ade*, has been extinct since the 1930s. The group is documented through agriculture, hunting, fishing, kinship, clothing, body adornment, oral histories, and linguistic records.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional Biloxi men's clothing included a *breechcloth* or *breechclout*, usually made of deerskin and passed between the legs beneath a belt. Belts were made of skin or beaded cord. Men also wore garments made from the skins of animals including bear, deer, panther, wildcat, beaver, otter, raccoon, squirrel, and bison. Some animal-skin garments were long and intended for winter wear, particularly for older people. Women processed and sewed animal skins into clothing, moccasins, and leggings. Leggings were worn in cold weather or to protect the legs from underbrush; their lower portions were tucked under moccasin rims, while their upper ends were usually attached to the belt with straps.

### Architecture

A village described by Pierre LeMoyne d'Iberville contained remnants of cabins made of mud, with roofs covered in tree bark.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Biloxi made tools and utensils from bison and deer horn.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Biloxi people wore ornaments made from cut and polished seashells. Some had traditional facial tattoos and wore nose rings or earrings.

## Music & performance

They do record oral histories recounting Biloxi traditions and linguistic documentation collected from Emma Jackson. James Owen Dorsey visited the Biloxi in Louisiana in 1892 and 1893, and Morris Swadesh and Mary Haas spoke with Emma Jackson in 1934 while confirming her knowledge of the Biloxi language.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe elements of funeral practice for deceased *ąyaaxi*, a Biloxi word meaning king, chief, medicine man, or shaman. Their bodies were dried in fire and smoke, then placed upright on red poles around the central interior of a temple. The deceased was set on a platform near the temple's front entrance, and visitors offered food daily.

## Foodways

The Biloxi were an agricultural society in which women cultivated varieties of maize, beans, and squash. Men supplemented the agrarian diet through hunting deer, bear, and bison, while the community fished year round. The Biloxi language contains the verb *duti*, “to eat,” and the nominalized form *aduti*, “food.”

## Oral tradition & literature

Information about Biloxi history has been derived from archaeological studies, oral histories recounting their traditions, and materials concerning related tribes. The Biloxi language was recorded in linguistic work, including James Owen Dorsey and James Swanton's dictionary. Dorsey compiled data during visits to Louisiana in 1892 and 1893, and the last known semi-speaker, Emma Jackson, was documented in 1934.

## Language & religion

Biloxi, called *Tanêksąyaa* in Biloxi, is an extinct Siouan-Catawban language. It is classified as an Ohio Valley, or Southeastern, Siouan language and is related to Ofo and Tutelo. The language was formerly spoken in present-day Mississippi, Louisiana, and southeastern Texas. By 1934, Emma Jackson was the last native speaker documented by Morris Swadesh and Mary Haas; another account describes her as the last known semi-speaker, who died in the 1930s. Traditional Biloxi political and spiritual authority centered on the *Yaaxitąąyą*, or “Great Sacred One,” with lesser nobles or deputies called *ixi*. The Biloxi word *ąyaaxi* or *yaaxi* could mean king or chief as well as medicine man or shaman.
