## Overview

The Tanana Athabaskans, also called Tanana Athabascans or Tanana Athapaskans, are an Alaskan Athabaskan people belonging to the Athabaskan-speaking ethnolinguistic group. They are the original inhabitants of the Tanana River drainage basin in east-central Alaska Interior, United States; a little part, identified with White River First Nation, lived in Yukon, Canada. Their culture is based on hunting, gathering, fishing, seasonal movement, and matrilineal kinship. In Alaska, the groups are identified through the languages they speak: Lower Tanana, Middle Tanana, Tanacross, and Upper Tanana. Tanana Athabaskans lived in semi-permanent settlements and moved between winter camps and seasonal hunting and fishing camps according to the season and availability of resources.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Birchbark containers** — Birch bark from paper birch was used to make baskets and cooking vessels.

**Spruce-root objects** — Spruce roots were used to make baskets, cooking vessels, tools, cradleboards, and canoes.

**Babiche and sinew** — Caribou supplied material for netting used in snowshoes and for babiche and sinew used in snares, cords, and lashings.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no motifs.

### Clothing & dress

They state that caribou supplied material for clothing, shelters, and boats, and that Dall sheep were a desired source of food and material for clothing and tools. The sources also state that caribou supplied babiche and sinew for snares, cords, and lashings.

### Architecture

Tanana Athabaskans lived in semi-permanent settlements and maintained a central winter camp with several seasonal hunting and fishing camps. Their settlement patterns were dispersed and depended greatly on the availability of subsistence resources. The sources mention shelters made from caribou material.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources describe a varied material culture including bone and antler projectile points, fishhooks, beads, buttons, birch bark trays, and bone gaming pieces. In the Upper Tanana region, native copper obtained through trading with Ahtna people, also called “Copper Indians,” was used to manufacture knives, projectile points, awls, ornaments, and axes. Birch bark and spruce roots were used for baskets, cooking vessels, tools, cradleboards, and canoes. Fish were stored in large subsurface caches, and dried fish were placed on drying racks.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention beads, buttons, and ornaments among the artifacts associated with Athabaskan culture. They also describe ornaments made from native copper in the Upper Tanana region. No information is provided about jewelry names, tattoos, henna, hair practices, or ritual functions of body adornment.

## Music & performance

They state that regional bands could meet at a predetermined place and time in mid-winter for a gathering ceremony called a potlatch, after which they separated again for beaver and muskrat trapping.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources identify a mid-winter gathering ceremony called a potlatch. A regional band might meet at a predetermined place and time during mid-winter, then separate again for beaver and muskrat trapping.

The sources describe a matrilineal system and matrilineal kinship. They state that each local band consisted of perhaps four nuclear families and that regional bands were larger bilateral groups, but the supplied material does not describe birth rites, coming-of-age ceremonies, weddings, funerals, or other life-cycle rituals.

The sources describe seasonal activities connected with subsistence. Fall hunting included moose, caribou, Dall sheep, and small terrestrial animals. Fishing began in the late spring and continued until mid-July at Mansfield Lake and Fish Creek. Caribou hunting occurred in early summer and mid-summer, while fall migration hunting used fence, corral, and snare complexes. These activities are described as part of subsistence rather than as named festivals.

## Foodways

Tanana Athabaskans used fish, birds, mammals, berries, roots, and other renewable resources. The primary hunting animals were caribou, moose, and Dall sheep. Caribou was the most valuable hunting animal and an important food animal in the Upper Tanana region. Moose was another major food animal, and its meat was eaten fresh or preserved. Migratory waterfowl, including ducks, geese, and swans, and upland game birds, including ptarmigans and grouse, supplied fresh meat.

Fishing took place near village sites. Important fish included whitefish and Pacific salmon, as well as pike, grayling, lingcod, and sucker. Fish not eaten fresh were processed and dried on drying racks. Fresh and dried fish were cooked in water boiled by placing heated stones into a birch bark basket.

Beginning in late spring and continuing through summer and early fall, adults and children gathered fruit, berries, edible roots, and plants. Indian potato or wild carrot and wild rhubarb were among the foods named. Upper Tanana people used lingonberries as food: they ate them raw, mixed them with sugar or grease, fried them with sugar or dried fish eggs, or made them into pies, jam, and jelly. They also preserved or froze them and used them in traditional medicine for colds, coughs, and sore throats.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that much has been learned about Athabaskan culture and history through ethnography, oral history, and a broad array of cultural items. No contemporary literary revival or preservation project is identified in the supplied material.

## Language & religion

Tanana Athabaskans belong to the Athabaskan-speaking ethnolinguistic group. The groups identified in Alaska are Lower Tanana, Middle Tanana, Tanacross, and Upper Tanana. The Tanana River is called *Tth'itu'* in Tanana languages, literally “straight water,” and *Tene No'* in Koyukon, literally “trail water.” Tanana Athabaskan peoples are called *Ten Hʉt'ænæ* in Lower Tanana and Koyukon, literally “trail people,” and *Tanan Gwich'in* in Gwich'in, literally “people of Tanana River.”

The sources state that Middle Tanana was an extinct separate dialect of Lower Tanana in 1993. The Lower Tanana and Tanacross languages have named regional varieties, and the Goodpaster River is described as a natural break in the Tanana Athabaskan language area. They state that missionaries, mission schools, and new religious beliefs affected traditional social organization and that by 1900 virtually all Alaskan Athabaskans were Christians, at least by name if not entirely by practice.
