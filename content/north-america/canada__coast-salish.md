## Overview

The Coast Salish are Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast living in British Columbia and Washington and Oregon. They speak fourteen related Salishan languages and number at least 56,590 people. Their art emphasizes a minimalist approach compared to northern neighbours, dividing into flat design and carving by men, and basketry and weaving by women. They are known for wool textiles, carved wooden objects, and ceremonies tied to spiritual power.

## Material culture

Coast Salish material culture centres on wood, bark, fibre and horn, shaped through weaving, carving and basketry with little ceramic or metalworking tradition.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Wool from mountain goat and the extinct Salish Woolly Dog was spun, woven and used for blankets, mats, clothing and wealth-indicating robes.

- **Mountain-goat and woolly-dog wool weaving** — Wool was mixed with diatomaceous earth, wetted and twisted between palm and thigh before being woven on a loom of two upright bars carrying two horizontal rollers.
- **Spindle whorl** — Whorls set on wooden spindles spun loose strands, and some carried elaborate designs depicting beings as a principal support of flat-design idiom.
- **Imbricated coiled slat basketry** — Circular trays and rectangular baskets were coiled from cedar or hemlock roots and decorated with imbricated designs in plain and black-dyed prunus bark.
- **Cedar-bark work** — Cedar bark served as both basketry fibre and sheet material for baskets and bailing tools used to clear water from canoes.
- **Carex twine and reed matting** — Woven reed mats were made into the mattresses and cushions of the longhouse, while Carex leaves were used for baskets and twine.

### Clothing & dress

Wool blankets and robes served as both everyday wear and public statements of wealth, while ceremonial dress for mask societies included feather-covered regalia and hoof-rattle leggings.

- **Wool robes and blankets** — Larger robes doubled as indicators of wealth, while trade blankets from the Hudson's Bay Company were among luxuries Coast Salish traders sought.
- **Sxwayxwey regalia** (*sxwayxwey*) — Ceremonial dress for the mask society consisted of regalia covered with feathers and leggings hung with hoof rattles.
- **Head shaping with cradle boards** — Many Coast Salish mothers bound the heads of free-born babies with cradle boards long enough to produce a steeply sloping forehead as a visible mark of rank.

### Architecture

Villages consisted of large shed-roofed cedar longhouses built of western red cedar planks and beams, housing extended families and sometimes stretching for miles along rivers.

- **Cedar longhouse** — Shed-roofed structures built of western red cedar split planks and beams over an earthen floor housed forty or more people with interior sleeping platforms, storage shelves and interior fires venting through roof slats.
- **House posts** — Large beams at the sides were carved or painted with ancestors, family history or supernatural beings.
- **Oleman House** — The Suquamish Oleman House (Old Man House) at Port Madison Reservation ran 500 by 40–60 feet around 1850 and held about a hundred people.
- **Gambrel roof** — A roof form unique to Puget Sound Coast Salish.
- **Kekuli** — Pit-houses were also used for shelter.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources describe no ceramic or metalworking tradition; the everyday repertoire centres on wood, bark, fibre and horn carved into functional and ceremonial forms.

- **House posts** — Men carved house posts with ancestors, family history or supernatural beings as supports of flat-design idiom.
- **Carved bowls** — Bowls ranged from simple forms with animal features to complex seated human figure bowls used in female puberty rituals and believed to aid women in giving birth.
- **Bentwood boxes** — Boxes were carved and decorated in flat-design idiom for storage and display.
- **Welcome figures** — Carved wooden figures displayed flat-design decoration.
- **Mountain-goat horn rattles** — Sheets of mountain-goat horn bent and sewn into volumetric triangles were hung with strands of mountain-goat wool for ceremonial use.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Jewelry is thinly documented, with the primary body practice being head shaping of infants as a visible mark of rank rather than ornamentation.

- **Mountain-goat horn bracelet** — A thin bracelet cut from mountain-goat horn with a shouldered end tapering to a pointed hitch was decorated with groups of parallel lines and lines of circles, worn or polished smooth inside and out.

## Music & performance

Performance is bound to spirit power, expressed through dances, masks and ceremonies that display the spiritual powers given to individuals and communities.

- **Spirit dancing ceremonies** — Common winter gatherings at which members show their powers through song and dance on the longhouse floor, with powers attached to spirit animals such as raven, woodpecker, bear or seal.
- **Sxwayxwey mask ceremony** (*sxwayxwey*) — Central to the central Coast Salish region, men from families holding hereditary rights are initiated into the society, wear the mask and dance, joined by women singers.
- **Mountain-goat horn rattles** — Percussion instruments of bent and sewn mountain-goat horn used in ceremonial performance.
- **Slahal** — A sleight-of-hand game on which gambling accompanied athletic contests as recreation.

## Dance & theatre

Two named dance traditions appear: the sxwayxwey mask dance of hereditarily entitled men, and winter spirit dancing as collective but individually owned display of acquired powers.

- **Sxwayxwey mask dance** (*sxwayxwey*) — Performed by hereditarily entitled men of central Coast Salish in masks with bulging cylindrical eyeballs, animal heads as horns and drooping tongues, crowned with large feathers, dancers wearing feather-covered regalia and hoof-rattle leggings while women sing.
- **Winter spirit dancing** — Collective but individually owned display of acquired spirit powers on the longhouse floor through song and dance.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual year turns on winter spirit dances and the potlatch, a public display of wealth that enhanced but did not buy status or power.

- **Vision quest** — Journeys involving other states of consciousness through which guardian spirits were sought, widely practised in individual ritual life.
- **Potlatch** — The great ceremonial display of wealth including land, food resources, household items and adornments, banned in Canada in 1885 and in the United States some years later, with suppression ending in 1934 in the US and in 1951 in Canada.
- **Soul recovery and journey ceremony** — The Duwamish held ceremonies in which shamans travelled to the underworld to recover souls in transit, described as a two-day walk along a trail through bushes and past a lake to a river-divided valley.
- **Female puberty ritual** — A life-cycle rite in which a seated figure bowl was used.

## Foodways

Salmon was the dietary staple, supplemented by a rich variety of seafoods, forage and cultivated plants managed through deliberate land management.

- **Salmon** — The dietary staple, taken in mass along rivers in summer and on river banks in January.
- **Shellfish** — Butter clams, horse clams and cockles were abundant and dried for trade.
- **Camas** — Among the most important plant foods, gathered on the prairies and also cultivated in forest gardens holding crabapple, hazelnut, cranberry, wild plum and wild cherry.
- **Wapato** — An important plant food especially for the Duwamish.
- **Muckleshoot Seasonal Rounds** — A calendar setting the year: salmon on river banks in January, salmonberry sprouts eaten with salmon eggs by May, steelhead and king salmon in mass in summer with berries abundant in forests.

## Oral tradition & literature

Coast Salish narrative traditions centre on guardian spirits and transformation between human and animal spirits, documented in published collections and modern oratory.

- **Legends of Vancouver** — Published in 1911 by Canadian author E. Pauline Johnson (Tekahionwake), a collection of Coast Salish as-told-to narratives from the author's relationship with Squamish chief Joe Capilano.
- **The Adventures of Yoo-Lah-Teen** — A later retelling for children published by Ellen Pugh and Laszlo Kubinyi in 1975.
- **Chief Dan George's 1967 speech** — Delivered at a Canadian Centennial ceremony in Vancouver's Empire Stadium, it riveted its audience and touched off public awareness and Native activism in British Columbia and Canada.
- **Lushootseed Dictionary** — Language documentation by Dawn Bates, Thom Hess and Vi Hilbert.

## Language & religion

Coast Salish languages form a branch of the Salishan family around most of the Georgia and Puget Sound basins, while the living religious practice is the spirit-power complex.

- **Halkomelem** — One of the named Coast Salish languages found in museum records and articles.
- **Lushootseed** — One of the named Coast Salish languages documented in the Lushootseed Dictionary.
- **Squamish** — One of the named Coast Salish languages.
- **Guardian spirits** — Spiritual powers sought through vision quests and attached to spirit animals, central to the living religious practice.
- **Winter spirit dances** — The survival of an ancestral religious practice in which members display their spiritual powers through song and dance.

## Glossary

- *sxwayxwey* — mask ceremony and dance tradition of hereditarily entitled central Coast Salish men
- *kekuli* — pit-house used for shelter
- *slahal* — sleight-of-hand gambling game
- *potlatch* — great ceremonial display of wealth including land, food, household items and adornments
- *vision quest* — journey involving other states of consciousness to seek guardian spirits
- *guardian spirits* — spiritual powers given to individuals through vision quests, attached to spirit animals
- *Halkomelem* — one of the fourteen contiguous Salishan languages
- *Lushootseed* — one of the fourteen contiguous Salishan languages, documented in dictionary
- *Squamish* — one of the fourteen contiguous Salishan languages
- *Duwamish* — one of the named Coast Salish peoples known for soul recovery ceremonies
- *Suquamish* — one of the named Coast Salish peoples
- *imbricated coiled slat basketry* — basketry technique using imbricated designs in plain and black-dyed prunus bark
- *spindle whorl* — disk set on wooden spindle shaft to spin wool strands, often decorated with elaborate designs
- *Carex* — plant whose leaves are used for baskets and twine
- *Salishan* — language family including Coast Salish and related peoples
