## Overview

Nakota, also called Nakoda or Nakona, is the endonym used by Native peoples usually known as Assiniboine or Hohe in the United States and Stoney in Canada. The Assiniboine separated from the Yankton-Yanktonai grouping around 1640 and moved west from the woodlands of what is now Minnesota into northern and northwestern Montana and North Dakota, as well as Manitoba, Saskatchewan, and Alberta. The Nakota are associated with the broader Sioux Nation, also known as the Oceti Sakowin, and use a Siouan mother tongue. Their importance here lies in their documented linguistic identity, relationship to Dakota and Lakota peoples, and musical traditions involving voice, songs, and drumming.

## Music & performance

Sioux music prominently features the human voice, with songs accompanied by drumming. Among the Dakota, traditional dance songs generally begin in a high pitch, led by a single vocalist whose phrase is repeated by a group; the phrase then descends to a lower pitch before a brief pause, after which the second half echoes the first. The second part may include “honor beats,” usually four beats representing cannon fire in battle, and the entire song may be repeated several times at the discretion of the lead singer.

Many songs use vocables, syllabic utterances without lexical meaning. In some songs, only the second half has lyrics; another form is the Lakota lullaby, described as a calming hum. Women sometimes sing one octave above men, although they do not sing the first presentation of the song or the lead line.

Dakota percussion uses drums, sometimes with syncopation. In contest songs, including men’s traditional, chicken, and fancy songs, irregular opening beats are followed by a swift regular beat. The Lakota Flag Song begins special events such as powwows and is not accompanied by a dance. Other songs honor veterans, warriors, or others. Non-Powwow types include Sun dance, Yuwipi, Inipi, courtship, flute, lullaby, peyote, and Christian hymns.

A named Nakota group is the Chiniki Lake Singers. Other listed singers and groups include Fort Peck Sioux, identified as Fort Peck Assiniboine and Sioux, alongside Lakota and Dakota performers.

## Festivals & rituals

 They state that the Lakota Flag Song begins special events such as powwows and identify Sun dance, Yuwipi, and Inipi among non-Powwow types of Dakota songs. Since 2008, representatives of Assiniboine and Stoney tribes have attended annual “Lakota, Dakota, Nakota Language Summits,” sponsored by Tusweca Tiospaye, a Lakota non-profit organization promoting and strengthening the language.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They do document recent “pan-Sioux” attempts to revive the native languages, supported by Assiniboine-Stoney tribes and connected with the annual “Lakota, Dakota, Nakota Language Summits.”

## Language & religion

Nakota is the endonym used by the Assiniboine and their Canadian relatives, the Stoney. The broader Sioux nation is called the Oceti Sakowin, or Seven Council Fires, and has historically been classified into Lakota, Eastern Dakota, and Western Dakota groups. The Assiniboine separated from the Yankton-Yanktonai grouping around 1640. Linguistic research conducted in 1978 determined that Santee and Yankton-Yanktonai referred to themselves as Dakota, while Nakota or Nakoda was exclusive usage of the Assiniboine and Stoney. The Stoney describe their language as a Siouan mother tongue.
