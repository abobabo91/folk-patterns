## Overview

The Dakelh (['takʰɛɬ]), long called **Carrier** in English, are a First Nations people of the Central Interior of British Columbia, Canada. They also call themselves *Yinka Dene*, "the people on the land"; Babine-Witsuwitʼen-speaking bands prefer the equivalent *Yinka Whutʼen*. Their territories, *keyoh*, run along the Fraser River (*Lhtakoh*, "rivers within one another") from north of Prince George (*Lheidli*, "confluence") to south of Quesnel, through the Nechako Country and the lakes — Stuart (*Nak'albun*), Trembleur (*Dzinghubun*), Takla (*Khelhghubun*), Fraser (*Nadlehbun*) and Babine — into the Bulkley Valley and west to the Hazelton and Coast Mountains. The country is sub-boreal forest and lake, with continental winters and a short growing season. Dakelh and its sister language Babine-Witsuwitʼen belong to the Central British Columbia branch of Northern Athabaskan; the Dakelh are one of the Dene peoples (*Dené*, "people"). In folk-culture terms they are known for a salmon-and-berry seasonal round organised by family *keyoh*, and for the "grease trails" that carried their goods to the Pacific coast.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here — the Wikipedia articles and the catalogue records of the objects shown — document no Dakelh weaving, embroidery or dyeing tradition, so no textile entries can be given. The one container form they record is a container rather than a cloth:

**Birchbark berry basket** — a berry basket made of birchbark, bound with split root (and, in the collected example, synthetic cord), the vessel form attached to the summer berry harvest described below.

**Motif vocabulary.** Concentric circles — ten of them ringing each end of a decorated bone fishing cylinder, the only ornament described on any object in this set.

### Clothing & dress

The sources say only that clothing came from the hunt. Hunting and trapping of deer, caribou, moose, elk, black bear, beaver and rabbit supplied meat, fur for clothing, and bone for tools; before the fur trade, trapping of other fur-bearing animals was a minor activity. No garment types, head coverings, belts, footwear or ceremonial dress are named in the material available, and the catalogue records of the objects shown contain no clothing. One incidental word survives in the language description: */kekˈetɬˈu/*, "sock", a compound built on the stem */ke/*, "foot".

### Architecture

The sources do not describe Dakelh house forms, materials or built decoration. What they record of the settled landscape is administrative and territorial rather than architectural: *keyoh* denotes at once a territory, a village and a trapline, held under a hereditary chief known as a *keyoh* holder or *keyoh-whudachun*, and band names are formed by suffixing *-xwoten*, "people of", or *-t'en*, "people", to a village or place name — *Tl'azt'en*, *Wet'suwet'en*. Archaeology gives the only sense of settlement density: in 1951 Charles Edward Borden of the University of British Columbia and his student Wilson Duff located over 130 sites of importance to Cheslatta T'en history in the Nechako Canyon, ahead of the flooding of a large part of Dakelh hunting territory in Tweedsmuir Park by the Kemano reservoir.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The objects shown here are stone, bone, wood and bark — a tool kit for fishing, butchering and woodworking. Flaked and ground stone includes a triangular arrow-head, a leaf-shaped arrow-head with square feet, an ovate knife blade of black stone flaked on both faces, and an adze blade carved of stone, grooved, with longitudinal lateral ridges. Fishing gear includes a hollow bone cylinder plugged at both ends with wood and ringed with concentric circles, and a wooden netting-needle for making nets. Bark and root give the berry basket. Nothing in the sources indicates Dakelh ceramics or metalworking; the arrival of metal shows instead in the vocabulary, where *tɬʼuɬ* widened from "rope" to "wire", *kʼa* shifted from "arrow" to "cartridge", and *ʔəɬtih* from "bow" to "rifle".

## Festivals & rituals

No annual festival calendar appears in the sources; the year is described instead as a seasonal round, with the greatest activity in summer, when berries are gathered and fish are caught and preserved, and a more limited winter of some hunting, trapping and fishing under the ice. The one life-cycle rite the sources treat at length is a mortuary practice, and they treat it as contested. The received explanation of the Sekani name *aɣelne* (or *aɣele*, "people who carry things around on their backs"), of which English "Carrier" is a translation, is that it refers to a Dakelh custom in which a widow carried her husband's ashes on her back through the period of mourning. Against this, there is little evidence that the custom existed: the report of it comes from Father Adrien-Gabriel Morice, and according to Lizette Hall's father Louie-Billy Prince, who had been Morice's houseboy, Morice pressed the Dakelh so persistently about the name that they told him the ashes story to satisfy him. The alternative explanation is that the name records the packing of trade loads over the Grease Trails, which the Dakelh did and the Sekani did not. Graves also figure in recent history: when Alcan opened the spillway gate to Skin's Lake in 1957, Cheslatta graves were desecrated, a matter that came to public attention during the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples.

## Foodways

Fish is the mainstay, and the several varieties of salmon were smoked and stored for winter in large numbers. Hunted and trapped game — deer, caribou, moose, elk, black bear, beaver and rabbit — supplied meat. Plants played a relatively minor role as food, the exceptions being berries and the sap and cambium of the lodgepole pine; the berries of *Vaccinium vitis-idaea* are made into jam. Trade with the coast along the grease trails shaped the diet from the other direction. The Dakelh exported hides, dried meat and mats of dried berries, and imported marine goods, above all "grease", the oil rendered from eulachons ("candlefish") by letting them rot, adding boiling water and skimming off the oil — a very nutritious oil containing desirable fatty acids. Smoked eulachons and dried red laver seaweed came the same way, and grease and smoked eulachons are still counted delicacies and prized gifts from visitors from the west. Plants were used extensively as medicine: a decoction of the whole plant of *Viola adunca* for stomach pain, and, among Southern Carrier, a strong decoction of *Orthilia* root as an eyewash. Fish, game and berries still make up a major part of the diet.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources name no epic cycle, folktale genre or riddling tradition, and the indexed term "Gidete storytelling" appears in none of them. What they do document is the written record and the work of transmission. Dakelh was formerly written in the **Carrier syllabics** (also Déné Syllabics), a system inspired by Cree syllabics, widely used from its inception in 1885 but fading from the 1930s and little read today. Father Morice's idiosyncratic phonetic system carries a large body of scholarly material, a 'Little Catechism' and a 'Prayerbook'; Dakelh people themselves never wrote in it, though many learned to read the Prayerbook. The writing system in general use now is the **Carrier Linguistic Committee** orthography, Roman-based, developed in the 1960s by missionaries working with a group of Carrier people and designed for an English typewriter, using digraphs such as ⟨gh⟩ and ⟨lh⟩ and an apostrophe for glottalization. The Carrier Bible Translation Committee published a New Testament in 1995, with a Blackwater-dialect adaptation in 2002. Lizette Hall's *The Carrier, My People* (1992) and Sage Birchwater's Ulkatcho collections, including *Ulkatcho: Stories of the Grease Trail*, are the community-authored books the sources list.

## Language & religion

Dakelh (ᑕᗸᒡ) is a Northern Athabaskan language of the central British Columbia subgroup, alongside Babine-Witsuwitʼen and Tŝilhqot'in. Current research divides it into a Stuart-Trembleur Lake group and a Southern group, the latter splitting into a Fraser-Nechako subgroup (Cheslatta, Stellako, Nadleh, Saik'uz, Lheidli) and a Blackwater subgroup (Lhk'acho, *Lhoosk'uz*, Nazko, *Lhtakoh*); all varieties are mutually intelligible, though they differ in phonology, lexicon, morphology and even syntax. The verb is extremely complex, a single verb having hundreds of thousands of forms, and gender classifiers mark whether the absolutive argument is round, stick-like or areal. Loanwords track contact: *maj* "berry" from Gitksan, *məsdus* "cow" and *sunija* "money" from Cree, *tʃikəmin* "money" and *daji* "chief" from Chinook Jargon, *liɡok* and *lisel* from French. UNESCO rates Carrier/Dakelh "Severely Endangered"; about 10% of Dakelh people speak it, hardly any of them children, against an estimate of some 10,000 speakers in 1979. The Yinka Dene Language Institute, founded in 1988, handles research, archiving, curriculum and teacher training, and mentor-apprentice programs and language nests run in several communities. On religion the sources are indirect: they record Catholic missionary activity and Bible translation, a shaman term *dəjən*, and that the sacredness of plants is appreciated by Dakelh people.
