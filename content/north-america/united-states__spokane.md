## Overview

The Spokane are an Indigenous people of the Plateau whose traditional territory was near the Spokane River in present-day eastern Washington and northern Idaho. The Spokane Reservation lies in eastern Washington between the Columbia and Spokane rivers and includes about 237 square miles. In 2019, the tribe reported a population of around 2,900 people. The Spokane are enrolled in the Spokane Tribe of Indians, headquartered in Wellpinit, Washington. Their traditional language is Salish, an Interior Salishan language, and they speak the Spokane dialect. Spokane communities historically followed game and plants for nine months of the year and lived in permanent winter villages for the other three. Their folk culture is documented through seasonal subsistence, fishing, plant gathering, baskets, bags, mats, spiritual dances, stories, and the continued teaching of the language.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Spokane women made coiled baskets out of birch bark or cedar roots. They made wallets and bags from strips of processed animal hide and also made mats and other objects. These goods were sometimes exchanged with other Native peoples, white traders, and settlers.

### Clothing & dress

 They state that women made bags from processed animal hide and made mats and other objects, while men created tools, fished, and hunted. The sources also mention that horses were used to carry or pull supplies and that men rode them during hunting and warfare.

### Architecture

 They state that Spokane people lived in permanent winter villages for three months of the year and followed game and plants during the other nine months.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They identify bows and arrows used by individual hunters, digging sticks used by women to uproot and gather food, coiled baskets, wallets, bags, mats, and tools. A young girl’s first digging stick marked a rite of passage, and women’s graves were often marked with these sticks.

## Music & performance

The sources describe spiritual dances. The Dreamer Cult, also called Washani, developed among Columbia Plateau tribes during the second half of the nineteenth century as a mixture of traditional spirituality and aspects of Christianity. The sources describe a spiritual world organized around Winter dances, personal vision quests, seasonal feasts, and the acquisition of guardian spirit powers.

The **Prophet Dance** and **Spirit Dance** were spiritual dances that took place in mid-January. Dancers sought to identify with the Prophet’s spirit. In the Spirit Dance, a shaman called upon the spirit to visit an individual.

The Dreamer prophets rejected non-Native culture and belief systems and advocated a return to traditional ways of life. The Dreamer Cult remained prominent among Columbia Plateau peoples until the early 1890s, when major prophets died and followers began to lose faith in the promise of a world free of white people. The closest contemporary religion to Washani is identified in the source as the Seven Drums Religion.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name the **Prophet Dance** and the **Spirit Dance**, both associated with the Dreamer Cult and performed in mid-January. Dancers sought identification with the Prophet’s spirit, while in the Spirit Dance a shaman called upon the spirit to visit an individual.

## Festivals & rituals

 They describe seasonal feasts connected to the annual subsistence cycle and the acquisition of guardian spirit powers.

Spokane life followed a seasonal pattern. The bands moved with game and plants for nine months of the year and settled in permanent winter villages for the other three. Fish, especially salmon, were central to diet and trade; fish were smoked or dried for exchange and winter storage. Women gathered camas roots, berries, and barks with digging sticks. The first digging stick given to a young girl was a rite of passage, and women’s graves were often marked with digging sticks. They state that the Spokane had a matrilocal custom: after marriage, a husband generally joined his wife and her people at their home, although sometimes the wife moved to the husband’s people.

## Foodways

The Spokane diet consisted of fish, local game, and plants, including nuts and roots. Salmon was an important food and a major part of the trade economy. The Spokane also ate trout and whitefish. Fish were smoked or dried for trade or for storage during winter, and fish eyes were considered delicacies.

Men hunted whitetail deer and mule deer, which provided protein and other nutrients during winter. Individual hunters tracked deer and killed them with a bow and arrow. Women gathered plants that supplied nearly half of the tribe’s caloric intake. These plants included camas roots, local berries, and barks. Women used digging sticks to uproot and gather food.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources name two Spokane stories: **“Spokane Lake of Long Ago,”** told by Chief Lot, and **“The Origin of the Spokane River.”**

The sources connect Spokane spirituality with personal vision quests, seasonal feasts, Winter dances, prophets, and guardian spirit powers. A vision attributed to the prophet Smohalla foresaw the disappearance of white people, the resurrection of Indian dead, and the restoration of the world to a pristine state. The transformation was described as requiring no violence, while Dreamers were instructed to follow the Creator’s teachings conveyed through prophets.

The sources document contemporary language-preservation efforts, including the Spokane Language Program, a Spokane Salish blog, the Learn Spokane Salish Picture Dictionary, and other Salish teaching resources.

## Language & religion

Spokane traditional language is Salish, an Interior Salishan language spoken from Washington to Montana. The Spokane dialect is also called Npoqínišcn in the language source. Salish is polysynthetic, and Spokane words are typically based on roots with many affixes. The language source states that Salish was spoken by about 50 elders of the Spokane Indian Reservation as of 2000 and was considered critically endangered in Montana and Idaho according to UNESCO as of 2012.

The Spokane name in Salish is *snxʷmeneʔ*, meaning “steelhead.” The Spokane dialect has a SUCCESS aspect morpheme, *-nu*, and the morphemes *-nt* and *-el'* mark transitivity and intransitivity. Salish language courses, immersion schools, teacher training, dictionaries, children’s books, language CDs, and online learning resources are documented in the sources.

The Dreamer Cult, or Washani, combined traditional spirituality with aspects of Christianity. The sources also mention Prophet Dance, Spirit Dance, vision quests, seasonal feasts, prophets, shamans, and the Seven Drums Religion.
