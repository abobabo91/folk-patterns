## Overview

The Crow, whose autonym is *Apsáalooke* — "children of the large-beaked bird" — are a Plains people of southern Montana. Their historical country was the Yellowstone River valley, called *E-chee-dick-karsh-ah-shay* ("Elk River"), running from present-day Wyoming through Montana into North Dakota, together with the Bighorn, Pryor, Wolf and Absaroka mountain ranges and the valleys of the Judith, Powder, Tongue, Big Horn and Wind rivers. Today they hold the Crow Indian Reservation south of Billings, with tribal headquarters at Crow Agency and their own institution, Little Big Horn College. Of some 14,000 enrolled citizens, an estimated 3,000 spoke the Crow language in 2007. The language belongs to the Missouri Valley branch of Siouan. In folk-culture terms the Crow matter as horse-breeding bison hunters whose beadwork, painted hide and ceremonial life — the Sun Dance, the Tobacco Society, the Crow Fair — are documented in museum collections and in a continuing reservation practice.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The catalogue records of the objects shown here identify materials and forms rather than named textile traditions, so the entries below are described as the museums describe them.

**Elk-tooth dress** — A woman's dress of blue felted wool, decorated with elk teeth and with red wool cloth at the neck, wrist and hem. The elk teeth are applied to the body of the garment as its principal ornament.

**Beadwork on cloth and skin** — Glass beadwork worked onto trade cloth and native-tanned skin appears across the collection: men's leggings of blue cotton or wool with coloured glass beadwork, beaded cuffs for men's leggings, beaded moccasins, and a blanket strip of native-tanned skin and glass beads. A deerskin paint bag, folded in half and sewn up one side with a thong through the top, carries glass beadwork floral designs on both faces.

**Painted bison robe** — A robe of buffalo hide painted with images of horses and riders.

**Quillwork** — Porcupine quill is recorded on a man's shirt of native-tanned skin (with factory-woven cloth, glass beads, horsehair, wool yarn and ermine) and on an armlet of hide and quills.

**Parfleche** — A bag of rawhide; the museum record names it simply as a parfleche bag made of hide.

The sources name no motifs beyond the floral designs on the beaded paint bag and the horses and riders painted on the bison robe.

### Clothing & dress

Men's dress in the collection centres on the skin shirt and the legging. A shirt of about 1860 combines native-tanned skin with factory-woven cloth, porcupine quill, glass beads, horsehair, wool yarn and ermine — trade materials worked into an older garment form. Leggings survive in several versions: blue cotton sewn with composite blue cloth and coloured glass beadwork, finished with skin tassels and metal bells; a fringed pair of skin, beads and grass; and separate beaded cuffs made for men's leggings. Moccasins of skin and beads are the recorded footwear. Women wore the blue felted wool dress ornamented with elk teeth and red cloth. A girl's dress of cotton is catalogued specifically for the tobacco dance ceremony, marking a garment made for ritual rather than daily use. Photographs from the Yellowstone valley show men in cloth shirts with a wrap at the waist, a wool blanket over one shoulder and moccasins; a studio portrait of the Crow man Lone Wolf, taken at Miles City, shows him in a blanket with a shell choker and feathers worn in hair dressed in two braids.

### Architecture

The Crow lived in tipis. A photograph in the British Museum album shows Crow people sitting inside a hide tipi opened along one side, with a second, fully enclosed tipi behind them and open plain beyond; another shows a camp being packed up, the tents coming down with a horse standing beside them. Before this, in the earlier Woodland and Upper Missouri phases of their history, the Crow and the related Hidatsa lived in settled villages of earth lodges and grew crops; the group that moved west from the Knife River and Heart River villages around 1675–1700 chose a site on the lower Yellowstone for a single earth lodge, though most families there lived in tipis or other perishable dwellings. Archaeologists know that "proto-Crow" place as the Hagen site. Two ceremonial structures are also built rather than dwelt in: the Sun Dance lodge or *Ashé Isée*, a forked central pole encircled by twelve cottonwood posts some forty feet out with the doorway east, and the sweat lodge or "Little Lodge", twelve poles with an east-facing door and a two-foot-square pit at the centre for heated rocks.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Horn and antler are the materials of the eating vessels recorded here: a spoon of buffalo horn, a spoon of bighorn sheep horn and a spoon of moose antler. A dish is made of wood with beaver skin. Storage and transport are represented by the rawhide parfleche bag and by the folded deerskin paint bag. A mace of stone, wood and hide is listed without further description. Ritual objects of everyday materials include a Sun Dance rattle made of gourd, a medicine whip of animal tail, hide and wood, and a medicine rope of buffalo and horse hair with wool cloth and beads. One photograph shows the Crow chief Black Foot (Kam-ne-but-se) holding a tomahawk. The sources record no Crow ceramic tradition.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment in the collection is dominated by medicine necklaces. One is made of horse teeth, beads, claws and a leather cord; another of feathers, horse hair, stone and leather cord. An armlet of hide and quills survives as one of a pair. The studio photograph of Lone Wolf records a shell choker worn with a blanket, feathers set in the hair, and hair worn in two braids. These necklaces belong to the class of objects Crow religion calls *Xapáaliia* — the physical manifestation of *Baaxpée*, sacred power, made by a vision-quester to represent the spirit patron who granted it. A *Xapáaliia* can be almost anything the owner judges sufficient: a man whose patron was an eagle might take an eagle feather. Unlike the vision itself, which cannot be transferred, the object is passed on — often to family members at death, or given to a Crow who has received no vision of their own.

## Music & performance

The sources name no Crow instruments apart from those used inside ceremony: the gourd rattle catalogued as a Sun Dance rattle, the eagle-bone whistles blown by dancers during the Sunrise Ceremony, and the drums. Drummers are central to the Sun Dance rather than accompanying it: regular rhythms help a dancer sustain the effort, irregular beating exhausts them, and when the drummers see a dancer entering a vision they strike harder to bring on the "hard fall". The Drum chief prays for the dancers with the aid of tobacco smoke. One named song survives in the sources — the Sunrise song, sung at dawn facing east as the first rays appear over the horizon, while participants dance, pray and blow the bone whistles to absorb the power of the sun. The sponsor of a Sun Dance is responsible for leading the key rituals and songs, which makes the repertoire part of the sponsor's obligation rather than a specialist performer's.

## Dance & theatre

Two dances appear in the sources. The **Sun Dance** (*aškišširissu-a*) is the major public ceremony, danced for three days in the Big Lodge: dancers fast, keep their eyes fixed on an idol on the central pole, and dance until they collapse into a vision, at which point others carry them out of the centre until the vision runs its course. First-day dress is plain, to show humility; on the second day dancers put on bright clothes, face paint and their strongest medicines for what is understood as spiritual warfare. The ceremony was banned on the reservation in 1887 and revived in 1941 by William Big Day after he attended a Shoshone Sun Dance; by the 1990s an average of five were held each summer, some blending Shoshone and Teton-Sioux forms. The **tobacco dance** is attested by a girl's cotton dress made for the ceremony. The sources record no Crow theatrical tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The **Crow Fair** began in 1918, when the Crow organised a gathering to display their culture and invited members of other tribes. It is now held yearly on the third weekend of August, with wide participation from other tribes. The **Sun Dance** is the summer ceremony, sponsored by an individual of repute who wants a particular blessing or who has been instructed to dance by a vision; the sponsor gathers the materials, has the forked tree cut with prayer, and sets the agenda. Sap running from the stump is a good omen, and Crows bless themselves with it. The **sweat lodge** precedes it, purifying body and soul; the first four rocks are laid in a cross for the four directions and the circle of life, and the ceremony runs in four quarters of four, seven, ten and an uncounted "quarter of a million" dips of water. The **vision quest**, *bilisshíissanne* — "to fast from water" — sends an individual alone to a prominent place, often a peak in the Wolf Mountains, to go without food and water for two or three days; self-mortification, most commonly the removal of a finger, could accompany it. The **Tobacco Society**, the *Bacu'sua*, cultivates the sacred plant and keeps its rites correct.

## Foodways

The American bison was the main food. Before horses, hunters stalked close on foot, often disguised in a wolf pelt, then ran the animals down and killed them with arrows or lances; with horses, riders panicked the herd into a stampede and shot or lanced the targeted animals from horseback. The Crow also hunted bighorn sheep, mountain goats, deer, elk, bear and other game. Buffalo meat was roasted or boiled in a stew with prairie turnips, and the rump, tongue, liver, heart and kidneys were all considered delicacies. Dried bison meat was ground with fat and berries to make *pemmican*. Gathered wild foods included elderberries and wild turnip. In their earlier villages south of Lake Winnipeg the Crow grew crops alongside bison hunting, and the group that left the Hidatsa villages on the Knife River abandoned adjacent cornfields in doing so. The horn and antler spoons and the wood-and-beaver-skin dish in this collection are the serving equipment of that diet. Tobacco is not a food but is described by Crows themselves as their "means of living".

## Oral tradition & literature

Crow narrative is preserved chiefly as creation story and sacred history. In one account **Old Man Coyote** — one of the Creator's names, alongside *Akbaatatdia* ("One Who Has Made Everything") and *Iichíkbaalee* ("First Doer") — is alone on a great ocean when he meets two male ducks; a duck dives and brings up a root, dives again and brings up mud, and from that soil Old Man Coyote builds an island and then all the lands, uses the root to make plants and trees, shapes rivers and mountains into the too-flat earth, and moulds Man and then Woman from clay. The story of the **Seven Bison** and their human companion, who defeats the armoured bison Bones Together by turning into a feather, explains the origin of the sweat lodge and ends with the bison ascending to become the Big Dipper and the man the Little Dipper. Tribal history is also carried in migration accounts — No Intestines' vision and the long search for sacred tobacco, No Vitals' discovery of the plant at Devils Lake — and in the winter counts of neighbouring peoples that record wars with the Crow. Language work includes a Crow dictionary, portions of the Bible published 1980–2007, a language immersion school sponsored in 2012 and the tribe's Apsaalooke language camp.

## Language & religion

Crow (*Apsáalooke*) is a Missouri Valley Siouan language, related only to Hidatsa; the two split perhaps 300 to 800 years ago and are not mutually intelligible. Dialect difference between River Crow and Mountain Crow speech is slight. UNESCO classified the language as definitely endangered in 2012, and 80% of the tribe prefers English, but R. Graczyk's *A Grammar of Crow* (2007) records fluent speakers of all ages and some children still acquiring it first. Crow religion centres on a single omnipotent Creator and a universe of three worlds — physical, spirit, and the world where God alone lives. Sacred power, *Baaxpée*, comes from a spirit patron, an *Iilápxe*, and is embodied in a *Xapáaliia*. Doing ceremony properly means doing it with *diakaashe*, "he really did it". Catholic Jesuits founded the St. Xavier mission in the Bighorn Valley in 1887 and a school in October 1888; Crow religion today includes both traditions, and Crow tolerance of multiple paths is expressed in Thomas Yellowtail's image of a wagon wheel whose every spoke reaches the same central beam.
