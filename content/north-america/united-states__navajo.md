## Overview

The Diné call themselves 'the people' and live on a vast reservation at the Four Corners with a living culture built largely after Spanish sheep and horses arrived. Their wool weaving, silverwork, and dry painting are among the most continuously practiced Indigenous craft traditions in North America. The ceremonial system is complex and living, built around chantways that can last up to nine nights and requiring specialized knowledge passed from singers to apprentices.

## Material culture

Diné material culture is shaped by a matrilineal economy where women owned the flocks and by the arrival of Spanish wool, metal, and trade goods that transformed older Pueblo-learned techniques into distinctive forms.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Diné weaving on upright looms uses handspun wool built into designs as the weaver works from bottom to top, with distinctive regional styles recognized by name.

- **Chief's blanket** — The oldest Diné wearing-blanket form, wider than long, with First, Second, and Third Phase variants distinguished by bands and diamond patterns.
- **Eye Dazzler** — Post-1868 style using commercial Germantown yarns in bright aniline colors massed into vibrating zigzags and diamonds.
- **Two Grey Hills** — Fine natural undyed wool in cream, grey, tan, and brown with dense concentric diamonds and a black border, reaching tapestry-grade thread counts.
- **Ganado red** — Saturated deep red ground with black border and white figuring, usually built around a central diamond and associated with Hubbell Trading Post.
- **Storm Pattern** — A central rectangle representing a hogan connected by zigzag lightning lines to four corner boxes representing the sacred mountains.

### Clothing & dress

The defining woman's garment is the biil, a two-panel dress that was largely displaced by the velveteen blouse and tiered skirt now worn as ceremonial dress.

- **Biil** — A two-panel dress made of two small twill-woven blankets in dark brown or black with deep red banded ends, belted with a woven sash.
- **Navajo velvet blouse** — A crushed velveteen blouse heavy with silver buttons, now the emblematic ceremonial top paired with a long tiered broomstick skirt.
- **Tsiiyeel** — An hourglass-shaped hair bundle tied with white wool cord, the single most legible marker of traditional Diné identity for both men and women.
- **Concha belt** — A row of oval stamped or repoussé silver plates originally slotted for a leather strap, worn by men as formal dress.
- **Pendleton trade cloth** — Commercial blankets acquired from the 1890s onward that became and remain formal wear for men and gifts at every life passage.

### Architecture

The hogan is the Diné dwelling built to a cosmological plan with the door facing east, including both conical male and round female forms.

- **Male hogan** (*ʼalchʼįʼ adeezʼá*) — A conical or forked-pole structure built on three or five leaning timbers with a distinct projecting rectangular entryway.
- **Female hogan** (*hooghan nimazí*) — A round-to-octagonal form built from about 1900 as a cribbed six- or eight-sided log structure, corbelled inward to a domed roof with a central smokehole.
- **Blessingway hogan narrative** (*hózhǫ́ǫ́jí*) — The Blessingway account tells of the first hogan built by Coyote with logs and instruction from the Beaver People as a house for First Man and First Woman.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Diné pottery is deliberately plain and utilitarian, coated with melted piñon pitch; silversmithing learned from Mexican plateros is the great metal tradition.

- **Diné cooking jar** (*Diné bizaad*) — Undecorated coiled pottery, thin-walled and fired in dung, then coated while hot with melted piñon pitch for a dark amber gloss.
- **Wedding basket** (*tsʼaaʼ*) — A coiled sumac basket with a stepped red-and-black band and a deliberate gap in the design, used as a container for cornmeal in nearly every ceremony.
- **Navajo silver work** — Sandcast and stamped silverwork learned from Mexican plateros around the 1850s, with dies cut from files.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Diné silver-and-turquoise jewelry is the most recognizable Native American jewelry in the world, with named forms derived from Spanish and Moorish sources.

- **Squash blossom necklace** — Hollow silver beads interrupted by fluted blossom units derived from the Spanish-Mexican pomegranate, closing in a crescent naja pendant of Moorish origin.
- **Concha belt** — A row of oval stamped or repoussé silver plates originally slotted for a leather strap, later joined by openwork butterfly spacers.
- **Ketoh** — A leather bowguard faced with a cast silver plaque, archery equipment converted into formal male ornament.
- **Navajo Pearls** — Hand-formed, hand-stamped hollow silver beads, each seamed and polished, strung plain as necklaces.

## Music & performance

Diné music is overwhelmingly vocal and ceremonial, centered on the hatááł, a fixed chantway of hundreds of songs performed across multiple nights by a singer.

- **Blessingway** (*hózhǫ́ǫ́jí*) — The backbone rite of blessing and protection that can be held any time and appears across many life-cycle ceremonies.
- **Nightway** (*Tłʼééʼjí*) — The nine-night winter healing ceremony with a public climax of masked Yeibichai dances and requiring hoof rattles and bullroarer.
- **Enemyway** (*Anaʼí Ndááʼ*) — A ceremony for those who have contacted death or foreigners, including returning soldiers, culminating in the Circle Dance and Skip Dance.
- **Water drum** — Hide stretched over a pottery or hide vessel, the minimal accompanying percussion used in ceremonial singing.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is a component of ceremony rather than an independent art, with the public climax of Nightway being the Yeibichai, danced by masked men impersonating the Holy People.

- **Yeibichai** — Teams of masked men impersonating the Yeʼii (Holy People) dancing on the final night of Nightway with a stamping, precisely synchronized step, led by Talking God.
- **Fire Dance** — The final night of Mountainway, in which bundles of burning cedar bark are carried against bare skin.
- **Circle Dance** — An openly social dance at Enemyway where unrelated young men and women dance together in unison lines.
- **Skip Dance** — A partner-choice social dance at Enemyway where a girl chooses a partner and can demand a small payment, a source of humor.

## Festivals & rituals

The ceremonial calendar is keyed to season rather than date, with great chantways held only after the first killing frost and before spring thaw.

- **First Laugh ceremony** (*Chʼídeedlóóh*) — A rite triggered when a baby first laughs, obliging the person who caused it to host a gathering distributing rock salt and food.
- **Kinaaldá** — A four-day Blessingway-based rite for a girl's first menstruation, in which she is dressed in biil and full jewelry and runs eastward each dawn.
- **Navajo Nation Fair** — The largest contemporary public gathering, held each September at Window Rock with rodeo, song-and-dance, agricultural competition, and Miss Navajo Nation pageant.

## Foodways

Farming of the Three Sisters (corn, beans, squash) came from the Pueblos, with corn the ritual center; mutton from Spanish sheep is the other pillar.

- **ʼalkaan** — A pit-baked ceremonial corn cake sweetened with sprouted-wheat flour, baked overnight in a pit lined with cornhusks and cut for the whole community at dawn.
- **Kneel-down bread** (*nitsidigoʼí*) — Fresh corn ground on a metate, wrapped in its own husks and baked in an earth pit.
- **Blue corn mush** (*tanaashgiizh*) — Stirred with juniper ash for its alkali and its mineral tang.
- **Corn pollen** (*tádídíín*) — Carried in a pouch and used as the essential offering and blessing medium in every rite.
- **Navajo taco** — Fry bread under beans, mutton or beef, chile and lettuce, a post-Bosque-Redondo food built from government-issue flour, lard and salt.

## Oral tradition & literature

The foundational narrative is the Diné Bahaneʼ, the emergence account of passage through four worlds, with chantway songs as episodes of it.

- **Diné Bahaneʼ** — The emergence account describing passage through the Dark World, Blue World, Yellow World, and into the Glittering World where death and seasons came into being.
- **Changing Woman** (*Asdzą́ą́ Nádleehé*) — The central figure who gave the original four clans and appears in initiation rites as the prototype for girls becoming women.
- **Monster Slayer and Born for Water** — The Hero Twins who cleared the world of monsters in the foundational narrative.
- **Coyote** (*Mąʼii*) — The trickster figure whose story cycle is told only in winter and occupies the comic register of Diné narrative.

## Language & religion

Diné bizaad is Southern Athabaskan, tonal, with verb morphology built on classificatory stems; the ceremonial system turns on hózhǫ́ (beauty, order, and balance).

- **Diné bizaad** — Southern Athabaskan language with two mutually intelligible geographic dialects, tonal with four vowel qualities, and a Latin-based orthography dating from the 1930s.
- **Hózhǫ́** — Beauty, order, and balance, the central concept of the ceremonial system; illness disrupts it and the chantways restore it.
- **Navajo Sign Language** — A dialect or daughter of Plains Sign Talk also used among the Diné.
