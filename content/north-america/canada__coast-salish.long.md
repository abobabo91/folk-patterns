## Overview

The Coast Salish are a large, loose grouping of ethnically and linguistically related Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast, living in British Columbia and the U.S. states of Washington and Oregon. Their territories run from the northern limit of the Salish Sea along the inside of Vancouver Island, cover most of southern Vancouver Island, all of the Lower Mainland, and most of Puget Sound and the Olympic Peninsula; the Tillamook or Nehalem around Tillamook, Oregon are the southernmost. These homelands coincide with the modern metropolitan areas of Victoria, Vancouver and Seattle. The term covers speakers of fourteen contiguous Salishan languages, sometimes extended to Tillamook and Nuxalk. A 2012 estimate put the population at a minimum of 56,590: 28,406 Status Indians registered to Coast Salish bands in British Columbia and 28,284 enrolled members of federally recognized tribes in Washington State. In folk-culture terms they matter for a weaving and carving complex — wool robes, spindle whorls, imbricated basketry, house posts — that is deliberately more minimalist than the art of their northern neighbours.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Coast Salish art divides into two major forms: flat design and carving, and basketry and weaving. Historically these followed gendered roles, men making figurative sculpture and painting depicting crests, shamanic beings and spirits, women producing baskets and textiles most often decorated with abstract designs.

**Mountain-goat and woolly-dog wool weaving** — Wool from the mountain goat and from the now-extinct Salish Woolly Dog was used for woven mats, blankets, clothing and robes. The wool was mixed with a diatomaceous earth that removed oils and added a white colour, then wetted and twisted between palm and thigh into a loose strand before spinning. Weaving was done on a loom of two upright bars carrying two horizontal rollers; some loom poles were themselves carved with supernatural characters or family history. Smaller textiles were functional, while many larger robes served as indicators of wealth.

**Spindle whorl** — Whorls were set on the shafts of wooden spindles to spin the loose strands. Some are plain; others carry elaborate designs and depicted beings, and the whorl is one of the principal supports of the flat-design idiom alongside house posts, welcome figures, combs, bentwood boxes and canoes.

**Imbricated coiled slat basketry** — The best-documented pattern-bearing form in the objects shown here: circular trays and rectangular slat baskets coiled from cedar or hemlock roots, decorated with imbricated designs in plain and black-dyed prunus bark, sometimes finished with an undulating decorative edge. Preparation tools included specially designed combs; a separate tool pushed the weft during weaving.

**Cedar-bark work** — Cedar bark was both a basketry fibre and a sheet material. A basket in the Museum of Ethnography collection is made of red cedar-bark fibre with a handle wrapped in a decorative band of probably red-dyed cedar-bark fibre; the same collection holds an *öskar*, a cedar-bark bailer of a type used to clear unwanted water from canoes on rivers, lakes and the sea.

**Carex twine and reed matting** — Coast Salish people use over 100 species of plants, among them the leaves of *Carex* for baskets and twine. Woven reed mats were made into the mattresses and cushions of the longhouse.

**Motif vocabulary.** Imbrication in plain and black-dyed prunus bark (the standard basketry surface pattern); crest figures, shamanic beings and spirits in men's figurative work; abstract design in women's weaving and basketry; animal features worked onto bowl surfaces, extended on more elaborate pieces into head, body, wings and limbs; the seated human figure intertwined with animals; and on a mountain-goat horn bracelet, groups of four or five scratched parallel lines separated by lines of three circles.

### Clothing & dress

Wool from mountain goat and woolly dog supplied blankets, clothing and robes, the larger robes doubling as public statements of wealth. A photographic postcard of three Northwest Coast men shows the Coast Salish sitter in a cloth cap with a feather, wearing a blanket over a shirt and a second blanket around the waist with leggings and footwear, while his Haida and Tlingit companions wear carved frontlet headdresses with shell, ermine tails and sea-lion whiskers — a useful contrast in ceremonial headgear. Trade blankets and ornamentation were among the luxuries Coast Salish traders took from the Hudson's Bay Company posts. Ceremonial dress for the *sxwayxwey* mask society is distinct from daily wear: regalia covered with feathers, and leggings hung with hoof rattles. Nineteenth-century studio and field photographs show the Euro-American clothing of the period — the Songhees chief James Sqwameyuks in jacket, floral-patterned waistcoat, shirt and trousers; a Sechelt woman in a shirt and long skirt, her child in a cap, long-sleeved top, trousers, socks and shoes. Many Coast Salish mothers shaped the heads of free-born babies, binding them with cradle boards long enough to produce a steeply sloping forehead; a British Museum model of a cradle demonstrates the stages of the practice.

### Architecture

Villages consisted of shed-roofed cedar longhouses built of western red cedar split planks and beams over an earthen floor, housing forty or more people, usually a related extended family. Interior walls were lined with sleeping platforms standing three or four feet above the ground, with storage shelves above them for baskets, tools and clothing and firewood stowed beneath; food hung drying from the ceiling, partitions separated families, and interior fires vented through roof slats used as chimneys. Large beams at the sides, called house posts, were carved or painted with ancestors, family history or supernatural beings. The wealthy built on an extraordinary scale: Simon Fraser described a Sto:lo house 640 by 60 feet, a Squamish longhouse measured 200 by 60 feet and held eleven families numbering about a hundred people, and the Suquamish Oleman House (Old Man House) at what became the Port Madison Reservation ran 500 by 40–60 feet around 1850. The gambrel roof was unique to Puget Sound Coast Salish. Pit-houses, called *kekuli* in Chinook Jargon, were also used. Villages stood near navigable water for dugout canoe transport, sometimes stretching for miles along a river. In the Fraser Canyon, boulder walls were built in the fifteenth century; these Salish Defensive Sites, one excavated by Kisha Supernant at Yale, British Columbia in 2008, may have served for defence, as fishing platforms and as house terraces.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources describe no ceramic or metalworking tradition; the everyday repertoire is wood, bark, fibre and horn. Men carved house posts, grave monuments, masks and ritual paraphernalia including rattles — sheets of mountain-goat horn bent and sewn into volumetric triangles, originally hung with strands of mountain-goat wool. The same flat-design idiom carries onto combs (one British Museum comb is of wood, possibly yew), bentwood boxes, welcome figures and canoes. Carved bowls range from simple forms with animal features to the complex seated human figure bowl; one seated human figure bowl was used in a female puberty ritual in Secwépemc territory and was believed to aid women in giving birth. Northwest Coast groups have worked wood and fibre for over 10,500 years, and waterlogged wet sites preserve the perishable end of the kit: wedges, fishhooks, basketry, cordage and nets. Photographs of the Fraser River show salmon caches raised on stilts, large wooden drying racks, long salmon spears and dip nets on poles.

## Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment is thinly documented in these sources. The one object shown is a bracelet cut as a thin length of mountain-goat horn, its shouldered end tapering to a pointed hitch that slots into a hole in the other end, decorated with four groups of roughly scratched parallel lines and two lines of three circles each, and worn or polished very smooth inside and out. Ornamentation was among the luxury categories Coast Salish traders sought from European ships. The most consequential body practice recorded is not jewellery but head shaping: the careful binding of free-born infants' heads with cradle boards to produce a sloping forehead, a visible mark of rank in a society divided into upper class, lower class and slaves.

## Music & performance

Performance among the Coast Salish is bound to spirit power. Coast Salish people hold that through dances, masks and ceremonies they express the spiritual powers given to them, and that those powers define a community's success in leadership, bravery, healing or artistry. Spirit dancing ceremonies are common winter gatherings at which members show their powers through song and dance; people often come together on the longhouse floor, where each member's power is their own alone to display in various songs, and the powers sought individually through trials of isolation attach to spirit animals such as raven, woodpecker, bear or seal. In the central Coast Salish region the *sxwayxwey* mask ceremony is central: men from families holding the hereditary right are initiated into the society, wear the mask and dance, joined by women singers and a special song. Rattles of bent and sewn mountain-goat horn, and leggings strung with hoof rattles, are the percussion named in the sources. The potlatch, the great ceremonial display of wealth, was banned in Canada in 1885 and in the United States some years later; suppression ended in 1934 in the US and in 1951 in Canada, after which some potlatching became overt immediately. Gambling on *slahal*, a sleight-of-hand game, accompanied athletic contests as recreation.

## Dance & theatre

Two named dance traditions appear in the sources. The *sxwayxwey* (also *Sx̱wáýx̱way* or *Skwayskway*) mask dance is performed by hereditarily entitled men of the central Coast Salish, in masks with bulging cylindrical eyeballs, "horns" rendered as animal heads, and drooping tongues, crowned dynamically with large feathers; dancers wear feather-covered regalia and hoof-rattle leggings, and women sing. Winter spirit dancing, by contrast, is the collective but individually owned display of acquired spirit powers on the longhouse floor through song and dance. The sources used do not describe a theatrical or puppet tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual year turns on the winter spirit dances and on the potlatch, the public display of wealth — wealth here including land, food resources, household items and adornments, which could not buy status or power but could enhance it, and which was never meant to be hidden. Individual ritual life began with the vision quest: journeys involving other states of consciousness, varied and widely practised, through which guardian spirits were sought. Belief in guardian spirits and in shapeshifting between human and animal was widely shared, and conceptions of the soul and of the lands of the living and the dead were complex and mutable. The Duwamish had a soul recovery and journey ceremony. Of the people recorded near Port Townsend, shamans held that every being has five spiritual components — body, inner and outer soul, life force and ghost — that illness follows the soul's removal from the body and death follows its arrival in the underworld, and that the shaman's work is to travel to the underworld and recover the soul in transit. The journey there was described as a two-day walk along a trail through bushes and past a lake to a river-divided valley, and the afterlife closely resembled the life lived, with work, hunting and family. Spirits appeared only at night, so people travelled by day and stayed close to others. Life-cycle rites in the sources include the female puberty ritual in which a seated figure bowl was used, and the head-shaping of free-born infants.

## Foodways

Salmon was the dietary staple, supplemented by a rich variety of other seafoods and forage, especially among the southern Coast Salish in their more temperate territories. Shellfish were abundant; butter clams, horse clams and cockles were dried for trade. Kokanee, a freshwater fish, was taken in the Lake Washington and Lake Sammamish watersheds. Land management was deliberate: forest gardens on the northwest coast of Canada held crabapple, hazelnut, cranberry, wild plum and wild cherry, and there is documentation of the cultivation of great camas, Indian carrot and Columbia lily. Anthropogenic grasslands were maintained for game habitat and for vegetable sprouts, roots, bulbs, berries and nuts; the most important plant foods were probably bracken and camas, and wapato especially for the Duwamish. Many varieties of berries were gathered, some with comblike devices not reported elsewhere; salal is the source of multiple tinctures and teas, and its berries are often eaten during feasts. Acorns were relished but not widely available, and regional tribes travelled in autumn to the Nisqually Flats to harvest them. Hunting was specialized into what were probably the professions of sea hunter, land hunter and fowler, with waterfowl taken on moonless nights using strategic flares. The Muckleshoot Seasonal Rounds set the calendar: salmon on the river banks in January; salmonberry sprouts eaten with salmon eggs by May, while men hunted deer and elk and women gathered camas on the prairies and clams on the beaches; steelhead and king salmon in mass along the rivers in summer, with berries abundant in the forests.

## Oral tradition & literature

*Legends of Vancouver*, published in 1911 by the Canadian author E. Pauline Johnson (Tekahionwake) and now available online from the UPenn Digital Library, is a collection of Coast Salish "as told-to" narratives stemming from the author's relationship with the Squamish chief Joe Capilano. The narrative world it draws on is one of guardian spirits and of transformation between human and animal spirits. A later retelling for children, *The Adventures of Yoo-Lah-Teen: A Legend of the Salish Coastal Indians*, was published by Ellen Pugh and Laszlo Kubinyi in 1975. Oratory has its own modern landmark: Chief Dan George's 1967 speech on what had happened to his people, delivered at a Canadian Centennial ceremony in Vancouver's Empire Stadium, riveted its audience and touched off public awareness and Native activism in British Columbia and Canada. Language documentation includes the *Lushootseed Dictionary* of Dawn Bates, Thom Hess and Vi Hilbert. Coast Salish characters have also entered genre fiction, in the Victoria author Stanley Evans's mystery series featuring the investigator Silas Seaweed.

## Language & religion

The Coast Salish or Central Salish languages form a branch of the Salishan family, spoken around most of the Georgia and Puget Sound basins; the Tsamosan and Tillamook languages are often treated by linguists as independent branches of Salishan rather than part of this one, and the position of Nuxálk (Bella Coola) is unsettled — it shares at least one phonological change with Coast Salish but also resembles the Interior Salish languages. The name Salish comes from the Salish people of Montana, whose Montana Salish is the easternmost Salishan language. Languages and peoples named in the museum records and articles include Halkomelem, Lushootseed, Squamish, Snuneymuxw, Sechelt/Shishalh, Songhees and Sto:lo; from 1970, such self-designations began to replace older names conferred by anthropologists, linguists and governments. Catholic missionaries arrived in Puget Sound around 1839–1840 and Methodists worked there from 1840 to 1842 without success. The living religious practice described in these sources is the spirit-power complex: guardian spirits, vision quests, shamanic soul recovery and the winter spirit dances.
