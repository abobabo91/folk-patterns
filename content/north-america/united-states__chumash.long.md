## Overview

The Chumash are a Native American people of the central and southern coastal regions of California, in portions of what became Kern, San Luis Obispo, Santa Barbara, Ventura and Los Angeles counties, from Morro Bay in the north to Malibu in the south and Mount Pinos in the east. Their territory includes three of the Channel Islands — Santa Cruz, Santa Rosa and San Miguel — with the smaller Anacapa likely occupied seasonally for want of fresh water. The name Chumash is glossed as "bead maker" or "seashell people". Before the mission period they lived in over 150 independent villages speaking variations of one language; the Chumashan family has no identified linguistic relatives. Estimates of the 1770 population range from about 8,000 to 20,400. Today an estimated 5,000 people are Chumash, and place names such as Malibu, Lompoc, Ojai, Pismo Beach, Piru and Simi Valley carry Chumash origins.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources describe Chumash basketry rather than woven cloth, and coiled basketry is the principal pattern-bearing craft they document.

**Coiled bowl basketry** — a coiled bowl or basket used in the preparation of acorn meal, made of deergrass, rush and sumac; the British Museum records the Chumash term *'ayakuy* for this form. Basketry is named alongside bead manufacture and rock art as a defining part of pre-mission Chumash culture.

**Coiled basketry hats** — a woven *somelelu* (sombrero or Padre's hat) in the British Museum is likely made from bulrush and sedge, materials used most commonly among the Obispeño Chumash of the San Luis Obispo region. *Somelelu* is the Barbareño/Šmuwič Chumash word for sombrero. Conversations with Chumash weavers and basketry experts in 2023–2024 established that the hat was likely woven to the right by a right-handed weaver, and that the outside of the hat was the work side. A second coiled basketry hat made for a child is recorded with a tentative Chumash name, *'epsu' ho' l-čičiwun*.

**Trinket baskets** — small coiled baskets collected as a distinct named form.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no basketry motifs. They do record incised decoration on stone: geometric designs carved on the outside of a steatite smoking pipe, and, on a ground stone tube in the form of a fish, a dorsal fin and tail incised with dots and eyes and gills rendered as incised lines. Rock art is named as part of Chumash culture, with water creatures often depicted in it, and the "scorpion tree" arborglyph carved a six-legged creature wearing a headdress with a crown and two spheres. Rock art and arborglyphs are thought to depict Polaris and Ursa Major.

### Clothing & dress

The sources say almost nothing about Chumash garments. What they do record is headgear and adornment: the coiled *somelelu* hat described above, a smaller coiled basketry hat made for a child, and bone hair-pins — one made from a pelican wing bone. Necklaces and ear-rings of shell disc beads, glass beads, stone and bone were worn (see Jewelry below). The one clothing detail the sources carry is social rather than technical: *'aqi*, a third Chumash gender, was defined by biological males who performed work and wore clothing traditionally of women, and is closely tied in the record to non-procreative sexual activity, with archaeological evidence drawn from mortuary practice.

### Architecture

What the sources do record of the built and shaped landscape is that large piles of shellfish waste were expanded over time to create ceremonial and residential mounds, and that the Chumash lived in over 150 independent villages before the mission period, in three environments — the interior of plains, rivers and mountains; the coast; and the Northern Channel Islands. Villages along the mainland coast emerged as intermediaries in trade between island, coastal and mainland groups. From 1772 the Franciscan missions replaced village life as the frame of Chumash settlement: Mission San Luis Obispo (1772), San Buenaventura (1782), Santa Barbara (1786), La Purisima Concepción (1789) and Santa Ynez (1804), with Mission San Fernando (1798) drawing in Chumash speakers from the middle Santa Clara River valley.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources record no Chumash pottery or metalwork — pottery reached the Chumash through trade networks reaching into modern-day Arizona, exchanged for shell beads. The objects here are of stone, bone, shell and wood. Stone mortars, including a round-based form, survive in quantity; the Millingstone Horizon (7000–4500 BC) shows seed processing with metates and manos. Monterey chert was worked into leaf-shaped points or knives, finely made lance points, and drills for making shell fish-hooks, with heavy percussion removals across the surface and lighter, occasionally pressure, flaking at the edge. Bone and wood served for spear-heads and for harpoon foreshafts with a bone barb lashed on with fibre cord. Bone, possibly elk antler, was carved into spoons with a wide oval bowl. A cylindrical steatite smoking pipe takes a bird-bone stem secured with bitumen. Perforated abalone (*Haliotis rufescens*) shells were worked into fish-hook blanks, beads and ornaments. The outstanding wooden object is the *tomol*, the sewn-plank canoe whose design allowed deepwater fishing — swordfish among the catches — and carried trade between villages.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Bead making is embedded in the people's own name. Shell beads were made from the marine olive snail (*Callianax biplicata*) from the Millingstone period onward and served as the currency of an exchange system reaching to Arizona. Surviving necklaces combine shell discs, single and multicoloured glass beads, stone pendants and cylindrical ivory or bone beads; one strings shell, stone and glass including two broken parts of a hookstone. Ear-rings alternate shell discs with blue-green glass beads. Hair was dressed with bone pins, including one of pelican wing bone. Red abalone shell was used for beads and ornaments as well as fishhooks. The sources record no tattooing or body painting.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual material in the sources is cosmological and astronomical rather than calendrical. Chumash worldview considers all things to be, in varying measure, alive, intelligent, dangerous and sacred. There is no creation story of the kind told by neighbouring Takic-speaking peoples; instead the universe, with three or in some versions five layers, has always been here. Human beings occupy the Middle Region, sometimes called *'antap*, resting on two giant snakes whose writhing causes earthquakes. The lower world, *C'oyinahsup*, is home to snakes, frogs and salamanders and is reached through springs and marshes; the upper world, *Alapay*, is reached through the mountains and is where the "sky people" live. *Itiashap* is the home of the First People. Sky figures include the Sun — source of life and also of disease and death — the Moon, Lizard, Sky Coyote (*Shnilemun*), a protector who looks out for the welfare of all in the world below, and Eagle (*Slo'w*), ruler of Polaris, who keeps the other stars in order. Shamans travelled in vision quests and took part in carving the scorpion tree, used in observing the stars and in the Chumash calendar; the position of Ursa Major around Polaris marked the time of year. Water creatures were depicted in rock art to bring water or to appease underworld spirits in times of hunger or disease. Modern practice includes the annual tomol crossing to the Channel Islands, first made on 9 September 2001, when the *Elye'wun* ("swordfish"), launched in 1997, was paddled to Limuw (Santa Cruz Island).

## Foodways

Acorns were the staple despite the work of removing their toxins; those of the coast live oak were best, ground into a paste easy to eat and to store for years, the mush usually served unseasoned with meat and fish. Deer was the most important land mammal, valued especially by interior Chumash, who hunted it by dressing as deer and grazing alongside the animals until within arrow range; rabbits and birds supplemented the meat. Abalone and clam are named as the basis of coastal cuisine, with mussels and a wide array of clams gathered from tidal zones, most abundantly from winter to early spring. Sea otters and seals were eaten, and coastal and island villages took larger fish, swordfish among them, from the *tomol*. Herbalism using local herbs produced teas and medicinal relief. Trade moved marine foods inland and terrestrial foods to the coast. Through the Santa Ynez Band's foundation, food programmes continue today: a $10,000 grant to the Foodbank of Santa Barbara County in December 2010 expanded its Produce Initiative and the Kids' Farmers' Market, and a community garden planted from 2013 supplies vegetables to the Elder's Council.

## Oral tradition & literature

Chumash oral narratives were collected and published by Thomas Blackburn as *December's Child: A Book of Chumash Oral Narratives* (1980); Susan Suntree and John M. Anderson (*Chumash Demonology*) have also written on the narrative cosmology summarised above. The great documentary resource is the unpublished fieldnotes of the linguist John Peabody Harrington, who worked on all the attested Chumashan languages; Barbareño, Ineseño and Ventureño are especially well documented, and he obtained least on Island Chumash, Purisimeño and Obispeño. The first Chumash dictionary, the six-hundred-page *Samala-English Dictionary* with 4,000 entries and more than 2,000 illustrations, appeared in April 2008. The documentary film *6 Generations: A Chumash Family History* features Mary Yee, and the short film *Return to Limuw*, produced by the Ocean Channel, records the 2001 tomol crossing.

## Language & religion

Chumashan is an extinct and revitalising family with no identified linguistic relatives; a proposed link to Salinan rests on a single shared word for 'white clam shell', used as currency and probably borrowed. Six languages are attested: the divergent Obispeño, the Central group of Purisimeño, Ineseño, Barbareño and Ventureño, and Island Chumash, which had separate dialects on Santa Cruz, San Miguel and Santa Rosa. The post-contact names come from the Franciscan missions where speakers were relocated between the 1770s and 1830s, and contemporary Chumash prefer native names. The languages are known for consonant harmony and low-vowel harmony, and the Central languages share a six-vowel system. Mary Yee, the last Barbareño speaker, died in 1965; revitalisation programmes are underway for four of the languages. Missionisation brought Catholicism; the Chumash Revolt of 1824, beginning at Mission Santa Inés, was one of the largest and most successful revolts of Native Catholic neophytes in the Spanish West.
