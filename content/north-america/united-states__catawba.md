## Overview

The Catawba, also known as Issa, Essa, Iswä, and most commonly Iswa, are a federally recognized tribe of Native Americans known as the Catawba Indian Nation. Their current lands are in South Carolina, on the Catawba River near Rock Hill, while their historical territory also extended into North Carolina. The Catawba language belongs to the Catawban language family. As of 2006, the Catawba Nation had increased to about 2,600 people, and the 2010 census recorded 3,370 people identifying as having Catawba ancestry. Catawba women are particularly noted for pottery and basketry, arts that the community continues to preserve. The Catawba are historically sedentary agriculturists who also fished and hunted game.

## Material culture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Catawba pottery is a prominent documented art. It was historically made primarily by women, although since the late 20th century some men have also made pottery. The Catawba are believed to have the oldest surviving pottery tradition east of the Mississippi, and possibly the oldest, or one of the oldest, on the American continent. Pottery has also been found along the Catawba River corridor dating to the Woodland period.

## Festivals & rituals

The Catawba reserve in South Carolina holds an annual celebration called *Yap Ye Iswa*, a name that roughly translates as “Day of the People” or “Day of the River People.” It is held at the Catawba Cultural Center, and proceeds fund the center’s activities. They state that Frank Speck documented Catawba religious beliefs and mortuary customs in the 20th century, but the supplied material does not summarize those customs.

## Foodways

The Catawba are historically sedentary agriculturists who also fished and hunted for game. Corn was among their crops, and deer was among their traditional game.

## Oral tradition & literature

 The supplied material states that Catawba texts and folklore were recorded in works by Frank Speck, including *Catawba Texts* and “Some Catawba Texts and Folk-lore,” but it does not reproduce or summarize those narratives.

The Catawba language is being revived and preserved. A Catawba alphabet was created by the Catawba Language Project as part of a revitalization effort and the creation of an app for the language.

## Language & religion

Catawba is one of two Catawban languages of the eastern United States; together with the Siouan languages, the Catawban languages form the Siouan–Catawban language family. The last native, fluent speaker was Samuel Taylor Blue, who died in 1959. Two documented dialects are Esaw and Saraw, both of which lasted into the 20th century. The Catawba Language Project created an alphabet for the language. The sources also record the use of the aspirated character ⟨ʰ⟩ and variant forms of the glottal symbol, including ⟨ɂ⟩ and ⟨ˀ⟩.

The sources state that missionaries of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints contacted tribal members in approximately 1883, after which numerous Catawba converted to the church.
