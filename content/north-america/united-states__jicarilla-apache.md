## Overview

The Jicarilla Apache, also called *Jicarilla Dindéi*, are members of one of the loosely organized autonomous bands of the Eastern Apache. They live in New Mexico, particularly on the Jicarilla Apache Indian Reservation and in Dulce, while their traditional territory extended through the Sangre de Cristo Mountains, the plains of southern Colorado and northern New Mexico, and the lands bounded by the Rio Grande, Pecos River, Arkansas River, and Canadian River. In 2022, the reservation had a population of 2,596; a 2019 census recorded 3,353 people living there. The Jicarilla speak Jicarilla, an Eastern Southern Athabaskan language. Their folk-culture significance is especially documented through pottery, basketry, beadwork, Apache fiddle-making, agricultural practices, sacred stories, and ceremonial traditions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Basketry* is one of the best-documented Jicarilla material traditions. The Jicarilla are renowned for fine baskets made with distinctive diamond, cross, and zig-zag designs, as well as representations of deer, horses, and other animals. The Ollero band subsidized its livelihood through sales of basketry and micaceous clay pottery.

**Beadwork** is another recognized Jicarilla art. The sources identify beadwork as a form of traditional art and state that the Jicarilla are renowned for it.

**Micaceous clay pottery** was associated especially with the Ollero, or mountain people, who became potters and lived partly in Pueblo-like villages. Their clay came from the Taos and Picuris Pueblo areas, and pottery making was described as an important source of income.

**Motif vocabulary.** Diamond designs, cross designs, zig-zag designs, deer representations, horse representations, other animal representations.

### Architecture

The Jicarilla lived in houses with a lifestyle similar to that of other Americans. The Llanero band traditionally lived as nomads in tipis, called *kozhan* by the Jicarilla, while the Ollero band settled as farmers and lived partly in Pueblo-like villages.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Jicarilla pottery was made from clay associated with the Taos and Picuris Pueblo areas. The Olleros were identified as a pottery-making clan and sold micaceous clay pottery. The Jicarilla also used small sealed baskets as drinking vessels; the term “jicarilla” comes from Mexican Spanish meaning “little basket.”

### Jewelry & body adornment

They identify beadwork as a Jicarilla art.

## Music & performance

The Jicarilla are known for keeping Apache fiddle-making alive. The sources name no specific fiddle form, musical scale, song genre, or repertoire.

The Little Beaver Celebration includes a pow-wow, rodeo, draft horse pull, and a five-mile race in mid-July. The Stone Lake Fiesta includes ceremonial dances, a rodeo, and footraces each September 14 and 15. These events are documented as annual gatherings.

## Festivals & rituals

The Jicarilla ceremonial calendar described in the sources includes the **Little Beaver Celebration**, held in mid-July, with a pow-wow, rodeo, draft horse pull, and a five-mile race. The **Stone Lake Fiesta** is held each September 14 and 15 and includes ceremonial dances, a rodeo, and footraces.

A major life-cycle ceremony is the puberty feast, called *keesta* in Jicarilla. It is a rite of passage for girls or young women.

Jicarilla sacred traditions also include shrines and places associated with communication with the Creator and spirits. The land enclosed by the four sacred rivers was understood as a sacred homeland containing places for ceremonial materials, including white clay, red ochre, and yellow ochre.

## Foodways

The Jicarilla practiced seasonal agriculture along rivers and learned farming from Puebloan peoples and Spaniards of New Spain. By the 1800s, they cultivated squash, beans, pumpkins, melons, peas, wheat, and corn, sometimes using irrigation. They also hunted buffalo into the 17th century and later hunted antelope, deer, mountain sheep, elk, and buffalo.

Jicarilla women gathered berries, agave, honey, onions, potatoes, nuts, and seeds from the wild. Hunting, gathering, cultivation, and seasonal travel formed part of a varied diet and seminomadic way of life.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Jicarilla creation story states that the land enclosed by the four sacred rivers was provided to the people by the Creator. The Jicarilla called themselves *Haisndayin*, translated as “people who came from below,” because they believed they were the sole descendants of the first people to emerge from the underworld. The underworld was the home of Ancestral Man and Ancestral Woman, who produced the first people. Hascin, the chief deity, created Ancestral Man and Ancestral Woman, as well as the animals, sun, and moon.

Traditional stories include **White Shell Woman**, **Killer of the Enemies**, and **Child of the Water**. These stories feature sacred people and places, including the Rio Grande Gorge, Picuris Pueblo, the spring and marsh near El Prado, Hopewell Lake, Taos Pueblo, and the four sacred rivers.

## Language & religion

Jicarilla, also called *Abáachi mizaa*, is an Eastern Southern Athabaskan language spoken by the Jicarilla Apache. The traditional homeland was called *Tinde* in the language source. The language has 34 consonants and 16 vowels, with high, low, and falling tones. In 2000, 680 people reported their language as Jicarilla; another report identified about 300 first-language speakers and an equal or greater number of semi-speakers out of a total ethnic population of 3,100.

In 2003, the Jicarilla Apache Nation became the first Tribe in New Mexico to certify community members to teach a Native American language. By 2012, revitalization efforts included a dictionary, classes, and seasonal camps for young people.

Jicarilla religious traditions center on Hascin, Ancestral Man, Ancestral Woman, the Creator, sacred rivers and mountains, shrines, and communication with spirits. As of 2000, about 70% of the tribe practiced an organized religion, many of whom were Christians.
