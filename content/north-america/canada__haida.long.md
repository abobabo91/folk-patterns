## Overview

The Haida (*X̱aayda*, *X̱aadas*, *X̱aad*) are an Indigenous people of the Pacific Northwest Coast whose territory centres on Haida Gwaii, the archipelago some 80 km off the northern British Columbia mainland across Hecate Strait, and extends to the southern half of Prince of Wales Island in Southeast Alaska, home of the Kaigani Haida who migrated north from K'iis Gwaii in the early eighteenth century. Archaeological evidence places habitation on the islands as early as 13,100 BP, with Haida oral history recalling ancestors living alongside glaciers and the arrival of the first lodgepole pine. Their language is an isolate with no demonstrated relatives. A smallpox epidemic in 1862 reduced the island population from roughly 6,607 to 829 by 1881. Haida matter to the study of folk culture as the originators — per Diamond Jenness — of the totem pole and the bentwood box, and as the authors of the most disciplined graphic system in Northwest Coast art.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Naaxiin (Chilkat weaving)** — *naaxiin* is a fingerwoven robe of mountain-goat wool twined over a warp of yellow cedar bark, worked on a one-bar loom with no shed and no weft insertion tool, so the weaver builds each curvilinear shape independently. It is the only weaving tradition on earth that can produce a free-form curve without tapestry-style stepping, which is what lets it carry the same formline crest images a carver would cut in cedar. The five-sided black, yellow, blue-green and white robe hangs with a deep warp fringe.

**Raven's tail weaving** — the older geometric twining tradition, in black and white (occasionally yellow), built from zigzag, diamond and chevron bands rather than crest figures. It largely fell out of use by the early nineteenth century as *naaxiin* spread, and was reconstructed in the late twentieth century from museum robes.

**Button blanket** — the post-contact ceremonial robe: a Hudson's Bay wool trade blanket, dark blue or black with a red flannel border, carrying a crest appliquéd in red and outlined in mother-of-pearl or shell buttons. The crest is read as lineage property, not decoration, and the robe is worn at potlatch.

**Spruce root basketry** — Sitka spruce roots dug, split, and twined into watertight hats, storage baskets and mats. The Haida *cedar bark hat*, woven with a painted crest field and sometimes with stacked cylinders (*skil*) at the crown indicating potlatches given, is the highest-status form.

**Formline** — the underlying two-dimensional design system rather than a medium: a continuous swelling-and-narrowing black primary line that encloses red secondary and blue-green tertiary fields, applied identically to woven robes, painted house fronts, bentwood chests and tattooed skin.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Ovoid* — the rounded rectangle with a convex top and slightly concave bottom, used for eyes, joints and body segments; *u-form* — the paired bracket that fills ears, feathers and fins; *s-form* — the connective filler between ovoids; *split-u*; *salmon-trout head* — the small face-like element inset in an ovoid; *eye socket*; *formline T* — the negative relief cut; *crest figures* — Raven, Eagle, Bear, Killer Whale (*sg̱aan*), Dogfish, Frog, Beaver, Thunderbird, Wasgo (sea wolf); *zigzag* and *diamond* bands in raven's tail work; *tlaajaang* — the flicker or split-form motif.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday Haida dress was cedar-bark-based: inner red-cedar bark was pounded soft and shredded into capes, aprons and skirts, and the same material made the conical rain hat that shed the islands' near-constant rain. Sea otter, marmot and bear pelts served as winter robes; both sexes went barefoot or wore simple hide moccasins for travel, since most movement was by canoe. Women's curing and processing of cedar for weaving and clothing was a defined household responsibility alongside berry-gathering and clam-digging.

Ceremonial dress separated sharply from this. A chief at potlatch wore a *naaxiin* or button blanket over the shoulders, a woven spruce-root or carved wooden crest hat, a beaded or hide dance apron hung with puffin beaks or deer-hoof rattlers, and leggings. Frontlets — small carved forehead masks inlaid with abalone and crowned with sea-lion whiskers and ermine — were worn with eagle down inside the crown, which scattered over guests as the dancer moved. Women of rank wore the *labret*, a lip plug of wood, bone or stone inserted through a perforation below the lower lip and enlarged over a lifetime; its size indexed age and standing.

### Architecture

The Haida town was a single row of *monumental cedar houses* facing the beach, each fronted by poles, with canoes drawn up below. The house is a post-and-beam structure of split red cedar: massive corner posts and two or more longitudinal roof beams carry a shed or low-pitched gable roof of overlapping cedar planks, with walls of vertical or horizontal planks slotted into sills. Two regional forms are distinguished — the six-beam house and the two-beam house, the latter with beams of extraordinary span.

Interiors were excavated into stepped rectangular pits, sometimes three or four descending terraces around a central hearth, with the chief's family quarters at the rear. A smoke hole was managed with an adjustable plank. The *house frontal pole* (*gyaa'aang*) stood against or was incorporated into the façade, and in many houses the entrance was cut through the pole itself as an oval doorway — one passed into the house through the crest. Named house types and the houses themselves carried personal names inherited within the lineage. *Mortuary poles* — broad-fronted posts with a cavity at the top holding a bentwood box of remains, capped by a painted frontal board — stood among them. The great surviving village of SG̱ang Gwaay (Ninstints) preserves this alignment.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Haida made no pottery; cedar did the work ceramics did elsewhere. The *bentwood box* is the signature: a single plank kerfed at three corners, steamed, bent square, pegged or sewn at the fourth, and fitted with a bottom and lid, producing a watertight container for grease, water, food, regalia or human remains. Large ones were painted or shallow-carved in formline on all four sides; the finest are attributed chests. Feast dishes, ladles of mountain-sheep and goat horn, oil dishes, and the *Chief's settee* — a carved bench-throne — belong to the same carving economy.

*Argillite carving* is post-contact and unique to Haida Gwaii: a soft black carbonaceous shale from a single quarry at Slatechuck Creek near Skidegate, worked since about 1820 into pipes, platters, chests and model poles, initially for sale to maritime traders. Copper was cold-worked into the *copper shield* (*tináa*), a shield-shaped plaque with a T-ridge that functioned as the highest denomination of potlatch wealth and could be ceremonially broken. Argillite quarrying rights remain restricted to Haida carvers.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Haida jewellery after contact centres on engraved silver and gold bracelets, hammered from coin silver and later sheet, incised with a crest in formline — Raven, Eagle, Killer Whale, Bear — and worn in stacks by women of rank. Bill Reid and Charles Edenshaw before him made this the most widely recognized Haida form. *Abalone inlay*, cut from California red abalone traded up the coast, sets the teeth, eyes and lip rims of masks, frontlets and rattles, and is inset in argillite and horn. Pre-contact ornament included the *labret*, ear and nose piercings, and pendants of bone, shell and copper.

Tattooing was a potlatch act rather than personal decoration: crest figures were pricked into the chest, forearms, backs of hands, thighs and shins of high-ranking children at the potlatch where their names were confirmed, publishing lineage affiliation on the body. Face paint in red and black formline was applied for dances and vision quests.

## Music & performance

Haida music is vocal and percussive; there are no melodic instruments in the pre-contact repertoire. Song is accompanied by the plank drum — a large painted cedar box or wall plank struck with the fist or a beater — the hand drum of hide over a hoop, and by rattles. The **raven rattle**, a carved bird form with a reclining shaman and frog on its back, is held by chiefs during entrance dances; the round globular **shaman's rattle** belonged to the *sg̱aaga*. Deer-hoof and puffin-beak clappers sewn to aprons and leggings sound with the dancer's step, and carved cedar whistles voiced supernatural beings during ceremonies.

Songs are owned property, inherited within a lineage exactly as crests and names are, and their performance at potlatch is itself an assertion of title. Genres include mourning and memorial songs, potlatch entrance songs, peace-making songs used to settle feuds, love songs, gambling songs sung over the stick game, cradle songs, and the shaman's curing songs. Texts are often archaic or in a ritual register not fully transparent to everyday speakers. John R. Swanton recorded Haida songs and texts at Skidegate and Masset in 1900–1901 for the Jesup North Pacific Expedition, the single largest early documentation. Contemporary performance runs through community dance groups at Old Massett and Skidegate and through the Rainbow Creek Dancers.

## Dance & theatre

Haida dance is masked, narrative, and staged inside the house at potlatch. The **transformation mask** is its central device: an outer mask, usually an animal or supernatural being, splits open on strings pulled by the dancer to reveal a human face carved within, enacting metamorphosis and reincarnation. Masks were made for a specific individual's spirit and were not meant for general viewing, which is why their presence in museums — many confiscated under the potlatch ban — remains contested.

Dance types include the chief's entrance dance with raven rattle and frontlet, scattering eagle down as a sign of peace; crest dances in *naaxiin* and button blanket, in which the wearer turns to display the crest; and the dramatic performances of the secret societies borrowed from Heiltsuk and Kwakwaka'wakw neighbours, in which an initiate is possessed and publicly tamed. Peace-making ceremonies were danced rather than negotiated. The 1884 potlatch ban drove all of it underground until its repeal in 1951.

## Festivals & rituals

The Haida ceremonial calendar is not solar but social: the potlatch (*gyáa isáaw*) is the institution around which everything turns. Potlatches are convened to raise a pole, build a house, mourn a death, confirm an heir's name, mark a marriage, or repair an insult, and are held in the winter months when food stores permit. The most consequential take years to prepare and run for days, and function simultaneously as a legal proceeding — witnesses are paid to remember and validate the transfer of names, crests, songs and territory — and as an economic redistribution. Rank and incorporeal wealth, not goods, are the substance being moved.

Life-cycle rites: at a boy's puberty his mother's brothers instructed him in lineage history and prescribed a diet held to confer specific capacities — duck tongues for breath-holding underwater, blue jay tongues for climbing. A girl at menarche was secluded in the family house, sleeping on a stone pillow with restricted food and drink, and instructed by her father's sisters. Both sexes historically undertook vision quests, going alone into the forest for days to find a guiding spirit; a successful return was celebrated with masks, face paint and costume. Mortuary practice placed the remains of chiefs and shamans in bentwood boxes atop mortuary poles, with a memorial potlatch given a year or more later by the heir.

The **Lyell Island** blockade of November 1985, in which 72 Haida were arrested opposing old-growth logging, has itself become a commemorated event, leading to the 1987 South Moresby Agreement and Gwaii Haanas.

## Foodways

Haida subsistence is marine. Five species of Pacific salmon — sockeye, coho, pink, chum and chinook — were taken at stone-walled intertidal traps and weirs, then split and smoked in dedicated smokehouses for winter. Halibut was the other pillar, hooked on the *ch'iiga*, a carved wooden V-hook whose barb geometry selects fish of a particular size and whose upper arm is often carved with a crest figure. Pacific herring spawn is harvested as *k'aaw* — roe deposited on hemlock branches or kelp set out in the shallows, then eaten raw, boiled or dried; it remains the most sought-after spring food.

Eulachon (candlefish) grease is the defining condiment and the defining trade good: the small oily smelt is rendered into an oil that accompanies nearly every dried food, and the routes over which it moved are called grease trails. Sea mammals, black bear, deer, waterfowl, razor clams, cockles, chitons, sea urchin roe, octopus, seaweed (*sGyuu*, black laver, dried into cakes) and herring-egg-laden kelp fill out the diet. Vegetable foods are gathered rather than farmed — salmonberry, huckleberry, salal, crabapple, highbush cranberry, cinquefoil and clover roots, the last two cultivated in tended estuarine plots. Berries were mashed and dried into cakes stored in bentwood boxes under grease. Potatoes, adopted from traders in the early nineteenth century, were grown in quantity on Haida Gwaii and traded to the mainland — an early Haida commercial crop. Feast food at potlatch is served in carved dishes, with grease poured over dried fish and berry cake.

## Oral tradition & literature

Haida oral literature is divided between *qqaayagaang* — the sacred narratives of the supernatural age — and lineage histories, which are property and may only be told by those entitled. The great cycle concerns **Nang Kilsdlaas** (Ne-kilst-lass), the Raven, who revealed the world and took an active hand in the creation of life, and who is simultaneously creator and glutton, benefactor and trickster. Related beings include *K'ing.gii*, who presides over the seas; *X̱yuu*, the northeast wind; and *Sin Sg̱aanuwee*, the encompassing cosmological super-being. The founding matrilineage ancestresses — SGuuluu Jaad (Foam Woman), Jiila Kuns (Creek Woman), KalGa Jaad (Ice Woman) — anchor the lineage histories.

The corpus was recorded by John R. Swanton in 1900–1901 from the Skidegate narrator Ghandl and the Masset narrator Skaay, and published as *Haida Texts and Myths*. Robert Bringhurst's controversial three-volume retranslation, beginning with *A Story as Sharp as a Knife* (1999), presented these as long-form poetry and provoked sustained debate about non-Haida custodianship. Contemporary revival runs through Michael Nicoll Yahgulanaas's *Haida manga*, which renders narrative in formline-bordered panels, and through *SG̱aawaay Ḵʹuuna* (The Edge of the Knife, 2018), the first feature film in Haida, directed by Gwaai Edenshaw and Helen Haig-Brown with an all-Haida cast who learned the language for the production.

## Language & religion

Haida is a language isolate with no demonstrated genetic relationship to any other family, including Na-Dene, to which it was once tentatively assigned. Three dialects survive in name — Skidegate, Masset (Northern) and Alaskan Kaigani — with an extinct Ninstints (Southern) dialect. It was suppressed through the residential school system from 1911 onward; fewer than four dozen fluent first-language speakers remain, almost all over seventy. Revitalization began in the 1970s and continues through immersion nests and the Skidegate Haida Immersion Program.

Religiously the Haida today include Anglicans, Methodists-turned-United Church members, Catholics and Bahá'í, following Anglican missionization at Masset from 1876 under William Collison and the Methodist mission at Skidegate from 1883. Pre-colonial belief persists alongside these, and potlatch retains its central position. The *sg̱aaga* (shaman) mediated between human and spirit worlds, healing and negotiating with supernatural beings; the role is no longer publicly practised.
