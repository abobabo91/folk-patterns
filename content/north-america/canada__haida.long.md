## Overview

The Haida are an Indigenous nation of the northern Northwest Coast whose homeland is Haida Gwaii, the archipelago off the north coast of British Columbia, with a further community in southeast Alaska descended from an eighteenth-century northward migration onto Prince of Wales Island. Their language, *Xaad Kil* (in the northern dialect) or *Xaayda Kil* (in the Skidegate dialect of the southern islands), has no demonstrated relatives and is generally classified as an isolate, a fact that reinforces a long and largely self-contained occupation of the islands — Haida oral tradition and the archaeological record both point to continuous settlement across many thousands of years, through the shifting sea levels that followed the last glaciation. Society was and remains organised into two matrilineal moieties, Raven and Eagle, subdivided into lineages that own houses, names, songs, resource territories and the crest figures displayed on monumental art; a person belongs to the mother's side and marries across the moiety line. By the late eighteenth century the Haida were the dominant maritime power of the northern coast, travelling in enormous cedar canoes to trade, raid and intermarry as far as the Columbia River. Smallpox and other introduced epidemics reduced a population plausibly in the tens of thousands to a few hundred survivors within a generation, and the surviving southern villages consolidated at Skidegate while northern families gathered at Masset. The ruined village of SGang Gwaay (Ninstints), with its standing poles, is inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List, and much of the southern archipelago is now co-managed as Gwaii Haanas National Park Reserve, National Marine Conservation Area Reserve and Haida Heritage Site. Haida contributions to the world's material record are disproportionate to the nation's size: the two-dimensional design system that art historians call formline, the monumental cedar pole and plank house, the black argillite carving developed in the maritime fur trade era, and a weaving and basketry tradition of extraordinary technical refinement.

## Material culture

Haida material culture is a culture of a single dominant material — western red cedar — disciplined by a single dominant design grammar. Cedar supplied houses, poles, canoes, boxes, mats, rope, clothing and hats; the yellow cedar and mountain goat wool supplied ceremonial textiles; and almost every surface of consequence carried crest figures rendered in the continuously swelling and tapering line known as *formline*. The characteristic units of that grammar are the rounded rectangle called the *ovoid*, used for eyes, joints and major fields, and the *u-form*, used for ears, feathers, fins and filler. Design is not decoration applied to an object but a reading of the object's shape: figures are split, bent, dislocated and nested to fill the field of a chest, a spoon handle or a house front, and the viewer is expected to recognise the lineage crest — raven, eagle, killer whale, bear, frog, dogfish, beaver, thunderbird — from a few conventional cues.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Ceremonial weaving is twined rather than loom-woven, worked downward on a warp suspended from a bar, and it carries formline design into cloth with a technical precision unmatched elsewhere on the coast.

- **Chilkat weaving** (*naaxiin*) — Curvilinear twined robe of mountain goat wool on a cedar bark core warp, woven from a painted pattern board and capable of true curves; the Haida name is *naaxiin*, and Haida weavers were among its makers and its major patrons although the technique is most associated with the Tlingit Chilkat.
- **Raven's tail weaving** (*yeil koowu*) — Older geometric twined robe in black-and-white or natural wool bands of diagonals, chevrons and lozenges, nearly lost by the nineteenth century and revived from museum examples in the later twentieth.
- **Spruce root basketry** — Finely twined baskets and hats from split spruce roots, dug, steamed and stripped into threads, often with false-embroidery or dyed skip-stitch bands; among the most technically demanding basketry traditions of North America.
- **Cedar bark work** — Inner bark of red cedar pounded to softness and woven into mats, capes, aprons, rope and cradle padding, with plaited geometric patterning in dyed and undyed strips.
- **Button blanket** (*gwaayGang*) — Post-contact dark wool trade blanket bordered in red flannel and outlined with pearl or abalone buttons, the crest figure rendered in appliqué; now the most widespread form of ceremonial regalia.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress was minimal and weatherproof in a rainforest climate; ceremonial dress was among the most elaborate on the coast, assembled as a matched set of robe, apron, leggings, headdress and rattle.

- **Cedar bark cape and skirt** — Shredded cedar bark capes and skirts that shed rain, worn with little else in mild weather and layered with fur robes in winter.
- **Cedar bark hat** (*dajangGa*) — Wide conical woven hat of spruce root or cedar bark, sometimes painted with crest designs; high-ranking versions carry stacked cylinders indicating potlatches given.
- **Potlatch regalia** — Full ceremonial ensemble of woven or button robe, dance apron hung with puffin beaks or rattling shells, leggings, and headdress, worn only for the feast hall.
- **Frontlet headdress** (*hlgidang*) — Carved wooden forehead plaque inlaid with abalone, mounted on a crown of sea lion whiskers and ermine, holding eagle down that scatters over guests as the dancer moves.
- **Chilkat robe as rank dress** — The *naaxiin* robe worn over the shoulders by a chief or high-ranking woman, its three-panel design read as a crest by the assembled guests.

### Architecture

Haida monumental architecture is the plank house and the pole, both of red cedar split with wedges from living or fallen trees, both raised by invited labour and paid for at a feast.

- **Monumental cedar house** (*na*) — Large rectangular post-and-beam house of massive corner posts and enormous roof beams sheathed in split planks, housing several related families of one lineage around a central hearth, with the floor often excavated in stepped terraces.
- **House frontal pole** (*gyaa'aang*) — Crest pole set against or into the house front, frequently pierced at the base by an oval doorway so that guests entered through the figure.
- **Totem pole** — Freestanding or attached carved cedar column recording lineage crests and rights; the term covers memorial, frontal, mortuary and interior house post forms, all of which were commissioned publicly and validated at a potlatch.
- **Mortuary pole** — Broad-topped pole with a cavity and a painted frontal board holding the remains of a high-ranking person, the most distinctively Haida of the pole types.
- **SGang Gwaay village** — Abandoned southern village whose standing poles and house depressions are inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site and left deliberately to return to the forest rather than be restored.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

There is no Haida pottery tradition: with cedar that splits cleanly, steams pliable and holds water, containers were made of wood, and the household inventory is a carpentry inventory. Metal entered chiefly as copper, first traded and later as sheet stock.

- **Bentwood box** (*GuuGaay*) — Container made from a single cedar plank kerfed at three corners, steamed, bent square and pegged or sewn at the fourth, with a fitted base and lid; painted or carved with formline crests and used for storage, cooking with hot stones, and burial.
- **Argillite carving** (*tlaajaang*) — Carving in the soft black carbonaceous shale quarried at a single site on Graham Island, worked from the early nineteenth century into pipes, platters, chests, model poles and figure groups, initially for sale to maritime traders; the material is reserved to Haida carvers.
- **Copper shield** (*tinaa*) — Shield-shaped beaten copper plaque, a store and display of wealth given away, broken or cut at potlatches to assert rank.
- **Dugout canoe** (*tluu*) — Ocean-going canoe hollowed and spread from a single red cedar log, with flaring sides, a vertical cutwater and a projecting prow, in lengths from small family craft to war and trade canoes carrying dozens of people; Haida canoes were traded up and down the coast.
- **Chief's settee** — Broad carved and painted bench-throne of bentwood construction on which a host sat during a feast, among the largest and most ambitious pieces of Haida joinery.
- **Feast dishes, horn spoons and ladles** — Carved wooden grease bowls, sometimes seal- or canoe-shaped, and mountain sheep or goat horn spoons with formline handles, produced in quantity for the feast hall.
- **Transformation mask** — Hinged mask with an outer face that opens by concealed strings to reveal a second, inner identity, staging the transformations narrated in lineage stories.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment marked rank and lineage rather than personal taste, and the two most characteristic media are abalone shell and, after contact, engraved silver.

- **Abalone inlay** — Iridescent shell, the northern local species supplemented by larger traded California abalone, set into masks, frontlets, boxes and argillite as eyes, teeth and rim edging.
- **Labret** — Lower-lip plug of wood, bone or stone worn by high-ranking women, its size increased over a lifetime; a marker of noble status widely noted by eighteenth-century visitors and abandoned during the nineteenth century.
- **Engraved silver and gold bracelets** — Bracelets hammered from coins and later sheet metal and engraved with formline crests, a form pioneered in the nineteenth century and now the most commonly worn Haida art object.
- **Crest tattooing** — Lineage crest figures tattooed on the chest, back, arms and legs, commonly applied at a potlatch as a public validation of the right to the crest.
- **Ear and nose ornaments** — Multiple pierced ear ornaments of shell and bone, their number commonly indicating the number of feasts a family had given.

## Music & performance

Haida music is predominantly vocal and owned: songs belong to lineages and to individuals, and the right to sing one is property transferred by inheritance or payment.

- **Potlatch and crest songs** — Lineage-owned songs performed to validate names, poles and crests, sung in unison with a strong percussive pulse rather than harmony.
- **Mourning and memorial songs** — Songs commissioned for the dead and performed at memorial feasts, among the most tightly held forms of lineage property.
- **Box drum** — Large bentwood box suspended and struck to give a deep resonance for house-front dances, distinct in sound from the hand drum.
- **Hide hand drum** — Frame drum of deer or sea mammal hide, often painted with a crest, the standard accompaniment for processional and welcome songs.
- **Raven rattle and round rattle** — Carved wooden rattle in the form of a raven carrying a reclining human figure, held by chiefs in dance; round painted rattles were used by shamans.
- **Whistles and horns** — Concealed wooden whistles sounded from behind screens to announce the arrival of supernatural beings in the winter ceremonial.

## Dance & theatre

Performance in the Haida feast house is theatrical in the fullest sense: a screened backstage, trapdoors, string mechanisms, sudden darkness and light, and masks built to change in view of the audience.

- **Masked dance drama** — Enactments of lineage histories and encounters with supernatural beings, performed at potlatches with carved masks, screens and rehearsed stage effects.
- **Transformation dance** — Sequence in which a hinged mask opens to reveal another being, dramatising the permeability of human and animal identity.
- **Dance screen** (*gwaa'ang*) — Large painted plank partition at the rear of the house behind which performers dressed and from which they emerged through a central opening.
- **Welcome and paddle dances** — Processional dances performed on the beach and at the house door to receive arriving canoes, revived and now central to public Haida performance.
- **Contemporary dance groups** — Community dance groups at Skidegate, Old Massett and Hydaburg that maintain repertoire, teach young dancers and perform at pole raisings and canoe journeys.

## Festivals & rituals

The organising institution of Haida ceremonial life is the potlatch, a feast at which a host lineage distributes wealth and the guests witness and thereby validate a claim — to a name, a title, a pole, a house, or the memory of the dead. Banned under Canadian law from 1884 until 1951, it was continued covertly and has been publicly resumed since.

- **Potlatch** (*waahlGahl*) — Multi-day feast of speeches, songs, masked dance and mass gift distribution, given to mark a house raising, a memorial, a name-taking or a marriage.
- **Pole raising** — The public raising of a carved pole by invited guests pulling on ropes, followed by a feast; the act of raising by guests is itself the validation of the crest displayed.
- **Memorial feast** — Feast held some time after a death to end mourning, settle the succession of names and titles, and commission a memorial pole.
- **Name-taking** — Conferral of a lineage name on a child or an adult, announced and witnessed at a feast, with the giving away of goods as payment for the witnessing.
- **Tattooing and piercing rites** — Crest tattooing and ear piercing performed on children of rank during their parents' potlatch, making the family's claims visible on the body.
- **Canoe journeys** — Contemporary intertribal paddles in which carved canoes travel between coastal communities and ask permission to come ashore, reviving protocol and navigation together.

## Foodways

Haida Gwaii is one of the richest temperate marine environments in the world, and the Haida diet was overwhelmingly a sea diet, supplemented by berries, roots, greens and the products of the rainforest. Salmon of five species were taken in weirs and traps and preserved by smoking and wind-drying for winter; halibut were fished from canoes with steam-bent wooden hooks of great ingenuity; herring were harvested less for flesh than for spawn, which was collected on hemlock boughs or kelp set in the shallows and is still a prized food and trade item. Eulachon, the small oily fish rendered into the grease that is the coast's great condiment and wealth good, does not run in Haida Gwaii and was obtained by trade with mainland nations — a dependency that helped make the Haida traders. Sea mammals, sea urchins, clams, cockles, chitons, octopus and seaweed filled out the marine inventory; black seaweed dried in sheets remains a household staple. On land, deer are a recent introduction, and the traditional plant diet centred on salal, huckleberry, salmonberry, crabapple and the highly valued soapberry, whipped with water into a pink foam and served as a feast dessert. Cooking was done by dropping fire-heated stones into water-filled bentwood boxes, and by roasting on split cedar frames beside the fire. Feast food was served in carved grease bowls with horn spoons, and the scale of what a host could put before guests was itself part of the claim being made.

## Oral tradition & literature

Haida oral literature is one of the most substantially recorded Indigenous literatures of North America, largely because of an intense collaboration in 1900–1901 between the ethnographer John Swanton and Haida narrators at Skidegate and Masset, above all Skaay and Ghandl, whose long narrative poems Swanton took down in Haida. The corpus divides broadly between accounts of the world's ordering — the doings of Raven, who steals light, brings water and fish, and reshapes the world through appetite and mischief — and lineage histories that establish a family's right to a crest by recounting an ancestor's encounter with a supernatural being. Recurring figures include the Sea Wolf *Wasgo*, the Bear Mother who marries a bear and bears his children, and the many chiefs of the undersea and sky worlds whose houses mirror Haida houses. Narrative and visual art are not separable: a pole is a citation of a story, and the ability to read it depends on knowing the text. The published Swanton material, retranslated in the later twentieth century and controversially reframed as classical poetry by Robert Bringhurst, sits alongside an ongoing community tradition of storytelling, place-name knowledge and songs held by families rather than by archives.

## Language & religion

*Xaad Kil* is generally treated as a language isolate, with northern (Masset and Alaskan) and southern (Skidegate) dialects differing enough to be mutually difficult; an older proposal linking it to Tlingit and Athabaskan within a Na-Dene grouping is not accepted by most specialists. The language is severely endangered, with fluent first-language speakers numbering in the low double figures or fewer, nearly all elderly, a decline driven directly by the residential school system; intensive documentation, immersion programming and adult apprenticeship at Skidegate and Old Massett have produced a small cohort of new speakers, and the Haida Nation has declared the language's revitalisation a state of emergency. Traditional religion centred on a populated world in which animals, sea, forest and weather were peopled by beings with their own houses and protocols, mediated by the shaman, and expressed socially through the crest system that binds a lineage to its supernatural counterparts. Anglican missions arrived in the 1870s and most Haida today identify as Christian, commonly Anglican; this coexists rather than competes with crest law, potlatch obligation and the protocol of speaking for a lineage, which continue to govern public life. The Council of the Haida Nation, the hereditary chiefs and the village councils together constitute a political order in which the ceremonial and the administrative are not cleanly separable.
