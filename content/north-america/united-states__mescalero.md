## Overview

Mescalero, or Mescalero Apache, are a federally recognized Apache tribe of Southern Athabaskan–speaking Native Americans. The Mescalero Apache Tribe is located in south-central New Mexico, on a reservation established in its present form in 1883. The reservation lies on the eastern flank of the Sacramento Mountains and borders the Lincoln National Forest; most of it is in Otero County, with a small section in Lincoln County southwest of Ruidoso. Its territory includes Sierra Blanca, sacred ground for the tribe, and the reservation’s largest community is Mescalero. The tribe comprised over 12,468 people, with 8,652 recorded according to the United States Census. Mescalero culture is documented through language, hunting and gathering, kinship, sacred geography, and historical Apache lifeways.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources used describe clothing only in relation to subsistence activities. Women dressed meat and skins after hunting, while men led hunting parties for buffalo, antelope, and deer. The sources also state that the Mescalero lived in tipis or wickiups.

### Architecture

The Mescalero practiced matrilocal residence patterns. After marriage, a couple moved into a new tipi or wickiup close to the woman’s parents’ home.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention stored mescal as a food resource and refer generally to tribal artifacts displayed in the Mescalero cultural center, but do not catalogue particular object forms or materials.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 The Mescalero cultural center is said to display tribal artifacts and historical information.

## Dance & theatre

 They describe the Mescalero as historically mobile mountain people and as innovative, stealthy, fierce, precise, and tactical warriors.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do record sacred geography and religious tradition. Sierra Blanca Peak is sacred ground for the Mescalero Apache Tribe, and access is not permitted without a permit. Mescalero identity includes traditions concerning four mountains that represent directions of everyday life: Sierra Blanca Peak, El Capitan within the Guadalupe Mountains, Three Sisters Mountain, and Oscura Mountain Peak; the Salinas Peak is sometimes listed instead of Oscura Mountain Peak. The sources also state that forefathers spoke of a creator giving life on White Mountain, where White Painted Woman gave birth to Child of Water and Killer of Enemies. No birth, coming-of-age, wedding, funeral, or seasonal rite is described.

## Foodways

Mescalero subsistence traditionally depended on hunting and gathering. Men led hunting parties for buffalo, antelope, and deer. Women accompanied men, dressed the meat and skins, and also hunted small game such as rabbits. Women gathered Mescal Agave in groups of 4–10 people, mainly female friends and family members, usually with several men; men also took an active role in processing mescal.

Spanish colonists associated the Mescalero with the mescal agave and named them Mescalero Apache. Neighboring Apache bands called them Nadahéndé, “People of the Mescal,” because mescal agave was a staple food source. In times of need and hunger, the Mescalero depended on stored mescal for survival.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They do record elements of Mescalero oral tradition concerning sacred geography and origins. Four mountains represent directions of everyday life, and forefathers spoke of a creator giving life on White Mountain. White Painted Woman gave birth there to two sons, Child of Water and Killer of Enemies.

The language article states that Chiricahua and Mescalero Apache Texts contains a grammatical sketch and traditional religious and secular stories. The work was associated with the anthropological linguist Harry Hoijer, and the online book was made available through the University of Virginia. Virginia Klinekole was known for efforts to preserve the language. The sources also state that at least one language-immersion school for children exists in Mescalero.

## Language & religion

Mescalero-Chiricahua, also known as Chiricahua Apache, is a Southern Athabaskan language spoken by Chiricahua and Mescalero people in Chihuahua and Sonora, México, and in Oklahoma and New Mexico. It is related to Navajo and Western Apache. Mescalero belongs to the southwestern branch of the Southern Athabaskan subfamily, itself part of the Athabaskan and Dené–Yeniseian families. It is very closely related to Chiricahua and more distantly related to Western Apache; these are described as three dialects of Apachean. Navajo is related but its language and culture are considered distinct from those of the Apache.

The sources describe traditional religious stories and sacred places, including Sierra Blanca Peak and White Mountain.
