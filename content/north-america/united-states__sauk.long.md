## Overview

The Sauk, also called Sac, are Native Americans and Indigenous peoples of the Northeastern Woodlands. Their historical territory was near Green Bay, Wisconsin, while their history also includes the St. Lawrence River, Saginaw Bay, northern Illinois, Wisconsin, Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, and Oklahoma. Today, three federally recognized tribes are based in Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, and Oklahoma: the Sac and Fox Nation of Missouri in Kansas and Nebraska, the Sac and Fox Nation in Oklahoma, and the Sac and Fox Tribe of the Mississippi in Iowa. The Sauk are closely allied with the Meskwaki. Their language belongs to the Algonquian language family. Sauk cultural life, as covered by the sources, includes clan organization, village life, hunting, farming, trade, lacrosse, oral traditions, language preservation, and material forms of dress and adornment documented in museum portraits.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Beaded necklaces and headdresses** — Museum records describe beaded necklaces and a beaded headdress worn by Sauk and Fox men in studio portraits. The catalogue records do not provide a named Sauk pattern system or a comparison with neighboring cultures.

**Fur and bear-claw necklaces** — Several museum portraits record necklaces made from fur and bear claws. These objects occur with shirts, blankets, leggings, moccasins, feather ornaments, and tomahawks.

**Embroidered moccasins** — Museum records describe embroidered moccasins worn by Big Bear, Dead Indian, and White Fish.

**Leggings with decoration** — The catalogue records mention embroidered leggings, leggings with feather decoration, leggings with quill or bead decoration and fringe, and wool leggings with beadwork decoration.

**Blankets and wool blankets** — Portrait records repeatedly describe blankets wrapped around the body, waist, shoulders, or legs, including a wool blanket worn by the Sea and a wool blanket worn by Che-Ko-Skuk.

**Shell and cloth necklaces** — The museum objects include beaded and shell necklaces, cloth and shell necklaces, and dentalium shell necklaces.

**Quill or bead decoration** — The catalogue records identify quill or bead decoration on leggings and beadwork decoration on wool leggings.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The museum catalogue records document shirts, cloth shirts, shirts with fringing, long-sleeved tops, blankets, wool blankets, leggings, embroidered leggings, decorated leggings, leg bands, moccasins, and decorated or embroidered moccasins. Men are shown wearing feather hair ornaments, eagle feathers, decorated fur headdresses, cloth wraps around the head, beaded headdresses, fur hats with eagle feathers, and cloth and shell necklaces. Other recorded adornments include ear ornaments, armbands, arm ornaments, beaded necklaces, fur and bear-claw necklaces, shell necklaces, cloth neck ties, scarves with gorgets, leather straps with conchos, and peace medals. The portraits also show men holding feather fans, feather pouches, walking sticks, ceremonial objects, and tomahawks.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Museum records document peace medals, ear ornaments, dentalium shell ornaments, conchos, tomahawks, feather fans, feather pouches, and a walking stick. The Sauk historical account also describes boats used to cross a river and villages in which the people raised crops and traded with other tribes.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The museum catalogue records include ear ornaments, beaded necklaces, shell necklaces, dentalium shell necklaces, cloth and shell necklaces, fur and bear-claw necklaces, bear-claw necklaces, chokers made of dentalium, cloth neck ties with shells, scarves with gorgets, armbands, arm ornaments, leather straps with conchos, peace medals, and feather ornaments. Portraits also show eagle feathers, feather hair ornaments, decorated fur headdresses, and a fur hat with an eagle feather. The Sauk clan system records charcoal as black and white clay as white in the symbolic painting of the two moieties.

## Music & performance

The sources record lacrosse as a practice associated with recreation, festivals, the preparation of young warriors for combat, and preparation for wars or raids. The Sauk language article includes written language samples, including a greeting and a narrative about turtles.

## Dance & theatre

Lacrosse is described as a recreational and martial practice that was also part of festivals, but the sources do not classify it as dance or theatre.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources state that lacrosse was played for recreation, as part of festivals, to toughen young warriors for combat, and as preparation for imminent wars or raids. The Sauk and Fox peoples were divided into two moieties, the *Kîshkôha* and the *Shkasha*, associated respectively with long hair and bravery. The moieties were symbolized through black charcoal and white clay. The tribe was governed by a council that included sacred clan chiefs, a war chief, heads of families, and warriors. Chiefs were categorized as civil, war, and ceremonial; only civil chiefs were hereditary.

## Foodways

The sources state that the Sauk lived in villages, raised crops, hunted American bison, and actively traded with other tribes after adopting a semi-sedentary lifestyle on the prairie.

## Oral tradition & literature

Sauk traditions state that the tribe occupied the vicinity of the Saginaw River, and one tradition explains the name “Saginaw” through the Ojibwe expression “O-Sauk-e-non,” meaning “land of the Sauks” or “where the Sauks were.” The account of the Battle of Skull Island is presented as a legend in which Sauk people escaped by boat to an island before the river froze sufficiently for their attackers to cross. Some Ojibwe oral histories also place the Sauk in the Saginaw Valley before the arrival of Europeans. The Sauk language article records a written narrative about two turtles, a thunderstorm, rain, and a lake.

## Language & religion

Sauk is also known as *Thâkiwâtowêweni* or Thâkîwaki language. It is either a dialect of Fox or a distinct language and belongs to the Algonquian language family. It is closely related to the dialects spoken by the Meskwaki and Kickapoo, and Sauk and Meskwaki are described as mutually intelligible to a point. The Sauk once called themselves *asakiwaki*, “people of the outlet.” Sauk has a syllabic orthography; a Primer Book was published in 1975 based on a syllabary that existed in 1906, and a newer orthography was proposed around 1994. Five elders are described as fluent speakers, and Sauk is considered endangered.
