## Overview

The Nipmuc or Nipmuck are an Indigenous people of the Northeastern Woodlands. Their historic territory, *Nippenet*, meaning “the freshwater pond place,” lay in central Massachusetts and nearby parts of Connecticut and Rhode Island. The sources describe communities associated with Massachusetts and Connecticut, including Hassanamisco, Chaubunagungamaug, Natick, and Connecticut Nipmuc groups. Historically, the Nipmuc most likely spoke Loup A, a Southern New England Algonquian language, and the language is undergoing revival within the communities. Their history includes contact with European traders, participation in King Philip’s War, settlement in praying towns, land loss, state recognition, and continuing efforts to preserve cultural and genealogical identity.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Square-edged splint baskets* were a cultural practice that survived into the nineteenth century. The sources identify them as handcrafted objects.

No other documented textile or pattern-bearing tradition is covered by the sources used.

### Clothing & dress

In the twentieth century, Plains Indian clothing and feathered headdresses were worn as statements of Indian identity and to demonstrate continued residence in the region after much of the original culture had been lost.

### Architecture

The sources describe praying towns, also called “Indian plantations,” where Native Americans were settled and instructed in European farming methods, culture, and language. The Hassanamisco Nipmuc reservation included a meetinghouse and the center of an old praying village in the Hassanamessit Woods in Grafton. The Cisco homestead in Grafton was still owned by direct descendants of Nipmuc landholders by the end of the nineteenth century. In 1897, five Dudley families were placed in a tenement house on Lake Street in Webster, Massachusetts.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do mention trade in foodstuffs, furs, and wampum for copper kettles, arms, and metal tools, and they refer to an experienced gunsmith who kept weapons in good working order during King Philip’s War.

## Music & performance

They mention dances and powwows as gatherings associated with twentieth-century cultural identity, including dances or powwows at Hassanamessit in 1924. The sources also state that pan-Indianism took root in the 1920s through gatherings such as the Algonquin Indian Council of New England, which met in Providence, Rhode Island.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name dances and powwows at Hassanamessit in 1924. No Nipmuc dramatic or theatrical tradition is covered.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe the Annual Clambake and elections on the 4th of July as occasions when Nipmucs gathered to discuss tribal business. They also mention annual remembrance services for members of the tribe lost over the winter during their internment on Deer Island in Boston Harbor during King Philip’s War. These services were connected with protest against the destruction of Indian gravesites.

They record that the Massachusetts state government repealed the General Court Act of 1675, which had banned Native Americans from the City of Boston during King Philip’s War.

## Foodways

The sources state that the first recorded European contact occurred in 1630, when John Acquittamaug took maize to sell to starving colonists in Boston, Massachusetts. During early colonial trade, Nipmuc and other Native Americans exchanged foodstuffs, furs, and wampum for colonial goods. Nipmuc communities farmed, hunted, fished, and later sold timber. The transition to the cattle industry disrupted the native economy when colonists’ cattle ate unfenced Nipmuc lands, while the Nipmuc adopted the husbandry of swine.

## Oral tradition & literature

They describe cultural memory, genealogical research, and the sharing of traditions among Nipmuc and related tribal members during the twentieth century.

The missionary John Eliot compiled an Indian Bible and a grammar of Massachusett with help from Native interpreters. The sources also mention Mary Rowlandson’s memoir of captivity after her capture during the siege of Lancaster, although it is not identified as a Nipmuc literary work. Contemporary preservation efforts include genealogical and cultural research by James L. Cisco and Sara Cisco Sullivan and the work of tribal members to maintain cultural identity.

## Language & religion

The Nipmuc most likely spoke Loup A, a Southern New England Algonquian language. The language is undergoing revival, and several second-language speakers exist. In the linguistic classification presented by Ives Goddard, Loup A varieties are described as Loup 1, Loup 2, and Loup 3; Nipmuck is identified with Loup 1. The related Loup terminology was used by French colonial missionaries and was inconsistent.

The sources describe Christian missionary activity beginning with John Eliot, who learned Massachusett, translated a Bible, and established praying towns where Native Americans were instructed in European customs and converted to Christianity. They also mention powwows, described as shamans, and colonial laws restricting their influence.
