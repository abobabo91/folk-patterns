## Overview

The Nottoway, also called Cheroenhaka, are an Iroquoian Native American tribe in Virginia. Historically, they lived just west of the Fall Line in the Piedmont region, including settlements on Stony Creek and the Rowantee Branch of the Nottoway River, in what is now Sussex County. The Nottoway language belongs to the Iroquoian language family and became extinct well before 1900; its last known speaker, Edith Turner, died in 1838. At European contact in 1650, the Nottoway numbered no more than 400 to 500. Their documented cultural life includes cultivation of maize, squash, and beans, hunting, fishing, longhouse residence, palisaded communities, wampum necklaces, and a recorded vocabulary that has supported language research and revival work.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

They state only that Nottoway girls wore wampum necklaces in the early 18th century.

### Architecture

Nottoway communities included multi-family dwellings known as *longhouses*. These communities were protected by stockade fences known as *palisades*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention shell beads called *roanoke* in connection with exchanges for deer and otter skins.

### Jewelry & body adornment

In the early 18th century, Nottoway girls wore wampum necklaces.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that tobacco was used in ceremonies by the Nottoway and Tuscarora, but provide no further account of ceremonial practice.

## Foodways

The Nottoway depended on cultivating staples known as the Three Sisters: varieties of maize, squash, and beans. Women typically cultivated and processed these crops and selected and preserved seed varieties to produce different types of crops. Men hunted game and fished in the rivers.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do document language-preservation work: John Wood collected more than 250 word samples from Edith Turner in 1820, and James Trezvant collected several additional words after 1831. A total of about 275 words was published by Albert Gallatin in 1836. The Nottoway people are undertaking work for language revival.

## Language & religion

Nottoway, also called Cheroenhaka and Nottoway-Meherrin, was historically spoken by the Meherrin and Nottoway peoples. It is an extinct language closely related to Tuscarora within the Iroquoian language family. The last known speaker, Edith Turner, died in 1838. Knowledge of the language comes primarily from a word list collected on March 4, 1820, from Turner by John Wood. Additional words were gathered by James Trezvant. The sources describe tobacco use in ceremonies.
