## Overview

The Dakota (*Dakȟóta*, *Dakhóta*) are a Native American tribe and First Nations band government whose name means "ally or friend"; their autonyms include *Ikčé Wičhášta* ("Indian people") and *Dakhóta Oyáte*. They form two of the three main subcultures of the Sioux, or *Očhéthi Šakówiŋ*, the third being the Lakota. The Eastern Dakota, or Santee (*Isáŋyathi*, "knife encampment"), comprise the Bdewákaŋthuŋwaŋ, Waȟpéthuŋwaŋ, Waȟpékhute and Sisíthuŋwaŋ bands and live in the eastern Dakotas, central Minnesota and northern Iowa; the Western Dakota — Yankton (*Iháŋktȟuŋwaŋ*) and Yanktonai (*Iháŋktȟuŋwaŋna*), collectively *Wičhíyena* — live in the Upper Missouri River country, with communities on the Yankton, Crow Creek, Lower Brule, Standing Rock, Spirit Lake and Fort Peck reservations and at Canadian reserves including Birdtail, Oak Lake and Whitecap. Their language belongs to the Mississippi Valley branch of Siouan. The sources used give no population figure.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Glass-bead embroidery** — The dominant pattern-bearing technique in the catalogued objects: a beaded diadem worked in strict geometry on a white ground, a beaded bandolier, shirts "decorated with beadwork and fringe", a beaded hide pouch, and the beaded top and bottom panels of a quiver and bow case. One British Museum cap of cloth and beads, its shape derived from a military form, carries a design incorporating the United States flag — beadwork absorbing an imposed emblem.

**Porcupine-quill mosaic** — A knife sheath of prepared hide (Museum of Ethnography 1854.02.0024) is faced on one side with what the catalogue calls a kind of mosaic work made from strung coloured quill segments of *Erethizon*. The same record notes that making and ornamenting weapon containers and cases, including sheaths for guns and ammunition kept dry inside them, belonged to an old tradition.

**Painted hide and the winter count** — A painted buffalo hide in Gothenburg is catalogued simply as a *wintercount*: a pictorial hide record, the one documented Dakota form in which painting rather than stitching carries the information.

**Feather regalia with beaded band** — A headdress (Museum of Ethnography 1928.01.0001) sets pale feathers into a casing of thick red cloth that continues as a back ornament, which the catalogue compares to the feather regalia of Dakota chiefs; its beaded diadem sits above a shorter, narrower beaded browband.

The sources name none of the stitch types sometimes attributed to Dakota beadwork, so no stitch terminology is given here.

**Motif vocabulary.** Three whole and two half pale-red stars, each divided into parallelograms; four figures of squares inscribed one within another in red, black, yellow and blue; a border of many small figures, mostly blue and green; four red slashes painted on the back of each bow limb, with red pigment on the belly of the bow.

### Clothing & dress

The nineteenth-century studio portraits of Yankton-Yanktonai, Santee and related Sioux leaders document a consistent male wardrobe: a cloth or decorated shirt, sometimes with feathers at the upper sleeves; embroidered leggings; moccasins; and a blanket or fur blanket wrapped at the waist or over the shoulder. Over this came an embroidered "warrior cloak" or a tasselled, embroidered warrior jacket, as worn by Smutty Bear (*Ma-to-sa-bi-tshi-a*) and Two Lances (*Tow-a-hu-kasa-no-pa*). Headgear ranges from a single eagle feather worn in the hair, through a headband or a cloth hair wrap, to a fur hat with one eagle feather (Struck by the Ree, *Pa-da-ni A-pa-pi*) and the full eagle-feather headdress or warbonnet worn by Sacred Hand Hawk (*Cetannapewakan*) and Yellow Thunder. Ear and neck ornaments of dentalium and abalone shell, and bone breastplates, complete it. Women's dress appears in one record: Two Strike's wife in a patterned cloth dress with dentalium ear ornaments, hair in two braids, a woven blanket across her knees. Standing Soldier (*A-ki-ci-ta-na-jin*), a Santee Dakota man, was photographed in a three-piece suit and tie holding a bowler hat and a pipe.

### Architecture

The only architectural trace is linguistic: the Dakota prefix *wi-* serves, among other functions, as the classifier for terms relating to the *thípi*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Stone, bone, wood and iron are all represented: a flaked red stone spear-head and a bone scraper from the British Museum, and a nineteenth-century frame drum of wood and various materials at the Metropolitan Museum. The pipe-tomahawk is the signature composite object — one from South Dakota is catalogued in Gothenburg as a *piptomahawk* with inlays, and tomahawks and pipes recur as held objects in the portraits. Yankton archery is described in unusual detail: a bow of ash or white oak, sinew-backed with the sinew coated in a white chalky material against moisture, 45.25 inches tip to tip and very strong, strung with two-ply sinew tied to the lower limb with a slip knot; arrows of split hickory rather than shoots, 23.75 to 26.25 inches, with iron broad-head points, fletched with two hawk feathers and a turkey cock feather set with animal glue and sinew, blue and green paint beneath the feathers and widely flared nocks. A brain-tanned buckskin quiver (26.5 inches) and bow case (46⅜ inches) carry beadwork and sinew-sewn fringe.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The documented centrepiece is a bear-claw necklace of otter skin (Museum of Ethnography 1854.02.0025): claws sewn along the lower edge over a roll of tightly twisted cloth and hide strips, small patches of red cloth set between them, the claws linked to one another by cotton thread passed through holes bored at their centres, their undersides painted, blue glass beads strung between them, and a tassel of several fabrics finishing the row. Portrait evidence adds dentalium and abalone-shell ear ornaments, bone breastplates, eagle feathers worn singly or in headdresses, and hair worn in two braids or bound on one side with a cloth wrap. A beaded hide pouch sewn in the form of a lizard is catalogued as an amulet; a comparable Teton Sioux example at the Pitt Rivers Museum held a girl child's umbilical cord, hung on the cradle and later worn by the mother.

## Music & performance

Dakota music centres on the voice over drumming. A traditional dance song opens high, led by one singer whose phrase the group repeats, then cascades to a lower pitch before a brief pause; the second half echoes the first in what is termed incomplete repetition, and the lead singer decides how many times the whole is repeated. Many songs use only vocables — syllabic utterances without lexical meaning — and sometimes only the second half carries words. Women sing an octave above the men but never on a song's first rendition and never the lead line. Percussion is drum-based, sometimes syncopated; in contest songs for men's traditional, chicken and fancy categories the beat begins as an irregular ruffle and then turns to a swift regular pulse. "Honor beats", usually four, represent cannon fire in battle. The Lakota Flag Song opens powwows and is not danced to; other songs honour veterans and warriors. Away from the powwow the genres are Sun dance, *Yuwipi*, *Inipi*, courtship, flute, lullaby, peyote and Christian hymn songs — the last still being provisioned for, with a Dakota-language hymnal funded in 2025 for the Episcopal Diocese of South Dakota. Named Dakota singing groups include Elk Soldier and Bad Nation.

## Dance & theatre

The sources treat dance mainly through the songs that carry it: the powwow contest categories of men's traditional, chicken and fancy, and the Sun dance as a song genre outside the powwow circuit. They note that the Flag Song, unlike dance songs, has no dance attached. They describe no Dakota masked, puppet or dramatic tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources name powwows as the principal public gathering, opened by the Flag Song and structured around the contest dance categories, with honour songs for veterans and warriors. Ceremonial contexts named outside the powwow are the Sun dance, *Yuwipi* and *Inipi*. The one life-cycle rite documented in the object records is the beaded lizard-form amulet holding an infant girl's umbilical cord, hung on her cradle and afterwards worn by her mother. The Yankton and Yanktonai retained, by treaty, their role within the *Očhéthi Šakówiŋ* as caretakers of the Pipestone Quarry, described as the cultural centre of the Sioux people; they were themselves engaged in quarrying pipestone, the material of the pipes and pipe-tomahawks that recur throughout the collection. The sources give no annual calendar of dated festivals.

## Foodways

Before the seventeenth century the Santee Dakota lived around Lake Superior, in what is now northern Minnesota and Wisconsin, where they gathered wild rice, hunted woodland animals and fished from canoes. On the prairie the Yankton hunted deer, antelope and small game with the bow, and were reported able to kill a bison with each arrow in a quiver. Food shortage enters the historical record directly: low food supplies, together with the United States' failure to make treaty payments on time, precipitated the Dakota War of 1862.

## Oral tradition & literature

Narrative is lexicalised in the language itself: *wóyakA* means "to tell a story", *wókiyakA* "to tell someone", and *wóyakapi* "a story, a narrative" or "they are all telling stories". The painted winter-count hide is the material counterpart — history kept in pictures on bison skin. Written Dakota literature has a documentary core in the *Dakota Prisoner of War Letters*, fluent Dakota-language letters written from the Camp Kearney prison camp at Davenport, Iowa, in 1863–1866 and edited by Clifford Canku and Michael Simon. *Remember This! Dakota Decolonization and the Eli Taylor Narratives* builds its history entirely from Indigenous oral tradition as primary source. Dakota authors in the record include Zitkala-Ša (Gertrude Simmons Bonnin, 1876–1938), the Yankton writer, educator and activist; Ohíyes'a (Charles Eastman); Ella Cara Deloria (1889–1971), ethnographer and linguist; and Vine Deloria Jr. (1933–2005). The University of Minnesota and Sisseton Wahpeton College are jointly preparing a Dakota Language Audio Journal of recorded conversations and stories.

## Language & religion

Dakota (*Dakhód'iapi*, *Dakȟótiyapi*) is a Mississippi Valley Siouan language within the Siouan-Catawban family, mutually intelligible with Lakota and more distantly related to Stoney and Assiniboine. It has two dialects with two sub-dialects each — Eastern Dakota (Santee, Sisseton) and Western Dakota (Yankton, Yanktonai) — differing in consonant clusters, in the diminutive suffix (*-daŋ*, *-da* in Santee against *-na* elsewhere), in ablaut grades and in vocabulary, with Yankton-Yanktonai lexically closer to Lakota. It is polysynthetic and agglutinative, subject-object-verb, written in the Latin script, with five oral and three nasal vowels. Revitalisation runs through the University of Minnesota's Dakota major and Dakota Language House, Sisseton Wahpeton College, Sitting Bull College, Cankdeska Cikana Community College, the Lower Sioux immersion Head Start, Dakota Wicohan, the *Speak Dakota!* textbooks and the 2023 app *Dakhód Iápi Wičhóie Wówapi*. On religion the sources attest only song genres — Sun dance, *Yuwipi*, *Inipi*, peyote and Christian hymnody.
