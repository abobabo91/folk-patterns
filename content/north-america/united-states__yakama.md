## Overview

The Yakama live mainly in eastern Washington and belong to the Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakama Nation. Their reservation covers about 1.2 million acres along the Yakima River. Their historic territory reached across the Columbia River Plateau. Documented cultural features include salmon fishing, hunting, gathering, beadwork, regalia, and longhouse life.

## Material culture

Documented material culture centers on beaded skin and cloth objects, leather, skin, glass beads, and textile fasteners.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Beadwork appears on gauntlets, bags, purses, and handbags, with flower, animal, and geometric designs.

- **Beaded skin gauntlets** — These light brown skin hand coverings have fringe and glass beads in geometric, floral, and animal designs.
- **Beaded cloth bags** — These sewn cotton, wool, velvet, or felted-wool bags have skin or braided-skin straps and beaded flower, bird, and animal designs.
- **Beaded purses and handbags** — A cloth purse has a metal clasp, red rose beadwork, and a black cotton strap.
- **Beaded skin bag** — A skin bag has flower-printed lining, an eagle design, and leather tassels decorated with blue glass beads.

### Clothing & dress

The sources document beaded skin gauntlets and photographs of Yakama people in regalia.

- **Skin gauntlets** — The documented gauntlets are made from skin, including smoked skin, with fringe and glass beadwork.
- **Regalia** — Photographs show Yakama men gathered in a longhouse and the sons of a Yakama chief wearing regalia.

### Architecture

The sources mention longhouse life but give no details about construction or design.

- **Longhouse** — Photographs show Yakama men gathered in regalia at a longhouse in Toppenish, Washington.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The documented everyday objects are textile items with metal fasteners and straps made from several materials.

- **Metal clasps** — Metal clasps appear on a cloth purse and another textile object.
- **Metal snap closures** — Metal snap closures are recorded on two textile objects.
- **Braided skin straps** — Some cloth bags have braided skin straps.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources document decorative beadwork, tassels, and fringe attached to skin and textile objects.

- **Glass beadwork** — Glass beads decorate gauntlets, bags, and purses.
- **Leather tassels** — A skin bag has nine sets of leather tassels hanging from its bottom.
- **Skin fringe** — Fringed skin elements are attached to gauntlets and bags.

## Music & performance

The sources name the Washane religion and its prophets.

- **Dreamer Religion** (*Washane*) — Washane is associated with the Wanapum dreamer-prophet Smohalla.
- **Smohalla** — Smohalla was a prominent Washane prophet whose name means “Dreamer” or “Preacher.”
- **Other Washane prophets** — The sources also name Chief Homli, Kotiakan, Lishwailait, and Ashnithlai.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe seasonal camps, fishing places, salmon trade, and treaty-protected fishing rights.

- **Summer and early fall campsite** (*Mool-mool*) — Mool-mool was a campsite during the summer and early fall seasons.
- **Fishing ground** — Priest Rapids was an important Wanapum settlement and fishing ground.
- **Annual salmon runs** — Yakama hunters and gatherers traded salmon harvested from annual runs in the Columbia River.
- **Boldt Decision** — United States v. Washington reaffirmed treaty-protected fishing rights in 1974.
- **Sohappy v. Smith** — United States v. Oregon, also called Sohappy v. Smith, reaffirmed treaty-protected fishing rights in 1969.

## Foodways

Salmon was the principal documented foodway, supported by fishing grounds and place names linked to fish.

- **Salmon** — The Yakama harvested and traded salmon from annual Columbia River runs.
- **Prosser Falls fishery** — Prosser Falls was an important historical fishery.
- **Cowlitz Falls fishery** — The Cowlitz Falls of the Cowlitz River was an important historical fishery.
- **Wanapum fishing grounds** (*Priest Rapids*) — Priest Rapids was a Wanapum fishing ground.
- **Salmon drying place** (*Teanaway River*) — Teanaway River means “[salmon] drying place.”

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources name the traditional language and the Dreamer Religion but do not summarize Yakama stories or literary traditions.

- **This language** (*Ichishkíin Sɨ́nwit*) — Ichishkíin Sɨ́nwit means “this language” and is promoted instead of the name Sahaptin.
- **Dreamer Religion** (*Washane*) — Washane is named in the sources, with Smohalla as its most prominent leader.
- **Ghost Voices** — Donald M. Hines’s book Ghost Voices is listed as further reading about Yakima myths, legends, humor, and hunting stories.

## Language & religion

Yakama is a Sahaptin dialect called Ichishkíin Sɨ́nwit, and the sources document the Washane religion.

- **Yakama language** (*Ichishkíin*) — The Yakama call their language Ichishkíin, also spelled Ichishkíin Sɨ́nwit, Íchishkin, Íchishkink, or Chishkíin.
- **In this way or manner** (*Ichishkínk*) — Ichishkínk literally means “in this way/manner.”
- **In this way or manner** (*Chishkín*) — Chishkín literally means “in this way/manner.”
- **Dreamer Religion** (*Washane*) — The Washane was led most prominently by Smohalla.
