## Overview

The Montaukett, more commonly known as Montauk, are an Algonquian-speaking Native American people from the eastern and central sections of Long Island, New York, United States. They spoke an Eastern Algonquian language: before the 17th century, their language was the Mohegan-Pequot language, also known as the Algonquian “N” dialect, and around 1600 they moved to the “Y” dialect. The Montaukett shared a common culture with Native Americans of the east end of Long Island and with Lenape groups along much of the northern shore of Long Island Sound. Their history includes farming, fishing, hunting, gathering, woodworking, basketry, wampum manufacture, and whaling. The population declined through disease, warfare, land loss, and colonial pressure. There is currently no state- or federally recognized Montauk tribe.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do record that Montaukett men and women participated in local and global markets and that descendants were employed as laborers, farmhands, domestic servants, and seamstresses.

### Architecture

Montaukett people lived in wigwams. In December 1741, Rev. Azariah Horton recorded visiting the wigwams of Montaukett people in Montauk who were suffering from illnesses. The sources also describe later houses at Indian Fields and Freetown, including the Pharoah Home or homes at Indian Fields. Montaukett woodworking included making bowls, scrubs, tools, toys, and later houses and mills. Relics and ruins of Montaukett settlements are visible at Theodore Roosevelt County Park, on the edge of the village of Montauk, New York.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Montaukett manufactured *wampompeag*, or wampum, from quahog clamshells available on Long Island. Before obtaining metal awls from Europeans, Montaukett artisans made disk-shaped beads from quahog shells for trade and tribute payments with nearby tribes. Other documented objects and crafts include wooden bowls, scrubs, tools, toys, baskets, eel pots, and rush and cane bottoms for chairs.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used document wampompeag or wampum beads made from quahog clamshells.

## Music & performance

They record that George Pharoah, described as the oldest man and chief of the Montauk tribe, provided John Lyon Gardiner with a vocabulary of the Indian language spoken by the Montauk tribe on March 25, 1798. The same account stated that only about seven people could then speak the language and that it would disappear within a few years. The sources also mention Rev. Azariah Horton and Samson Occom preaching and ministering among Montaukett people.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record religious instruction and mission activity. During and after the 1730–1740 First Great Awakening, Montaukett people received attention from New Light preachers, especially James Davenport and Azariah Horton. Horton ministered to them from 1740 to about 1750, preaching in their wigwams, and Samson Occom came to Montauk in 1749 to minister and educate them from 1749–1761. In 1773, Samson Occum and David Fowler formed the “Brothertown Plan” with members of the neighboring Shinnecock and Christian Algonquins, including contingents of the Mohegan, Pequot, and Narragansett, to move to the Oneida Territory.

## Foodways

The Montaukett farmed, fished, hunted, and gathered food. Women harvested corn, squash, and beans. Men fished and hunted whales, using dugout canoes made by hollowing out large trees. Traditional skills also supplied game and fowl for colonists’ tables. Montaukett craftwork included eel pots, while agricultural life included fields of winter wheat. An agreement from 1703 allowed the Montauketts to keep a 30-acre field enclosed to protect crops of winter wheat and limited their livestock to 250 swine and 50 head of cattle or horses.

Whaling was an important economic activity. Montaukett men were skilled at whaling and were sought after by those engaged in the trade. Between 1677 and 1684, an indigenous man and his family could purchase goods from local merchants in exchange for his share, or “lay,” of the catch during the following whaling season. In-shore operations eventually over-fished local seas, and Montaukett labor became important to deep-sea whaling through the late eighteenth century. Indigenous men continued to work in whaling through the early twentieth century.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record language documentation and literary translation. On March 25, 1798, John Lyon Gardiner wrote down a vocabulary supplied by George Pharoah. Cockenoe, a Montaukett native captured during the Pequot War, worked with John Eliot in Boston in the 1640s to translate the first parts of the Eliot Indian Bible before returning to Long Island. Rev. Thomas James allegedly composed a catechism in the Montauk language, described as a variant of Mohegan-Pequot, but the catechism has never been found.

Today, the Montauk Tribe of Indians is working to revitalize the Montauk language and culture. The sources also describe archaeological and historical research on Montaukett houses and settlements, including Allison Manfra McGovern’s study of Montaukett life from about 1750–1885.

## Language & religion

The Montaukett are an Algonquian-speaking Native American people. Their language belonged to the Eastern Algonquian group and was identified as the Mohegan-Pequot language, also known as the Algonquian “N” dialect, before the movement to the “Y” dialect around 1600. In 1798, George Pharoah stated that the language was spoken with little difference by Indians across the east end of Long Island and perhaps the whole island and adjoining islands, and that the Moheags of Connecticut spoke the same language.

Christian mission activity became prominent during the eighteenth century. New Light preachers visited the Montaukett, Azariah Horton ministered among them, and Samson Occom provided ministry and education.
