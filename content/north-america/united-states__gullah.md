## Overview

The Gullah, also known in some areas as Geechees or Gullah–Geechees, are an African American ethnic group rooted in the coastal areas of North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia and Florida, from roughly Pender County, North Carolina, to St. Augustine, Florida. They descend from West and Central Africans enslaved on plantations in the islands, waterways and coasts of this region. Today, Gullah is spoken by about 5,000 people, mostly in coastal South Carolina and Georgia; as of 2021, an estimated 300 people were native speakers. Their English-based creole language and distinctive culture developed through geographic isolation, preserving African linguistic and cultural influences in crafts, farming, fishing, foodways, music, beliefs and storytelling.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Sweetgrass baskets** — *Sweetgrass baskets* are coil straw baskets made by descendants of enslaved peoples in the South Carolina Lowcountry. The source describes them as nearly identical to traditional coil baskets made by the Wolof people in Senegal.

**Strip quilts** — *Strip quilts* imitate the design of cloth woven with the traditional strip loom used throughout West Africa. The source compares this construction with Kente cloth from the Ashanti and Ewe peoples and Akwete cloth from the Igbo people, all of which are woven on the strip loom.

Gullah rice farmers also used winnowing fanners resembling tools used by West African rice farmers.

**Motif vocabulary.** Strip-loom design — the source identifies this as the design principle imitated by Gullah strip quilts.

### Architecture

They do describe plantation landscapes, rice fields, ditches, banks, sluices, earthen dams and levees. In late 2024, underwater sonar mapped 45 previously unknown irrigation devices used to control water flow for rice fields over an area of 2,000 acres (800 hectares) at the northern end of Eagles Island, North Carolina, US. The investigation was presented as evidence of Gullah–Geechee engineering and technological skills in rice cultivation.

Gullah cultural survival is also associated with rural areas of the Lowcountry mainland, the Sea Islands, and urban communities such as Charleston and Savannah. The Gullah custom of painting porch ceilings haint blue to deter haints, or ghosts, survives in the American South.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do document large wooden mortars and pestles used to process rice and large round winnowing baskets used to separate grain and chaff. Gullah rice farmers also made and used mortar and pestles and winnowing fanners similar in style to tools used by West African rice farmers.

## Music & performance

Gullah culture has a musical tradition shaped by relative isolation and strong cultural links with Africa. Much Gullah music follows a call-and-response structure common in African musical traditions. The most well-known proponents named in the sources are the Georgia Sea Island Singers, a group founded in the early 1900s on St. Simon's Island. Led mostly by Bessie Jones, they released many albums, were recorded by Alan Lomax, and performed at Carnegie Hall and the Newport Folk Festival.

The Gullah ring shout is described as similar to ecstatic religious rituals performed in West and Central Africa. Gullah–Geechee people brought the ring shout to northern African American communities in Harlem, where the ritual dance later evolved to include jazz piano.

An African song preserved by a Gullah family in coastal Georgia was identified in the 1940s by Lorenzo Turner as a Mende song from Sierra Leone. In the 1990s, Joseph Opala, Cynthia Schmidt and Taziff Koroma located a remote village in Sierra Leone where the song was still sung and determined that it was a funeral hymn. The resulting reunion between a Gullah family and a Mende family is recounted in *The Language You Cry In* (1998).

## Dance & theatre

The sources name the Gullah ring shout, a ritual involving song and dance that is compared with ecstatic religious rituals in West and Central Africa. They also state that the ritual dance brought to Harlem evolved to include jazz piano.

Gullah culture has appeared in documentary films and popular media. Julie Dash wrote and directed *Daughters of the Dust* in 1991, the first feature film about the Gullah, set at the turn of the 20th century on St. Helena Island. The sources also name *Family Across the Sea* (1990), *The Language You Cry In* (1998), and *Priscilla's Legacy* as documentary films concerning Gullah history and connections with West Africa.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe annual Gullah cultural festivals in towns across the Lowcountry. Hilton Head Island hosts a “Gullah Celebration” in February, including the “De Aarts ob We People” show, the “Ol’ Fashioned Gullah Breakfast,” “National Freedom Day,” the “Gullah Film Fest,” “A Taste of Gullah,” a “Celebration of Lowcountry Authors and Books,” an “Arts, Crafts & Food Expo,” and “De Gullah Playhouse.” Beaufort hosts “The Original Gullah Festival” in May. Penn Center on St. Helena Island holds “Heritage Days” in November. Other festivals are held on James Island, South Carolina, and Sapelo Island, Georgia.

The Gullah *seekin* ritual is compared with coming-of-age ceremonies in West African secret societies such as the Poro and Sande. Gullah beliefs about hags and haunts are compared with African beliefs about malevolent ancestors, witches and devils, described as forest spirits. Gullah root doctors protect clients from dangerous spiritual forces with ritual objects similar to those used by African traditional healers, while Gullah herbal medicines resemble traditional African remedies.

The sources also describe three Gullah homecomings to Sierra Leone, in 1989, 1997 and 2005.

## Foodways

Rice is a staple food in Gullah communities and continues to be cultivated in abundance in coastal Georgia and South Carolina. The Gullah have preserved foodways involving Sea island red peas, Carolina Gold rice, Sea island Benne, Sea island Okra, sorghum and watermelon, all described as crops brought from West Africa. Rice was a core commodity of the Gullah food system: a meal was not considered complete without it.

Gullah rice preparation involved removing darker grains and washing the rice by hand numerous times. Enough water was added for the rice to steam on its own, without requiring stirring or draining. The sources state that these techniques were passed down during slavery and remain important in Gullah rice preparation.

Named Gullah foods include red rice and okra soup. They are compared with West African jollof rice and okra soup, while jollof rice is described as a traditional rice preparation brought by the Wolof people of West Africa. The Gullah version of “gumbo” has roots in African cooking; the word “gumbo” derives from *ngombo*, meaning okra in several western Bantu languages of the Bakongo and Ambundu people of Angola.

The first high-profile book on Gullah cooking was published in 2022 by Emily Meggett, an 89-year-old Gullah cook.

## Oral tradition & literature

Gullah storytelling is strongly influenced by African oral traditions and by the historical experience of Gullah people in America. Stories include animal trickster tales about “Br'er Rabbit,” “Br'er Fox,” “Br'er Bear” and “Br'er Wolf”; human trickster tales about clever and self-assertive slaves; and morality tales intended to teach children.

Gullah stories about “Br'er Rabbit” are compared with West and Central African trickster tales involving the clever rabbit, spider and tortoise. White writers collected Gullah stories in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The sources identify Charles Colcock Jones Jr. from Georgia, Albert Henry Stoddard from South Carolina, Abigail Christensen, and Ambrose E. Gonzales as collectors or writers associated with Gullah storytelling.

Lorenzo Dow Turner’s research in rural communities of coastal South Carolina and Georgia identified African influences in Gullah phonology, vocabulary, grammar, sentence structure and semantics. Turner identified over 300 African loanwords and almost 4,000 African personal names used by Gullah people. He also recorded songs, story fragments and simple counting in Mende, Vai and Fulani. His findings appeared in *Africanisms in the Gullah Dialect*, published in 1949 and reprinted in a fourth edition in 2002.

## Language & religion

Gullah is an English-based creole language, also called Gullah-English, Sea Island Creole English and Geechee. It is historically spoken by Gullah people in the coastal areas of North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia and Florida. Its vocabulary comes primarily from English, while African languages influenced its grammar, sentence structure, pronunciation and vocabulary. The sources state that Gullah resembles English-based creoles in West Africa and the Caribbean Basin, including Krio of Sierra Leone, and is most closely related to Afro-Seminole Creole.

The sources name loanwords including *cootuh* (“turtle”), *oonuh* (“you,” plural), *nyam* (“eat”), *buckruh* (“white man”), *pojo* (“heron”), *swonguh* (“proud”) and *benne* (“sesame”). They also record *bigyai* (“greedy”), *pantap* (“on top of”), *ohltu* (“both”), *tif* (“steal”), *yeys* (“ear”) and *swit* (“delicious”). In 2005, Gullah community leaders announced the completion of a modern Gullah translation of the New Testament, a project that took more than 20 years. In 2017, Harvard University began offering Gullah/Geechee as a language class.

They do describe beliefs about hags, haunts, malevolent ancestors, witches and devils, as well as root doctors, herbal medicines, the seekin ritual and the ring shout.
