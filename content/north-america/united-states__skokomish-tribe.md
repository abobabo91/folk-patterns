## Overview

The Skokomish are one of nine tribes of the Twana, a Native American people of western Washington state in the United States. They live along Hood Canal, a fjord-like inlet on the west side of the Kitsap Peninsula and the Puget Sound basin, and are enrolled in the federally recognized Skokomish Indian Tribe. The tribe moved onto the Skokomish Indian Reservation in the central part of modern-day Mason County, Washington, at the southeast base of the Olympic Peninsula around 1855. The reservation has a land area of 21.244 km2 and had a 2000 census resident population of 730 persons. The Skokomish, or Twana language, belongs to the Coast Salish family of indigenous languages. Fishing, cedar working, basketry, potlatch, songs, stories, and communal gatherings are central subjects in the available account.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Cedar bark and root work** — Cedar bark and roots were used to make mats, clothing, ropes, and baskets. The source describes basketry as highly developed and says it often incorporated intricate twined designs unique to the Twana peoples.

**Cedar canoes** — Canoes were carved from large cedar logs and formed part of the material culture associated with life along the river and Hood Canal.

**Cedar longhouses** — Longhouses were also carved from large cedar logs and provided an important architectural setting for community life.

## Music & performance

The Skokomish practiced the Coast Salish tradition of the potlatch, a ceremonial event that included feasting, dancing, singing, and the giving of gifts. Potlatches reaffirmed social bonds and status and marked major life events such as marriages, inheritance, and memorials. The sources also state that many traditional stories and songs reflected relationships with the salmon, cedar, and river, which were regarded as sources of life. These stories and songs were passed down orally through generations.

## Dance & theatre

## Foodways

Historically, the Twana were hunters, fishers, and gatherers. They had a nomadic lifestyle during the warmer months and lived in more permanent homes during the winter months. Traditional Skokomish life centered particularly on fishing, with salmon holding both economic and spiritual significance. The sources also state that potlatches included feasting.

In recent decades, declining dissolved oxygen levels in the lower Hood Canal have contributed to reduced harvests of spot shrimp, an important traditional and subsistence resource for the Skokomish people, particularly in Anna’s Bay. Researchers have linked hypoxia, or low oxygen, to nutrient runoff, septic leakage, and algae blooms resulting from land-use changes in the surrounding watershed. The Skokomish Tribe has participated in regional restoration efforts intended to improve water quality and revive the spot shrimp population, combining traditional ecological knowledge with modern environmental science.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that many traditional stories and songs reflected relationships with the salmon, cedar, and river, which were understood as sources of life. These stories and songs were passed down orally through generations.

The Skokomish maintained much of their cultural identity despite pressure to abandon traditional fishing and spiritual practices. The sources connect this pressure to U.S. assimilation policies and the introduction of boarding schools during the late nineteenth century. They also identify fishing and communal gatherings along Hood Canal as important areas of cultural continuity.

## Language & religion

The Skokomish, or Twana language, belongs to the Coast Salish family of indigenous languages. The name “Skokomish” comes from the Twana *sqʷuqʷóbəš*, also spelled *sqWuqWu'b3sH*, meaning “river people” or “people of the river.”

Traditional spiritual beliefs were closely tied to the natural world, with particular respect for the salmon, cedar, and river. These were described as sources of life, and the relationships were expressed through stories and songs transmitted orally.
