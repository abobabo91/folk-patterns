## Overview

The Aleuts are Indigenous people of the Aleutian Islands, between the North Pacific Ocean and the Bering Sea. The islands and their communities are politically divided between Alaska and Kamchatka Krai. They are also known as Unangan, Unangas, and *Unangax̂*, while their language is called Unangam Tunuu. The Aleut language belongs to the Eskaleut language family and has Eastern Aleut, Atkan, and Attuan dialects. Aleuts historically lived throughout the Aleutian Islands, the Shumagin Islands, and the far western Alaska Peninsula. Before European contact, approximately 25,000 Aleut lived in the archipelago; the 2020 Census recorded 13,805 people identifying as Aleut. Their material culture centers on marine hunting, skin clothing, boats, basketry, carving, and bone and ivory work.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Parkas** — Aleut women made waterproof parkas from seal gut and sea-otter skin, while men used bird skin and waterproof garments made from seal or sea-lion guts. Gut garments were sewn from prepared intestines and decorated with bird feathers, seal and sea-lion beard bristles, dyed leather, beaks, claws, and caribou hair.

**Kameikas** — *Kameikas* were parkas made from downy eagle skin, with tanned bird-skin caps for children. The term is explained in the source as meaning “rain gear” in English.

**Gutskin raincoats** — Kamleikas were waterproof parkas or shirts made from sewn strips of intestine, sometimes identified in the museum records as sea-lion intestine. Some examples had hoods, colored gut-skin strips, wool yarn, and embroidered white animal hair.

**Bird-skin parkas** — Men wore bird-skin parkas, with the feathers turned inward or outward according to the weather. Museum records also describe trousers made from bird skin, sewn from rectangular pieces with edging at the seams.

**Grass baskets** — Aleut women made fine baskets from sea-lyme grass. The traditional basketry method was false embroidery, in which strands of grass or reeds were overlaid on the basic woven surface.

**Qiigam aygaaxsii** — *Qiigam aygaaxsii* is the Aleut term for a grass basket. Aleut women made baskets and woven mats of high technical quality using only a sharpened thumbnail as a tool.

**Woven grass pieces** — Aleut weavers continue to produce woven grass pieces with a cloth-like texture. Birch bark, puffin feathers, and baleen are also used in basketry.

**Motif vocabulary.** Anthropomorphic creatures in masks, local animals such as seals and whales, hunters in kayaks, walruses, fish, and animal heads.

### Clothing & dress

Both men and women wore parkas extending below the knees. Women wore seal or sea-otter skin, while men wore bird-skin parkas. For hunting on the water, men wore waterproof parkas made from seal or sea-lion guts, or from the entrails of bear, walrus, or whales. These parkas had hoods and wrist openings that could be cinched against water. Men also wore breeches made from the esophageal skin of seals.

Children wore downy eagle-skin parkas and tanned bird-skin caps. The preparation of gut clothing involved removing muscle tissue and fat, cutting and stretching the gut, drying it on stakes, and sewing it into parkas, bags, and other receptacles. Museum records describe kamleikas with hoods, colored gut-skin decoration, wool yarn, and animal-hair embroidery.

### Architecture

Aleuts historically built partially underground houses called *barabara*. A barabara was made by digging an oblong square pit, usually 50 by 20 feet or smaller. Driftwood formed the roof frame, which was thatched with grass and covered with earth for insulation.

Trenches were dug along the inside walls and covered with mats to keep them clean. Bedrooms were at the back of the lodge, opposite the entrance. Several families could stay in one house, each with a designated area. Instead of a central fireplace or bonfire, lanterns were hung inside.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources document tools and household objects made from local minerals, stone, wood, bone, ivory, and animal materials. Archaeological tools included obsidian and chert blades, bird-bone needles and awls, and mammal-bone wedges, spoons, and harpoon points.

Museum records identify slate knives with one sharpened side, bone harpoon points, harpoon shafts, harpoon arrows, a walrus-ivory mouthpiece for a harpoon bladder, and a wooden eye screen. A British Museum record describes a spoon or ladle carved from Mountain Sheep horn. Aleut hunters also used throwing boards, barbed darts, and harpoons.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Aleut body adornment included walrus-ivory jewelry, beads, animal hair, feathers, and sea-lion whiskers. Jewelry designs were specific to the region of each people, and clans had styles signifying their origin. Ornaments were made for the lips, nose, and ears, as well as for necklaces. Museum records include a neck ornament made from carved teeth and ornaments consisting of bundled sea-lion whiskers threaded with beads.

Before the 19th century, piercings and tattoos were common, especially among women. Nose pins could be made from bark, bone, or an eagle’s feather shaft and could be decorated with amber and coral. Ears were pierced with dentalium shells, bone, feathers, dried bird wings or skulls, and amber. Men wore sea-lion whiskers in their ears as hunting trophies. Walrus ivory, beads, or bones were worn through the lower lip. Tattoos could mark physical maturity, hunting achievement, social class, or family accomplishments.

## Music & performance

The sources state that masks were used in ceremonies ranging from dances to praises, with each mask having its own meaning and purpose.

## Dance & theatre

The sources describe wooden masks representing figures from myths and oral history, including anthropomorphic beings associated with the belief among the Atka people that another people had lived in their land before them. The masks were used in ceremonies involving dances and praises.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources record several ritual or life-stage practices associated with body adornment: nose piercing was usually performed a few days after birth; women began tattooing after physical maturity and menstruation, at about age 20; and men received their first tattoo after killing their first animal.

Aleut masks were used in ceremonies, including dances and praises. Tattoos and piercings were connected with religious views, beauty, social status, reputation, spiritual authority, and protection from evil entities.

## Foodways

Traditional Aleut subsistence depended on hunting and gathering. Aleuts fished for salmon, crabs, shellfish, and cod, and hunted seal, walrus, whales, and other sea mammals. Fish and sea mammals were dried, smoked, or roasted. Caribou, muskoxen, deer, moose, whale, and other game were roasted or preserved for later use.

Berries were dried and sometimes processed as *alutiqqutigaq*, a mixture of berries, fat, and fish. Boiled whale skin and blubber, as well as walrus skin and blubber, are described as delicacies. Aleuts also used locally available plants and resources; a full ethnobotanical list records 65 documented plant uses.

The sources state that many Aleut continue to eat customary and locally sourced foods while also buying processed foods from abroad, which is expensive in Alaska.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources identify masks as representations of figures from myths and oral history. The Atka people believed that another people had lived in their land before them, and masks portrayed these ancients as anthropomorphic creatures named in the Aleut language.

The sources also record a Japanese account of the Aleut uprising against Russian traders in 1784 and state that the encounter of Daikokuya Kōdayū with Russians and Aleuts was depicted in the 1992 Japanese film *Dreams of Russia*. The sources do not describe contemporary Aleut literature, although they record ongoing revitalization efforts for the Aleut language and the teaching and revival of Aleut basketry.

## Language & religion

Aleut people traditionally speak Unangam Tunuu, the Aleut language, which belongs to the Eskaleut language family. Its three dialects are Eastern Aleut, Atkan, and the extinct Attuan dialect. Eastern Aleut was spoken on the Eastern Aleutian, Shumagin, Fox, and Pribilof Islands; Atkan on Atka and Bering islands; and Attuan was formerly spoken in the western islands. Beginning in 1829, Aleut was written in Cyrillic; from 1870 it was written in Latin script.

An estimated 150 people in the United States and five people in Russia speak Aleut, while most Aleuts now speak English and Russian in their respective countries. The Pribilof Islands have the highest number of active Unangam Tunuu speakers. The sources describe Russian Orthodox missionaries arriving in Kodiak in 1794 and many Aleuts becoming Christian. They also describe religious beliefs concerning spirits, evil entities, animal spirits, tattoos, and piercings.
