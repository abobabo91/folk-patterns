## Overview

The Clatsop (*tɬác̓əp*) are a Chinookan-speaking Indigenous people of the Pacific Northwest of the United States. In the 18th and early 19th centuries, they occupied the south bank of the Columbia River from Point Adams to Youngs Bay and the Pacific coast from Point Adams to Tillamook Head. Their descendants are members of the Confederated Tribes of Siletz Indians, the unrecognized Chinook Indian Nation, and the Clatsop-Nehalem Confederated Tribes. The Clatsop are one of the Lower Chinook, a cultural-geographical and linguistic grouping whose villages were at the mouth of the Columbia River. Their history documents village life, large cedar-plank longhouses, river and coastal foodways, trade, and changing relations with European visitors.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Clatsop villages included large longhouses constructed of cedar planks. In the account of William Clark, the Clatsop comprised about 200 people living in three separate villages. Individual families also affiliated with one another in small villages and seasonal camps located near food sources. The Vancouver expedition recorded a village at Point Adams and noted the presence of burial canoes.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that Clatsop members traded furs and other goods for European manufactured goods and shared salmon, berries, and hunting tips with the Corps of Discovery.

## Music & performance

The sources state that the Clatsop spoke a dialect of the Lower Chinookan language and that most Clatsops spoke Chinook Jargon by the time Lewis and Clark's Corps of Discovery made contact with them.

## Festivals & rituals

The Vancouver expedition noted the presence of burial canoes at a village at Point Adams. The sources also state that the Clatsop had designated headmen and that their society was socially flexible.

## Foodways

The Clatsop shared salmon, berries, and hunting tips with the Corps of Discovery. The name *tɬác̓əp* is explained as meaning “those who have pounded salmon,” and the Lower Chinook and Kathlamet called the Clatsop *tɬák̓ilak*, meaning “where there is pounded salmon.” The latter name derived from the main Clatsop village, *niák̓ilaki*.

## Oral tradition & literature

The journals of the Lewis and Clark expedition regularly named only two Clatsop, Coboway and Cuscalar. The sources also state that the first major European account to describe the Clatsop was the account of the Lewis and Clark expedition in 1805.

## Language & religion

The Clatsop are a Chinookan people and spoke a dialect of the Lower Chinookan language, which is now extinct. Most Clatsops spoke Chinook Jargon when Lewis and Clark's Corps of Discovery made contact with them, while some spoke Nehalem, reflecting intermarriage and cohabitation with that tribe. Chinook Jargon was a trade language used throughout much of the Pacific Northwest. In Chinook, the Columbia River is called *imaɬ* or *iyagaytɬ imaɬ*, “great water”; in Chinook Jargon, it is called *hayásh-tsəqʷ*, “great water.”
