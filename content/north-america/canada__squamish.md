## Overview

The Squamish people, who call themselves *Sḵwx̱wú7mesh*, are an Indigenous people of the Pacific Northwest Coast. Their traditional territory lies in southwestern British Columbia, extending from Point Grey northward to Roberts Creek on the Sunshine Coast and up Howe Sound, including the Squamish, Cheakamus, Elaho and Mamquam rivers, Indian Arm, Burrard Inlet, False Creek and English Bay. Today they live mainly in communities in West Vancouver, North Vancouver and the District of Squamish. In 2012, the Squamish Nation had 3,893 band members. Their language, *Sḵwx̱wú7mesh sníchim*, belongs to the Coast Salish languages and was considered nearly extinct, with 10 fluent speakers in 2010. Their folk-culture significance lies in oral tradition, longhouse life, canoe travel, cedar working, weaving, potlatch practices and continuing cultural revitalization.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Cedar-bark weaving** — Cedar bark was used to make baskets, hats and raincoats, and the art of cedar-bark weaving continues to be passed on. Cedar-bark clothing is part of a cultural tradition that has changed in form while remaining present.

**Cedar-bark clothing** — Clothing could be made from the inside bark of cedar trees. The sources distinguish this material practice from everyday modern clothing, which is worn alongside the continuing production of cedar-bark garments.

**Coast Salish textile weaving** — Weaving included blankets and clothing made from cedar bark, plant fibers and animal wool. Weavers were traditionally women of noble families, and blankets with geometric patterns were displayed at potlatches, given as gifts and worn during important ceremonies.

**Mountain goat wool blankets** — Blankets woven from mountain goat wool were particularly valued. The wool was collected in high alpine areas, and the blankets represented the work of highly skilled weavers.

**Basketry** — Baskets were among the visual forms associated with Squamish culture. The sources identify baskets and hats as objects made through weaving, including work with cedar bark.

**Cedar-bark raincoats** — Raincoats made from the inside bark of cedar trees formed part of the documented textile tradition.

**Motif vocabulary.** Formline elements, ovoids, U-shapes; the sources describe these as design principles of Northwest Coast Indigenous carving and do not assign them specifically to named Squamish textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe clothing made from cedar bark, plant fibers and animal wool, including cedar-bark clothing, raincoats, blankets and ceremonial regalia. Modern clothes are also worn, while cedar-bark weaving and cedar-bark clothing continue. Mountain goat wool blankets were worn during important ceremonies and could be given away at potlatches.

Ceremonial dress included beautiful regalia with colours and ornaments. During a marriage ceremony, the bride could wear regalia, have a blanket placed over her head like a veil, and carry personal belongings such as mats, baskets, blankets, wooden platters and spoons. In a puberty ceremony, a blanket could be draped over a young woman’s shoulders.

### Architecture

The principal traditional dwelling was the Coast Salish-style *longhouse*, built from cedar planks, posts and ties. Extended families lived in different quarters of the same house, and wooden platforms along the walls formed living and sleeping areas. Posts could be carved with ancestors, legendary events, family history and family rights in fishing, hunting or gathering. Beams crossed the posts to support the roof, while the posts on one side stood slightly taller, producing a small pitch or shed-like form.

A standard house was described as 9 metres wide, 12 metres long and 4–6 metres high, although sizes varied. Larger structures accommodated ceremonies, festivals, potlatches and spiritual work. These included potlatch houses, called *tl'e7enkáẃtxw*. One longhouse at X̱wáýx̱way was described as 200 feet long and 60 feet wide, while another at Chi'ch'elxwikw' measured 50 feet by 20 feet. Longhouses remain in use for traditional gatherings and ceremonies, and their architectural style has also been incorporated into modern community buildings.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Documented wooden and household objects include cedar boxes lined with cedar bark, canoes, fishing hooks, harpoon heads, wooden platters, spoons, baskets and architectural elements. Carvers also produced ceremonial masks, regalia and totem poles. Cedar was especially important, and its harvesting was described as a sacred and carefully regulated practice governed by ceremonies and protocols. Families used canoes, fishing equipment and other objects connected with gathering food and travelling between villages and resource sites.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They do describe ceremonial regalia with colours and ornaments, as well as several forms of bodily adornment and grooming. In one puberty rite, strands of mountain goat wool were tied to each side of a girl’s forehead. Women of her family pulled out irregular hairs from the edges of her eyebrows, and a plant mixture was used to prevent the hairs from growing again. After bathing, she could be painted with red ochre. During marriage ceremonies, the bride’s regalia and the blanket placed over her head were part of her ceremonial appearance.

## Music & performance

The sources describe singing, drumming and ceremonial speech rather than named instruments or song genres. At the bone game or *slahal*, two opposing teams played with bones and sticks, while drumming and singing accompanied the game. The team possessing the bones sang, and musical accompaniment could also be used to taunt the opposing team.

Performance was central to potlatches and other community events. At a potlatch, the host invited guests to a feast and hired a speaker to speak for the family. The speaker called out the names of guests and distributed blankets. In puberty ceremonies, people could beat on plank drums throughout the house while a young girl danced with a shawl. Singing accompanied the dancing.

The sources also describe ceremonial events involving feasting, speeches, dancing and the distribution of wealth.

## Dance & theatre

Dance occurred in ceremonies, festivals and potlatches. During one puberty ceremony, a young girl stood, danced around the house with a shawl and was accompanied by people beating and singing. Potlatches also included dancing as part of the feast and ceremonial proceedings.

The sources describe carved masks, totem poles and figures representing supernatural beings, animal ancestors and human figures.

## Festivals & rituals

The potlatch, called *tl'e7enk* in Squamish, was a major community event. A host or host family invited guests to a feast, dancing and ceremonies, and distributed food, blankets and other material wealth. Potlatches marked births, coming of age, naming ceremonies, marriages and memorial events for the deceased. They usually took place during the winter months, while summer was associated with travelling and harvesting.

The Canadian government banned the potlatch and made it illegal from 1884 to 1951. During that period, ceremonies and events went underground and were later revived. The sources also record a potlatch on Burrard Inlet in 1885 and a large potlatch at Xwáýxway in 1875, hosted by Supple Jack.

Life-cycle rites included puberty, naming, marriage, funerals and memorials. A young woman could fast for four days, avoid bathing and fires, receive instruction from female relatives, and later receive an ancestral name. Children first received a *ninamin* or nickname, while ancestral names called *kweshamin* could be bestowed through a naming ceremony or potlatch. Marriage involved fasting, family negotiations, canoes, blankets, gifts and feasts.

## Foodways

Salmon was the main staple and was once abundant in the territory. Squamish cuisine also included herring, shellfish, seal, berries and roots. The sources state that all foods eaten by the ancestors were considered traditional foods and were usually served at feasts celebrating Indigenous culture.

Food preparation was especially important in potlatches. The host invited guests to a feast, and food was prepared and shared with the community alongside dancing, speeches, ceremonies and the distribution of gifts.

Food also appears in oral tradition. In a story about the Great Flood at *Chʼiyáḵmesh*, the Thunderbird gave food to a man who had survived the flood and gathered more food for him as he travelled down the river.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral tradition transmitted history, literature, law and other knowledge verbally across generations without a writing system. The passing on of knowledge was regarded as the duty of responsible elders, and people who possessed extensive knowledge were regarded as aristocrats. Stories included the Transformer brothers, ancestral characters, the origins of the world and the Great Flood.

One origin account told by Mel̓ḵw's to Charles Hill-Tout described a time when water was everywhere. The tops of the mountains emerged from the sea, land was formed, and the first man, *X̱i7lánexw*, appeared. He received a wife, an adze and a salmon trap, and the Squamish descended from these ancestors. Other traditions named *Chʼiyáḵmesh*, *Sch'enḵ*, *Chekw'élhp*, Tseḵanchtn and Sx̱eláltn.

Charles Hill-Tout recorded Squamish oral history in 1886, and later anthropologists and linguists worked with Squamish elders and informants. Oral tradition continues to be passed on, while language documentation and revitalization projects support the preservation of cultural knowledge.

## Language & religion

Squamish, or *Sḵwxwú7mesh sníchim*, is a Coast Salish language spoken in southwestern British Columbia, particularly in Squamish, North Vancouver and West Vancouver. It is most closely related to Sechelt, Halkomelem and Nooksack. The language was historically oral; a writing system was developed in the 1960s, and the Squamish-English bilingual dictionary was published in 2011. In 1990, the Chief and Council declared Squamish the official language of their people. In 2010, it was considered critically endangered and nearly extinct, with 10 fluent speakers.

The sources describe spiritual beliefs, shamans, prophets, medicine doctors, ceremonialism and spiritual work in longhouses.
