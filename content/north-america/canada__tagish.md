## Overview

The Tagish or Tagish Khwáan (*Tā̀gish kotʼīnèʼ* in Tagish and *Taagish ḵwáan* in Tlingit) are a First Nations people of the Athabaskan-speaking ethnolinguistic group. They historically lived around Tagish Lake and Marsh Lake in Yukon, Canada, and their territory extended into southern Yukon Territory and northern British Columbia. Today, Tagish people live mainly in Carcross or Whitehorse and are members of the Carcross/Tagish First Nation or the Kwanlin Dün First Nation. The Tagish language is a Northern Athabaskan language closely related to Tahltan and Kaska. Tagish intermarried heavily with Tlingit from the coast, and their culture developed through connections between coastal indigenous cultures and those of the interior. Members of the Tagish First Nation made the gold discovery associated with the Klondike Gold Rush.

## Festivals & rituals

The Tagish historically practiced shamanism or animism. Belief in nature spirits, reincarnation, and personal spirit guides was widespread among men and some women. By the early 20th century, most Tagish were Christian.

The sources do describe historical activities connected with trading furs, fishing, and hunting. Tagish people maintained close trading contacts with the Tlingit until the late 19th century, when the Klondike gold rush disrupted the relationship. The culture of the Tagish people reflects both coastal indigenous cultures and those from the interior, and trade and travel across the Chilkoot pass contributed to cultural mixing.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used describe Tagish traditional stories and efforts to document and preserve them. Angela Sidney worked with Julie Cruikshank on documenting and authoring traditional stories. Lucy Wren participated in recordings and stories used on the First Voices website, including the “Our Elders Statement.” Her son Norman James continued work to record more of the language and culture of the Tagish and Tlingit people for the Yukon Native Language Centre and the First Voices website.

Tagish was one of the first languages added to the FirstVoices digital multimedia archive of endangered indigenous languages. FirstVoices is described as an Indigenous-language computer database and web-based teaching and development tool. Its resources include sound files of name pronunciation, word lists, and children’s books written in Tagish.

## Language & religion

Tagish is an extinct Northern Athabaskan language and part of the large Na-Dene language family. It is closely related to Tahltan and Kaska; these languages are sometimes grouped as Tahltan-Kaska-Tagish or Tagish-Tahltan-Kaska, and are sometimes considered dialects of the same language. Tagish is also closely related to Southern Tutchone. The Tagish people refer to themselves as */ta:gizi dene/*, literally “Tagish people,” while */ta:gizi/* is a place name meaning “it (spring ice) is breaking up.” The name also gave its name to Tagish Lake.

As of 2004, one native fluent speaker, Lucy Wren (Agaymā/Ghùch Tlâ), was documented; she died in 2008. Tlingit increasingly replaced Tagish as the language of the majority, and English became the majority language after the Yukon Gold Rush in 1898. The language uses the Latin writing system. Its documented features include aspiration, glottalization, nasal sounds, resonance, tones, oral and nasal vowels, and vowel length. Some women’s names contain the nasalized prefix *Maa*, translated as “mother of.”

The Tagish historically practiced shamanism or animism, including belief in nature spirits, reincarnation, and personal spirit guides. By the early 20th century, most Tagish were Christian.
