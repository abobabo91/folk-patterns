## Overview

The Apache are several Southern Athabaskan language-speaking peoples of the Southwest, the Southern Plains and Northern Mexico. They are linguistically related to the Navajo and migrated from the Athabascan homelands in the north into the Southwest between 1000 and 1500 CE. Historically, Apache homelands included high mountains, sheltered and watered valleys, deep canyons, deserts and the southern Great Plains, collectively known as Apacheria. Today, Apache tribes and reservations are headquartered in Arizona, New Mexico, Texas and Oklahoma, while Apache people in Mexico are settled in Sonora, Chihuahua, Coahuila and areas of Tamaulipas. Each tribe is politically autonomous. The sources describe Apache groups, languages, migration, warfare, plant uses and material objects, including baskets, clothing, water containers, rattles, masks and figures.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**San Carlos Apache coiled tray basket** — A museum catalogue describes a coiled tray basket from San Carlos, Arizona. Its conventionalized floral design is characteristic, and the yellow may be yucca. The encircling line is broken near the end of the coil on the rim; the catalogue records an explanation that this opening prevents the maker’s spirit from being caught in the design.

**White Mountain Apache bowl basket** — A White Mountain Apache bowl basket is described as having a characteristic design and a close and regular Apache stitch. The purple units are probably yucca. The basket was obtained from Tom Pavatea, a Hopi Indian trader at Polacca, Arizona, and was collected for resale to the Hopis, who used such baskets for decoration.

**San Carlos Apache burden basket** — A small twined burden basket from San Carlos Apache is made with sumac ribs and twining, a white cottonwood border and black decoration of martynia. It also has buckskin applied decoration. The catalogue records that it was made by Mrs. Jennie McAdoo and purchased at the San Carlos Apache Fair in October 1934.

**Jicarilla Apache water jar** — A Jicarilla Apache water jar from the Jicarilla Apache Reservation, New Mexico, is made of coiled willow material and lined inside with pitch. The catalogue states that these water bottles were made in large quantities by Apache people and Pah-utes, and formerly by Navajos; they are watertight and equipped with a stopper.

**Apache basketry** — Other museum records identify baskets and a basket from New Mexico with Jicarilla Apache and Apache associations. One basket is recorded simply as “Apache?” and as having been acquired in the Southwest.

**Apache shirt** — A shirt in the Museum of Ethnography catalogue is made of light tanned skin. Its sleeves are cut open with long fringes along the lower edge, and strips of the skin hang from the front and back, coloured red and yellow.

**Apache hide doll** — An Apache doll is made of skin painted black over the body except for red-painted shoes. Clothing is suggested with glass-bead embroidery in white, light blue, red and black, including beadwork on the feet, knees, legs, upper arms, chest and shoulders.

**Motif vocabulary.** Conventionalized floral design; parallel lines in sections; red and yellow strips; white, light blue, red and black beadwork; black decoration of martynia.

### Clothing & dress

The sources document clothing made from skin and decorated with fringes, coloured strips and beadwork. A shirt is made of light tanned skin, with sleeves cut open into long lower fringes and strips hanging from the front and back; the strips are coloured red and yellow. A museum record also describes Apache figures and photographs showing shirts, fringed shirts, cloth dresses, blankets, shell chokers, jackets, waistcoats, embroidered headscarves, ties and feathered hair ornaments. These records distinguish particular garments and adornments. A cap with five upright eagle feathers is described as typical of Apache use in the Southwest of North America. The source states that such caps combine feathers and meaningful coloured beadwork on a leather cap.

### Architecture

The Wikipedia material states that the Apache nomadic way of life complicates the dating of dwellings and that they constructed less substantial dwellings than other Southwestern groups. It also mentions tents made with tanned and greased cowhides among mobile groups on the plains, with dogs used to carry tents, poles and belongings.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum records describe household and practical objects rather than a developed ceramic or metalworking tradition. The Jicarilla Apache water jar is a coiled willow bottle lined with pitch and used to carry water. Apache basket forms include coiled trays, bowl baskets, small twined burden baskets and baskets from Arizona and New Mexico. Other recorded objects include a leather tobacco canteen, a wooden medical figure, rattles made with bound hooves, and a bow and arrow shown in a photograph of a Jicarilla Apache man.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Museum records document beadwork and body adornment. The Apache doll has glass-bead embroidery in white, light blue, red and black, with long and round beads in the ears and a necklace made of long red and white beads. Photographs record shell chokers, shell ear ornaments, necklaces, medals, feather ornaments, embroidered headscarves and an embroidered horn. The source describing the eagle-feather cap identifies feathers and meaningful coloured beadwork on a leather cap.

## Music & performance

The sources provide limited information about Apache music and performance. A Metropolitan Museum of Art catalogue record identifies a flute, described as an end-blown flute, made of wood, skin, shell and beads. Another flute is made of wood, polychrome material and sinew. The Museum of Ethnography records two rattles, including one with hooves, and describes one as a rattle for a medicine man. The rattles are associated with the Southwest and Apache.

A museum record describes a skin-and-wood mask representing a mountain spirit. It has symbolic decorations in black and blue and a rattle cluster of four sticks on the horn tips. Another record describes a wooden “medicine figure” associated with fertility, wrapped with differently coloured yarn and fitted with a piece of stone and blue and red feathers.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name an Apache crown dancer in a British Museum photographic record. They also describe a mask representing a mountain spirit, with symbolic black and blue decoration and a rattle cluster on its horn tips. The catalogue identifies the mask as a head ornament for a medicine man. These records establish the presence of a crown dancer and a masked ritual object. No additional Apache dance or theatre tradition is covered by the supplied sources.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources mention an Apache Alliance, a nonprofit organization formed by nine Apache tribes, whose tribal leaders convene at Apache Alliance Summits hosted by a different Apache tribe each time. The sources also state that, in 2021, “Lipan Apaches were present” at the summit.

Museum records associate certain objects with medicine men and spiritual practice. One rattle is described as a rattle for a medicine man, while a skin-and-wood mask represents a mountain spirit. A wooden figure is described as a “medicine figure” intended for fertility.

## Foodways

The historical material states that mobile groups on the plains hunted bison and other game. A quoted historical description says that the people followed bison, ate raw meat and drank the blood of the cattle they killed. The same account describes clothing and tents made from cattle skins.

The sources also state that, when trade between Pueblo peoples and Southern Athabaskan groups was established, Pueblo people exchanged maize and woven cotton goods for bison meat, hides and materials for stone tools. The sources mention plant use in ethnobotanical terms: hundreds of different plants were used by the Mescalero tribe, while 134 plant uses were identified in the Western Apache, 165 in the White Mountain Apache and 14 in the San Carlos Apache. No plant names, Apache dish names, tea or coffee traditions are supplied.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources record historical accounts by Spanish observers and later writers, including a description attributed to Francisco Coronado in April 1541 of mobile people following bison, living in cowhide tents and using dogs to carry their possessions. The sources also mention dime novels as contributing to the European fame of Apache tenacity and fighting skills. No Apache literary revival, oral-history project or contemporary preservation programme is described in the supplied material.

## Language & religion

Apache languages belong to the Southern Athabaskan, or Apachean, subfamily of Athabaskan languages. The family is spoken primarily in the Southwestern United States, including Arizona, New Mexico, Colorado and Utah, with outliers in Oklahoma and Texas, and also in Sonora, Chihuahua, Coahuila and, to a much lesser degree, Durango and Nuevo León. The seven Southern Athabaskan languages are divided into Plains Apache and a Southwestern group containing Western, Navajo, Mescalero, Chiricahua, Jicarilla and Lipan.

Apache language speakers refer to themselves and their people with the term *Indé*, meaning “person” or “people.” The sources give *N'dee biyat'i* as a self-designation for Western Apache and *Diné bizaad* or *Naabeehó bizaad* for Navajo. Mescalero and Chiricahua are considered different languages although they are mutually intelligible. Lipan Apache and Plains Apache are nearly extinct, Chiricahua is severely endangered, and Mescalero, Jicarilla and Western Apache are considered endangered. The sources describe mountain-spirit and medicine-man objects.
