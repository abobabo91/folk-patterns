## Overview

The St. Lawrence Iroquoians were an Iroquoian Indigenous people who lived along the shores of the St. Lawrence River in present-day Quebec and Ontario, and in northern New York and northernmost Vermont. Their settlements extended through the river lowlands and east of the Great Lakes, from Lake Ontario to east of Île d’Orléans. They spoke Laurentian, probably comprising several distinct dialects within the Iroquoian language family. Estimates of their population range from 8,000–10,000 people to the much larger estimate of 120,000 people in 25 nations, although the latter is considered too expansive by many scholars. Archaeological evidence identifies them as distinct from the Haudenosaunee and the Wendat. They shared cultural traits, but were not politically united.

### Architecture

The principal documented house form was the *longhouse*, built within villages protected by earthworks and palisades. Longhouses were typical of neighboring Iroquoian peoples, measured from 18 metres to 41 metres in length, and housed several families. Villages were commonly located inland, a few kilometers from the St. Lawrence River. By the end of the 15th century, many were surrounded by defensive earthworks and palisades. Villages generally occupied 2 hectares to 3.25 hectares. The St. Lawrence Iroquoians occupied settlements for ten or more years, until longhouses deteriorated and agricultural soil lost fertility, after which they built new villages and cleared nearby land.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Archaeological evidence records pottery vessels, copper axe heads, terracotta vessels, ceramic pipes, birch bark canoes, and agricultural tools and materials. Copper axe heads found near what is now Quebec City date from c.1000–400 BCE, while terracotta vessels date from c.1350–1600. Ceramic pipes found in present-day Jefferson County, New York, are about 500 years old and have been associated with the St. Lawrence Iroquoians and Haudenosaunee. The people produced pottery vessels and traveled in birch bark canoes, including during fishing and hunting expeditions.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document several social and political practices. The St. Lawrence Iroquoians had matrilineal, clan-based social organization and a political system sufficiently structured to permit confederation at times. Ceramic pipes appear to have been associated with diplomatic visits. Most groups engaged in guerrilla warfare, and the people grew and used tobacco. The sources also describe an herbal infusion made from *annedda*, recorded by Jacques Cartier as the St. Lawrence Iroquoian name of the region’s white cedar; the St. Lawrence Iroquoians provided it to Cartier’s crew when they suffered scurvy during their first winter in Canada.

## Foodways

The St. Lawrence Iroquoians had a mixed economy based on growing maize, squash, and beans, together with hunting, fishing, and gathering. Sunflowers were cultivated for their oily seeds. Archaeological investigations indicate that maize and fish were among their most important foods, while white-tailed deer and other game were hunted. Most villages were agricultural settlements, although subsistence patterns varied by region.

In 1535, Jacques Cartier described large and productive maize fields around Hochelaga and characterized its inhabitants as sedentary. The people of Stadacona were more migratory and lived closer to the salt-water resources of the lower St. Lawrence River and the Gulf of St Lawrence, including fish, seals, and whales. They ranged widely in birch bark canoes while seeking marine animals. Agriculture was riskier in the Quebec area, particularly during the cooler temperatures of the Little Ice Age in the 16th century, so Stadaconans probably relied less on agriculture and more on sea mammals, fishing, and hunting.

## Oral tradition & literature

Knowledge of the St. Lawrence Iroquoians has been constructed from surviving oral accounts of the historical past among current Native people, writings by the French explorer Jacques Cartier, earlier histories, and archaeological and linguistic research. Haudenosaunee oral histories have also contributed to interpretation of relations between the peoples and the significance of ceramic pipes.

Jacques Cartier compiled two vocabulary lists during his voyage in 1535–1536, together containing about 200 words. His records included the term *kanata*, meaning village or settlement, and *annedda*, the name recorded for white cedar and its medicinal infusion.

## Language & religion

The St. Lawrence Iroquoians spoke Laurentian, a language or group of dialects within the Iroquoian language family. Linguistic studies indicate that they probably spoke several distinct dialects, and they may have spoken two or more distinct languages across a territory stretching more than 600 km from Lake Ontario to east of Île d’Orléans. The Iroquoian family also includes Mohawk, Huron-Wyandot, and Cherokee. Jacques Cartier’s vocabulary lists provide sparse evidence for the language.

They do document matrilineal, clan-based social organization and the cultivation and use of tobacco.
