## Overview

The Dene are an Indigenous group of First Nations who inhabit the northern boreal, subarctic and Arctic regions of Canada. The name is the common Athabaskan word for “people” and is used most narrowly for Athabaskan speakers of the Northwest Territories who form the Dene Nation: the Chipewyan, Tłı̨chǫ, Yellowknives, Slavey, Sahtu and Gwichʼin. It is also used more broadly for Northern Athabaskan speakers across Alaska and northern Canada. Dene communities extend through the Mackenzie Valley, west of Nunavut, western Yukon, northern British Columbia, Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Alaska and the southwestern United States. Behchokǫ̀, Northwest Territories is the largest Dene community in Canada. The Dene speak Northern Athabaskan languages and are known for oral storytelling, music, dances and drum traditions.

## Music & performance

Dene music is associated with Northern Athabaskan-speaking First Nations peoples, including the Chipewyan, Tlicho, Yellowknives, Slavey and Sahtu. The term generally refers to traditional musical compositions and dances. Leela Gilday identifies four main genres: Dene love songs, *Ets’ula*; tea dance songs, *Iliwa*; handgames songs; and drum dance songs. While visiting Fort Liard in the 1800s, George Keith observed love songs, lamentation songs and ceremonial songs.

The traditional music described in the sources uses melodies similar to European scales, with the coloration of blue notes. Michael Asch describes Dene music as including a melodic scale, melody and metric rhythm. Traditional Dene music uses one instrument, a frame drum called *egheli*. Drum dances, also known as tea dances, are traditional to Dene living in the Mackenzie River valley. Syncopation and pulsating vocal styles are common, while melodies generally follow a descending pattern. Many songs, especially drum dances, end with a vocal glissando and percussion break, followed by the spoken thank you *mahsi*. Vocables are also very common.

Songs are typically composed anonymously, although the sources state that there are no taboos on anyone writing most songs. In the contemporary era, Johnny Landry is identified as one of the best-known songwriters in this tradition. He wrote “Hina Na Ho Hine”, described as the de facto Dene anthem, in 1980. A version recorded with Dene drummers appears on Susan Aglukark’s 1995 album *This Child*.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used describe Dene drum dances and tea dances. Drum dances are traditional to Dene living in the Mackenzie River valley and are discussed as part of Dene music.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources identify ceremonial songs as one category observed by George Keith while visiting Fort Liard in the 1800s, and describe drum dances and tea dances as traditional Dene performance forms.

The sources do record a healing ceremony involving Dene and Inuit representatives at Bloody Falls in 1996. The ceremony was held to reconcile centuries-old grievances associated with historical ethnic feuds between Dene and Inuit.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Dene are known for oral storytelling.

The sources identify *Drum Songs: Glimpses of Dene History* by Kerry Margaret Abel as a work concerning Dene history. They also identify Michael Asch’s *Kinship and the Drum Dance in a Northern Dene Community*, which concerns kinship and drum dance in a Northern Dene community.

## Language & religion

Dene languages belong to the Athabaskan branch of the Na-Dene language family of North America. Athabaskan is also known as Dene, Dené or Dené languages. Linguists conventionally divide the Athabaskan family into Northern Athabaskan, Pacific Coast Athabaskan and Southern Athabaskan, or Apachean. The Northern Athabaskan languages are spoken throughout the interior of Alaska and the interior of northwestern Canada, including Yukon, the Northwest Territories, British Columbia, Alberta, Saskatchewan and Manitoba.

The sources identify 38 Northern Athabaskan languages and state that five Athabaskan languages are official languages in the Northwest Territories, including Chipewyan, Dogrib or Tłı̨chǫ Yatıì, Gwichʼin, and the Northern and Southern variants of Slavey. They also list Dëne Sųłiné, Gwichʼin, Slavey and Tłįchǫ Yatiì/Dogrib among languages associated with the Northwest Territories.
