## Overview

The Saponi are a Native American tribe historically based in the Piedmont of North Carolina and Virginia. Their settlements extended into the New River in West Virginia, and communities were associated with the Roanoke, Staunton, and Yadkin rivers. Their primary town was called Saponi, and a nearby settlement visited by John Lederer in 1670 was Pintahae. The Saponi were part of the Monacan confederacies, and Saponi and Tutelo were both called Nahyssan. Their language is extinct and has been described as Siouan-Catawban, similar to Tutelo, although the surviving word list may have recorded a trade jargon shared among several tribes. In 1716, the combined Saponi, Tutelo, and Manahoac population at Fort Christanna was 200.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

In the 17th century, Saponi men wore breechclouts and women wore deerhide aprons. Important leaders, including medicine men, wore feather cloaks.

### Architecture

The Saponi lived in settled villages and built houses with post-and-pole frames and central hearths.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do record that chiefs used staffs of hickory wood.

## Music & performance

The Saponi language is known from a list of 46 terms and phrases recorded by John Fontaine at Fort Christanna in 1716 and from William Byrd II’s *History of the Dividing Line betwixt Virginia and North Carolina* from 1728.

## Festivals & rituals

They describe political, military, and religious matters only briefly. German explorer John Lederer reported that Sepy was a female immortal in Saponi religion and suggested that four tribes or clans were named for this spirit and three related female spirits from whom the Saponi believed they descended.

## Foodways

Historically, Saponi people hunted deer, bear, beaver, squirrel, turkey, and other fowl. They may have hunted woodland bison and elk. They fished in rivers and the Atlantic Ocean, farmed maize, beans, and squash, and gathered wild plants including various nuts, berries, and stone fruits.

## Oral tradition & literature

The surviving language materials include John Fontaine’s list of 46 terms and phrases from Fort Christanna, recorded in 1716, and place names recorded by William Byrd II in 1728. Byrd’s list has been found to include several names from unrelated tribes. The sources also mention a short list of names given by the missionary Samuel Kirkland in connection with Saponi religious traditions.

## Language & religion

The Saponi language is extinct. It has been described as a Siouan-Catawban language similar to Tutelo, while Brooke Bauer writes that the Saponi spoke a Catawban language. David H. Pentland argued that the word list from Fort Christanna contained Siouan and Algonquian words that could have come from any of the five tribes there: the Tutelo, Saponi, Occaneechi, Meipontski, and Stuckanox. He suggested that the list documented a trade jargon and that Saponi possibly spoke an unrelated language. The Saponi were described as having a religion involving Sepy, a female immortal, and three closely related female spirits. Their society was matrilineal.
