## Overview

The Nuu-chah-nulth are one of the Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast, living on the west coast of Vancouver Island in British Columbia, Canada, in territory they call Nuu-chah-nulth Hahoulthee. The name, adopted in 1979 as a collective term, means "all along the mountains and sea"; before that outsiders called them Nootka, from a misheard instruction to Captain James Cook's ship to "come around" when he reached Yuquot in 1778. Fifteen related tribes are described by the term, and as of March 2025 more than 9,900 people are registered among fourteen band governments, all but Pacheedaht First Nation represented by the Nuu-chah-nulth Tribal Council. Their language belongs to the Wakashan family. In folk-culture terms they matter as one of the few Pacific Coast peoples who hunted whales — a practice carbon-dated to more than 4,000 years ago — and as the origin of the word *potlatch*.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Weaving here is fibre work in cedar, spruce root and grass rather than loom cloth, and the museum objects shown with this profile are where the pattern vocabulary is visible.

**Chief's whaling hat** — a conical twined hat of cedar bark with an onion-dome shape at the top, the upper half worked in imbricated stripes and the lower half carrying whaling scenes, with a hide strap headband tied inside. A second example is twined of cedar root and ornamented with a thunderbird and whaling scenes. Nothing comparable in subject matter appears on the plainer neighbouring forms recorded in the same collections.

**Cedar-bark capes and robes** — fringed capes and cloaks made of cedar bark and nettle fibre, some with fringes along the bottom and sides. One robe is woven of cedar-bark thread combined with mountain-goat wool, finished with a border of sea otter fur and painted designs on the surface; another cloak of woven fibre is bordered with netted cord and edged with sea otter fur along the lower hem.

**Rain hats** — brimless, dome-shaped hats twined from the bark fibre of giant cedar (*Thuja plicata*), the crown started in plain weave and the rest worked in tight twining, with heavier fibre at the lower edge and finer fibre above the decorative band. Other rain hats are recorded simply as cedar bark or as unspecified vegetable fibre. A further hat, twined of cedar or spruce root and conical with a concave dip at the centre of the top, is painted at the side in a black and red formline design.

**Basketry mats** — oval and circular mats made from grass. The oval example carries a whaling scene in which a canoe hunts a whale and is itself hunted in turn, with two birds separating the scenes and a central geometric panel; the circular one is worked in concentric geometric patterns. Both use red, purple, green, orange and black dyed grass, now much faded from historic light exposure.

**Basketry-covered glass** — a discarded clear glass bottle sheathed in twined basketry with a cedar-bark warp and grass weft, begun at the base in checkerweave and banded up the sides with eight zones of dyed geometric lines and zigzags.

**Twined bags** — a flat oblong bag of finely woven twine, decorated with parallel brown bands.

**Motif vocabulary.** Whaling scenes (canoe pursuing a whale, and the hunting canoe hunted in turn), thunderbird, paired birds flanking a scene, formline design in black and red, imbricated stripes, concentric geometric bands, zigzags.

### Clothing & dress

The garments in these collections are woven of cedar bark and nettle fibre: fringed capes, fringed cloaks with fringes down the bottom and sides, and larger robes. The most elaborate is a ceremonial robe or blanket of cedar-bark thread and mountain-goat wool, fringed with sea otter fur and painted with designs — sea otter fur also edges a woven fibre cloak, which marks both as high-status pieces rather than working dress. Headgear divides clearly between the two. Practical rain hats are brimless and dome-shaped, tightly twined from cedar-bark fibre, with no ornament beyond a band of finer weave. Against these stand the conical hats with an onion-dome or knobbed top, twined of cedar bark or cedar root, carrying whaling scenes and thunderbird imagery, or painted in black and red formline — one is recorded as a chief's hat, and both have an interior hide headband, one with a twisted cord attached. The sources say nothing about footwear, belts or the distinction between men's and women's dress.

### Architecture

The sources describe the built environment only in passing. Two archival photographs taken at Yuquot on the west coast of Vancouver Island show houses standing with totem poles — one with two poles and houses, the other with a single pole and house behind Lord and Lady Willingdon and Captain Jack, chief of the Mowachaht-Muchalaht band. Cedar is the material behind this: artists and woodworkers carved full logs of red and yellow cedar into totem poles and ocean-going canoes, and cedar wood served as a building material generally. One ritual structure is documented in detail: the **Yuquot Whalers' Shrine**, a house-like enclosure used in the spiritual preparations for whale hunts, made up of a series of memorial posts carved as spirit figures together with the bones of whaling ancestors. It was removed to the American Museum of Natural History in New York City, and the Mowachaht First Nation, its original owners, have sought its return.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Wood, bone, stone and whalebone carry the everyday repertoire. Bowls are carved from alder and pale brown wood, several in the form of a human figure lying on its back clutching its own knees and feet, one with a necklace of white china beads at the neck; another bowl is supported by two figures whose heads are raised skyward and whose arms straddle the vessel. Tools include a **D-adze** with a yew handle pierced by an oval hole, its upper edge carved as a squatting singing figure and a repurposed steel axe blade lashed on with commercial string; a whalebone barkcloth beater with a roughly carved head at the upper end; a wooden comb of fourteen teeth pierced through the centre; and a basalt pounder or hand maul flaring at both base and top. Fishing gear includes a bone-and-sinew trolling hook, a halibut hook whose bone barb is lashed with spruce root and secured with tightly wound fabric thread, and a fishing line knotted from lengths of kelp.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources record ornament on objects rather than on the body. Dentalium shells serve as the teeth and talc as the eyes of a wolf frontlet whose forehead is studded with further teeth; talc covers the eyes of two other masks; white china beads form a necklace on a carved bowl figure; and long black human hair is fringed onto two human-face masks, whose eyebrows are strips of hide. Personal jewelry, tattooing and hair practice are not covered by the sources used here.

## Music & performance

Whaling is carried in song as well as in story: it is reflected in stories, songs, names, family lines and place names throughout Nuu-chah-nulth territories. Two bird-shaped rattles survive in these collections — one formed in two halves of painted alder secured with skin ties, the other of wood painted white with a design in black. A D-adze handle is carved with a squatting singing figure. Beyond this the sources used here do not describe instruments, song genres or performance occasions.

## Dance & theatre

The masks are the evidence of masked performance. They include a wolf forehead frontlet carved from two pieces of alder as upper and lower jaws, with a long muzzle, raised nostrils, sawtooth teeth and eyes excavated as ovoids filled with white paint; a second wolf frontlet with dentalium teeth, talc eyes and a broad fringed leather headband; a bird's-head mask with moveable jaws, feather ornament, black-and-white painted body and a sleeping human face elaborately carved on the underside of the lower jaw; and human-face masks — one with a domed forehead, very long raised black eyebrows, deep-set eyes and an exaggerated faceted black surround to a red mouth, another of cedar with hide teeth and eyebrows and a fringe of human hair, and a third with closed eyes and open mouth. The sources name no dances and do not describe the ceremonies in which these were worn.

## Festivals & rituals

The **potlatch** is the central public ceremony, and the word itself is of Nuu-chah-nulth origin. At a potlatch the host honours guests with generous gifts, and its purposes are several at once: redistributing wealth, maintaining and publicly recognising social status, cementing alliances, celebrating and solemnising marriage, and commemorating important events. Redistribution was not only ceremonial — chiefs were responsible for it year-round, and a chief's standing rested on his ability to provide for the members of his nation and on his control of when and how resources were used. Whaling carried its own ritual apparatus, and it varied: each of the fourteen nations has its own traditions, ceremonies and rituals. Recorded elements include ceremonial bathing, abstinence, prayer and ceremony before and after the hunt, performed by the chief who led the hunting party and by his wife, and understood as a key factor in the hunt's outcome. Hunting an orca, though dangerous and difficult, was undertaken as a display of bravery. Social rank governed who could join the hunting party and how the meat and blubber were divided afterwards. The spiritual preparation had a dedicated structure in the Yuquot Whalers' Shrine. Life-cycle rites beyond marriage at a potlatch are not covered by the sources used here.

## Foodways

Whale supplied both meat and blubber, the latter rendered into oil; grey and humpback whales were taken most often because they are more docile and come close to shore, while orca meat and blubber were rated higher in quality than that of the larger whales. From the sea came halibut, herring, rockfish and salmon, caught along the coast, and clams, sea urchins and mussels gathered on the shoreline at low tide. Salmon streams were tended to keep the runs strong. Fish were cooked in large wooden vessels using water and hot stones, or dried for winter eating. From the land came camas root, fern rhizomes and berries including blueberry and huckleberry; some nations tended camas and crabapple trees to maintain them as food sources. Knowledge of when and where to find all of this passed from elders to youth through oral histories and by having children take part in gathering from an early age. Halibut fishing had its own specialised gear, including hooks with bone barbs lashed with spruce root.

## Oral tradition & literature

Nuu-chah-nulth oral history is populated by animal spirits, among them **Chulyen** (crow) and **Guguyni** (raven), both trickster figures. In one story the brothers **Tihtipihin** and **Kwatyat** let a monster swallow them in order to rescue their mother, who had been swallowed already, then cut through its stomach and escaped with her. In another, **Andaokut** is born from the mucus or tears of a woman whose children have been stolen by **Malahas**, a malicious forest goddess; he recovers the children and kills her. **Matlose** is a hobgoblin whose head is not quite a man's, whose bulk bristles with black hair, and whose teeth and curved claws tear prey apart at a single stroke; anyone who hears his voice falls as though struck. A tale of Raven bothering Octopus — a woman with her hair in eight braids, digging for clams with a digging stick while the old people watch from the shore — is told in the same corpus. A separate written strand begins with the captivity narrative published in 1815 by John R. Jewitt, the sole surviving crewman but one of the ship *Boston*, held nearly three years at Nootka Sound; it describes manners, mode of living and religious opinions.

## Language & religion

Nuu-chah-nulth, formerly called Nootka and earlier recorded as *Tahkaht* (the root *aht* meaning "people"), is a Southern Wakashan language related to Nitinaht and Makah, spoken from Barkley Sound to Quatsino Sound. It has 35 consonants, phonemic short and long vowels plus a traditional "variable length" class, a unicase alphabet with no capitals, VSO word order and polysynthetic morphology marking mood, aspect and tense. Twelve dialects are recorded, among them Ahousaht, Hesquiat, Kyuquot, Mowachaht, Clayoquot and Ucluelet. It supplied much of the vocabulary of Chinook Jargon, including *hiyu* ("many", from the word for ten) and *siah* ("far", from the word for sky). The 2021 census recorded 665 people able to hold a conversation in it, 280 with it as a mother tongue — a 23% increase over 2016. It was the first Pacific Northwest Coast language to be documented in writing. The sources describe spiritual life through whaling ritual and animal spirits rather than any organised religion.
