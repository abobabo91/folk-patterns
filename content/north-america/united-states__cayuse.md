## Overview

The Cayuse are a Native American tribe in what is now Oregon in the United States. They called themselves *Liksiyu* in the Cayuse language. Their traditional territory included northeastern Oregon and southeastern Washington, from the Blue Mountains to the Deschutes River and along the Umatilla, Grand Ronde, Burnt, Powder, John Day, Snake, Tucannon, Walla Walla, and Touchet rivers. They lived adjacent to the Nez Perce and had close associations with the Walla Walla. Today they share a reservation and government in northeastern Oregon with the Umatilla and Walla Walla as part of the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation, near Pendleton at the base of the Blue Mountains. The Cayuse language was an isolate and is extinct.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources used describe clothing only in general terms. Women processed animal skins to make materials for clothing, while adults among the Cayuse captives made clothing for the tribe.

### Architecture

The Cayuse were seminomadic and maintained summer and winter villages at sites along rivers in present-day Washington and Oregon. They lived in teepees, which the sources describe as portable dwellings used by nomadic tribes.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They record horses, dogs, animal skins, salmon, roots, berries, and other materials connected with movement, hunting, fishing, gathering, shelter, and clothing.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe seasonal movement between summer and winter villages, winter fishing along rivers, annual journeys over the Rocky Mountains to hunt buffalo, and harvesting practices involving salmon, game, berries, wild celery, and roots. The sources also record the Cayuse attack on the Whitman mission in 1847, the subsequent Cayuse War, and the 1855 Treaty of Walla Walla, but do not present these as ritual traditions.

## Foodways

Salmon from the Columbia River was one of the Cayuse’s main food sources. During winter, many Cayuse traditionally gathered in winter villages along rivers to fish for sustenance. They also ate trout and lamprey, hunted elk and deer, and hunted small game such as rabbits and fowl. Women gathered berries, including serviceberries, chokeberries, and currants, and gathered wild celery and camas; they also dug and processed roots. The men hunted and fished, while the women gathered and processed plant foods and animal skins. In some cases, traditional harvesting practices continue to the present day.

## Oral tradition & literature

They record the Cayuse language, historical accounts, village traditions, and named leaders, including Tawatoy, Weatenatemany, Five Crows, and Anna Minthorn Wannassay.

## Language & religion

Cayuse was an extinct language isolate once spoken by the Cayuse people, whose autonym was *Liksiyu*. The first written vocabulary was published by Horatio Hale in 1846. The language was discussed in relation to Molala and the proposed Waiilatpuan and Plateau Penutian classifications, but later analysis questioned the relationship between Cayuse and Molala. The Cayuse language is described as extinct since the 1930s; the sources also state that it was believed to have become extinct by 1902. *Weyíiletpuu* was a dialect of the Nez Perce language spoken by Cayuse inhabitants of the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation and has not been used since the 1940s.
