## Overview

The Kumeyaay, also known as *'Iipai-Tiipai* and historically as Diegueño, are Indigenous people whose territory extends across the northern border of Baja California in Mexico and the southern border of California in the United States. Their historical homelands include the San Diego River region, the Laguna Mountains, Ensenada, Tecate, Mexicali, and areas bordering the Salton Sea. The *'Iipai* lived to the north, the *Tiipai* to the south, and the *Kamia* to the east. The Kumeyaay language belongs to the Yuman–Cochimí family and the Delta–California branch of the Yuman family. Kumeyaay folk culture is documented through oral narratives, song cycles, ceremonial practices, houses, clothing, foodways, basketry, and community language-preservation work.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

During warm seasons, men wore a hide breechcloth, which also held tools. Women wore an apron or skirt made from willow or elderberry bark. In colder months, blankets were made from willow bark or rabbit skins. For long distances, sharp rocks, or hot sand, Kumeyaay people wore agave sandals made from yucca and agave fibers. Bead necklaces were made from clam, abalone, or olivella shells. Men could have their noses pierced, while women might have their chins tattooed. After contact with the Spanish, the Kumeyaay began abandoning much of this clothing and adopted European-style clothing used in Latin America.

### Architecture

Kumeyaay houses were generally dome-shaped structures made from branches and covered with leaves of willow or tule. They were called *'ewaa*. A hole at the top allowed smoke to escape, while rocks placed along the base helped keep out wind and small animals. Some Kumeyaay living in the mountains made houses from slabs of bark. These structures were often temporary. When families moved, or when someone died in the house, it was often burned down. The sources also describe Kumeyaay villages and communities, including Cosoy or Kosa'aay, Nipaquay or Nipawai, San Pasqual pueblo, and villages in Valle de Las Palmas and Peña Blanca.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention tools carried in a hide breechcloth, manos and metates used to grind food, basket granaries made from willow leaves, and baskets associated with Kumeyaay craftwork.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Kumeyaay bead necklaces used beads made from clam, abalone, or olivella shells. Men could have their noses pierced, and women might have their chins tattooed.

## Music & performance

Kumeyaay language and cultural knowledge have traditionally been transmitted through oral narratives, song cycles, and ceremonial practices. Bird songs are described as one of the most culturally significant forms of Kumeyaay verbal art. They recount migratory journeys and ancestral histories.

Ceremonial specialists formed part of the traditional social structure. The *Kuseyaays* included male or female priests, doctors, and other specialists in health, ecology, resource management, tradition, and religion. The *Kwaapaay*, or chief, was responsible for protecting traditions and holding ceremonies, as well as for political, religious, and economic activities of the *sib*.

Contemporary preservation includes recordings of Tiipay stories, conversations, and wordlists from Nejí and La Huerta. These recordings have been uploaded to the Archive of the Indigenous Languages of Latin America and, in some cases, transcribed through the Endangered Languages Archive at the University of London.

## Festivals & rituals

They describe ceremonial practices as part of the traditional transmission of language and culture, and identify the *Kwaapaay* as the person responsible for holding ceremonies.

Marriage was arranged by the parents of people from different *sibs*. The future husband was expected to demonstrate his ability to hunt and present the future bride with game he had killed. After marriage, the bride moved into the husband’s *sib*. Marriage relations could also connect different *sibs* or neighboring tribal groups as gestures of peace between warring groups or as part of trade relationships.

The sources describe houses as being burned when a family moved or when someone died in the house. They also mention protected graves and artifacts in connection with the Kumeyaay Border task force and the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act of 1990. No additional birth, coming-of-age, or funeral rites are covered.

## Foodways

Acorns were a staple of the Kumeyaay diet. They made acorn mush called *shawii*, and ground acorns with a mano and metate; the resulting meal could be used in dough to make bread. Pinon nuts and chia seeds were also stone-ground and consumed. These grains were stored in basket granaries made of willow leaves.

Kumeyaay people consumed the leaves and fruits of prickly pear and copal cactus, as well as cherries, plums, elderberries, and Manzanita berries. Many of these plants were fermented with water and honey to create alcohol. Hunting provided birds, rabbits, squirrels, woodrats, antelope, deer, and mountain sheep. Crickets, grubs, and grasshoppers were also eaten.

Kamia Kumeyaay in the Imperial Valley practiced forms of agriculture and produced maize, beans, and teparies. This differed from the practices of their mountain and coastal counterparts. Before European contact, Kumeyaay communities along the Pacific Ocean had a diet rich in maritime protein.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kumeyaay language was traditionally transmitted through oral narratives, song cycles, and ceremonial practices. Bird songs recount migratory journeys and ancestral histories and remain among the most culturally significant forms of Kumeyaay verbal art.

Contemporary documentation and preservation efforts include a 2019 trilingual book of stories and oral histories from Baja California Tiipay communities of Nejí and La Huerta, published by Margaret Field with other translators and native speakers. Audio and video recordings of stories, conversation, and wordlists in Tiipay variants from Nejí and La Huerta are available through the Archive of the Indigenous Languages of Latin America and the Endangered Languages Archive. Community-based language classes, documentation, curriculum development, and a language immersion program are also described.

## Language & religion

Kumeyaay languages belong to the Yuman language family and the Delta–California branch of that family. The Kumeyaay consist of the related groups *'Iipai*, *Tiipai*, and *Kamia*. One scholarly consensus recognized three dialects: *'Iipai*, Kumeyaay proper, and *Tiipay*. Katherine Luomala considered the range of variation to reflect two distinct languages, *'Iipai* and *Tiipai*. The Kumeyaay language is also called Kumiai, Central Diegueño, or Campo in the language source.

The traditional social structure included the *Kwaapaay*, or chief, and *Kuseyaays*, who could be priests, doctors, and other specialists in religion, health, ecology, resource management, and tradition.
