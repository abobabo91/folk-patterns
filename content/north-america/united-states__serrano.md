## Overview

The Serrano are an Indigenous people of California whose autonyms include *Taaqtam*, meaning “people,” *Maarrênga'yam*, meaning “people from Morongo,” and *Yuhaaviatam*, meaning “people of the pines.” They historically lived in the San Bernardino Mountains, San Bernardino Valley, Mojave River area, Western and Central Mojave Desert, San Gabriel Mountains, Sierra Pelona Mountains, and southern Tehachapi Mountains. The Mojave River connected Serrano communities with other Indigenous groups and supported trade. The Serrano language belongs to the Takic subset of the Uto-Aztecan language family. Today, Maarrênga'yam are enrolled in the Morongo Band of Mission Indians, Yuhaaviatam in the Yuhaaviatam of San Manuel Nation, and other Serrano people in the Soboba Band of Luiseno Indians.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Southwestern textiles** — Historical accounts describe textiles woven by Southwestern groups and traded through Mojave desert traders.

**Shell beads and shell jewelry** — Coastal California groups traded shell beads and shell jewelry to Southwestern groups including the Serrano in exchange for ceramics and textiles.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no Serrano textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

 They state that textiles were involved in long-distance trade through Mojave desert traders.

### Architecture

 They mention villages, mountain camps, an irrigation system called Mill Creek Zanja, and permanent residence near hot springs.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Serrano communities used stone tools, grinding stones, fire pits, traps, and curved throwing sticks. Archaeological materials from a mountain camp near Baldwin Lake included non-local jasper and obsidian, ash and charcoal, grinding stones, and fire pits. Serrano trade involved ceramics, textiles, shell beads, and asphaltum.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention shell beads and shell jewelry as traded goods found in pre-modern Southwestern burial sites.

## Music & performance

 They state that one remaining *hümtc* medicine man revived religious ceremonies in the early 1900s after cultural suppression during the Mission Period. The sources name *tuwituaim* as a dance associated with the revival of Serrano religious, spiritual, communal, and familial practices, but provide no musical details.

## Dance & theatre

*Tuwituaim* is identified as a dance connected with the revival of Serrano religious and spiritual practices, as well as communal and familial practices.

## Festivals & rituals

 They describe the revival of religious ceremonies in the early 1900s, including *tuwituaim*, after cultural suppression during the Mission Period. Female practitioners followed spiritual practices associated with the pursuit of good health, including the hot sand pit. Women also practiced health rituals intended to remove bad energy associated with taboo, including menstruation periods.

## Foodways

Serrano foodways varied according to environment. Desert communities relied on honey mesquite, pine nuts, yucca, and fruits from cacti, while people in foothill areas primarily consumed acorns. Hunting groups pursued deer, mountain sheep, and antelope; rabbits and rodents were captured with traps or curved throwing sticks; and quail was the most significant game bird named in the sources. Chia seeds also played an important role in the food supply.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They state that Dorothy Ramon and linguist Eric Elliot wrote *Wayta' Yawa'* (“Always Believe”), a book in Serrano and English about Serrano culture and Ramon’s life. The book is presented as helping preserve the Serrano language from complete extinction. The sources also mention oral accounts from Native Serrano concerning trade along the Mojave River.

## Language & religion

Serrano, also called *Maarrênga'twich* in Serrano, belongs to the Serran branch of the Uto-Aztecan family and is closely related to Tongva, Tataviam, Kitanemuk, and Vanyume. It has free word order, with verbs usually coming last, and is agglutinative. The language was traditionally spoken; an alphabet was not used until the 1990s, and a 47-letter alphabet began development in 2005. The language is now considered dormant, while revitalization efforts continue through the San Manuel Band of Mission Indians, Morongo Band of Mission Indians, the Limu project, and other programs. Serrano religious practice included ceremonies, medicine work, spiritual health practices, and the *tuwituaim* dance.
