## Overview

The Niantic are an Algonquian-speaking American Indian tribe whose communities occupied parts of Connecticut and Rhode Island during the early colonial period. Their name, *Nehântick* or *Nehantucket*, is explained as meaning “of long-necked waters,” referring to the “long neck” or peninsula known as Black Point in the village of Niantic, Connecticut. Intrusions by the more numerous Pequots divided the people into Western and Eastern groups. Western Niantics lived just east of the mouth of the Connecticut River and were subject to the Pequots, while Eastern Niantics became closely allied with the Narragansetts. Eastern Niantic speech is classified as a dialect of Narragansett, and Western Niantic speech as Mohegan-Pequot. Today, only western Nehantics are known to exist in coastal New London County, Connecticut.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

By the end of the 1700s, the Niantic peoples had adopted aspects of Yankee New England culture, including the dominant culture’s style of dress.

### Architecture

Niantic settlements included semi-permanent settlements and dispersed villages around salt ponds, mainly in what is now coastal Rhode Island. In October 1761, Ezra Stiles encountered a Western Niantic community in the present-day village of Niantic and sketched its wigwams, noting similarities between their design and those used by the Kickapoo.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

A record from 1733 states that Western Niantics travelled from East Lyme, Connecticut, to Woodstock, Connecticut, to barter skins and furs for powder, shot, rings, knives, cloth, pipes, tobacco, beads, lace, whistles, and other commodities.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources state that Niantic people crafted shell artworks.

## Music & performance

The sources mention that Western Niantic elders were consulted by Mohegan people in the early 20th century for guidance concerning sacred traditions, medicine, symbolism, and tribal history. The sources also record that Niantics attended a gathering at Mashapaug Pond in Providence, Rhode Island, in the 1930s, alongside Narragansetts, Nipmucks, Wampanoags, Passamaquoddys, and Misquamicuts; no performance traditions are identified.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that Eastern Niantic communities resisted Christian conversion efforts beginning by the autumn of 1713, while more concentrated efforts took place in the 1720s. Widespread interest in Christianity did not begin among Western or Eastern Niantics until 1743, after which distinct congregations formed for each group.

The sources describe social organization in which the Niantic community valued both personal autonomy and group unity, with individual families responsible for providing for themselves. In the early 20th century, Mohegan people of southeastern Connecticut regarded Western Niantic people as elders and sought their guidance concerning sacred traditions, medicine, symbolism, and tribal history.

## Foodways

Before European colonization, Niantics spent their summers fishing and digging shellfish, which were abundant in the region. They cultivated the Three Sisters: maize, beans, and squash. They also hunted, fished, and collected nuts, roots, and fruits. During the Late Woodland period, they ate snake and turtle meat.

The sources identify fishing, shellfish gathering, cultivation, hunting, and collecting as important subsistence activities.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that the Niantics spoke an Algonquian Y-dialect similar to the speech of the Pequots, Mohegans, and Narragansetts in New England and the Montauks on eastern Long Island.

The sources describe later efforts to research and preserve Niantic history. In 1998, about 35 Connecticut families claiming Niantic descent incorporated as the Nehantic Tribe and Nation nonprofit association. They established a three-person governing board, researched their history more fully, and began the petition process for recognition from the federal government as an Indian tribe.

## Language & religion

Niantic speech belonged to the Algonquian language family. The Niantics spoke an Algonquian Y-dialect, and the division between Eastern and Western Niantics was reflected in linguistic classification: Eastern Niantic is described as a dialect of Narragansett, while Western Niantic is classified as Mohegan-Pequot. The related Mohegan-Pequot language is also known as Mohegan-Pequot-Montauk, Secatogue, and Shinnecock-Poosepatuck; its New England dialects included Mohegan, Pequot, and Niantic, while Long Island dialects included Montaukett and Shinnecock.

Mohegan-Pequot was traditionally an oral language without a standardized writing system. In the 21st century, a practical orthography using the Latin alphabet was created as part of language revitalization.

Christian missionaries attempted to convert Eastern Niantics beginning by the autumn of 1713. Organized efforts expanded in the 1720s, and distinct congregations formed for Western and Eastern Niantics after 1743. By the end of the 1700s, Niantic peoples had adopted aspects of Yankee New England religious beliefs and social culture.
