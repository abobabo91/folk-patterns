## Overview

The Bitterroot Salish peoples, also known as Flathead and Salish, are a Salish-speaking group of Native Americans and one of three tribes of the Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes of the Flathead Nation in Montana. Their historic homeland extended from an area west of Billings, Montana, to the continental divide and from south of Great Falls, Montana, to the Montana–Wyoming border; they later moved into the Bitterroot Valley. In 1891, they were forcibly moved to the Flathead Reservation, where they created a community with the Kootenai and Pend d’Oreilles. Their cultural history includes seasonal movement, buffalo hunting, oral accounts of Coyote, Salish language traditions, Catholic practices adopted alongside existing beliefs, and continuing efforts to preserve traditional knowledge.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources mention St. Mary’s Mission, a Catholic mission established in the Bitterroot in 1841.

## Music & performance

The sources describe Catholic chant, prayer, and devotional hymns as practices that some Salish people adopted alongside elements of their own beliefs. They also mention the veneration of sacramental objects and sacred sites, a sacred calendar associated with sacred colors, and water used for purification.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe a seasonal round in which the Bitterroot Salish traveled across the continental divide once or twice each year to hunt buffalo. The buffalo herds’ devastation in the 1870s and 1880s forced them toward farming and ranching, while an unprecedented drought in 1889 contributed to food scarcity and suffering. Salish religious life also included practices adopted from Catholicism, such as chant, prayer, devotional hymns, a sacred calendar associated with sacred colors, sacramental objects, sacred sites, and purification with water. Elders later remembered the three-day, sixty-mile removal journey of October 15, 1891, as a funeral march.

## Foodways

The sources state that the Bitterroot Salish practiced a seasonal round and traveled across the continental divide to hunt buffalo. Following the devastation of the buffalo herds in the 1870s and 1880s, they turned to farming and ranching, with some agricultural success before the drought of 1889. They record that, when Jesuits sought to teach agriculture, most Salish people chose to continue their seasonal round. During the removal period, the government promised rations until the people moved or received money from the sale of their Bitterroot lands, but the sources state that the people later received insufficient rations.

## Oral tradition & literature

Bitterroot Salish oral history tells that the tribes were placed in their Indigenous homelands, present-day Montana, after Coyote killed the *nałisqelixw*, translated in the source as “people-eaters.” The Coyote stories describe the Creator sending Coyote ahead because the world was full of evils and not yet fit for mankind. Coyote and Fox came to what elders call a big island, freed it from these evils, created geographical formations, and provided skills and knowledge for humans. The stories also describe greed, jealousy, hunger, envy, and other imperfections associated with Coyote.

Other Coyote stories contain descriptions of geological events near the last ice age, including glaciers extending down what is now Flathead Lake, the flooding of western Montana beneath a great lake, the retreat of cold weather, and the disappearance of large animals such as giant beaver. Salish oral histories and newspaper accounts also record the 1891 removal, while elders remembered the journey as a funeral march. In the 1980s, Agnes Vanderburg established an annual camp to teach traditional skills to the next generation.

## Language & religion

The Bitterroot Salish are an Interior Salish group of Native Americans whose language is Salish, the namesake of the Salishan language family. The Interior Salish group consists of the Flathead, or *Séliš*, Kalispell, or *Qlispé*, and Spokane. The source gives *npoqínišcn* for the Spokane language, *qlispé* for the Kalispel language, and *séliš* for the Bitterroot Salish language. Salish history describes an earlier large nation whose region stretched from Montana to the Pacific Coast, followed by dispersion and the development of distinct dialects.

Religious life combined Salish beliefs with selected elements of Catholicism. Salish people adopted ideas of generosity, community, obedience, and respect for family, as well as chant, prayer, devotional hymns, sacred colors, sacramental objects, sacred sites, and purification with water. They rejected the doctrines of hell and sin. Jesuit efforts to suppress Salish traditions included ordering medicine men to throw sacred bundles into a hole near the church, causing the loss of much generational knowledge.
