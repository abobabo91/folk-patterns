## Overview

The Mohegan are an Indigenous people based in what became southeastern Connecticut in the United States. They belong to the Eastern Algonquian linguistic and cultural family and historically shared close ties with the Pequot, from whom they separated in the early 17th century. Mohegan identity is associated with kinship, spirituality, oral tradition, community life, language preservation, and a reverent relationship with the natural world. The Mohegan refer to themselves as the “Wolf People.” The sources describe approximately 2,400 enrolled Mohegan tribal members as of 2024, while also distinguishing the ethnic and cultural group from any single political or legal entity.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no documented textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional clothing was typically made from deerskin and other animal hides, which were softened through tanning. Garments could be decorated with porcupine quills, shell beads, and natural dyes; these decorations could signify clan identity, age, or social role. In colder seasons, layered clothing and fur robes provided insulation, while lighter garments were used in warmer months.

### Architecture

Traditional Mohegan homes included *wigwams*, dome-shaped dwellings framed with saplings and covered with bark or woven mats. Their construction provided warmth and ventilation, and the structures could be modified for seasonal use. Families often relocated between summer and winter sites according to food sources and climate conditions. The sources also describe Shantok as a fortified village along the Thames River that served as a cultural and political center.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They describe tools and implements made from plant materials, including strong yet lightweight silver maple wood, and mention tools associated with fishing, such as weirs, nets, and spears.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention shell beads and porcupine quills as decorations on clothing, together with natural dyes.

## Music & performance

They state that the Green Corn Ceremony includes offerings of food, song, and prayer. Ceremonial life traditionally followed agricultural, lunar, and natural cycles, and oral teaching connected song and ceremony with harvest, renewal, gratitude, spiritual cleansing, and community renewal.

The sources also describe oral storytelling as central to Mohegan cultural life. Stories transmit history, ethics, spiritual knowledge, practical guidance, and ancestral experience. Storytelling takes place in family settings, community gatherings, and ceremonial spaces, and elders are regarded as custodians of memory. The act of retelling ancestral experiences is described as a form of spiritual continuity and a way to strengthen identity across generations.

## Dance & theatre

They state that the Trail of Life is sometimes visualized in ceremonial art and regalia and may be invoked during rites of passage, including birth, coming-of-age observances, and funerals.

## Festivals & rituals

Mohegan ceremonial life traditionally followed seasonal, agricultural, lunar, and natural cycles. The principal named observance in the sources is the **Green Corn Ceremony**, a seasonal celebration of harvest, renewal, and gratitude. It was revived in the 20th century by Mohegan leader Emma Fielding Baker and includes offerings of food, song, and prayer. The ceremony marks a time of spiritual cleansing and community renewal.

Other practices include naming rituals, coming-of-age recognitions, and commemorations of ancestors. Ceremonies are understood as acts of continuity that express relationships, responsibilities, and values connecting living people with those who came before. The sources describe the Trail of Life as a symbolic path connecting past, present, and future generations and extending seven generations backward and seven generations forward.

Traditional spirituality emphasizes balance and reciprocity with the natural world. The Creator is often referred to in English as Mundo. Ancestors are honored through oral storytelling, seasonal ceremonies, and the preservation of family knowledge. Offerings and prayers may accompany gathering plants, harvesting, hunting, and other relationships with the natural world.

## Foodways

Mohegan foodways combined agriculture, hunting, fishing, and foraging within a seasonal subsistence system. Women traditionally managed agriculture, especially the cultivation of the “Three Sisters”: corn, beans, and squash. These crops were grown together in an interdependent system associated with cooperation and balance, and fields were often located near village sites and rotated to maintain soil health.

Men traditionally hunted and fished according to seasonal cycles. Deer, turkey, and small game provided meat and hides, while rivers and coastal areas supplied fish, shellfish, and other aquatic resources. Fishing used weirs, nets, and spears and was sometimes carried out communally. Foraging added nuts, berries, medicinal plants, and wild roots to the diet.

The sources mention silver maple and sugar maple among culturally important plants. Sugar maple sap was boiled into syrup and sugar for everyday and ceremonial use. Food gathering was accompanied by ceremony and protocol: offerings could be made before a harvest or hunt, and some practices were guided by moon phases and ancestral instruction.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral tradition is described as the primary means of transmitting Mohegan history, ethics, identity, spiritual knowledge, practical guidance, and ancestral experience. Narratives emphasize relationships with the land, humility, balance, and the roles of cultural figures and animals. Elders share teachings in family settings, community gatherings, and ceremonial spaces, and are regarded as custodians of memory.

The sources describe oral histories concerning the separation of the Mohegan and Pequot and the leadership of Uncas. In one teaching, Uncas is said to have “broken the arrows of peace” with Sassacus, symbolizing the end of political unity and the reaffirmation of Mohegan independence.

Language preservation is connected with written records. Fidelia Hoscott Fielding kept four diaries written phonetically in English that documented Mohegan words, phrases, and syntactic patterns. These diaries are foundational sources for contemporary language revitalization and preserve elements of worldview, metaphor, and oral tradition.

## Language & religion

Mohegan-Pequot is an Algonquian language formerly spoken and currently being reclaimed by Indigenous peoples of southern present-day New England and eastern Long Island. Its New England dialects included Mohegan, Pequot, and Niantic, while Long Island dialects included Montaukett and Shinnecock. The language belongs to the Eastern Algonquian branch of the Algonquian language family.

Mohegan-Pequot was traditionally oral and had no standardized writing system. In the 21st century, a practical orthography using the Latin alphabet was created as part of revitalization efforts. The language has not been spoken conversationally for approximately 100 years; the last native speaker, Dji’s Butnaca, known in English as Fidelia Fielding, died in 1908. The Mohegan Language Project has created lessons, a dictionary, and online learning materials, while a complete grammar is in development.

Mohegan spirituality centers on balance and reciprocity with the natural world. All living beings are considered related, and animals, plants, and geographic features are treated as part of a shared cosmology. Christianity influenced some aspects of Mohegan spiritual practice during the colonial era, while traditional beliefs and values persisted in blended or parallel forms.
