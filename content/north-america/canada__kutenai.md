## Overview

The Kutenai, also known as the Ktunaxa, Ksanka, Kootenay, and Kootenai, are an Indigenous people of Canada and the United States. Their communities live in southeastern British Columbia, northern Idaho, and western Montana, especially along the Kootenay River and near places including Creston, Cranbrook, Windermere, and the Flathead Reservation. The people are divided broadly into Upper Kutenai and Lower Kutenai according to the sections of the Kootenay River where their bands live. The Kutenai language is generally considered a language isolate, unrelated to neighboring Salishan languages. Their documented culture includes fishing, hunting, portable dwellings, canoes, vision quests, dances, ritual tobacco use, oral stories, and continuing language-revitalization work.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state that Kutenai traditional dress had more in common with Plains peoples than with Coastal Salish peoples.

### Architecture

The sources describe the Kutenai use of teepee-style portable dwellings, identifying these as one feature that connected their traditional customs with Plains peoples.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Kutenai used the sturgeon-nosed canoe, a watercraft also used by the Sinixt. The sources describe its bark construction: a tree was ringed with an elk-horn chisel or flint knife, the bark was separated from the trunk with a stick, and the bark sheet was wrapped to prevent drying before being taken to camp. The tree-side of the bark became the canoe’s exterior, while the outer surface became the interior. The sources also document stone tools and points, quarrying, flint-knapping, quartzite, tourmaline, fish traps, and hooks.

## Music & performance

The sources name several Ktunaxa ceremonial and social dances: the Sun Dance, Grizzly Bear Dance, Blue Jay Dance, and other social and ceremonial activities. They also describe a midwinter festival and the ritual use of tobacco.

The Ktunaxa language has been documented through stories and songs recorded for language-revitalization work. FirstVoices materials include audio recordings of Kutenai words and phrases, and the Ktunaxa webpage had stories and songs recorded as of November 2017. The Ktunaxa Language app uses audio recordings, while the FirstVoices Tutor provides lessons and practices. These resources are directed toward teaching fluency in the Kutenai language, especially among younger generations.

## Dance & theatre

The Kutenai practiced the Sun Dance, Grizzly Bear Dance, and Blue Jay Dance. The sources identify these as ceremonial or social activities and also mention a midwinter festival.

## Festivals & rituals

Kutenai ritual life included vision quests, particularly for a young man passing into adulthood, as well as the ritual use of tobacco. The sources name the Sun Dance, Grizzly Bear Dance, Blue Jay Dance, and a midwinter festival. They also describe men’s membership in societies or lodges, including the Crazy Dog Society, Crazy Owl Society, and Shamans’ Society. These organizations carried responsibilities in battle, hunting, and community service. They state that European observers at the turn of the 20th century recorded a detailed ritual calendar, but the supplied material does not reproduce that calendar in full.

The Ktunaxa encountered Christianity as early as the 18th century. By the 1830s, some Ktunaxa had adopted Christian elements in a syncretic blend of ceremonies. Christian missionaries later worked in Ktunaxa territories, and Father Pierre-Jean de Smet toured the region in 1845–6.

## Foodways

Fishing was fundamental to Lower Kootenay diet and culture. Their economic life centered on fishing with fish traps and hooks, while travel took place on waterways in the sturgeon-nosed canoe. Seasonal and sometimes ritual hunts included bear, deer, caribou, gophers, geese, and other fowl. Upper Kootenay communities often crossed the Rockies to participate in bison hunts, whereas the Lower Kootenay did not participate in communal bison hunts, which were not important to their economy or culture.

The sources state that the Lower Kootenay region was rich in fish, birds, and large game. The supplied museum catalogue records contain no objects for the foodways section.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kutenai oral tradition includes stories and myths. The sources identify *Kutenai Tales*, published by Franz Boas in 1918, as a transcription and translation of multiple Ktunaxa stories. The stories were gathered by Alexander F. Pierce in 1891 and by Boas in 1914, and were told by Andrew Pierre, Numan Pierre, Joe Mission, Andrew Felix, and a major contributor referred to as Barnaby.

The Ktunaxa are also recording oral stories and myths and videotaping traditional crafts and technologies with spoken directions. These activities form part of contemporary efforts to revive cultural practices and encourage language study.

## Language & religion

Kutenai, also called Kootenai, Kootenay, Ktunaxa, and Ksanka, is generally considered a language isolate. It is unrelated to the Salishan family of languages spoken by neighboring peoples. The Kutenai also use ʔa·qanⱡiⱡⱡitnam, Ktunaxa Sign Language. Kutenai is written in an alphabet derived from the Latin alphabet and is generally described as an agglutinative language with prefixes and suffixes.

In 1969, the language was spoken by 446 registered Indians in Canada. Statistics Canada reported 210 Ktunaxa speakers as of 2021, while Ktunaxa scholar Christopher Horsethief stated that 24 fluent speakers remained and that all were over the age of 65. Language work includes FirstVoices recordings, the Ktunaxa Language app, Ktunaxa classes, and community projects such as wupnik' natanik.

Traditional spiritual practices named in the sources include vision quests, ritual tobacco use, the Sun Dance, and the Grizzly Bear Dance. The sources also describe a syncretic adoption of Christian elements beginning by the 1830s.
