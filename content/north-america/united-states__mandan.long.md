## Overview

The Mandan are a Native American people of the Great Plains who have lived primarily in what is now North Dakota and historically along the Upper Missouri River and the Heart and Knife rivers. They are enrolled in the Three Affiliated Tribes of the Fort Berthold Reservation; about half live in the reservation area, while others live elsewhere in the United States and in Canada. The Mandan developed a settled, agrarian culture in permanent villages of large, round earth lodges. They were a major trading nation whose corn surpluses were exchanged for bison meat and fat, horses, guns, and other goods. Their language belongs to the Siouan family and is closely associated historically with Hidatsa and Crow through long contact.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Buffalo robes** — The museum records describe Buffalo Dance figures wearing buffalo robes. The same drawings show the buffalo figures elaborately painted and carrying rattles and long sticks.

**Painted buffalo figures** — British Museum catalogue records describe painted buffalo figures associated with the Buffalo Dance. The records do not specify the pigments or painting technique.

**Hide painting** — The sources used mention elaborately painted buffalo robes and painted buffalo figures.

**Motif vocabulary.** Buffalo figures, buffalo robes, wheat bundle, rattle, long stick.

### Clothing & dress

The sources used provide very little information about everyday Mandan dress. The museum records identify a shoe from the Plains United States associated with the Mandan and preserve an inscription referring to “Chief Yonktonau Long mandan,”. Catalogue descriptions of the Buffalo Dance show figures wearing buffalo robes, elaborately painted and carrying rattles and long sticks. No documented information in the sources distinguishes men’s and women’s daily clothing or identifies Mandan head coverings, belts, ceremonial footwear, or other named garments.

### Architecture

Mandan villages were permanent settlements containing large, round earth lodges, some 40 feet (12 m) in diameter, arranged around a central plaza. Matrilineal families lived in the lodges. The sources also describe defensive ditches and palisades around villages, including two trenches at Double Ditch Village and stronger fortifications at Huff Village. Double Ditch Village stood on the east bank of the Missouri River, north of where present-day Bismarck developed. Its remaining depressions mark former lodges and cache pits used to store dehydrated corn. Like-a-Fishhook Village was built upriver after the survivors of the 1837–38 epidemic joined the Hidatsa; the Hidatsa later moved some 20 miles north and constructed the village with common protection in mind.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Mandan sources describe cache pits for storing dehydrated corn, defensive works, paddles, canoes, rattles, long sticks, and an object called the Ark or Big Canoe. A museum catalogue identifies a paddle from the Fort Berthold Indian Agency at Elbowoods, North Dakota, and describes it as intended for a canoe. British Museum records describe rattles and long sticks used in Buffalo Dance scenes.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The Mandan sources mention a band called the Is’tope, glossed as “those who tattooed themselves.”

## Music & performance

The sources identify rattles and a medicine pipe in Mandan ceremonial contexts. British Museum catalogue records describe buffalo figures in the Buffalo Dance holding rattles and long sticks. The dance represented in the drawing is connected to a Mandan account in which the Buffalos’ creation supplied the people with food during the coming year. Other catalogue records describe the Medicine Lodge, where the annual “Religious Ceremony” of the Mandans was held, and an Ark or Big Canoe standing in the centre of the village. The Master of Ceremonies is shown leaning against it with the Medicine Pipe in his hands.

The Mandan sources also mention the annual Okipa ceremony. George Catlin was permitted to watch it in 1832 after Four Bears invited him. David Thompson recorded that Mandan amusements included dancing and singing and that these were conducted with order and decorum.

## Dance & theatre

The Buffalo Dance is represented in a series of British Museum drawings. One catalogue record identifies the scene as “Bel-lohk Na-pic (the Buffalo Dance)” and describes two of the eight Buffalos performing the dance. The Buffalo figures are elaborately painted, wear buffalo robes, and hold rattles and long sticks. Further drawings show the Evil Spirit, O-ke-hee-de, entering the area where the eight Buffalos are dancing after being defeated by the medicine pipe. The women and children then surround and pelt him with sticks and dirt, rejoicing over his defeat.

The sources also identify the Okipa as a sacred annual ceremony and describe the Medicine Lodge as the setting of the Mandans’ annual “Religious Ceremony.”

## Festivals & rituals

The sources document the annual Okipa ceremony and an annual religious ceremony held in the Medicine Lodge. George Catlin attended the Okipa after Four Bears allowed him to watch it in 1832. During an attack on the Mandan village Nuptadi around 1785, the “turtles” used in the Okipa ceremony were saved; Mandan woman Scattercorn recounted that the turtles produced water that protected them.

The Buffalo Dance was connected with the creation of the Buffalos and with the supply of food during the coming year. In the Medicine Lodge, the Master of Ceremonies is shown with the medicine pipe beside the Ark or Big Canoe. The sources document epidemics, warfare, trade, and village relocation as major events in Mandan history, including the smallpox epidemics of 1781 and 1837–38.

## Foodways

Mandan foodways were based on settled agriculture and extensive farming carried out by women. Corn was a major crop and a principal article of trade. Mandan villages contained cache pits for storing dehydrated corn, and the Mandan exchanged large corn surpluses with other tribes for bison meat and fat. Crops and other goods circulated through Mandan-Hidatsa settlements, which were major trade centres in the Great Plains.

Bison products were important both as food and as trade goods. The Buffalo Dance was associated with the creation of Buffalos to supply the people with food during the coming year. The Mandan also traded for horses, guns, and other goods, and used horses for transportation, carrying packs, pulling travois, and hunting.

## Oral tradition & literature

Mandan oral tradition states that the Hidatsa were once nomadic until their encounter with the Mandan, who taught them to build stationary villages and cultivate agriculture. Mandan tradition also refers to an eastern place near a lake as an ancestral point of origin. Stories connected with the Okipa include the account of turtles producing water to protect people when Nuptadi Village was burned by the Sioux.

The Mandan winter count of Butterfly records warfare in 1835–1836, including the destruction of fifty Sioux tepees and the loss of thirty men in a war party. The sources also preserve statements attributed to Mandan people, including Scattercorn and Four Bears. Language-preservation work includes school programs, materials produced by the MHA Language Project, a vocabulary app, a dictionary, books in Mandan, a summer learning institute, and teacher materials. The 2020 documentary *To Save A Language* portrays Indrek Park’s efforts with Edwin Benson.

## Language & religion

Mandan, also called *Núu’etaa íroo* in the Mandan-language article, belongs to the Siouan language family. It has two main dialects, Núu’etaa/Nuu’etaare and Rúptaa/Rúptaare; the Mandan article also names Nuptare and Nuetare. Only the Nuptare variety survived into the 20th century, and its speakers were bilingual in Hidatsa. The sources describe Mandan as a separate branch of the Siouan family because long contact with Hidatsa and Crow obscured its exact relationship to other Siouan languages.

Mandan has grammatical forms that vary according to the sex of the addressee. The supplied sources describe medicine men as having their own secret language. Religious practices documented in the sources include the Okipa, the Medicine Lodge ceremony, the medicine pipe, the Ark or Big Canoe, and the Buffalo Dance.
