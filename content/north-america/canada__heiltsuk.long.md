## Overview

The Heiltsuk — *Híɫzaqv* or *Haíɫzaqv*, historically called Bella Bella in English — are an Indigenous people of *Waxvwuisaxv* along the Central Coast of British Columbia, centred on the island village of Bella Bella (also *Waglisla* / *Wágḷísḷa*), with a second Heiltsuk-speaking community at Klemtu (*Ɫṃ́du̓ax̌sṃ*). A 2017 archaeological study of Triquet Island places their ancestors in the region by at least 7190 BCE. The 1862 Pacific Northwest smallpox epidemic killed about 72% of the population, which fell to just under 225 by 1919 and had recovered to well over 2,500 by September 2014. Their language belongs to the Northern Wakashan branch of the Wakashan family. In folk-culture terms they matter as carpenters, carvers, painters and ceremonial specialists: they were renowned among their neighbours for artistic, military, ceremonial and spiritual expertise, and their artisans' bentwood boxes, chests, canoes and horn spoons were famous well beyond their own territory.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used document Heiltsuk surface pattern chiefly in painted and carved wood rather than in cloth; they say nothing about loom weaving, basketry or appliquéd blankets, so only the attested pattern-bearing traditions are listed here.

**Formline** — the two-dimensional design style also called Northwest Coast art, extending along the north coast, the central coast and down to Vancouver Island. The Heiltsuk are part of this tradition, and several painters of the historic period are recorded by name. A British Museum speaker's staff is described as showing "good formline delineation" of a killer whale's head, fins and tail; a large Heiltsuk face mask carries painted formline designs in black, green and red, the paint apparently including mica.

**Village painting styles** — before the mid-nineteenth century Heiltsuk-speaking tribes occupied numerous independent villages, twenty-four permanent villages and established temporary camps having been recorded. Diverse styles of painting were practised in this period, most likely originating within individual villages or social groups rather than forming one uniform school.

**Bentwood boxes and chests** — a significant feature of Bella Bella society was a cadre of highly skilled artisans noted for the construction and decoration of bentwood boxes and chests, alongside canoes and horn spoons and ladles. After contact these skills were turned to market demand for canoes and boxes.

**Cedar-bark rings** — figures on Heiltsuk speaker's staffs wear circular cedar bark head rings and headdresses, the worked bark serving as a ceremonial marker on carved figures as it did on dancers.

**Motif vocabulary.** Killer whale, the dorsal fin set over the head; raven, with eyes inlaid with abalone; bear; eagle and hummingbird, birds associated with mythological stories of the coast; the skull or skull-like head; the three-dimensional copper form; the human figure riding a fish or whale; and the stacked potlatch rings of a chief's hat. Documented palettes are black, green and red on masks; black, red and white on a carved whale; red, black and blue on a house model.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress is not described in the sources used. Ceremonial dress is: a carved frontlet in the Museum of Ethnography collection is part of a head decoration worn as part of a ceremonial outfit of the Heiltsuk Nation, executed in the style of the Northwest Coast and drawing on the bird imagery — eagle, raven, hummingbird — tied to local mythological stories. Carved figures on speaker's staffs wear circular cedar bark head rings, and one staff shows a head joined to the potlatch rings of a hat, the ring count marking rank. The officiating medicine man in the *Tánis* (Hamatsa) ceremony might wear a skull headdress. Women of high rank wore the elliptical lip ornament, or labret, described below. Blankets figure in the record as trade goods rather than as described garments: fur-trade accounts complain that the Heiltsuk demanded extra large blankets, cut them to standard size for retrade, and sewed the extra pieces together to make further blankets.

### Architecture

Heiltsuk settlement followed seasonal movement to harvest resources, centred on large permanent winter villages. A house model collected on Anna and Gustaf Retzius's 1893 journey, with a British Columbia provenance, shows the form in miniature: a traditional Northwest Coast house painted red, black and blue, complete with a smoke hole. In front of the house entrance sits a large carved raven head, its eyes inlaid with abalone; beneath the beak a male figure rides a fish or whale whose own head rests on a star-like formation, and the raven's body is painted across the house gable. Chiefs' houses were the setting for feasts, including the Christmas feasts held when the potlatch was banned. Between 1832 and 1900 some Heiltsuk built a village at McLoughlin Bay — Bella Bella, or *Qlts* / *Q̓ḷ́c* ('Qélc, "Slippery") — adjacent to the Hudson's Bay Company's Fort McLoughlin; by 1903 they had founded and largely moved into the present village of Bella Bella.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources describe no ceramics. Woodwork dominates: bentwood boxes, chests, canoes, and horn spoons and ladles made by the artisan cadre of Bella Bella, and ceremonial spoons possibly used in the ritual feeding of the skull during the *Tánis* ceremony. Two British Museum speaker's staffs show the carver's range — stacked killer whales, hatted figures, a wedge-shaped finial perhaps representing a spear point with simple geometric designs, and a three-dimensional copper form carved on one side with a human head in high relief. Mask construction is documented in detail: a large face mask mostly of alder with a separate red cedar mandible, moving eyes of yew and maple, bear-skin side and chin whiskers, pegged baleen, and skin attached with nails and spruce gum. Canoe-making extended to western-style vessels, several trading schooners being built at Bella Bella by canoe makers. Clam gardens, rediscovered through collaboration between archaeologists and traditional knowledge-holders, extend throughout the BC coast.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The best-documented Heiltsuk adornment is the labret, the lip ornament of the northern Northwest Coast. Museum of Ethnography records describe it as a defining trait of that region, worn by Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian, Haisla and Heiltsuk women and especially by women of high rank, carved in wood, bone or horn in an elliptical form and set into a perforation made in the lower lip; a woman typically began with a small ornament and exchanged it for larger ones as her rank rose. A second example is cut and filed from abalone shell — probably Californian abalone, introduced to the coast by traders and more popular than the smaller, thinner, less colourful local species — and may instead be a nose ornament, those also being common. Abalone also appears as inlay, used for the eyes of the carved raven on the house model.

## Music & performance

The sources used name no Heiltsuk instruments or song genres. They record that the Heiltsuk were and are renowned for their ceremonies, that the language has both conversational and ceremonial forms, that singing is among the arts that have grown in strength during the post-1951 cultural resurgence alongside carving and language work, and that the 'Qatuwas canoe gatherings of 1993 and 2014 featured cultural sharing including dancing, singing, the sharing of stories and food.

## Dance & theatre

Masked performance belongs to the winter sacred season, when most ceremonies were conducted. The central documented society is *Tánis*, the Hamatsa, practised by the Heiltsuk and the Kwakwakaʼwakw: a secret society involved with ceremonial cannibalism and with rituals of return to humanity, in which young men are initiated through a four-part ritual that symbolically transforms them from flesh-eating cannibals — a state equated with death — into well-behaved members of society. Skull imagery is tied to this ceremony, the skull standing for the rebirth of initiates as they come back from the dead; skull objects are used in the final stages, where ritual feeding of the skull, possibly with special ceremonial spoons, precedes a ceremonial meal for the initiates, and the officiating medicine man might wear a skull headdress. Transformation is built into the masks themselves: a British Museum bird's head mask of wood has its central part flanked by transformation flaps depicting a bear.

## Festivals & rituals

The Heiltsuk year divides into two primary parts, the winter ceremonial season and the harvest season — a secular summer of harvesting and a winter sacred season. Some harvest occurs in winter and an occasional ceremony is required during the harvest, but the distinction is clear. The culminating institution is the **potlatch**, which missionaries saw as the basis of Heiltsuk social and political organization and the most obvious expression of non-Christian belief. The Canadian federal government, spurred by Christian missionaries, outlawed it under the Indian Act; the ban took effect in the 1870s, was enforced most vociferously after 1923, and lasted until the Act was amended in 1951 — though by Heiltsuk oral tradition no one informed the Heiltsuk when it was lifted. Forced underground rather than extinguished, the feast system surfaced in Chiefs' Christmas feasts, where gift distribution could not be stopped, and in gatherings that outlasted the missionary's bedtime. Potlatching and feasting revived from the late 1960s through the 1980s, and by the 1990s were regularly hosted again. Canoe ceremony is the other great public occasion: the Heiltsuk paddled from Bella Bella to Vancouver for Expo 86, joined the 1989 Paddle to Seattle, and hosted the **'Qatuwas** gatherings of 1993 — which more than doubled the community's population for ten days — and of July 2014, with close to 60 canoes. The carved canoe *Glwa* has made many trips since the 1980s. In September 2014 the Heiltsuk travelled to Masset, Haida Gwaii to renew the peace treaty ending the nineteenth-century Heiltsuk–Haida wars; it was finalized and ratified at a potlatch in Bella Bella in 2015 with Haida chiefs attending. Of life-cycle rites, the sources describe only Hamatsa initiation.

## Foodways

The Heiltsuk have always based their food gathering significantly on the sea, and the pattern of resource use has remained remarkably stable over two hundred years of pressure. Herring is the keystone: the Heiltsuk have relied on it for thousands of years, and the traditional harvest of herring and of herring spawn is both a major part of their culture and part of a long history of trade. That history carried legal weight — the Supreme Court of Canada's 1997 *R v Gladstone* decision recognised a pre-contact commercial Aboriginal right to herring, particularly herring eggs, a first for Canada. Disputes with the Department of Fisheries and Oceans over implementing Gladstone boiled over in the 2015 season, when the Heiltsuk occupied a DFO office for four days over a seine fishery they opposed on conservation grounds; the crisis ended when the gillnet fleet left without fishing. Clam gardens, now recognised along the coast, represent a second managed marine food system. Feasting is the ceremonial frame for food: gift-giving feasts in Chiefs' houses, a ceremonial meal for Hamatsa initiates, and the sharing of food at the 'Qatuwas gatherings. The sources name no individual dishes, cooking methods or beverages.

## Oral tradition & literature

Linkage to origin stories is one of the organizing features of Heiltsuk cultural expression, and oral tradition carries historical memory — it is Heiltsuk oral tradition that records that no one told the community when the potlatch ban was lifted. The Heiltsuk word for story is *núyṃ́*. Franz Boas published *Bella Bella Texts* (1928) and *Bella Bella Tales* (1932), and the Oowekeno oral traditions told by the late chief Simon Walkus, Sr. were published in 1982. Formal oratory had its own regalia in the carved and painted speaker's staffs now in museum collections. Testimony is a documented Heiltsuk genre in its own right: before the McKenna–McBride Royal Commission at Bella Bella in 1913 the community declared, "We are the natives of this Country... The Government have not bought any land from us so far as we know and we are simply lending this land to the Government." Contemporary Heiltsuk writers include the author Eden Robinson and the television journalist Carla Robinson. Barbara Greene's 1975 National Film Board film *Bella Bella* records a period of rapid change in the community.

## Language & religion

Heiltsuk is a dialect of Heiltsuk-Oowekyala within the Northern Wakashan group, alongside Haisla and Kwakʼwala; Heiltsuk and Wuikyala are tonal, Kwakʼwala is not. It is spoken at Bella Bella (*Wágḷísḷa*) and Klemtu, with two subdialects — *Híɫzaqvḷa* at Bella Bella and *X̌íx̌íc̓ala* or *X̌íx̌ís* at Klemtu, whose speakers mostly share ancestry with the Kitasoo. The language has both conversational and ceremonial forms, three vowel phonemes, syllabic resonants written *ṃ, ṇ, ḷ*, and high and low tone. Seven fluent speakers remain; a 2016 memorandum of understanding links the Heiltsuk Cultural Education Centre, the Bella Bella Community School and UBC's First Nations and Endangered Languages Program in revitalization work. During the fur trade the Heiltsuk also used Chinook Jargon, calling Americans *booston* ("Boston-man") and a Briton a *King-George-Man*. Religiously, the community was once dominated by a strict version of Methodism under a missionary who served as religious authority, doctor and magistrate; the ceremonial and spiritual practices of the potlatch and the Hamatsa have since been publicly restored.
