## Overview

Inuit (singular *Inuk*, "the people") are the Indigenous inhabitants of the North American Arctic coast and Greenland: from the Bering Strait shore of Chukotka and northern Alaska eastward across Nunavut, the Inuvialuit Settlement Region of the Northwest Territories, Nunavik in northern Quebec, and Nunatsiavut and NunatuKavut in Labrador, to the Kalaallit settlements of west Greenland and the Tunumiit of the east coast. Roughly 180,000 people, of whom about 70,000 live in Canada and 50,000 in Greenland. Their languages — Inupiaq, Inuvialuktun, Inuinnaqtun, Inuktitut, Kalaallisut — form the Inuit-Inupiaq branch of the Inuit-Yupik-Unangan (Eskaleut) family. Inuit descend from the Thule people, who spread east from the Bering Strait around 1000 CE, reaching west Greenland by 1100 and east Greenland in the 12th century, displacing the earlier Dorset culture known in Inuktitut as the *Tuniit*. In folk-culture terms they matter as the world's most refined cold-weather engineers: a tailoring, skin-working and snow-building tradition in which every seam is a survival calculation and also an aesthetic one.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Amauti** — the Eastern Arctic woman's parka, cut with an oversized shoulder pouch (*amaut*) and a wide hood so an infant rides bare-skinned against the mother's back and can be swung around to the front to nurse without exposure. Sealskin or caribou hide in older examples, duffel and stroud with embroidered trim in the twentieth century; the enormous rounded hood and the low rear tail distinguish it sharply from the fitted Alaskan *atikluk*.

**Atigi and qulittaq** — the two-layer caribou-skin suit: the *atigi* worn fur-side in against the body, the *qulittaq* over it fur-side out, the air trapped between them doing the insulating. Winter skins were taken in late summer when the hair was dense but not yet brittle, and the two layers were cut from different animals for different loft.

**Kamik (Greenlandic kamik / kamiit)** — waterproof boots of dehaired, chewed and stretched sealskin, sewn with sinew using a blind welted seam that swells shut when wet. Greenlandic women's dress boots are dyed scarlet or white and topped with *avittat*, the sealskin mosaic described below; Canadian *kamik* keep the caribou-leg fur for dry cold and are worn over *mitsi*, the inner slipper-liner of hare or bird skin.

**Avittat** — Greenlandic sealskin mosaic: hundreds of dyed skin slivers, some only a couple of millimetres wide, spliced edge to edge into geometric bands for boot tops, mitts and the trousers of the national costume. Executed entirely in skin rather than thread, it is the single most labour-intensive Arctic decorative technique.

**Annuraaq and silapaaq** — *annuraaq* is the general term for clothing (the source of English "anorak"); the *silapaaq* is the outer windshell layer, in gut-skin or later cotton drill, worn over the fur suit to shed snow and spray.

**Greenlandic national costume (kalaallisuut / annoraaq)** — the assembled formal dress of west Greenland: glass-bead cape (*nuilarmiut*) covering the shoulders in a solid mesh of thousands of beads, sealskin shorts, thigh-high dyed *kamiit* with *avittat* inlay, and an embroidered anorak for men. Worn at confirmations, weddings and Greenland's National Day.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Avittat* zigzag and saw-tooth splices; stepped triangles; the *nuilarmiut* bead-cape rosette and horizontal colour banding; ulu-blade crescents; delta-wing insets at the boot vamp; skin-appliqué "teeth" fringes at hem and cuff; white-on-dark caribou belly-skin panels in Copper Inuit parkas; tattoo-derived line-and-Y patterns (*kakiniit*) transferred onto duffel trim; bird-track and caribou-hoof lines in modern embroidered parkas.

### Clothing & dress

Clothing is the Inuit master art, and it was women's work: a seamstress's reputation was a survival asset, and Captain George Comer's Inuk wife Shoofly was known across Hudson Bay for her sewing and dress. Both sexes wore the paired *atigi*/*qulittaq* caribou suit with fur-side-in inner layer, sealskin trousers, *kamik* boots over *mitsi* liners, and mitts (*pualuk*). Men's parkas were cut short and straight for kayak work; women's carried the *amaut* pouch and a long front-and-back flap for kneeling on ice. Copper Inuit parkas used white belly-skin insets against dark back-skin in deliberate contrast panels. Hoods rather than separate hats, ruffed with wolverine or wolf, whose stiff guard hairs break the wind without frosting. Snow goggles (*iggaak*) of wood, bone or antler with a hairline slit cut glare on spring ice. Formal dress in Greenland is the beaded *nuilarmiut* cape with *avittat* trousers and boots; in Nunavut, duffel parkas with braid trim and embroidered hood surrounds serve the same ceremonial role.

### Architecture

Inuit built seasonally rather than permanently. The *iglu* — a snow dome of blocks cut with a snow knife and laid in a rising spiral, sealed by glazing the interior with a *qulliq* flame — holds a sleeping platform above a cold-sink entrance tunnel, and can be raised in an hour by two hunters. Interiors were lined with skins hung from pegs to keep meltwater off the sleepers; a gut-skin or ice pane admitted light. The *qarmaq* is the cold-season semi-subterranean house: excavated floor, whale-rib or driftwood rafters, sod and stone walls, a skin or snow roof, rebuilt each autumn. Summer meant the *tupiq*, a conical or ridge tent of sealskin held down by a ring of stones. Across the landscape stand *inuksuit* — stacked stone figures used as navigation marks, caribou drive lines and cache indicators; the *inunnguaq*, built in human form, is one variant. Greenlandic post-colonial settlements added the painted timber house, colour-coded by function (red for church and trade, yellow for hospital), now itself a regional signature.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

There is no Inuit pottery tradition of note; the working materials are stone, bone, antler, ivory and driftwood. The **ulu**, the women's crescent-bladed knife with an antler or bone handle, is the central tool of skin-working, butchery and sewing-pattern cutting, and remains an identity emblem. The **qulliq**, a shallow soapstone lamp burning seal oil along a moss or Arctic-cotton wick, gave light, heat, cooking and the iglu's interior glaze from a single flame; it is lit ceremonially to open assemblies in Nunavut. Soapstone also yielded cooking pots and, in the twentieth century, the carving industry. Iron was scarce and precious: Inuit raided Basque whaling stations at Red Bay, Labrador, in winter for worked iron, and Moravian missionaries later traded it deliberately. *Tupilaq* figures — grotesque composite creatures in whale tooth, bone and antler from East Greenland — were originally the material form of a revenge charm.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The defining Inuit body art is *kakiniit* (also *tunniit*), women's tattooing, documented in the Arctic for nearly 4,000 years. Lines across the chin, forehead, cheeks and hands encoded where a woman came from, her family, her achievements and her standing; the technique was skin-stitching, pulling sinew or bone-needle thread soaked in soot and suet through the skin, or poking with a needle. Christian missionaries suppressed the practice in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries alongside drum dancing and Inuktitut; the Inuit Tattoo Revitalization Project and a generation of Inuit women have brought it back since the 2010s, now with modern ink. Ornament otherwise runs to carved ivory and bone: *sakiak* and other bead- and ivory-trimmed labrets, hair ornaments, combs, and amulets (*aarnguaq*) of claw, tooth or miniature carving sewn into clothing. Glass beads arrived through the whaling trade and were absorbed wholesale into the Greenlandic *nuilarmiut* cape and Canadian parka trim.

## Music & performance

The central instrument is the *qilaut* (Greenlandic *qilaat*), a single-headed frame drum: a hoop of bent driftwood or baleen with a skin or bladder membrane, struck on the **rim** rather than the head with a slim wooden beater, producing a dry knock with a ringing overtone. Drum dance (*qilaujjarniq* / *qilaatersorneq*) belonged to shamans (*angakkuit*), to welcome and settlement gatherings, and to dispute settlement — the song duel, in which two men traded improvised mocking verses before the community until one lost face, resolved grievances without violence.

The best-known genre internationally is *katajjaq*, Inuit throat singing, practised in Nunavik and Nunavut as a paired game between two women standing face to face: interlocking voiced and unvoiced breath patterns over rhythmic ostinati, named after their subjects — the saw, the river, the goose, the sled dog — and played until one breaks into laughter. It is competitive and social, not liturgical. Greenland's counterpart performance culture includes *piseq* / *pisiit*, personal songs composed by an individual about their own hunting life or grievances and owned by the composer, and the mask performances of the east coast.

Mission churches introduced hymnody early, and Greenland developed a distinctive four-part Kalaallisut hymn and brass-band tradition; the button accordion and *kalattuut*, a polka-derived dance music, came with whalers and remain the sound of a Greenlandic party. Contemporary Inuit performers — Tanya Tagaq, Susan Aglukark, the Nunavik throat-singing duos — work directly from *katajjaq* and drum-dance material.

## Dance & theatre

Drum dancing is the core performance form: a single dancer inside the circle, knees flexed and torso pumping, drumming their own song while the community answers the refrain, in a *qaggiq* — a large ceremonial iglu built for winter gatherings. There is no proscenium theatre tradition, but there is developed masked performance: East Greenlandic and Alaskan-adjacent mask dances used carved wooden faces with asymmetrical, deliberately distorted features to embody spirits and to provoke laughter, and the *uaajeerneq* mask dance of Greenland — face painted black, red and white, mouth stuffed to distort the features — is an explicitly grotesque, sexual and frightening solo form used to test and toughen the audience, particularly children. It has been consciously revived in Greenlandic theatre since the 1970s. Both drum dance and mask dance were banned or shamed out of practice by missionaries across much of the Canadian Arctic, and much of what is danced today in Nunavut was relearned from Greenlandic and Copper Inuit holdouts.

## Festivals & rituals

The Arctic calendar is solar rather than agricultural. The return of the sun after weeks or months of polar night is the pivotal moment: in Greenland and the high Arctic it is marked by community gatherings, feasting and children running to greet the first rim of light. Midwinter drum-dance assemblies in the *qaggiq* brought scattered camps together to sing, hold song duels, trade and arrange marriages. In Greenland, **Ullortuneq** (National Day, 21 June, the solstice) is the great occasion for wearing the national costume; **Kalaallit** communities also keep a strongly observed Christmas and a confirmation season at which a girl's first full costume is presented.

Life-cycle practice is dense with naming: a newborn receives the *atiq*, the name-soul of a recently deceased relative, and is then addressed by that person's kin terms, so a child may be called "grandmother" by their own parent. Birth was attended by a midwife who retained a lifelong sponsor relationship with the child. A boy's first seal or first caribou was distributed through the camp rather than kept, marking entry into the hunt. Marriage was arranged early, often in infancy, and was undramatic — no wedding ceremony of note before the missions. Death involved strict taboos on naming the dead and on work in the days following. Christian conversion came through the Moravians in Labrador from the late eighteenth century and through Anglican and Catholic missions in the twentieth; the *siqqitiq* ritual, in which converts ate forbidden food to break the old taboos, formally marked the transition.

## Foodways

The traditional diet is almost entirely animal — roughly 75 percent of daily energy from fat — and is eaten substantially raw or fermented, which is what makes it nutritionally complete: ringed-seal liver and *muktuk* (whale skin with the blubber attached) carry enough vitamin C to prevent scurvy, as Vilhjalmur Stefansson demonstrated living on the diet in the 1920s and later researchers confirmed. Core species are ringed, harp, bearded and common seal, bowhead and beluga whale, walrus, caribou, muskox, polar bear, Arctic char, cod, ptarmigan and waterfowl, plus eggs and, in season, berries, roots, plant stems and *kuanniq* (edible seaweed).

Named dishes: *quaq* / *quaqtaq*, frozen raw meat or fish shaved into chips with an *ulu*; *igunaq*, meat or walrus aged in its own hide in a stone cache until fermented; *mattaq* / *muktuk*, raw whale skin and blubber chewed in cubes; *misiraq*, rendered seal or whale oil used as a dip for everything dry; *suaasat*, the Greenlandic national soup of seal, whale or seabird with onion, potato and rice or barley; *kiviak*, East Greenlandic little auks packed whole into a sealskin and fermented under stone for months; *panitsiaq* / *bannock*, the fried or baked flour bread adopted from the trade posts and now universal; *akutaq*, whipped fat with berries. Tea, drunk strong and very sweet, arrived with the Hudson's Bay Company and is the default hospitality offering. Sharing rules are the real cuisine: a large kill is divided by fixed shares among the hunters and the camp, and country food still moves through kinship networks and community freezers rather than markets.

## Oral tradition & literature

There is no single national epic; the corpus is a shared body of narrative held across the whole Arctic. Central is **Sedna** (*Sanna*, *Nuliajuk*, *Takannaaluk*), the woman at the bottom of the sea who withholds the game animals when human taboos are broken and must be placated by a shaman's soul-journey to comb her hair. Other recurring cycles: **Kiviuq**, the wandering hero whose episodic travels among shape-shifters and giants run to dozens of hours in a full telling; the blind boy and the loon; the woman who married a dog; *Qalupalik*, the sea creature who takes children from the ice edge; and stories of the *Tuniit*, remembered as giants stronger than Inuit who receded before them. Narrative genres are distinguished: *unikkaaqtuat* are the old accounts held to be true, *unikkaaqtaaq* the tellable story. Stories were told on the sleeping platform through the dark months, and the storyteller was expected to pass a version on intact.

Frobisher's 1576 sailors who stayed on Baffin Island entered oral tradition as a story of their own. Twentieth-century documentation rests heavily on Knud Rasmussen's Fifth Thule Expedition and on Inuit-led collection since the 1970s; the *Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit* framework now formalises traditional knowledge in Nunavut law and schooling, and Nunavut's oral-history projects, Isuma's Inuktitut film work and Markoosie Patsauq's *Harpoon of the Hunter* (1970), the first Inuktitut novel, carry the tradition into print and screen.

## Language & religion

The Inuit languages belong to the Inuit-Inupiaq branch of Inuit-Yupik-Unangan, and form a continuum from Inupiaq in Alaska through Inuvialuktun, Inuinnaqtun and Inuktitut in Canada to Kalaallisut, Inuktun and Tunumiit in Greenland; Inuktitut and Kalaallisut, the two prestige varieties, are the most closely related pair. They are polysynthetic, building sentence-length words from a root and stacked postbases. Eastern Canadian Inuktitut is written in the syllabics adapted by missionaries from Cree; Greenlandic and the western Canadian languages use Latin orthographies. Inuit Sign Language, a deaf language isolate, has around 50 users. Almost all Inuit are now Christian — Moravian and Anglican in Labrador, Anglican and Catholic in Nunavut, Lutheran in Greenland — but the pre-Christian cosmology persists in practice and language: *inua*, the indwelling person of an animal or place; *tuurngait*, helping and hostile spirits; *angakkuit*, shamans who negotiated with Sedna and Sila, the weather-air; and the taboo system whose breach caused game to withdraw.
