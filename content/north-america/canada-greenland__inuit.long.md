## Overview

The Inuit are the Arctic-dwelling peoples of Canada and Greenland, speakers of a remarkably continuous dialect chain — Inuvialuktun, Inuinnaqtun, Inuktitut, Inuttitut and Kalaallisut — that stretches from the Mackenzie Delta across the Canadian Arctic Archipelago to the fjords of East Greenland, such that a speaker from Iqaluit and one from Nuuk can recognise much of each other's speech across some three thousand kilometres of tundra and sea ice. They descend from the Thule culture, maritime hunters who spread eastward out of coastal Alaska from roughly the thirteenth century, carrying with them the dog sled, the skin boat and the toggling harpoon, and displacing or absorbing the earlier Dorset people whom Inuit oral tradition remembers as the *Tuniit* — strong, shy, and out-competed. Their homeland is one of the most demanding environments any human population has permanently occupied, and their material culture is correspondingly the product of extreme optimisation: almost everything was made from sea mammal, caribou, bird, fish, stone, bone, ivory, driftwood and snow, and almost every object served more than one purpose. Population today numbers roughly 150,000 across the circumpolar world, concentrated in Greenland — where Kalaallit constitute the national majority and Kalaallisut is an official language — and in the Canadian territory of Nunavut, created in 1999 with Iqaluit as its capital, alongside the regions of Nunavik in northern Quebec, Nunatsiavut in Labrador, and the Inuvialuit Settlement Region in the western Arctic. Inuit signature contributions to world material culture are unusually consequential for so small a population: the tailored, two-layer fur clothing system that made sustained Arctic habitation possible and that European polar explorers eventually copied wholesale; the *qajaq*, ancestor of every kayak in the world; the domed snow house that became, fairly or not, a global shorthand for the Arctic; and — in the modern era — the Cape Dorset and Kinngait printmaking cooperatives, which since the late 1950s have made Inuit graphic art one of the most internationally recognised Indigenous art movements anywhere.

## Material culture

Inuit material culture is built almost entirely from animals, and its genius lies in tailoring, insulation and hydrodynamics rather than in applied surface ornament. Decoration exists — fur mosaic, bead panels, incised ivory — but it is typically structural, arising out of how pelts are cut and pieced.

### Textile & pattern traditions

There is no loom weaving in the Inuit tradition and no indigenous textile fibre; the equivalent craft is skin sewing, in which pattern is generated by piecing contrasting furs and by inserted decorative strips.

- **Fur mosaic inlay** (*avittat*) — Narrow strips and geometric insets of contrasting light and dark skin, commonly bleached and natural sealskin, pieced into trims and borders; in Greenland this develops into dense polychrome bands of extraordinary fineness worked into boot tops and jacket hems.
- **Waterproof gut seam** — Sinew-sewn seams worked so that the thread never fully penetrates the outer hide, swelling shut when wet, the technique that made skin clothing and *qajaq* covers watertight.
- **Sakiak beadwork** — Dense beaded shoulder and collar panels, particularly associated with women's ceremonial dress; imported glass beads were adopted enthusiastically after contact and worked into broad, heavily patterned yokes.
- **Nuilarmiut** — Decorative fringing and trim elements used to finish garment edges, hoods and hems in some Greenlandic and Canadian regional styles.
- **Sealskin mosaic** — Larger-scale pieced sealskin work in wall hangings, bags and, more recently, in contemporary fashion, using the natural range of seal pelt tones as a palette.

### Clothing & dress

The Inuit clothing system is the single most sophisticated cold-weather garment tradition ever developed, based on two layers worn with air trapped between them — an inner layer with fur turned inward against the skin, an outer layer with fur turned outward.

- **Amauti** (*amauti*) — Woman's parka with a deep back pouch and oversized hood in which an infant rides naked against the mother's back, able to nurse without being exposed to the air; the definitive Inuit garment and a continuing symbol of Inuit motherhood.
- **Inner parka** (*atigi*) — Lighter inner coat worn with the fur against the body, forming the first layer of the two-layer system.
- **Outer parka** (*qulittaq*) — Heavier outer coat, commonly of caribou with the hair outward, sometimes edged in wolverine or wolf fur at the hood ruff because frost brushes off those hairs rather than building up.
- **Boots** (*kamik*, also *kamiit*) — Sealskin or caribou boots, sewn waterproof for summer wear and built with an insulating inner stocking for winter; Greenlandic women's white *kamik* with fur mosaic bands are among the most elaborate.
- **Mittens** (*mitsi*) — Skin mittens, often caribou or sealskin, frequently attached by a cord across the shoulders so they cannot be lost.
- **Annuraaq and silapaaq** (*annuraaq*, *silapaaq*) — General terms for clothing and for the outer windproof shell garment respectively, the latter sometimes made of gut-skin in a translucent, rustling waterproof form.
- **Greenlandic national costume** (*kalaallisuut*) — The modern ceremonial dress of Greenland, combining a beaded yoke, patterned sealskin shorts, red or white *kamik* with fur inlay, and imported cloth elements, worn at confirmations and national occasions.

### Architecture

Inuit building is seasonal and transportable rather than monumental: winter forms of snow or sod, summer forms of skin, and no permanent settlement architecture before the twentieth century.

- **Snow house** (*iglu*) — Domed winter dwelling of cut snow blocks laid in a rising spiral, with a sunken entrance tunnel to trap cold air, a raised sleeping platform and often a window of clear freshwater ice; heated and lit by a single oil lamp, it could hold interior temperatures far above the outside air.
- **Sod house** (*qarmaq*) — Semi-subterranean winter dwelling of stone, whalebone or driftwood framing covered with turf and skins, used where snow conditions or longer residence made it preferable; the standard Thule-era winter house.
- **Skin tent** (*tupiq*) — Summer tent of sealskin or caribou hide over a light frame, weighted at the edges with stones, the ring of which frequently survives as the visible archaeological trace of a camp.
- **Stone marker** (*inuksuk*) — Stacked stone figure built to mark a route, a cache, a good fishing place or a caribou drive line; the human-shaped variant, properly *inunnguaq*, became the emblem of Nunavut and of the 2010 Vancouver Olympics.
- **Meat cache and drying rack** — Stone-built caches raised against dogs and foxes, and raised racks for drying fish and meat, are the other durable built forms of the landscape.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

There is no pottery tradition of consequence and no indigenous metallurgy beyond cold-worked meteoric and native copper; the material repertoire is instead stone, bone, antler, ivory and driftwood, worked to a very high standard.

- **Woman's knife** (*ulu*) — Crescent-bladed knife with a handle set above the blade, used for skinning, butchering, cutting snow blocks and trimming skins; held in slate before contact and steel after, and passed between generations of women.
- **Oil lamp** (*qulliq*) — Shallow crescent of carved soapstone burning rendered seal or whale oil along a ridge of trimmed moss wick; the single source of heat, light, cooking and drying in a winter dwelling, tended by the woman of the household and now lit ceremonially to open public occasions across Inuit Nunangat.
- **Kayak** (*qajaq*) — Single-hulled skin-on-frame hunting boat of driftwood or bone lashed with sinew and covered with sealskin, built to the individual hunter's body measurements; the Greenlandic rolling repertoire, with dozens of named recovery techniques, survives as a competitive discipline.
- **Skin boat** (*umiaq*) — Large open skin boat rowed or paddled by a crew, used for moving families and camp goods and, in some regions, for whaling; often called the "woman's boat" from the composition of its usual crew.
- **Carving in stone and ivory** (*tupilak*) — In Greenland, the *tupilak* was originally an assembled malevolent object created by a shaman from animal parts; the carved ivory or bone figures sold under the name today are representations of such beings and constitute a major East Greenlandic carving tradition.
- **Scrimshaw** — Fine incised drawing on ivory and bone, filled with soot or pigment, including the well-known nineteenth-century engraved cribbage boards and tusks of the western and central Arctic.
- **Cape Dorset stonecut** — Printmaking tradition established at Kinngait (Cape Dorset) in the late 1950s, using incised stone blocks and stencils; artists such as Kenojuak Ashevak and Pitseolak Ashoona brought Inuit graphic art to international attention, and the annual print collection remains a fixture of the Canadian art calendar.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Personal ornament is modest in quantity compared with the clothing tradition, but tattooing carried heavy social meaning and has become a central site of cultural revival.

- **Facial tattoos** (*tunniit*) — Lines and chevrons tattooed on women's faces — most characteristically on the chin, cheeks and forehead — marking maturity, accomplishment and belonging; suppressed under missionary pressure and revived from the 2010s onward through community tattoo projects.
- **Body tattoos** (*kakiniit*) — Tattooed bands and patterns on hands, arms, thighs and shoulders, applied by skin-stitching with sinew drawn through the skin or by hand-poking, each mark commonly tied to a life event.
- **Amulets** — Small objects of bone, claw, tooth or skin sewn to clothing, especially children's, to attach the qualities of an animal to the wearer.
- **Beaded ornament** — Post-contact glass beadwork applied to collars, headbands and bag fronts, often in dense geometric or fringed panels.
- **Ivory and bone pendants** — Carved toggles, combs, hair fasteners and small figural pendants, frequently doubling as functional fastenings.

## Music & performance

Inuit music is overwhelmingly vocal and percussive, with the single-membrane drum as the only traditional instrument of significance; melodic instruments are absent from the old repertoire and enter only after contact.

- **Drum dance** (*qilaut* or *qilausiq*) — Large hoop drum with a skin or, latterly, synthetic membrane and a short handle, struck on the rim rather than the face with a wooden beater, played by a soloist who sings and moves with it; the central performance form of Inuit ceremonial life.
- **Throat singing** (*katajjaq*, in Greenland related forms) — Competitive vocal game between two women standing face to face, producing rhythmic voiced and unvoiced sounds on the inbreath and outbreath in interlocking patterns until one laughs or runs out of breath; suppressed by missionaries and now widely revived and performed on international stages.
- **Personal song** (*pisiq*) — Composed song belonging to an individual, recounting a hunt, a journey or a grievance, and sometimes passed on as a form of property.
- **Song duel** — Formalised public exchange of mocking composed songs used in some regions to settle disputes without violence, the loser being decided by the audience's response.
- **Greenlandic fiddle and accordion music** — Post-contact European instruments absorbed into Greenlandic dance music, most visibly in the *kalattuut* polka repertoire.

## Dance & theatre

Performance is not divided into distinct dance and theatre genres; the drum dance carries narrative, satirical and dramatic functions together, and masked performance belongs mainly to the western reaches of the Inuit world.

- **Drum dancing** — Solo performance combining song, percussion and bent-knee, swaying movement inside the gathering house, historically opening and closing communal events and now performed at festivals, graduations and political ceremonies.
- **Greenlandic polka** (*kalattuut*) — Couple dance in European form adopted in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and thoroughly naturalised, danced to accordion at celebrations throughout Greenland.
- **Mask performance** — Wooden and skin masks used in dance and mimetic performance, most strongly developed among related Yupik peoples to the west and present in attenuated form in parts of the Inuit region.
- **Mimetic and comic play** — Imitation of animals, of absent persons and of outsiders formed a large part of winter entertainment, shading from mime into outright satire.
- **String figures** (*ajaraaq*) — Elaborate string games accompanied by chanted narration, functioning as both pastime and storytelling medium, traditionally restricted to particular seasons.

## Festivals & rituals

Ritual life followed the year of the hunt and the return of the sun rather than an agricultural calendar, and much of it was reorganised around Christian observance after the nineteenth century.

- **Return of the sun** (*Qaumaniq*, variously named) — Celebration of the sun's reappearance after the polar night, marked in many communities by extinguishing and relighting the lamps, by games and by feasting.
- **Bladder and sea-mammal rites** — Ceremonies returning the bladders or bones of hunted sea mammals to the water so the animal's soul could be reborn, expressing the reciprocal obligation between hunter and prey.
- **First-hunt celebration** — Communal marking of a young hunter's first significant catch, the meat commonly distributed to elders rather than kept.
- **Nunavut Day and Greenland's National Day** — Modern civic festivals — 9 July in Nunavut, 21 June in Greenland — at which national costume, drum dance and traditional food are publicly displayed.
- **Games and athletics** — Arctic Winter Games and community contests preserving traditional trials of strength and endurance, including the one-foot high kick, knuckle hop and mouth pull, once used to keep hunters conditioned through the dark months.

## Foodways

Inuit diet was, until the twentieth century, almost entirely animal, and the traditional cuisine — now called *country food* in Canada — remains a central marker of identity, a political issue in food-security debates, and a widely shared practice of hunting and distribution.

Seal, walrus, whale, caribou, muskox, Arctic char, birds and their eggs form the core, supplemented seasonally by berries, seaweed, roots and the partially digested stomach contents of caribou. Much is eaten raw or frozen — *quaq*, frozen raw meat sliced thin, and *mattak* (*muktuk*), the skin and adjacent blubber of narwhal or beluga, valued both as a delicacy and, as later nutritional research confirmed, as a significant source of vitamin C in a plant-poor diet. Fermentation and ageing in skin containers and under stone caches produced *igunaq*, aged meat with a strong flavour, and in Greenland *kiviaq*, small auks packed whole into a sealskin and left to ferment under stone. Rendered seal oil serves as a dipping condiment as well as lamp fuel. The ethic surrounding food matters as much as its content: a catch is shared across a community according to established relationships, and sharing networks remain a functioning institution rather than a remembered one.

## Oral tradition & literature

Before the nineteenth century, Inuit literature was entirely oral, carried in narrative, song and the memorised knowledge of ice, weather, route and genealogy that Canadian Inuit now formalise as *Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit* — the body of traditional knowledge and values that Nunavut's government is legally obliged to incorporate.

The best-known narrative cycle concerns *Sedna* (*Nuliajuk* or *Takannaaluk* in various dialects), the woman cast from a boat whose severed fingers became the sea mammals and who governs the sea's willingness to give them up; a shaman's most demanding task was the spirit journey to comb her hair and release the animals. Other widespread cycles include the orphan Kaujjajjuk who grows strong through spirit aid, the blind boy and the loon, and the sun and moon siblings whose pursuit explains their separation in the sky. Writing arrived twice: in Greenland through the Roman alphabet, producing a printed Kalaallisut literature from the nineteenth century onward — including Mathias Storch's 1914 novel, generally counted the first Greenlandic novel — and in the eastern Canadian Arctic through the syllabic script adapted by missionaries from Cree syllabics, which Inuktitut speakers took up rapidly and which remains in official use in Nunavut alongside Roman orthography. Twentieth-century collectors, above all Knud Rasmussen on the Fifth Thule Expedition of 1921–24, recorded a large body of songs and narratives that remains foundational, though it is now read critically alongside community-held versions.

## Language & religion

Inuktut and Kalaallisut are polysynthetic languages of great morphological density, in which a single word built from a root and a long chain of suffixes can carry the content of an English sentence, and in which a fine-grained vocabulary distinguishes conditions of snow, ice and sea state that matter for travel and hunting. Vitality differs sharply by region: Kalaallisut is the sole official language of Greenland and is transmitted normally across generations, and Inuktitut remains strong in Nunavut and Nunavik, while Inuvialuktun and the Labrador dialect are seriously endangered after decades of residential schooling and English-medium administration.

Religious life today is overwhelmingly Christian — Lutheran in Greenland following the eighteenth-century Danish-Norwegian mission of Hans Egede, Anglican and Catholic across much of Arctic Canada — but the older cosmology has not disappeared so much as been reframed. It held that persons, animals and features of the land possessed *inua*, an indwelling person or owner, and that human wellbeing depended on observing an extensive system of prohibitions governing how animals were killed, shared and disposed of. Breaches were understood to cause illness and hunting failure, and the *angakkuq* — the shaman — was the specialist who diagnosed them, made confession public, and travelled in trance to petition the beings who controlled the game. Naming practice remains widely observed: a child given the name of a recently deceased relative is addressed by that person's kin terms and understood to carry something of their identity, a custom that persists comfortably alongside church membership.
