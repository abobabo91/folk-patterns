## Overview

The Cupeño, also called *Kuupangaxwichem*, are a Native American tribe of Southern California whose traditional homeland lay in the mountains of the San José Valley at the headwaters of the San Luis Rey River, about 50 miles inland and 50 miles north of the modern-day Mexico–United States border. Their villages were Wilákalpa and Kúpa, north of present-day Warner Springs, California, with homelands extending to Agua Caliente east of Lake Henshaw. Several groups combined to form Cupeño culture around 1000 to 1200 AD. The language belonged to the Cupan group of the Takic branch of the Uto-Aztecan family. The Cupeño population was estimated at 500 in 1770, between 500 and 750 in 1795, 150 in 1910, fewer than 150 in 1973, and about 200 in 2000. Their cultural record includes village life, basket making, beading, Bird Songs, and a history of forced relocation.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The women made lace and took in laundry, while the men carved wood and manufactured saddle pads for horses. The Cupa Cultural Center hosts basket-making and beading classes, but the sources provide no named basket forms, patterns, materials, or techniques.

### Clothing & dress

 They record that Cupeño women made lace and washed laundry in the hot springs, while men manufactured saddle pads for horses. The sources also state that Cupeño travelers sold craftwork.

### Architecture

The Cupeño traditionally lived in two autonomous villages, Wilákalpa and Kúpa, near Warner Springs, California. At Agua Caliente, they built an adobe ranch house in 1849 and a barn in 1857; both were still standing as of 1963. After the forced relocation to Pala, the United States government provided Ducker Patent Portable Houses, which an Indian Office report described as “very unsatisfactory”; some quickly fell into disrepair or collapsed.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention carved wood, manufactured saddle pads, cattle, cultivated land, and craftwork sold to travelers. The Cupa Cultural Center exhibits artwork and offers basket-making and beading activities, but the catalogue of named household objects, tools, and techniques is not provided.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 Beading is mentioned among the activities hosted by the Cupa Cultural Center, but no beadwork objects, materials, motifs, or ceremonial uses are identified.

## Music & performance

Young people still learn to sing in Cupeño, particularly **Bird Songs**. Roscinda Nolásquez is described as the last truly fluent Cupeño speaker and, in the language article, as the last native speaker of Cupeño. The Cupeño language is now widely regarded as extinct; in 1994, Leanne Hinton estimated that one to five people still spoke it, while nine people in the 1990 US census reported speaking the language.

The sources describe the Cupa Cultural Center in Pala as a place that exhibits artwork and hosts classes and activities, including basket making, beading, and Cupeño language classes. Cupa Days is celebrated there during the first weekend of every May.

## Festivals & rituals

Cupa Days is celebrated at the Cupa Cultural Center in Pala during the first weekend of every May.

Cupeño society is divided into two moieties, the Coyote and Wildcat, which are divided into several patrilineal clans. Clans are led by hereditary male clan leaders and assistant leaders, and marriages were traditionally arranged.

The forced relocation of the Cupa Indians to Pala took place on May 13, 1903. The move was 75 miles away, to Pala, California, on the San Luis Rey River. Cupeño descendants continue to regard Kúpa as a significant cultural and religious area, and the Cupa site serves as a rallying point for contemporary land claims and efforts to regain cultural and religious areas.

## Foodways

Traditional Cupeño foods included acorns, cactus fruit, seeds, berries, deer, quail, rabbits, and other small game. The people also raised cattle and cultivated 200 acres of land.

Before the forced eviction, Cupeño villagers sold milk and fodder to travelers on the Southern Immigrant Trail and to passengers on the stagecoaches of the Butterfield Overland Mail, which stopped at Warner’s Ranch and passed through the valley.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They preserve several historical recollections connected with the forced relocation, including Roscinda Nolásquez’s account of the last morning at Cupa and Cecilio Blacktooth’s statement in 1903 that the Cupeño could not live anywhere else because they were born there and their fathers were buried there.

Educational materials for the Cupeño language exist, and young people continue to learn to sing in Cupeño, particularly Bird Songs. The Cupa Cultural Center, founded in 1974 in Pala and expanded in 2005, offers Cupeño language classes and hosts cultural activities.

## Language & religion

Cupeño, also called *Páʼanexily*, is an Uto-Aztecan language of the Cupan group, within the Takic branch. The language was originally spoken in Cupa, Wilaqalpa, and Paluqla in San Diego County, California, and later around the Pala Indian Reservation. Its native name *Kupangaxwicham* means “people from the sleeping place,” referring to the traditional homeland of Ktipa at the base of Warner’s Hot Springs; the smaller village to the south was Wildkalpa. The Cupeño language shows influence from earlier languages and from the Yuman-speaking Ipai. They state that Cupeño people are seeking to regain cultural and religious areas at Kúpa.
