## Overview

The Navajo call themselves *Diné*, "the people," and number more than 399,494 enrolled citizens of the Navajo Nation — the second-largest federally recognized tribe in the United States. Their reservation straddles the Four Corners across Arizona, Utah and New Mexico, covering over 27,325 square miles: slightly larger than West Virginia, bounded by the four sacred mountains and cut by Canyon de Chelly, the Chuska Mountains, the San Juan River and Mount Taylor. Arizona holds 140,263 Diné and New Mexico 108,305. *Diné bizaad* is a Southern Athabaskan (Na-Dené) language, closely related to Apache, carried south from northwestern Canada and eastern Alaska roughly a thousand years ago. In folk-culture terms the Diné matter disproportionately: their wool weaving, silverwork and dry painting are among the most fully documented and most continuously practiced Indigenous craft traditions in North America, and the whole complex is young — built after Spanish sheep arrived, within a matrilineal economy in which women owned the flocks.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Diné weaving** — Upright-loom tapestry weave in handspun wool, worked from the bottom up with no shuttle and no fixed pattern draft; the weaver builds the design in her head as the web rises. The technique was learned from Pueblo cotton weavers, then transformed when Spanish *churro* sheep made wool the fiber. Blankets, not rugs, were the original product; the shift to rugs came with reservation-era trading posts.

**Chief's blanket** — The oldest surviving Diné wearing-blanket form, wider than long, worn across the shoulders. First Phase is plain banded brown, white and indigo; Second Phase adds red rectangular blocks to the bands; Third Phase breaks the bands with nine partial and whole diamonds. Never restricted to chiefs — the name reflects who could afford them, mostly Plains buyers.

**Eye Dazzler** — Post-1868 style built from raveled or commercial *Germantown* three-ply yarns in aniline reds, oranges, greens and purples, massed into serrated zigzags and stacked diamonds that vibrate optically. The style marks the moment industrial yarn and dye reached the reservation.

**Two Grey Hills** — Undyed natural wool only: cream, grey, tan, brown and a carded black, in dense concentric-diamond and stepped-terrace compositions with a black border. The finest examples reach tapestry-grade thread counts. Distinctively colorless in a region where everything else is red.

**Ganado red** — A saturated deep red ground, black border and white and grey figuring, usually built around a single dominant central diamond. Associated with Hubbell Trading Post at Ganado, Arizona, where the trader pushed a fixed palette on weavers to create a recognizable product.

**Teec Nos Pos** — The most visually crowded regional style: outlined figures, serrated elements and a wide, busy, contrasting border, often with intricate feather-like and zigzag fill. **Wide Ruins** and **Crystal** by contrast are borderless banded rugs in vegetal dyes — soft golds, greens, tans and rust from plant sources, with Crystal's characteristic wavy-line banding. **Burntwater** combines the Ganado-style bordered layout with the vegetal-dye palette, and **Chinle** is a banded vegetal revival style.

**Storm Pattern** — A central rectangle representing a hogan or the center of the world, joined by zigzag lightning lines to four corner boxes read as the sacred mountains. One of the few Diné layouts with a stable iconographic reading.

**Yei rug and Yeibichai rug** — Figurative weavings showing the elongated, frontal *Yeʼii* (Holy People) as they appear in *dry painting*, or the masked *Yeibichai* dancers in profile procession. These are commercial textiles, not ritual objects, and their appearance in the 1890s caused controversy among Diné who considered the imagery restricted.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Serrated diamond* (the dominant Eye Dazzler unit), *stepped terrace* (cloud or mesa terracing), *whirling logs* (four-armed swastika-form motif, abandoned after the 1930s), *lightning zigzag* (Storm Pattern connector), *comb* or *feather* (Teec Nos Pos fill), *Yeʼii figure* (elongated frontal Holy Person), *water bug* and *spider* (small filler figures), *weaver's pathway* or *spirit line* (a thin line breaking the border so the weaver's thought escapes the finished rug), *cornstalk* (fertility, corn as the wedding food), *banded ground* (Chief's blanket and Wide Ruins organization).

### Clothing & dress

The defining woman's garment is the *biil* — a two-panel dress of two small twill-woven blankets in dark brown or black with deep red banded ends, sewn together at the shoulders and sides and belted with a woven sash. It replaced an earlier two-piece hide dress and was itself largely displaced after the Long Walk by the *Navajo velvet blouse* and long tiered *broomstick skirt*, sewn in imitation of mid-nineteenth-century Anglo dress and now the emblematic ceremonial outfit: crushed velveteen blouse heavy with silver buttons, satin or cotton skirt, moccasins, and the full weight of turquoise and silver. Men wear velveteen shirts, *concha belt*, and formerly wool leggings and woven garters. Footwear is the wrap-around buckskin moccasin with a rawhide sole and, for women, an extended legging wrapping the calf. Hair for both sexes is drawn back and bound into the *tsiiyeel*, a hourglass-shaped bundle tied with white wool cord — the single most legible marker of traditional Diné identity. *Pendleton trade cloth* blankets, acquired commercially from the 1890s, became and remain formal wear for men and gifts at every life passage.

### Architecture

The *hogan* is the Diné dwelling and the only structure in the Southwest built to a cosmological plan the builders can still narrate. The door faces east to meet the morning sun. The older "male" hogan (*ʼalchʼįʼ adeezʼá*) is conical or forked-pole, built on three or five leaning timbers with a distinct projecting rectangular entryway; the "female" hogan (*hooghan nimazí*) is the round-to-octagonal form — from about 1900 built as a cribbed six- or eight-sided log structure, corbelled inward to a cribbed dome roof with a central smokehole, and banked over with earth. *The Blessingway* (*hózhǫ́ǫ́jí*) tells of the first hogan built by Coyote with logs and instruction from the Beaver People, as a house for First Man, First Woman and Talking God. Interior space is assigned by direction and gender; the fire is central. Seasonal summer shelters are open brush *ramadas*. Most Diné now live in frame houses, trailers or town apartments, but log hogans remain in use — and hogans kept purely for ceremony are common, since many rites cannot properly be held anywhere else.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Diné pottery is utilitarian and deliberately plain: undecorated coiled *Diné bizaad* cooking and water jars, thin-walled and fired in a dung fire, then coated while hot with melted piñon pitch, which gives the surface a dark amber gloss. Painted decoration is absent; the only ornament is a fillet of clay around the neck, itself interrupted by a break analogous to the weaver's pathway. Basketry survives almost entirely in the *tsʼaaʼ*, the coiled sumac "wedding basket" with a stepped red-and-black band, a deliberate gap in the design aligned with the end of the coil, and a role in nearly every ceremony as a container for cornmeal and yucca suds. Silversmithing, learned from Mexican *plateros* around the 1850s, is the great metal tradition — sandcast and stamped, with dies cut from files. Wooden objects include the *bįįh* weaving batten, the upright loom frame itself, and the cradleboard.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Diné silver-and-turquoise work is the most recognizable Native American jewelry in the world, and its forms are nameable. The *squash blossom necklace* strings hollow silver beads interrupted by fluted blossom units — derived from the Spanish-Mexican pomegranate — and closes at the bottom in the *naja*, a crescent pendant of Moorish origin transmitted through Spanish horse bridles. The *concha belt* is a row of oval stamped or repoussé silver plates, originally slotted for a leather strap, later joined by openwork butterfly spacers. The *ketoh* is a leather bowguard faced with a cast silver plaque — archery equipment converted into formal male ornament. *Navajo Pearls* are hand-formed, hand-stamped hollow silver beads, each seamed and polished, strung plain. Turquoise from Cerrillos, Lone Mountain and Kingman is set in serrated or scalloped bezels; cluster, row and channel-inlay work distinguishes Diné setting from Zuni lapidary and Hopi overlay. Jewelry functions as wealth, as ceremonial dress, and as pawn — the trading-post pawn system made silver a working currency.

## Music & performance

Diné music is overwhelmingly vocal and overwhelmingly ceremonial. The core repertoire is the *hatááł* — a "sing," a ceremonial chantway of hundreds of fixed songs performed in prescribed order across two, five or nine nights by a *hataałii* (singer, often glossed "medicine man"), with error carrying real consequence. Named chantways include *Blessingway* (*hózhǫ́ǫ́jí*, the backbone rite of blessing and protection), *Nightway* (*Tłʼééʼjí*, the nine-night winter healing ceremony), *Enemyway* (*Anaʼí Ndááʼ*, for those who have contacted death or foreigners, including returning soldiers), and *Mountainway*. Accompaniment is minimal and deliberately so: a water drum of hide stretched over a pottery or hide vessel, gourd rattles, hoof rattles, notched-stick rasps, and the bullroarer used in Nightway. The *tsiiʼédoʼii* flute exists but is peripheral. Secular music includes *Circle Dance* and *Skip Dance* songs sung at Enemyway with a driving, falsetto-topped unison line, and *Diné* versions of the intertribal powwow and "49" song. Hastiin Klah, the singer who co-founded the Wheelwright Museum of the American Indian in Santa Fe in 1937 with Mary Cabot Wheelwright, allowed recordings and sandpainting tapestries to be made precisely because he expected the chantways to be lost. That archive of sound recordings and manuscripts remains a primary documentation project. Country, blues, punk and hip-hop have Diné-language practitioners; the *Navajo Nation Band* and gospel-in-Diné are both long established.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is a component of ceremony rather than an independent art. The public climax of *Nightway* is the *Yeibichai*, danced on the final night by teams of masked men impersonating the *Yeʼii*, the Holy People: Talking God (*Hááshchʼééłtiʼí*) leading with a call, paired male and female masked dancers in strict lines, motion built on a stamping, precisely synchronized step, with the *Water Sprinkler* clown moving against the pattern. Teams compete informally in precision through the night. Masks are sacred objects with their own maintenance requirements. *Fire Dance*, the final night of *Mountainway*, features bundles of burning cedar bark carried against bare skin. At *Enemyway* the *Circle Dance* and the partner-choice *Skip Dance* are openly social — the one context in which unrelated young men and women dance together, with a girl's choice of partner and the small payment she can demand the source of much of its humor. Diné culture has no masked theatre or puppet tradition; the dramatic content is carried inside ritual, where the dancer is not representing a Holy Person but temporarily is one.

## Festivals & rituals

The ceremonial calendar is keyed to season rather than date: the great chantways — *Nightway*, *Mountainway*, the nine-night rites requiring masks and sandpainting — are held only after the first killing frost and before the spring thaw, when snakes are underground and thunder silent. *Blessingway* can be held any time. Contemporary public gatherings follow the Anglo calendar: the **Navajo Nation Fair** at Window Rock each September, the largest, with rodeo, song-and-dance, agricultural and rug competition, and the Miss Navajo Nation pageant — which still requires contestants to butcher a sheep and answer in *Diné bizaad*; the **Western Navajo Fair** at Tuba City in October; **Shiprock Northern Navajo Nation Fair** in early October, which retains a Nightway at its core.

Life-cycle rites are distinct and living. A baby's first laugh obliges the person who caused it to host a *First Laugh ceremony* (*Chʼídeedlóóh*), distributing rock salt and food. A girl's first menstruation triggers the *Kinaaldá*, a four-day Blessingway-based rite held in a hogan: she is dressed in *biil* and full jewelry, her hair washed in yucca suds and bound in the *tsiiyeel*, molded by an older female relative into the form of Changing Woman, and she runs eastward each dawn, farther each day. A giant corn cake, the *ʼalkaan*, is baked overnight in a pit lined with cornhusks and cut for the whole community at dawn. Weddings are held in a hogan, the couple eating white corn mush from the *tsʼaaʼ* wedding basket. Death is avoided rather than ritualized — traditional practice minimized contact with the *chʼį́įdii*, the residue of the dead, and a hogan in which someone died was abandoned or destroyed.

## Foodways

Farming came from the Pueblos and the crops are the "Three Sisters" — corn, beans and squash — with corn the ritual center: *white corn* for the wedding ceremony and male ceremonial use, *yellow corn* female, and *corn pollen* (*tádídíín*) carried in a pouch and used as the essential offering and blessing medium in every rite. Corn foods are elaborate: *ʼalkaan* (the pit-baked ceremonial corn cake, sweetened with sprouted-wheat flour), *kneel-down bread* (*nitsidigoʼí*, fresh corn ground on a metate, wrapped in its own husks and baked in an earth pit), *blue corn mush* (*tanaashgiizh*, stirred with juniper ash for its alkali and its mineral tang), and *ʼatoo* corn stew. Mutton is the other pillar — sheep arrived with the Spanish and became simultaneously food, currency and a measure of family standing, which is exactly why the Livestock Reduction from 1933 was experienced as an attack on the culture and not merely the economy. *Mutton stew* with potatoes and hominy, grilled ribs, and *ach'íí'* (small intestine wrapped around fat and roasted) are the standards, served with *fry bread* or the thin, dinner-plate *Navajo tortilla*. The Navajo taco — fry bread under beans, mutton or beef, chile and lettuce — is a fair-food institution and a post-Bosque-Redondo food, built from government-issue flour, lard and salt. Gathered foods persist: piñon nuts, yucca fruit, wild spinach, sumac berries, juniper ash, and *Navajo tea* (*Thelesperma*, greenthread) boiled to a red-gold infusion.

## Oral tradition & literature

The foundational narrative is the *Diné Bahaneʼ*, the emergence account: passage upward through three worlds — the First or Dark World where the four *Diyin Diné*, First Man and First Woman came into being; the Second or Blue World of the Swallow Chief *Táshchózhii*, from which the beings were expelled for offending him; the Third or Yellow World where the four sacred mountains stood and a great flood forced another ascent — into this Fourth or *Glittering World*, where death, the seasons, moon, stars and sun came into existence. The cycle continues with Changing Woman (*Asdzą́ą́ Nádleehé*), who gave the original four clans, and with the Hero Twins, Monster Slayer and Born for Water, who cleared the world of monsters. These are not folktales but the libretto of ceremony: chantway songs are episodes of it, and *Coyote* (*Mąʼii*) stories — the trickster cycle, told only in winter — occupy the comic register. Written Diné literature is substantial and self-consciously grounded in this material: Luci Tapahonso, Rex Lee Jim, Laura Tohe, Sherwin Bitsui, Esther Belin. Diné College and the Navajo Language Academy sustain a written orthography whose existence traces back, ironically, to the same missionary and boarding-school linguistics that punished children for speaking the language.

## Language & religion

*Diné bizaad* is Southern Athabaskan (Na-Dené), with two mutually intelligible geographic dialects and a close relationship to the Apachean languages; it is tonal, has four vowel qualities with nasal and length contrasts, and a verb morphology built on classificatory stems. A Latin-based orthography with hooks and acute accents dates from the 1930s. It is the most widely spoken Indigenous language north of Mexico yet is declining in child transmission — an outcome of the English-only boarding schools at Fort Defiance and elsewhere from 1870 onward, which punished the language and, after 1945, took six times as many children as before the war. *Navajo Sign Language*, a dialect or daughter of Plains Sign Talk, is also used. The ceremonial system turns on *hózhǫ́*: beauty, order and balance, which illness disrupts and the chantways restore. Alongside it the Native American Church (peyote), Mormonism and various Protestant denominations are all substantially represented, frequently in the same family.
