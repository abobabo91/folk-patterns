## Overview

The Pueblo peoples, also referred to as Puebloans, are Native Americans of the Southwestern United States who share agricultural, material, and religious practices. Pueblo communities are concentrated especially in New Mexico and Arizona, with others in Texas and along the Rio Grande and Colorado rivers and their tributaries. Exact numbers are unknown; in the 21st century, some 75,000 Pueblo people live predominantly in New Mexico and Arizona. Pueblo peoples speak languages from four different families: Keresan, Tanoan, Uto-Aztecan, and Zuni. Their communities differ in kinship systems, agricultural practices, languages, and religious organization, while all cultivate varieties of corn. Pueblo culture is significant in folk-cultural terms for its continuity of farming, architecture, pottery, textiles, ritual life, dances, and community traditions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Woven cloth** — Pueblo people have woven cloth and used twined and embroidered textiles, natural fibers, and animal hide in cloth-making. The sources describe woven clothing as laborious and time-consuming, so everyday clothing for work around the villages was sparer.

**Turkey feather blankets** — By the 13th century, Pueblo people crafted turkey feather blankets for warmth.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday clothing was adapted to work around the villages. The sources state that men often wore breechcloths, while everyday clothing was generally sparer because woven clothing required substantial labor and time. Pueblo people also made cloth from natural fibers and animal hide, using weaving, twining, and embroidery. They do, however, distinguish ordinary clothing from ritual objects such as prayer sticks and from turkey feather blankets, which were crafted for warmth by the 13th century.

### Architecture

Pueblo architecture includes multistory villages and apartment-like complexes made from adobe, stone, earth, wooden beams, and other local materials. Earlier Ancestral Puebloan homes and villages included pit-houses, while later structures consisted of connected rectangular rooms arranged in apartment-like buildings. By about 1050, Pueblo people had developed planned villages with large terraced buildings containing many rooms. These settlements were often placed on ledges of massive rock, flat summits, or steep-sided mesas, locations that provided protection from raiding parties.

Ancestral Puebloan villages were also built along cliff walls, especially during the Pueblo II and Pueblo III eras, from about 900 to 1350 CE. Their buildings surrounded open plazas and viewsheds and could be multistoried and multipurpose. Pueblo Bonito in Chaco Canyon, New Mexico, contained around 700 rooms in five stories and may have housed as many as 1000 persons.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pueblo communities have different traditions of making and decorating pottery artifacts. Present-day archaeologists date Pueblo pottery to the early centuries of the Common Era. The sources specifically identify ceramic storyteller figurines associated with Cochiti Pueblo, as well as drums, turquoise work associated with Kewa Pueblo, and prayer sticks decorated with beads, fur, and feathers.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention turquoise work associated with Kewa Pueblo and beads used to decorate ritual prayer sticks.

## Music & performance

Pueblo religious ceremonies commonly feature traditional dances held outdoors in large common areas and courtyards, accompanied by singing and drumming. Since time immemorial, Pueblo communities have celebrated seasonal cycles through prayer, song, and dance. These performances connect participants with ancestors, community, and traditions and honor gifts from the Creator.

The sources mention drums as objects associated with Cochiti Pueblo. Traditional dances may be open to non-Pueblo people, unlike private ceremonies conducted inside kivas. Strict rules of conduct apply to those attending public traditional dances, including no clapping and no walking across the dance area or between dancers, singers, or drummers.

## Dance & theatre

Traditional dances are an important part of Pueblo ceremonial life. They are usually held outdoors in large common areas and courtyards and are accompanied by singing and drumming. Unlike kiva ceremonies, traditional dances may be open to non-Pueblo people, although attendance involves rules of conduct. The sources state that public dances are forms of prayer and that visitors were traditionally offered a meal afterward in a Pueblo home; because of outside tourism since the late 20th century, such meals are now open to outsiders by personal invitation only.

## Festivals & rituals

Pueblo communities hold annual sacred ceremonies, some of which are open to the public. Seasonal cycles are marked through prayer, song, and dance. Public observances may also include Roman Catholic Mass and processions on a Pueblo’s feast day. Spanish missionaries assigned feast days to Roman Catholic patron saints, arranging them to coincide with existing traditional ceremonies. Some Pueblos also hold sacred ceremonies around Christmas and other Christian holidays.

Private sacred ceremonies take place inside kivas, and only tribal members may participate according to rules specific to each Pueblo’s religion. Kivas are also connected with sacred ceremonies among the Hopi, Zuni, Keres, and Jemez. The sources describe prayer materials, including ground-up maize or white cornmeal. A person might bless a son, land, or town by sprinkling maize meal while uttering a blessing. Pueblo peoples also used prayer sticks, or “talking sticks,” decorated with beads, fur, and feathers.

The sources discuss the 1680 Pueblo Revolt as a major historical and religious event. Spanish attempts to suppress Pueblo religion and replace it with Catholicism contributed to resistance. After the Spanish re-conquest, Pueblo communities maintained their ceremonial life out of Spanish view while adopting a veneer of Roman Catholicism.

## Foodways

Corn, or maize, is a primary staple food for Pueblo peoples, and all Pueblo communities cultivate varieties of corn. Pueblo prayer commonly used ground-up maize, especially white cornmeal, as a sacred substance. Pueblo agriculture also includes fruit growing, bean production, and livestock ranching with cattle and sheep. Blue Lake served as the main water supply supporting the agricultural activities of the Taos community.

The sources state that the Western or Desert Pueblos of Zuni and Hopi specialize in dry farming, while the Eastern or River Pueblos are irrigation farmers. Irrigation canals were important in the broader history of the Southwest, particularly among the Hohokam culture. The sources also describe Pueblo communities as horticulturalists and mention fruits and vegetables in historical accounts.

No named Puebloan dishes, cooking methods, beverages, dietary rules, or ceremonial meals are identified. The sources state that visitors to public dances were traditionally offered a meal in a Pueblo home.

## Oral tradition & literature

Pueblo religious traditions include stories about the relationships among people and nature, including plants and animals. Spider Grandmother and kachina spirits appear prominently in some myths. Pueblo creation stories differ among cultural groupings: the Hopi, Zuni, Keres, and Jemez describe the emergence of humans from underground, while Tanoan-speaking Pueblos other than Jemez describe the emergence of people from underwater.

Katsina spirits were important in Pueblo belief during the 16th century. They were understood as supernatural beings representing Pueblo ancestors. They lived for part of the year in the underworld with the gods and the rest of the year with their descendants on earth. Katsinas could take the form of clouds, bring rain to agricultural fields, heal disease, and cause disease.

The sources identify Tewa cultural practices, rituals, myths, and knowledge as subjects of Alfonso Ortiz’s research. His book *The Tewa World: Space, Time, Being and Becoming in a Pueblo Society* was published in 1969. Jemez Pueblo historian Joe Sando became the first Pueblo historian to publish several books about Pueblo history in the 1990s.

## Language & religion

Pueblo peoples speak languages from four distinct language families. Keresan includes Western and Eastern Keres and is spoken at Acoma, Laguna, Santa Ana, Zia, Cochiti, Kewa, and San Felipe. Tanoan includes Tewa, Towa, and Tiwa. Tewa is spoken at Ohkay Owingeh, San Ildefonso, Santa Clara, Tesuque, Nambé, and Pojoaque; Towa is spoken at Jemez; Northern Tiwa is spoken at Taos and Picuris; and Southern Tiwa is spoken at Sandia and Isleta. Hopi belongs to Uto-Aztecan, while Zuni is a language isolate. English now functions as the regional lingua franca.

Pueblo religion includes sacred ceremonies, kivas, prayer, songs, dances, maize offerings, prayer sticks, and beliefs concerning ancestors, clouds, rain, and agricultural fertility. Pueblo communities also developed syncretic Pueblo Christianity. Some public observances include Roman Catholic Mass and processions, while private ceremonies remain restricted according to each Pueblo’s religious rules.
