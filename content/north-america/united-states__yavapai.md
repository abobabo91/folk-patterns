## Overview

The Yavapai are a Native American tribe in Arizona whose historical territory covered about 10 million acres in west-central Arizona, between the San Francisco Peaks, the Pinaleno Mountains, the Mazatzal Mountains, the Colorado River, and almost to the Gila River and the Salt River. Today Yavapai people are enrolled in the Fort McDowell Yavapai Nation, the Yavapai-Apache Nation of the Camp Verde Indian Reservation, and the Yavapai-Prescott Indian Tribe. Their population was about 1,550 in 1992; the Fort McDowell tribal community consists of 900 members, and the Yavapai-Prescott Tribe consists of 159 official members. Yavapai is an Upland Yuman language with four dialects. Their culture includes hunter-gatherer foodways, brush-shelter architecture, masked dances, bird singing and dancing, and oral traditions connected with creation and place.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name painted crosses, painted designs on faces, and various designs painted on gourds.

### Clothing & dress

They state that Yavapai moccasins were rounded, while Apache moccasins had pointed toes. Yavapai people often acquired tattoos, whereas the Apache seldom used tattoos. The sources also mention painted designs on faces, but give no further description of clothing, head coverings, belts, hair practices, or ceremonial garments.

### Architecture

Yavapai brush shelters were called *Wa'm bu nya:va* (*Wom-boo-nya-va*). In summer, people built simple lean-tos without walls. During winter, they constructed closed huts called *uwas* from ocotillo branches or other wood, covering them with animal skins, grasses, bark, and/or dirt. In the Colorado River area, the Tolkepaya built *Uwađ a'mađva*, rectangular huts with dirt piled against the sides for insulation and flat roofs. Yavapai people also sought shelter in caves or abandoned pueblos during cold weather. At Camp Verde, the Yavapai constructed irrigation systems, including a five-mile (8 km) ditch, which supported harvests and helped the community become relatively self-sufficient.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that, after contact with other tribes, Yavapai people adopted some metal tools and weaponry. The sources also mention baskets, animal skins, and agave as items connected with exchange between the Tolkepaya and Quechan groups.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They state that Yavapai people often acquired tattoos and created different painted designs on faces.

## Music & performance

Yavapai musical traditions include Bird Singing and Dancing, a practice originally associated with the Mojave people of the Colorado River region and adopted by modern Yavapai culture. The sources state that the bird songs tell a story about the creation of the Yuman people and how they came to be. An entire night is needed to sing the whole cycle, from sundown to sunup. Bird songs are accompanied by a gourd, usually painted with various designs and fitted with a handle made of cottonwood. Modern bird singing and dancing is used for mourning, celebration, and social purposes.

The sources also describe singing in the Apache Sunrise Dance Ceremony. A medicine man is joined by other tribal members in singing a series of songs, up to 32, believed to have first been sung by Changing Woman. The Yavapai language has been recorded in work relating to phonology, syntax, and grammar. A Yavapai language program for adults supports learning the language and passing it to future generations. Poetry and stories have been published in Yavapai, including works accompanied by English translations.

## Dance & theatre

The Yavapai practiced the Mountain Spirit Dance, War Dances, Victory Dances, and Social Dances. The Mountain Spirit Dance was a masked dance used for guidance or healing a sick person. Masked dancers represented Mountain Spirits associated with Four Peaks, McDowell Mountains, Red Mountain near Fort McDowell, Mingus Mountain near Camp Verde, and Granite Mountain near present-day Prescott. The sources also connect the Mountain Spirits with the caves of Montezuma Castle and Montezuma Well in the Verde Valley.

Yavapai people participate in the Apache Sunrise Dance and in the Bird Singing and Dancing of the Mojave people. The sources describe no Yavapai theatre, dramatic genre, or puppet tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document the Sunrise Dance Ceremony, which may have come to the Yavapai through the Apache. It is a four-day rite of transition for young Apache girls and typically takes place from March through October. The ceremony is associated with Changing Woman, whose power is transferred to the pubescent girl through songs sung by the medicine man. Apache and Yavapai often intermarried and adopted elements of each other’s cultures, and both tribes reside on the Camp Verde and Fort McDowell reservations.

Yavapai dances also serve mourning, celebration, social, guidance, and healing purposes. The sources describe the Mountain Spirit Dance as a healing practice and describe Bird Singing and Dancing as being used for mourning, celebration, and social purposes.

## Foodways

Before being confined to reservations, the Yavapai were mainly hunter-gatherers who followed annual movements of edible plants and game animals. Some communities supplemented this diet with small-scale cultivation of the “three sisters”: maize, squash, and beans. The Tolkepaya practiced agriculture more than other Yavapai because their territory was less supportive of food gathering. They traded animal skins, baskets, and agave to Quechan groups for food.

Gathered plant foods included walnuts, saguaro fruits, juniper berries, acorns, sunflower seeds, manzanita berries and apples, hackberries, the bulbs of the Quamash, and the greens of Lamb’s quarters, Scrophularia, and Lupinus plants. Agave was the most crucial harvest because it was the only plant food available from late fall through early spring. Agave hearts were roasted in stone-lined pits and stored for later use.

Animals hunted included deer, rabbit, jackrabbit, quail, and woodrat. Most Yavapai groups avoided fish and water-borne birds, although some Tolkepaya began eating fish after contact with Quechan neighbors.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Yavapai creation story explains that Yavapai people originated “in the beginning,” or “many years ago,” when either a tree or a maize plant sprouted from the ground in what is now Montezuma Well and brought the Yavapai into the world. Pai oral history describes a dispute between Pai and Yavapai that began with a “mudball fight between children”; scholars believe the split occurred around 1750.

Mohave elders state that the bird songs tell the creation story of the Yuman people and how they came to be. Yavapai stories appear in *Spirit Mountain: An Anthology of Yuman Story and Song*. Poetry and stories have been published in Yavapai with English translations, and an effort exists to revitalize the language. Alan Shaterian published a dictionary of Northeastern Yavapai, while Pamela Munro is working on a dictionary and grammar for Tolkepaya.

## Language & religion

Yavapai is an Upland Yuman language spoken by Yavapai people in central and western Arizona. It has four dialects: Kwevkepaya, Wipukpaya, Tolkepaya, and Yavepe. Yavapai was once spoken across much of north-central and western Arizona and is now mostly spoken on the Yavapai reservations at Fort McDowell, the Verde Valley, and Prescott. It is a subject-verb-object language with short, mid, and long vowels in stressed syllables and high-level and falling tones on stressed syllables.

Yavapai people adopted elements of Christianity in a syncretic way after contact with Europeans. Their creation story and Mountain Spirit traditions connect cultural practice with particular places, including Montezuma Well, Montezuma Castle, Four Peaks, and the Verde Valley.
