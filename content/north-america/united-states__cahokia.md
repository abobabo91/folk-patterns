## Overview

The Cahokia, also known as *Kahokiaki* in Miami-Illinois, were an Algonquian-speaking Native American tribe and a member of the Illinois Confederation. Their territory lay in what is now the Midwestern United States, including areas later organized as Illinois, Iowa, Missouri, and Arkansas. In the 17th century, the Cahokia lived near the precontact earthwork complex known as the Cahokia Mounds, although they were not related to the residents who built it. In 1673, Cahokia and Tamaroa communities occupied western Illinois and eastern Missouri. The total population of the Illinois Confederation was estimated at around 10,000. The Cahokia later declined, merged with neighboring peoples, and continued through the federally recognized Peoria Tribe of Indians of Oklahoma.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Cahokia communities established villages and used built structures associated with French missions. In 1699, French missionaries built the Tamaroa/Cahokia mission. In 1735, the Cahokia established a new village on Monks Mound, and the French built the River L’Abbė mission on its first terrace within the city complex.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Cahokia used waterways for travel and seasonal hunting camps. The sources also state that they adopted some European practices, including the use of iron equipment and the domestication of hogs. No further Cahokia ceramics, metalwork, wooden objects, tools, or household goods are described.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that French missionaries built missions to convert the Cahokia people and that Christianization contributed to cultural changes that disrupted their society.

## Foodways

Cahokia society centered on the cultivation of maize and hunting game on the prairie. The people hunted bison, buffalo, deer, elk, and waterfowl, and also practiced small-scale agriculture and gardening. During times of scarcity, smaller groups divided for hunting and gathering, including the collection of wild plants. Controlled burns were used to confuse herds of game. The Cahokia traveled on the Illinois River and used waterways to establish camps for seasonal hunts.

## Language & religion

The Cahokia were Algonquian-speaking, and the name *Kahokiaki* is identified as Miami-Illinois. French missionaries built missions in an effort to convert the Cahokia people. Christianization is identified as one of the cultural changes that disrupted Cahokia society. The surviving cultural traditions of the Cahokia continue through the federally recognized Peoria Tribe of Indians of Oklahoma.
