## Overview

The Umatilla are a Sahaptin-speaking Native American tribe traditionally associated with the Columbia Plateau of the northwestern United States, along the Umatilla and Columbia rivers. They are called *Imatalamłáma*, while an individual Umatilla person is called *Imatalamłá*. Today, the Umatilla share land and governmental structures with the Cayuse and Walla Walla tribes as the federally recognized Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation. Their reservation is near Pendleton, Oregon, and the Blue Mountains. The Umatilla language belongs to the Sahaptin division of the Penutian language family and is currently spoken by a few dozen elders and some adults. The sources document language, territorial history, and revitalization more fully than material or ceremonial culture.

## Music & performance

 They do identify language-preservation activities connected with the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation, including online video resources, a Flash Story Camp held by First Nations Development in collaboration with Tamastslikt's Language Enhancement Program and Education Department, and the Wíyat'ish Naknúwit “For the Future” Language Project.

## Dance & theatre

 They identify Acosia Red Elk as a World Champion Jingle Dress Dancer, yoga instructor, and notable Umatilla, but provide no description of Jingle Dress Dance practice.

## Festivals & rituals

 The historical sources state that in 1855 inland Sahaptin-speaking nations were forced to surrender their historic homelands under treaty to the United States government in exchange for territorial set-asides on reservations.

## Foodways

 The language sources include the lexical example *Wáylatpu* or *Wáylatpuuma*, glossed as “Ryegrass People” or “Cayuse People,” but this is an ethnonym rather than a documented foodway.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They do document contemporary language preservation. As of 2013, about 50 first-language speakers of Umatilla were reported. The language was taught at Nixyaawii Community School, which had offered Umatilla, Walla Walla, and Nez Perce language classes for the previous decade. A Cay-Uma-Wa Head Start program was being developed, and the Tamaluut immersion school was described as a new immersion program for three- to five-year-olds. The Wíyat'ish Naknúwit “For the Future” Language Project trained speakers through a Master-Apprentice program. The Umatilla Dictionary was published in 2014.

## Language & religion

Umatilla, also called *Tamalúut* or *Imatalamłaamí Sɨ́nwit*, is described as a variety of Southern Sahaptin within the Sahaptian subfamily of the Plateau Penutian group. It was spoken during late aboriginal times along the Columbia River and is also called Columbia River Sahaptin. A Umatilla person is called *imatalamłá*, and the Umatilla people are called *imatalamłáma*; the Nez Perce term is *hiyówatalampoo*. The language has free word order and a complex case-marking system. Nouns, adjectives, and pronouns inflect for singular, dual, and plural number and for case. The sources describe no Umatilla religious system, denomination, syncretic practice, script, or named spiritual tradition.

Language revitalization includes instruction at Nixyaawii Community School, six full-time language instructors in the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation, the Tamaluut immersion school, online video resources, and the Umatilla Dictionary. In 2015, Umatilla instruction was scheduled to be given at the high-school level. The dictionary records words, pronunciations, phrases, grammar, comparative information, and known words, phrases, and constructions of the language.
