## Overview

Laguna is a Pueblo community in west-central New Mexico. Its history includes farming, pottery, Keresan language, Pueblo religious traditions, and a historic Spanish mission. Laguna pottery uses local clay, natural pigments, geometric designs, and outdoor pit firing. The community also maintains ties to sacred ceremonies, Feast Days, and Roman Catholic traditions.

## Material culture

Laguna material culture is best known for hand-coiled pottery, painted vessels, mission architecture, and a small number of documented steel ornaments.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources do not document a distinct Laguna textile tradition, but they describe strong geometric and natural motifs in pottery.

- **Geometric pottery patterns** (*geometric patterns*) — Laguna vessels used precise geometric polychrome patterns.
- **Interlocking line designs** (*interlocking fine lines*) — Some pottery used interlocking fine lines.
- **Cloud designs** (*cloud motifs*) — Cloud motifs appeared on Laguna vessels.
- **Animal designs** (*animal motifs*) — Animal motifs appeared on Laguna vessels.

### Architecture

Mission San José de la Laguna is the best-documented Laguna building and combines Spanish Catholic and Pueblo features.

- **Mission San José de la Laguna** — This church was built between 1699 and 1701 and has Pueblo-style murals, timber roofing, and a historic altar screen.
- **St. Joseph Church** — St. Joseph Church is another name for Mission San José de la Laguna.
- **Reredos** — The sanctuary contains a hide-and-wood altar screen made between 1800 and 1808.
- **Kiva** — Traditional Keresan religious structures include kivas.
- **Old Laguna** — Old Laguna is the old pueblo mentioned by the sources.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Laguna craftwork centers on hand-coiled pottery made with local materials and outdoor pit firing.

- **Hand-coiled pottery** (*hand-coiling*) — Laguna pottery uses hand-coiling and indigenous clay sources.
- **Natural pigments** (*natural mineral and vegetal pigments*) — Potters restored natural mineral and vegetal pigments for their vessels.
- **Pit-fired pottery** (*outdoor pit firing*) — Outdoor pit firing was reintroduced in Laguna pottery production.
- **Steel necklace** — A museum record identifies a steel necklace associated with Laguna material culture.
- **Steel bracelet** — A museum record identifies a steel bracelet associated with Laguna material culture.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources document a steel necklace with squash blossoms and a steel bracelet with a mountain design.

- **Squash-blossom necklace** (*steel necklace*) — The necklace has a flexible band, hooks at both ends, and 19 steel squash-blossom forms.
- **Mountain-line bracelet** (*steel bracelet*) — The bracelet has two silver bands on the front divided by a zig-zag mountain line.

## Festivals & rituals

Laguna residents return for sacred ceremonies and Feast Days, and some communities celebrate Grab Day.

- **Sacred ceremonies** — Laguna residents of the Richmond boxcar community returned to New Mexico for sacred ceremonies.
- **Annual Feast Days** (*Feast Days*) — The same community returned to New Mexico for annual Feast Days.
- **Grab Day** — On Grab Day, candies and gifts were thrown from boxcar roofs to village children.
- **Keresan altar tradition** (*traditional altars*) — Laguna religious life includes traditional altars and Keresan religious structures.

## Foodways

The sources mention farming, waterfowl, and community gardens.

- **Rio San José farming** (*farming*) — Laguna people historically farmed along the Rio San José.
- **Waterfowl resources** (*waterfowl*) — The historic lake supported ducks, geese, and swans.
- **Community vegetable gardens** — The Richmond boxcar community maintained community vegetable gardens.

## Oral tradition & literature

Laguna oral history supports knowledge of long habitation, but the sources do not preserve named stories or literary genres.

- **Pueblo cosmological histories** (*Pueblo cosmological oral histories*) — The mission’s celestial symbols are linked to traditional Pueblo cosmological oral histories.
- **Richmond boxcar archives** (*Bancroft Library archives*) — The material and oral histories of the Richmond boxcar community are preserved in UC Berkeley’s Bancroft Library archives.
- **Ancestral pottery teaching** (*ancestral techniques*) — Evelyn Cheromiah transmitted ancestral pottery techniques to her daughters and grandchildren.

## Language & religion

Laguna traditionally speaks Western Keresan, and its religious life combines Keresan traditions with historic Roman Catholic ties.

- **Western Keresan** — Laguna traditionally speaks Western Keresan, a variety of Keres.
- **Kawaika** — Kawaika is another name for Laguna in Western Keres.
- **Kʼawaika** — Kʼawaika is the language’s name for Laguna in one spelling system.
- **Keres** — Keres is described as a Native American language and as either a small language family or a language isolate.
- **Sacred spoken language** (*sacred language*) — Keresan religious beliefs treat Keres as a sacred language that must exist only in spoken form.
