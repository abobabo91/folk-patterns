## Overview

The Koyukon, also called Dinaa or Denaa, are an Alaska Native Athabascan people whose traditional territory lies along the Koyukuk and Yukon rivers in the western interior of Alaska. Their Denaakk'e name is Tl’eeyegge Hut’aane. The Koyukon belong to the Athabascan-speaking ethnolinguistic group, within the larger Na-Dené language family. Archaeological evidence indicates that they have inhabited their region for at least 1,000 years, with cultural roots extending thousands of years earlier. They traditionally subsisted through hunting and trapping, and many Koyukon continue to live in a similar manner. In 2007, the total Koyukon ethnic population was reported as 2,300, while approximately 300 people spoke the Koyukon language.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources mention cloth apparel as an item that had reached Koyukon territory through trade with coastal Eskimos before the arrival of the Russians in 1838.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources record iron pots, glass beads, cloth apparel, and tobacco among the items that had reached the Koyukon through trade with coastal Eskimos before the Russians entered their territory.

## Music & performance

The Koyukon language was documented by Jules Jetté, a French Canadian Jesuit missionary who began recording the language and culture of the Koyukon people in 1898. His notes included material on Koyukon culture, beliefs, and language. The sources also state that traditional stories recorded by Catherine Attla were published in 1983 by the University of Alaska Fairbanks. No specific musical performance tradition is identified.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that Jules Jetté recorded information about Koyukon beliefs and culture, but the supplied material does not specify particular rituals. An epidemic of smallpox preceded the arrival of the first Europeans in Koyukon territory, and subsequent European infectious diseases drastically reduced the population.

## Foodways

Koyukon subsistence traditionally centered on hunting and trapping along the Koyukuk and Yukon rivers. The sources state that the Koyukon freeze lingonberries for winter use. No other specific dishes, staple crops, cooking methods, beverages, dietary rules, or ceremonial foods are documented in the supplied material.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Koyukon Athabaskan Dictionary is described as more than a conventional dictionary: it also records Koyukon culture and traditions. Eliza Jones, a Koyukon, worked from Jules Jetté's notes and consulted Koyukon tribal elders in preparing the work. It was edited by James Kari and published in 2000 by the Alaska Native Language Center at the University of Alaska Fairbanks. The book includes traditional stories recorded by Catherine Attla and published in 1983 by the University of Alaska Fairbanks.

## Language & religion

Koyukon, also called Denaakk'e, is an Athabascan language spoken along the Koyukuk and middle Yukon rivers in western interior Alaska. In 2007, it had approximately 300 speakers, generally older adults who were bilingual in English. As of 1978, three dialects were identified: Lower, Central, and Upper Koyukon. Lower Koyukon was spoken in Kaltag and Nulato; Central Koyukon in Galena, Ruby, Koyukuk, part of Tanana, Huslia, Hughes, and Allakaket; and Upper Koyukon in Stevens Village, Rampart, and part of Tanana. In 2012, Denaakk'e classes were held at Yukon-Koyukuk School District offices in Fairbanks and Huslia. The sources mention Koyukon beliefs.
