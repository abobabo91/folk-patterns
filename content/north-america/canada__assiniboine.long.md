## Overview

The Assiniboine — *Nakoda* (also *Nakota*, *Nakona*) in their own language, and *Hohe* to others — are an Indigenous people of the Northern Plains, living today chiefly in Saskatchewan with communities in Alberta and southwestern Manitoba, and in northern Montana and western North Dakota. At the height of their power they held a territory stretching from the North Saskatchewan River south to the Missouri. Their population fell from roughly 10,000 in the late 18th century to about 2,600 by 1890 under smallpox and other introduced epidemics; the 2000 US census recorded 3,946 tribal citizens, and the Fort Peck and Fort Belknap communities today enroll some 11,786 and 5,426 members respectively. Their language belongs to the Mississippi Valley branch of Siouan, within the Western Siouan family. In folk-culture terms they matter as a bison-hunting, horse-mounted people who were also the indispensable middlemen of the northern fur trade — a position legible in the trade wool, glass beads and metal in their surviving objects.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources behind this profile — two Wikipedia articles and the catalogue records of the twelve objects shown here — describe materials and object classes rather than named weaving or embroidery traditions, so the entries below are limited to what those records document.

**Hide-work** — Women processed and cured the skins of the game the men hunted, and the hides, tendons and horns went into clothing, bedding, tools and cord; every part of the animal was used. Women made all the clothing and instruments, and the photographs here show the results worn: a fringed hide tunic on George Rustler, a plain hide tunic on Rattlesnake, moccasins on Paul Bigstone and his wife.

**Glass-bead decoration on tanned skin** — Glass beads were among the standard goods the Assiniboine took from the Hudson's Bay Company, the North West Company and, south of the border, the American Fur Company and Rocky Mountain Fur Company, in exchange for beaver furs and bison hides. The Metropolitan Museum's *tobacco bag* of 1860–70 combines native-tanned skin, glass and wool; Irene Rock, photographed beside a wigwam, wears a beaded chest ornament over a cloth dress.

**Pigmented rawhide and sinew assembly** — The Met's **club (skullcracker)** of 1850–60 is built from stone, wood, rawhide, sinew, pigment and feathers: a composite weapon in which binding and colouring are part of the finish.

### Clothing & dress

The photographic record for the Assiniboine is unusually dense in the second half of the 19th century, and it shows dress in transition. Men appear in wool coats over an inner garment, with studded waist belts and belts carrying a knife in a sheath — Black Raven, Big Elk, Jumping Sheep and Muddy are each photographed this way; Jumping Sheep wears a second studded belt over his clothing and a waist belt with bullets. Wool coats, wool leggings and wool blankets, including the Hudson's Bay point blanket, came from the fur trade, and Rattlesnake is photographed on horseback in a hide tunic with a blanket and leggings. Older and newer forms sit side by side: Paul Bigstone wears a shirt, trousers, a lower over-garment, a cloth tied at the neck, a belt with a pouch and moccasins, while his wife wears a dress, a shawl and moccasins. Headgear and hair are treated as part of dress — a hat on George Rustler, a head scarf on Irene Rock, two feathers in Rattlesnake's hair, and "hair and neck-ornament" recorded together for Black Raven and Big Elk. A group portrait of Assiniboine men shows an assortment of head-gear, neck-ornaments, garments and footwear.

### Architecture

The Assiniboine were semi-nomadic, following the plains bison herds through the warmer months, and their dwelling was the tipi — *Wiʼį́kceya tíbi* or *įkcéwąga*. The smallest social unit, the *Tiwáhe* or nuclear family, normally occupied one tipi or two neighbouring ones; several *Tiʼóšpaye* (local groups) of one or more extended families made up a band, and the *Nakoda Oyadebi* (Assiniboine Nation) was divided into as many as 40 such bands. Two of the British Museum photographs document the camp as a built environment: a group portrait with tipis and a horse behind the sitters, and a stereoscope view at Fort Union of two wooden buildings and two tipis standing together in a clearing with people and horses beyond — the trading post and the camp in one frame. A third shows Irene Rock standing beside a wigwam with a saddled horse tied to its side. The sources do not describe tipi construction, covering or painted decoration in any further detail.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

No ceramics appear in these sources. Cooking was done by stone-boiling: hot stones were dropped into water to bring it to the boil for cooking meat, a practice so characteristic that neighbouring peoples named the Assiniboine for it — Ojibwe *asinii-bwaan* and Cree *asinîpwâta*, both "Stone Sioux," from *asiniy*, 'rock, stone'. Metal entered the household through the fur trade, which supplied metal pots and metal tomahawks alongside guns and ammunition. Everyday equipment otherwise came from the hunt: hides for bedding, tendons for cord, horns for tools. The surviving named objects in this gallery are weapons and personal gear rather than vessels — the stone-headed club of 1850–60 and the beaded tobacco bag of the following decade.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment in the record is worn at the neck and in the hair. Neck-ornaments are listed for Black Raven, Big Elk and the men in the group portrait, where they appear alongside varied head-gear; Irene Rock wears a beaded chest ornament. Hair itself is catalogued as part of a sitter's appearance — "wearing hair and neck-ornament" — and Rattlesnake wears two feathers in his. Feathers also finish the Met's club. The sources used do not cover tattooing, face or body painting, or metal jewellery.

## Music & performance

The sources used do not describe Assiniboine instruments, song genres or performance occasions. The only musical or performative trace in them is indirect: one band was called *Wiciyabina* or *Wichiyabina*, 'Ones That Go to the Dance', shortened to *Wįcį́jana*, 'Girl Band'.

## Dance & theatre

The one named dance in these sources is the **Sun Dance**, which the Assiniboine performed as other Plains peoples did. No dramatic, masked or puppet traditions are documented for them here.

## Festivals & rituals

Assiniboine religious life as these sources describe it centred on the Sun god and the Thunder god, held to be the most important manifestations of the Great Spirit. The Sun Dance was the collective rite; individually, people took guidance from personal visions sought in vision quests. Ritual authority was vested in the *Wócegiye įtącą*, the medicine man, who was at once religious leader and traditional healer, working with plants and herbs as women also did in treating illness. Alongside him stood the *Hųgá* or *Hunga*, the band chief, with an advisory council of *Hungabi* ('little chiefs'); the *įtą́cą* or war chief led men in battle, the *Hogíyesʼa* (camp crier) announced war deeds, news and council decisions, and the *Agícida* (soldier or camp watcher) kept order in camp, on the hunt and in war. Descent was patrilineal: leadership passed through the male line and children belonged to the father and his clan. Girls, among the Assiniboine and Stoney as among the Lakota and Dakota, were encouraged to ride, hunt and fight, and women occasionally fought in battle and in defence of the camp. The sources used do not set out an annual festival calendar or describe birth, marriage or funeral rites.

## Foodways

The staple was the plains bison, hunted from horseback with bow and arrows by men whose horsemanship the sources single out; horses first came to the Assiniboine by trading with the Blackfeet and the Gros Ventre and by raiding and trading with the Crow and the Sioux. Meat was cooked by dropping heated stones into water to boil it — the practice that gave the people their best-known name. Women processed and preserved meat for the winter and gathered and cultivated plants, which also served as medicine. The destruction of the bison herds by Canadian and American hunters removed the most important food source of the Iron Confederacy nations and contributed directly to the confederacy's defeat and break-up. No named dishes, beverages or dietary rules appear in these sources.

## Oral tradition & literature

The central narrative in the sources is the Assiniboine creation myth of **Iktome**, among the best-known creator-trickster figures in Native North American mythology. It is an "earth-diver" story of the kind also told by the Anishinaabe and Ojibwe: Iktome sends animals down to find land beneath a primeval sea, and only the muskrat succeeds, floating back dead with earth clutched in his hands, from which Iktome makes land. Iktome is amoral rather than benevolent — he kills a frog that challenges his plan for an endless winter, but then relents and cuts winter to seven months; he makes horses and people out of dirt, and teaches the Assiniboine how to steal horses. The presence of horses marks parts of the modern tellings as later additions, since horses were introduced to North America by the Spanish. A second body of narrative survives in the band names themselves, which carry their own stories — *Wadópaȟna Tųwą*, 'Canoe Paddlers Who Live on the Prairie', was also called Watopachnato, "Big Devils," by traders, and later Gauche's Band after the chief Tchatka, "Left Hand."

## Language & religion

Assiniboine is a Nakotan language of the Northern Plains within Mississippi Valley Siouan, one of the five divisions of Dakotan alongside Santee-Sisseton, Yankton-Yanktonai, Lakota (Teton) and Stoney. It is an *n*-variety — *Nakʰóta*, *Nakʰóda*, *Nakʰóna* against *Dakʰóta* and *Lakʰóta* — the pattern formalised as the D-N-L classification, which some scholars question because the substitutions are not rigid and the varieties form a continuum. It is closer to the Sioux dialects than to Stoney, with which it is hardly mutually intelligible. The inventory runs to 27 consonants with aspirated, plain and ejective stops, five oral and three nasal vowels; word order is SOV; there are no articles, no nominal case and no verbal tense marking. Numerals run *wąži, nųba, yamni, tópa, záptą, šákpe, iyušna, šaknoğą, napcuwąga, wikcémna*. Speaker estimates range from under 50 to about 150, mostly elderly, with a 2021 Canadian study giving 350; most Assiniboine now speak only English. It holds no official status anywhere.
