## Overview

The Ho-Chunk are a Siouan people whose ancestors built thousands of effigy mounds across Wisconsin and the surrounding region. They lived in permanent villages and moved seasonally to hunt, fish and gather, cultivating corn, squash, beans and wild rice. Today they are enrolled in two federally recognized tribes, one in Wisconsin and one in Nebraska. Their oral traditions, spiritual practices and material culture reflect a deep connection to the land and spirit world.

## Material culture

Ho-Chunk material culture blends traditional hide work, beadwork and weaving with European trade textiles and metals acquired through contact.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Ho-Chunk weavers and beaders combined European trade cloth and glass beads with wool yarn and metal to create distinctive textiles.

- **Bandolier bag** — A wool and cotton trade-cloth bag decorated with glass beads and metal, combining European textiles with traditional beadwork.
- **Woven baskets** — Women wove baskets, shown in photographs at the Eaton County Fair in Michigan.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress was blankets, cloth headscarves, shirts, leggings and moccasins; men's formal dress featured feather headdresses, bone ornaments and beaded garments.

- **Blankets and wraps** — Blankets wrapped around the body and over the head were the foundation of everyday dress for both men and women.
- **Feather headdress** — Chiefs and men of rank wore elaborate feather headdresses or fur bonnets with single or multiple feathers.
- **Beaded leggings and loin coverings** — Men's formal dress included beaded leggings and beaded loin coverings, worn with bone ornaments.

### Architecture

Ho-Chunk built permanent villages of domed wigwams and also used tipis; their ancestors constructed thousands of effigy mounds.

- **Wigwam** — A domed single-room dwelling that served as the main house type in permanent Ho-Chunk villages.
- **Effigy mounds** — Thousands of earth mounds raised in the shapes of animals during the Late Woodland period (650–1200 CE), claimed by Ho-Chunk as ancestral work.
- **Dugout canoes** — Canoes carved from logs and recovered from lakes and rivers near Madison, Wisconsin, used for fishing and travel.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Ho-Chunk men crafted silver and copper ornaments and tools; bundles of ceremonial objects held spiritual power.

- **Medicine bundle bowl and bag** — A wood, wool and skin container holding feathers, bones, skins, flutes and paints that embodied spiritual blessings received from spirits.
- **Whistle** — A ceremonial instrument made of wood, sinew, ribbon, metal thimbles and buckskin, showing how trade goods were incorporated into sacred equipment.
- **Fishing spear and club** — Tools used by men to hunt fish in rivers and lakes.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ho-Chunk men and women wore multiple strands of beaded necklaces, shell ornaments and bone chokers; men produced silver and copper decorations.

- **Bear-claw necklace** — A necklace made from bear claws, worn by men of rank including head chiefs.
- **Shell ear ornaments and necklaces** — Shell worked into earrings and necklaces, worn by women as a sign of status.
- **Bone choker with gorget** — Bone carved into chokers and decorative chest plates worn by men of rank.
- **Baby-carrier charm ornaments** — Wooden baby-carriers wrapped in cloth and decorated with charm ornaments hung around the infant's neck.

## Music & performance

Ho-Chunk had flutes and whistles used in ceremonial contexts, though detailed musical practice is not recorded in available sources.

- **Whistle** — A ceremonial wind instrument made of wood, sinew, ribbon, metal thimbles and buckskin.
- **Flute** — A wind instrument that could be gathered into a war-bundle.

## Dance & theatre

Ho-Chunk dance and dramatic performance are not documented in the available sources.

## Festivals & rituals

Ho-Chunk spiritual life centered on acquiring guardian spirits through fasting, performing war-bundle ceremonies, and moving seasonally to find food.

- **Guardian-spirit quest** — Boys fasted at puberty to acquire a protective guardian spirit; without one their lives would be miserable.
- **War-bundle feast** — A two-part ceremony honoring night-spirits and the Thunderbird spirit, performed before a man went on the warpath.
- **Seasonal movement and berry gathering** — Families moved through the year to find food in different places, and many returned to Black River Falls to pick berries in summer.
- **Clan system** — Twelve clans, each tied to an animal spirit and a specific role; chiefs came from the Thunderbird Clan and the Bear Clan enforced discipline.
- **Exogamous marriage** — Marriage was required outside one's own clan, linking families across clan lines.

## Foodways

Ho-Chunk women cultivated corn, squash, beans and wild rice in permanent villages while men hunted and fished; seasonal foods were preserved for travel.

- **Corn and squash cultivation** — Women deliberately grew several varieties to ensure different types ripened across the growing season.
- **Wild rice harvesting** — Zizania spp. harvested and processed, often alongside maple sugar production.
- **Maple sugar and syrup** — Sap from sugar maples tapped and boiled down into syrup and candy, harvested at the same time as wild rice.
- **Preserved travel food** — Dried meats combined with berries, prepared by women to sustain families on seasonal moves.
- **Hunting and fishing** — Men hunted muskrat, mink, otter, beaver and deer, and speared or clubbed fish; women processed all game.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ho-Chunk oral traditions were carefully recorded by anthropologist Paul Radin and include creation narratives, trickster tales and heroic cycles.

- **Migration myth** — A Menominee chief assembled supplies on Lake Michigan's shore; a flock of spirit ravens descended and each bird became a kneeling man of the Hocąk nation.
- **Trickster tales** — Oral stories using animals and nature to teach lessons through trickery, such as 'The Trickster and Talking Bulb' about disrespecting nature.
- **Red Horn Cycle** — An epic narrative following Red Horn and his companions Turtle and Storms-as-He-Walks against the Man-Eaters, a race of giants; Red Horn married a red-haired giant woman.

## Language & religion

Ho-Chunk is an agglutinating Siouan language with about 200 native speakers; the people believe the language was given by Earthmaker, their creator.

- **Dorsey's law** — A sound law unique to Ho-Chunk in which the vowel after a resonant is copied back into a preceding voiceless-obstruent cluster.
- **Syllabics writing system** (*Ba-Be-Bi-Bo*) — An adapted syllabic script used briefly in the mid-to-late 1800s to write Ho-Chunk.
- **Americanist Latin script** — The official alphabet of the Ho-Chunk Nation since 1994, using the ogonek for nasal vowels with diacritics named in Ho-Chunk (sįįc for ogonek, wookąnąk for haček).
- **Thunderbird spirit** — A powerful spirit honored in war-bundle ceremonies and associated with the chief's clan.
- **Herešgúnina** — The solar deity born with reversed legs, creator of crawfish, grizzly bears and gambling whose severed head formed the solar disk.

## Glossary

- *Hoocąk* — The Ho-Chunk name for themselves, meaning 'People of the Sacred Voice'
- *Wigwam* — A domed single-room dwelling used as the main house type in permanent villages
- *War-bundle* — A bundle of sacred objects (feathers, bones, skins, flutes, paints) embodying spiritual blessings
- *War-bundle feast* — A two-part ceremony honoring night-spirits and Thunderbird spirit before going to war
- *Guardian spirit* — A protective spirit acquired by boys through fasting at puberty
- *Thunderbird* — A powerful spirit associated with the chief's clan and invoked in ceremonies
- *Red Horn* — A heroic figure in Ho-Chunk mythology, 'He Who Wears (Human) Faces on His Ears'
- *Storms-as-He-Walks* — Mą'e-manįga, a thunderbird companion of Red Horn in mythic narratives
- *Herešgúnina* — The solar deity born with reversed legs, creator of crawfish, grizzly bears and gambling
- *Effigy mounds* — Earth mounds shaped like animals, built in the Late Woodland period (650–1200 CE)
- *Zizania* — Wild rice, cultivated and harvested by Ho-Chunk women alongside maple sugar
- *Bandolier bag* — A trade-cloth bag decorated with beads and metal, combining European and traditional materials
- *Menominee* — Kaǧi in Ho-Chunk; referenced in the migration myth
