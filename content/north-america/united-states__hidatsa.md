## Overview

The Hidatsa are a Missouri River Siouan people enrolled in the federally recognized Three Affiliated Tribes of the Fort Berthold Reservation in North Dakota. Their traditional territory included the Knife River area, the Heart River, the Middle Missouri River, the Yellowstone River, the Mouse River, and the Devils Lake region. The Hidatsa formed through the amalgamation of three related village groups with distinct histories and dialects: the Awaxawi or Amahami, the Awatixa or Awadixá, and the Hidatsa proper, or Hiraacá. The Hidatsa language is related to Crow and is endangered. In 2019, fewer than 65 fluent speakers were estimated, while research in 2007 recorded an ethnic population of 600. Their cultural history is documented through village traditions, agriculture, craft, ceremonies, songs, and ethnographic work.

### Architecture

The Hidatsa lived in earthlodge villages along the Knife River and the Missouri River. The sources describe the Hidatsa, Mandan, and Arikara as village peoples and state that the Hidatsa adopted agriculture and permanent villages after settling with the Mandan north of the Knife River. The Awaxawi name is glossed as “Village on the Hill,” the Awatixa name as “Village of the Scattered Lodges” or “High Village,” and Like-a-Fishhook Village is identified as *Mua iruckup hehisa atis* or *Mu'a-idu'skupe-hi'cec*.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe Hidatsa creation traditions associated with the three ancestral village groups. The Awaxawi or Amahami tradition tells of emergence from the Earth at Devil’s Lake, identified as *Miri xopash*, *Mirixubáash*, or *Miniwakan*, “Holy Water there.” The Awatixa tradition says that its first people came from the sky, led by Charred Body, and lived near Painted Woods “where they were created.” The Hidatsa proper or Hiraacá tradition identifies them as “People of the Willows” and describes their movement from what is now western Minnesota to Devil’s Lake and then toward the Mandan settlements.

## Foodways

The sources describe the Hidatsa as agricultural village people and state that agriculture produced a surplus supporting their role in Great Plains Indian trading networks. They also record that the Hidatsa hunted upstream from their earthlodge villages and hunted deer and other small game along the Little Missouri. Buffalo Bird Woman’s testimony was used by Gilbert Livingston Wilson to describe traditional economy and day-to-day practices, and an external work is identified as *Agriculture of the Hidatsa Indians: An Indian Interpretation*.

The sources mention cached corn in connection with the burning of parts of Like-a-Fishhook Village in December 1862.

## Oral tradition & literature

Hidatsa oral tradition includes creation accounts associated with the Awaxawi, Awatixa, and Hidatsa proper. The Awaxawi or Amahami emerged from the Earth at Devil’s Lake, while the Awatixa originated from the sky under the leadership of Charred Body. The Hidatsa proper or Hiraacá are associated with the meaning “People of the Willows.”

The sources also preserve Hidatsa names and traditional explanations. The autonym is Hiraacá, described as a corruption of *midah-hutsee-ahti*, meaning “house or lodge made with willows.” The Mandan called the Hidatsa *Mį́nįtaree*, “to cross the water.” The Hidatsa called the Crow Nation *Gixaa'iccá* or *Gixáa-iccá*, “Those Who Pout Over Tripe.”

Buffalo Bird Woman’s recollections were recorded through the ethnographic work of Gilbert Livingston Wilson. Frances Densmore recorded ceremonial and personal songs before 1920.

## Language & religion

Hidatsa is an endangered Siouan language related to Crow. It is spoken primarily in North Dakota and South Dakota. The language has five vowels and two diphthongs, lacks nasal vowels, and has subject-object-verb word order. Hidatsa nouns do not change form to mark singular and plural, and the language has five simple pronouns. A description of Hidatsa-Mandan culture, including a grammar and vocabulary, was published in 1877 by Washington Matthews.

The three ancestral village groups spoke different dialects before coming together along the Missouri River. Children in early Mandan-Hidatsa communities were taught their mother’s language but understood the dialect of either tribe. In 2019, fewer than 65 fluent speakers were estimated. Research in 2007 recorded 200 Hidatsa speakers among an ethnic population of 600, including 6 monolingual speakers and 50 semi-fluent speakers. Conversations primarily took place among elders in the privacy of the home.

The sources describe creation traditions, the Naxpike, and ceremonial songs, but do not define a current religious landscape or religious affiliation.
