## Overview

The Shawnee are a Native American people of the Northeastern Woodlands whose precontact homeland was likely centered in southern Ohio. In the 17th century, they dispersed through Ohio, Illinois, Maryland, Delaware, and Pennsylvania; later movements took them across West Virginia, Kentucky, Indiana, and other regions. Following removal by the United States, Shawnee communities were relocated west of the Mississippi River and subsequently to Indian Territory, which became Oklahoma in the early 20th century. Today, Shawnee people are enrolled in the Absentee-Shawnee Tribe of Indians of Oklahoma, Eastern Shawnee Tribe of Oklahoma, and Shawnee Tribe, all headquartered in Oklahoma. Their language is an Algonquian language, and Shawnee communities are documented through their language, towns, council houses, oral histories, mythology, and intertribal political and religious movements.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Historical accounts describe a Shawnee town as containing approximately forty to one hundred bark-covered houses, similar in construction to Iroquois longhouses. Each village usually had a meeting house or council house, perhaps sixty to ninety feet long, where public deliberations took place. Shawnee communities were documented at places including Eskippakithiki in Kentucky, Sonnionto in Ohio, Chalahgawtha at the site of modern Chillicothe, Ohio, Old Shawneetown in Illinois, and Suwanee in Georgia. The sources also mention Prophetstown, a village associated with Tecumseh's confederacy, which Harrison's troops burned after the Battle of Tippecanoe.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do mention bark-covered houses, earthwork mounds associated with Fort Ancient and Hopewell cultures, and a boatload of supplies seized during the conflicts preceding Tecumseh's War.

## Music & performance

The sources used mention a Shawnee song collected by C. F. Voegelin in which *šaawaki* is attested as the spirit of the South, or the South Wind. A Shawnee tale also identifies “Sawage,” written *šaawaki*, as the deity of the south wind; Jeremiah Curtin translated Sawage as “it thaws,” referring to the warm weather of the south.

Shawnee religious and political teachings were also communicated through public councils and intertribal speeches. Tenskwatawa, known as The Prophet, called upon Native peoples to return to their ancestral traditions, while Tecumseh travelled extensively to recruit allies and organize resistance to American expansion. The sources describe these activities as political and religious movements.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do record religious interpretations of major historical events. Tecumseh told the Choctaw, Chickasaw, Muscogee, and many others that the comet of March 1811 had signaled his coming and that further signs would confirm that the Great Spirit had sent him. On December 11, 1811, the New Madrid earthquake shook Muscogee lands and much of the Midwestern United States; many Native American peoples viewed the earthquake and its aftershocks as spiritually significant and as a sign that the Shawnee cause should be supported.

The sources also describe Tenskwatawa as a spiritual leader whose teachings were connected to the formation of a broad intertribal alliance.

## Foodways

 They mention maize, apple, raspberry, and tobacco in a discussion of Shawnee grammatical gender: these nouns belong to the animate class, while strawberry does not. This linguistic classification does not provide evidence about cultivation, preparation, or consumption.

The sources also state that Fort Ancient peoples practiced a “horticulture-centered, sedentary way of life” before changes documented after 1525.

## Oral tradition & literature

Shawnee oral histories connect the people with Fort Ancient peoples, alongside similarities in material culture, artistic traditions, and mythology. The Shawnee regarded the Lenape, or Delaware, as their “grandfathers,” reflecting a perceived ancestral relationship. A Shawnee tale identifies “Sawage,” or *šaawaki*, as the deity of the south wind. Another historical tradition associated some Shawnee with a party sent by Chief Opechancanough, ruler of the Powhatan Confederacy from 1618 to 1644, to settle in the Shenandoah Valley; the party was reportedly led by his son, Sheewa-a-nee.

The Shawnee language has a dictionary, and portions of the Bible were translated into Shawnee. A 1929 translation of the four gospels of the New Testament was produced by Thomas “Wildcat” Alford, although the orthography used in that translation never attained wide usage. Conversational Shawnee booklets, CDs, and a Learn Shawnee Language website are available. The Shawnee Tribe launched a language immersion program in 2020 with virtual and in-person classes.

## Language & religion

Shawnee is a Central Algonquian language spoken in parts of central and northeastern Oklahoma. Historically, it was spoken across a wide area of the Eastern United States, primarily north of the Ohio River, including present-day Ohio, West Virginia, Kentucky, and Pennsylvania. It is closely related to Mesquakie-Sauk and Kickapoo. The language is polysynthetic, has a fairly free word order, and most commonly uses Verb-Subject order. Shawnee has two noun classes, animate and inanimate, and grammatical distinctions involving proximate and obviative third persons.

The language is written in the Latin script. A Roman-based alphabet designed by the missionary Jotham Meeker during the 19th century was never widely used; Thomas “Wildcat” Alford later devised an orthography for his 1929 translation of the four gospels. The sources describe Shawnee as severely threatened and state that approximately 200 remaining speakers were older adults; a 2015 census reported 260 speakers, although the number was decreasing. They do describe Sawage, or *šaawaki*, as a south-wind deity and Tenskwatawa as a spiritual leader.
