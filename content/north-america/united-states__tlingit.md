## Overview

The Tlingit are an Indigenous people of the Pacific Northwest Coast, Alaska Natives in the United States and First Nations in Canada. Their society is divided into two moieties, Raven (*Yéil*) and Eagle, subdivided into matrilineal clans and house groups; clan crests are displayed on totem poles, canoes, feast dishes, house posts, weavings and jewelry. Names, stories, songs, dances and artistic designs are clan property, and even spoons and storage boxes are decorated. Their economy combined fishing, hunting, gathering and trade, with salmon above every other food.

## Material culture

Tlingit art is crest art: clan crests and figures from oral history in the Northwest Coast formline style of ovoids, U-forms and S-forms, carved in wood, horn and ivory, woven in mountain-goat wool and painted.

### Textile & pattern traditions

- **Chilkat robe** (*naaxein*) — Worn by high-ranking people on ceremonial occasions, including dances; woven from mountain-goat wool, dog fur and yellow cedar bark in black, white, yellow and blue, with curvilinear formline crests inside the weave and a long fringe. Named after the Chilkat region near Klukwan and shared with the Haida and Tsimshian.
- **Ravenstail robe** (*yéil koowú*) — Finger-twined geometric weaving with sharp lines and few colours, thought to be the precursor of Chilkat weaving; about a dozen historical robes survive in museums, one at the British Museum in goat wool with a sea-otter fur strip at the top.

### Clothing & dress

- **Painted bark hat** — Woven bark hats painted with formline crests.
- **Body paint** — White, black and red; black marked death, anger, sorrow and war.
- **Tattoo** — A mark of higher-status families, done by women with needle and stained sinew; forbidden to slaves.

### Architecture

- **Clan house** — Cedar plank houses, square or rectangular, built without nails or fastenings, with front designs and totem poles showing clan and moiety.
- **Totem pole** — Cedar poles carved with crests in a column, placed at house corners, supporting interiors or on shores; mortuary poles held the ashes of the dead.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

- **Copper** — Shield-shaped copper plates used as valuables.
- **Dagger** — Copper, iron or steel daggers, some with pommels carved as ravens, bears or other crests; symbols of status passed down as heirlooms.
- **Halibut hook** — Carved wooden hooks with fibre lashings.
- **Ladles and combs** — Ladles of wood and horn; a wooden comb carved as the measuring-worm crest.
- **Canoe and totem pole models** — Carved and painted miniatures.

### Jewelry & body adornment

- **Ear ornaments** — Worn by higher-class adults and children over eight; of abalone (*haliotis*) shell, copper, wood and bone.
- **Nose ring** (*tunás*) — Given to the dead.
- **Shaman's amulet** — Carved ivory amulets.

## Music & performance

Songs and dances are clan property; serious songs, whether of love or mourning, may not be sung without the owning clan's permission, while lullabies are public.

- **Rattles** — Carved and painted wooden rattles filled with pebbles, some in bird form.
- **Lute** — Two 19th-century lutes at the Met are attributed to the Yakutat Tlingit.

## Dance & theatre

- **Masks** — Carved and painted wooden masks: a beaver, a mosquito, a shaman's mask.
- **Transformation puppet** — Bone, sinew and shell inlay, late 18th to early 19th century (Met).
- **Dance regalia** — Chilkat robes, whose fringe sways as the wearer dances.

## Festivals & rituals

- **Memorial potlatch** (*koo.éex'*) — Held a year or two after a death so the family can stop mourning; the opposite moiety receives gifts and performs songs and stories, and a chief's or shaman's successor is chosen.
- **Other potlatches** — Held for births, naming, marriages, raising totem poles and honouring leaders.

## Foodways

- **Salmon** — The most emphasised food, with seal and game close behind.
- **Seasonal round** — Halibut, shellfish and seaweed in spring; seal and salmon in summer; salmonberries, soapberries and currants; sea otter in autumn; herring and eulachon, fresh or dried.
- **Beach food** — Eating only beach food was a sign of poverty, and shamans' families avoided it.

## Oral tradition & literature

- **Raven cycle** — Raven is a central figure who stole the sun, the waters and the fish; many of its humorous tales are shared freely, while other stories belong to particular clans.

## Language & religion

- **Tlingit language** — An independent branch of Na-Dené with a complex grammar and several rare sounds; revitalised through Sealaska Heritage Institute, Goldbelt Heritage Institute and the University of Alaska Southeast.
- **Shamans** (*íxt'*) — Mostly men, who cured disease, influenced the weather and aided hunting and war; reincarnation of humans and animals is a central belief.
- **Russian Orthodoxy** — Adopted by many between 1886 and 1895, with the liturgy translated into Tlingit.

## Glossary

- *Lingít* — The people's name, "People of the Tides"
- *Yéil* — Raven, one of the two moieties
- *naaxein* — Chilkat robe
- *yéil koowú* — Ravenstail weaving
- *koo.éex'* — Potlatch
- *at.oow* — Heirloom, such as a blanket, passed down and held in trust
- *íxt'* — Shaman
- *tunás* — Nose ring given to the dead
- *haliotis* — Abalone shell, used for ear ornaments
