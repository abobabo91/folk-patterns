## Overview

The Aghuls are a Northeast Caucasian ethnic group native predominantly to Agulsky district of Dagestan, with communities partly on the plain and in cities including Dagestanskiye Ogni, Derbent, and Makhachkala. According to the 2010 census, there were 34,160 Aghuls in Russia, compared with 7,000 in 1959. The Aghul language belongs to the Lezgic language family within the Northeast Caucasian family and is primarily spoken by Aghuls in Azerbaijan and Southern Dagestan in Russia. Four groups are associated with four gorges: Aguldere, Kurakhdere, Khushandere, and Khpyukdere. Ethnically, the Aghuls are close to the Lezgins. The available sources document social organization, family life, language, and local government.

## Language & religion

Aghul is a Lezgic language within the Northeast Caucasian family. It belongs to the Eastern Samur group of the Lezgic branch; Glottolog divides it between the Qushan/Koshan group and other Aghul dialects because the Qushan dialects are unintelligible with the other dialects. The language is spoken primarily by Aghuls in Azerbaijan and Southern Dagestan in Russia, and the source gives approximately 33,200 speakers in the 2020 census. In 2002, Aghul was spoken by 28,300 people in Russia, mainly in Southern Dagestan in the Agulsky and Kurakhsky Districts, and by 32 people in Azerbaijan. The Aghul alphabet was devised in the 1990s and has since been used for education, with primers, textbooks, and dictionaries published. Aghul has four core cases—absolutive, ergative, genitive, and dative—as well as a large series of location cases. The sources state that the Aghuls were converted to Islam after the Arab conquest of the eighth century.

## Social organization and family life

As elsewhere in Daghestan, Aghuls were divided into *tukhums*, or clans, comprising twenty to forty households. Each *tukhum* had its own cemetery, pastures, and hay fields, while members were bound by obligations of mutual support and defense. Each village had a council representing three or four *tukhums*, headed by an elder. The village mullah and qadi also played important roles in local affairs, and wealthier *tukhums* could exert disproportionate influence over village government.

The sources describe a tendency toward endogamy within the *tukhum*, with marriages to outsiders very rare. In the past, Aghuls lived in extended-family households averaging fifteen to twenty members. A senior male, either the father or eldest brother, functioned as chief and held broad authority over household affairs and members. When an extended family divided, sisters—including those already married and no longer living in the household—received land and movable property. Each sister received one-half of the land share given to each brother, a practice described as unusually generous by Daghestanian standards.
