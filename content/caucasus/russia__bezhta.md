## Overview

The Bezhta, also known as Kapuchi, are an Andi–Dido people living in the Tsuntinsky region of southwestern Dagestan, Russia. In the 1930s, they were classified with the other Andi-Dido peoples as Avars, although some people identified themselves as Bezhta in the 2002 census of Russia. They numbered 1,448 in 1926, while the 2002 census recorded 6,184 self-identified “Bezhtins”; the real number was probably higher. They speak Bezhta, and many also speak Avar, Russian, or other Tsezic languages. Their documented cultural life includes livestock breeding, terrace farming, Islam, and a language with several dialects and a developing written tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The Bezhta are primarily Sunni Muslim. The presence of Islam in their land can be traced to the 8th and 9th centuries, and most Bezhta adopted the religion around the 16th and 17th centuries through the influence of Sufi missionaries.

## Foodways

The Bezhtas used to be livestock breeders and mainly raised sheep, horses, and oxen. They also practiced terrace farming and grew rye, wheat, and other grains.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Bezhta language article records that the first book ever printed in Bezhta was the Gospel of Luke in 1999.

## Language & religion

Bezhta, also called Bezheta or Kapucha, belongs to the Tsezic group of the North Caucasian language family. It is spoken by about 6,200 people in southern Dagestan, Russia. Its closest linguistic relatives are Hunzib and Khwarshi. Bezhta has three dialects: Bezhta Proper, Tlyadal, and Khocharkhota. It is unwritten, although attempts have been made to develop an official orthography; the Bezhta people use Avar as their literary language. The language has a rich consonantal system, 16 distinct vowel phonemes, vowel length and nasalization, and a mostly agglutinative morphology. It is an ergative language with a particularly rich system of locative cases. The Bezhta are primarily Sunni Muslim, with Islam associated with the 8th and 9th centuries and the influence of Sufi missionaries in the 16th and 17th centuries.
