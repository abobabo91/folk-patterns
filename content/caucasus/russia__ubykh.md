## Overview

The Ubykh are one of the Circassian tribes and historically inhabited the eastern coast of the Black Sea, including Sache, identified with present-day Sochi in Krasnodar Krai, Russia. Their territory lay between the Shapsug tribe near Tuapse and the Sadz (Dzhigets) north of Gagra. The Ubykh were semi-nomadic horsemen with a finely differentiated vocabulary concerning horses and tack. After the Russian conquest of the Northwestern Caucasus was completed in 1864, the Ubykh left their homeland and settled in villages in western Turkey around Manyas. Today, the Ubykh diaspora is scattered through Turkey and, to a much lesser extent, Jordan. The Ubykh nation per se no longer exists, although people of Ubykh ancestry continue to identify as Ubykh.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that some Ubykh practiced favomancy and scapulimancy. Ubykh society was patrilineal, and Ubykh descendants may know five, six, or even seven generations of agnatic ancestry. Women were especially venerated, and the language retained a special second-person pronoun prefix used exclusively with women, *χa-*.

## Foodways

The Ubykh language article includes translated examples involving eating, drinking, milk, and reaping, but these linguistic examples do not describe Ubykh cuisine.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ubykh oral literature included folktales and some cycles of the Nart saga. A few phrases were transcribed by Evliya Çelebi in his *Seyahatname*, and Tevfik Esenç narrated stories, including the story of the two travellers and the fish. Georges Dumézil published a collection of Ubykh folktales in the late 1950s. The Ubykh language article also preserves an English translation of a narrative in which a sheep and a goat go into a field, encounter a gully, and quarrel after the goat laughs at the sheep.

The sources identify Bagrat Shinkuba’s historical novel *The Last of the Departed* as a work about the Ubykh people. Before Tevfik Esenç’s death, thousands of pages of material and many audio recordings were collected and collated by linguists including Georges Charachidzé, Georges Dumézil, Hans Vogt, George Hewitt, and A. Sumru Özsoy, with assistance from Tevfik Esenç and Huseyin Kozan.

## Language & religion

Ubykh was a Northwest Caucasian language once spoken by the Ubykh people. It was ergative, highly agglutinating, and polysynthetic, with polypersonal verbal agreement and a very large consonant inventory. The language article describes 84 distinct consonants and three phonemic vowels. Ubykh had no standard written form and was never written by its speech community, although Tevfik Esenç learned to write it using the transcription devised by Georges Dumézil.

Ubykh was spoken on the eastern coast of the Black Sea around Sochi until 1864, when the Ubykhs were driven from the region by the Russians. They later settled in Turkey, founding the villages of Hacı Osman, Kırkpınar, Masukiye, and Hacı Yakup. Arabic and Circassian became preferred languages for everyday communication, and Turkish displaced Ubykh among the Ubykh population. The language died out on 7 October 1992, when its last fluent speaker, Tevfik Esenç, died. The sources state that the Ubykh were Muslim and that the term *wɨrɨs*, “Russian,” became a slang term meaning “infidel,” “non-Muslim,” or “enemy.”
