## Overview

The Chechens are a Northeast Caucasian ethnic group of the Nakh peoples, native to the North Caucasus. Historically they were also known as Kisti and Durdzuks, while they refer to themselves as *Nokhchiy*. They are the largest ethnic group in the region. Most Chechens are Muslims and live in Chechnya, a republic within the Russian Federation. Significant populations also live in Aukh, Ingushetia and Moscow, while diaspora communities are found in Kazakhstan, Turkey, Jordan, Iraq and elsewhere. Chechen society is largely egalitarian and organized around autonomous local clans called *teips*, which are informally grouped into confederations called *tukkhums*. Their cultural history includes distinctive social codes, oral epic traditions, religious practices and clan-based forms of organization.

## Festivals & rituals

Before adopting Islam, Chechens practiced a blend of religious traditions and beliefs. Their rites and rituals included practices connected with farming, among them rain rites, a celebration on the first day of plowing, the Day of the Thunderer *Sela* and the Day of the Goddess *Tusholi*.

Chechen customary culture is also associated with the code of honor *quonahalla* and the social code *nokhchallah*, which implies moral and ethical behavior, generosity and the safeguarding of women’s honor. The sources describe Chechnya as predominantly Sunni Muslim, with the Shafi‘i and Hanafi schools of jurisprudence represented. Some Chechens follow the Sufi tradition of *muridism*, and about half of Chechens belong to Sufi brotherhoods, or *tariqah*. The two Sufi *tariqas* identified are the *Naqshbandiyya* and the *Qadiriyya*.

## Oral tradition & literature

Chechen oral tradition includes *illesh*, a collection of epic poems and stories through which history has traditionally been remembered. Mongol invasions are especially prominent in Chechen folktales, which are often linked to military accounts of Alan-Dzurdzuk conflicts with the Mongols.

One narrative concerns an old hunter named Idig and his companions, who defended Mount Dakuoh against Tatar-Mongols during a twelve-year siege. In the story, the enemies stole the Chechens’ livestock, later killed many who descended from the mountain after receiving assurances of mercy, and took others into slavery. Idig and a few companions survived because they remained on the mountain and eventually escaped.

The sources also connect Chechen folktales with the Mongol siege described by Giovanni da Pian del Carpine and with arrowheads and spears reportedly found near the mountain. No named written literary revival or contemporary preservation project is described.

## Language & religion

Chechen is the main language of the Chechen people and belongs to the Nakh branch of the Northeast Caucasian languages. Literary Chechen is based on the central lowland dialect. Related languages include Ingush, spoken in neighboring Ingushetia, and Batsbi, spoken in the adjoining part of Georgia. Chechens have used Georgian, Arabic and Latin alphabets at different times; as of 2008, the official script is Russian Cyrillic.

Chechnya is predominantly Sunni Muslim. The Shafi‘i and Hanafi schools of jurisprudence are represented, with the Shafi‘i school described as the most practiced. Some Chechens follow Sufi *muridism*, and Sufi brotherhoods include the *Naqshbandiyya* and *Qadiriyya*. Earlier traditions included rites connected with farming, the Day of the Thunderer *Sela*, the Day of the Goddess *Tusholi* and the code of honor *quonahalla*.
