## Overview

The Jeks are a small Northeast Caucasian ethnic group indigenous to northern Azerbaijan. Their traditional homeland lies in the mountainous part of the Quba District, particularly around the village of Jek, in the northeastern Greater Caucasus. Their population is estimated at approximately 11,000 people, although the number of fluent Jek speakers is considerably smaller. The Jek language belongs to the Lezgic branch of the Northeast Caucasian language family and is closely related to Kryts, Budukh, and Khinalug. The Jeks are one of the Shahdagh peoples, historically distinct mountain communities whose geographic isolation helped preserve separate languages, village identities, oral traditions, and local customs. Their folk culture is associated with mountain agriculture, livestock breeding, handicrafts, folklore, clothing, music, and customary practices.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that Jek cultural traditions include traditional clothing.

### Architecture

The sources describe the Jek homeland as a mountainous environment of steep slopes, isolated settlements, mountain valleys, and limited agricultural land. They state that the Jeks historically lived in villages organized around local institutions, fortified settlements, customary practices, and kinship networks.

## Music & performance

The sources identify music as part of Jek cultural traditions, alongside oral folklore, local historical narratives, traditional clothing, and customary practices. The sources state that oral transmission played a major role in preserving cultural knowledge because the Jek language historically lacked a written tradition. They also state that the Jeks maintained cultural and economic relations with Lezgins, Azerbaijanis, and other Shahdagh communities, producing cultural exchange and shared elements of material culture while preserving a distinct Jek identity.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources state that the Jeks are traditionally Sunni Muslims and that religious life historically centered around village mosques, Islamic scholars, and religious customs shared with neighboring Muslim communities of Azerbaijan and the eastern Caucasus. During the Soviet period, religious institutions were restricted as part of state atheistic policies; after the independence of Azerbaijan, Islamic practices experienced a revival among many communities, including the Jeks.

## Foodways

The sources describe traditional Jek society as based on agriculture and livestock breeding. Traditional activities included the cultivation of grain and vegetables, sheep and cattle breeding, horticulture, handicrafts, and exchange with neighboring villages.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Jek language traditionally existed mainly as an oral language transmitted within families and villages. The sources state that the Jeks have oral folklore and local historical narratives, and that oral transmission played a major role in preserving cultural knowledge. The Jeks historically lacked a written literary tradition, while religious and administrative texts affecting the community were generally written in Arabic, Persian, Azerbaijani, or Russian, depending on the period. Modern linguistic studies have documented Jek vocabulary, grammar, and oral traditions as part of broader efforts to preserve the smaller languages of the Caucasus.

## Language & religion

Jek, also known as Cek or Dzhek, is a Northeast Caucasian language in the Lezgic branch. It is closely related to Kryts, Budukh, and Khinalug but remains a separate linguistic system. Jek is not a written language, and Azeri serves as the literary language of the Jek and of the Shahdagh peoples. Historically, Jek was transmitted orally within families and villages, while Arabic, Persian, Azerbaijani, and Russian were used for religious and administrative texts affecting the community. Many Jeks are bilingual or primarily Azerbaijani-speaking, especially younger generations. The Jeks are traditionally Sunni Muslims. Religious life developed within the broader Islamic culture of northern Azerbaijan and the eastern Caucasus and included village mosques, Islamic scholars, and shared religious customs.
