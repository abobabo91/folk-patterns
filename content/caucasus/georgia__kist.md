## Overview

The Kists are a Chechen sub-ethnic group in Georgia, primarily living in the Pankisi Gorge in the eastern Georgian region of Kakheti. Approximately 5,700 Kist people live there. The community is small and scattered across northeast Georgia, with six Kist villages in Pankisi: Duisi, Dzibakhevi, Jokolo, Shua Khalatsani, Omalo, and Birkiani. Their origins are traced to lower Chechnya, from which they migrated to the Pankisi Gorge and adjoining lands of Tusheti and Kakheti in the 1830s and 1870s. They are closely related culturally, linguistically, and ethnically to other Nakh-speaking peoples, including Ingushes and Chechens, while also sharing customs with eastern Georgian mountaineers.

## Festivals & rituals

Kist religious life combines syncretized Sunni Muslim beliefs with animistic folk religion, while small pockets of Christian Kists remain in Pankisi, Tusheti, and Kakheti. Kists worship Khevsur sacred places known as *jvari* and make sacrifices to the Anatori jvari near the Khevsureti village of Shatili, at the Georgian-Chechen border. The Anatori jvari was also considered sacred by Chechens in Maisti and Melkhisti. Highlanders from the northern Caucasus and Georgia participated together in religious celebrations until the borders were closed.

Kists pray in the mosque in Duisi and also pray at sites of old, ruined Christian sanctuaries. Christians among them and some folk followers pray in Saint George church in Joqolo and attend Alaverdoba at the Alaverdi Monastery of Kakheti. Kists also celebrate Tetri Giorgoba, described as a local variation of St George’s Day.

## Oral tradition & literature

The early history of the Kist people is not well known, and the sources state that few sources mention their traditions, culture, and history. The available historical sources about ethnic Kists in the Pankisi area are found in the Georgian press and date from the 1880s; the individuals named in connection with them are E. Gugushvili, Zakaria Gulisashvili, Ivane Bukurauli, and Mate Albutashvili, who was an ethnic Kist.

## Language & religion

Kists are closely related to Nakh-speaking peoples such as Ingushes and Chechens and are typically bilingual in Chechen and Georgian. They identify themselves as Chechen but, for official purposes, declare themselves of Georgian nationality. Their religious landscape includes syncretized Sunni Muslim beliefs, animistic folk religion, Christian practices, and indigenous religious traditions.

When Kists arrived in the valley in the early 19th century from Chechnya and Ingushetia, their religious practices included Islam and their original Nakh religion, with overlap with the indigenous beliefs of highland Georgian neighbors and with Christian influences. In the latter half of the 19th century, the Russian government pressured Kists to convert to Orthodox Christianity, resulting in episodes of mass baptisms and church construction. Muslim Kists constructed a mosque in Duisi in 1902, but the Russian government refused to recognize it. The mosque was forcefully closed after the Bolshevik revolution and reopened in 1960.

The position of Islam strengthened during the Soviet period, partly through the work of “wandering” mullahs, and this conversion process continued into the 1970s. Many Kists who had become Christian later reconverted to Islam. Until around 1970, a considerable part of the villagers of Jokolo, Omalo, and Birkiani were Christian; a Christian chapel was built in Omalo in the 1960s. In the 1970s, many Christians in Jokolo and Omalo returned to Islam. Birkiani has a majority Christian population today and had two mosques as of 2022.
