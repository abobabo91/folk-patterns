## Overview

The Karapapakhs, also called Terekeme, are a Turkic tribe whose original language was Karapapakh, a western Oghuz language closely related to Azerbaijani and Turkish. The name Karapapakh translates as “black hat” in Oghuz Turkic. In the Middle Ages, they moved into Western Asia with other Turkic-speaking and Mongol nomads and settled along the Debed river in eastern Georgia, near the present-day Georgian-Armenian border. Later movements took them to the Ottoman Empire and Qajar Iran. Historical communities were documented in Kars, Solduz district, Naqadeh, the Dagestan Oblast, southern Georgia, northern Armenia, Soviet Central Asia and the Caucasus. The sources describe them as a small ethnic group whose language and cultural practices were increasingly assimilated by Azerbaijanis and Turks.

The available material instead concerns historical, linguistic and religious classification. The Karapapakhs originally spoke their own western Oghuz language, but the language was largely supplanted by Azerbaijani and Turkish. In the Georgian SSR, Karapapakh was often confused with Azerbaijani. The sources state that Karapapakhs in modern times no longer speak their Turkic language as a distinct community language and have switched to Turkish or Azerbaijani.

## Festivals & rituals

The religious information that is available identifies the Karapapakhs as traditionally including Sunnis, Shias and adherents of Ali-Illahism. Olson and colleagues described the Karapapakhs in the Soviet Union as primarily adherents of the Ali-Illahism syncretic religion and noted a traditional element of secretiveness and fanaticism in Karapapakh religious practice.

The sources do record several historical descriptions of the group. George Bournoutian referred to the Karapapakhs as “Turkicized Kazakhs (Qazzaqs),” while other cited works describe them as a small ethnic group and a Turkmen tribe. These classifications belong to historical and ethnographic writing rather than to a documented Karapapakh oral-literary tradition.

## Language & religion

Karapapakh was originally a western Oghuz language closely related to Azerbaijani and Turkish. During the Soviet period and in Turkey, the Karapapakhs underwent linguistic and cultural assimilation. By 1926, according to the cited account, barely any Karapapakh could converse in the original language. The language was often confused with Azerbaijani in the Georgian SSR, and the Karapapakhs later switched to Turkish or Azerbaijani. In Naqadeh, Richard Tapper reported that Karapapakhs were linguistically and culturally Kurdicized in 1985; some were Sunni, in contrast to other Azerbaijani Turks of the region.

The sources describe a religious landscape including Sunnis, Shias and adherents of Ali-Illahism. In the 1926 Soviet census, most Soviet Karapapakhs were Sunnis, with a Shia minority. Olson and colleagues described Ali-Illahism as the primary religious affiliation of Karapapakhs in the Soviet Union.
