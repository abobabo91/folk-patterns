## Overview

The Ingush, historically known as Durdzuks, Gligvi and Kists, are a Northeast Caucasian ethnic group mainly inhabiting the Republic of Ingushetia in the central Caucasus. They also inhabit Prigorodny District and the town of Vladikavkaz in modern-day North Ossetia. The Ingush speak Ingush and are predominantly Sunni Muslims. The language is spoken by about 350,000 people, while another source gives approximately 350,000–400,000 speakers in 2020. Ingush historical settlement included the Sunzha and Terek basins, the Nazran Valley, mountain gorges, and areas around Magas, Nazran and Vladikavkaz. Their cultural history is documented through territorial societies, tower-associated ethnonyms, mountain settlements, oral legends, religious change, writing systems and the historical experience of deportation and return.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Architecture

Ingush settlements included villages grouped along local gorges, and the sources associate the endonym *Ghalghai* with *ghāla*, meaning “tower” or “fortress,” together with a plural suffix meaning people or inhabitants. The medieval and later settlement landscape included fortified and complex settlements, including Alanian settlements in the area covering parts of modern Magas and Nazran and the villages of Yandare, Gazi-Yurt, Ekazhevo, Ali-Yurt and Surkhakhi. Settlements were sometimes located in groups or “nests” within sight of one another, with a larger, fortified and more complex central settlement. The sources mention Tkhaba-Yerdy as a historical Christian Ingush church and state that churches like it emerged in Ingushetia during the 8–12th centuries.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention archaeological items in Ingushetia bearing Georgian text, the Lugovoe settlement, monuments of the early Bronze Age, and the use of American-supplied Studebaker trucks during the 1944 deportation.

## Music & performance

 They record that the first newspaper in the Ingush language, *Serdalo*, was published on May 1, 1923, but provide no information about music.

## Festivals & rituals

 They state that the Ingush are predominantly Sunni Muslims and connect the spread of Arabic writing among the Ingush with the spread of Islam.

The sources document several historical and communal events. On March 4–6, 1770, 24 Ingush elders swore an oath of allegiance to the Russian Empire near Angusht, at a clearing called “Barta-Bos” (“Slope of Agreement”). In 1973, Ingush demands for the return of the Prigorodny district reached an apogee at a rally in Grozny. The sources also describe the deportation of the Ingush and Chechens on 23 February 1944 in Operation Lentil, their return, and the formal rehabilitation of the Ingush on April 26, 1991.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ingush oral tradition appears in legends recorded during the 19th century and later. One legend recorded by ethnographer Albast Tutaev concerns representatives of the Galgaï Society of Mountainous Ingushetia. Another legend, recorded in the mountain village of Pkhamat by I. A. Dakhkilgov, describes eminent men from several territorial communities gathering to unite the country. In the account, the participants decided to use the single name “Ghalghaï,” end strife and begin an organized movement from the mountains to the plain.

The sources also preserve historical names and narratives connected with the Ingush past, including the Dzurdzuks, Makhli, Malkh, Kists, Gargareans and Gelae. They report that the first newspaper in Ingush, *Serdalo*, appeared on May 1, 1923. No Ingush epic, proverb, riddle collection or contemporary literary preservation project is described.

## Language & religion

Ingush is a Northeast Caucasian language belonging, with Chechen and Bats, to the Nakh branch of the Northeast Caucasian language family. There is pervasive passive bilingualism between Ingush and Chechen. Ingush is not divided into dialects except for Galanc̈oz̈, whose native name is *Галай-ЧӀож/Галайн-ЧӀаж* and which is considered transitional between Chechen and Ingush. Ingush is an official language of Ingushetia alongside Russian.

The language has used several writing systems. A Georgian-based writing system may have existed during the period of the 8–12th centuries, when churches such as Tkhaba-Yerdy emerged. Arabic writing appeared in Ingushetia after the spread of Islam and later than the second half of the 17th century. Ingush became a written language with an Arabic-based system at the beginning of the 20th century. A Latin alphabet was introduced in 1923, and a new Cyrillic script was announced in July 1938. The Ingush are predominantly Sunni Muslims, while the sources also describe a historical Christian past associated with Tkhaba-Yerdy.
