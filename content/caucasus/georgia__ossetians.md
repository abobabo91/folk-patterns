## Overview

The Ossetians, also known as Ossetes, Ossets and Alans, are an Iranic ethnic group indigenous to Ossetia, a region extending across the northern and southern sides of the Caucasus Mountains. Their homeland is politically divided between North Ossetia–Alania in Russia and the de facto country of South Ossetia, which is de jure part of Georgia. They speak Ossetian, an Eastern Iranian language of the Indo-European family, and most are also fluent in Russian. The Ossetians descend historically from the Alans, an eastern Iranian branch of the Sarmatians. Their culture includes distinct folk-song genres, named musical instruments, the Nart literature, and a religious landscape combining Eastern Orthodoxy, the Ossetian ethnic religion of Uatsdin, and Islam.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources identify Ossetian towers and temples known as *kuvandon*.

## Music & performance

Ossetian folk songs are divided into ten genres: historic songs, war songs, heroic songs, work songs, wedding songs, drinking songs, humorous songs, dance songs, romantic songs, and lyrical songs. The sources name a number of instruments used in Ossetian music.

The *Dyuuadæstænon* is a twelve-stringed harp, while the *Fændyr* is a harp with two or three plucked strings. The bowed-string instruments include the *Hysyn*, a two- or three-string fiddle, and the *Hyyrnæg*, a double-bridged instrument described as a kind of cello. Wind instruments include the *Uadyndz*, a flute; the *Khyozyn*, a reed flute; the *Lalym*, bagpipes; the *Udaevdz*, a double-reed instrument; and the *Fidiuæg*, an instrument made from a bull’s horn. Percussion instruments include *Kartsgænæg* rattles, the *Gymsæg* drum, and the *Dala* tambourine.

The sources associate wedding songs with weddings and drinking songs with drinking.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name dance songs as one Ossetian folk-song genre.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources state that Assianism, also called Uatsdin or Aesdin in Ossetian, includes ritual traditions such as animal sacrifices, holy shrines, and annual festivities. Temples known as *kuvandon* are found in most villages.

The sources identify wedding songs as an Ossetian folk-song genre.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ossetian folk mythology includes the Narts, the Daredzant, and the Tsartsiat, which the sources describe as basic literature of folk mythology in the region. Ossetian native beliefs are rooted in Sarmatian origin and have been syncretized with a local variant of Folk Orthodoxy, in which some pagan gods have been converted into Christian saints.

The sources also describe the historical background of the Alans, from whom the Ossetians descend. They mention the medieval kingdom of Alania, the Alanian ruler Durgulel, the Alanian king Os-Bagatar, and chronicles that used the ethnonym Alanac, As-Digor.

## Language & religion

Ossetian belongs to the Eastern Iranian, or Alanic, branch of the Indo-European language family. It is divided into two main dialect groups: Ironian, spoken in North and South Ossetia, and Digorian, spoken in Western North Ossetia. Subdialects include Tualian, Alagirian, and Ksanian. The Ironian dialect is the most widely spoken. Ossetian is among the remnants of the Scytho-Sarmatian dialect group and is not mutually intelligible with any other Iranian language.

Before the 10th century, Ossetians were strictly pagan. They were partially Christianized by Byzantine missionaries in the beginning of the 10th century, and by the 13th century most of the urban population had gradually become Eastern Orthodox Christian through Georgian missionary work. Islam was introduced during the 1500s and 1600s among the Digor after contact with Circassians of the Kabarday tribe. Today, most Ossetians follow Eastern Orthodoxy, while Uatsdin remains widespread and Islam is practiced by a minority.
