## Overview

The Udi, also called Uti, are a Lezgic people native to the Caucasus, living mainly in Russia and Azerbaijan, with smaller populations in Georgia, Armenia, Kazakhstan, Ukraine, and other countries. Their total number is about 10,000 people. Historically, they inhabited the eastern Caucasus along the coast of the Caspian Sea, in a territory extending north to the Kura River. Today, concentrated Udi settlement is found in Nij in Azerbaijan and Zinobiani in Georgia; smaller communities live in several regions of Russia, Armenia, Georgia, Kazakhstan, and Ukraine. They speak Udi, a Northeast Caucasian language of the Lezgic branch, and many also speak Azerbaijani, Russian, Georgian, or Armenian. Their Christian faith, language, historical association with Caucasian Albania, and continuing folk traditions make them important for the study of Caucasian cultural history.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do document Christian religious affiliation and several historical developments within Udi Christianity. The Udi of Vartashen remained in the Armenian Apostolic, or Gregorian, Church and formerly conducted services in Armenian. The Udi of Nij changed from the Armenian Apostolic Church to the Eastern Orthodox Church soon after the beginning of Russian rule. In 2003, the Albanian-Udi Christian Religious Community was founded in Azerbaijan and sought to restore the Albanian Apostolic Church as a church independent from the Armenian Apostolic Church.

The sources also preserve a religious statement from an Udi community from Shaki, which on 31 March 1724 petitioned Peter the Great for protection. The petition connected Udi ancestry with Caucasian Albania and stated that their ancestors became believers in God through the preaching of the apostle Elisaeus.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Udi language article states that collections of Udi folklore were published using Latin- and Cyrillic-based alphabets. In 2007, Vladislav Dabakov published a collection of Udi folklore in Astrakhan using a Latin-based alphabet. A 1996 collection titled *Nana oččal* used a Cyrillic-based alphabet, and in 2013 an Udi primer titled *Nanay muz* was published in Russia with a Cyrillic-based alphabet.

The Udi people article identifies George Kechaari as a Udi writer. It states that a new Latin-based Udi alphabet created in Azerbaijan in the mid-1990s was used for a primer and two collections of works by Georgy Kechaari, as well as for educational purposes in the village of Nic. The sources also identify the History of the Land of Ałuank by Movsēs Kałankatuatsʻi as an important Armenian source containing extensive information about the Udi.

## Language & religion

Udi is also called Uti or Udin. It belongs to the Lezgic branch of the Northeast Caucasian language family. The two primary modern dialects are Nij, also called Nidzh, and Vartashen. The Nidzh dialect has bottom, intermediate, and top subgroups, while the Vartashen dialect has Vartashen and Oktomberry sub-dialects. Udi is commonly spoken alongside Azerbaijani, Russian, Georgian, or Armenian, depending on residence and work. Many speakers use Udi in daily life but use the language of the country where they reside for official purposes.

An earlier form of Udi was associated with Caucasian Albania. The Old Udi language, also called the Caucasian Albanian language, was used as a liturgical language, and major Bible texts were translated into it. In the 5th century, the Caucasian Albanian script was created by the Armenian monk Mesrop Mashtots; the alphabet had 52 letters. Old Udi documents from Saint Catherine’s Monastery in Egypt date from the 7th century and used 50 of those 52 letters. After the fall of the Caucasian Albanian state, Armenian gradually replaced Caucasian Albanian as the church language. Udi is classified as “severely endangered” by UNESCO’s Red Book of Endangered Languages.
