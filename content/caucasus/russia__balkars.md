## Overview

The Balkars are a Turkic ethnic group of the North Caucasus and one of the titular populations of Kabardino-Balkaria. They share their language with the Karachays of Karachay-Cherkessia and have strong linguistic similarities with Kumyks from Dagestan; Balkars and Karachays are sometimes treated as a single ethnicity. The sources locate Balkar communities in the mountains of the Caucasus, including the upper reaches of the Kuban River and the regions around the rivers Chegem and Cherek. Their documented cultural history includes mountain settlement, Islam, bilingual education, migration, deportation, return, and close historical interaction with Circassians, Karachays, Kabardians, Kumyks, Nogais, Crimean Tatars, and other peoples of the North Caucasus.

## Music & performance

 They mention organs of mass culture, newspapers, magazines, and literary activity in Balkar and Russian, but provide no description of musical traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

 They state that most Balkars adopted Islam in the eighteenth century through contact with Kumyks, Circassians, Nogais, and Crimean Tatars, and that the Balkars are considered deeply religious. The Sufi Qadiriya order has a strong presence in the region.

## Foodways

 They mention bread, salt, cattle, rams, and other property in historical accounts of exchange, raiding, and restitution, but these references do not constitute an account of Balkar foodways.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources identify Kaisyn Kuliev as a Balkar author and poet who emphasized his love for the Balkarya land and traditions.

The historical material includes extensive quoted reports and accounts by Kabardian, Russian, British, and other observers concerning the Balkars, Karachays, Circassians, and neighboring peoples. These records describe military alliances, negotiations, migration, and resistance.

## Language & religion

Balkar belongs to the Ponto-Caspian subgroup of the Northwestern, or Kipchak, group of Turkic languages. In the Cyrillic alphabet used by the Balkars, there are eight vowels and twenty-seven consonants. Arabic was formerly used for religious services and Turkish for business matters. Balkar became the language of instruction in primary schools from 1920, while later instruction was carried out in Russian. Arabic letters were used until 1928, and Cyrillic was used after 1937.

The sources state that ninety-six percent of the population was bilingual in Balkar and Russian, and that in 2015 the number of bilingual people had risen by 1.3 percent, resulting in 97.3 percent speaking both languages. Most Balkars adopted Islam in the eighteenth century, and the Sufi Qadiriya order has a strong presence in the region.
