## Overview

Armeno-Tats are a distinct group of Christian Tat-speaking Armenians who historically populated eastern parts of the South Caucasus, in what constitutes the modern-day Republic of Azerbaijan. They formerly lived in Madrasa and Kilvar, but have almost entirely moved to Armenia and Russia. Most scholars researching the Tat language, including Boris Miller, identify them as ethnic Armenians who underwent a language shift and adopted Tat as their first language. Their history reflects contact among Armenian, Tat, Azeri, Persian, and Turkic-speaking communities. By the early twentieth century, Tat-speaking Christian Armenians remained in only Madrasa and Kilvar. In 1989, the Armenians of Madrasa moved collectively to Aragatsotn Province of Armenia and founded Dprevank. There are 6,000 Armenians living in Edissia.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources mention Armenian settlements and religious architecture. Adam Olearius referred to a community of Armenians in Shamakhi in 1637, and Vladimir Sysoyev described the ruins of a mediaeval Armenian church there in 1925.

## Music & performance

They do record several languages and speech forms associated with Armeno-Tat communities. Armeno-Tats of Madrasa and Kilvar referred to their language as *p'arseren*, meaning “Persian,” while Armeno-Tat migrants to the North Caucasus and Astrakhan called it *keghetseren*, meaning “village talk,” and used it as an in-group language. Armenians of Edissia and those living in the suburbs of Kizlyar were also reported to speak a Turkic idiom called *bizimja*, meaning “our talk,” which they had adopted while still in Shirvan. These speech forms are documented as language practices rather than musical genres.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record several major historical movements. In 1796, after the Persian Expedition of 1796 led by Valerian Zubov, most residents of Kilvar and Talabi and some residents of Garajally—about 50 families altogether—left with the troops and founded Edissia in the present-day Stavropol Krai of Russia. The remaining Armeno-Tats lived in Madrasa and Kilvar until the First Nagorno-Karabakh War, when they were forced to leave for Armenia. Armenians of Madrasa had planned a population exchange with the Azeri-populated village of Shidli in Armenia, but the Spitak earthquake destroyed the village and made the plan unrealisable. In 1989, the Madrasa community moved to Aragatsotn Province and founded Dprevank.

## Foodways

They mention Persian, Tat, Armenian, Azeri, and Turkic linguistic and historical connections but provide no food-related information.

## Oral tradition & literature

The historical account does record community claims about ancestry. Armenians of Kilvar claimed descent from mediaeval migrants from Edessa, identified in the source with present-day Şanlıurfa, Turkey. About 50 families who founded Edissia named the village after Edessa because they believed their ancestors had come from there.

## Language & religion

Armeno-Tats are Christian Tat-speaking Armenians. Most scholars researching the Tat language, including Boris Miller, consider them ethnic Armenians who underwent a language shift and adopted Tat as their first language. Armeno-Tats of Madrasa and Kilvar called their language *p'arseren*, while migrants to the North Caucasus and Astrakhan called it *keghetseren*. Boris Miller likened the Armeno-Tat dialect to central varieties of Muslim Tat, with which it was mutually intelligible, rather than to Judæo-Tat. Residents of Balakhany and Surakhany, described as speakers of southern Muslim Tat, also reported ease in understanding Armeno-Tat.

The Christian dialect displays typical Tat rhotacism, described as the mutation of Persian /d/ into /r/, and differs from other Tat dialects in lacking the pharyngeal consonants /ʕ/ and /ħ/. Armeno-Tats of Kilvar were often bilingual in Tat and Azeri and historically used Azeri to communicate with Armenian-speaking Armenians as late as 1912. Public education in the early twentieth century led them to acquire Armenian, which they used for communication with outsider Armenians or as a written language. This process intensified in the Soviet times, producing an almost complete shift from Tat to Armenian by the late 1980s.

Today the Armeno-Tat dialect is considered nearly extinct, with most Armeno-Tats having switched to Armenian and Russian. In 2002, only 36 Armenians in Russia spoke Tat as a first or second language. The number of speakers in Armenia is unknown, and all are over 50.
