## Overview

Azerbaijanis, also called Azeris or Azerbaijani Turks, are a Turkic ethnic group living mainly in the Azerbaijan region of northwestern Iran and the Republic of Azerbaijan. They are the largest ethnic group in the Republic of Azerbaijan and the second-largest ethnic group in neighboring Iran and Georgia. Between 12 and 23 million Azerbaijanis live in Iran, mainly in the northwestern provinces, and approximately 9.1 million live in the Republic of Azerbaijan. A diaspora of over a million is spread throughout the rest of the world. They speak Azerbaijani, an Oghuz language of the Turkic language family, and predominantly practice Shia Islam. Their history includes the Turkification of earlier local populations, the Safavid period, Russian imperial rule, and the formation of modern Azerbaijani national identity.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources identify the *dastan* as an epic-poetry form through which the emerging dominance of the Turkic language was chronicled. The oldest named example is the *Book of Dede Korkut*, whose allegorical tales concern the early Turks in the Caucasus and Asia Minor. The sources also mention Azerbaijani-language newspapers and literary-cultural activity. In 1880, the expression “Azerbaijani nation” appeared in the newspaper *Kashkul* in reference to those known as Tatars of the Caucasus. Articles in *Kaspiy* and *Kashkül* in 1891 are described as early expressions of a cultural Azerbaijani identity.

## Language & religion

Azerbaijani belongs to the Oghuz branch of the Turkic languages. The sources state that ancient residents of the area, known as Azaris, spoke Old Azeri, an Iranian-branch Indo-European language, and that Oghuz Turkic tribes began moving across the Iranian Plateau into the Caucasus and Anatolia in the 11th century AD. Turkic settlement and the gradual Turkification of the local population continued through the Mongol and Safavid periods. The Old Azeri language gradually lost ground and was widely replaced by Turkic speech.

Azerbaijanis predominantly practice Shia Islam. The sources describe the Safavids as encouraging and spreading Shi'a Islam, while also recording earlier Christianity among Caucasian Albanians and the prominence of Zoroastrianism throughout the Caucasus before Christianity and Islam.
