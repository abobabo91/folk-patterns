## Overview

The Tat people, also called Transcaucasian Persians, Tat, Parsi, Daghli and Lohijon, are an Iranian people living in Azerbaijan and Russia, mainly Southern Dagestan. They are part of the indigenous peoples of Iranian origin in the Caucasus. Tats use Tat, a southwestern Iranian language somewhat different from standard Persian, as well as Azerbaijani and Russian. They are mainly Shia Muslims, with a significant Sunni Muslim minority. The sources give several population figures rather than one current total: the 1979 Soviet census counted 22,441 Tats, while investigations in 2005 indicated 15,553 Tats in villages of the Republic of Azerbaijan. Their folk culture includes carpet-making, hand-weaving, metal manufacture, embossing, incrustation, ornamental design, miniature, spoken folk art, farming and distinctive domestic architecture.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Carpet-making and hand-weaving** — The sources state that carpet-making and hand-weaving are highly developed among the Persians of the South Caucasus.

**Metal manufacture, embossing and incrustation** — These arts are described as highly developed.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional Tat female clothing consists of a long shirt, wide trousers worn outside, a slim line dress, an outer unbuttoned dress, a headscarf and “Moroccan” stockings. Traditional male clothing consists of a Circassian coat and a high fur-cap. The sources do not distinguish everyday clothing from ceremonial clothing, or describe belts, footwear, jewelry, hair practices or separate dress for particular life-cycle events.

### Architecture

Traditional Tat houses are one- or two-storeyed buildings made of rectangular limestone blocks or river shingles. They have blank walls facing the street and a flat roof with an opening for a stone fireplace chimney. The upper floor is used for habitation, while living areas where the family gets together, including the kitchen, are situated on the ground floor. A living-room wall typically has several niches for storing clothes, bed linens and sometimes crockery. Rooms are illuminated by lamps or by a skylight through an opening in the roof.

A property usually has a walled or fenced yard and almost always includes a garden. Other features include a veranda, or *eyvan*, a paved drain or small basin, or *tənu*, a covered cattle-pan, stable and hen-house. Fireplaces, braziers and ovens are used for heating in winter and cooking throughout the year.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources mention metal manufacture, embossing and incrustation, together with crockery stored in household niches.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention ornamental design and miniature as popular arts.

## Music & performance

Tat spoken folk art has a rich tradition. Genres of national poetry including *ruba’is*, *ghazals* and *bayts* are highly developed.

The sources state that the long coexistence of Tats and Azerbaijanis produced common features in farming, housekeeping and culture. They also note that literature, theatre and music created by Mountain Jews during the Soviet period was sometimes attributed to the Tats because the term “Tat” was used in research literature as a second or first name for Mountain Jews. The sources distinguish the two groups and state that speakers of Mountain-Jew dialect and Tat are representatives of two different nations, each with its own religion, ethnic consciousness, self-designation, way of life, material and spiritual values.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources state that the Persians were originally Zoroastrian and that Islam became widespread after the conquest by the Rashidun Caliphate. Today, Tats are mainly Shia Muslim, with a sizeable Sunni Muslim minority.

## Foodways

Tat traditional occupations include agriculture, vegetable-growing, gardening and cattle-breeding. The main crops are barley, rye, wheat, millet, sunflower, maize, potatoes and peas. Large vineyards and fruit gardens are widespread. Sheep, cows, horses, donkeys, buffalos and rarely camels are kept as domestic cattle.

The sources describe household ovens, fireplaces and braziers used for cooking year-round.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tat spoken folk art includes *ruba’is*, *ghazals* and *bayts*. Distinctive features of the Tat language have been revealed through the study of the works of Persian medieval poets of the South Caucasus, including Khaqani and Nizami Ganjavi.

On December 14, 1990, the Azeri cultural and educational society for studying and development of Tati language, history and ethnography was founded by the board of the Ministry of Justice of the Azerbaijan SSR. A primer and textbook of the Tat language, together with literary and folklore pieces, were published.

## Language & religion

Tat is a southwestern Iranian language somewhat different from standard Persian. The Tat language was widely spread in the eastern portion of the South Caucasus and was also used up to the 20th century by non-Muslim groups, including Mountain Jews, part of the Armenians and the Udins. The article on Tat language distinguishes Tat language in the Caucasus, in Dagestan and Azerbaijan, from Tati in Iran, a group of Northwestern Iranian dialects.

The majority of the Tat population of Azerbaijan and southern Dagestan uses the Turkic exonym *Tati* or *Tat* as a self-designation. Tats of Absheron use *Parsi* as a self-designation and *zuvan Parsi* for the Tat language. Citizens of Lahij in the Ismailli district call themselves *Lohijon*, after their village *Lohuj*. Tats in Khizi district and parts of Devechi and Siyazan districts use *Daghli*, meaning mountaineers.

Originally Zoroastrian, the Persians became widely Muslim after the Rashidun Caliphate conquest. Tats today are mainly Shia Muslim, with a significant Sunni Muslim minority.
