## Overview

Meskhetian Turks, also referred to as Turkish Meskhetians, Ahiska Turks, and Turkish Ahiskans, are a Turkish ethnic subgroup historically associated with the Meskheti region of Georgia, along the border with Turkey. Their presence in Meskheti began with an Ottoman military expedition in 1578, although Turkic tribes had settled there as early as the eleventh and twelfth centuries. Today they are widely dispersed throughout the former Soviet Union, as well as in Turkey and the United States, following forced deportations during World War II. Approximately 115,000 were deported to Central Asia in 1944. The group speaks an Eastern Anatolian dialect of Turkish and includes Sunni Muslims and a minority of Shiite Muslims.

## Music & performance

The sources describe music in connection with life-cycle ceremonies. The religious male circumcision ceremony of the Ahiska Turks involves dance, music, guests, recitation of the Koran, and a special *Kirve (Sandek)*. Music is therefore documented as part of a communal religious ceremony.

The sources also describe wedding performance. At the end of the wedding, a dance follows, with men and women dancing separately. The newlyweds then perform their last dance, called the *Waltz*, which completes the wedding. No further information is provided about vocal music, instrumental music, or professional musicians.

## Dance & theatre

Meskhetian Turks’ weddings include gender-separated dancing. After the wedding ceremony, men and women dance separately, followed by the newlyweds’ final dance, called the *Waltz*. Dance and music are also part of the religious male circumcision ceremony of the Ahiska Turks.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document wedding and circumcision rites.

A Meskhetian Turk wedding begins with a traditional proposal from the groom’s parents. If the bride’s parents accept, an engagement party called *Nişan* is held, and everyone at the Nişan receives a ceremonial sweet drink called *Sharbat*. The wedding itself lasts two days. On the first day the bride leaves her house; on the second day the marriage takes place. Before entering her husband’s house, she breaks two plates with the heel of her shoe and applies honey to the doorway. The sources state that this practice expresses a wish for happiness for the newly married couple. The wedding concludes with dancing and the newlyweds’ final *Waltz*.

The religious male circumcision ceremony of the Ahiska Turks includes dance, music, guests, recitation of the Koran, and a special *Kirve (Sandek)*.

The sources mention the Turkish language and identify several writers, journalists, poets, linguists, literary critics, and educators of Meskhetian Turk origin, including Ali Fuat Azgur, Ömer Faik Nemanzade, Muharrem Ergin, Adil Efendiyev, Şefika Şeyhzade-Efendizade, and Asif Hacılı.

## Language & religion

The Meskhetian Turks speak an Eastern Anatolian dialect of Turkish associated with the regions of Kars, Ardahan, Igdir, and Artvin. The dialect has borrowed from Azerbaijani, Georgian, Kazakh, Kyrgyz, Russian, and Uzbek, languages with which Meskhetian Turks came into contact during Russian and Soviet rule. It is commonly written using the Cyrillic script.

Most Meskhetian Turks are Sunni Muslims, while a minority are Shiite Muslims. The sources describe the group’s traditional culture as similar to Turkish culture while also containing some Georgian elements. They also report that the Meskhetian Turks’ religious male circumcision ceremony includes recitation of the Koran, guests, music, dance, and a special *Kirve (Sandek)*.

The group is known by several names. These include Meskhetian Turks, Turkish Meskhetians, Ahiska Turks, and Turkish Ahiskans. In Turkish, one form is *Ahıska Türkleri*. The majority of Meskhetian Turks call themselves “Turks” or “Ahiskan Turks,” referring to the Ahiska Region. The term “Meskhetian” came into use in the late 1950s, while the community had earlier been referred to simply as “Turks” in Soviet documents.
