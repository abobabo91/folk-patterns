## Overview

The Akhvakhs, also known as Akhwakh, Akhvakhtsy or G'akhevalal, are one of the Andi–Dido peoples of Dagestan and have their own language. They call themselves Atluatii or Ashvado. They live in the Akhvakhsky District of Dagestan between the Avar and Andi Rivers, and also in Zagatala, Azerbaijan, where Akhakhdere is the only village in which Akhvakh people live. In 1926 they numbered 3,683; by the early 1990s, about 8,000 people were estimated to be Akhvakh, including people fully assimilated as Avars who still recognized Akhvakh ancestry. The Akhvakh language belongs to the Avar–Andic branch of the Northeast Caucasian languages. The sources document their language, religious history and assimilation.

## Festivals & rituals

The Akhvakhs are mainly Sunni Muslims. The sources state that they adopted Islam by the sixteenth century through the influence of Sufi missionaries.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that a few publications have appeared in the Akhvakh language, including the newspaper *Zaman*, meaning “Time,” published since the early 1930s, and the newspaper Ахвахцы — Ашвадо, published since the 2000s.

## Language & religion

Akhvakh is a Northeast Caucasian language of the Avar–Andic branch. It is also spelled Axvax or Akhwakh and is described as the most divergent of the Andic languages. The sources give differing speaker figures: Ethnologue lists 210 speakers based on the 2010 census; Magomedova and Abulaeva list 20,000 speakers; and the 2021 Russian census gives 7,521 speakers in Russia. Akhvakh-Dere in Zagatala District, Azerbaijan, is said to have between 1,000 and 2,000 speakers. The language has Northern and Southern varieties; the Southern varieties listed are Tlyanub, Tsegob, Cegob and Ratlub, although sources differ in their classifications. An alphabet was devised in the 2000s, while many speakers have adopted Avar as their literary language. Akhvakh has a five-vowel system with distinctive vowel length, three agreement classes in the singular, and an ergative-absolutive case-marking system with twenty cases. The Akhvakhs are mainly Sunni Muslims and adopted Islam by the sixteenth century through Sufi missionaries.
