## Overview

The Karachays, also called Karachais, are a North Caucasian–Turkic ethnic group whose ancestral lands lie primarily in the Karachay–Cherkess Republic of Russia, in the North Caucasus. Together with the Balkars, they occupy valleys and foothills of the Central Caucasus, including the river valleys of the Kuban, Big Zelenchuk River, Malka, Baksan, and Cherek. Their language belongs to the Kipchak branch of the Turkic family and comes from the northwestern branch of Turkic languages. Karachay communities are divided into families and clans called *tukums*, with roughly thirty-two named in the source. Their cultural history reflects interaction among Caucasian, Iranian, and Turkic peoples, while their relative mountain isolation supported distinctive cultural practices.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Karachay settlement architecture is not described in the sources used. The sources mention the *aul* of Kart-Dzhurt and the towns of Mikoyan-Shahar, Kislovodsk, and Batalpashinsk, but provide no architectural description.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state only that, after annexation to the Russian Empire, Karachay soldiers were taken from Karachai Amanat, pledged an oath of loyalty, and were assigned arms.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document religious and social practices in historical contexts: interactions with neighboring Muslim peoples took place according to folk customs and Sharia law, and the majority of Karachays are followers of Islam. The sources also state that some Karachays began adopting Islam in the 17th and 18th centuries through contact with the Kumyks, Nogais, the Crimean Tatars, and, most significantly, the Circassians. No life-cycle rites, wedding customs, funeral practices, or named festivals are described.

The history of the Karachays includes several politically significant dates and collective events. The Battle of Khasauka took place on October 20, 1828. The Karachay Uprising of 1855 formed part of a broader North Caucasian rebellion against Russian expansion. The Karachay Uprising of 1930 began in March and was suppressed by April. In 1942, the Germans permitted the establishment of a Karachay National Committee, while the deportation of the Karachays followed the return of Russian control in November 1943. These events are political and historical episodes rather than festivals or ritual observances.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do describe historical interpretations of Karachay origins and ethnogenesis, including debates among scientists, historians, ethnographers, archaeologists, and ethnolinguists. Ismail Miziev is identified as a Balkar historian, ethnographer, and archaeologist specializing in North Caucasian studies, while Ufuk Tavkul is identified as an Ankara University professor and specialist. The sources also refer to the preservation of Karachay culture through the American Karachay-Kavkaz Benevolent Association, which advocates for the preservation of the Karachay people’s culture.

## Language & religion

Karachays speak Karachay-Balkar, a language of the northwestern branch of the Turkic languages and the Kipchak branch of the Turkic family. The Kumyks of northeast Dagestan speak the closely related Kumyk language.

The majority of Karachays are followers of Islam. Some began adopting Islam in the 17th and 18th centuries through contact with the Kumyks, Nogais, the Crimean Tatars, and especially the Circassians. The Sufi Qadiriya order has a presence in the region. Historical interactions with neighboring Muslim peoples were based on both folk customs and Sharia law.

The sources present Karachay identity within a wider North Caucasian setting. Karachays and Balkars are described as having a common origin, culture, and language. Their history is connected in the sources with Caucasian and Iranian-speaking peoples, including Ossetians, as well as with Turkic groups such as the Bulgars, Khazars, and Kipchaks. The sources also state that Karachays and Balkars have a connection to the prehistoric Koban culture.
