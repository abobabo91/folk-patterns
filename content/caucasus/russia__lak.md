## Overview

The Laks, also known as Gazikumuks, are a Northeast Caucasian ethnic group native to Lakia, an inland region within Dagestan in the North Caucasus. They historically lived in the Lakskiy and Kulinskiy districts of Dagestan, and there are about 200,000 ethnic Laks in the world. Their native language is Lak, a Northeast Caucasian language forming its own branch within the family. Kumukh was historically a fortress-city, the residence of the rulers of the Lak people, and the ancient capital of Lakia. Lak society has included mountain pastoralism, agriculture, artisanry, crafts, clan organization, Islamic institutions, and a distinctive system of free societies and feudal classes.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Rug weaving** — Lak women engaged in cottage industries including rug weaving, spinning, and textile making.

**Textile making** — Textile making was among the cottage industries associated with Lak women.

**Leather working** — Leather working was associated with Lak men, and leather working and shoe making remained dominant industries in the region.

**Shoe making** — Lak villages specialized in crafts, and the sources state that shoe and boot making was associated especially with Shovkra.

**Spinning** — Spinning formed part of the cottage industries in which Lak women engaged.

**Ceramics** — Ceramics were among the cottage industries associated with Lak women, while Balkar was known for ceramics and jug makers.

**Textiles and clothing production** — Textiles and clothing remained among the dominant industries of the Lak region.

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that textiles and clothing were among the dominant industries of the Lak region, and that Lak women engaged in textile making while Lak men engaged in leather working and shoe making.

### Architecture

The sources describe Lak territory as mountainous, very dry, isolated, and lacking forests, with a chronic shortage of wood for building and fuel. Kumukh is identified as a fortress-city and the ancient capital of Lakia, but its architecture is not described.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Kumukh was famed for its jewelers and coppersmiths, while Balkar was known for ceramics and jug makers. Ubra was known for masons and tinsmiths, and Unchukatl for saddle and harness makers. The sources also mention leather working and tool making.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Kumukh was famed for its jewelers.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe religious and life-cycle institutions. Most Laks believe in Sunni Islam of the Shafi'i school. Sunni Islam is described as encouraging group solidarity through assistance with work and housing, marriage arrangements, payment of the kalim, maintenance of burial societies, and dispute resolution. The first mosque of the Lak people was built in 777–778 in Kumukh. Shamkhals of Kumukh embraced Islam at the end of the 13th century, and Kumukh became a gazi-center in the 14th century.

## Foodways

Traditional Lak economy and diet were shaped by mountainous and dry lands. Meat and milk products were major components of the Lak diet, while barley, peas, wheat, and some potatoes were also grown. Wheat, fruits, and vegetables were cultivated in lower areas, especially in the new Lak areas in northern Dagestan. Sheep and goats were important, with some horses, cattle, and mules also raised. The sources mention the production of meat, cheese, and butter, and identify Kuma as a village known for candy makers.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do preserve historical statements about Lak self-designations and expressions, including *zhu Lak buru* (“we are Lak”), *zhu Lakral khalq buru* (“we are Lak people”), and *Lakku maz* (“Lakian language”). These expressions are presented as evidence concerning ethnonym, language, and place names rather than as an oral-literary tradition.

## Language & religion

Lak is the native language of the Laks and belongs to the Northeast Caucasian language family, forming its own branch. It is spoken by about 157,000 people and is one of six official languages in Dagestan. Its dialects are Kumukh, Vitskhi, Arakul, Balkhar, Shadni, Shalib, Vikhli, Kuli, and Kaya; the standard language is based on the dialect of Kumukh. Lak was written using the Arabic script until 1928, with a Latin alphabet used for ten years, and has been written in Cyrillic since 1938. Most Laks believe in Sunni Islam of the Shafi'i school. The sources describe Islam’s development in Kumukh, including the mosque built in 777–778 and the later role of Kumukh as a gazi-center.
