## Overview

The Ryukyuans are a Japonic-speaking East Asian ethnic group indigenous to the Ryukyu Islands, which extend from Kyushu to Taiwan. In Japan, most live in Okinawa Prefecture or Kagoshima Prefecture, including the Amami Islands; Okinawa Prefecture alone has more than 1.4 million residents identified in the source. They speak the Ryukyuan languages, a branch of the Japonic language family alongside Japanese and its dialects. The group has a distinct culture with matriarchal elements, an indigenous religion, and a cuisine in which rice was introduced comparatively late. Their history includes the Ryukyu Kingdom, maritime trade and tributary relations with Ming China, political annexation by Japan, and the suppression of Ryukyuan ethnic identity, tradition, culture, and language during the Meiji era.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources mention *gusuku*, castle-like fortifications built on higher ground during the Gusuku period. These structures became centers of power as political authority moved inland, but the supplied material does not describe their construction materials, roofs, decoration, internal arrangement, or domestic architecture. The source also refers to Shuri as the royal capital of the Ryukyuan Kingdom and to developed castle towns and harbor facilities in the Chūzan polity.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Archaeological material described in the sources includes Jōmon pottery, Yayoi-type pottery, metal tools, and ancient Chinese knife money found near Naha. The Southern Ryukyus had a Shimotabaru period characterized by a specific style of pottery, followed by an Aceramic period during which no pottery was produced in that region. The sources also record the importation of ceramics, copper, and iron from China, and the use of weapons, grains, and cloth in Eiso’s taxation system.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They do mention tattooing among the people of the eastern islands in records dating from 57 CE, and state that this practice survived among Okinawan women, Ainu in Hokkaido, and Atayal people in Taiwan.

## Music & performance

They do, however, describe the social importance of Ryukyuan *Noro* priestesses, who were closely associated with local political power until the 20th century. The sources also describe women sorceresses as having special influence in the political-social institutions of western and southern Japan and Okinawa until the 2nd century CE. These references concern religious and political authority rather than documented musical performance.

## Festivals & rituals

They describe an indigenous religion and the political-religious significance of *Noro* priestesses, who were associated with local political power until the 20th century. The sources also describe a tradition in which the founder of the Tenson dynasty was descended from the goddess Amamikyu, although they state that this tradition appears in later royal tales influenced by Chinese and Japanese political interests. The Tenson dynasty was said in those tales to have ruled for 17,000 years and to have had 25 kings or chieftains. The supplied material does not describe birth, coming-of-age, wedding, funeral, or other life-cycle rites.

## Foodways

The sources state that Ryukyuans have a distinct cuisine and that rice was introduced comparatively late, becoming widely cultivated only before the 12th century CE. During the Jōmon period, communities in the Northern Ryukyus lived in a hunter-gatherer society; later archaeological evidence indicates engagement in fishery. The sources also describe a Ryukyuan swine-economy culture that continued until World War II. In the Gusuku period, rice, wheat, and millet were introduced alongside overseas trade in agricultural goods. Ryukyu’s maritime commerce involved horses, sulphur, seashells, ceramics, copper, iron, tin, ivory, spices including pepper, and sappanwood. The supplied material does not name specific dishes, cooking methods, beverages, ceremonial foods, or dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe later royal tales from the 17th century that attempted to explain the divine right of local royal authority and reflected Chinese and Japanese influence. These tales include the Tenson dynasty, the goddess Amamikyu, and the claim that the dynasty ruled for 17,000 years. They also relate the career of Shunten, who ruled from 1187 to 1237, and whose parental origin became the subject of competing traditions involving a local Okinawan chief’s daughter and a Japanese adventurer usually identified in the source as Minamoto no Tametomo. The supplied material does not name a Ryukyuan epic, proverb, riddle tradition, or modern literary preservation project.

## Language & religion

Ryukyuans speak the Ryukyuan languages, which belong to the Japonic language family alongside Japanese and its dialects. In English they are also called Okinawans or Lewchewans. “Ryukyuan” derives from Ryūkyū, the Japanese pronunciation of the Chinese name Liuqiu; in the Okinawan language it is pronounced Ruuchuu. In the indigenous language of the main island, people refer to themselves and their identity as Okinawan or Uchinaanchu, while another endonym is Shimanchu. These terms are described as politicized markers of a distinct culture. The sources describe an indigenous religion with matriarchal elements and identify Noro priestesses as closely connected with local political power. Buddhism was introduced into Okinawa during the reign of Eiso.
