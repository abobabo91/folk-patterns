## Overview

The Tubalars are an ethnic subgroup of the Altaians native to the Altai Republic in Russia. According to the 2010 census, there were 1,965 Tubalars in Russia. Their villages include Artybash, Iogach, Novotroitsk, Tuloi, Tondoshka, Kebezen, Ust-Pyzha, Biyka, Yailu, Chuyka, Torochak, Paspaul, Salganda, Karakoksha, Tunzha, Krasnoselskoye, Uskuch, Uimen, and Karasuk. Tubalar emerged from the merging of Turkic tribes with Ket, Samoyedic, and other native Siberian groups. Their language, Tuba-Kiji or Tubalar, is a Turkic language classified within the Siberian Turkic languages and considered a Northern Altai dialect.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Traditional Tubalar dress included short breeches, linen shirts, and single-breasted robes. The sources do not distinguish men’s and women’s clothing, ceremonial dress, head coverings, belts, footwear, or other forms of body adornment.

### Architecture

Traditional Tubalar dwellings included polygonal yurts made from bark or logs and topped with a conic bark roof. Other dwellings included conic yurts made from bark or perches.

## Festivals & rituals

The sacred tree of the Tubalars is the cedar, which symbolizes the power, beauty, and courage of the taiga. The Holiday of Cedar is a celebration of this tree.

## Foodways

The Tubalars were originally hunters, and animals living in the taiga were vital to the local subsistence economy. Around the 19th century, they took up picking cedar nuts as an additional economic activity.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that there has been a recent push by the Tubalars to conserve their culture and language.

## Language & religion

Tuba-Kiji, Tubalar, or Tuba is a Turkic language spoken in the Altai Republic in Russia by the Tuba-Kiji, who are also called Tubalars. The language is classified in the Siberian Turkic languages and is considered a Northern Altai dialect, although this classification is disputed and it may be a Kipchak language. Tubalars who know Tubalar can also understand Chelkan and Kumandin. A Russian-Tubalar phrasebook was published in 2010. Most Tubalars are Orthodox Christian, while a significant minority still practice shamanism.
