## Overview

The Tofalar, also known as Karagas or Tofa, are a Turkic people living in Tofalariya, in the southwestern part of Nizhneudinsky District, Irkutsk Oblast of Russia, near the Eastern Sayan mountain range. According to the 2010 census, there were 762 Tofalar in Russia. Their population is highly mixed with Russians because of settlement and intermarriage. Historically, the Tofalar were nomadic hunters and reindeer herders in the taiga, but Soviet policies resettled them into permanent settlements and discouraged hunting and shamanism. Their culture combines Ket (Yeniseian), Samoyedic, Turkic, and Mongol influences. Hunting, fishing, reindeer transport, conical tents, music, wrestling, archery, horse racing, and the endangered Tofa language are central subjects documented in the sources.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state that reindeer were used for clothing, and that winter *chum* tents were made from animal hide. Before Soviet rule, Tofalar traders acquired treated sheepskin, felt saddlecloths, harnesses, saddles of Buryat and Mongol manufacture, and diverse textiles and ornaments.

### Architecture

The traditional Tofalar dwelling was the conical tent called a *chum*. It was made of animal hide in winter and birch bark, called *polotnishch*, in summer. Historically, nomadic households moved their tents between migration routes, and women were responsible for taking down and reassembling the tents. Today, the Tofalar primarily live in modern timber houses, while traditional tents continue to be used as storage.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention hunting knives, axes, rifles, harnesses, saddles, and wooden tools and objects. Men traditionally created various tools and objects from wood. Reindeer were used for transportation, clothing, shelter, and food, while hunting equipment and household goods were obtained partly through trade with Russians, Buryats, and Mongol traders.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They mention that Tofalar traders historically acquired diverse textiles and ornaments through exchange with Russians, Buryats, and Mongol traders.

## Music & performance

Traditional Tofalar musical instruments include the *chadygan*, a stringed instrument compared in the source to the gusli, and the *charty-hobus*, described as similar to a balalaika. Music was played to accompany songs and dances at festivals.

Music and dance were part of wedding feasts. The wedding typically lasted three days and included special rituals, songs, and dances. They state that the Tofalar continued to adhere to shamanism after conversion to Christianity.

## Dance & theatre

They state that songs and dances accompanied festivals and wedding feasts. The Tofalar also practice wrestling, archery, and horse racing. These activities are described as cultural and recreational practices.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that music, songs, and dances accompanied festivals and that weddings were marked by a three-day feast with special rituals, songs, and dances. Marriage was exogamous and followed preliminary courtship, agreement between the parents, and payment of bride-price to the father of the bride. After the wedding, the groom took his wife to a separate nomad camp, where they established an independent family unit.

The sources describe funerary beliefs and rites. The Tofalar believed that the dead went to the Kingdom of Erlik. The deceased were buried with personal belongings because these were thought to be needed in the next life. Since everything in Erlik was believed to be “the wrong way around,” objects accompanying the dead had to be damaged. The death ritual was strongly influenced by Christianity, and the Tofalar marked the ninth day, fortieth day, sixth month, and first year after the death of a relative.

## Foodways

Traditional Tofalar subsistence centered on reindeer husbandry, trapping, and hunting. Reindeer milk was drunk and used to make cheese and curdled milk. Meat was cured for winter provisions, and reindeer milk was dried. The Tofalar hunted and ate deer, bear, waterfowl, and fish from autumn ponds during the spawning season. They also gathered dried tubers called *saran*, wild onions, and pine and cedar nuts.

Historically, sables, ermine, Siberian polecats, and squirrels were hunted for their fur. Flour, groats, salt, sugar, tea, tobacco, and alcohol were purchased from traders in exchange for furs. During the Soviet era, many Tofalar worked on state farms, while today hunting and fishing remain primary economic activities.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that the Tofa language had no written form until 1989, when the linguist Valentin Rassadin created a writing system based on the Cyrillic alphabet. In the 1990s, Tofalar activists successfully campaigned for local schools to teach children in Tofa.

The sources describe the Tofa language as critically endangered. Estimates for speakers range from 67 to fewer than 40 individuals, while approximately 40 speakers of varying fluency were reported in 2002.

## Language & religion

Tofa, also known as Tofalar or Karagas, is a Turkic language spoken in Irkutsk Oblast by the Tofalar. It forms a dialect continuum with the closely related Tuvan language and is especially close to the language of Tozhu Tuvans and Soyots. The Tofa language has two dialects, Alygdzher and Gutar, and contains hundreds of loanwords from medieval Mongolian and Russian and dozens from Buryat.

Tofa uses a Cyrillic alphabet. Its writing system was created in 1989 by Valentin Rassadin; an earlier version dates from 1988. The Tofa language is critically endangered. The Tofalar converted to Christianity early, but the conversion was largely nominal and they continued to adhere to shamanism. Shamans remained among the Tofalar until their suppression by the Soviet state in 1930.
