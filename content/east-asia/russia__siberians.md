## Overview

Siberians, also called Siberian Tatars, are an indigenous Turkic-speaking population of the forests and steppes of southern Western Siberia, in Russia, in a territory extending from somewhat east of the Ural Mountains to the Yenisey River. They call themselves *Yerle Qalıq*, meaning “older inhabitants,” distinguishing themselves from more recent Volga Tatar immigrants. The 2010 census counted more than 500,000 people in Siberia defining their ethnicity as “Tatar,” while about 200,000 were considered indigenous Siberian Tatars; only 6,779 called themselves “Siberian Tatars.” The group comprises three autochthonous groups: Tobol-Irtysh Tatars, Baraba Tatars, and Tom Tatars. Their folk-culture record includes regional dialects, hunting, horse raising, porterage, foods, clothing, community institutions, Sunni Islam, and surviving shamanistic influences.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Ancient Siberian Tatar outerwear is described as similar in cut and color to Central Asian and Sayan-Altai clothing, with a Uyghur-Chinese lapel. Women’s dresses are described as similar to Bashkir dresses, including several rows of frills along the hem. Costumes from the early 20th century and later were influenced by Volga-Ural Tatar clothing.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Traditional occupations included hunting, raising horses, and porterage, while some Siberian Tatars later worked in tanneries and sawmills.

## Music & performance

The sources used mention a Siberian Tatar song performed by the Naza ensemble and a song in the Siberian Tatar language.

## Festivals & rituals

Siberian Tatars profess Sunni Islam, and the sources state that shamanistic influences remain in certain funerary and spiritual customs. The role of the *tugum*, a group of related families originating from one ancestor, included regulating family, household, and domestic relations and performing religious and folk rituals. The sources also describe the religious community as regulating certain relations in the wider community.

## Foodways

Siberian Tatar cuisine includes barley, *kattama*, *boortsog*, noodles, and *peremech*, together with dairy products such as *kaymak* and *qurut*. Traditional occupations included hunting, raising horses, and porterage.

## Oral tradition & literature

N. F. Katanov studied Siberian Tatar legends and recorded a tradition in which the army of Kuchum Khan was divided into four wings: Kordak, Turaly, Ayaly, and Baraba. The sources also mention a Siberian Tatar song and materials concerning the customs and traditions of the Siberian Tatars of Vagaysky district.

## Language & religion

Siberian Tatar is a Turkic language with distinct Tobol-Irtysh, Baraba, and Tom dialects. It has been classified in the Kipchak–Nogay and Kipchak–Kyrgyz groups, with Upper Altaian elements, Karluk influences, and influence from Kipchak-Bulgar among communities living in ethnically mixed villages. Siberian Tatars used an alphabet based on Arabic script until the 1920s, adopted Latin script in 1928, and adopted Cyrillic script in 1939. A Siberian Tatar language classification code, “sty,” was awarded in 2013. The group is Sunni Muslim and follows the Hanafi madhhab; Islamization first occurred around the 14th century, adoption began by the early 15th century, and most were Muslim by the late 18th century. Before conversion, Siberian Tatars practiced shamanism.
