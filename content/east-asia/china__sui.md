## Overview

The Sui people, also called Shui people, are an ethnic group living mostly in Guizhou Province, China. They are one of the 56 ethnic groups officially recognized by the People’s Republic of China. The Sui are descended from the ancient Baiyue peoples, who inhabited southern China before the Han dynasty, and the name “Sui,” meaning “water” in Chinese, was adopted during the Ming Dynasty. Today, 93% of Sui people—322,000 individuals—reside in Guizhou, with 63% living in Sandu Shui Autonomous County. Other communities live in Guangxi, Yunnan, and northern Vietnam. They speak a Kam–Sui language belonging to the Kra–Dai language family. Their folk culture includes distinctive houses, a lunar calendar, elaborate funeral ceremonies, communal foodways, festivals, oral literature, ancestor worship, and the ritual use of the Sui script.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no Sui textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

They do record several practices concerning women’s hair and funerary belongings. A widowed woman covers her hair with a fabric of white color for three years. The sources also state that, after an elder’s death, the deceased person’s clothing and personal items must not fall to the ground, and that the deceased must have an odd number of belongings. No specific garment types, belts, footwear, men’s dress, or ceremonial costume are documented.

### Architecture

Traditional Sui houses are usually made of fir or pine, although houses are increasingly made with bricks. Three principal forms are described. The *ɣaan2 faaŋ1*, called ganlan mulou in Chinese, is a stilted house with two or three stories. The second floor contains the living quarters, while the first floor is used primarily as a stable and storage area.

The *ɣaan2 hum5* is a one-story ground house. A third form is the split-level house, called diaojiaolou in Chinese and described as a “hanging foot” building. It is built on a hillside, with longer pillars supporting the downhill-facing side. It is also called a “hanging house,” or diaojiao, because the supporting pillars are sometimes located outside the walls. Villages usually have a few hundred inhabitants, most of whom share the same family name.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do mention the bronze drum, which is often played during festivals, and the bamboo steamer used to prepare rice. The sources also describe the Sui script as a logographic writing system with pictographic characters, traditionally used by shamans for geomancy and divination rather than for ordinary daily activities.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They record the practice of widows covering their hair with white fabric for three years.

## Music & performance

Sui oral literature includes myths, songs, and folk tales, and the sources list several song categories. These include life-related songs such as the song of creating cotton, the song of creating grain, the song of making wine, the song of planting trees, and the song of suffering. Custom-related songs include the song of the Duan festival and the song of mourning. The sources also name love songs such as The girl lovely as brocade, It is hard to miss you, and Sister will go with the brother together.

Antiphonal choirs are important in festival performance. On New Year’s Day during the Duan festival, antiphonal choirs, horse racing, and other festivities are held. During the Mao festival, antiphonal-style choirs sing traditional Sui love songs. Married women are traditionally not permitted to perform in these choirs. Singing and dancing are also listed among typical festival activities.

The bronze drum is often played during festivals. They also state that excerpts of Sui songs can be found in Fang-Kuei Li’s 1977 book Shuihua yanjiu, or Research on the Sui language.

## Dance & theatre

The sources state that dancing is typical of Sui festivals, alongside singing, bronze-drum playing, slaughtering livestock for food, and giving thanks to family ancestors.

## Festivals & rituals

The Sui possess a lunar calendar that begins in the ninth lunar month. The Duan festival, *tsjə1 twə3*, is a harvest festival similar to the Chinese New Year. It is held from around September, the beginning of the Sui New Year, to November, the second month of the Sui calendar. Livestock meat is not eaten on Sui New Year’s Eve, *hət7*. On New Year’s Day, *ʁaai3*, antiphonal choirs, horse racing, and other festivities take place. Because different Sui areas celebrate Duan at different times, festivities from different locations together last for more than two months.

Five groups of locations are given for Duan celebrations: Wangsi in Duyun (Pandong) City; Malian, Layou, Miaocao, and Shuidong in Sandu County; Tingpai, Hengfeng, Heyong, and Tianxing in Sandu, Libo, and Dushan Counties; Zhonghe, Dixiang, and Jiuqian in Sandu County; and Sandong, Shuinian, and Xingxiang in Sandu County.

The four-day Mao festival, *tsjə1 mau4*, is celebrated by people who do not celebrate Duan. It occurs after rice seedlings are transplanted, during the tenth month of the Sui calendar. Mao Day is celebrated in the Libo County villages of Shuili, Yaoqing, Shuirao, Shuipu, and Yongkang. The spring festival, *tsjə1 tseŋ1*, is especially elaborate in Bannan, Shuimei, Yanggong, Zhouqin, and Yang’an in Sandu County; these villages celebrate neither Duan nor Mao. Other named festivals include *su3 njen2 hi5*, the small Sui New Year’s festival; *tsjə1 ʔau4 hmai5*, the “eat new rice” festival; Liuyueliu, the “six month six day,” or June 6, festival; *si3 ming2*, the Qingming festival; Duanwu; and *sup8 hi5*.

Funeral services are elaborate and lengthy, with animal sacrifices carried out in honor of the dead. Except for fish, villagers usually refrain from eating meat after a person’s death. Sui religion includes ancestor worship, and festival observances include giving thanks to family ancestors. Numerous taboos govern death, marriage, pregnancy, burial, farming, and festival conduct.

## Foodways

Glutinous rice is the staple food of the Sui. Supplementary grains and tubers include corn, wheat, barley, millet, and sweet potatoes. Rice is steamed in a bamboo steamer or cooked in a covered pot over a low fire. Popular rice-based foods include *ʔjut7*, identified in Chinese as zongzi, and cooked glutinous rice with chrysanthemum and puffed rice. Sui women give glutinous rice to relatives when visiting them.

Fish is one of the most important food sources. Many Sui raise carp in village fishponds. A popular summer dish is *lu5 hum3*, a kind of sour broth. Sui families regularly hold communal hot pots. Kippered fish, *hum3 mom6*, kippered meat, *hum3 naan4*, and the meat of suckling pigs are also popular.

Rice spirits are consumed during marriages, funerals, festivals, and building-raising events, and the Sui are famed for their jiuqian wine. Dietary rules are connected with festivals and death. Livestock meat is not eaten on Sui New Year’s Eve, and land-animal meat is forbidden during specified periods of the Duan festival. Members of a clan also refrain from eating the meat of land animals after the death of one of their elders.

## Oral tradition & literature

Sui oral literature includes myths, songs, and folk tales. Ancient myths and songs concern the creation of heaven and earth, the origin of humanity, and the origin of new life, in which a brother and sister plant a pumpkin from which new life sprouts. The song of creating humans involves a fight among humans, dragons, and tigers.

Named legends include the song of Pan Xinjian, a rich magnate; the story of Jingui; a man named Niu; a poor teacher; and a stone horse who shot out the sun. Legends about customs include the origin of the Duan festival, in which a brother marries his younger sister, the origin of the Mao festival, and the planting of the fir upside down. Other legends concern the origins of the Duliujiang River and dinosaurs and Yueliang, or Moon, Mountain.

Folk tales include The origin of bronze drums, The grape girl, Why the tiger hates the buffalo and tiger, and The eye and the foot. Sayings and singing include The crane and the crow and The sparrow and the thrush.

## Language & religion

Sui is a Kam–Sui language of the Kra–Dai family. It is spoken mainly in Guizhou, where the language is divided into three dialects with minor differences: Sandong, spoken by 90% of Sui speakers; Yang’an; and Pandong. In Vietnam, Sui is spoken in Hồng Quang Village, Chiêm Hoá District, Tuyên Quang Province. The language has its own writing system, the Sui script, called *le1 sui3* in Sui and Shuishu in Chinese. It is a logographic system with pictographic characters, used traditionally by shamans for geomancy and divination. There are at least 500 Sui characters. Today, written Chinese is used for daily activities.

The Sui are mainly polytheists and also practice ancestor worship. Shamans were traditionally hired to perform prayers and sacrifices in the houses of people who were sick or close to death. Sui religion includes more than 900 ghosts and gods capable of causing good fortune or misfortune. Some deities and legendary figures are borrowed from Chinese folk religion.
