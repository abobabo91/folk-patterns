## Overview

The Tai Nua — also written Tai Nuea, Tai Neua, Tai Nüa or Dai Nua, and known as Dehong Dai or Chinese Shan — are one of the Tai peoples of Southeast Asia, concentrated in the borderlands where southwest China meets northeast Myanmar and spread onward into Laos, Thailand and Vietnam. Their settlements follow the Shweli and Salween river valleys; in China most live in the Dehong Dai and Jingpo Autonomous Prefecture of western Yunnan, particularly around Mangshi, Ruili and Lianghe County, where the population is estimated at roughly 650,000 as of 2026, with a further 100,000–150,000 speakers in Myanmar's Northern Shan State towns of Muse, Namkham and Kutkai. Their language belongs to the Tai family, and they matter in folk-culture terms above all for possessing their own script, the Tai Le or Dehong Dai script, and for a body of festival practice, theatre and weaving distinct from that of the Tai Lue of Sipsongpanna to the south.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The catalogue records behind this gallery document two kinds of cloth, both woven rather than printed or embroidered.

**Shoulder cloth** — a narrow woven cloth patterned with red and blue supplementary weft yarns, which the cataloguer describes as carrying typical Tai geometric patterning. The supplementary-weft technique, in which extra coloured wefts float above a plain ground weave, is what produces the banded ornament.

**Offering cloth** — cotton cloths made for presentation, held in the collection as a pair. The records give the material and the purpose but no further detail of weave, dye or motif.

Beyond these, the sources used here do not describe a named repertoire of Tai Nua pattern traditions, and no motif names are recorded in them.

### Clothing & dress

The sources used do not describe Tai Nua dress in any systematic way. From the collection records, the only garment or accessory documented besides the shoulder cloth is a hat made of straw. The shoulder cloth itself belongs to the shawl-and-wrap class of Tai dress, worn over the upper body rather than tailored, but the records do not say by whom, on what occasions, or how men's and women's wear differ. One dress-adjacent practice is documented in the festival material: at New Year, children present their elders with an offering of flowers when asking forgiveness, a gesture described in terms of the offering rather than of costume. For everyday versus ceremonial wardrobes, for head coverings other than the straw hat, and for belts and footwear, the sources consulted offer nothing.

### Architecture

The sources used for this profile do not cover Tai Nua house form, building materials, roof construction or architectural ornament. The only built structure they name is the Buddhist temple, which serves as the site of the final and largest day of the New Year festival, where monks give their blessing; nothing is said about its plan or decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Metalwork is represented in the collection by a **sword** made of metal and rattan — a blade with rattan used in its mounting or binding, the combination of worked metal with a plaited plant fibre being characteristic of Southeast Asian edged weapons. The same plant-fibre skill appears in the straw hat. Two festival object types are documented in the New Year material: the *Ki Tong Ja*, a swing used during the holiday, and the spinning tops played with alongside it; a straw effigy of a bull is built for the last day of the festival and burned. The sources used do not describe Tai Nua ceramics, lacquer, basketry beyond the hat, or household utensils.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used for this profile record no Tai Nua jewelry, silverwork, tattooing or hair practice.

## Music & performance

Music reaches the sources chiefly through the theatre. **Daiju** (傣剧), the Tai Nua and wider Dai dramatic form, combines Dai music and dance with narrative material, and it is the vehicle in which the group's melodic and choreographic repertoire is documented. It is most popular in the Dehong Dai and Jingpo Autonomous Prefecture and in neighbouring Baoshan, at the western edge of Yunnan along the Kachin and Shan State borders. Its music is syncretic by formation: when Dai performers and intellectuals built the genre in the late Qing period, they translated scripts out of Beijing opera, Sichuan opera and Dianju (滇剧, Yunnan opera) into the Dai language, and the resulting theatre blends those Han Chinese stage traditions — along with *piyingxi* (皮影戏), shadow puppet play — with Dai song and dance.

Broadcast is the other documented performance channel: Yunnan People's Radio Station transmits in Tai Nuea, which has official status in parts of Yunnan and appears on road and shop signs in Mangshi.

The festival calendar supplies the main secular performance occasions. The New Year week is given over to swinging on the *Ki Tong Ja*, spinning tops, and competitive games — *Toh Mark Khang* and the bean-bag tossing game *Peak Gon* — culminating in a temple gathering with the monks' blessing. The sources used do not name Tai Nua instruments or song genres individually.

## Dance & theatre

**Daiju** is the named dramatic tradition. It is classified in Chinese scholarship as a form of minority drama (*shaoshan minzu xiqu*, 少数民族戏曲) and takes its plots from long narrative poems and from folk tales, many of the latter drawn from Buddhist tradition. Its origin is precisely dated in the sources: during the reign of the Tongzhi Emperor (同治帝, r. 1861–1875), Shang He (尚贺), a resident of Yingjiang County (盈江县, then called Ganya, 干崖) in what is now Dehong Prefecture, worked from older Dai literature to compose the first Daiju play, *Xiang Meng* (相勐). The genre as a whole took shape in the late Qing Dynasty out of the translation effort described above, so Daiju stands as a deliberately constructed theatre for Dai audiences rather than an inherited village form. The sources used do not name individual Tai Nua dances apart from the dance element within Daiju.

## Festivals & rituals

The documented centrepiece of the year is the New Year festival, *Jin Leun Sam*, which normally falls on the first day of the third month of the lunar calendar. It draws people living away from home back to their birthplaces, and the festivities can run for a week. During that week the swing *Ki Tong Ja* is set up, spinning tops are played, and the competitions *Toh Mark Khang* and *Peak Gon* (bean-bag tossing) are held. It is the occasion for *Su Ma*, when children ask their elders' forgiveness and present an offering of flowers. Households bake festival cakes and invite friends and relatives to a succession of special meals through the week, which peak on New Year's Day itself, *Meu Ho Leun Sam*. On the final day an effigy of a bull is made from straw and burned in a ceremony marking the departure of the old year, and the closing celebration is held at the temple with the blessing of the monks. The cotton offering cloths in this gallery belong to the same world of presented gifts, although the records do not tie them to a specific rite. The sources used do not describe Tai Nua birth, marriage or funeral observances.

## Foodways

Tai Nua festival food is rice food. Two named cakes are made for the New Year: *Khao Pook Nga*, sesame rice cakes, and *Kao Ke Mah*, rice ball cakes. These are not solitary dishes but the material of hospitality — friends and family are invited in for special meals repeatedly across the festival week, with the sequence culminating in the New Year's Day meal on *Meu Ho Leun Sam* before the celebration moves to the temple. Beyond these cakes and the pattern of invited meals, the sources used give no account of staple crops, cooking methods, everyday dishes, drink or dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Tai Nua have their own literature, and two of its genres are named in the sources as the material Daiju playwrights drew on: long narrative poems and folk tales, the tales frequently Buddhist in origin. Older Dai literature was the direct source for *Xiang Meng*, the first Daiju play, composed in the 1860s or early 1870s from that written inheritance. Proverb collecting is the one documented modern preservation effort: in Thailand a collection of 108 Tai Nua proverbs has been published with translations into Thai and English. In China the situation is uneven — Tai Nuea has official standing in parts of Yunnan and is used in education and radio broadcasting, yet very little printed matter appears in the language.

## Language & religion

Tai Nuea belongs to the Tai language family and is closely related to Shan, of which it could be counted a dialect; it should not be confused with Tai Lü. Speakers mostly call themselves *Tai Le*, 'Upper Tai' or 'Northern Tai'; another autonym means 'Tai of the lower Hong River', the Hong being the Tai name for the Salween, and it is this phrase that gives the name Dehong. Zhou's classification divides the language into the Dehong dialect (332,000 speakers) and the Menggeng dialect (209,000), for a total of 541,000. The Laotian situation differs: Tai Nua speakers in Houaphanh Province use the Neua language, which has Northern Tai features, while the Yunnan variety is Southwestern Tai. The Tai Le script belongs to the Mon-Burmese family of writing systems, is closely related to the Ahom script and is thought to date to the 14th century; the reformed orthography was officially introduced in 1956, tone diacritics came in 1963, and in 1988 these were replaced by tone letters, giving a modern alphabet of 35 letters including five tone letters. On religion the sources are brief: Buddhist monks bless the close of the New Year festival at the temple, and Buddhist narrative supplies much of the folk-tale repertoire.
