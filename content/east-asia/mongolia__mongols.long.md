## Overview

The Mongols are an East Asian people native to Mongolia and to China — Inner Mongolia and eleven other autonomous territories — as well as to parts of southern Siberia; the contiguous area they chiefly inhabit is called the Mongol heartland. They are the principal member of the wider family of Mongolic peoples, and their indigenous dialects are grouped together as the Mongolian language. The Oirats and the Buryats are classified either as separate ethno-linguistic groups or as Mongol subgroups. A common descent from the Proto-Mongols binds them, and their history as a single political body runs from the Mongol Empire of Genghis Khan through the Northern Yuan to the Qing submission of 1691. In folk-culture terms they matter as mobile pastoralists: nearly 80 per cent of Mongolian territory is pasture, and livestock — more than twenty million head of wool-bearing sheep, cattle, camels, goats and horses — remains the country's most important economic activity.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The catalogue records behind this gallery document three pattern-bearing textile objects rather than a set of named weaving schools, so the entries below are object-level traditions.

**Khalkha woman's silk costume** — A complete set of dress for a wealthy Khalkha woman, built around a blue silk suit and worn with a braided cloth headpiece, a silver crown, pearl-band ornaments, earrings and a chest ornament. The Museum of Ethnography set was stolen and recovered twenty-five years later.

**Torghut silk damask robe** — A mid-green robe of silk damask from Etsin gol, its woven ground patterned with flowers and ornaments and overlaid with applied bands in black and gold-metal thread worked in a complex pattern weave; the applied motif may be lotus flowers. Cords, braid and machine-woven bands edge the standing collar, the fastening and the waist, and two embroidered breast pockets of green and purple plain-weave cloth are set into the front.

**Piled-wool prayer rug** — A square prayer carpet woven of piled red wool and backed with off-white cotton.

**Motif vocabulary.** Dragons (on knife handles, sheath mounts and an amulet case); twin fishes at the end of a knife handle; the eternity knot, worked in silver on a wooden bowl; the eight Buddhist emblems, likewise in silver on a bowl; whirling emblems on a knife sheath; a bird on both faces of a silver snuff bottle; a bat framing the glass front of an amulet case; flowers and ornaments woven into silk damask; fish, as the whole form of a silver pipe cleaner.

### Clothing & dress

Men's and women's dress in this collection diverge sharply in ornament. The wealthy Khalkha woman's outfit described above is a layered assembly of blue silk, silver and pearl, crowned with a silver headdress and finished with earrings and a hanging chest ornament; a Torghut woman's robe of green silk damask carries its decoration in applied bands and braid rather than in embroidery alone. Photographs taken at Chailar (Chulun) show women in a long dress worn with a short coat and a cap, standing before wooden houses. Men's dress in the same photographs is working dress: a herder posed with a steppe horse, horse-dealers seated on the sill of a wooden house holding a saddled animal by the rein. One man's hat survives in detail — conical, built up from coiled and plaited bast, with long red-dyed yak hair glued to the sides, a domed silver base at the top (its finial now missing), a lining of red tabby cotton and a padded headband and straps of the same cloth. Belt furniture was its own category of dress: a knife in its sheath, a fire-steel and a pair of bone chopsticks were carried together on a hanger. Ceremonial dress at the far end of the range is a Mongolian warrior's armour in thirteen parts — helmet, jacket, chest guard and back guard among them — still worn as parade costume by the bodyguards of certain chieftains when it was collected.

### Architecture

The characteristic dwelling is the *ger*, the demountable house of Mongolia's nomads, built from the raw materials to hand and framed in carved and polychrome-painted wood. Its logic is movement: a pastoral life that covers long distances in search of new grazing requires a house that can be struck and reassembled, and the *ger* answers that. Photographs from the Mongolian steppe show a man standing in front of one beside a section of plaited wicker enclosure — the pen and the tent together forming the working unit of the camp — and other camp views show two-wheeled carts drawn up among the tents. At Chailar (Chulun) the same photographic series records fixed wooden houses, with people posed at their doors and window sills, so settled timber building and portable felt-and-wood building existed side by side in the places the collectors passed through. Religious architecture appears only indirectly, through its furniture and through the temples where objects were bought: the lama temple Tung Scheng Chao, visited on 30 April 1930, and the monastery of Khadain-sume, also written Hattin sume, where much of the Swedish collection was acquired.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Metal dominates. Vessels include a copper teapot with movable handles and a sunken lid, blackened by use and identified as Chinese in type; a brass vessel with hanging-mount handles and a copper loop for a knob, used to ladle food from the cauldron at feasts and carry it into the tent; and a two-handled bronze temple jug with visible casting seams. Wooden bowls were lined and mounted in silver, one with the eternity knot, another with a silver foot and the eight Buddhist emblems — its wood especially valued because it came from Tibet. Lighter household gear includes a birch-bark box with a wooden base and handled lid, recorded as a milk container, and a back-carrier called *urch* or *urck*: two double wooden bows with crossbars, joined below by leather straps and slung from braces of sewn cattle-hair rope. Folding altar tables, *Taxile'en sjirae*, were made in pairs by Sagetjin Baell Mongols, assembled without nails or screws and painted red with gilded ornament on the visible faces.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver set with coral is the signature. The collection holds silver rings each set with a single coral; silver bracelets, one slightly triangular in section, decorated with a Chinese good-fortune character; and a pair of silver hair ornaments for a woman, coral-studded, brass-backed with a hook and joined by a thread, 14.5 cm each and 49 cm overall. Two silver-and-coral side pendants were hung at a woman's sides and used to carry small objects: inside the outer ring runs a band of movable coral beads, and at the centre sits a movable openwork filigree ball of silver. Amulet cases were worn as well as kept — one round, of copper, pierced in openwork, from Sunit; another of white metal with a glass front framed by a bat and dragon-and-serpent ornament, an engraved sign on the back, and inside it a pale cloth holding powder. Of hair practice the Wikipedia source records only the thirteenth-century style in which men shaved the head but for three locks, the side strands plaited to hang at the shoulders.

## Music & performance

One instrument is documented in detail, and it is enough to be specific about. The *hore*, described in the collection notes as a kind of violin, is a two-stringed bowed chordophone with a wooden resonator whose sides are dovetailed together; the underside is closed with riveted sheet brass and the upper face covered with leather, pierced at the centre by a coin-shaped sound hole. The sides are painted gold and decorated with trailing scrollwork, the neck carries three bands of bone, and the instrument has two tuning pegs. Its bow is wood and horsehair, the wooden stick painted red. The instrument reached Sweden in 1929 in a consignment of religious objects from Mongolia and Tibet, which places it in the same acquisition context as altar tables and amulet cases rather than in a described performance setting.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used here say nothing about Mongol dance or theatrical traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

Two ritual occasions surface in the catalogue records. The first is a New Year festival addressed to the god of heaven, at which priests wore a chain of 108 wooden beads strung with four large pale-red glass beads and finished with wound thread and brass fittings, wrapped around the neck; an attached label identifies the piece as an official's chain of the Qing period, so the same object type served both office and liturgy. The second is the feast, for which a brass vessel with hanging handles was used to empty cooked food from the cauldron and carry it into the tent where the guests were served — an ordinary vessel whose recorded use is entirely festive. Monastic ritual is present in the objects without a described calendar: paired folding altar tables with gilded fronts, amulet cases containing powder wrapped in cloth, and three undecorated skull bowls forming a linked set. Collecting itself was timed to the ritual year — the 1930 trip into Mongolia set out three days before New Year's Eve, with a Mongolian lama named Chagdur travelling with the party, and the amulet cases and hair ornaments were bought at Khadain-sume on 3 and 5 January.

## Foodways

Foodways here are the foodways of herders. Pastoralism is the basis of the traditional economy, and the herds named in the collection record — sheep, cattle, camels, goats and horses, more than twenty million head in Mongolia — define what the kitchen handled: meat and milk. Milk was stored and carried in a birch-bark box with a wooden base and a handled wooden lid. Cooking was done in a cauldron, and at feasts the food was lifted out into a handled brass vessel and carried into the tent to be served. Eating equipment travelled on the body: a knife with an iron blade and a wooden handle riveted in brass or iron and faced with white metal, carried in a wooden sheath with silver or white-metal mounts, with a pair of bone chopsticks pushed into two holes in the sheath and, on the fuller set, a fire-steel on the same belt hanger. Drinking vessels were wooden bowls lined inside with raised silver, one a tea bowl whose Tibetan wood was prized in itself. Tea was boiled or served in a copper pot with movable handles, blackened by long use. Alongside food, two habits of consumption are documented: snuff, taken from a round footed silver bottle called *Kuchur*, its lid originally set with five corals and fitted with a small spoon to dip into the flask; and smoking, attested by a fish-shaped pipe cleaner of silver said to have belonged to a chieftain near Khalun usu. The sources name no dishes.

## Oral tradition & literature

The written tradition is better documented in these sources than the oral one. Mongol-language historical writing includes the Secret History of the Mongols, cited as a thirteenth-century contemporary source, and two later chronicles, the *Erdeni-yin tobči* and the *Erdeni tunumal neretü sudur*; the latter records the embassy in which Altan Khan's envoys reminded the rulers of Moghulistan of their shared descent from Genghis Khan, and the former preserves a Chinggisid prince's remark, fleeing the Oirat ruler Esen Taishi, that "the khans of Toγmaγ, the descendants of Jochi, are our kindred." Mongol affairs were also written up from outside, in the Persian *Jami' al-tawarikh* and in the *Zafarnama* of Yazdi, where the Mongols of fifteenth-century Mongolia are called Qalmaqs. The ethnonym itself carries a folk etymology, derived in one proposal from a phrase meaning "eternal sky fire." The sources used here do not cover folktales, epic song, proverbs or riddles, nor present-day preservation work.

## Language & religion

Mongol dialects are collectively the Mongolian language, the central branch of the Mongolic family, whose earlier stages are traced through the Donghu confederation and the Xianbei; Middle Mongol sources of the late fourteenth century contain the first written references to the plough. Writing in the region predates Mongol script as recorded here: the Khitan, a Mongolic people, had two scripts of their own, and many Mongolic words are legible in their half-deciphered writings. Religion is layered. The culture of the post-Xianbei nomadic kingdoms is described as shamanist or Buddhist; by the seventeenth century Tibetan Buddhist factional politics ran through Mongol rule, with the Gelugpa, or Yellow Hat, sect backing the Manchus and the last khagan Ligdan supporting the Kagyu, or Red Hat, sect. Lamas, lama temples, altar tables, amulet cases and the eight Buddhist emblems on silverwork all belong to that establishment. Among the Kalmyks on the Volga, the Russian Orthodox church pressed Buddhists to convert.
