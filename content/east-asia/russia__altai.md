## Overview

The Altai peoples, also called Altaians, are related Turkic-speaking ethnic groups mainly living in the Altai Republic, a federal subject of Russia. Several thousand also live in the Altai Mountains of Mongolia and in Altay Prefecture, Xinjiang, although they are not officially recognized there as a distinct group. Others live in Kazakhstan, where they number around 200. The population of Altaians residing within the Altai Republic was 69,963 according to the 2010 Russian census. The people comprise Northern Altaians—including Chelkans, Kumandins, and Tubalars—and Southern Altaians, including Altai-Kizhi, Teleuts, and Telengits. Their languages belong to the Turkic family. Folk-culture sources describe distinct forms of subsistence, dwellings, clothing, cuisine, religion, and oral tradition.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Historically, Southern Altaian men’s and women’s clothing was broadly similar. Average clothing consisted of long shirts, wide breeches, robes, and fur coats. Fur hats, high boots, and sheepskin coats were also worn. Northern Altaians and some Teleuts traditionally wore short breeches, linen shirts, and single-breasted oriental robes. The sources do not distinguish ceremonial dress from everyday clothing, nor do they provide specific vernacular names for garments, belts, or footwear. Although most Altaians now wear modern clothing, traditional wear remains in use.

### Architecture

Most Southern Altaians traditionally lived in *yurts*. Many Northern Altaians built polygonal yurts with conic roofs made from logs and bark. Some Altai-Kizhi lived in mud huts with birch-bark gable roofs and log or plank walls. Teleuts and a few Northern Altaians lived in conic homes made from perches or bark. Russian influence contributed to the construction of large huts with two to four sloping roofs. Many modern settled families still keep a yurt in their yards, usually using it as a summertime kitchen or extra room.

## Music & performance

 No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription supplied for this profile concerns the Altai people.

## Festivals & rituals

In 1904, a millenarian indigenist religious movement called *Ak-Jang* or Burkhanism arose among the Altaians. Burkhanism is described as a monotheistic religion named after Ak-Burkhan, a deity recognized by its adherents as the sole god. It opposed both Russian rule and traditional Altai shamans.

## Foodways

Altai cuisine includes soups made from horseflesh or mutton. Dishes containing souslik, badger, marmot, fermented milk, cream from boiled milk, blood pudding, butter, fried barley flour, and certain vegetables are also described as staples. A popular drink is *aryki*, a hard liquor made from kumis.

## Oral tradition & literature

Traditional Altai shamanism is described as rich in mythology and supernatural beings. Popular deities included Erlik, the god of the underworld, and Oyrot-Khan, a heroic figure combining historical Zungarian or Oirat Khans with ancient legendary heroes. The sources state that ancient religious beliefs survived partly because they could be transmitted orally from one generation to another. They also mention Altai folk tales in a published collection, *Алтайские народные сказки* [Altai Folk Tales], and other studies of Altay folktales.

## Language & religion

Altai is a set of Turkic languages spoken officially in the Altai Republic alongside Russian. Southern and Northern Altai are now generally considered separate languages, although they were traditionally treated as one language and are not fully mutually intelligible. Southern Altai includes Altai proper, Mayma, Telengit, Tölös, Chuy, and Teleut; Northern Altai includes Tuba, Kumandy, Turachak, Solton, Starobardinian, and Chalkan. The languages were called Oyrot before 1948. Altai was written with the Latin script from 1928 to 1938 and has used Cyrillic since 1938.

Altai religious life has included traditional shamanism, Burkhanism, Russian Orthodox Christianity, Buddhism, Islam, Evangelical Christianity, and Tibetan Buddhism. The contemporary “Altai Faith” is described as a synthesis of Burkhanism, remnants of Altai shamanism, Mongolian and Tibetan Buddhism, and other tribal beliefs and customs. Shamanism remains practiced among many Telengits, while Burkhanism is described as the main religion of the Altai-Kizhi. Russian Orthodoxy is predominant among the majority of Kumandins, Tubalars, Teleuts, and Chelkans, though shamanism also remains present.
