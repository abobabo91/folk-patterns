## Overview

The Kam people, also officially known in China as Dong people, are a Kam–Sui people and one of the 56 ethnic groups recognized by the People’s Republic of China. They live mainly in eastern Guizhou, western Hunan and northern Guangxi, with small pockets of Kam speakers in Tuyên Quang Province in Vietnam. The 2020 census recorded 3,495,993 Kam in China. Their language belongs to the Tai–Kadai family and is divided principally into Southern Kam and Northern Kam. Kam culture is especially associated with polyphonic choir singing, glutinous rice cultivation, carpentry, silverwork, wickerwork and distinctive village architecture, including covered “wind-and-rain” bridges. Kam cultural life also includes oral literature, operas, elaborate life-cycle rituals, traditional medicine, festivals and a religious system combining polytheism, animism, ancestor worship and influences from Buddhism, Taoism and Confucianism.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no specific textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

 In funeral practice, mourners wear white headcloths, a custom also practiced by the Han Chinese. During weddings, silver jewellery is passed to the bride by her mother. The sources also state that cotton is cultivated for textile production.

### Architecture

Kam villages commonly contain *ganlan*-style wooden houses, described as stilt houses, together with ancient and sacred trees, covered “wind-and-rain” bridges, wayside pavilions with wooden or stone benches, bullfighting arenas, wells surrounded by stone rims, fish-ponds, grain-drying racks and granaries. Village entrances protect against intruders and are also places where “blocking the way” ceremonies are held. Drum towers are usually found only in southern Kam areas and may be village towers or extended-family towers. Altars to Sa Sui, the principal deity of the Kam pantheon, are also part of the village environment. An average-sized village has 200–300 homes; the smallest villages have 10–20 homes and the largest more than 1,000. Kam carpentry is widely known, and the covered bridge is a particularly distinctive architectural form.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do identify carpentry, silverwork and wickerwork as major economic activities. Baskets and other wickerwork are usually made by men from glutinous rice straw, cogongrass, Guangxi grass, bamboo and rattan. Wooden objects include houses, bridges, pavilions, grain-drying racks and granaries. Wells are usually dug near trees and surrounded by stone rims. The sources also mention suona performance during a funeral vigil.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver jewellery is passed onto the bride by her mother during the wedding cycle. In funerals, mourners wear white headcloths. The sources also state that cotton is cultivated for textile production, without describing particular forms of dress or adornment made from it.

## Music & performance

Kam musical culture is especially associated with polyphonic choir singing called *Kgal Laox* in the Kam language, translated as Kam Grand Choir or Grand song. Kam choral songs include nature songs, narratives and children’s songs. The Kam Grand Choir has been listed by UNESCO as a world-class intangible cultural heritage since 2009 in the supplied source material, although no UNESCO inscription record is provided among the sources.

One-part songs may be sung by one person or by many people. The named genres include *Duo Ye* songs, love songs accompanied by the pipa or niutuiqin, drinking songs, bride’s songs, mourning songs and pipa songs. Songs are also connected with medicine: when gathering plants, Kam practitioners sing songs relating to medicine. Courtship is structured through singing and poetry. In the early meeting phase, men and women sing songs and recite poems; during the deepening love phase, the songs become more spontaneous.

Operas are highly popular and are performed by local troupes. The sources name Wu Wencai and Zhang Honggan as famous Kam playwrights. Wu Wencai wrote *Mei Liangyu*. Instruments named in the sources include the pipa, niutuiqin, suona and lusheng. The suona is played during the arrangement of the “dream bed” at a funeral vigil. The lusheng is also associated with a taboo: it should not be played between the sowing and transplanting of rice seedlings because it could attract insect plagues.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name operas performed by local Kam troupes and identify Wu Wencai and Zhang Honggan as famous Kam playwrights. They also describe dragon dances and spring buffalo dances as rituals involving supernatural elements. Bullfighting is historically popular, with village bullfighting arenas described as fields.

## Festivals & rituals

The Kam festival calendar includes Kam New Year and Chinese New Year. One-day work-related festivals include Sowing seeds, Planting cotton, Washing water buffaloes and Eating new rice. Four harvest festivals last 1–3 days: Mid-Autumn Festival, Pumpkin Day, Lusheng Day and Cultivating new land. Singing festivals include the Commemoration of lovers killed by lightning and the Gaoba Singing Festival.

Remembrance festivals include Girls’ Day, King Lin’s Day, A Dianlong Day, Jiaxu Day and Best Weather Day. King Lin’s Day commemorates Lin Kuan, described as a northern-Kam hero of the 14th century. Best Weather Day celebrates Jiang Yingfang, described as a “Robin Hood” of the Kam people who led a rebellion in the 19th century. Other festivals include Tidying the graves, also called the Qingming Festival or “tomb sweeping,” Sweet rice cakes festival, Fireworks Day, Dragon Boat Festival, Zongba Festival and the Bull intestines eating festival.

Birth is accompanied by several rites. These include planting fir trees, inviting neighbors who bring food and gifts, announcing the birth to the mother’s family, visiting female relatives on the third day or so, and expressing homage to the land god for the birth of a male child among the Northern Kam. A child’s hands may be wrapped to discourage stealing later in life. The first haircut takes place at one month, the first eating of fermented rice at about one month, and the first eating of meat dipped in wine at six months.

Weddings last three days. They begin at the bride’s family home, followed by a reception and all-night feast at the groom’s home. The next day includes a “blocking the horse” ceremony in which hosts block guests while singing songs. The bride typically remains at her parents’ home for a few months or even years.

## Foodways

The Kam cultivate dozens of varieties of glutinous rice, known locally as “Kam” or “good” rice. Non-glutinous rice is called “Han rice” by the Kam. Other cultivated or gathered foods include maize, millet, vegetables, plums, peaches, pears, mushrooms, mandarin oranges, pomelos and watermelons. Fish are raised in fish-ponds and sometimes hunted. Commonly raised animals include water buffalo, pigs, chickens, ducks and geese.

The “four pillars” of Kam cuisine are glutinous rice, sour or pickled food, hot pepper and rice wine. Named dishes and condiments include barbecued fish, intestines sauce, purple blood pork, chicken-blood sauce, oil tea, *gongguo* and *bianmi*. Gongguo is a glutinous rice snack sweetened with liana, while bianmi is another glutinous rice snack. The giant salamander is described as a rare local specialty. Zongba is a type of dumpling made from glutinous rice, similar to zongzi. Chicken, fish and glutinous rice are eaten at one-day work-related festivals.

Two hot meals, breakfast and dinner, and one cold meal, lunch, are served every day.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kam oral literature contains legends and folk tales, including stories about leaders of past uprisings. Named figures include Xing Ni, Wu Mian, Lin Kuan and Wu Jinyin. Xing Ni is an ancient figure whose legend possibly dates from the Tang dynasty, 618–907. Wu Mian led a rebellion in 1378 during the Ming dynasty. Lin Kuan led a rebellion in 1397 and was later executed. Wu Jinyin revolted in 1740 against grain taxes and was killed in 1741.

Named tales include *The two orphan brothers*, *The unfriendly eldest brother*, *Ding Lang and the dragon princess*, *Zhu Lang and Niang Mei*, *Shan Lang and E Mei*, *Liang Shanbo and Zhu Yingtai*, *Suo Lao*, *Mei Dao*, *The frog and the swallow*, *The dog*, *The singing tree*, *Liang Niangni* and *Lou Niang*. The sources describe the frog and the swallow and the dog as rice-agriculture tales, the singing tree and Liang Niangni as origin-of-singing tales, and Lou Niang as a drum-tower tale. Songs and poetry also form part of courtship, weddings, funerals and plant-gathering practices.

## Language & religion

Kam, also called Gam or Dong, is a Kam–Sui language in the Tai–Kadai family. It has two major subdivisions, Southern Kam and Northern Kam; Northern Kam has been more strongly influenced by Chinese. Ethnologue distinguishes Northern Dong, Southern Dong and Cao Miao as closely related varieties. Kam has no traditional script of its own. Chinese characters are sometimes used to represent Kam sounds, and a Latin alphabet was developed in 1958, though it is not widely used. Most Kam speakers are not literate in Kam.

Kam religion is traditionally polytheistic and contains many elements of animism. Sa Ma Qing Sui, or Sa Sui, is the most important deity in Kam mythology. Other sacred beings and phenomena include village entrance and bridge goddesses, land gods and goddesses, love deities, the spirit of the sky and earth, rivers, wells, fire spirits, ancient trees, water buffalo spirits and rice seedling spirits. Ancestor worship, divination, shamanism and traditional medicine remain important themes. Kam people have also been influenced by Buddhism, Taoism and Confucianism; today Buddhism and, to a lesser extent, Christianity are practiced.
