## Overview

The Hani, also known as the Ho, are a Lolo-speaking ethnic group living in southern China, northern Laos, and northern Vietnam. In China, more than ninety percent of Hani people live in Yunnan, across the Ailao Mountains between the Mekong River and the Red River, also called the Yuanjiang river. Hani communities are associated with Pu’er, Honghe, Yuxi, and surrounding areas. There are 12,500 Hani living in Lai Châu Province and Lào Cai Province of Vietnam, while the Ho live in the mountainous northern regions of Phongsaly Province in Laos. The Hani are one of the 56 officially recognized nationalities of the People’s Republic of China and one of the 54 officially recognized ethnic groups of Vietnam. Their folk-cultural significance is especially associated with vocal polyphony, oral traditions, distinctive clothing, and terraced rice agriculture.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The traditional clothing of the Hani is made from dark blue fabric. Men wear short jackets and long, wide pants. They also wear white or black turbans. Women’s clothing depends on the clan to which they belong. Children under the age of seven do not have a gender difference in clothing according to the cited description.

### Architecture

Hani houses are usually two or three stories high. The sources state that they are built with bamboo, mud, stone, and wood. The Hani agricultural landscape includes forests, villages, terraced rice paddies, and river systems, forming a connected settlement and farming environment in the mountainous regions where they live.

## Music & performance

The Hani are known for vocal polyphonic singing. Eight-part polyphony was recorded in the 1990s.

Two traditional musical instruments are named. The *labi* (俄比) is an end-blown flute. The *lahe* is a three-stringed plucked lute.

Music is connected in the available material with Hani oral tradition and cultural continuity. The agricultural sections describe a culture organized around forests, villages, terraced rice paddies, water systems, and seasonal maintenance of the landscape.

## Festivals & rituals

The Hani religious hierarchy includes three principal religious personages. The *zuima* directs the main celebrations. The *beima* performs exorcisms and magical rituals. The *nima* carries out predictions and administers medicinal herbs; this last role can be performed by both men and women.

The Hani are polytheists and show special adoration toward the spirits of their ancestors. They practice rituals to venerate different gods and obtain their protection. The available source describes these religious offices and practices.

They state that the Hani return frequently for traditional events, especially when they take part-time non-farm jobs close to home.

## Foodways

The sources describe Hani agriculture in detail. Cultivated terraced rice paddies are a central agricultural practice. The Hani began rice cultivation in terraced hillside fields about 1,300 years ago, developing an agroecosystem composed of forests, villages, terraced rice paddies, and river systems.

The Hani maintain numerous traditional rice seed varieties adapted to the cold and dry conditions of mountainous regions. In 1984, Yunnan Province, Republic of China reported “5285 rice varieties,” while Yuanyang County reported 196 traditional varieties. The sources also describe integrated duck and fish farming, the use of green manure such as compositae and crofton weeds, and water management based on channeling water from forested mountains without reservoirs.

The terraces remain moist through the combination of water systems and a hot and humid climate that frequently produces thick fog. The sources present this farming arrangement as a relationship among terraces, rivers, villages, and forests.

## Oral tradition & literature

Hani oral tradition states that the Hani are descended from the Yi people and that they separated as a distinct tribe fifty generations ago. One oral tradition is the recital of Hani ancestors’ names from the first Hani family down to oneself.

The origins of the Hani are not precisely known. Their ancestors, described as the ancient Qiang tribe, are believed to have migrated southward from the Qinghai–Tibetan plateau prior to the third century CE. Oral tradition also tells of an ancient written script that was lost during migration from Sichuan. They do describe the preservation of oral knowledge through ancestry recitation and the transmission of agricultural practices. Traditional terraced farming knowledge is under pressure because fewer people, particularly younger people, know the complete process. In Aichun village, about 600 people are between 20 and 40 years old, but only 40 know the whole procedure of growing the red rice.

## Language & religion

Hani belongs to the Lolo-Burmese branch of the Sino-Tibetan language family, while the Hani language is also described as part of the Loloish, or Yi, branch of the Tibeto-Burman linguistic group. It is spoken in China, Laos, Myanmar, and Vietnam. In China, it is spoken mostly east of the Mekong River in south-central Yunnan, especially in Pu’er and Honghe prefectures. It is also spoken in Lai Châu and Lào Cai provinces of Vietnam and in Phongsaly Province of Laos.

The Hani of Vietnam are described as having two distinct dialects, one east of Muong Te and one to the west. Their speech varieties differ mostly in vocabulary. Hani has three main tones and two types of short vowels, while vowel length is distinctive. Standard Hani in China is based on the Lüchun County dialect and is written with a Latin-based script developed by the Chinese government during the 1950s. The orthography uses final consonant letters to represent tone, and *-v* marks tense vowels.

The Hani are polytheists who venerate gods and ancestral spirits. Their religious hierarchy includes the *zuima*, *beima*, and *nima*. Some Hani also practice Theravada Buddhism.
