## Overview

The Basay are an aboriginal people of Taiwan. Their ancestors spoke the Basay language, a Formosan language spoken around modern-day Taipei in northern Taiwan by the Basay, Qauqaut, and Trobiawan peoples. During the 1600s, Basay people monopolized control over river and coastal trading in North Formosa. Basay traders supplied information and goods to Dutch East India Company garrisons and were also involved in coal and iron mining.

## Language & religion

Basay was a Formosan language spoken around modern-day Taipei in northern Taiwan by the Basay, Qauqaut, and Trobiawan peoples. Trobiawan, Linaw, and Qauqaut are identified as other dialects. Basay data is mostly available from Erin Asai’s 1936 field notes, collected from an elderly Basay speaker in Shinshe, Taipei, and from Ipai, a speaker in Yilan whose Trobiawan speech had heavy Kavalan influence. The Shinshe informant’s speech was heavily influenced by Taiwanese.

Li (1992) mentions four Basaic languages: Basay, Luilang, Nankan, and Puting. Nankan and Puting are close to Kavalan, whereas Luilang is divergent. Basay has four optional case markers: *a* for nominative and ligature in the Shinshe dialect, *ta* for nominative in the Trobiawan dialect, *li* for locative in the Shinshe dialect, and *u* for oblique in the Trobiawan dialect. Function words include *pai*, meaning “future.” Trobiawan negators include *mia*, “not,” *asi*, “don’t,” and *(m)upa*, “not to want”; the Shinshe dialect has *mayu*, “not yet,” *manai*, “don’t,” and *kualau*, “not exist.” Yes–no questions are marked by *u* ~ *nu*. Basay verbs distinguish between agent-focus and patient-focus verbs, and the perfective prefixes *na-* and *ni-* are allomorphs.
