## Overview

The Rukai (*Drekay*) are an Austronesian people of southern Taiwan, numbering 12,699 in 2014 — the seventh-largest of the island's officially recognized indigenous groups. They live in the slate-bearing mountain country where Pingtung, Kaohsiung and Taitung counties meet, on the western and eastern flanks of the southern Central Mountain Range and along the upper Ailiao, Laonong and Beinan river drainages. Six communities — Budai, Labuan, Tanan, Maga, Mantauran and Tona — each speak a distinct dialect of Rukai, which is divergent enough within the Formosan branch of Austronesian that it is often treated as a primary subgroup of its own. Their earlier exonym, *Tsarisen* (or *Tsalisen*), means "people living in the mountain." In folk-culture terms the Rukai matter for three reasons: a hereditary aristocracy that encodes rank directly into cloth, beads and carved house timbers; a slate architecture unmatched elsewhere in Austronesia; and an iconography built on two animals held to be ancestral — the clouded leopard (*Neofelis nebulosa*) and the hundred-pace snake (*Deinagkistrodon acutus*).

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Rukai backstrap weaving** — Cloth is woven on a horizontal loom tensioned by a strap around the weaver's back, in the squared-cloth system shared by all Taiwanese indigenous groups: narrow woven panels are cut minimally and seamed into garments rather than tailored. The base fibre is linen made from flax retted, split and spliced by hand; cotton and wool entered later through Han trade. Weaving is women's work, and while a woman works in the weaving hut men may not enter it.

**Inlay (*inlay* weft-float patterning)** — Differently coloured linen wefts are carried through the warp to build geometric figures inside the fabric structure itself, so the pattern is woven rather than applied. This produces the banded rhombic fields that read as snake-back at a distance.

**Cross-stitch embroidery** — One of five Rukai stitch families, alongside chain, straight, circle and satin stitch. Bamboo needles were the older tool, replaced by Han-introduced metal needles; before the Japanese period embroidery thread was obtained by tearing imported coloured cloth into strands. The anise-star motif, signifying holiness, is worked in cross-stitch.

**Satin-stitch embroidery (*kamanungan* snake pattern)** — The most exacting Rukai technique and, in this form, found in no other Taiwanese indigenous dress. Stitches may run in any direction but must lie strictly side by side with no overlap and no gap; a single error is unpicked back to the start. It is the technique reserved for the rhombus, the Rukai shorthand for the hundred-pace snake.

**Beadwork bib and *glass bead regalia*** — Pearl embroidery: single-colour glass beads (plastic today) laid out as a design and couched onto cloth, massing into breast-covering bibs, collars and headband bands. Old wound-glass beads are heritable aristocratic property; commoners could acquire them from the nobility by barter in pigs, millet and pots.

**Patch embroidery (*human-head ancestor motif*)** — Black cloth cut into figures and appliquéd onto white or indigo ground, or the reverse. Snake, human-head and full human-figure motifs are conventionally executed this way, and the paired sun discs embroidered on the chest are a noble prerogative.

**Motif vocabulary.** *taraw* (lily — chastity for women, hunting prowess for men, worn as insignia rather than woven); hundred-pace snake (ancestral serpent, the dominant field pattern); rhombus (the snake's dorsal lozenge, in satin stitch); sun disc (paired, aristocratic, chest-placed); human head (ancestor/trophy reference, patch-worked); standing human figure (lineage ancestors, often ranked in rows); anise star (holiness, cross-stitch); pig (wealth, bridewealth); deer (hunting); string/chevron banding (frame and register divider).

### Clothing & dress

Rank governs dress: elaborate ornament is a noble right, and commoners wear plain cloth. Dyes are plant-based — red from a vine root chopped and steeped in water, brown from *Dioscorea matsudae* by the same method, yellow from ginger-root juice, dark blue from the leaf juice of *danadana*, green from *rasras*.

Men's ceremonial dress comprises an insignia-bearing headdress, headscarf, short top, skirt, shawl and leggings; everyday male wear runs to leather headgear, shoulder straps, girdle, a leather rain cape, deer-hide coat and deer-hide leggings, with a tobacco bag and gunpowder bag at the belt. The **leopard fur cape** and **clouded leopard pelt vest** are the apex male garments, restricted by rank and hunting achievement.

Women's ceremonial dress is layered and heavily beaded: a flower garland, headscarf, earrings, necklaces (including prized lazurite strands), bead bracelets, arm rings, a long gown, skirt, girdle, leggings and shoulder ornaments. Daily wear is a headscarf, long robe, skirt, leggings, gloves, mesh belt, leather rain cape and cloth bag. The **lily headdress** — fresh or fabricated *taraw* lilies mounted on the headband — is the signature Rukai crown. Noble families bought Han cotton, silk and wool specifically to make display clothing.

### Architecture

The **Kucapungane slate house** is the defining Rukai building: a rectangular, partly sunken dwelling of dry-laid **slate slab masonry**, with walls of stacked unmortared plates and a roof of overlapping slate shingles laid on timber purlins, shedding typhoon rain and holding cool in summer. Old Kucapungane (Haocha), in the upper Ailiao valley, is the canonical settlement — terraced house rows stepping down a slope, each house fronted by a paved forecourt used for threshing, drying and assembly, with the **Wutai slate plaza** serving the same function at village scale as a dance and council ground. Chiefly houses are marked architecturally: a **chieftain ancestral pillar** (a standing slab or plank carved with frontal human figures) set at or beside the main post, and a **carved lintel relief** over the door bearing snake bands, human heads and standing ancestors — carving rights belonging to the noble line alone. Stone slab benches, a hearth, sleeping platforms and interior burial beneath the floor complete the plan. Granaries on posts, men's meeting platforms and slate-walled paths tie the settlement together. Several villages were relocated to lowland permanent housing after Typhoon Morakot in 2009, which severed communities from these house sites and triggered community-based conservation and land-sovereignty programmes.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The **Budai Rukai pottery jar** stands at the centre of Rukai material ideology: large coil-built earthenware jars, sometimes bearing applied snake-relief bands, treated not as containers but as **sacred pottery vessels** — heritable regalia of noble houses, named, ranked by sex and lineage, and central to origin narratives in which ancestors emerge from a jar. Jars circulated as bridewealth alongside pigs, millet and beads. Woodwork is the everyday counterpart: **wooden spoon carving** produces long-handled serving and eating spoons with carved snake-band or human-head terminals, alongside carved bowls, pestles, back-carrying frames and lidded storage boxes. Bamboo supplies water tubes, needles and cooking vessels. Iron for knives, spearheads and adze blades came in through Han and Paiwan trade rather than local smelting; the hunting knife in its carved wooden sheath is a male status object.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Old glass beads are the highest-value Rukai property — wound and drawn beads of varied eye-and-stripe patterning, individually named, inherited within noble houses, and deployed as bridewealth, compensation and rank display. Strung as multi-row necklaces, breast bibs, headbands, bracelets and arm rings, they are supplemented by **shell-disc ornaments** (ground and perforated shell plates sewn in rows or hung at the chest), boar-tusk arm rings, brass bells, and imported lazurite beads. The lily insignia doubles as body adornment: a woman's right to wear *taraw* is a public statement about her conduct, a man's about the number of wild boar he has taken. Clouded-leopard teeth and claws and eagle feathers mark hunting and martial achievement on headdresses. Han-traded silver and brass plaques appear on later noble headbands.

## Music & performance

Rukai music is predominantly vocal and heterophonic: a leader sets a line and the group answers, with the melody ornamented differently by each singer over a slow, wide-spaced pulse — the same aesthetic that makes southern Formosan singing distinct from the tight polyphony of the Bunun to the north. Repertoire is functional and rank-bound: *senai*-type songs for work in the millet fields, courting songs exchanged antiphonally between young men and women at gatherings, wedding songs praising the lineage of both houses, mourning laments, and praise songs for a named chief or a successful hunter that cannot be sung by or for the wrong rank. The nose flute and the mouth flute — the paired aerophones shared with the neighbouring Paiwan — are played by men, historically in courtship and in mourning, the nose flute's breathy dyad being the most recognisable Rukai instrumental sound. The mouth harp (bamboo-framed jew's harp) accompanies flirtation and dance. Percussion is minimal: hand-clapping, stamping on the slate forecourt, and pestle-on-mortar rhythms during millet pounding. Bamboo tube-beating marks time for line dances. Song texts carry archaic vocabulary no longer used in speech, so elders' recordings function as linguistic documents; Taiwan's Council of Indigenous Peoples and Academia Sinica archives hold the principal collections. Rachel Liang, a pop singer of Rukai descent, is the best-known contemporary Rukai musician.

## Dance & theatre

The Rukai have no masked or scripted theatre. Dance is communal, non-mimetic and performed on the paved village plaza: interlinked line and circle dances in which participants join by crossed or hooked hands, moving with a weighted side-step and knee-bend to their own singing rather than to instruments. Position in the line is not free — rank and lineage determine who stands where, and who leads, so the dance is itself a legible diagram of the community's aristocratic order. The great occasions are the millet harvest festival, weddings, and the reception of a chief. The most distinctive Rukai performance form is the **swing ceremony** attached to noble weddings, in which a tall wooden swing rigged from lashed poles lifts the bride above the plaza — a Rukai and Paiwan speciality with no equivalent elsewhere in Taiwan. Men's dance includes stamping and spear-brandishing sequences tied to hunting and defence; women's dance emphasises restrained upper-body carriage that keeps the beaded bib and lily headdress legible. Since the 1990s, staged troupe versions of these dances have become the main public face of Rukai culture at Taiwanese indigenous festivals.

## Festivals & rituals

The Rukai year turns on millet. The **millet harvest festival** (*kalalisian*, in July or August, the pivot of the ceremonial calendar) combines thanksgiving, the formal presentation of first-fruits to the chief, the swing ceremony, communal dance on the plaza, and rites of adult recognition for young men. Sowing rites in winter, weeding rites in spring and a millet-tasting rite before general harvest divide the agricultural year; hunting rites open the winter hunting season, with strict divination by bird-call and dream before a party departs. Life-cycle rites are heavily rank-inflected: birth is followed by naming from a lineage-specific stock of names, so a name announces a person's house and status; marriage proceeds through formal go-between negotiation, a bridewealth transfer of pottery jars, beads, pigs and iron goods, and the swing; funerals traditionally involved flexed burial beneath the house floor, keeping the dead inside the household. Tribute in the form of a portion of every harvest and of every large game animal flows to the chief's house, which redistributes at festivals — the economic backbone of the aristocratic system. Historically, when an enemy intruded into Rukai territory his head was taken, carried out of the village to a designated place, and honoured with solemn prayer; Rukai informants in Wutai are explicit that this was territorial defence and not the offensive head-taking practised elsewhere, and that the life taken required respect.

## Foodways

Foxtail millet is the ancestral staple and the ritual grain — brewed into millet wine for every ceremony, pounded in wooden mortars, and steamed. Taro is the everyday carbohydrate and the food most associated with Rukai mountain agriculture: peeled, sliced and dried on slate racks over low fire into hard *taro chips* that store for years and travel as provisions, then reconstituted in soup or eaten dry. Sweet potato, Job's tears, maize and upland rice fill out the field crops; wild greens, bamboo shoots, ferns and mountain mushrooms are gathered. Protein comes from hunting — wild boar above all, plus muntjac, sambar deer, flying squirrel and freshwater fish and crabs — and from pigs raised for bridewealth and sacrifice. The signature ceremonial dish is *abai* (also *cinavu*): millet or glutinous rice dough wrapped around minced pork, parcelled in shell ginger or *Trichodesma* leaves and steamed, made in quantity for weddings and harvest and distributed by rank. Meat is also preserved by salting and fermenting in jars, and by smoking over the hearth. Game is butchered and divided according to fixed rules, with named portions owed to the hunter, to the chief and to the landowner of the hunting ground. Millet wine is offered to ancestors by finger-flicking before anyone drinks.

## Oral tradition & literature

Rukai narrative centres on origins and on the two ancestral animals. The best-known cycle is the legend of the **Lake of the Ghosts (Dalupalringi / Xiaoguihu)**, in which a Rukai noblewoman, Balenge, marries the lake's serpent lord — the hundred-pace snake in human form — and departs into the water, which grounds both the snake's kin status and the nobility's claim to descent. A parallel migration narrative credits the founding of Kucapungane to hunters led to the site by a clouded leopard that refused to leave the spot, fixing the settlement and the leopard's protected status: Rukai hunters do not kill the animal. Other genres include lineage genealogies recited to validate rank and land claims, hunting anecdotes, etiological tales about the origin of millet and taro, and proverbs about restraint and obligation to the chief's house. Transmission is oral and household-based; there is no indigenous script. Since the 2001 introduction of indigenous-language curricula, Rukai has been taught in Wutai and Maolin schools using a romanized orthography, and researchers including Ching-Ting Hsin have documented how Rukai children's literacy development intersects with identity formation.

## Language & religion

Rukai is a Formosan language of the Austronesian family, and one of the most divergent — its verbal morphology departs so far from the Formosan norm that it is frequently set apart as a primary branch, a key data point in the debate over Austronesian's Taiwanese homeland. The six dialects (Budai, Labuan, Tanan, Maga, Mantauran, Tona) are not all mutually intelligible; Mantauran is the most aberrant and the most endangered. There is no traditional script; a Latin orthography standardized by the Council of Indigenous Peoples is now used in schools. The older religious system is ancestor- and spirit-centred, with the hundred-pace snake and clouded leopard as ancestral beings, taboo places and species, and ritual specialists who divine by dream, bird-call and pig liver. Presbyterian and Catholic mission work from the 1950s converted most Rukai; churches now host the singing that formerly belonged to the plaza, and harvest festivals are frequently opened with Christian prayer.
