## Overview

The Soyot are an ethnic group who mainly live in the Oka region of the Okinsky District in Buryatia, Russia. Their traditional territory lies in the Eastern Sayan Mountains, including the mountain range dividing the Oka River and Irkut River. Like other taiga peoples, they traditionally practiced reindeer breeding and hunting and lived nomadically, although most Soyot now live in villages. According to the 2021 census, there were 4,368 Soyots in Russia. The Soyot language belongs to the Turkic family and is closely related to the Tofa language. Soyot culture has been shaped by contact, intermarriage, and historical assimilation involving Buryat, Tofa, Tozhu Tuvan, Dukha, Mongolian, and Tibetan traditions. Their reindeer herding, hunting economy, language revitalization, and recovery of ethnic identity are central themes in the available sources.

## Music & performance

A Norwegian scientific expedition in the early 1910s recorded information about Soyot customs and published a book in 1915 containing photographs, a short list of Soyot words, and a retelling of a Soyot shamanistic tale of divination. The same account described shamanistic and Buddhist rituals.

The available material mentions ritual performance only in religious contexts. Soyot religious and shamanistic practices show influence from Khalkha Mongols and Altai Turkic peoples, while Mongolian and Tibetan Buddhism became influential from the mid 1700s. Buryat Buddhist lamas attempted to end Soyot shamanism, according to Valentin I. Rassadin. No further performance details are supplied by the sources.

## Festivals & rituals

The available sources do describe shamanistic and Buddhist ritual practice. The Norwegian scientific expedition in the early 1910s recorded a Soyot shamanistic tale of divination and accounts of shamanistic and Buddhist rituals. Soyots had been exposed to Mongolian and Tibetan Buddhism since the mid 1700s, although many only began to properly follow Buddhism in the 1800s and 1900s. The sources state that Soyot religious practices show influence from Khalkha Mongols and Altai Turkic peoples and that they share cultural and religious similarities with the Tofa people.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources document one Soyot shamanistic tale of divination, recorded during a Norwegian scientific expedition in the early 1910s and published in 1915. No Soyot epic, proverb, riddle tradition, or named storytelling genre is described.

A published account by Ferdinand Ossendowski also includes descriptions of Soyots encountered during his journey through the Siberia and the Sayan Mountains in 1920. His book, *Beasts, Men and Gods*, portrays Soyot communities, migrations, religious identity, and avoidance of warfare.

The sources describe contemporary cultural and linguistic revitalization. In the early 2000s, Valentin I. Rassadin developed an alphabet for Soyot and later produced the first Soyot dictionary and primer. In 2002, V. I. Rassadin published a Soyot–Buryat–Russian dictionary, and in 2020 he published a children’s picture dictionary with Russian, Mongolian, and English translations.

## Language & religion

Soyot is a Turkic language of the Sayan Turkic branch and is closely related to Tofa. The Soyot language has Buryat and medieval and contemporary Mongol loanwords. Two dialects or languages are identified in Russia and Mongolia: Soyot in the Okinsky District of the Republic of Buryatia and Tsaatan in the Darkhad valley of Mongolia. Soyot is not commonly written; Rassadin uses a Cyrillic-based writing system in dictionaries and grammars. The language nearly disappeared by 1996 but is being revitalized in schools.

Soyot religion combines shamanistic practices with influences from Khalkha Mongols, Altai Turkic peoples, Mongolian Buddhism, Tibetan Buddhism, and Buryat Buddhism. The sources state that exposure to Mongolian and Tibetan Buddhism began in the mid 1700s, while wider adherence developed in the 1800s and 1900s.
