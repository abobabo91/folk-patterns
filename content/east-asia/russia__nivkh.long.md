## Overview

The Nivkh — *Nivkhgu* around the Amur, *Nʼiɣvŋgun* in East Sakhalin, both meaning simply "the people" — are an indigenous group of the Russian Far East, living on the northern half of Sakhalin Island and along the lower Amur River and the adjacent mainland coast, from the Amur estuary at Nikolayevsk south through the Strait of Tartary toward De Castries Bay. Russian censuses count 3,842 (2021), down from 4,652 (2010) and 5,287 (2002). Nivkh is a language isolate, or a small family of two or three languages, grouped for convenience with the Paleosiberian languages; fewer than five percent of ethnic Nivkh speak it. Their older name in Western literature, **Gilyak**, is the Russian rendering of Tungusic *Gileke* and Manchu-Chinese *Gilemi*. In folk-culture terms they matter as fishermen, sea-mammal hunters and dog breeders whose material world was built almost entirely from fish skin, birch bark, wood and hide, and whose winter Bear Festival is among the best-documented ceremonies of northeast Asia.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources describe Nivkh dress and containers rather than a named weaving tradition; the materials, not looms, carry the identity.

**Fish-skin garment work** — A child's jacket or coat in the British Museum is made from many pieces of salmon skin stitched together, flared, high-waisted and pleated below, with the left side overlapping the right as worn. Its curved left edge is piped with blue-black (indigo-dyed) tabby cotton cloth, and oval areas of skin have been cut out and covered with separate pieces of skin, worked regularly enough to read as decoration rather than repair. Salmon and other fish skin also appears in a travelling bag, a tobacco pouch and a birch-bark vessel bound with fish skin and strings.

**Embroidered women's robes** — Women's robes (*hukht*) were light and multicoloured, with intricate embroideries and ornaments sewn to the sleeves, collar and hem; men's robes (*skiy*) were darker and more sparingly ornamented, on the sleeve and left lapel.

**Applied Chinese silks** — A headman's padded cap in the British Museum is built from several stitched fabrics, including a tapestry-woven (*kesi*) section with Chinese blue "cloud" patterns on a light brown ground, blue silk damask, and printed blue-and-white and cream/red/blue tabby cottons — a direct trace of the Manchurian and Chinese trade the Wikipedia sources describe.

**Birch-bark containers** — Water buckets, boxes, lids, vessels and a tobacco box are made of birch bark with wood, basketry and creeper bindings; birch bark also formed the conical summer hat (*hiv hak*).

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name motifs only on the traded Chinese cloth of the headman's cap: *cloud* patterns, two complete *bat* motifs, and parts of a *five-clawed dragon* with *waves* and *rocks*. Wikipedia notes that Chinese motifs including the dragon, spirals and scrolls spread among Amur peoples under Ming rule, but names the Udeghe, Ulchi and Nanai rather than the Nivkh in that passage.

### Clothing & dress

Nivkh robes had three buttons and fastened on the left side of the body: *skiy* for men, *hukht* for women. Winter garments used skins of fish, seal and sable and furs of otter, lynx, fox and dog. Women's *hukht* fell below the knee, light and multicoloured, embroidered and hung with ornaments of coins, bells, or beads of wood, glass or metal — most of them obtained from Manchurian and Chinese traders — along the sleeves, collar and hem. Men's *skiy* were shorter and darker, with pockets built into the sleeves, and men added a loose kilt called a *kosk* when hunting or travelling by dog sledge. Boots of fish, seal or deerskin were made very watertight; the British Museum holds two pairs of hide and fur boots. Fur hats (*hak*) were worn in winter, often with the animal's tail and ears left on the back and crown; summer hats (*hiv hak*) were conical birch bark, and the museum's bark hat is of birch bark with wood, vegetable fibre and rope. A headman wore the padded multi-fabric cap described above, falling below the chin with a wavy hemline. A man's leather belt in the collection carries a needle-case, knife and sheath, fire-lighting equipment and a pouch with a coin, in leather, iron, wood, fish skin, horn, bone, ivory and brass. Since Soviet collectivization most Nivkh wear mass-produced Western clothing.

### Architecture

Two winter dwellings are documented. The older is the *ryv* (also *to*), a round dugout about 7.5 metres (23 feet) across, shored with wooden poles and covered with packed dirt and grass, with a fireplace at the centre and a smoke hole serving for both light and smoke. The second is the *chad ryv*, a one-room structure with a gable roof heated by a *kang*, the Chinese furnace — modelled, like the Nanai *dio* it resembles, on Manchurian and Chinese dwellings of the Amur. A shed beside the house held sledges, skis, boats and dogs. Villages were small: three or four households shared by several families, larger settlements rare and mostly on the Amur estuary, with households often containing unrelated families and the village drawing on two to eight clans, four being standard. Settlements were semi-sedentary, summer sites near the coast and winter sites inland along rivers for the salmon spawn; they lasted decades but were vulnerable to flooding, and many were destroyed in the Amur floods of 1915 and 1968. Before Russian colonization, villages stood roughly every 5 km along the Tym' and Poronai. Soviet resettlement cut Sakhalin's settlements from 82 to 13 between 1962 and 1986.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The catalogued objects are overwhelmingly wood, bark, hide and imported iron rather than ceramic. Hunting and fishing gear dominates: bows of wood, bark, string, root, vegetable fibre and leather; arrows of wood, or of iron, wood and feathers; a four-section seal spear of wood, iron and leather; an iron-and-wood knife with an incised leather sheath; and a salmon club cut from a wood root, also used on seals. Domestic wood includes bowls, a dish or tray, a spoon and a box; birch bark supplies water buckets, box lids, tobacco boxes and lidded vessels bound with creeper. Travel and work equipment covers a pine paddle, pine-and-leather snow-shoes (possibly skis), a wooden netting needle for net-making, a wood-and-iron drill, straw-and-tinder fire-lighting equipment, a model sledge that served as a toy, and pouches and bags of seal skin, fish skin, hide and fur, one travelling bag combining salmon skin with an animal jaw. Iron tools reached Sakhalin from mainland Asia through the Ming tribute trade.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources document adornment as garment ornament rather than jewellery: coins, bells and beads of wood, glass or metal, mostly of Manchurian and Chinese origin, sewn to the sleeves, collar and hem of women's *hukht*, and more sparingly to men's *skiy*. A brass-mounted belt set with horn, bone and ivory fittings is the closest thing in the catalogue to worn metalwork. Shamans (*ch'am*) wore elaborate coats with belts often made of metal, and gave or used talismans to prevent sickness. Alongside these, the collection holds a group of carved charms: a human head with a fish tail, a fish vertebra, a jointed human figure, a torso, seal or fish figures in wood, one with fish skin and one with cloth, and a four-footed carving with four anthropomorphic heads, a different fish or animal carved beneath each head.

## Music & performance

The sources record that the Nivkh have extensive folklore and songs but name no instruments, song genres or performance occasions, so the musical repertoire cannot be described here from them.

## Festivals & rituals

The **Bear Festival** is the one named festival in the sources, held between January and February depending on the clan and presided over by shamans. A bear was captured and raised in a corral for several years by the women of the settlement, who treated it as a child; it was understood as a sacred earthly manifestation of Nivkh ancestors and of the gods in bear form. At the festival the bear was dressed in a specially made ceremonial costume and offered a banquet to carry back to the realm of the gods as a plea for benevolence toward the clans; it was then sacrificed and eaten in an elaborate ceremony, with dogs often sacrificed as well. Its spirit was thought to return to the mountain gods content and to repay the Nivkh with abundant forests. The festival was normally arranged by relatives to honour the death of a kinsman, and functioned between clans: a clan of wife-takers restored its tie to a clan of wife-givers across the break the death had made. Suppressed under Soviet rule, it has had a modest revival since the Soviet decline, now as a cultural rather than religious ceremony. The Ainu of Japan practise a very similar ceremony, *Iomante*. Everyday ritual centred on "feeding": an open flame was fed a tobacco leaf, spices or a tipple of vodka, and the sea was fed an object of value so that the sea god would protect travellers.

Marriage was controlled by the clan (*khal*) and organised through a three-clan alliance group (*pandf*), with exogamous sub-clans and cross-cousin marriage as the older custom; the dowry was shared by the clan, and bride price was probably a later introduction. Because women were few, wealthier men took more than one wife and poor men often none.

## Foodways

Nivkh subsistence was fishing, hunting and dog breeding, with salmon caught at the winter spawning grounds inland along the rivers — the Tym' and Poronai are named as especially rich in fish — and sea mammals taken on the coast, as the seal spear and salmon club in the collection show. Men left the villages in late fall to hunt game in the surrounding grounds while women gathered food in the forests. Russian and Japanese over-exploitation of the Tartar Strait and lower Amur fisheries, beginning with an incident in 1898, together with bans and heavy taxes on cached fish, drove many Nivkh into starvation when they could not afford imported Russian food. With the streams and seas now overfished and polluted, the Nivkh have adopted many foods from Russian cuisine. The sources name no individual dishes or ceremonial foods, and record only vodka, tobacco and spices as offerings to the fire.

## Oral tradition & literature

Nivkh folklore is extensive: songs and myths of the creation of humans and the universe, and of heroes, spirits and beasts battling in ancient times. Individual folk tales (*tylgund*) were first recorded from oral tradition by Bronisław Piłsudski and published by L. Ya. Sternberg; the corpus was later collected and worked over by A. Veselovsky, E. A. Kreinovich, G. A. Otaina and V. Sangi. Besides *tylgund* there are *nastund*, an improvisational myth, *ker-aind*, a short epic tale, and animal tales. The recurring subjects are myths of the moon and the sun, tales of finding good luck, and dealings with the other world; despite stories of battles with forest, mountain, taiga and underground peoples, the heroic component is largely absent, and Veselovsky held that the Nivkh epic had not yet fully formed when it was studied — an opinion the Nivkh writer and folklore researcher Sangi, who published Nivkh legends in literary adaptations, rejected. One tale recorded by Piłsudski contains the *vagina dentata* motif. Chuner Taksami, an anthropologist, is counted the first modern Nivkh literary figure and an advocate of Siberian rights. The monthly newspaper *Nivkh Dif*, founded in 1990 and published in the West-Sakhalin dialect from the village of Nekrasovka, and Marina Temina's textbooks in the Amur and Sakhalin dialects, carry the written language today; Nivkh is taught in grades 1–3 in several schools on Sakhalin and in Khabarovsk Krai.

## Language & religion

Nivkh (self-designation *Nivhgu dif*) is a language isolate or small family, conventionally placed in the Paleosiberian group. It forms a dialect continuum usually divided into four clusters — lower Amur, North Sakhalin, East Sakhalin and South Sakhalin — differing enough that specialists count two or three mutually unintelligible languages. It is agglutinating, SOV, with eight to ten cases, two tenses (non-future and future), and a system of consonant alternation at morpheme boundaries comparable to Celtic mutation. It is written in a modified Cyrillic alphabet. Native speakers fell from 100% of the ethnic population in 1897 to 1,079 in 1989 and only 198 in the 2010 census. Religion is animist, worked through shamanism: a pantheon of loosely defined gods (*yz*, *yzng*) over mountains, rivers, seas and sky; Sakhalin itself imagined as a giant beast lying on its belly, its trees the beast's hair, earthquakes its trembling when angered. Fire is venerated as the symbol of clan unity and a deity of the ancestors. Shamans (*ch'am*) diagnosed and cured disease with plant and animal remedies and warded off the spirits that cause death. Some Nivkh have converted to Russian Orthodoxy.
