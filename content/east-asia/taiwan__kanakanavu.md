## Overview

The Kanakanavu are an indigenous people of central southern Taiwan. They live in the two villages of Manga and Takanua in Namasia District, Kaohsiung City. Kanakanavu is a Southern Tsouic language of the Austronesian family. The language is moribund, with only 4 speakers recorded in the 2012 census. The government recognized Kanakanavu as the 16th group of Taiwanese indigenous peoples on 26 June 2014. Their documented livelihood included agriculture, hunting, and fishing, while their material culture included leather, linen, cotton, silk, satin, bamboo, feathers, and embroidered clothing. Their documented spiritual practices included offerings, fertility rituals, shamanism, and a polytheistic nature religion.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Men’s linen-based red corset** — Men commonly wore a linen-based red corset suspended diagonally from the nape of the neck. It included a pouch for personal items or tools, while geometric patterns woven in blue, black, or yellow decorated its upper section.

**Women’s embroidered jacket** — Women’s clothing included a lengthy short top secured with a tied strap at the back or wrapped around the waist. Red, yellow, blue, black, green, and purple embroidery decorated the collar, front edge, hem, and cuffs.

**Women’s black long skirt** — A black long skirt was draped from left to right around the lower body and tied with a belt. The belt was red or green, usually made of cotton or silk, and decorated with embroidered patterns.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name geometric patterns and embroidered patterns.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional Kanakanavu attire included a leather hat and vest made from furry deer skin, exposing the chest. Increased trade and interaction with other communities brought linen and cotton garments, which had once been associated with the affluent. Men commonly wore a linen-based red corset suspended diagonally from the nape of the neck, with a pouch for personal items or tools, and a rectangular short skirt made from overlapping black cotton layers. A bamboo belt secured the skirt and traditionally emphasized chest muscles as a sign of prowess in combat or hunting. Men also wore leather shoes, hunting bags, headdresses, earrings, and wrist ornaments. Long feathers from eagles and blue-bellied pheasants signified social status and achievement: young males displayed one to four feathers, while elders displayed five to eight. Women’s clothing developed from leather to include cotton, silk, satin, and linen. Women wore embroidered tops, black long skirts, red or green belts, earrings, neck ornaments, headscarves, hats decorated with colorful garlands, and knee pants during events or ceremonies.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Kanakanavu accessories included earrings, wrist ornaments, neck ornaments, headscarves, hats decorated with colorful garlands, leather shoes, hunting bags, and headdresses. Men’s headdresses incorporated long feathers from eagles and blue-bellied pheasants, whose number indicated social status and achievement.

## Festivals & rituals

Kanakanavu practiced a polytheistic nature religion involving offerings, fertility rituals, and shamanism. Headhunting was common until Christianization took over.

## Foodways

Agriculture was the main facet of Kanakanavu society, followed by hunting and fishing. Maize, rice, millet, taro, sweet potatoes, beans, and soybeans were staple crops.

## Language & religion

Kanakanavu, also spelled Kanakanabu, is a Southern Tsouic language of the Austronesian family. It is spoken by the Kanakanavu people in Manga and Takanua in Namasia District, formerly Sanmin Township, Kaohsiung. The language is moribund, with only 4 speakers recorded in the 2012 census. It has 14 consonant phonemes and 6 vowels, and its syllable structure is generally CV. Several texts have been transcribed in the orthography of the Council of Indigenous Peoples of Taiwan, and a dictionary is available. The sources describe an earlier polytheistic nature religion involving offerings, fertility rituals, and shamanism; they also state that Christianization took over from the practice of headhunting.
