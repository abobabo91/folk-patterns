## Overview

The Yakuts are a Turkic people who arrived in North Siberia from the Lake Baikal region in the 13th century, fleeing Mongol pressure. They settled in the basins of the Middle Lena, Aldan, and Vilyuy rivers, becoming northern horse and cattle herders whose material culture centers on reindeer fur, wood, and mammoth ivory. By the 1820s they had converted to Russian Orthodoxy while retaining traditional tengri practices. Today they are the least russified minority in Russia, with strong language retention and a rich heritage in music, folklore, and traditional crafts.

## Material culture

Yakut material culture is built from reindeer fur, carved wood, and mammoth ivory, reflecting their northern environment and herding economy.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Yakut textile traditions center on reindeer-fur appliqué on sewn garments rather than weaving or embroidery.

- **Reindeer-fur appliqué** — Garments pieced from many sections of reindeer fur and decorated with rows of red woollen cloth triangles with grey or brown fur filling, creating geometric patterns on the front, sides, and hood.
- **Tumat red-dyed garments** (*Dyirikinei*) — Bundles of deer fur dyed with red ocher were sewn into jackets and hats of the Tumat, an earlier Vilyuy people, whose distinctive red coats earned them the name 'chipmunk people.'

### Clothing & dress

The sources provide limited documentation of Yakut clothing beyond a child's reindeer-fur hooded coat with appliquéd decoration.

- **Child's hooded reindeer-fur coat** — A pieced and fur-lined coat with a hooded design and slight flare, decorated with reindeer-fur appliqué in red cloth and grey or brown fur.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Yakut everyday objects are crafted from wood and mammoth ivory, with no recorded ceramics or metalwork.

- **Choron** — A wooden cup or jar used for holding kumis, fermented mare's milk, which is central to Yakut cuisine and culture.
- **Mammoth-ivory game-pieces** — Carved from fossil mammoth tusk are game-pieces in the forms of cocks and animal heads.
- **Ivory dog-sled model** — A carved mammoth-ivory model of a dog sled, representing traditional Yakut transportation.
- **Summer encampment model** — An ivory-figured model of a Yakut summer encampment mounted on a wooden board with carved ivory figures.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Yakut jewelry and body adornment are barely documented in the available sources.

- **Mammoth-ivory lip-or-ear ornament** — A single catalogued ornament carved from mammoth ivory, uncertain whether worn as a lip or ear ornament.

## Music & performance

Music is recognized as a field of Yakut achievement, though specific instruments and genres are not documented.

- **Yakut ethnomusicology** — Eduard Yefimovich Alekseyev is recognized as a Yakut ethnomusicologist studying music in the Sakha Republic.
- **Yakut musicians** — Sarantuya, a mezzo-soprano, and Kjuregej, described as painter, actor and musician, represent Yakut musical achievement.

## Festivals & rituals

Yakut rituals center on sacred rock formations and a retained reverence for the Raven within Russian Orthodox practice.

- **Kigilyakh rock formations** (*Kigilyakh*) — Certain rock formations held in high esteem by Yakuts as sacred places.
- **Raven reverence** — Among the Khoro Sakha, the Raven is addressed as 'Our ancestor,' 'Our deity,' and 'Our grandfather,' revered for its gift of flint and tinder to struggling humans in ancient times.
- **Aisyt** — The mythic mother goddess in Yakut belief, associated with fertility and traditional practices.
- **Yakut shamanism** — Shamanic practices retained alongside Russian Orthodox conversion.

## Foodways

Yakut cuisine centers on animal husbandry products, especially fermented mare's milk, with fish and horse meat as essential pillars.

- **Kumis** — Fermented mare's milk, the signature drink at the heart of Yakut cuisine and culture, served in choron vessels.
- **Stroganina** — Sliced frozen salted fish, a traditional Yakut preparation.
- **Oyogos** — Loaf meat dishes made from horse or other meat.
- **Salamat** — A millet porridge with butter and horse fat, a traditional staple food.
- **Kuerchekh** — A popular dessert made of cow milk or cream with various berries.

## Oral tradition & literature

Yakut oral histories preserve origin traditions and genealogies, while written literature rose in the late 19th century with a national revival in the early 20th.

- **Northward migration tradition** — Oral accounts tell of a northward migration from the southern Yenisey watershed to the Lena basin.
- **Khoro folklore** — Traditional stories of the Khoro Sakha center on the Raven, with some tales of the Eagle.
- **Džaardaakh** — A renowned Tumatian woman known for physical strength and archery skill who married a Yakut man and is counted as a notable ancestor of the Vilyuy Yakut.
- **Modern Yakut writers** — Sardana Oyunskaya (folklorist), Natalia Kharlampieva and Pyotr Toburokov (poets), Anastasia Syromyatnikova (writer), and Aita Shaposhnikova (translator and critic) represent early 20th-century Yakut literary revival.

## Language & religion

Yakut, a Northern Siberian Turkic language most closely related to Dolgan, is spoken by 87% of Yakuts despite widespread Russian influence.

- **Yakut language** (*Sakha*) — The Northern Siberian Turkic language of the Yakuts, spoken by 87% of the population in the Sakha Republic as of 2010, making the Yakuts the least russified people in Russia.
- **Russian Orthodox conversion** — By the 1820s almost all Yakuts had converted to the Russian Orthodox church while retaining tengri practices.
- **Tengri practices** (*tengri*) — Traditional spiritual practices retained by Yakuts alongside Russian Orthodox Christianity.

## Glossary

- *Sakha* — The Yakuts' own name for themselves; also used for the Republic of Sakha and the Yakut language
- *kumis* — Fermented mare's milk, the signature drink of Yakut culture
- *choron* — A wooden cup or jar for serving kumis
- *stroganina* — Sliced frozen salted fish
- *oyogos* — Loaf meat dishes
- *salamat* — Millet porridge with butter and horse fat
- *kuerchekh* — A dessert made of cow milk or cream with berries
- *Kigilyakh* — Sacred rock formations held in high esteem by Yakuts
- *Aisyt* — The mythic mother goddess in Yakut belief
- *tengri* — Traditional spiritual practices and beliefs retained by Yakuts
- *Khoro* — A Yakut group whose progenitor is held to be Uluu Khoro
- *Raven* — A deity revered by Khoro Sakha as ancestor, deity, and grandfather
- *Dyirikinei* — Yakut name meaning 'chipmunk people' for the Tumat, referring to their red-dyed fur garments
- *Tumat* — An earlier Vilyuy people remembered in Yakut tradition
- *Dolgan* — A Turkic people and language most closely related to Yakut
- *shamanism* — Spiritual practices retained alongside Russian Orthodox Christianity
