## Overview

The Qiang are an ethnic group in China, officially recognised as one of the 56 ethnic groups of the People’s Republic of China. In 2020, their population was approximately 312,981. They live mainly in the mountainous northwestern part of Sichuan, on the eastern edge of the Tibetan Plateau, especially in the Ngawa Tibetan and Qiang Autonomous Prefecture, Beichuan Qiang Autonomous County, and the counties of Mao, Wenchuan, Li, Heishui, and Songpan. The Qiang speak Qiangic languages, a subfamily of the Tibeto-Burman languages, although communication between different Qiang groups often takes place in Mandarin. Their folk culture includes fortress villages, stone watchtowers, distinctive wool clothing, mountain-deity worship, village shamans, oral traditions, ritual instruments, and practices shaped by Han, Tibetan, Muslim, and Christian influences.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Wool clothing** — Wool is the primary material used for Qiang clothing, reflecting the importance of sheep as a source of village livelihood. Men and women wear gowns made of gunny cloth, cotton, and silk with sleeveless wool jackets.

**Yangpi Gua** — The *Yangpi Gua* is a sleeveless jacket made of sheepskin or goatskin. It is part of clothing adapted to the harsh climate of the Qiang mountain regions.

**Yunyun Shoe** — The *Yunyun Shoe* is a sharp-pointed, embroidered shoe described as having typical Qiang characteristics.

**Women’s embroidered dress** — Women’s clothing is trimmed with lace, and collars are decorated with silver ornaments. The source describes embroidery and drawn work as being done extemporaneously, without designs.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no specific textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Qiang men and women wear gowns made of gunny cloth, cotton, and silk, together with sleeveless wool jackets. Wool is the principal clothing material, and white is a sacred colour in Qiang culture. The *Yangpi Gua*, made from sheepskin or goatskin, is a common sleeveless jacket. People bind puttees around their legs to protect against cold weather. The *Tou Pa* is a cloth head covering. Clothing also includes a waistband and an apron tied around the waist. The pointed and embroidered *Yunyun Shoe* is a characteristic item of footwear. Women’s dress is trimmed with lace, and collars are decorated with silver ornaments. The sources do not distinguish a separate ceremonial costume from everyday dress, although clothing, wool, white colour, and silver decoration are all described in relation to Qiang cultural identity.

### Architecture

Qiang settlements include fortress villages known as *zhai*, generally composed of 30 to 100 households. Villages are often located in small valleys along mountain streams, and two to five fortress villages may form a village cluster known locally in Chinese as *gou* or *cun*. Qiang houses are granite stone buildings generally consisting of two to three stories. The first floor is used for livestock and poultry, the second for living quarters, and the third for grain storage. Qiang people also constructed watchtowers and houses with thick stone walls and small windows and doors. These stone towers, described as Himalayan Towers, remain distinctive features in some villages. Consecrated white stones are traditionally placed on top of towers and are believed to carry the powers of gods.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do describe wooden carving marks, used before the development of a writing system to represent events or communicate. Qiang people are also skilled in constructing roads and bamboo bridges, using wooden boards and piers; some bridges stretch up to 100 meters. Other skilled workers are described as masons who dig wells and undertake chiseling and digging in neighbouring places during poor farming seasons.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention silver ornaments decorating the collars of women’s clothing.

## Music & performance

Qiang musical performance is associated with songs, dances, traditional instruments, and religious ceremony. Traditional songs concern topics such as wine and the mountains, and are accompanied by dances and instruments including leather drums. The sheepskin drum is one of the two most important symbols of Qiang religion and forms part of the ritual equipment of the *duangong*, known as *bi* in the Qiang language.

The *duangong* are village shamans who preserve Qiang culture and perform ceremonies ranging from funerals to healing rituals. They function as priests, scholars, medicine men, and village elders, while also being believed to communicate with spirits and ghosts. Their ritual equipment includes a monkey skin hat, a sheepskin drum, a holy stick, and the *AbaMullah*, a preserved monkey skull.

## Dance & theatre

The sources state that traditional songs are accompanied by dances. Dance is also associated with the performance of songs concerning wine and the mountains.

## Festivals & rituals

Qiang religious life includes ceremonies directed by the *duangong*, whose responsibilities include funerals and healing rituals. The *duangong* also administers a coming-of-age ceremony for 18-year-old boys called “sitting on top of the mountain.” The boy’s entire family goes to mountain tops to sacrifice a sheep or cow and plant three cypress trees.

Two important religious holidays are the Qiang New Year and the Mountain Sacrifice Festival. The Qiang New Year falls on the 24th day of the sixth month of the lunar calendar, although it is now fixed on October 1. It involves sacrifice to the god of Heaven. The Mountain Sacrifice Festival is held between the second and sixth months of the lunar calendar and is dedicated to the god of mountains.

Another important rite is the *gaigua*, an initiation and graduation ceremony through which a person is confirmed as a *duangong* by the village and receives ritual tools. These tools may be handed down over fifteen generations. White stones are consecrated through rituals and placed on towers as symbols of good luck and as representatives of gods.

## Foodways

The Qiang cultivate narrow fluvial plains, mountain terraces, and neighbouring woods, while also herding yaks and horses on mountain pastures. Millet, highland barley, potatoes, winter wheat, and buckwheat are staple foods. Northwestern communities rely more heavily on animal husbandry and consume more dairy and meat products, whereas southeastern communities practise more agriculture and consume more grains and wheat.

The sources state that sheep are especially sacred because they provide much of a village’s livelihood. Sheep or cows may be sacrificed during the “sitting on top of the mountain” coming-of-age ceremony. Wine is popular among the Qiang, and smoking orchid leaves is also described as a common practice.

## Oral tradition & literature

Qiang oral tradition is preserved especially by the *duangong*, who are keepers of Qiang theology, history, and mythology. During restoration work after the 2008 Sichuan earthquake, recording repositories were created for tales remembered only by the *shibi* or *duangong*. The Qiang language historically had no script, and people used carved marks on wood to represent events or communicate.

The Qiang were heavily affected by the 8.0 magnitude earthquake on 12 May 2008, when over 30,000 Qiang Tibetic people were killed. Restoration efforts in A’er village included preserving Qiang languages, which have no written form. The A’er stone tower was restored in 2012 by A’er villagers using authentic materials and techniques.

## Language & religion

Qiang, also called Qiangish or Rma languages, is a Sino-Tibetan language cluster of the Qiangic branch spoken by approximately 140,000 people in north-central Sichuan Province. It consists of Northern Qiang, a non-tonal language, and Southern Qiang, a tonal language, together with numerous dialects and dialectal continua. Speakers call the language *Rma* or *Rme*. The Qiang also use Chinese characters. A Latin-alphabet writing system based on the Qugu variety of a Northern dialect was developed in the late 1980s, while the Qiang Phonetic Alphabet Scheme was completed in late 1990 and approved for trial operation in 1991. A Rma script was finalised in 2017.

Qiang folk religion is pantheistic and involves guardian mountain deities, nature gods, ancestral spirits, and white stones believed to receive divine power through rituals. The supreme god is called Abamubita or Mubyasei, the god of Heaven. Qiang religious life also incorporates Han, Tibetan, Muslim, and Christian influences, with Tibetan Buddhism more evident near Tibetans and Han Chinese religious practice more prevalent in southern and eastern areas.
