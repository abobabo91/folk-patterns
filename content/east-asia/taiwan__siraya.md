## Overview

The Siraya are a Taiwanese indigenous ethnic group and Plains Indigenous people who traditionally settled the flat coastal plains of southwestern Taiwan and corresponding sections of the east coast, in areas identified today with Tainan and Taitung County. At least four communities comprise the group: Mattauw, Soelangh, Baccloangh, and Sinckan, corresponding to modern-day Madou, Jiali, Shanhua, and Sinshih. They are Austronesian people, and Sirayan belongs to the Formosan languages. The group matters to folk-culture research through its village organization, gendered social roles, language records, religious practices, Sinckan Manuscripts, and continuing cultural and language revitalization.

## Music & performance

The Siraya language article records that a group of Siraya children in Sinhua District of Tainan are able to speak and sing in Siraya as part of a cultural and language revitalization movement.

## Festivals & rituals

Traditionally, the Siraya lived in villages that were in constant conflict with one another. Women handled agriculture and religion, while men handled hunting, warfare, and decision-making. Married couples lived separately, with the wife residing with her parents and the husband in the men’s quarters; meetings between them took place secretly. The sources describe childbirth and child rearing as associated with warfare: childbirth was postponed until the husband retired from being a warrior, generally around age forty, and pregnancy before a woman’s mid-thirties commonly resulted in an abortion performed by the village shaman.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Siraya language was recorded in the Sinckan Manuscripts, a set of documents from the 17th and 18th centuries written using a system of romanization introduced by the Dutch. Dutch missionaries studied Sirayan in order to convert the Siraya to Christianity, devised a romanized script, taught it to Siraya people, and began translating the New Testament. Copies of the Dutch missionaries’ translation of the Book of Matthew into Sirayan survive, and several manuscripts are bilingual, with Sirayan and Chinese versions presented side by side. A translation of the Gospel of St. John into Siraya by Daniel Gravius in 1661 was reported in a paper published in 2021; its discovery in the Royal Danish Library expanded scholarly understanding of the language. The Tainan Pe-po Siraya Culture Association published a modern Siraya glossary in 2008, and reconstructed Siraya began to be taught in schools and used in new literature in 2002.

## Language & religion

Siraya is a Formosan language of the Austronesian language family and was spoken until the end of the 19th century. Siraya, Taivoan, and Makatao are described as three different languages forming the Sirayaic languages, although the sources note that some scholars previously treated Taivoan and Makatao as Siraya dialects. Siraya proper was spoken in the coastal area of the Tainan Plain; Taivoan was spoken mostly inland to the north, and Makatao in Kaohsiung and Pingtung Prefectures to the south. The last recorded regular use of Siraya was in 1908. After the port in the Siraya area was annexed in 1683 by Qing Dynasty China, gradual acculturation reduced the language’s use, while Taiwanese became the mother tongue of most Siraya families. The sources describe women as handling religion and identify the village shaman in relation to abortion.
