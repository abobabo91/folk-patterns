## Overview

The Evenki, long known to outsiders as *Tungus*, are a Tungusic people of North Asia whose homeland lies in the Siberian taiga between Lake Baikal and the Amur River, and whose settlements now stretch from the River Ob west to the Okhotsk Sea east and from the Arctic Ocean south to Manchuria and Sakhalin — roughly 2,500,000 km², a territory exceeded in Russia only by the Russians themselves. The 2021 Russian census recorded 39,226 Evenki; China counted 34,617 in 2020, concentrated in the Hulunbuir region of Inner Mongolia near Hailar, and Mongolia 537, known there as *Khamnigan*. Their language is the largest of the northern Manchu-Tungusic group. In folk-culture terms they matter as reindeer people: conical birch-bark and hide tents, a shoulder-set reindeer saddle found nowhere else, and beaded hide clothing cut for mobility in a cold, dry climate.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources name no vernacular terms for Evenki textile techniques, so the traditions below are described from the museum catalogue records of the objects shown here and from the ethnographic summary in the Wikipedia articles.

**Appliqué on reindeer and elk hide** — Garments are cut from soft reindeer, elk or deer skin and ornamented with applied strips of trade cloth: blue and red tabby cotton, red tabby wool (hand spun and hand woven), striped silk and printed cotton. On one knee-length man's coat the applied blue cloth runs along the centre-front opening, waistline, shoulders and upper back, where it forms a large-scale geometric pattern.

**Hide dyed with alder bark and soot** — An apron-type garment is sewn from reindeer hide dyed reddish with alder bark and blackish with soot, the two tones set in concentric bands around a central cloth panel.

**Glass-bead embroidery on cloth grounds** — Beads are stitched not directly to the hide but onto the applied cloth strips: cream, turquoise, black, light brown, red, green and transparent beads in interrupted lines, or, on a woman's beaded trousers, couched in dense strings that almost cover a rectangular hide panel, finished with a row of silver-coloured metal discs and suspended larger blue and green beads.

**Pigment painting on leather** — Short men's summer trousers carry chevron bands in brown and tan pigment, with four identical human figures painted in black.

**Painted and embroidered bow-cases** — A buff leather case, fringed along one edge, is embroidered and painted with angular bars of red, black and natural buff alternating, plus narrower bands of blue and red circular spots.

**Fringe and hair finishing** — Hems are closed with leather fringing, deer-hair fringing, or tufts of horsehair.

**Impressed birch bark** — Fine birch bark covered with impressed lines sheathes the face, edges and mid-section of a large wood-and-whalebone bow.

**Motif vocabulary.** Angular bars (alternating red, black and buff); circular spots in blue and red; chevrons; large-scale appliqué geometric shapes; the cross (+) set at the centre of a saddle-bag flap with small diamonds between its arms; stylized human figures, in rows of six beside a cloth border or repeated in black pigment on trousers; engraved narrative figures on a lance blade — men in sledges drawn by dogs, and fish.

### Clothing & dress

Evenki dress was built for cold, dry air and constant movement: brief garments of soft reindeer or elk skin around the hips, leggings, and either moccasins or long supple boots reaching the thigh. Over this went a deerskin coat left open at the front and covered instead with an apron-like cloth; the constant elements of the costume were a hide loincloth, leggings, and boots of varying lengths. Catalogued examples fill this out. A tan summer coat is knee-length, collared, fastened with four pairs of leather strips, its back panels shaped to flare from the waist; another is waisted, leather above and red woollen cloth below. Men's summer trousers are short, pointed at the middle back and drawn with a leather string. Summer boots have brown leather soles, legs stained pink and blue, applied wool, silk and printed cotton in geometric shapes, and a vertical strip of cream cloth at the heel. Women's beaded underpants are the most heavily worked garment in the group. Facial tattooing was common. Among the Solons and Khamnigans, who took up horse breeding from the Mongols, the Mongolian *deel* was adopted as well.

### Architecture

The Evenki lived in conical tents built of birch bark or reindeer skin tied to birch poles — a dwelling form already present at sites believed to be Neolithic, alongside bone fish-lures and birch-bark boats. When a camp moved, the pole frameworks were left standing and only the more portable coverings were carried on, so the taiga held a dispersed stock of reusable house frames. Camp size tracked the season: through winter, the hunting season, most camps held one or two tents, while spring encampments grew to as many as ten households. Very early in spring the winter camps broke up and shifted to ground suitable for calving, where several households pastured their animals together through the summer and fenced off special areas to keep newborn calves from being trampled in a large herd. The sources do not describe permanent Evenki building types, roofing or architectural ornament; the Evenki of China today tend to be settled pastoralists and farmers, and Soviet policy in Russia forced sedentarization into villages.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

No Evenki pottery appears in the sources. Ironwork does: a large lance-head for bear hunting, inlaid with copper and brass in figures of men in dog-drawn sledges and fish; a harpoon head with two barbs, perforated for a looped cord; arrows tipped in iron. The composite bow is wood faced with whalebone strips, rimmed with ivory, sheathed in impressed birch bark, bound at ends and middle with leather thong, with knobs under each bend to catch the string; it travelled in its own case, with a quiver of wood, hide, cotton, beads and hair. Smoking pipes are numerous — iron, iron in a beaded cloth cover, wood with brass or bone-and-brass bowls, white metal with wood and leather. Other objects include a wooden reindeer-call horn banded with birch bark, a double leather saddle-bag, a comb of mammoth ivory with an openwork relief panel, and the Evenki-specific reindeer saddle set on the animal's shoulders to spare its back, ridden with a balancing stick in place of stirrups. Hunters also carried a "pike," a large knife on a long handle used as an axe in thick taiga or as a bear spear.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources document body adornment through dress rather than separate ornaments: facial tattooing was very common, and clothing was embellished with imported glass beads in transparent, light brown, black, cream, turquoise, red, green and blue, with silver-coloured round metal discs used in rows at a hemline and in pairs on beaded motifs. A beaded cloth cover for an iron smoking pipe shows the same bead-on-cloth treatment applied to a personal accessory. No named jewelry types, metals or workshop traditions appear in the sources used.

## Music & performance

The sources are thin here and name no Evenki instrument beyond the wooden reindeer-call horn, strengthened with birch bark at its wide end, which is a herder's signalling tool rather than a musical one. For China's Evenki there is a documented song collection: the Evenki scholars who produced the Evenki–Mongolian–Chinese dictionary of 1983 (Kesingge, Cidaltu and Alta) also published a collection of folk songs transcribed in IPA and Mongolian script with Chinese-style numbered musical notation, with long vowels sometimes written with an intervening silent ᠭ under Mongolian orthographic influence. Shamanic performance is better attested than secular music: Richard Noll and Kun Shi's 1994 fieldwork with the shamaness Dula'r (Han Chinese name Ao Yun Hua), born 1920 in Yiming Gatsa village in the Evenki Banner of Hulunbuir, recorded that most of her shamanic paraphernalia was destroyed during the Cultural Revolution of the 1960s and that she kept hidden an Abagaldi (bear spirit) shaman mask of a type also documented among the Mongols and Daur. A carved wooden ritual figure with beads and hide, from the group of objects shown here, belongs to the same domain.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources give no annual festival calendar and no life-cycle ceremonies, but they describe the ritual frame closely. The oldest layer of Evenki belief is the animation and personification of all natural phenomena, belief in an upper, middle and lower world, belief in the soul (*omi*), and totemistic concepts; A. A. Sirina calls the result an "ecological ethic," a system of mutual responsibility between people, nature and nature's spirit-masters. Various magical rituals attached to hunting and to guarding the herds; these were later taken over by shamans, and shamanism in turn developed the figure of the spirit-master. Hunting carries explicit prohibitions: an animal, bird or insect must not be tormented, a wounded animal must be finished off at once, the blood of a killed animal must not be spilt or defiled, and animals that escaped predators to a person or came for help in a natural disaster must not be killed. The ritual year that the sources do mark is pastoral — winter hunting camps of one or two tents, the spring break-up toward calving grounds, joint summer pasturing. Shamanic vocation is described through Dula'r's initiatory illness and a multi-year apprenticeship with a Mongol shaman before she was permitted to heal at 25 or 26. Christianity, imposed nominally in the 18th century, was "confined to the formal performance of Orthodox rites which were usually timed for the arrival of the priest in the taiga." Olga Kudrina (c. 1890–1944) is remembered as a shaman among the Reindeer Evenki of northern Inner Mongolia along the Amur's Great Bend.

## Foodways

The sources name no Evenki dishes, so what follows is the food economy they do describe. The Evenki were a mixture of pastoralists and hunter-gatherers: domesticated reindeer supplied milk and transport, while meat came from hunted large game. Most households kept only about 25 head of reindeer, bred for transport rather than slaughter — wild reindeer were hunted and eaten, domesticated ones were not. For taiga dwellers the key game were elk, wild reindeer and fowl, with roe deer, bear, wolverine, lynx, wolf, Siberian marmot, fox and sable also taken; Evenki near the Okhotsk Sea hunted seal. Fishing completed the diet, and bone fish-lures are among the oldest elements of the culture. Trapping became economically important only with the fur tax imposed by the tsarist government, and guns arrived in the 18th century, replacing steel bows and arrows. Today the population divides between hunting and reindeer-breeding Evenki, horse and cattle pastoralists, and some farming Evenki; in China they tend to be settled pastoralists and farmers. The sources record no ceremonial foods, tea customs or dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources name no Evenki epic. They do document a written literature built fast and deliberately: G. M. Vasilevich prepared a *Memo to Tungus-vacationers* for Evenk students in Leningrad in May 1928 and a *First Book for Reading in the Tungus Language* (*əwənkil dukuwuntin*) a year later; from the 1930s onward "folklore, novels, poetry, numerous translations from Russian and other languages," textbooks and dictionaries were published in Evenki. The ethnographer S. M. Shirokogoroff attacked the new literary language as "child-like" and predicted its quick extinction, but it survived within a dialect base of roughly 5,000 people. Named writers include the poet and prose writer Nikita Sakharov (1915–1945), the poet Alitet Nemtushkin (1939–2006), the poet, children's writer and storyteller Maria Fedotova-Nulgynet (b. 1946), the writer, philologist and folklorist Galina Varlamova (1951–2019), and Ureltu (b. 1952). In Tura, former administrative centre of the Evenk Autonomous Okrug, the local newspaper carries a weekly Evenki supplement, and the Evenki DoReCo corpus compiled by Olga Kazakevich and Elena Klyachko preserves narrative recordings with phone-level transcription.

## Language & religion

Evenki is the largest northern Tungusic language, closely related to Even, Negidal and Oroqen. Bulatova counted 14 dialects and 50 sub-dialects in Russia, grouped on phonological grounds into northern, southern and eastern; in China the Hihue (Hoy) dialect is taken as standard, alongside Haila'er, Aoluguya, Chenba'erhu and Morigele. The language is highly agglutinating with 13 cases, back-front vowel harmony, and subject–object–verb order. Manchu script was used for Evenki (Solon) words in the Qianlong-era *Imperial History of the National Languages of Liao, Jin, and Yuan*; a Latin alphabet was officially approved in May 1931 and replaced by Cyrillic from 1937, while Chinese Evenki scholars experimented with Mongolian script and a pinyin-like Latin spelling in the 1980s. The language is endangered: 5,831 speakers among 39,226 Evenki in 2021, maintained by young people in only one village, Iyengra, in the southern Sakha Republic. Belief was animistic before Russian contact; shamanism persists, many Evenki have adopted Tibetan Buddhism, and Mongol and Buddhist Lamaist influence is visible in Inner Mongolian practice.
