## Overview

The Atayal — *Tayal* in their own language, the form the people themselves almost always use, where "Atayal" is the spelling used in the Taiwanese government's English-language documents — are an Austronesian-speaking Indigenous people of central and northern Taiwan. They number around 90,000, roughly 15.9% of Taiwan's Indigenous population, making them the third-largest Indigenous group on the island. The word *tayal* means "human" or "man". The earliest record of Atayal settlement is near the upper reaches of the Zhuoshui River; in the late 17th century groups crossed the Central Mountain Ranges eastward and settled the Liwu River valley, where seventy-nine Atayal villages are found. Their northernmost village is Ulay (Wulai in Chinese), about 25 kilometres south of Taipei. In folk-culture terms the Atayal are known above all for women's ramie weaving, for the facial tattoo, and for *gaga*, the unwritten customary order that governs hunting, marriage and ritual alike.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Ramie weaving** — Weaving is a skill expected of every Atayal woman. Thread is made from ramie (*Kgi*): the plant is shredded, pestled and worked into smooth threads (*Nuka*), which are then cleaned by broiling. Tapioca roots and Shoulang yams are used to dye the thread a red ochre. Weavers work on the ground at a backstrap loom, using a distinctive warping frame built on a base with three pillars, with a weaving box set on the ground to hold the wound threads. A wooden Atayal loom with an unfinished ramie web still attached, together with bamboo spindles and shuttles carrying ramie-fibre string, are among the objects shown here; a 1920s photograph in the Museum of World Culture records a weaving woman in northern Formosa, published after J. B. M. McGovern.

**Lukkus-kaxa** — *Lukkus-kaxa* is the most valuable Atayal garment, reserved for leaders, warriors and the weavers able to make it. Thousands to millions of tiny beads cut from giant clam shell (*tridacninae*) are knitted onto the cloth. Because shell beads are precious, *lukkus-kaxa* served as an ancient form of currency, as a family heirloom, as an engagement gift and as compensation in settling conflicts.

**Rattan and bamboo plaiting** — The Atayal plait rattan and bamboo containers named *kiri'*, *qbun*, *tokan*, *bluku'* and *gitu'*, large-capacity vessels used by women to harvest crops and carry goods. The best material is fresh bamboo from March to April, when the shoots have just dropped and the leaves are beginning. For a medium round skip the bamboo is split with a small knife (*buli'*) into about 350 strips (*rruma'*) some 2 feet 5 inches long; these are woven horizontally and vertically into a large quadrilateral, trimmed round, and finally edged with yellow rattan (*qwayux*). Baskets and sieves of the same technique carry hunting gear and harvests, their size varying with function; the carrying basket here has a forehead strap.

**Motif vocabulary.** Diamonds or argyles — the eye of the ancestors, standing for their protection and blessing; horizontal and parallel lines — the rainbow bridge crossed in the afterlife by face-tattooed hunters and weavers on their way to the ancestral spirits (*rutux*); red, the favoured colour, for blood and power.

### Clothing & dress

Atayal dress is woven in bright colours and built from geometric figures, mostly argyles and horizontal lines. The museum objects here show the repertoire concretely: men's and women's sleeveless short upper garments, or jackets, of ramie and wool sewn with shell beads; a 19th-century overvest of cotton and wool with bone beads; capes of ramie, cotton and wool trimmed with buttons, copper bells and beads; and a plain woven *apron*-cape of ramie. Chest-cover cloths (breast-cloths) are woven of ramie and wool and fastened with a button, one of them red with a blue-black border. Women wear skirts of ramie and wool and red leggings patterned with diamonds and stripes. Belts are woven of cotton in turquoise, black and white stripes, or in blue cotton hung with white buttons, woollen tassels and bells. A woman's jacket of later make substitutes plastic beads and metal bells for shell. The man's head covering is the rattan hat *qbubu'*, most often a rimless bowl, sometimes with a brim added; hunters faced theirs with animal skins or teeth, and a successful headhunter sewed a white circular shell plate called a *mayon* at the centre of the forehead.

### Architecture

Traditional Atayal houses are rectangular, their roofs built up in several layers of bamboo, bark, stone, wood and thatch for insulation and weather protection. Because Atayal settlements are spread widely across Taiwan, house styles vary; the common type is the half-underground house, found especially in Nantou, Yilan and Taichung counties. In the Nan'ao community, family houses are made of bamboo, rock, rattan and wood and dug down so that half the structure sits below ground; the length of a Nan'ao-style house is the arm span of three adults and its width that of two. Nan'ao roofs are laid over a base of bamboo and cypress bark, paved with stone slabs and secured with wooden strips. Windows pierce three sides for light and air but open only halfway, against enemy attack, and the steps are flanked by bamboo screens as protection from spears. Upper framing is lashed with yellow rattan, which tightens as it is smoked by the hearth. Beside dwellings the community builds the pavilion *Pslyan*, larger than a house, rectangular, with a central fire pit; it began as a place for discussion and negotiation under Japanese colonial administration and later became where elders and members meet over governance, ritual and disputes.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources describe no Atayal ceramic or metalworking tradition. Wood and bamboo carry the household instead: sheaths and furniture are made of wood, and daggers and swords are strictly men's work, since women may not touch them. The *Lalaw Behuw*, a long knife, was an Atayal weapon. A hunting knife is unique to its owner, buried with him when he dies and never inherited by a younger hunter. Cooking and heating centre on the three-stone stove (*hka'*), whose stability follows the principle of three points forming a plane; each stone is buried slightly at one end and stands 20 to 30 cm high, leaving three channels for the fire's draught. A half-underground house holds two such stoves. Smoke rises to the shelves called *gaga*, keeping hunting tools, rattan hats and weapons dry. Also shown here are a smoking-pipe bowl of bamboo root and rattan, and a coarse ramie mat in yellow and blue, fringed at both ends.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ear ornaments are carved from bamboo, sometimes combined with grass skin and wool, and worn in pairs; shell beads from giant clams, white buttons, woollen tassels and small bells ornament belts, capes and jackets. The great Atayal body art is the facial tattoo, *ptasan* in Squliq Atayal, applied with a cluster of needles lashed to a stick (*atok*) tapped in with a hammer (*totsin*), the punctures then rubbed with black ash; healing could take a month. Men received two bands, on the forehead at coming of age and on the chin after fathering a child; women were tattooed from the ears across both cheeks to the lips in a V, work that could take ten hours and was performed only by women. Teeth filing accompanied the rite. Eligibility required strict conduct, including abstention from premarital and extramarital relations.

## Music & performance

The instrument the sources document in detail is the bamboo Jew's harp, *lubuw*, long in wide use among the Atayal and played mostly solo. Its body is a piece of bamboo about 10 cm long with a long slot cut in the middle, into which a thin tongue-shaped reed of bamboo or copper is set; holes drilled at both ends take hemp ropes. The player holds the instrument between the lips with the left hand and plucks the rope at the right end with the right hand, the reed sounding by vibration; pitch is shaped by the mouth and the scale by the width and thickness of the reed. The *lubuw* is played for self-entertainment, for communication and in courtship between men and women, and suits young and old alike, but it is prohibited during hunting and at funerals. It can stand in for speech: a message that would be delivered orally is conveyed instead through the harp. The Atayal also have culturally specific dances, and the sources note that their musical instruments are distinctive to them, but give no further detail on song genres or performance occasions.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used here record no annual festival calendar, but they describe the life-cycle and hunting rites closely. Facial tattooing and teeth filing formed the coming-of-age initiation: before a face could be tattooed the candidate had to prove an adult accomplishment — for a man, taking the head of an enemy; for a woman, mastering weaving, learned from about the age of 10 or 12. Only the tattooed could marry, and only the tattooed could cross the *hongu utux*, the spirit bridge or rainbow, to the hereafter; the absence of a tattoo barred marriage and pushed a person to the margins of the community. Taken heads were highly honoured, given food and drink, and expected to bring good harvests. Hunting is ritually bounded: boys are trained from a young age under the elders' guidance, hunters are taught not to overhunt, not to deforest and not to take young animals, group hunts follow the leader's order, and women are forbidden to join a hunt or touch hunting tools on pain of hunters returning empty-handed or meeting danger. The ancestors lie buried beneath the house's beds, and the Atayal hold that smoke from the *hka'* carries messages to them, so that the ancestors hear promises made and vows are not broken. Headhunting was made illegal in the Japanese colonial era (1895–1945), and tattooing was banned in 1930 for its association with it; by 2018 only one tattooed elder survived, Lawa Piheg, tattooed at the age of 8, who died on 14 September 2019.

## Foodways

What the sources do record is the subsistence base and the hearth: the Atayal were traditionally self-sufficient through fishing, hunting, gathering and slash-and-burn farming, and common game included wild boar, Reeves's muntjac and goats. Cooking happens on the three-stone stove inside the living space, which serves at once for meals and for warmth, and whose fire also keeps the house dry, deters insects and slows the decay of the building's materials. Lighting a fire signals that someone is home, and fire stands for the life of the community.

## Oral tradition & literature

Atayal elders tell that the first ancestors appeared when a stone, *Pinspkan*, cracked open. Three people emerged and one went back into the stone. Of the man and woman who remained, the man was too shy to approach her, so the woman left home, blackened her face with coal to pass for a stranger, crept back after some days, and was taken for another woman; they lived happily and bore children, and the custom of facial tattooing is traced to her blackened face. The great body of transmitted knowledge is *gaga*, an unwritten code passed down the generations and interpreted and enforced by leaders and elders, who mediate disputes; it governs marriage, community conduct, ritual and hunting, and violation could bring ostracism or exclusion from ceremonies. Seniority is inscribed in names: a child takes the father's first name as a second element — the son of Yukan Dayung is Yumin Yukan. Documentation and revival have moved into print and online: Søren Egerod published an Atayal–English dictionary in 1980, a Bible translation was completed in 2003, Goderich (2020) reconstructed over 1,000 Proto-Atayal words, and an Atayal-language Wikipedia launched in April 2020 through Taiwan's Ministry of Education and National Chengchi University.

## Language & religion

Atayal is an Austronesian language, grouped into two dialect clusters, Squliq and C'uli' (Ts'ole'), with seven dialects recognised by Goderich (2020): Squliq, the prestige and most widespread form; S'uli in Hsinchu County and along the Da'an River; Klesan, relocated by the Japanese in the early 1900s and now spoken in villages such as Pyahaw, Ropoy, Ləlaŋan/Buta, Iyu and Kəŋyan; Matu'uwal (Mayrinax) along the Rinax River in Tai'an Township, Miaoli; Plngawan at Sasi village in Ren'ai Township, Nantou; Skikun in Datong Township, Yilan; and Matu'aw in Daxing, Tai'an. Matu'uwal and Pa'kuali' are unusual in distinguishing male and female speech registers. The language is written in the Latin script, with a standard orthography set by the government in 2005. In four communities of Datong and Nan'ao townships the everyday language is Yilan Creole Japanese, the island's only Japanese creole, which arose in the 1930s–40s from contact between colonists and Atayal speakers. Religious life turns on the ancestral spirits (*utux*, *rutux*) and the rainbow bridge; the introduction of Christianity hastened the decline of facial tattooing.
