## Overview

The Nganasans are a Samoyedic-speaking people native to the Taymyr Peninsula in north Siberia and recognized in the Russian Federation as one of the indigenous peoples of the Russian North. They live primarily in Ust-Avam, Volochanka, and Novaya in the Taymyrsky Dolgano-Nenetsky District of Krasnoyarsk Krai, with smaller populations in Dudinka and Norilsk. Traditionally semi-nomadic, they subsisted mainly through wild reindeer hunting and followed reindeer across the tundra between winter and summer territories. The 2002 Russian census recorded 862 Nganasans in Russia, while estimates cited in the source place their population at approximately 1,000. Their culture is especially notable for its hunting-based lifeways, animistic and shamanistic religion, and seriously endangered Samoyedic language.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources provide limited information about Nganasan clothing. They state that women worked as seamstresses decorating reindeer boots, indicating the importance of reindeer footwear in the material culture described. No vernacular names for garments, head coverings, belts, or ceremonial dress are supplied. The sources distinguish the work of women as seamstresses from the employment of most men as hunters.

### Architecture

They state that the Nganasans traditionally followed a semi-nomadic way of life and that, in the early 1970s, the state settled them with the Dolgans and Enets in the villages of Ust-Avam, Volochanka, and Novaya. These settlements were created around combined Nganasan kolkhoz.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention tools, machinery, consumer goods, and Soviet equipment as products that entered Nganasan life through trade or the planned economy. Russian traders exchanged sable furs for alcohol, tobacco, tea, and various tools. The sources also state that domesticated reindeer were used primarily for transport or eaten during periods of famine before collectivization.

## Music & performance

They do, however, document shamanic performance as an important part of traditional religion. Nganasan religion is described as animistic and shamanistic, and shamanism remained a living phenomenon into the beginning of the 20th century because of the group’s geographic isolation. The last notable Nganasan shaman’s séances were recorded on film by anthropologists in the 1970s. The source bibliography also refers to the Nganasan shamanistic tradition and to the incantations of Tubyaku Kosterkin. The Nganasan language source states that Radio Taimyr, broadcasting from Dudinka, has transmitted daily programs in Nganasan since 1990; these broadcasts are described as language media rather than as a musical tradition.

## Dance & theatre

They mention films documenting Nganasan shamanism and the last notable shaman’s séances.

## Festivals & rituals

The best-documented ritual practice is shamanism. Traditional Nganasan religion is animistic and shamanistic, and the group’s geographic isolation allowed shamanism to remain relatively free of foreign influence until recent history. It continued as a living phenomenon into the beginning of the 20th century. Anthropologists filmed the last notable Nganasan shaman’s séances in the 1970s.

## Foodways

Nganasan foodways are documented chiefly through hunting, trade, and changes associated with Soviet collectivization. Wild reindeer hunting was traditionally the main form of subsistence, in contrast to the reindeer herding practiced by the Nenets. Domesticated reindeer were used primarily for transport or eaten during periods of famine. After collectivization, the Nganasans were compelled to expand their small stock of domesticated reindeer, while later settlement and state hunting enterprises reorganized their food economy. After the Nganasans began working for gospromkhoz Taymirsky, hunted meat was supplied to the industrial center Norilsk. With new Soviet equipment, the yield of hunted wild reindeer reached 50,000 in the 1980s. The sources also state that Nganasans exchanged sable furs for alcohol, tobacco, tea, and various tools.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do identify the Nganasan language as a subject of grammatical and lexicographic documentation, including the *Chrestomathia Nganasanica*, a collection involving texts, translation, glossary, and grammar. The bibliography also includes work on Nganasan incantations, including “The Incantations of Tubyaku Kosterkin,” and studies of Nganasan shamans from the Kosterkin family. The Nganasan language source records that the language was spoken rather than written until the 1980s and that a Cyrillic-based alphabet was devised in the 1990s. These materials represent preservation and scholarly documentation.

## Language & religion

Nganasan is the most divergent language of the Samoyedic branch of the Uralic language family. Its two main dialects are Avam and Vadeyev. The language is considered highly endangered: the supplied sources variously state that about 30 Nganasan people speak it, that only 125 could speak it in 2010, and that at most 500 could still speak it with very limited proficiency among those 18 and younger. Russian has become the first language of many Nganasans. The language has a Cyrillic-based alphabet devised in the 1990s. Its traditional religion is animistic and shamanistic, and shamanism remained active into the beginning of the 20th century.
