## Overview

The Itelmens are an indigenous ethnic group of the Kamchatka Peninsula in Russia, residing primarily in the valley of the Kamchatka River. Their language, Itelmen, is distantly related to Chukchi and Koryak within the Chukotko-Kamchatkan language family, but it is now virtually extinct, with the vast majority of ethnic Itelmens being native speakers of Russian. The 2010 Russian census recorded 3,193 Itelmens; the 1989 census recorded 2,400, and the 2002 census recorded 3,180. Before the Russian conquest, native peoples of Kamchatka had a substantial hunter-gatherer and fishing society. Itelmen culture includes distinctive river-based settlement patterns, seasonal houses, fishing and food-processing practices, gendered labor, and a polytheistic religious system.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Itelmen clothing was made from dog and reindeer skins, which were tanned, dyed, and sewn into garments worn by men and women. Women performed the tanning, dyeing, and sewing of these skins, while the source describes the resulting garments only in general terms.

### Architecture

Itelmens used different houses in summer and winter. The winter house was inhabited beginning in November and was dug into the soil 90–150 cm in the shape of a rectangle. Its walls were covered with sticks and straw, and four central beams supported a roof formed from rafters, straw, and excavated dirt. An opening in the roof served as a smoke hole and entrance. A passageway opposite the fireplace faced the river and was left open only when fires were lit. Sleeping quarters were separated by pieces of wood, with straw mats and reindeer or seal skins used as bedding.

In summer, families lived in raised houses called *pehm* or *pehmy*. The Cossacks called these structures balagans. They were pyramids on raised platforms, with doors on the south and north sides, allowing storage beneath the raised floor in the wet climate. Villages also contained ground-level straw huts used for cooking, boiling salt from seawater, and rendering fat. Before the arrival of the Russians, villages were surrounded by an earthen wall or palisade.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do describe tools and storage objects. Women made mats, bags, baskets, and boxes from grass for storage and transportation. Women also cut straw with bone sickles made from bear shoulder blades. The sources mention wooden construction, including beams, rafters, walls, and the woodwork carried out by men.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do describe religious beliefs and a yearly practice connected with the devil Kamma. Kamma was said to live in a tree outside Nizhnoi village, and the tree was annually shot with arrows.

The Itelmens subscribed to a polytheistic religion. The creative god was called *Kutka* or *Kutga*. He was regarded as the creator of all things, although Steller described a lack of veneration for him. The Itelmens attributed life’s difficulties to his stupidity and scolded or cursed him. Kutka was believed to be married to Chachy, an intelligent woman who corrected him and prevented much foolishness. He was said to have lived on the greatest rivers of the Kamchatka Peninsula and to have left a son and daughter for each river. They state that many Itelmens later accepted baptism into the Russian Orthodox Church, while animism remained widespread in practice.

## Foodways

Fish was central to Itelmen foodways. The sources state that Itelmens seldom observed fixed eating times except when entertaining and seldom ate as a family except when eating *opana*, meaning warm food, or fresh fish. They generally ate cold food rather than fried food. Fish eggs with willow or birch bark were a common staple.

A festive food called *selaga* was a mash made from sarana, pine nuts, fireweed, cow parsnip, bistort roots, and various berries. These ingredients were cooked in seal, whale, or fish oil. In Lopatkan, a fermented berry drink was consumed, although the source gives no indication that other Itelmen settlements made fermented drinks.

Women prepared the whole fish supply except fermented fish and dog food, which were left to men. Women also gathered seeds, berries, and fireweed, which was used as a type of tea. Straw huts were used for cooking food, boiling salt from seawater, and rendering fat.

## Oral tradition & literature

 Georg Wilhelm Steller provided one of the few sources describing the Itelmens before assimilation.

They do state that the Itelmen language is the subject of revival attempts. By 1993, fewer than 100 elderly speakers remained, while the 1989 census recorded that fewer than one Itelmen in five could speak the language.

## Language & religion

Itelmen is the language of the Itelmens. Its name is given as Итәнмән, romanized as Itənmən; the Russian name is Ительмены, romanized as Itel'meny. Itelmen is distantly related to Chukchi and Koryak and belongs to the Chukotko-Kamchatkan language family. The language is now virtually extinct, and Russian became the norm in schooling in the 1930s. Steller described substantial dialect variation from river to river because Itelmens primarily communicated with communities sharing the same river.

Itelmen religion was polytheistic. The Itelmens worshiped spirits including Mitgh, who lived in the ocean in the form of a fish; forest sprites called *ushakhtchu*, said to resemble people; and mountain gods called *gamuli*, or little souls, who lived in the high mountains, especially volcanoes. The god *billukai* was believed to inhabit the clouds and to cause thunder, lightning, and storms. Kamma was the devil associated with the tree outside Nizhnoi village. Later, Russian Orthodox baptism became widespread, while animism continued in practice.
