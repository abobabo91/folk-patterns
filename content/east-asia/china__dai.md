## Overview

The Dai are Tai-speaking peoples in southern Yunnan counted as one of China's 56 officially recognized ethnic groups. They practice Theravada Buddhism and maintain their own script, calendar, and monastic schools where boys learn to read and write. The Dai are known for their patterned woven cloth that reached European museums in the twentieth century. Their material culture reflects a subtropical setting with bamboo, rice, and water-based customs.

## Material culture

Dai material culture centers on woven and embroidered cloth, with patterned fabrics as the most documented tradition, alongside bamboo work, oil-pressing, and winemaking.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Dai weavers create patterned cloth using supplementary wefts, indigo patterning, and embroidery, with eight-sided star motifs and transverse stripes as common designs.

- **Supplementary-weft cloth** — Fabric where the pattern is carried by extra weft floats rather than printed or applied.
- **Indigo patterned lengths** — Lengths of indigo cotton cloth with multi-coloured patterning and fringes worked at both ends.
- **Patterned bedding cloth** — Cotton bedsheets and bed blankets in patterned weave, some fringed at one end only.
- **Embroidered cloth** — Worked textiles of cotton ground with cotton thread, and two-part belts combining cotton and silk.
- **Plaited leaf containers** — Lidded boxes of leaf strips in plain weave, dyed black and red or left natural yellow, with surfaces that mimic patterned cloth.

### Clothing & dress

Dai dress consists of women's tube skirts and jackets in plain cotton, cotton with silk, or silk alone, completed by embroidered belts.

- **Tube skirts** — Women's garments in three material combinations — plain cotton, cotton with silk, and silk alone — suggesting a graded wardrobe from workaday to best.
- **Embroidered belt** — A two-part belt combining cotton and silk, the most decorated single garment element recorded.
- **Cotton jacket** — A plain cotton jacket worn by Dai women as part of everyday dress.

### Architecture

Dai villages stand on bamboo plains near rivers with raised stilt houses, some square in plan and a few two-storeyed, alongside Buddhist temples that serve as schools.

- **Raised stilt houses** — Houses raised on stilts, some square in plan, with open undercrofts for storage beneath the dwelling.
- **Two-storeyed houses** — Some Dai houses have an upper floor for living space and a lower level without walls, left as open storage.
- **Buddhist temples** — Every Dai village had at least one temple, and larger villages two to five, serving as schools where boys learned to read and write as novices.
- **Jinghong Golden Hall Kingdom** — A twelfth-century kingdom with its capital at Jinghong.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Dai are known for plant-fibre craft including bamboo work, with no documented ceramic or metalworking tradition.

- **Bamboo tube** — A fragrant bamboo tube in which glutinous rice is baked, used as both a cooking vessel and serving dish.
- **Plaited leaf box** — A lidded box plaited from dyed leaf strips used for storage.
- **Woven vegetable fibre flag** — A model flag of woven and dyed vegetable fibre catalogued as religious or ritual equipment.

## Festivals & rituals

Dai festivals are mostly religious, centring on Buddhist observance and the New Year, with three major festivals marking the religious calendar.

- **Door closing festival** — Falls on the fifteenth day of the ninth month of the Dai calendar, the middle of July, when everyone goes to the temple offering food, flowers and coins to the Buddha.
- **Door opening festival** — Falls on the fifteenth day of the twelfth Dai month, the middle of October, marking the end of the three-month closed season.
- **Water Splashing Festival** — The Dai New Year falling in the late sixth or early seventh month of the Dai calendar, the middle of April, when young men and women pour water over one another as a blessing.
- **Monastic noviceship** — Parents commonly sent sons between seven and eighteen to a monastery to stay three years or more learning to read, write and practise the faith.

## Foodways

Rice is the staple, with glutinous bamboo rice and pineapple purple rice as the best-known snacks, and Dai cooking characterized as raw, fresh, sour and spicy.

- **Bamboo rice** — Glutinous rice packed into a fragrant bamboo tube, soaked in water and then baked over a fire.
- **Pineapple purple rice** — A well-known Dai dish.
- **Sour bamboo shoots** — A sour dish built around sourness, the finest flavour in Dai cuisine.
- **Sour pork** — A sour pork dish exemplifying the Dai preference for sour flavours.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Dai have a substantial written and oral literature including historical documents, legends, stories, poetry, fables and astronomical texts written in Dai script.

- **Dai calendar** — A calendar that begins in the year 638, with astronomical literature including many books on calculating solar and lunar eclipses.
- **Historical documents** — Plentiful written records along with legends, stories, poetry, fables and children's stories.

## Language & religion

Dai peoples speak Southwestern Tai languages of the Tai–Kadai family and practice Theravada Buddhism, with earlier animism continuing within Dai Buddhist belief.

- **Tai Lü** (*Sipsongpanna Dai*) — One of the four major Tai languages spoken by the Dai, with roughly 400,000 speakers.
- **Tai Nüa** (*Dehong Dai*) — One of the four major Tai languages spoken by the Dai, with roughly 400,000 speakers.
- **Dai script** — The written script used by the Dai, with literacy among Dai men reported today to exceed 80 percent.
- **Theravada Buddhism** — The dominant religion of the Dai, adopted during the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, with earlier animism continuing within Buddhist belief.
- **Dai Paxi** (*Dai Hui*) — A small Muslim minority descended from Hui merchants who settled in Xishuangbanna in the early nineteenth century and share language, customs, rituals and diet with Buddhist Dai.

## Glossary

- *Dai* — Han Chinese umbrella term for several Tai-speaking peoples of southern Yunnan
- *Tai Lü* — A Southwestern Tai language, also called Sipsongpanna Dai
- *Tai Nüa* — A Southwestern Tai language, also called Dehong Dai
- *Tai Dam* — A Southwestern Tai language spoken by the Dai
- *Tai Ya* — A Southwestern Tai language also called Tai Hongjin
- *Dai Paxi* — Muslim minority Dai descended from Hui merchants
- *Theravada Buddhism* — The form of Buddhism practiced by the Dai since the fifteenth to sixteenth centuries
- *Dai calendar* — The Dai calendar beginning in the year 638
- *Xishuangbanna* — Dai Autonomous Prefecture in southern Yunnan
- *Dehong* — Dai and Jingpo Autonomous Prefecture in southern Yunnan
- *Water Splashing Festival* — The Dai New Year celebration in the middle of April
- *door closing festival* — Buddhist observance on the fifteenth day of the ninth Dai month
- *door opening festival* — Buddhist observance on the fifteenth day of the twelfth Dai month
- *bamboo rice* — Glutinous rice baked in a fragrant bamboo tube
- *Dai script* — The written script of the Dai with high male literacy rates
- *supplementary wefts* — Extra weft floats that carry pattern in woven cloth
- *Tai–Kadai* — Language family that includes Southwestern Tai languages spoken by the Dai
- *novices* — Boys sent to monasteries to learn reading, writing and Buddhist practice
