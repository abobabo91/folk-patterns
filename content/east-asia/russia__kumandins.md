## Overview

The Kumandins, natively *Kumandy* or *Kuvandy(g)*, are a Turkic Indigenous people of Siberia who reside mainly in the Altai Krai and Altai Republic of the Russian Federation. They speak the Northern Altai Kumandin language. According to the 1926 census, 6,335 Kumandins lived within Russia; the 2010 census recorded 2,892, although the earlier figure may have included related peoples. Some Kumandins lived along the Biya River, from the Kuu River downstream almost to Biysk, and along the lower Katun River. Their folk-culture record includes taiga hunting, cedar-nut gathering, bark and log dwellings, distinctive dress, hunting legends, fairy tales, shamanism, and Burkhanism.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Traditional Kumandin dress included short breeches, linen shirts, and single-breasted robes. The sources do not distinguish everyday and ceremonial clothing, or provide information about head coverings, belts, footwear, or separate men’s and women’s dress.

### Architecture

Traditional Kumandin dwellings included polygonal yurts made from bark or logs and topped with a conic bark roof. Other dwellings included conic yurts made from bark or perches.

## Festivals & rituals

 They state that most modern Kumandins are Orthodox Christians, while shamanism and Burkhanism are also practiced by some.

## Foodways

The Kumandins were originally hunters, and animals living in the taiga were vital to the local subsistence economy. Around the 19th century, they took up picking cedar nuts as an additional economic activity.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kumandin hunting legends and fairy tales preserve memories associated with remote ancestors of the *Ton* seok, including stories about milking deer, which is attributed to Kumandy mountain spirits. The sources explain the name *Ton* as an ethnonym connected with “deer” and “reindeer breeder.”

## Language & religion

Kumandins speak the Northern Altai Kumandin language, described in the source as belonging to the Turkic linguistic sphere. The source connects Kumandin language with the Kypchak group and states that it resembles the language of the Tobol-Irtysh and Baraba Tatars. Most modern Kumandins are Orthodox Christians, while shamanism and Burkhanism are also practiced by some.
