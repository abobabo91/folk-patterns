## Overview

The Bouyei (*Buxqyaix*; Chinese 布依族; also spelled Puyi, Buyei, Buyi) are a Tai-speaking people of the plains of southern Guizhou in China, concentrated in the Qianxinan and Qiannan prefectures and present also in Yunnan, Sichuan and the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, with newer communities in the coastal provinces of Zhejiang and Guangdong. The 2020 census recorded 3,576,752 Bouyei, making them the tenth largest of the 56 ethnic groups officially recognised by the People's Republic of China; some 3,000 more live in northern Vietnam, where the Bố Y are one of 54 recognised groups. Their language belongs to the Northern Tai group of the Tai–Kadai family. In folk-culture terms they are known regionally for their handicrafts and batik, and the objects this atlas shows are almost entirely women's and girls' dress.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Batik** — wax-resist patterning on cotton; the sources state that Bouyei batiks, together with their other handicrafts, are renowned throughout the region. The British Museum woman's jacket in this collection combines batik with embroidery and appliqué on a single cotton garment.

**Embroidery and appliqué on cotton and silk** — the dominant technique in the catalogued objects: a girl's apron and a woman's apron, a pair of apron-ties, a pair of tie-belts and a baby-carrier are all recorded as cotton (sometimes with silk) worked in embroidery, appliqué or both.

**Embroidered ribbons** — narrow cotton-and-silk bands made to be sewn onto clothing; three are held as separate catalogued objects, indicating that ornament was produced as stock and applied to garments afterwards rather than worked only in place.

**Striped and patterned cloth** — the woman's head cover is a length of striped, patterned and embroidered cotton, the one piece in the group whose pattern is described as woven rather than applied.

The sources name no motifs, so no motif vocabulary is given here.

### Clothing & dress

The sources document dress only through the catalogued objects, and these are all female garments and accessories. A woman's outfit is represented by a cotton jacket decorated with batik, embroidery and appliqué; cotton trousers; an embroidered apron of cotton and silk with a matching pair of embroidered, appliquéd apron-ties; and a pair of cotton-and-silk tie-belts, recorded as belts worn in pairs. The head is covered with a length of striped, patterned, embroidered cotton rather than a shaped hat. Footwear is represented by a single pair of straw sandals. A small girl's apron, appliquéd and embroidered in cotton and silk, shows the same construction as the adult garment at child scale, and an embroidered baby-carrier of cotton and probable silk extends the same decorative repertoire to infant care. The sources do not describe men's dress, nor do they distinguish ceremonial from everyday clothing.

### Architecture

The sources used do not cover Bouyei house form, building materials or architectural decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources record no Bouyei pottery, woodwork or agricultural tools. The only everyday objects catalogued are a pair of straw sandals and the embroidered baby-carrier; the only metalwork is silver personal ornament, treated below. The wider economic picture the sources give is agricultural and commercial rather than artisanal: the Bouyei farm rice, millet, wheat, potatoes, maize, cocoa, tea and silk for consumption or sale, and have long acted as intermediate merchants in their region, now operating businesses at both small and large scale.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver is the documented material for Bouyei personal ornament. The collection holds a woman's silver bracelet and a woman's silver finger-ring, both catalogued simply by form and metal. The sources describe no other jewellery types and say nothing about tattooing, hair practices or other body adornment.

## Music & performance

The sources used do not cover Bouyei instruments, song genres or performance occasions.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used do not describe Bouyei dance. They do record a related textual tradition: *Nuo* books and Nuo scripts, written in the ancient Bouyei script, have circulated among the Bouyei of the Libo region for more than a thousand years, praising goodness, condemning evil, advocating filiality and promoting truth, kindness and beauty, to the point of serving as a local code of conduct. The sources present these as scriptures and texts and do not say how, or whether, they were performed.

## Festivals & rituals

Two annual observances are documented. **Ox King's Day** (牛王节), on April 8, honours oxen and their contribution to agricultural work. **June 6** — the sixth day of the sixth lunar month — is the important traditional holiday for ancestral worship, and the sources give the myth attached to it: Pangu, having mastered rice farming after creating the world, married the daughter of the Dragon King, and from their union the Bouyei arose. Their son Xinheng (新横) disrespected his mother, who returned to heaven and would not come down again despite the pleas of husband and son; Pangu remarried and died on the sixth day of the sixth month. Xinheng's stepmother mistreated him and nearly killed him, but when he threatened to destroy her rice harvest she recognised her fault, made peace with him, and the two began paying their respects to Pangu on that date each year. Ritual practice is also attested in writing: the collected ancient-script manuscripts are mostly shamans' books of the Bouyei ancestors, used to select auspicious days, lucky numbers and directions and for divination. Alongside these traditions the Bouyei celebrate festivals derived from Han culture, which the sources mention without naming. The sources describe no birth, coming-of-age, wedding or funeral rites.

## Foodways

The sources name no Bouyei dishes, cooking methods or ceremonial foods. They record only the crops grown: rice, millet, wheat, potatoes, maize, cocoa and tea, cultivated for consumption or for sale, with rice prominent enough to anchor both the Pangu origin myth and the quarrel that gave June 6 its meaning.

## Oral tradition & literature

The one named literary work is the epic poem ***Wang Yulian***, understood as a retelling of a Chinese story in the Bouyei language; hand copies of it remain popular in Zhexiang Township, Wangmo County, in the Qianxinan Bouyei and Miao Autonomous Prefecture. The Nuo books described above form the other body of traditional literature, with the shamans' divination manuscripts beside them. Mythology is represented by the Pangu and Dragon King's daughter narrative that accounts for Bouyei origins and for the June 6 observance. Documentation and standardisation have continued through the modern period: Yu Cuirong published an introduction to the language in 1980 and Wu Qilu a Bouyei–Chinese dictionary in 2002, while the survey work of Wu, Snyder and Liang (2007) remains the most comprehensive on Bouyei speech, and Edmondson and Gregerson (2001) documented the related varieties in Vietnam.

## Language & religion

Bouyei (*Haausqyaix*) is a Northern Tai language of the Tai–Kadai family with over 2.5 million native speakers, about 98% of them in China, and stands in a dialect continuum with Standard Zhuang. A 1950s government survey divided Guizhou Bouyei into Southern, Central and Western groups, the western dialects the least spoken and the most distinctive, some of them retaining aspirated stops uncommon in Northern Tai. Writing has three layers: an ancient script built from borrowed or imitated Chinese characters, similar to Sawndip, used for shamans' and Nuo manuscripts; a Latin alphabet based on the Longli County dialect, approved in 1956 and used from 1957 to 1960; and the current script, redesigned between 1981 and 1985 on Wangmo County speech and official since March 1985. Religious life combines ancestral worship and divination with Christianity: Catholicism entered Guizhou in 1714, missionaries devised a Latin-based Bouyei script for religious education around 1797–1800, the Diocese of Nanlong was established in 1879 at Nanlong (now Anlong County), and churches remain in Guizhou and Yunnan.
