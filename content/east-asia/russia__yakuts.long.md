## Overview

The Yakuts, who call themselves Sakha (Yakut: саха, *saxa*; plural сахалар, *saxalar*), are a Turkic people of North Siberia, concentrated in the Republic of Sakha (Yakutia) in the Russian Federation and also present in some districts of Krasnoyarsk Krai. Their historic core lies in the basins of the Middle Lena, the Aldan and the Vilyuy, which their ancestors reached from the Lake Baikal region beginning in the 13th century under pressure from the rising Mongols. The 2021 Russian census recorded 469,348 Yakuts in the Sakha Republic, 55.3% of its population. They speak Yakut, of the Siberian (Northern) branch of the Turkic languages. In folk-culture terms they matter as northern horse- and cattle-breeders — the northern groups hunted, fished and herded reindeer, the southern raised cattle and horses — whose material repertoire, as the objects here show, is built from reindeer fur, wood and mammoth ivory.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used name no Yakut weaving or embroidery tradition by a vernacular term, so the seed terms cannot be confirmed here and are omitted. What the catalogue records document instead is fur appliqué on sewn garments:

**Reindeer-fur appliqué** — A child's hooded coat in the British Museum is pieced and lined from many sections of reindeer fur and decorated with four rows of red triangular pieces of woollen cloth forming a panel 11 cm deep, with grey fur filling the gaps between the triangles on the front and brown fur used on the back; further vertical lines of red cloth triangles with grey fur run on the upper sides front and back near the sleeve ends and towards the back of the hood, with a single line worked around the neck flap and beside the hood.

One scholar cited in the Wikipedia account treats the consistency of related artistic embellishments on the traditional clothing of the Buryat, Samoyed and Yakut as evidence that these peoples are related — the only comparative claim about Yakut garment ornament the sources make.

A separate body of description concerns the **Tumat**, an earlier Vilyuy people remembered in Yakut tradition rather than the Yakuts themselves: bundles of deer fur dyed with red ocher were sewn into Tumatian jackets as adornment and Tumat hats were likewise dyed red, and from the Tumatian "tail-coat" the Yakuts called them "Dyirikinei", "chipmunk people" (Yakut: Sдьирикинэй). The sources compare this to red-dyed hats and sable tail-coats from Pazyryk culture sites and to Khakas and Kumandin clothing, and note similar garments recorded for 17th-century Evenks on the upper Angara and early 19th-century Evens on the lower Kolyma.

### Clothing & dress

Nothing in them distinguishes men's from women's dress.

### Architecture

Two things bear on the built environment indirectly. First, the British Museum holds a model of a summer encampment of the Yakuts, with figures carved from ivory and mounted on a wooden board; the record describes the model, not the structures. Second, in the account of the Tumat of the Vilyuy — remembered in Yakut tradition as a numerous and powerful society in constant conflict with the Yakuts — households are described as semi-subterranean with sod roofing, comparable to traditional Samoyed dwellings. Toponymic evidence from Yakutia is likewise read as showing former Paleoasian and Samoyed habitation in the region. Nothing in the sources extends either description to Yakut dwellings themselves.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The objects shown here are of wood and mammoth ivory; the sources record no Yakut ceramics or metalwork.

**Choron** — A *choron* is a wooden vessel for holding *kumis*, fermented mare's milk; the collection includes both a cup and a jar of this name, the drink being the one the cuisine section of the sources also singles out.

**Mammoth-ivory carving** — Carved from mammoth tusk are a set of game-pieces in two forms, cock figures and animal heads, together with an ivory model group of a dog sled and the ivory-figured model of a summer encampment noted above. The use of fossil tusk as the carving material is what the records consistently specify.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used record a single item of adornment: a mammoth-ivory ornament catalogued, with the museum's own uncertainty, as either a lip-ornament or an ear-ornament. They document no other jewellery types, no materials beyond ivory, and no tattooing, hair or body-marking practices.

## Music & performance

What the sources do record is that music is a recognised field of Yakut achievement and scholarship: Eduard Yefimovich Alekseyev is listed as a Yakut ethnomusicologist, the mezzo-soprano Sarantuya and Kjuregej — described as painter, actor and musician — as Yakut musicians, and "Music in the Sakha Republic" appears as a related subject in its own right. Beyond these names the sources carry no description of repertoire, tuning, ensemble or occasion. Two of the objects shown here touch on performance only obliquely: the *choron* vessels belong to the serving of *kumis*, and the ivory game-pieces — cock figures and animal heads — to play rather than to music.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used do not cover Yakut dance or dramatic traditions; they name no dance, no masked or puppet form and no performance occasion. The only adjacent detail is that Albina Borisova is listed among notable Yakuts as a theatrical translator.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources record several ritual attachments. Certain rock formations named *Kigilyakh*, and places such as Ynnakh Mountain, are held in high esteem by Yakuts. By the 1820s almost all Yakuts claimed to have converted to the Russian Orthodox church, but they retained — and still retain — a number of tengri practices. Among the Khoro (Khorin, Khorolors, Khori) Sakha, whose progenitor is held to be Uluu Khoro rather than Omogoy or Ellei, religious devotion focused on the Raven, noted in the mid-18th century by Lindenau as being addressed as "Our ancestor", "Our deity" and "Our grandfather"; the reverence is traced to the Raven's gift of a flint and tinder box to a struggling human, either the first Khoro man or his mother. The sources place this within a wider circum-Bering pattern of Raven reverence shared with the Haida, Tlingit and Tshisham of the North American Pacific Northwest Coast and with the Chukchi, Itelmen and Koryaks of the Siberian coast. The mythic mother goddess Aisyt (Ajysyt/Ajyhyt) is named as a related subject, as is Yakut shamanism, without further description.

## Foodways

Yakut subsistence rests on animal husbandry — horses, chiefly the Yakutian horse, reindeer, and the Sakha Ynagha or "Yakutian cow", a hardy cattle breed adapted to the local weather. The cuisine of Sakha, which the sources state is used only in Yakutia, prominently features the drink *kumis* and dairy products of both cow and mare milk. Fish and horse are the other two pillars: *stroganina* (строганина), sliced frozen salted fish; frozen fish generally; fried carp and carp soup; horse meat; and *oyogos*, described as loaf meat dishes. *Salamat* is a millet porridge with butter and horse fat, and thick pancakes are also named. *Kuerchekh* (Куэрчэх), also given as kierchekh, is a popular dessert made of cow milk or cream with various berries. *Indigirka* is a traditional fish salad. The *choron* cups and jars shown in this atlas are the vessels for *kumis*, tying the object set directly to the drink at the centre of the cuisine. The sources describe no tea or coffee custom and no dietary prohibitions.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used do not name a Yakut epic, but they show oral history carrying the weight of origin tradition. Nikolai S. Schukin's mid-19th-century "A Trip to Yakutsk" was built on local oral accounts of a northward migration from the southern Yenisey watershed to the Lena. Traditional Yakut histories tell of the aboriginal peoples of Yakutia: in the Bulunsky and Verkhoyansky districts, that the Black Yukaghir (Yakut: хара дъукаагырдар) descended from migrants pushed north from the Lena; in Ust'-Aldanskiy Ulus and the Megino-Kangalassky District, that tribes left under Yakut pressure, some remaining to intermarry while most went to the northern tundra. Khoro folklore centres on the Raven, with some tales of the Eagle. Remembered individually is Džaardaakh (Russian: Джаардаах), a Tumatian woman renowned for physical strength and repute as an archer, who married a Yakut man and is counted a notable ancestor of the Vilyuy Yakut; her name has been linked to a Yukaghir word for ice (Yukaghir: йархан). Written Yakut literature began to rise in the late 19th century, with a national revival in the early 20th; Sardana Oyunskaya is listed as a folklorist, alongside the poets Natalia Kharlampieva and Pyotr Toburokov, the writer Anastasia Syromyatnikova and the translator and critic Aita Shaposhnikova.

## Language & religion

Yakut, or Sakha, belongs to the Northern branch of the Siberian Turkic languages and is most closely related to Dolgan, and more distantly to Tuvan and Shor. By the 2010 census some 87% of Yakuts in the Sakha Republic were fluent in Yakut and 90% in Russian; the sources report a widespread view among other minorities in Russia, including the neighbouring Buryats, that the Sakha are the least russified people in Russia and that knowledge of the language is correspondingly broad. The ethnonym itself is contested: Alexey Kulakovsky derived Russian *yakut* from Evenki екэ, *yekə̄*, Marjorie Mandelstam Balzer from a Tungusic form, and Dávid Somfai Kara from Buryat *yaqud*, plural of *yaqa*. Religiously the population is Russian Orthodox by the conversion of the 1820s while retaining tengri practices, with shamanism and the mother goddess Aisyt named as associated subjects.
