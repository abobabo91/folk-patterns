## Overview

The Khalkha, also called Halh, have been the largest subgroup of the Mongols in what is now Mongolia since the 15th century. Most live in the modern state of Mongolia, while smaller Khalkha groups live in Inner Mongolia, Qinghai, and Rehe in China. The Khalkha are associated with central Mongolia, the northern part of Inner Mongolia, the Khangai Mountains, and the Khalkhin Gol. They number 2,659,985, or 83.8% of Mongolia’s population, according to the source. Most speak Khalkha or Halh, the standard written language of Mongolia. Their history includes the Khalkha Tumen, the Northern and Southern Khalkha groupings, relations with the Manchu-led Qing dynasty, and leadership of the Mongolian independence movement in the 20th century.

## Festivals & rituals

The source describes a special Khangai Mountain worshipping ceremony associated with Southern Khalkhas living in Inner Mongolia. Every new lunar year, Southern Khalkhas perform the ceremony, face northwest, and pray. The source states that this practice commemorates and signifies their origin in the Khangai Mountains and that it is maintained only by Southern Khalkhas, with no other Southern Mongols described as having such rituals. They also mention the influence of the Tibetan Dalai Lama and the “Yellow Hat” order in the history of Tsogtu Khong Taiji’s Khalkhas.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Wikipedia source records a historical text concerning the formation and purpose of the Khalkha Tumen. Its English translation describes dwelling in the Khangai Mountains, protection against alien enemies, support for precious life, and the Khalkha Tumen as a guard. The source presents this material in transliteration, Cyrillic, Mongolian script, and English translation, and refers to Mongolian historical sources such as *Erdeniin Erih* (“The Beads of Jewel”).

The source also identifies Byambyn Renchin as a Mongolian academician, writer, and scholar associated with this ethnic group.

## Language & religion

Most Khalkha speak Khalkha or Halh, which the source identifies as the standard written language of Mongolia. The source also distinguishes Khalkha from Oirat and Khorchin varieties spoken by other Mongol groups. It gives the names Khalkha and Halh in Latin transliteration, Cyrillic, and Mongolian script, and discusses possible connections between the term Khalkha and words glossed as “shield,” “to protect,” “to cover,” “to shield,” “to hide,” and “to intercept.”

 They do document historical references to the Dalai Lama, the “Yellow Hat” order, the “Red Hat” order, dGe-lugs-pa monasteries, and the Fifth Dalai Lama in connection with Khalkha groups in Qinghai.
