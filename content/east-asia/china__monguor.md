## Overview

The Monguor, also known as Tu people, White Mongol, or Tsagaan Mongol, are a Mongolic people and one of China’s officially recognized ethnic groups. They mostly live in Qinghai and Gansu provinces, with particularly dense Monguor settlement in Minhe County and communities in Huzhu and Datong counties. Their population was 281,928 according to the 2020 census. The Monguor language belongs to the Mongolic language family and has been strongly influenced by local Chinese and Tibetan dialects; nearly all Tu people also speak Chinese. Most are farmers, while some keep livestock. Their cultural life has been influenced by Tibetan Buddhism, Confucianism, Taoism, and local beliefs.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources describe no Monguor house form, building type, construction material, roof type, or architectural decoration. They do state that Monguor communities developed in Qinghai and Gansu after people from Western Xia crossed the Qilian Mountains during the Mongol assaults.

## Music & performance

They describe an *Older Brother’s Song*, also called “the Song of A Gan,” associated with Murong Wei after Tuyühu departed from the northeast. In the account given, the song expressed sadness and longing for Tuyühu; legends state that Murong Wei often sang it until his death and that it spread into central and northwest China.

The sources also state that the Monguor have carnival-like festivals and masked fertility rituals with similarities to Dionysian representations in Hellenistic times. No musical instruments, melodies, performance ensembles, or detailed performance contexts are provided. The Mongghul language has been documented in language materials and in long narrative songs from the Mongghul of Northeast Tibet, with texts in Mongghul, Chinese, and English.

## Festivals & rituals

They do mention carnival-like festivals and masked fertility rituals, describing similarities with Dionysian representations in Hellenistic times rather than with Byzantine Christian-era Greek celebrations.

The sources also describe a wedding ceremony attributed to Madam Lushi. In the account, she organized an ambush through an elaborate banquet combined with liquor and singing in order to subdue a bully named “Wang Mang.” This is the only specifically described wedding-related tradition in the supplied material. Birth, coming-of-age, funeral, mourning, and other life-cycle rites are not covered.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources preserve several traditions concerning Monguor and Xianbei origins. A wedding ceremony attributed to Madam Lushi is connected with an account of the Donghu. Legends explain the separation of Tuyühu from Murong Xianbei through a fight between their horses, while the historical account given describes an intense struggle over the Khanate position and disagreement about future directions.

The sources also discuss the *Older Brother’s Song*, or “the Song of A Gan,” associated with Murong Wei’s longing for Tuyühu. Monguor historical memory includes the figure of Tuyühu Khan, whom Monguor refer to as *Huozhou didi*. The sources further identify a publication of long narrative songs from the Mongghul of Northeast Tibet, with texts in Mongghul, Chinese, and English. Proverbs, riddles, named epics, and storytelling settings are not described.

## Language & religion

Monguor is a Mongolic language of the Shirongolic branch and part of the Gansu–Qinghai sprachbund, also called the Amdo sprachbund. The sources distinguish Mongghul in Huzhu Tu Autonomous County from Mangghuer in Minhe Hui and Tu Autonomous County. Mongghul has been strongly influenced by Amdo Tibetan, while Mangghuer has been influenced by Sinitic languages. A writing system for Huzhu Monguor was devised in the late 20th century but has been little used. A Cyrillic-based alphabet was developed in 1958, and a Pinyin-based Latin alphabet was developed from the 1970s to the 1980s.

Monguor cultural and social organizations have been influenced by Tibetan Buddhism, Confucianism, Taoism, and local beliefs. A few Tu in Huzhu and Minhe are Christian as a result of American and Korean missionary work in the area.
