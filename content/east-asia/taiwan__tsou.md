## Overview

The Tsou, also called *Cou*, are an Austronesian indigenous people of central southern Taiwan. They live in Chiayi County and Nantou County, with Tsou-speaking communities concentrated in the west-central mountains southeast of the Alishan area. The Tsou are traditionally based in the Alishan area, between Yushan and the Chianan Plain. They numbered around 6,000, approximately 1.19% of Taiwan’s total Indigenous population, making them the seventh-largest indigenous group. Their cultural profile is documented through oral histories of clan migrations, multi-clan settlements, distinctive clothing, and the endangered Tsou language. Their language has been affected by imposed education systems, migration to cities, and the increasing use of Mandarin.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

### Clothing & dress

Tsou traditional attire differs by gender and material. Men’s clothing is made of leather, while women’s clothing uses cotton, silk, or brocade. Men’s garments include fur hats, chest covers, long-sleeved jackets, fur vests, fur leg covers, and fur shoes. Women’s clothing comprises a black scarf, chest cover, long-sleeved jacket, dress, and leggings.

Feathers on a leather hat symbolize men’s bravery. Men may decorate their hats with feathers from eagles, Taiwan blue pheasants, Mikado pheasants, or condors. Warriors, chiefs, and marshals can add a red decorative band with pearls, jade, and seashells to the front of their hats. Red is associated with courage and strength and is particularly common in men’s clothing. The dress code is connected with age and social status, and leaders and warriors may wear special decorations. Those who have hunted wild boars can wear bracelets with wild boar tusks.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention copper bracelets and arm ornaments as forms of bodily adornment, but provide no further account of Tsou metalworking or production techniques.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Tsou body adornment includes copper bracelets and arm ornaments. People who have hunted wild boars can wear bracelets with wild boar tusks. Leaders, warriors, chiefs, and marshals may wear a red decorative band with pearls, jade, and seashells on the front of their hats. Feathers from eagles, Taiwan blue pheasants, Mikado pheasants, and condors are used on men’s hats to symbolize courage.

## Music & performance

They state that children may sing Tsou folk songs in kindergarten and may continue encountering cultural programs through elementary school. The sources also mention Tsou radio recordings archived with the Robert Blust collection at Kaipuleohone.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that Tsou is used among community elders in ceremonies and certain gatherings. Community members are also described as taking part in events intended to maintain the language, music, and culture.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tsou oral histories describe the migrations of the ancestors of ancient clans into the area between Yushan and the Chianan Plain. Each clan originally had its own settlement, and the first multi-clan town, Tfuya, formed approximately 1600 CE. The earliest written record of the Tsou dates from the Dutch colonial period and described Tfuya as a multi-clan settlement with approximately 300 people in 1647.

Contemporary preservation efforts include community programs, cultural learning programs, Tsou folk songs in kindergarten, and language instruction for elementary and middle school students. Elders are concerned that the language may not survive and welcome assistance from linguists. A lack of good teaching materials and the demands of studying English, Mandarin, and entrance-exam materials limit language learning.

## Language & religion

Tsou is an Austronesian language spoken by the Tsou people of Taiwan. The name Tsou literally means “person,” from Proto-Austronesian *Cau through regular sound changes. Tsou has traditionally been classified within a Tsouic branch of Austronesian, although Chang (2006) and Ross (2009) dispute that classification.

Four dialects are recorded: Tapangʉ, Tfuya, Duhtu, and Iimcu. Tapangʉ and Tfuya are still spoken; Iimcu has not been well described, and Duhtu and Iimcu are described as extinct in the village and dialect listings. Tsou is predicate-initial and has verbal, declarative, imperative, interrogative, equational, and existential clauses. The language is endangered because of Dutch, Spanish, Japanese, and Chinese education policies, the influence of Mandarin, and the movement of younger people to big cities.
