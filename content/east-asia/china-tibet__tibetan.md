## Overview

Tibetans live mainly on the Tibetan Plateau, with major centers in Lhasa, the Yarlung Tsangpo valley, Amdo, Khams, Shigatse, and Gyantse. Their culture reflects high-altitude herding, barley farming, regional trade, Tibetan Buddhism, and Bön. These influences shape clothing, houses, food, painting, music, and ritual life. Tibetan communities also live in Ladakh, Nepal, Bhutan, India, and around the world.

## Material culture

Tibetan material culture uses wool, sheepskin, stone, timber, metal, turquoise, coral, barley, and yak products to meet the needs of cold mountain life and religious practice.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Tibetan textiles include warm robes, striped aprons, religious paintings, offering scarves, carpets, brocades, and felt work.

- **Wrap robe** (*chuba*) — The chuba is a long wrap-style robe made from wool, sheepskin, cotton, or silk.
- **Monastic lower robe** (*shamthab*) — This wrapped, skirt-like lower garment forms part of Tibetan Buddhist monastic dress.
- **Striped married women’s apron** (*pangden*) — The pangden is a striped wool or wool-and-cotton apron that marks married and regional identity.
- **Religious scroll painting** (*thangka*) — A thangka is a portable religious painting on cotton or linen, mounted in silk brocade.
- **Offering scarf** (*khata*) — A khata is a pale ceremonial scarf offered during greetings, ceremonies, and visits to religious teachers.

### Clothing & dress

Tibetan clothing is made for cold, wind, high altitude, and pastoral work.

- **Wrap robe** (*chuba*) — The chuba is gathered at the waist to make a warm pouch and is worn by men and women.
- **Striped apron** (*pangden*) — Women may wear a pangden with a blouse, sash, jewelry, and outer robe.
- **Women’s headdress** (*perak*) — The perak is an elaborate headdress decorated with stones and worn especially in Ladakh and western Tibetan areas.
- **Monastic robes** — Monastic robes vary by school and rank and use red, maroon, yellow, or saffron textiles.

### Architecture

Tibetan buildings use thick walls, sunny sites, flat roofs, and local materials to handle mountain weather.

- **Fortified religious complex** — The Potala Palace in Lhasa is a major fortified complex with an administrative White Palace and a religious Red Palace.
- **Monastery** (*gompa*) — Gompas and village temples combine painted interiors, carved timber, murals, prayer wheels, and mani stones.
- **Yak-hair tent** — Nomadic families traditionally use black yak-hair tents.
- **Portable felt shelter** — Pastoral communities also use felt-covered portable structures.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Tibetan households use practical wooden, woven, leather, metal, and carved objects alongside ritual tools.

- **Prayer wheel** — Prayer wheels made of wood, metal, or stone contain written mantras or sacred texts.
- **Ritual vajra** (*vajra*) — Vajras are religious metal objects used in Tibetan ritual.
- **Ritual shrine box** (*ga'u*) — A ga'u is a decorated metal shrine box used to hold an image, text, or sacred substance.
- **Wooden bowl** — Wooden bowls are common Tibetan household objects.
- **Butter churn** — Butter churns are used in Tibetan homes and pastoral communities.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Tibetan adornment uses bright stones and metal to show wealth, regional identity, and religious protection.

- **Protective agate bead** (*dzi bead*) — A dzi bead is a polished agate bead marked with bands or eyes and worn as protection.
- **Women’s headdress** (*perak*) — The perak can cover much of the hair and extend down the back.
- **Protective shrine box** (*ga'u*) — A ga'u is worn or carried as a protective reliquary.
- **Turquoise and coral jewelry** — Turquoise inlay and coral necklaces are especially prominent in women’s dress.

## Music & performance

Tibetan music ranges from regulated monastic chant to participatory songs for work, weddings, festivals, and social gatherings.

- **Long-necked lute** (*dranyen*) — The dranyen is a well-known long-necked lute with a waisted wooden body and several strings.
- **Two-stringed fiddle** (*piwang*) — The piwang is a two-stringed fiddle.
- **End-blown flute** (*lingbu*) — The lingbu is an end-blown flute.
- **Long trumpet** (*dungchen*) — The dungchen is a long trumpet used in Tibetan music and ritual.
- **Central Tibetan song** (*lu*) — Lu is a Central Tibetan song tradition performed at gatherings, festivals, banquets, and courtly events.

## Dance & theatre

Tibetan performance combines sung stories, dance, masks, dialogue, percussion, and ritual action.

- **Tibetan opera** (*lhamo*) — Lhamo combines sung narrative, dance, dialogue, percussion, and brightly colored masks; UNESCO lists it as intangible cultural heritage.
- **Monastic masked dance** (*cham*) — Cham dancers wear masks of deities, protectors, animals, skeletons, or demons and enact the defeat of obstacles.
- **Circle and line dances** — Secular circle and line dances accompany festivals, weddings, and regional celebrations.

## Festivals & rituals

Tibetan ritual life centers on festivals, pilgrimage, offerings, family ceremonies, and Buddhist and Bön practices.

- **New Year festival** (*Losar*) — Losar is the New Year festival, when families clean houses, prepare foods, make offerings, visit monasteries, and exchange greetings.
- **Yogurt festival** (*Shoton*) — Shoton includes large thangka unveilings, opera, picnics, and public celebrations, especially around Lhasa.
- **Pilgrimage mantra** (*Om mani padme hum*) — Pilgrims recite Om mani padme hum while walking around sacred places, turning prayer wheels, and visiting mani walls.
- **Bön mantra** (*Om matri muye sale du*) — Om matri muye sale du is among the distinctive mantras used by Bön practitioners.
- **Barley-flour offering** (*torma*) — Torma are shaped barley-flour offerings used in Buddhist and Bön ceremonies.
- **Medicinal bathing** (*Lum*) — Tibetan Sowa Rigpa practitioners use hot springs, herbal water, or steam for health and illness prevention; UNESCO documents this living practice.

## Foodways

Tibetan food relies on barley, tea, yak products, meat, and foods that travel and cook well at high altitude.

- **Roasted barley flour** (*tsampa*) — Tsampa is roasted barley flour mixed with tea, butter, and sometimes sugar or salt.
- **Butter tea** (*po cha*) — Po cha is tea churned with salt and yak butter and served throughout the day.
- **Steamed dumplings** (*momo*) — Momo are steamed dumplings filled with meat or vegetables.
- **Yak dairy foods** — Yak yogurt, butter, and cheese are important foods in pastoral households.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tibetan oral and written traditions preserve heroic stories, religious knowledge, advice, history, and poetry.

- **Heroic epic** (*Gesar Epic*) — The Gesar Epic tells of King Gesar of Ling through long cycles of sung and recited performance, a tradition documented by UNESCO.
- **Tea and chang debate** (*The Dispute Between Tea and Chang*) — The Dispute Between Tea and Chang is a work of secular Tibetan writing.
- **Advice text** (*Khache Phalu’s Advice*) — Khache Phalu’s Advice is a work of secular Tibetan literature.
- **Epic king** (*Gesar*) — Gesar is the heroic king whose deeds are preserved by specialist bards.

## Language & religion

Tibetan religious and literary life uses several Tibetic languages, the Tibetan script, Buddhist traditions, and Bön practice.

- **Central Tibetan language** (*Ü-Tsang*) — Ü-Tsang varieties, including Lhasa Tibetan, are major forms of Central Tibetan.
- **Tibetan script** — The Tibetan script was developed in the imperial period and remains central to religious and literary transmission.
- **Buddhist stone** (*mani stone*) — Mani stones connect formal religion with everyday practice and pilgrimage.
- **Prayer flag** — Prayer flags mark sacred landscapes and are used in household and religious observances.
- **Religious teacher** (*lama*) — Lamas are part of Tibetan religious life and guide rituals and teachings.
