## Overview

The Buryats are a Mongol ethnic group indigenous to south-eastern Siberia and northernmost Mongolia. Their principal homeland is the Republic of Buryatia, which extends along the southern fringe of Russia and partially straddles Lake Baikal. Buryat populations also live in Ust-Orda Buryat Okrug, Agin-Buryat Autonomous Okrug, north-eastern Mongolia, and Inner Mongolia in China. Many Buryats reside in and around Ulan-Ude, the capital of the Buryat Republic, while others live in the countryside. They speak Buryat, a central Mongolic language, classified by UNESCO’s 2010 Atlas of the World’s Languages in Danger as “severely endangered.” Buryat folk culture includes nomadic herding, ger dwelling, Buddhism, shamanism, pastoral foodways, and traditions connected with Lake Baikal and the surrounding steppes, taiga, Sayan and Altai Mountains.

### Architecture

The Buryats share the use of gers for shelter with other Mongolic peoples. Eastern, or Transbaikal, Buryats may live in yurts, while western Buryats west of Lake Baikal and Olkhon became more Russified and abandoned nomadism for agriculture.

## Festivals & rituals

Buryat religious life includes shamanic and Buddhist ritual traditions. Traditional Buryat shamanism, also called Tengrism, is based on the worship of nature and a “triple division” of the physical and spiritual world. The spirit world is divided into the *tengeri*, the *bōxoldoy*, and the lower spirits. Human beings are likewise understood as consisting of the body, the “breath and life,” and the soul; the soul is further divided into a first, second, and third soul.

The number three and its multiples are sacred in Buryat tradition. The sources mention three major annual sacrifices, rituals in which shamans prolong the life of the sick by three or nine years, and a total of 99 *tengeri*. Animal sacrifice requires care to avoid damaging bones, because injury to the bones is understood to harm the soul and could cause the deity to reject the offering.

Buryat marriages traditionally involved the *kalym*, an exchange combining bride-wealth and dowry. The husband delivered an agreed number of cattle, while the bride’s kin supplied a yurt and essential household goods. The ceremony included the bride stoking the fire in the groom’s tent with three lumps of fat and sprinkling fat on the clothing of the groom’s father. Polygamy was permitted for men of considerable wealth.

## Foodways

Traditionally, Buryat subsistence centred on semi-nomadic pastoralism. Herds included cattle, sheep, goats, and camels, while local resources supplemented the diet. Buryat communities in Siberia rely mainly on dairy cattle and the cultivation of berries; some also grow wheat and rye. Communities on the slopes of the Sayan and Altai Mountains centre their way of life on breeding reindeer. Mongolian Buryats farm while remaining typically semi-sedentary, construct sheds and fences for livestock, and use hay as the principal fodder.

Buryat cuisine resembles Mongolian cuisine and includes *buuz* and *khuushuur*. Dairy products form a central part of the diet, and traditional main courses are generally hearty, plain, and meat-based. Fish, particularly *omul*, is common around Lake Baikal. Traditional Buryat medicine prescribed a balanced diet of meat, offal, plants, and herbs, and placed importance on mineral and thermal springs.

## Oral tradition & literature

The name “Buriyad” is first recorded as the name of one of the forest peoples in *The Secret History of the Mongols*, possibly dating to 1240. The text relates that Jochi, the eldest son of Genghis Khan, marched north in 1207 to subjugate the Buryats. The sources also describe historical traditions concerning the Barga, Khori-Tumed, Tumad, Bulagad, Khori, Ekhired, and Khongoodor groups, as well as the incorporation of Oirats, Khalkha, Tungus, Evenks, and others into the Buryat population.

Buryat shamanic traditions preserve records of shamanic lineage, and a descendant is preferred when a person receives the calling. Beliefs differ among traditional groups. Western Buryats along the Kuda river, for example, hold a belief in reincarnation of the third soul, associated in the source with exposure to Buddhism.

## Language & religion

Buryat is a central Mongolic language, also described as a Buryat-Mongol dialect of Mongolian. The Buryat language is classified as “severely endangered” in UNESCO’s 2010 edition of the Atlas of the World’s Languages in Danger. Around 1958, the Mongolian script was banned and replaced by the Cyrillic alphabet.

Buryat religious affiliation in the Republic of Buryatia is principally divided among Russian Orthodoxy, Buddhism, and those professing no religion. Shamanism has undergone a rural revival and includes Yellow shamanism, Black shamanism, or combinations of the two. The Buryats traditionally practised shamanism, with an emphasis on nature worship and a tripartite spiritual order. The majority of Buryats are adherents of Buddhism and converted to Tibetan Buddhism in the early eighteenth century under the influence of Tibetan and Mongolian missionaries. Christianity is present but is not regarded as a “Buryat” religion.
