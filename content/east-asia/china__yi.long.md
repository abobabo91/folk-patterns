## Overview

The Yi, who call themselves *Nuosu* (ꆈꌠ) and are known in Chinese as Yízú (彝族), are an ethnic group of southern China and the sixth largest of the 55 ethnic minorities recognized by the Chinese government; the 2020 census recorded 9,830,327 Yi. Over 4.5 million live in Yunnan, 2.5 million in southern Sichuan and 1 million in the northwest corner of Guizhou, with further populations in Guangxi; the Liangshan Yi Autonomous Prefecture alone holds about two million. Across the border in northern Vietnam, 4,827 Lô Lô — a Yi subgroup — were counted in 2019 in Hà Giang, Cao Bằng and Lào Cai. Nearly all Yi live in mountainous country, often on steep slopes far from cities, and the sharp altitudinal differences between Yi districts produce the local saying that "the weather is different a few miles away." They speak Loloish (Yi) languages of the Tibeto-Burman family, closely related to Burmese, and possess their own script, used for centuries by *bimo* priests.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here name no Yi weaving, embroidery, felting or dyeing tradition by its vernacular name, so no pattern tradition can be described. What they do document are the finished textile objects held in the museums this atlas draws on, all collected among the Yi at the Stone Forest in Yunnan in 1949 by Acher Eskenasy and L. van Bussel:

**Shoulder bag** — recorded in the Dutch catalogue as a travel bag (*reistas*) and in the collectors' own note simply as a "sholder bag"; several examples entered the collection together from the same Stone Forest acquisition.

**Baby carrier** — a cloth baby sling from the same 1949 Stone Forest group, catalogued as a baby carrier.

### Clothing & dress

Two points bearing on dress and appearance do appear in them: black is a revered colour in Yi culture, and the autonym Ni — the element behind Nuosu, Nasu, Nesu and Nisu — is sometimes translated as black for that reason. The museum objects shown alongside this profile are carried and worn accessories rather than garments: cotton shoulder bags and a baby carrier from the Stone Forest in Yunnan.

### Architecture

The sources record only the settlement pattern and one monumental practice: Yi villages lie on mountain slopes, the aristocratic and commoner castes historically lived in separate villages, and Old Yi stone tablets and steles stand in the Yi area, carrying texts in the pre-standardized script.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources name no Yi ceramic or metalworking tradition. One ritual object is catalogued in detail: a **family tablet** in the British Museum, classed at once as tablet, sculpture and religious or ritual equipment, and made of straw, wood, cotton cloth, bamboo and paper — a composite of perishable household materials rather than of carved stone or cast metal. The remaining documented everyday objects are the Stone Forest shoulder bags and baby carrier acquired in 1949. Two further material traditions are attested in the sources without being described as objects: the written transmission of Yi medical knowledge, which exists in records as well as in oral form, and the Old Yi stone tablets and steles.

## Music & performance

The Yi play a range of instruments documented across their territory. Wind instruments include the *bawu* (巴乌) and the *mabu* (马布). They also play the *hulu sheng*, but — unlike other minority peoples of Yunnan — not for courtship or love songs (*aiqing*); the gourd pipe sits outside the Yi courtship repertoire. Large plucked and bowed string instruments are played as well. Among the Liangshan Yi the most commonly encountered instrument is the *kouxian*, a small four-pronged instrument comparable to a Jew's harp. Kouxian pieces are usually improvised rather than fixed, and are expected to reflect either the mood of the player or the surrounding environment; occasionally they too serve the *aiqing* function that the hulu sheng does not. Against this instrumental repertoire stands a body of ritual sound that the sources treat as religious rather than musical: the ritual texts transmitted in the Yi script by *bimo* priests from one generation to the next, and the exorcisms conducted against the ghost Jiegujienuo. The most publicly visible Yi performance form is dance, which the sources describe as the most commonly recognized kind of Yi musical performance.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name no individual Yi dance and no Yi dramatic or puppet tradition. They establish the performance context: Yi dance is the most widely recognized form of Yi musical performance and is frequently performed at publicly sponsored holidays and festival events — which, in the sources' account of the Yi calendar, means above all the Torch Festival.

## Festivals & rituals

The **Torch Festival** is one of the Yi people's main holidays and falls on the 24th day of the sixth month of the lunar calendar. Its legend tells of two men of great strength, Sireabi in heaven and Atilaba on earth. Hearing of Atilaba's strength, Sireabi challenged him to wrestle; after two defeats he was killed in a bout, and the enraged bodhisattvas sent a plague of locusts against the earth. Atilaba cut down pine trees and used them as torches to burn the locusts and save the crops, and the festival is held in his honour.

Life-cycle practice in the sources is dominated by descent, caste and death. Descent and inheritance were patrilineal, men practised polygamy and levirate marriage, and women were excluded from the oral genealogies. Caste membership in all four strata — the aristocratic *nuohuo*/nzymo Black Yi, the commoner *qunuo*/*quho* White Yi, the *ajia* and the *xiaxi* — passed by patrilineal descent, and the two upper castes did not intermarry; as late as the 1980s and early 1990s nuohuo clans still blocked or punished marriage with quho, and the ethnographer quoted in the sources notes that families feared being boycotted by other nuohuo if they married out. Funerary custom was a flashpoint with the state: under the Qing policy of *gaitu guiliu* and assimilation pressed by the Viceroy Ortai, Yi living near Kunming were forced to give up cremation for burial, which triggered rebellions that the dynasty suppressed. Ritual against misfortune is also recorded: the ghost Jiegujienuo, blamed for dizziness, slowness, dementia and anxiety, was fought with exorcisms for which the bimo erected sacred sticks, the *kiemobbur*, at the ritual site.

## Foodways

The sources note only that Yi women drank and smoked noticeably more than Han Chinese women, and that farming was done by the White Yi and by slaves rather than by the Black Yi aristocracy.

## Oral tradition & literature

Yi oral tradition opens with an origin myth in which all life came from water, water came from snowmelt, and the dripping snowmelt formed a creature called the Ni, which gave birth to all life — Ni being another name for the Yi themselves. The common ancestor was *Apu Dumu* (ꀉꁌꅋꃅ / ꀉꁌꐧꃅ, Axpu Ddutmu or Axpu Jjutmu), whose three wives each bore two sons; the six sons migrated to the area of present-day Zhaotong and founded the Wu, Zha, Nuo, Heng, Bu and Mo clans. The great hero of Yi mythology is *Zhyge Alu*, son of a dragon and an eagle, possessed of supernatural strength and of powers against magic and ghosts, who rode a nine-winged flying horse called "long heavenly wings." He was helped by a magical peacock, *Shuotnyie Voplie*, whose cry deafened those who heard it but which consumed evil and expelled leprosy when invited indoors, and by the python *Bbahxa Ayuosse*, which he defeated by transforming into a dragon and wrestling it in the ocean, and which could detect leprosy, cure tuberculosis and eradicate epidemics. Like the Chinese archer Hou Yi, Zhyge Alu shoots down the suns to save the people; in Bimoism he aids the bimo in curing leprosy and fighting ghosts. Genealogical recitation is its own verbal art, chained father to son (Aho Ddezze—Ddezze Zuluo—Zuluo Jjiha—Jjiha Lomusse—Lomu Shuogge). Works of history, literature and medicine and ruling-family genealogies written in the Old Yi script remain in use. In contemporary documentation, the artist Colette Fu, great-granddaughter of the Yi military governor of Yunnan Long Yun, has photographed Yi communities in Yunnan from 1996 onward for her pop-up book series *We are Tiger Dragon People*.

## Language & religion

The Yi speak Loloish (also Ngwi or Nisoic) languages, a sub-branch of 50–100 Tibeto-Burman languages centred on Yunnan and most closely related to Burmese. The Chinese government recognizes six mutually unintelligible Yi languages: Northern Yi (Nuosu), Western Yi (Lalo), Central Yi (Lolopo), Southern Yi (Nisu), Southeastern Yi (Sani) and Eastern Yi (Nasu). Northern Yi, with some two million speakers, is the literary standard; code-switching with Standard Chinese is common in Yunnan, Guizhou and Guangxi. The Yi script, logosyllabic in origin and dating to at least the 13th century, appears independent of any other known script; it had perhaps 10,000 largely regional characters, was the preserve of bimo priests until the early 20th century, and was standardized into a syllabary in the 1970s and 1980s, now used in books, newspapers, street signs and schooling. The indigenous religion is **Bimoism**, served by the bimo. Some Yi in Yunnan have adopted Buddhism through contact with the Dai and Tibetans, their chief deity being Mahākāla; many converted to Christianity in the 20th century after Gladstone Porteous arrived in 1904 and China Inland Mission medical missionaries followed, and in 1991 as many as 1,500,000 Yi Christians were reported in Yunnan, concentrated in Luquan County with its more than 20 churches.
