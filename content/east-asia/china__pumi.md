## Overview

The Pumi, also called Primi or Premi, are one of the 56 ethnic groups officially recognized by China. They are an officially recognized minority nationality associated with Yunnan and are ethnically related to the Tibetans of Mili Tibetan Autonomous County and Yanyuan County in Sichuan. Their population is given as 30,000. Communities are found in Lanping Bai and Pumi Autonomous County, Ninglang Yi Autonomous County, Lijiang Old Town, Yulong Naxi Autonomous County, Weixi Lisu Autonomous County and Yongsheng County, typically at elevations above 9,000 feet (2,700 m). Their language belongs to the Qiangic branch of the Tibeto-Burman family. Pumi folk culture combines Tibetan influence, local religious practices, distinctive clothing, food preservation and the household rituals associated with cooking fires.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Pumi clothing varies by locality and gender. In Ninglang and Yongsheng, women often wear jackets buttoned down one side, long pleated skirts and multi-colored wide belts. Because of the cold weather, a goatskin is worn over the back. In Lanping and Weixi, women tend to wear colored long-sleeved jackets beneath vests, together with long trousers fastened by embroidered belts.

Pumi men tend to wear sleeveless goatskin or linen jackets with long trousers and a Tibetan hat. Hunters and warriors may carry a long Tibetan sword and deerskin bags. At the age of thirteen, Pumi boys undergo ablution rites of manhood; only after ablution may they put on adult clothing and participate in society’s activities.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do identify the cooking fire ring as an important object within many Pumi homes. Guests should never touch the ring, and a *Guazei*, consisting of small stone towers, is located behind the fire rings.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Pumi women plait their hair with yak tail hairs and silk threads, after which their heads are wrapped in large cloths. Prized jewelry, including silver earrings and bracelets, is worn among the rich.

## Music & performance

They mention religious texts read by Muli Bonpo-Buddhist priests in Tibetan and interpreted into Prinmi.

## Festivals & rituals

The Pumi celebrate the Lunar New Year during the first fifteen days of the new year according to the lunar calendar. Losar is also celebrated by some Pumi.

Religious observances are connected with local mountain gods. Almost all Pumi villages have their own mountain gods, worshipped during festive seasons. On these occasions, the entire family gives food offerings and prays for the family’s good health and prosperity. The minor mountain god is worshipped on the 15th day of each month, while a grand ceremony is held on the 15th day of the 7th lunar month to venerate the Great Mountain God.

Household ritual centers on the cooking fire ring. During meals, the oldest male takes a spoonful of food and places it on each small stone tower of the *Guazei*. One spoonful is thrown into the fire as a symbolic offering to the spirits of ancestors. The Zanbala religion includes veneration of three gods and ancestral spirits; only old men preside over its rituals and prayers.

A Pumi boy undergoes ablution rites of manhood upon reaching thirteen. Marriage is associated with the traditional drink *pri*: in Prinmi, the expression for “to marry” is expressed as “to drink pri.”

## Foodways

Rice has become the staple food for most Pumi. In villages where the climate is too cold for rice cultivation, many people trade potatoes for rice at the market. Chinese cabbage, beans, eggplant and melons are among the vegetables and fruits used as supplements.

A traditional beer-like drink called *pri* is brewed by every Pumi family in villages. It is essential at wedding ceremonies, and the Prinmi expression for marriage is connected with drinking it.

Pumi pork preservation involves removing the inner organs and feet of a large pig. Lean meat is made into sausages, while the slaughtered pig is salted inside and sewn up. The salty body is wrapped in pork skin and dried. Its shape, large at the bottom and small at the head, resembles a pipa; the entire preserved body can be kept for years and is known by the Chinese nickname of Pipa meat.

During household meals, food is also used in ancestor veneration. The oldest male places a spoonful on each tower behind the cooking fire and throws another spoonful into the fire as a symbolic feeding of ancestral spirits.

## Language & religion

Pumi, also known as Prinmi, is a Qiangic language of the Tibeto-Burman family. It is used by the Pumi people in Yunnan and by the Tibetan people of Muli in Sichuan. Most native speakers live in Lanping, Ninglang, Lijiang, Weixi and Muli. The sources identify Western, Central and Northern Prinmi, with Western Prinmi spoken in Lanping, Central Prinmi in southwestern Ninglang, Lijiang, Yulong and Yongsheng, and Northern Prinmi in northern Ninglang and Sichuan. Earlier descriptions divided Pumi into southern and northern branches that were not mutually intelligible.

In the past, Pumi in Muli and Ninglang used Tibetan script mainly for religious purposes, and Muli Bonpo-Buddhist priests read religious texts in Tibetan that required interpretation into Prinmi. A pinyin-based Roman script has been proposed but is not commonly used or widely promoted.

Pumi religion has been influenced by Tibetan Buddhism and the older practice of Bön. This amalgamation is known as *Hangui* and shares features with Dongba practices of the Nakhi and Daba practices of the Mosuo. Ancestor worship is also present. Some Pumi have adopted the Gelugpa and Kagyu lineages of Tibetan Buddhism. The Zanbala religion, also called *Dingba*, means “white earth” and includes three gods and ancestral spirits. Local mountain gods, household fire rituals and offerings to ancestral spirits remain important in the practices described by the sources.
