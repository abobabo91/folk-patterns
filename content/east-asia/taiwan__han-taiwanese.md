## Overview

Han Taiwanese, also called Taiwanese Han, Taiwanese Han Chinese, or Han Chinese Taiwanese, are Taiwanese people of full or partial ethnic Han Chinese ancestry. According to the Executive Yuan of Taiwan, they comprise 95 to 97 percent of the Taiwanese population, although estimates elsewhere in the source range from 95 to 98 percent. They live in Taiwan, Kinmen, Matsu, Wuqiu Township, and other associated islands. Major immigration waves occurred from the 17th century through the end of the Chinese Civil War in 1949, apart from the Japanese colonial period. Their principal Sinitic languages are Mandarin, Hokkien, and Hakka. Han Taiwanese culture includes regional linguistic traditions, Confucian temples, traditional houses, Hakka indigo dyeing, written and oral literature, religious temples, and folk toys.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Hakka indigo dyeing** — Hakka Taiwanese have long traditions of indigo dyeing.

### Architecture

Taiwanese architecture refers to buildings constructed by Han people and is described as a branch of Chinese architecture. The style generally concerns buildings constructed before modernization under Japanese occupation in the 1930s. Different Han immigrant groups developed different architectural styles. Because Taiwan was distant from the political center of Beijing, buildings were constructed without centralized construction standards. The source also attributes divergence from mainland styles to the expertise of artisans and craftsmen and to Japanese colonization.

Many traditional houses have been designated national monuments by the Taiwanese government. Named examples include the **Lin Family Mansion and Garden** and the **House of Tēⁿ Iōng-sek**. Confucian temples also formed an important part of the life of early Han immigrants. The source names the **Taiwan Confucian Temple** and **Taipei Confucius Temple**.

## Festivals & rituals

Observance of traditional Han festivals is described as one criterion sometimes used to determine whether a Taiwanese person is Han.

The most popular religions of Han Taiwanese are Taoism and Buddhism. Taiwan has 11,796 temples, of which 78.4 percent are Taoist and 19.6 percent are Buddhist. Confucian temples formed an important part of the life of early Han immigrants, and the source names the Taiwan Confucian Temple and Taipei Confucius Temple.

## Foodways

The source does record a historical linguistic example connected with land measurement: *Kah* is a unit of land area used in Taiwanese Minnan and equals 0.9699 acre. It derives from the Dutch word for “field,” *akker*, through the forms *阿甲* and *甲*. This is a linguistic example rather than a foodway.

## Oral tradition & literature

One of the best-known Han folktales in Taiwan is **Aunt Tiger**.

One of the earliest written records of Taiwanese Hakka is **A Tragic Ballad about Hakka Sailing to Taiwan** (*渡台悲歌*). It was written in the Raoping dialect and concerns the life and struggle of Hakka immigrants to Taiwan under Ching rule.

The source also mentions **On Reconciliation** (*勸和論*), written by Tēⁿ Iōng-sek to mediate violent ethnic conflicts during the Qing dynasty. Tēⁿ Iōng-sek is identified as the first Taiwanese to achieve the highest degree, *jinshi* or “Doctor,” in the imperial examination of the Qing dynasty. His dates are 10 June 1788 to 21 March 1858.

## Language & religion

Han Taiwanese mainly speak Mandarin, Hokkien, and Hakka. Other languages named in the source include Mindong, used in Matsu; Puxian, used in Wuqiu Island, Kinmen; and other Han languages spoken by some post-World War II immigrants or immigrants from mainland China since the 1990s. Mandarin is used throughout the country, Hokkien in Taiwan proper and Kinmen, and Hakka in Taiwan proper.

Writing systems include Han characters and Han phonetic notations. The source names Mandarin Phonetic Symbols for Mandarin, Taiwanese Phonetic Symbols for Hokkien and Hakka, and the Latin alphabet in romanization systems including Tongyong Pinyin, Wade–Giles, Gwoyeu Romatzyh, Mandarin Phonetic Symbols II, POJ, Taiwanese Minnan Romanization System, and Hakka Romanization System.

Taiwanese linguist Uijin Ang divided Taiwan, excluding Kinmen and Matsu, into seven linguistic regions: one Austronesian, five Han, and one mixed. The most popular religions are Taoism and Buddhism, alongside the importance of Confucian temples.
