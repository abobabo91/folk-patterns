## Overview

The Taivoan, also called Taivuan or Tevorangh, are a Taiwanese indigenous ethnic group whose historical homeland included hill and basin areas in Tainan, especially the Yujing Basin, which they called Tamani. Their communities later extended through hilly areas of Tainan and Kaohsiung, as well as Hualien and Taitung. Some scholars estimate that more than 20,000 Taivoan people live today, based on records from Japanese rule of Taiwan. Taivoan is an Austronesian language that was formerly regarded as a dialect of Siraya; since the January 2019 code release, SIL International has recognized it as an independent language and assigned it the code tvx. Taivoan culture is documented through ceremonial music, ancestral-spirit worship, embroidery, bamboo baskets, oral traditions, and surviving community festivals.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Embroidery** — Embroidery is described as one of the most notable Taivoan handicrafts. Its variety of decorative patterns and colours distinguishes it culturally from nearby indigenous peoples, including Cou, Bunun, Rukai, and Siraya.

**Bamboo basket** — The bamboo fishing basket, called *agicin* or *kikiz*, is used for fishing and religious purposes. Taivoan people sanctify the fishing basket and give it a role in religious activity.

Every Taivoan Shrine, called *Kuba*, *Kuva*, or *Kuwa*, contains a *kogitanta agisen*, the “Seat of Hiang Deities.” It combines an *agicin* with the shrine’s central axial column, called *Kayu*. The *kogitanta agisen* is regarded as the place where the Highest Ancestral Spirits rest and as the most sacred space inside the Shrine. Unlike an ordinary bamboo basket, it must be made from thorny bamboo, *Bambusa stenostachya Hackel*.

**Motif vocabulary.** Square or diamond shapes; thistle flowers, called *ayxaw*; extended diamond shapes; straight lines and mountain shapes; abstract geometric shapes; geometric flowers and leaves; geometric human faces and figures; insects, birds, and snakes; human-in-boat shapes; and geometric shapes such as swastikas.

The thistle-flower pattern is described as especially distinctive in Taivoan embroidery. Some local Taivoan people associate it with *Cirsium lineare*, while others identify it with *Gomphrena globosa*.

### Clothing & dress

They do describe ceremonial and festival clothing indirectly: during the former Women’s Night in Xiaolin, Taivoan women dressed beautifully, played games, and sang and danced in the streets.

### Architecture

They do describe the Taivoan Shrine, called *Kuba*, *Kuva*, or *Kuwa*, as a central religious structure. A thorny bamboo called *Malubiw* is erected in front of the Shrine during the Night Ceremony as a ladder for the Highest Ancestral Spirits to descend to the earth. The Shrine also contains the *kogitanta agisen*, a sacred combination of a bamboo basket and the shrine’s central axial column, *Kayu*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They identify the bamboo fishing basket, *agicin* or *kikiz*, as an important everyday and religious object. The fishing-trap instrument called *Hiang-Kô* is made from a fishing trap and is used in worship of the Highest Ancestral Spirits; it is also called *agicin* or *kikiz*.

## Music & performance

Taivoan people are described as having abundant folk music among the Taiwanese Plain indigenous peoples. Their repertoire ranges from hymns for the Highest Ancestral Spirits, which may be sung only in front of the Shrine or during *Khui-Hiang*, to antiphonal work songs that mock Chinese immigrants. Many songs have been recorded and some have been taught in local elementary schools in Taivoan communities.

*Taboro*, also called “the Song in the Shrine,” is a ceremonial song that may be sung only in the Shrine during the Night Ceremony. Singing it on another occasion is strictly prohibited. *Kalawahe*, “the Song out of the Shrine” or “Out-of-the-Shrine Song,” is normally sung while indigenous people leave the Shrine after worshipping the Highest Ancestral Spirits during the Night Ceremony. Its lyrics include *tamaku*, “cigarette,” *saviki*, “betel nut,” and *rarom*, “water,” which are described as offerings to the Highest Ancestral Spirits.

*Lawkhema* is a cheerful Taivoan work song sung by men and women while working in the mountains. Most of its lyrics are in Taivoan. The title is a Hakka Chinese term meaning “Old Hen” and implying a stingy person; the singers use it to mock Hakka people.

The sources also describe *Banana colloquial speech*, a mixed language developed by Taivoan people from Xiaolin, Alikuan, Pualiao, and Liuchongxi. It combines vowels and consonants from Taiwanese or Taivoan and produces a different form of speech. The sources say it developed during resistance to the Japanese colonial government in the early 20th century so Japanese authorities could not understand it.

## Dance & theatre

They do describe *Unaunaw*, also called *Khan-Hì* in Taiwanese, a religious practice during the Night Ceremony in which Taivoans hold hands, sing religious songs, and dance in a large circle before the Shrine. The sources call it the commonly regarded highlight of the Night Ceremony.

## Festivals & rituals

The Night Ceremony is held on the full moon of the ninth lunar month every year. It is both the day on which the taboo is lifted, *Khui-Hiang*, and the most important day for Taivoan people to worship the Highest Ancestral Spirits. The six months following the Night Ceremony are called *Khui-Hiang*, when hunting, weddings, and several religious songs are permitted. On the full moon of the third lunar month in the following year, *Kìm-Hiang* begins; hunting and wedding practices are prohibited, and several religious songs should not be sung until the next *Khui-Hiang*.

The Highest Ancestral Spirits were formerly called *Anag*. They are now commonly called *Thài-Tsóo*, “the Grandmothers,” or *Huan-Thài-Tsóo*, “the Indigenous Grandmothers,” in Taiwanese. Some Taivoan elders call them *Kuba-Tsóo*, “the Grandmothers in Kuba.”

Night Ceremony activities include *Patahim*, a running race formerly practiced by young men as a display of manhood and as men’s training; it is now open to indigenous people of all genders and ages. *Too’ pulaw*, the exchange of bottle gourds after *Patahim*, and *Samaok*, in which boys and girls chased each other, are no longer practiced. During *Malubiw*-erection, thorny bamboo is placed before the Shrine.

The Women’s Night formerly began at 8:00 pm or 9:00 pm on the full moon of the first lunar month in Xiaolin. It was prohibited by Japanese police officers and teachers from 1940 and began to be revived in Sunlight Xiaolin in 2014. The Taivoan Cultural Festival has been held annually in spring in Sunlight Xiaolin since 2015.

## Foodways

They do mention religious offerings associated with the Night Ceremony and *Kalawahe*: *tamaku*, “cigarette,” *saviki*, “betel nut,” and *rarom*, “water.” The Highest Ancestral Spirits are also offered *Hiang-tsiú*, “Hiang-wine,” and *Hiang-tsuí*, “Hiang-water,” which are wine and water blessed by the Highest Ancestral Spirits. In Taivoan, these are called *mimaw rarom* and *mimaw palinlin*.

## Oral tradition & literature

Not much folklore has been retained by modern Taivoan communities. Two examples remain well known among younger generations. In “Soldiers of Hiang-Water,” people in Xiaolin believe that *mimaw-pilinlin*, or *Hiang-water*, spilled by Taivoans fleeing the Japanese army transformed into hundreds of soldiers who helped them defeat the Japanese.

In “Ancestral Spirit That Escaped,” Taivoan people in Pualiao believe that the local Highest Ancestral Spirits took the form of seven pearls that returned to the village only during the Night Ceremony. When a Taoist deity from Pingtung attempted to subdue them, the youngest spirit became a pearl and escaped. The sources say that local Taivoans believe this spirit is still hiding on a certain tree.

The earliest written works in Taivoan include the Gospel of St. Matthew, translated into Taivoan and Siraya in 1661. The Sinckan Manuscripts include leases, mortgages, and other commerce contracts written in Siraya, Taivoan, and Makatao using the Roman script taught by Dutch missionaries.

## Language & religion

Taivoan or Taivuan is a Formosan language of the Austronesian language family. It was spoken until the end of the 19th century and was previously classified as a dialect of Siraya. Documentary and linguistic evidence led many linguists to regard it as a separate language. The Dutch records *De Dagregisters van het Kasteel Zeelandia* document translation from Sinckan to Tarrocquan, from Tarrocquan to Taivoan, and from Taivoan to Cannacannavo. The Taivoan language was written in Roman script in materials associated with Dutch missionaries, including the Gospel of St. Matthew and the Sinckan Manuscripts.

Modern-day Taivoan people practice traditional animism together with Taoism influenced by Chinese immigrants. Only very few Taivoans practice Buddhism and Christianity. The central religious concept is *Hiang* or *Xiang*, associated with sorcery, taboo, and magic. Religious objects and substances connected with this concept include *Hiang-tsiú*, *Hiang-tsuí*, *Hiang-tik*, and *Hiang-Kô*.
