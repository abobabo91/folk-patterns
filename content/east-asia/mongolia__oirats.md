## Overview

Oirats, also called Oirds and formerly Eluts or Eleuths, are the westernmost group of Mongols. Their ancestral home lies in the Altai region of Siberia, Xinjiang, and western Mongolia. Historically, the Oirats consisted of four major tribes: Dzungar, also called Choros or Olots; Torghut; Dörbet; and Khoshut. Other groups associated with Oirat history include the Ölöt, Khoid, Baatud, Zakhchin, Bargut, Buzava, and Tumed. Oirat communities have also lived in Kalmykia on the Caspian Sea, in Amdo, along the Ejin River, in Alasha, and in Xinjiang. The Russian 2010 Census recorded 176,800 Kalmyks, of whom 80,546 could speak the Kalmyk language. Oirats share history, geography, culture, and language with the Eastern Mongols.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources mention the Potala in Jehol, also called Chengde, and a smaller copy erected to mark the return of Kalmyks from the Volga region. A model copy of this “Little Potala” was later made in China for the Swedish explorer Sven Hedin and displayed at the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago in 1893.

## Music & performance

They do mention a *yada*, or “thunder stone,” used by the Oirat chief Qutuqa Beki in an attempt to unleash a storm against Genghis Khan’s army, but the source presents this as part of a historical episode rather than as a musical instrument or performance tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe several religious and political events. The Oirats converted to Tibetan Buddhism around 1615. In 1637, Güshi Khan, leader of the Khoshuts in Koko Nor, defeated Choghtu Khong Tayiji, who supported the Karma Kagyu school, at the request of the Gelug school. The unification of Tibet followed in the early 1640s, when Güshi Khan was proclaimed Khan of Tibet by the 5th Dalai Lama and the Khoshut Khanate was established. In 1717, the Dzungars invaded Tibet and killed Lha-bzang Khan, a grandson of Güshi Khan and the fourth Khan of Tibet. The sources also record a large migration of Kalmyks from the Volga region to Dzungaria in January 1771, and the deportation of the entire population of 120,000 Kalmyks to Siberia in 1943. Life-cycle rites, funeral customs, initiation ceremonies, and marriage rituals are not described.

## Foodways

They state that the Kalmyks’ migration from the Volga region to Dzungaria involved thirst, cold, and starvation, and that the Oirats of the Dzungar Khanate developed a mixed agro-pastoral economy. No named food tradition is provided.

## Oral tradition & literature

The earliest historical reference discussed in the source is the *Secret History of the Mongols*, a 13th-century chronicle of Genghis Khan’s rise to power. It counts the Oirats among the “forest people” and describes them as living under a shaman-chief known as *bäki*. The same account records the Oirat chief Qutuqa Beki’s use of a *yada*, or “thunder stone,” in an attempt to create a storm against Genghis Khan’s army. The source also refers to the Ongin “rune” inscriptions, dated to the sixth century, as the first documented reference to Elut and Yelut. No Oirat folktale cycle, proverb collection, riddle tradition, contemporary literary revival, or preservation project is described.

## Language & religion

Oirat is presented as a Mongolic language distinct from Mongolian in some lexical and grammatical features. In the 17th century, Zaya Pandita, a Gelug monk of the Khoshut tribe, devised *Clear Script* for Oirats. It was based on the older Mongolian script and used a more developed system of diacritics to prevent misreading. Clear Script remained in use in Kalmykia until the mid-1920s, when it was replaced by a Latin alphabet and later by the Cyrillic script. It was replaced in Mongolia by the Cyrillic alphabet in 1941, while some Oirats in China continued to use Clear Script and Mongolian script. The Oirats converted to Tibetan Buddhism around 1615. The sources also describe pressure on Buddhist Kalmyks to adopt Russian Orthodoxy and the suppression of Buddhism and collectivisation of herds after the Russian revolution.
