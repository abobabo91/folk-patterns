## Overview

The Torghut are one of the four major subgroups of the Four Oirats. Their nobles traced their descent to the Turco-Mongol Keraite ruler Toghrul, and many Torghuts descended from the Keraites. They are scattered in Xinjiang in China, Kalmykia in Russia, and Khovd in Mongolia. The Torghut clan first appeared as an Oirat group in the mid-16th century. After the collapse of the Four Oirat Alliance, most Torghuts under Kho Orluk moved west to the Volga region in 1630, forming the core of the Kalmyks. Other Torghuts became associated with Qinghai Lake, Ejin River, Dzungaria, and western Mongolia. The sources provide historical and geographical information.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources state that Qing authorities coerced Torghuts into giving up their nomadic lifestyle and adopting sedentary agriculture.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The historical account does mention cattle, sheep, horses, camels, and dogs among the animals accompanying the departing group in 1771.

## Music & performance

The Wikipedia article identifies Purevjal as a famous Mongolian singer and lists several Torghuts described as heroes of Mongolia or heroes of Labor of Mongolia.

## Dance & theatre

The Wikipedia article mentions a lively narrative by Thomas De Quincey about the Torghut movement from the Volga region, written in 1837, but does not characterize it as a Torghut performance tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

They record that 500 Torghuts went on pilgrimage to Tibet in 1698 and were unable to return, after which they were resettled in Ejin River by the Kangxi Emperor of China’s Qing dynasty. The sources also describe the mass departure of Torghuts from the Volga region on January 5, 1771, and the later settlement of approximately 85,000 survivors near the Ejin River with permission from the Qing Emperor.

## Foodways

The historical account names cattle, sheep, horses, camels, and dogs among the animals associated with the Torghut departure from the Volga region.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that there is a lively narrative by Thomas De Quincey, written in 1837, about the Torghut movement from the Volga region to the Qing Empire. The article also notes that the group’s departure has been variously estimated between 150,000 and 400,000 people and that approximately 85,000 survivors reached Dzungaria. An exhibition commemorating the exodus is found at the Potala Palace in Chengde.

## Language & religion

The article gives the Mongolian form Торгууд, the forms ᠲᠤᠷᠭᠣᠣᠠ and Torguud, and the Chinese forms 土尔扈特 and 土爾扈特. It states that the name Torghut or Torgut probably derives from the Mongolian word “torog,” meaning “silk.” The sources also mention pilgrimage to Tibet and describe the Torghuts as an Oirat group.
