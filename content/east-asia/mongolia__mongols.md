## Overview

The Mongols are an East Asian people native to Mongolia and parts of China and southern Siberia. Nearly eighty per cent of Mongolian territory is pasture, and livestock remains the country's most important economic activity. Their history as a single political body runs from the Mongol Empire of Genghis Khan through the Northern Yuan to the Qing submission of 1691. They matter as mobile pastoralists whose material culture reflects a life of movement and trade.

## Material culture

Metal and silver dominate Mongol material culture, paired with wood, felt, and coral in objects designed for portability and pastoral life.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Mongol textiles feature symbolic woven and applied patterns including dragons, fish, Buddhist emblems and ornaments.

- **Khalkha woman's silk costume** — A complete set of blue silk dress worn with braided cloth headpiece, silver crown, pearl-band ornaments, earrings and chest ornament.
- **Torghut silk damask robe** — A mid-green robe patterned with flowers and overlaid with applied bands in black and gold-metal thread, with embroidered breast pockets.
- **Piled-wool prayer rug** — A square prayer carpet woven of piled red wool and backed with off-white cotton.

### Clothing & dress

Men's and women's dress diverge sharply in ornament, from layered silk and silver for wealthy women to practical working dress for herders.

- **Man's conical hat** — Built up from coiled and plaited bast, with long red-dyed yak hair glued to the sides, a domed silver base at the top, and a red cotton lining.
- **Belt furniture** — A knife in its sheath, a fire-steel and a pair of bone chopsticks carried together on a hanger.
- **Mongolian warrior's armour** — Thirteen parts including helmet, jacket, chest guard and back guard, still worn as parade costume by bodyguards of certain chieftains.

### Architecture

The ger, a demountable house framed in carved and polychrome-painted wood, serves the logic of nomadic pastoral life.

- **Ger** — The demountable house of Mongolia's nomads, built from raw materials to hand and struck and reassembled as herds move to new grazing.
- **Plaited wicker enclosure** — A pen for animals forming a working unit of the camp together with the tent.
- **Tung Scheng Chao** — A lama temple visited on 30 April 1930 where religious objects were acquired.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Metal vessels dominate, with copper, brass and bronze pieces designed for cooking, serving and ceremonial use.

- **Copper teapot** — A Chinese-type vessel with movable handles and a sunken lid, blackened by use.
- **Brass vessel with hanging handles** — Used to ladle food from the cauldron at feasts and carry it into the tent.
- **Back-carrier** (*urch*) — Two double wooden bows with crossbars, joined by leather straps and slung from braces of sewn cattle-hair rope.
- **Folding altar table** — Made in pairs by Sagetjin Baell Mongols, assembled without nails or screws and painted red with gilded ornament on the visible faces.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver set with coral is the signature of Mongol jewelry, worn as rings, bracelets, hair ornaments and side pendants.

- **Silver rings with coral** — Each set with a single coral stone.
- **Silver bracelets** — Slightly triangular in section, decorated with a Chinese good-fortune character.
- **Silver hair ornaments** — Coral-studded, brass-backed with a hook and joined by thread, 49 cm overall.
- **Silver-and-coral side pendants** — Hung at a woman's sides to carry small objects, with movable coral beads and a movable openwork filigree ball of silver at the centre.
- **Amulet case** — Round or with glass front framed by a bat and dragon ornament, containing powder wrapped in pale cloth.

## Music & performance

The hore, a two-stringed bowed chordophone, is the documented instrument, placed in the context of monastic rather than secular performance.

- **Hore** — A kind of violin with a wooden resonator whose sides are dovetailed together, closed with riveted sheet brass, upper face covered with leather and pierced with a coin-shaped sound hole.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used here say nothing about Mongol dance or theatrical traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

New Year festival and feasting mark the ritual calendar, with monastic altar tables, amulet cases and skull bowls serving religious practice.

- **New Year festival** — A festival addressed to the god of heaven at which priests wore a chain of 108 wooden beads strung with four large pale-red glass beads.
- **Official's chain** — A chain of 108 wooden beads with four large pale-red glass beads, wound thread and brass fittings, serving both Qing office and religious liturgy.
- **Feast** — A ceremonial occasion for which brass vessels with hanging handles were used to carry cooked food from the cauldron into the tent.

## Foodways

Foodways reflect pastoral herding, built on meat and milk from sheep, cattle, camels, goats and horses.

- **Birch-bark box** — A milk container with a wooden base and handled wooden lid.
- **Cauldron** — The cooking vessel from which food was lifted out for feasts.
- **Knife** — An eating tool with an iron blade and wooden handle riveted in brass or iron, faced with white metal, carried in a wooden sheath with silver or white-metal mounts.
- **Copper pot** — A tea vessel with movable handles, blackened by long use.
- **Snuff bottle** (*Kuchur*) — A round footed silver bottle whose lid was originally set with five corals and fitted with a small spoon.

## Oral tradition & literature

Written historical sources in Mongolian and Persian document Mongol affairs from the thirteenth century onward, while oral traditions remain undocumented here.

- **Secret History of the Mongols** — A thirteenth-century contemporary historical source written in Mongol.
- **Erdeni-yin tobči** — A later Mongol chronicle preserving a Chinggisid prince's remark about the khans of Toγmaγ as kindred.
- **Erdeni tunumal neretü sudur** — A Mongol chronicle recording an embassy in which Altan Khan's envoys reminded the rulers of Moghulistan of their shared descent from Genghis Khan.
- **Jami' al-tawarikh** — A Persian source documenting Mongol affairs from outside.
- **Zafarnama** — A work by Yazdi where the Mongols of fifteenth-century Mongolia are called Qalmaqs.

## Language & religion

Mongolian language belongs to the Mongolic family traced through Donghu and Xianbei, while religion blends shamanism and Tibetan Buddhism.

- **Mongolian language** — The central branch of the Mongolic family, with Mongol dialects collectively forming the group.
- **Mongol script** — A writing system developed by the Mongols, with earlier regional scripts like Khitan predating it.
- **Gelugpa** (*Yellow Hat*) — A Tibetan Buddhist sect backing the Manchus in seventeenth-century Mongol rule.
- **Kagyu** (*Red Hat*) — A Tibetan Buddhist sect supported by the last khagan Ligdan in seventeenth-century Mongol rule.
