## Overview

The Zhuang are a Tai-speaking ethnic group concentrated in the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region in Southern China, with communities also in Yunnan, Guangdong, Guizhou, and Hunan. Their population is estimated at 19 million, making them the largest minority in China; Guangxi alone has around 14.5 million Zhuang. Zhuang languages belong to the Tai family and are spoken mainly in Guangxi and adjacent parts of Yunnan and Guangdong. Zhuang culture includes song traditions, Sawndip writing, folk stories, ceremonial music, dances, handicrafts, animist practices, and a long history of interaction with Han Chinese communities. The sources describe the Zhuang as one of the 56 ethnic groups officially recognized by the People’s Republic of China.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Traditional brocades and weavings* are listed among Zhuang handicrafts.

*Paeng'vae* is described as a type of linen made from grass leaves and hemp fibers. It is produced by weaving the two materials 90 degrees with each other.

*Totem back carriers* are listed as Zhuang handicrafts and consist of fish, bird, and abstract symbolic designs.

*Embroidered balls* are named among Zhuang folk handicrafts.

*Straw mats* are also listed, without further catalogue or technical information.

**Motif vocabulary.** Fish, bird, abstract symbolic designs.

### Clothing & dress

 They state that, after the Song period, Chinese-style dress influenced Zhuang clothing: people began fastening clothing on the right, women wore bodices, trousers were replaced by skirts, and hair was worn in the Chinese style. In an account of offerings to the sun in Shangguo Village, Zhuang women are described as bathing in the river, putting on traditional clothing and headdresses, and making offerings with yellow glutinous rice.

### Architecture

 “The house-raising song” describes the construction of a traditional stilt house and customs intended to ward off evil from the new home. The sources also mention wooden halls: in Bahao, a wooden hall on Bozhe Mountain is dedicated for Sky Sacrifices. No further information is provided about house plans, roofs, building materials, village organization, or architectural decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention bronze drums, including “The Origin of the Bronze Drum,” which tells of their origins and describes drums with a star in the middle. The sources also list wood prints, paper, traditional waterwheels, bamboo basket weaving, rattan, straw mats, and spinning tops among Zhuang handicrafts. Traditional waterwheels are named without a technical description.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver jewelry is listed among Zhuang handicrafts. The sources mention headdresses in connection with women’s offerings to the sun and describe hair being worn in the Chinese style after Chinese dress influenced Zhuang communities. No specific jewelry names, tattoo practices, cosmetic traditions, or other forms of body adornment are documented in the sources.

## Music & performance

Zhuang musical culture includes ceremonial music, classical orchestras, wind instruments, string instruments, singing, and dance-song performance. *Masa classical music* is associated with the classical orchestras of Masa Village in Maguan County, Yunnan. *Bawu* is described as a type of Zhuang folk music dating back to ancient times, while *Mubala* is an ancient Zhuang wind instrument. *Huqin* is a stringed instrument made from a water buffalo horn or horse bone; it may have two strings or four strings.

The Zhuang use song in social, literary, historical, and ritual contexts. During “Sam Nyied Sam,” the 3rd day of the 3rd month of the Chinese lunar calendar, young men and women traditionally sing antiphonal songs to each other. *Svaeu'na* involves toasting through dancing and singing. The sources also name “The cutting down the banana tree song” (*Kau Tam Gvoa*), “The song of the origin of bronze” (*Gvau Dong*), “The rice paddy-planting song,” “Village visitation songs,” “Wine banquet songs,” “The national partition song,” “Tea origin songs,” and other songs.

Zhuang ceremonial music and songs are also connected with religious activity. The sources mention performances at the Longdvaen festival and a shrine dedicated to the song immortal in Ake Village, Guangnan County.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name several Zhuang dances: bronze drum dance, straw man dance, water buffalo dance (*longh yah vaiz*), paper horse dance, hand towel dance, and sun dance (*tang'vaenz*).

Dance and song occur together during *Svaeu'na* toasting. The sources also describe Longdvaen festival as involving theatric performances in Guichao Township, Funing County. Zhuang opera scripts preserve “The orphan girl and the rich girl,” an early version of the story Cinderella. The original Chinese opera “The Legend of Wenlong” has been lost, but the story survives in Zhuang texts. No further information is provided about staging, masks, puppets, costumes, or professional performers.

## Festivals & rituals

“Sam Nyied Sam,” the 3rd day of the 3rd month of the Chinese lunar calendar, is one of the main Zhuang festivals. It is celebrated through singing, dancing, games, and special food; young men and women traditionally sing antiphonal songs. Other named observances include the festival of the ancestor sacrifice of the 7th lunar month, the Duanwu medicine festival, the Buffalo King Festival, the bullfighting festival, the Firstfruits Festival, and the Zhuang children’s festival. Dates for the children’s festival are chosen by each individual village.

The Buffalo King Festival is celebrated in Niutouzhai Village, Wenshan County, where there is a Buffalo King Temple. The Zhuang have long worshipped the water buffalo. Religious practices named in the sources include sacrificing to the field deity, mountain sacrifices, worship of Zoa'nongz, sacrifices to Zoachu, sacrifices to the mother tree or *mih maix*, sacrifices to bronze drums, and sacrifices to Nong Zhigao in the 6th and 7th lunar months. Purple-dyed glutinous rice, and sometimes cattle, are offered to Nong Zhigao.

Other ceremonies include opening the seedlings’ door, in which a healthy, beautiful girl plants the first seedling; repelling insects by floating insects away on paper boats while drums and gongs are beaten; sweeping the village to chase away evil; and banners used during sacrifice ceremonies.

## Foodways

The sources used provide limited information about Zhuang foodways. “Sam Nyied Sam” includes special food. The sources mention glutinous rice in ritual contexts: purple-dyed glutinous rice is offered during sacrifices to Nong Zhigao, while yellow glutinous rice is carried up Sun Mountain during offerings to the sun in Shangguo Village, Xichou County.

The Zhuang children’s festival, the Firstfruits Festival, wine banquet songs, and the Duanwu medicine festival indicate food and drink contexts. *Laeujyaq* is identified as medicinal liquor in the discussion of Zhuang folk medicine. No further named dishes or cooking traditions are documented.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Zhuang peoples of Yunnan and Guangxi have written and unwritten story traditions. Folk stories often take the form of songs and include folk tales, myths, legends, historical poems, and chants. For over one thousand years, the Zhuang have used Sawndip to write folk songs, operas, poems, scriptures, letters, contracts, and court documents.

“The orphan girl and the rich girl,” also known as “Dahgyax Dahbengz,” is an early version of the story Cinderella and is found in Zhuang opera scripts. A 9th century Chinese translation of a Zhuang story entitled Ye Xian appears in the Miscellaneous Morsels from Youyang, and the Sawndip versions are described as similar. “The house-raising song” concerns the construction of a traditional stilt house and customs intended to protect the new home. “The Origin of the Bronze Drum” tells of bronze drums and their power to ward off evil spirits.

The Zhuang have scriptures in poetical form, including the *Baeu Rodo* about the creation of the world. Other named works include “Banlong and Banli,” “Yiluo and Diling,” “Buloakdvo,” and the Poems of the Poya Songbook. *Swqmo* records Zhuang history, ethnic relations, astronomy, farming technology, literary arts, religious beliefs, customs, and traditions.

## Language & religion

The Zhuang languages are mutually unintelligible languages of the Tai family and are heavily influenced by nearby Chinese dialects. Northern and Southern Zhuang form different dialect continua, and Standard Zhuang is based on the Northern Zhuang dialect of Wuming. The sources describe more than 60 distinct tonal systems, with 5–11 tones depending on the variety. A 1980s survey reported that 42% of Zhuang people were monolingual in Zhuang and 55% bilingual in Zhuang and Chinese.

Zhuang was traditionally written using Sawndip, logograms based on Chinese characters, for over one thousand years. A script based on Latin letters, with Cyrillic- and IPA-derived letters, was introduced in 1957 and updated in 1982 to use only Latin letters.

Most Zhuang follow traditional animist practices known as Mo or Shigong, including ancestor worship. Mo has its own sutra and professional priests known as *Bu Mo*, who traditionally use chicken bones for divination. Buddhists, Taoists, and Christians are also present among the Zhuang.
