## Overview

The Dai are several Tai-speaking peoples of southern Yunnan, concentrated in the Xishuangbanna Dai Autonomous Prefecture and the Dehong Dai and Jingpo Autonomous Prefecture, and counted by the People's Republic of China as one of its 56 officially recognized ethnic groups. "Dai" is a Han Chinese umbrella term with no equivalent in the Tai languages themselves, which have only broader words for Tai people in general; the groups it covers — speakers of *Tai Lü* (Sipsongpanna Dai) and *Tai Nüa* (Dehong Dai), of *Tai Dam* and of *Tai Ya* — are distinct culturally and linguistically. Figures compiled for Yunnan give roughly 400,000 speakers each for Tai Lü and Tai Nüa, about 40,000 for Tai Rui and about 20,000 for the Tianxin variety. In folk-culture terms the Dai matter as one of the few peoples native to China who practise Theravada Buddhism, with their own script, calendar and monastic literacy, and as cotton-and-silk weavers whose patterned cloth reached European museums in the twentieth century.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources document the Dai mainly through woven and embroidered cloth. Wikipedia names weaving first among Dai handicrafts, alongside oil-pressing, winemaking and bamboo work; the museum records give the fabrics themselves, without vernacular names for the techniques.

**Supplementary-weft cloth** — A fabric collected in 1949 at the Jinghong market in Xishuangbanna by Acher Eskenasy and L. van Bussel, and catalogued by the National Museum of World Cultures Foundation simply as untreated cloth woven with supplementary wefts: the pattern is carried by extra weft floats rather than printed or applied.

**Indigo patterned lengths** — Lengths of indigo cotton cloth with multi-coloured patterning and fringes worked at both ends, held by the British Museum.

**Patterned bedding cloth** — Cotton bedsheets and bed blankets in patterned weave, some fringed at one end only, together with a long narrow patterned mattress-cover; the same weaving repertoire serves the household as well as dress.

**Embroidered cloth** — A worked textile of cotton ground and cotton thread, and a two-part belt combining cotton and silk, both embroidered.

**Plaited leaf containers** — A lidded box of leaf strips in plain weave, dyed black and red or left natural yellow, whose plaited surface deliberately imitates patterned cloth.

**Motif vocabulary.** Eight-sided star motifs; transverse stripes used as a ground for them; on the plaited box, a surface that mimics cotton textile with supplementary woven gold threads; fringed end-finishes on cloth lengths and blankets.

### Clothing & dress

Dai garments in the collections are women's tube skirts and a jacket. The skirts occur in three material combinations — plain cotton, cotton with silk, and silk alone — which suggests a graded wardrobe from workaday to best, though the catalogue entries do not say so outright; the jacket is cotton. Dress is completed by an embroidered belt made in two parts, of cotton and silk together, the most decorated single garment element recorded. The sources do not describe Dai head coverings, footwear, men's dress or any ceremonial costume distinct from daily wear, and give no vernacular garment names. One point of comparison is recorded: the small Muslim minority known as *Dai Paxi* or *Dai Hui*, descended from Hui merchants from Dali and elsewhere in Yunnan who settled in Xishuangbanna in the early nineteenth century and intermarried locally, wear dress similar to that of their Buddhist neighbours and share their language, customs, rituals and diet — their material culture is described as identical.

### Architecture

Dai villages stand on bamboo plains near rivers or streams. Houses are usually raised on stilts, and some are square in plan. A few are two-storeyed, the upper floor given over to living space and the lower to storage; that lower level is sometimes left without walls altogether, an open undercroft beneath the dwelling. The religious landscape of the village is equally built: until very recently every Dai village had at least one Buddhist temple, and larger villages two to five, these temples also serving as schools where boys sent as novices learned to read and write. Many were destroyed during the Cultural Revolution. Of the older monumental architecture, the sources record only that the twelfth-century Jinghong Golden Hall Kingdom had its capital at Jinghong, and that under the Ming the manorial lords of the region maintained their own armies, prisons and courts. No decorative scheme, roof type or construction detail beyond stilts and bamboo is described.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources name no Dai ceramic or metalworking tradition. What they do document is plant-fibre craft: bamboo work listed among the developed Dai handicrafts, a lidded box plaited from dyed leaf strips, and a model flag of woven and dyed vegetable fibre catalogued as religious or ritual equipment. Bamboo is also a cooking vessel — the fragrant bamboo tube in which glutinous rice is baked. Oil-pressing and winemaking round out the handicraft economy, which since the 1980s has sat alongside tourism revenue in Xishuangbanna following the opening of airports at Jinghong and Mangshi.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used do not cover Dai jewelry, tattooing or hair practices.

## Music & performance

The sources used — the Wikipedia articles and the museum catalogue records of the objects shown here — contain no account of Dai instruments, song genres or performance occasions.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used do not describe any Dai dance or dramatic tradition; the only performance-like practice they record is the mutual pouring of water by young men and women at the New Year, described under Festivals below.

## Festivals & rituals

Dai festivals are mostly religious, and three are named. The **door closing festival** falls on the fifteenth day of the ninth month of the Dai calendar, the middle of July in the Gregorian calendar; the **door opening festival** on the fifteenth day of the twelfth Dai month, the middle of October. On both days everyone goes to the Buddhist temple for ritual observance, offering food, flowers and coins to the Buddha. The three months between them are the "closed" season, the most intensely religious part of the year.

The **Water Splashing Festival** is the Dai New Year — glossed as the New Year of June, since it falls in the late sixth or early seventh month of the Dai calendar, the middle of April in the Gregorian calendar, about ten days after Qingming. It is called the most beautiful day of the year and usually lasts three days. At first light villagers go to the temple to wash the figure of the Buddha; after the temple ceremony young men and women pour water over one another, and then move through the settlement in groups sprinkling passers-by as a blessing. The equivalent festival among related Tai-speaking peoples is Songkran.

The central life-cycle rite recorded is monastic: parents commonly sent sons between seven and eighteen to a monastery as novices, where they stayed three years or more learning to read, write and practise the faith before most returned to secular life. Marriage was historically contracted between families of similar social and economic standing, and polygamy was common among chieftains.

## Foodways

Rice is the staple; in the Dehong area it is mainly japonica rice. The best-known Dai snack is **bamboo rice**, glutinous rice packed into a fragrant bamboo tube, soaked in water for about fifteen minutes and then baked over a fire. **Pineapple purple rice** is a second well-known dish. Dai cooking is characterized as raw, fresh, sour and spicy, and the preferences are explained in the sources as dietary reasoning: sour food is held to brighten the eyes, aid digestion and relieve heat, sweet to lift fatigue, and spicy to sharpen the appetite. Sourness is reckoned the finest flavour, and most dishes and snacks are built around it — **sour bamboo shoots** and **sour pork** are the examples given. The subtropical setting supplies the rest of the larder: rice, coffee, rubber, tea, sugar and many kinds of fruit are grown, and winemaking is one of the household crafts. The *Dai Paxi* Muslim minority is recorded as keeping a diet similar to that of Buddhist Dai.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Dai have a substantial written and oral literature: historical documents, legends, stories, poetry, fables and children's stories are described as plentiful. Alongside them stands an astronomical literature — many books on calculating solar and lunar eclipses, most of them written in Dai script — and the Dai calendar itself, which begins in the year 638. The transmission mechanism is the village monastery: because boys were routinely sent to learn reading and writing there, literacy in Dai script among Dai men is reported today to exceed 80 percent. The sources name no single epic or storytelling genre, and do not describe proverb or riddle traditions.

## Language & religion

Dai peoples in China speak Southwestern Tai languages of the Tai–Kadai family: Tai Lü, Tai Nüa (also called Dehong Dai, and identified with Shan), Tai Dam, and Tai Ya or Tai Hongjin; four major varieties are usually distinguished, Tai Lü, Tai Nüa, Tai Rui and Tianxin, the last represented by the dialect of Tianxin in Wuding County. Two further written languages used by the Dai are Tày Pong and Tai Dam. Related Tai–Kadai languages are spoken from Assam to Hainan and Guizhou. The Dai are predominantly Theravada Buddhists, having converted during the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries although Buddhism had been present in Yunnan since at least the seventh; earlier animism was not displaced, and belief in natural spirits continues within Dai Buddhism. A small minority are Muslim.
