## Overview

The Saisiyat (*SaySiyat*; Chinese 賽夏, pinyin *Sàixià*; Hakka Pha̍k-fa-sṳ *Sòi-hà*; the Pe̍h-ōe-jī form *Sai-siat*, 獅設, is glossed "true people") are an indigenous people of Taiwan living in the western part of the island, astride the boundary between Hsinchu County and Miaoli County. They are divided into a Northern Branch, in the mountainous Wufeng area of Hsinchu, and a Southern Branch, in the Nanzhuang and Shitan highlands of Miaoli; each branch has its own dialect. In 2000 they numbered 5,311, about 1.3% of Taiwan's total indigenous population, which makes them one of the smallest indigenous groups in the country. Their language, Saisiyat, is a Formosan language of the Austronesian family. In folk-culture terms they are known above all for the *Pasta'ay* festival, held every two years, and the small body of their weaving, basketry and bead and shell ornament held in museum collections. The name is also written Saisiat, and has been rendered Saiset, Seisirat, Saisett, Saisiett, Saisirat, Saisyet, Saisyett, Amutoura and Bouiok.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here name no vernacular terms for Saisiyat textile types or techniques; what they document is the material and colour of the garments themselves, as catalogued by the British Museum.

**Woven ramie-and-wool garments** — The Saisiyat clothing in the atlas is woven from ramie combined with wool, in two forms: an upper long garment or jacket, and a sleeveless jacket worked in white, red and black. The combination of a bast fibre (ramie) with wool in a single woven cloth is the distinguishing technical fact the catalogue records; the white-red-black triad is the only colour scheme the records attest.

**Coiled rattan basketry** — Rattan is coiled into closed, deep forms, including a jar-shaped basket, and into basket-worked rattan vessels. Rattan also serves for headgear (see below), so the same material spans container and dress.

### Clothing & dress

The documented wardrobe consists of woven ramie-and-wool upper garments in two cuts — a long jacket and a sleeveless jacket — and a brimless rattan cap. The sleeveless jacket's white, red and black ground is the clearest surviving indication of Saisiyat dress colour. The sources do not distinguish men's from women's dress, do not describe belts, footwear or lower garments, and do not state which of these pieces were everyday wear and which were reserved for ceremony; the extensive bead, seed and shell ornament catalogued alongside them (below) indicates that body adornment, rather than tailoring, carried much of the visual weight of Saisiyat dress. A loom appears in Saisiyat cosmology as well as in their material record: in the flood account, the man who survives floats away seated on a weaving loom.

### Architecture

The sources used for this profile say nothing about Saisiyat house forms, building materials, roofing or settlement layout.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

No Saisiyat ceramics or smithing traditions appear in these sources. The everyday objects documented are instead of rattan: coiled baskets, one of them jar-shaped, and vessels described as basket-worked rattan, together with the brimless rattan cap. Metal appears only as a component of ornament — brass combined with black glass beads in a necklet — and not as an object class of its own, so the sources give no basis for describing a Saisiyat metalworking craft. Bone likewise appears only as one material among several in a bracelet.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ornament is the best-documented part of Saisiyat material culture in this collection, and it is built from three materials: glass beads, shell and plant seeds, with bone and brass as occasional additions.

- **Ear ornaments and ear pendants** — made of shell alone, or of shell combined with glass beads, and catalogued in pairs.
- **Necklaces and necklets** — one strung from plant seeds and string; one a necklet of black glass beads and brass; and a plain string of glass beads.
- **Bracelets** — worn in pairs, combining glass beads, shell beads and bone in a single piece.

The sources do not describe who wore these, on what occasions, or what the materials signified, and they document no tattooing, hairdressing or other body-marking practices.

## Music & performance

The sources name no Saisiyat instruments and no song genres. They record only that ritual practice at *Pasta'ay* has been the subject of documentary attention — BBC News reported on Saisiyat efforts to keep the *pas-ta'ai* rituals alive — and, in the flood narrative, that the god Otspoehobong chanted an incantation over the pounded flesh of the survivor before casting it into the sea. Nothing further about performance can be stated from them.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used do not describe any Saisiyat dance or dramatic tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The Saisiyat hold a festival called *Pasta'ay*, also written *pas-ta'ai*, every two years. It is the single named element of Saisiyat ceremonial life in these sources, and its biennial rhythm — rather than an annual calendar — is what they specify. The rituals attached to it have been the object of deliberate maintenance by the Saisiyat themselves, reported on by BBC News under the heading of keeping the *pas-ta'ai* rituals alive. The sources used here give no dates or season for the festival, no account of its sequence or of who officiates, and no description of Saisiyat life-cycle rites — birth, coming-of-age, marriage or funeral. A Museum of Saisiyat Folklore exists and is the institutional point of reference for the group's ceremonial and material culture.

## Foodways

The sources used for this profile contain no information on Saisiyat staples, cooking, dishes, ceremonial food or drink.

## Oral tradition & literature

The one narrative the sources transmit in full is the **Saisiyat flood account**. Human beings were created by a god and lived in an original land, until a flood scattered them. One man, seated on a weaving loom, floated to *Airubia Mountain*, where a god called *Otspoehobong* (also given as *Oopenhaboon*) seized him. Fearing that the flood had left humanity to die out, the god killed the survivor, pounded his flesh, chanted an incantation, and threw the flesh into the sea, where it turned into human beings — the ancestors the god named *Siasiat*. He then cut out the intestines and threw them into the sea, and they became the ancestors of the Taiwanese; the bones, thrown in after, became the ancestors of the Atayal. The account thus derives the Saisiyat, their lowland neighbours and the Atayal from one body, with the Saisiyat taking the flesh. The sources name no epic cycle, proverb or riddle tradition beyond this.

## Language & religion

Saisiyat is a Formosan language of the Austronesian family with roughly 4,750 speakers, spoken in a small area of northwestern Taiwan wedged between Hakka Chinese and Atayal territory (Wufeng in Hsinchu; Nanzhuang and Shitan in Miaoli). Its two dialects are *Ta'ai* in the north and *Tungho* in the south. The extinct Formosan language Kulon is closely related, though the Taiwanese linguist Paul Jen-kuei Li treats it as separate. The language is strongly subject-initial while retaining features of split ergativity, and its case marking distinguishes personal from common nouns (Hsieh & Huang 2006). It carries loans from Japanese, from the period of Japanese occupation, from Mandarin, and — unevenly across dialects — from Hakka. About a thousand Saisiyat do not use the language; many young people use Hakka or Atayal instead, few children speak it, and despite its comparatively large speaker count it is endangered. The sources describe no religious affiliation beyond the creator god of the flood account.

## Historical note

Two events anchor the Saisiyat in Taiwan's recorded history. A series of major conflicts with the Kingdom of Tungning left the Saisiyat decimated and much of their land in the Kingdom's hands; the details remain obscure, but historians agree the outcome was negative for the Saisiyat. Later, the first indigenous victim of the White Terror was Jih Chin-chun, a Saisiyat man executed in 1952.
