## Overview

The Tuvans, also called Tyvans, are a Turkic ethnic group indigenous to Siberia who live in Tuva, Mongolia, and China. They speak Tuvan, a Siberian Turkic language. Tuvans form the majority of the population in the Tuva Republic; the 2010 Russian census recorded 249,299 Tuvans within Tuva, representing 82.0% of the republic’s population. In Mongolia, Tuvans include the Dukha of Khövsgöl Aimag and the Tsengel Tuvans of the Tsagaan Gol River Valley in Altai Tavan Bogd National Park. In China, most Tuvans live in the Xinjiang Autonomous Region, where they are included under the Mongol ethnicity. Their folk culture includes semi-nomadic livestock herding, felt-covered yurts and conical hide tents, throat singing, shamanistic practices, and longstanding relationships with the Sayan and Altai mountain regions.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state that the *bogtag* headdress worn by women seems to have been restricted to married women of very high rank.

### Architecture

Tuvan mobile dwellings included circular *yurts*, usually used in the steppes, and conical hide tents used near or inside a forest. Earlier descriptions also identify yurts covered by felt or *chums*, layered with birch bark or hide. These dwellings were relocated seasonally as herding groups moved to newer pastures. The sources also mention the Uyghur fortress Por-Bazhyn in lake Tere-Khol in southeastern Tuva, and plans to restore its remains.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention livestock equipment indirectly through the traditional use of skis and a lasso in elk hunting.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They identify the *bogtag* as a women’s headdress associated with married women of very high rank.

## Music & performance

A distinctive Tuvan musical form is throat singing, commonly known as *khoomei*. The sources describe techniques in which singers emphasize overtones, producing the effect of multiple tones. They name Yat-Kha, Huun-Huur-Tu, Chirgilchin, and the Alash Ensemble as groups from Tuva that feature throat singing.

The sources also identify Kongar-ool Ondar as a famous *Khoomeizhi* and one of the masters of *Khoomei*. The American blues and jazz musician Paul Pena taught himself overtone singing and travelled to Tuva to compete in a throat-singing competition. His experience was the subject of the 1999 documentary *Genghis Blues*.

The Tuvan folk song “Tooruktug Dolgai Tangdym” is mentioned in connection with the historical importance of livestock herding.

## Festivals & rituals

They describe traditional religion as a form of Tengriism or Turkic animistic shamanism. During the 18th century, Tuvans converted to Tibetan Buddhism through contact with Mongolians, while many shamanistic elements continued to be practiced alongside the new religion.

## Foodways

They state that the Tuvans historically herded goats, sheep, camels, reindeer, cattle, horses, and yaks. The sources also mention that the Dukha were reported as resorting to eating their herds, but provide no broader account of Tuvan foodways.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources mention the Tuvan folk song “Tooruktug Dolgai Tangdym” and state that its subject reflects the historical importance of livestock herding.

The sources identify Galsan Tschinag as a shaman and writer, Mongush Kenin-Lopsan as a shaman and ethnographer, and Vladimir Oidupaa as a throat singer and bayanist.

## Language & religion

Tuvan belongs to the Northern or Siberian branch of the Turkic language family. Four dialects are recognized: Central, Western, Southeastern, and Northeastern, the last also called Todzhinian. Tuvan is written using a variety of the Cyrillic script. The sources state that a talking dictionary has been produced by the Living Tongues Institute.

Traditional Tuvan religion is described as Tengriism or Turkic animistic shamanism. During the 18th century, Tuvans converted to Tibetan Buddhism through contact with Mongolians, but many shamanistic elements continued alongside Buddhism. The sources also describe genetic and historical relationships between Tuvans and other Siberian Turkic peoples, including the Altaians and the Khakas.
