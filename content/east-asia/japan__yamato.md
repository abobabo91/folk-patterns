## Overview

The Yamato, also known as the Wajin or Japanese, are an East Asian ethnic group comprising over 98% of Japan’s population. The term can also refer to the first people settled in Yamato Province, now Nara Prefecture. Present-day Japanese primarily descend from Yayoi people, with ancestry from the indigenous Jōmon people and continental East Asian migrants. Japanese is the principal language of the Japonic language family, which also includes the Ryukyuan languages and the Hachijō language. The Yamato are significant to folk-culture study because the sources describe their historical settlement, language, writing systems, religious development, and changing ethnic terminology, while also documenting the contested and propagandistic uses of the term Yamato.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe an early Yamato belief system featuring ancestor worship that was elevated into a national religion known as Shinto. They also state that people of Wa clapped their hands in worship, a practice still performed at Shinto shrines.

## Foodways

Third-century Chinese sources described the Wa as eating raw fish, vegetables, and rice served on bamboo and wooden trays.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that Chinese historical texts recorded the Wa and that the Wei Zhi mentioned Yamataikoku and Queen Himiko in the 3rd century. They also describe the Kojiki as the earliest Japanese text, dating to the early eighth century, and state that it was written in Chinese characters.

## Language & religion

Japanese is the principal language of the Japonic language family. The family also includes the Ryukyuan languages and the variously classified Hachijō language. Japanese has no official status in Japan but is the de facto national language; a standard form is known as *hyōjungo*, or *kyōtsūgo*, and is taught in schools and used in television and official communications. Japanese uses kanji together with the syllabaries hiragana and katakana; Latin script, or *rōmaji*, is also used to a limited extent. The language is normally subject–object–verb, uses particles to mark grammatical functions, and has a complex system of honorifics. The sources describe Shinto as a national religion developed from ancestor worship and identify the Ryukyuan languages as a distinct branch of Japonic, classified by UNESCO as endangered.
