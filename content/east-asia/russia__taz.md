## Overview

The Taz are a mixed Sinitic and Tungusic ethnic group whose communities are primarily located in Primorsky Krai, Russia, especially in the upper Ussuri River basin and the Olginsky district. Their ancestry developed through marriages between Han Chinese men and Tungusic women belonging to the Udege, Nanai, and Oroch peoples in Outer Manchuria. The Taz traditionally spoke Taz, a variety of Northeastern Mandarin with borrowings from Udege and Nanai, but Russian is now dominant and the Taz dialect is likely extinct. The 2021 Taz population was 235. Their folk culture combines influences from northern Chinese cuisine, clothing, agriculture, Buddhism, ancestor worship, Manchu folk religion, shamanism, and the lifeways of the Tungusic peoples of the Ussuri region.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Chinese-style clothing** — By the late 19th century, Taz people wore Chinese-style clothing, reflecting the influence of Han Chinese settlers and the agricultural culture adopted in Outer Manchuria.

**Ceremonial robes** — The Udege, Ulchi, and Nanai used Chinese dragons on ceremonial robes, alongside other Chinese-influenced designs.

**Black jackets and trousers** — Taz women occasionally wore traditional outfits consisting of black jackets and trousers.

**Motif vocabulary.** Chinese dragons on ceremonial robes, scroll and spiral bird designs, monster mask motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Taz clothing changed substantially after settlement in the Russian Empire and during the Soviet period. Their clothing largely became indistinguishable from that of the local Russian population. Taz women nevertheless occasionally wore traditional outfits consisting of black jackets and trousers, while elders retained traditional attire for funerals. Earlier Taz dress was described as Chinese-style clothing, and the wider Chinese influence in Outer Manchuria included silk and cotton.

### Architecture

By the late 19th century, the Taz lived sedentary lives in permanent homes called *fanzas*. After most Taz from villages in the Olginsky, Kavalerovsky, and Tetyukhinsky districts were relocated to Mikhailovka in 1938, they transitioned to living in wooden houses.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do mention iron cooking pots among Chinese influences adopted by Tungusic peoples of Outer Manchuria, as well as spears, nets, hooks, rifles, and traps used in fishing and hunting.

## Music & performance

They mention that the Taz dialect was used by Taz servicemen who could intercept Chinese military communications conducted in Mandarin during the Soviet era, but this concerns language use rather than music.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe several religious and ritual influences. Before Primorye’s incorporation into the Russian Empire, Taz religious beliefs were influenced by Buddhism, ancestor worship, Manchu folk religion, and shamanism, together with local elements. Chinese influence included celebrations of the Chinese New Year. Taz religious beliefs also included the idea that humans possessed 99 sequentially dying souls.

The sources provide limited information about funerary practice. Taz elders retained traditional attire for funerals, indicating a distinction between funeral clothing and ordinary dress. After the region’s annexation in 1860, the Taz were Christianized, and Taz people who identify as religious are now predominantly Russian Orthodox Christians.

## Foodways

Taz foodways combine hunting, fishing, gathering, agriculture, and northern Chinese culinary influence. Their fishing traditionally focused on salmon species, caught with spears, nets, and hooks. Hunting targeted deer, elk, wild boar, and fur-bearing animals, using spears, rifles, and traps. Gathering included ginseng, tree fungi, seaweed, trepang, and scallop shells. After adopting agriculture from the Chinese, the Taz also adopted Chinese crop names, farming techniques, and methods of field cultivation.

Taz culinary practices differed from those of Chinese settlers in the region. While raw animal products were avoided by the Chinese, the Taz, like the Udege and Nanai, consumed raw meat and fish dishes. Popular dishes included *mantou*, *baozi*, and *jiaozi*. After relocation to Mikhailovka, Taz households continued gardening and began raising cows, pigs, chickens, and ducks.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Taz language was a purely oral language with no writing system. A 400-word dictionary is mentioned. The language was grammatically Chinese and included loanwords from Nanai and Udege. The sources also record that Taz and Standard Chinese were mutually understandable in 1992, except mainly for place names and social terms. No contemporary literary revival or preservation project is described.

## Language & religion

Taz is a dialect of Northeastern Mandarin and belongs to the Chinese linguistic tradition, with loanwords from Tungusic languages, especially Udege and Nanai. It has no writing system and was historically transmitted orally. The sources describe a 400-word dictionary and state that the language was used by a thousand people in 1880. By the beginning of the 21st century, only a few elderly people still spoke it. The 2010 census found 274 Taz people but no Taz speakers, so the Taz language is likely extinct.

Taz religious life historically combined Buddhism, Chinese ancestor worship, Manchu folk religion, shamanism, and local elements. After annexation in 1860, the Taz were Christianized. Today, religious Taz people are predominantly Russian Orthodox Christians.
