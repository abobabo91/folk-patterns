## Overview

The Hui are an East Asian ethnoreligious group predominantly composed of Chinese-speaking adherents of Islam. They are distributed throughout China, especially in the northwestern provinces and the Zhongyuan region, and China had 11,377,914 Hui people according to the 2020 census. Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region and Gansu Province each have a Hui population of more than one million; the Hui are also a major minority in Qinghai, Gansu and Xinjiang, and the overall major minority in Henan. The Hui predominantly speak Chinese while using some Arabic and Persian phrases. Unlike many Chinese ethnic minorities, they are not associated with a non-Sinitic language. Their folk-cultural distinctiveness is expressed through Islamic dietary laws, Chinese Islamic cuisine, white caps worn by some men, headscarves worn by some women, and historical connections with Silk Road migration, trade and cultural assimilation.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Hui traditional dress includes white caps, or *taqiyah*, worn by some men, and headscarves worn by some women. The sources compare these practices with customs in many Islamic cultures. The sources also record historical colour terminology connected with the word *Huihui*: Arab were associated with white caps, Persians with black caps and Jews with blue caps. This terminology belonged to the wider historical use of *Huihui* for Muslims, Persian Christians and Jews during the Ming and Qing dynasties, rather than to a documented Hui clothing system.

### Architecture

 They mention Islamic mosques and Jewish synagogues in connection with the historical term *Qīngzhēnsì*, translated in the source as “Temple of Purity and Truth,”.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention Islamic dietary practices and halal food preparation.

## Music & performance

 The Hui language article states that merchants in the later imperial period sponsored operas and performances in the Hui dialect. It also says that some works of literature produced in Yangzhou, including *Qingfengzha*, contain Jianghuai Mandarin, and that large numbers of merchants from Huizhou lived in Yangzhou. These statements concern the Hui dialect region and performances in the Hui dialect rather than a documented musical tradition of the Hui ethnic group.

## Dance & theatre

 They mention operas and performances in the Hui dialect in the context of merchants in the later imperial period, but provide no title, theatrical form, movement vocabulary, performance setting or ceremonial function.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do document Islamic dietary rules and historical disputes over halal and kosher food preparation. During the Yuan dynasty, *Huihui* was used for Muslims and other groups, and a passage concerning Kublai Khan describes regulations requiring Muslims to eat animals slaughtered by others and to cease slaughtering sheep themselves and cease the rite of circumcision. They also state that Hui identity can include people who do not practise Islam, and that some Hui clans in Fujian and Taiwan no longer practise Islam while retaining Hui ancestry.

## Foodways

Hui foodways are connected in the sources with Islamic dietary laws and a distinctive variation of Chinese cuisine. Hui reject the consumption of pork, described as the most commonly consumed meat in China. The usual term for a mosque is *qīngzhēn sì*, while *qīngzhēn* is also commonly used for halal eating establishments and bathhouses. The source glosses *Qīngzhēn* as “pure and true” and links the term with Muslim culture since the Yuan or Ming dynasty. They also distinguish Hui identity from the simple label “Chinese Muslims”: not all Hui are Muslims, and not all Chinese Muslims are Hui. Some Hui clans in Fujian and Taiwan do not practise Islam and do not offer pork during ancestral worship. The sources mention a historical regulation concerning halal food preparation under Kublai Khan.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They mention a mythical Hui legendary folklore account in which 3,000 Chinese soldiers were supposedly exchanged for 300 “Hui” soldiers during the conflict against An Lushan, with only 3 Hui said to have survived and populated Ningxia. The source presents this as a mythical account rather than established history.

The sources also mention *Qingfengzha*, a novel produced in Yangzhou that contains Jianghuai Mandarin, and describe identity formation based on dialect.

## Language & religion

The Hui predominantly speak Chinese and use some Arabic and Persian phrases. The Hui ethnic group is described as unique among Chinese ethnic minorities because it is not associated with a non-Sinitic language. The Hui language article separately describes Hui Chinese as one of the ten primary varieties of Chinese spoken in the Hui cultural region, including the historical Huizhou Prefecture in southern Anhui, part of the historical Yanzhou Prefecture in Zhejiang and part of Raozhou Prefecture in Jiangxi. Hui Chinese has substantial internal variation and has been classified in different ways, including in relation to Jianghuai Mandarin, Wu and Gan.

The Hui have a distinct connection with Islamic culture, including Islamic dietary laws. The Chinese government defines the Hui as an ethnicity without regard to religion and includes people with Hui ancestry who do not practise Islam. Some Hui groups in Fujian and Taiwan have adopted Chinese folk religion, Buddhism or Taoism. The sources also mention historical terms including *Dashi Jiao*, *Huijiao*, *Yīsīlán jiào*, *Huihui*, *Huizu* and *Huimin*.
