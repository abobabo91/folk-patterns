## Overview

The Georgians, or Kartvelians, are a Caucasian ethnic group native to present-day Georgia and surrounding areas historically associated with the Georgian kingdoms. Significant Georgian diaspora communities are present in Russia, Turkey, Greece, Iran, Ukraine, the United States, and the European Union. Georgians are ethno-linguistically separate from neighboring nations and primarily speak Georgian, a Kartvelian language with no known relation to another language family. The Georgian nation formed from diverse geographic subgroups with characteristic traditions, manners, dialects, and, among Svans and Mingrelians, regional languages. Unofficial estimates cited by the State Ministry on Diaspora Issues of Georgia place the number of Georgians in the world at about 5 million. Georgian culture is documented through its writing, Orthodox Christian art, cuisine, polyphonic singing, dance, and regional traditions.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Georgian architectural traditions are represented in the sources chiefly through churches and monasteries. Medieval Georgian culture, strongly influenced by Eastern Orthodoxy and the Georgian Orthodox Church, produced churches and monasteries as well as icons and hagiographies of Georgian saints. Russian clergy later took control of Georgian churches and monasteries, prohibited the Georgian liturgy, and desecrated medieval Georgian frescoes in churches across Georgia.

## Music & performance

Georgia has a long-established tradition of polyphonic singing, regarded in the source as one of the earliest polyphonic cultures in the Christian world. The folk repertoire is divided into fifteen or sixteen regional musical dialects. In eastern Georgia, including Kartli, Kakheti, and Mtiuleti, table songs typically use a sustained drone in the bass with two ornamented upper voices. The best-known example named in the source is *Chakrulo*.

Western regions, including Imereti, Svaneti, Racha, Mingrelia, Guria, and Adjara, preserve forms of contrapuntal polyphony. Gurian singing is characterized by developed three- and four-part textures and *Krimanchuli*, a local variety of yodeling. Georgian polyphonic singing uses ostinato patterns, drone techniques, and sharp dissonances. The source identifies the C–F–G sonority as the “Georgian Triad.” Non-tempered intonation has survived most consistently in Svaneti.

Georgian polyphony was among the first traditions placed on the list of Masterpieces of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2001. It was relisted on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2008 and entered the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Georgia registry in 2011. Early gramophone recordings preserved by Anzor Erkomaishvili, large Soviet-era choirs, and ensembles such as Rustavi and Georgian Voices helped give Georgian polyphony international visibility. From the 1960s, Orera and later The Shin incorporated traditional material into popular and jazz-influenced idioms. The International Research Centre for Traditional Polyphony was established in 2003.

## Dance & theatre

Georgian folk dances have an ancient history documented by historical sources and archaeological findings. The ancient Greek geographer Strabo described pagan rituals of the Georgians that included dance. Christianity changed the religious context of these practices, but many rituals were preserved and adapted; dance gradually assumed a leading role as religious content diminished.

By the 4th century BCE, secular music was widespread among Georgian tribes, and Colchian tribes used martial songs and dances in battle. During the medieval period, dance became increasingly professionalized, with performances frequently held in the palaces of kings and feudal lords. Solo and paired dances developed, and canonical movements became established.

Georgian dance uses movements intended to express strength, agility, bravery, smoothness, and grace. Young men demonstrate strength, agility, and bravery through sharp and vigorous movements, including turns, leaps, jumps, and steps performed on the toes or knees. Female dance is distinguished in the source by smoothness and grace.

Pagan beliefs, the cult of Mithras, and Zoroastrianism were practiced in Georgia in the first centuries A.D., while Christianity became the state religion of Kartli, or Iberia, in 319 or 326. The conversion of Kartli to Christianity is credited to St. Nino of Cappadocia.

The sources state that Georgian tribes preserved rituals after the arrival of Christianity, adapting them to the new religion. They also describe Orthodox churches and monasteries, icons, and hagiographies of Georgian saints as important expressions of medieval Georgian culture. In the cuisine section, a Caucasian feast called *supra* is described as a major cultural setting involving a large assortment of dishes, large amounts of wine, and meals lasting for hours. The *tamada*, or toastmaster, holds an important and honoured position at such a feast.

## Foodways

Georgian cuisine is specific to Georgia and includes influences from European culinary traditions and surrounding Western Asia. Each historical province has its own culinary tradition, including Megrelian, Kakhetian, and Imeretian cuisines. The sources state that Georgian cuisine includes various meat dishes and a variety of vegetarian meals.

Food and drink are especially important during the Caucasian feast, or *supra*. At a *supra*, a large assortment of dishes is prepared and accompanied by large amounts of wine; dinner can last for hours. The *tamada* leads the feast as toastmaster, a role described as important and honoured.

Georgian food became popular in countries of the former Soviet Union because of Georgian immigration to other Soviet republics, particularly Russia. Major Russian cities have many Georgian restaurants, and Russian restaurants commonly include Georgian food items on their menus.

## Oral tradition & literature

The earliest known Georgian literary work named in the source is *The Martyrdom of the Holy Queen Shushanik* by Iakob Tsurtaveli, composed between 476 and 484 CE. It belongs to the genre of hagiography. In the 9th and 10th centuries, Christian theological literature flourished alongside a growing Georgian national identity, represented by *Praise and Exaltation of the Georgian Language* by John Zosimus.

The source states that David IV’s unification of Georgia in the 11th century marked a golden age for culture. Byzantine-influenced Christian literature flourished, and secular literature emerged. In the modern period, Prince Ilia Chavchavadze contributed to poetry, novels, short stories, and essays. Akaki Tsereteli became known as “the immortal nightingale of the Georgian people.” Chavchavadze, Akaki Tsereteli, Niko Nikoladze, and Iakob Gogebashvili were central to the modern Georgian cultural renaissance. From 1855 to 1907, Chavchavadze supported schools, the national theatre, and the newspaper *Iveria*.

## Language & religion

Georgian is a Kartvelian language and the primary language for Georgians of all backgrounds, including Svans, Mingrelians, and the Laz. The Georgian language has its own writing system and an extensive written tradition dating from the 5th century. It has at least 18 dialects, including Imeretian, Rachan, Lechkhumian, Gurian, Adjarian, Imerkhevian, Kartlian, Kakhetian, Ingiloan, Tushetian, Khevsurian, Mokhevian, Pshavian, Tianetian, Mtiuletian-Gudamaqrian, Fereydanian, Meskhetian, and Javakhian. Svan and Mingrelian are regional languages of the west, while Laz is spoken in Turkey and Georgia.

Most Georgians are Orthodox Christians and follow the Georgian Orthodox Church. The source gives 83.9% of the Georgian population as followers of Eastern Orthodox Christianity, most of whom are ethnic Georgian. Christianity was first preached in Georgia, according to Orthodox tradition, by the Apostles Simon and Andrew in the 1st century. Georgia also has Georgian Muslim communities, including Sunni Hanafi Muslims in Adjara, Shia Muslims among Iranian Georgians, and mostly Sunni Muslim Georgians in Turkey, as well as Georgian Jews and irreligious segments of society.
