## Overview

The Sakizaya, also called *Sakuzaya*, are Taiwanese indigenous peoples whose culture is centered in Hualien, formerly known as Kiray. They are an Austronesian people with cultural, linguistic, and genetic ties to other Austronesian ethnic groups, including peoples from the Philippines, Malaysia, Indonesia, Madagascar, and Oceania. Their population is approximately 1,000, while 985 people are registered as Sakizaya in one account. The Sakizaya language has about 2,000 speakers and is closely related to Amis. The community was recognized by the Taiwan government as a distinct ethnic group on 17 January 2007, after having been classified as Amis following the Karewan Incident of 1878. Sakizaya folk culture includes matrilinear social organization, seasonal rituals, ancestor worship, distinctive clothing, and efforts to preserve language and identity.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Sakizaya clothing** — The Sakizaya redesigned their attire after the Cultural Revitalization Movement in 2007, incorporating elements from their history and legends to distinguish it from Amis clothing. Its colors include gold yellow for earth and homeland, light brown for identity, red for historical battles, and blue for peace and friendship with the Amis.

**Women’s attire** — Women’s clothing includes earthy gold vests and blood-colored inner robes. These colors symbolize mature women’s responsibility for the land and family continuity, while unmarried girls wear mainly earthy gold without the dark red.

**Skirt and leg coverings** — Brown and green on the skirt and leg coverings represent branches, grass, and mud from the clothes of the ancestors. They recall the challenges faced while escaping from enemies.

**Men’s vest-style tops** — Men wear short vest-style tops associated with the strength and agility of male warriors.

**Back-panel skirt** — Men’s pants with a back-panel skirt use blue, red, and yellow colors and are intended to allow easy movement.

**Long golden robes** — During special celebrations, important community leaders usually wear long golden robes.

**Motif vocabulary.** Snow-white marble triangle, oval flakes, long tubes, white drops, green beads.

### Clothing & dress

Sakizaya clothing consists of upper garments, vests, leg coverings, betel nut bags, and headgear. Women wear earthy gold vests and blood-colored inner robes; unmarried girls wear mainly earthy gold. Men wear short vest-style tops and pants with a back-panel skirt in blue, red, and yellow. Important community leaders wear long golden robes during special celebrations.

Men’s headwear refers to the story of two tribesmen who sacrificed their lives for the whole tribe by jumping into a flood. The triangular piece of snow-white marble rock created afterward on Shabolu mountain is represented on men’s headwear. Women’s headwear represents the hardships of the battle of 1878. Its dark red refers to bloodshed, oval flakes to execution, long tubes to thorny bamboo, white drops to tears, and green beads to tribal defense.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention polished stone adzes and pottery in connection with Neolithic peoples arriving in Taiwan 6,000 years ago.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They identify betel nut bags as part of Sakizaya clothing and mention colored beads as elements of women’s headwear.

## Music & performance

They do describe ritual specialists called *mapalaway*, who communicate with the ancestral spirits and deities known as *dito*. This is a religious role rather than a documented musical tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

Sakizaya traditional rituals are organized according to the seasons: spring, or *pasavaan*; summer, or *ralod*; fall, or *sadinsing*; and winter, or *kasinawan*. One named ritual is *Palamal*, the “Worship of the Fire God.”

A Japanese document associates several rituals with the main staples millet, or *havay*, and dry rice, or *tipus*. These include the “Millet Sowing Ritual,” “Fishing Ritual,” “Collecting Ritual,” “Harvest Ritual,” and “Storing Ritual.” The rituals are based on the growth of millet.

The Sakizaya practice ancestor worship involving a pantheon of ancestral spirits and deities called *dito*. The spirits are considered “fickle as the weather,” and *mapalaway* are necessary to communicate with them. The homeland of the *dito* is Meilun Mountain in Hualien, which is also described as the place where the deceased pass through before finally resting in the sea.

## Foodways

Fishing and hunting are important to Sakizaya survival, while rice cultivation is a significant part of food production. Millet is important both as a food source and in determining the occasions of festivals. The sources state that rice cultivation is thought to have been acquired through the Kavalan.

The sources identify millet, or *havay*, and dry rice, or *tipus*, as main staples. They connect these foods with the “Millet Sowing Ritual,” “Fishing Ritual,” “Collecting Ritual,” “Harvest Ritual,” and “Storing Ritual.” No named dishes, cooking methods, beverages, dietary rules, or ceremonial recipes are described.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do describe historical and legendary themes preserved in clothing design, including the story of two tribesmen who sacrificed their lives by jumping into a flood and the history of the battle of 1878.

Sakizaya preservation efforts include the 2007 Cultural Revitalization Movement, during which clothing was redesigned to express Sakizaya history and legends. The government has also initiated efforts to preserve Sakizaya culture. The language has been supported through indigenous language textbooks and teaching and learning materials.

## Language & religion

Sakizaya is a Formosan language in the Austronesian language family and is closely related to Amis. After the 1878 Takobowan incident, Sakizaya people hid among the Nataoran Amis, and scholars consequently classified the language as an Amis dialect. In 2002, the Center of Aboriginal Studies of National Chengchi University corrected this classification in indigenous language textbooks. Sakizaya remains 60–70% different from Amis according to the account cited.

Traditional religion combines ancestor worship and animism. The *dito* are ancestral spirits and deities, and named gods include Malataw‧Otoki, Olipong, and Talaman or Takonawan. Some Sakizaya practice Christianity, and many have converted to Christianity. The exact number of Sakizaya practicing Christianity is uncertain.
