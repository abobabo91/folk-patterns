## Overview

The Yao are a classification for various ethnic minorities in China and Vietnam; the majority branch is also known as Mien. They originated around Changsha, today the capital of Hunan province, and reside chiefly in the mountainous southwest and south of China, especially Hunan, Guangdong, Guangxi, Guizhou, and Yunnan. They numbered 3,309,341 in the 2020 Chinese census. Yao languages belong mainly to the Hmong–Mien family, although some Yao speak Lakkja, a Tai–Kadai language, or Chinese dialects. In China, they are one of the 56 officially recognized ethnic groups. Their folk culture includes distinctive indigo clothing, annual observances such as the Pan Wang festival, patrilineal social organization, ancestor worship, shamanic practices, and numerous regional endonyms and subgroups.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Indigo clothing** — During the Southern Song, Zhou Qufei described the Yao as wearing distinctive fine blue clothing produced using indigo.

**Red Yao long-hair tradition** — The women of the Red Yao group of Longsheng Various Nationalities Autonomous County are known for keeping their hair extremely long and for dancing with it. The tradition is associated with the village of Huangluo, which became a tourist destination.

**Dao Áo Dài** — In northern Vietnam, Dao Áo Dài is listed as a subgroup name among Làn Tiẻn communities, together with Dao Tuyển, Dao Chàm, and Dao Slán Chỉ.

**Dao Quần Trắng** — Dao Quần Trắng is listed among the Khố Bạch groups in Vietnam and is also called Dao Họ in Yen Bai and Lao Cai.

### Clothing & dress

The Book of the Later Han Dynasty described the Yao as “liking five-colored clothes,” going barefoot, and being colorfully dressed. During the Southern Song, Zhou Qufei recorded distinctive fine blue clothing produced using indigo. The source also lists Vietnamese subgroup names associated with clothing, including Dao Quần Chẹt, Dao Quần Trắng, Dao Áo Dài, Dao Đầu Bằng, and Dao Đội Ván. These names are attached to regional Dao classifications.

The Red Yao women of Longsheng Various Nationalities Autonomous County traditionally keep their hair extremely long and cut it only at three and eighteen years of age.

## Music & performance

They do document performance connected with the Red Yao long-hair tradition: women of the Red Yao group of Longsheng Various Nationalities Autonomous County are known for keeping and dancing with their long hair.

Religious specialists called shaman priests formed part of Yao communities. They engaged in exorcism, spiritual communication, and divination using chicken bones or bamboo sticks. These practices are described as religious activities rather than as musical performances.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name no Yao theatrical tradition, dramatic genre, mask dance, or shadow-puppet tradition. They record that the women of the Red Yao group of Longsheng Various Nationalities Autonomous County keep and dance with extremely long hair. The village of Huangluo became a tourist destination because of this tradition, but the source gives no further description of the dance or its ritual and entertainment contexts.

## Festivals & rituals

The Yao celebrate the **Pan Wang (King Pan) festival** annually on the sixteenth day of the tenth lunar month. The festival celebrates the mythical original story of the Yao people and has developed into a holiday associated with a good harvest and ancestor worship.

The origin story connected with Pan Wang concerns the Chinese Emperor Gao Xin and a faithful dog named Pan Hu. According to the Yao tale, Pan Hu saved the emperor from an enemy chieftain, was transformed into a man, and married the emperor’s daughter. Their descendants became the Yao people. The story was used as a basis for associating the Yao with the Mo Yao, highlanders exempt from forced labour during the Tang dynasty.

Yao religious life includes patrilineal ancestor worship, celebration of the lunar new year, and recognition of a set of 18 gods and goddesses, mostly of Han Chinese origin. Shaman priests performed exorcism, spiritual communication, and divination with chicken bones or bamboo sticks. Taoism has historically been important.

## Foodways

The Yao have been farmers for over a thousand years, chiefly cultivating rice through plowing. A few practice slash-and-burn agriculture, and Yao living near forested regions also engage in hunting. These are the only subsistence and food-producing practices described in the sources.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Yao origin tale tells how the faithful dog Pan Hu saved the Chinese Emperor Gao Xin, became a man, married the emperor’s daughter, and founded the ancestry of the Yao through their descendants. This story is connected with the Pan Wang festival and with the historical association of the Yao with the Mo Yao.

The Yao lacked their own written language until recently, so much of their ancient history was transmitted through ancient Han Chinese sources. The Book of the Later Han Dynasty described their clothing and manner of dress. The source also records that the Yao celebrate the mythical original story of their people during the Pan Wang festival.

## Language & religion

Yao languages belong chiefly to the Hmong–Mien family. Mienic languages include Iu Mien, Kim Mun, Biao Mon, Dzao Min, and Biao Min; other Yao languages include Bunu, Pa-Hng, Younuo, Kiong Nai, and Lakkja. Lakkja is described as a Tai–Kadai language, while about 500,000 Yao speak Chinese dialects. The Iu Mien comprise 70% of the Yao population. A unified Yao writing system was developed after 1982, finalized in 1984 in Ruyuan County, Guangdong, and designed on the Latin alphabet. It distinguishes 30 syllable initials, 121 syllable finals, and eight tones. Bunu has a separate written standard.

Yao religion includes patrilineal ancestor worship, lunar-new-year observance, 18 gods and goddesses, shaman priests, exorcism, spiritual communication, and divination using chicken bones or bamboo sticks. Taoism has historically been important, while the sources describe the endurance of pre-Daoist folk religion and some Buddhist beliefs. Zhang Youjun argues that the Yao are resolutely Taoist despite Buddhist expression in ritual texts.
