## Overview

The Salar people, also called Salır Turkmens or Salır Turks, are a Turkic ethnic minority in China who speak Salar, a Turkic language of the Oghuz sub-branch. They numbered 165,159 people in 2020. Salars live principally in the Qinghai–Gansu border region on both sides of the Yellow River, especially in Xunhua Salar Autonomous County and Hualong Hui Autonomous County of Qinghai and the adjacent Jishishan Bonan, Dongxiang and Salar Autonomous County of Gansu. Communities also occur in Henan, Shanxi and northern Xinjiang, including the Ili Kazakh Autonomous Prefecture. They are described as a patriarchal agricultural society and are predominantly Muslim. Their folk-culture profile includes agricultural production, distinctive language varieties, Islamic practice, clothing, kinship customs, Hua’er singing and the women’s Kouxuan instrument.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The typical clothing of the Salar is described as very similar to that of the Hui people in the region. Men commonly wear beards, white shirts and white or black skullcaps. Men’s traditional clothing includes jackets and gowns. Young unmarried women are accustomed to wearing Chinese dress in bright colors, while married women use a traditional veil in white or black colors.

### Architecture

They record that some Salar customs involve placing stones on the corners of houses, following a compromise associated with prayer flags and Tibetan Buddhist practice.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention the Quran brought by Haraman and Ahman, which is preserved at Jiezi Grand Mosque in Haidong, Qinghai, and was repaired by the Nanjing Museum to protect it from decay. The sources also state that prayer wheels with mantras were part of the demands made by a Tibetan ruler, although the Salars initially refused these demands on religious grounds.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They describe beards, skullcaps and women’s veils as elements of dress, but provide no further account of adornment or its ritual functions.

## Music & performance

Singing is part of Salar culture. A style of singing called *Hua'er* is shared among Han, Hui, Salar and Tibetans in Qinghai province.

The Salar have a musical instrument called the *Kouxuan*. It is a string instrument manufactured in silver or copper and played only by women. No other Salar instruments, song genres, court traditions, religious performance forms or documentation projects are covered by the supplied sources.

## Festivals & rituals

Salars profess Sunni Islam and follow the Hanafi school of law. Many adhere to the Naqshbandi Sufi order, which spread throughout the region in the 17th and 18th centuries. Islamic education is associated with Gaizi Mişit Madrasah in Jiezi Village.

The sources describe marriage as an institution in which matchmakers and parents arrange unions among the Salar. They state that Salar women had an average first-marriage age of 18.69 years in 2000, while Tibetan women were married at an average age of 23.8 years in 1990. The sources also discuss intermarriage with Hui and Tibetans, including Tibetan women as spouses of Salar men who married outside their ethnicity. Birth, coming-of-age and funeral rites are not covered.

## Foodways

The Salar practice agriculture and horticulture. They cultivate chili and pepper in their gardens, and grow buckwheat, millet, wheat and barley. Other crops named in the sources are melons, grapes, apples, apricots and walnuts. A few Salar raise livestock, and the local timber industry provides another source of income for some villages.

They state that Salars are predominantly Muslim and profess Sunni Islam, but do not specifically describe dietary rules or food practices associated with Islam. The sources also record that the Salar are an entrepreneurial people involved in multiple businesses and industries.

## Oral tradition & literature

Salar tradition and Chinese chronicles identify the Salars as descendants of the Salur tribe, belonging to the Oghuz Turk tribe of the Western Turkic Khaganate. An Islamic legend tells of two brothers, Haraman and Ahman, who fled the Samarkand area with eighteen members of the tribe on a white camel carrying water, soil and a Quran. The group traveled through the northern route of the Tian Shan range, passed through Gansu and other named regions, and eventually reached Qinghai. Later, another forty people from Samarkand joined them, while twelve settled in the present Guide County.

A folk ballad about the 1781 rebellion and Su Sishisan’s decision to fight against the Qing Empire was still told by the Salars as late as 1937. The Salar-language article also gives an excerpt from the *kiş yiğen ğadın kiş* (“man-eating woman”) story.

## Language & religion

Salar is a Turkic language and an independent primary branch of the Oghuz languages. It is mainly spoken in Qinghai and Gansu, with a distinct variety in Ili, Xinjiang. The language has two large dialect groups: one in Xunhua County and Gansu, influenced by Tibetan and Chinese, and another in the Ili Kazakh Autonomous Prefecture, influenced by Uyghur and Kazakh. The Gaizi and Mengda dialects are also described, with Gaizi or Jiezi as v-Salar and Mengda as b-Salar.

Most Salar do not use a written script for ordinary purposes and use Chinese characters, while Salar serves primarily as a spoken language and Chinese as both spoken and written language. Arabic and Latin alphabets have also been used, and two internet writing variants are identified as TB30 and TB31. Salars profess Sunni Islam and follow the Hanafi school of law. Many adhere to the Naqshbandi Sufi order; the sources also describe the historical spread of Khufiyya and Jahriyya teachings.
