## Overview

Miao is a modern Chinese designation for a category of ethnic groups living in southern China and Mainland Southeast Asia. In China, the Miao live primarily in mountainous areas of Guizhou, Yunnan, Sichuan, Hubei, Hunan, Guangxi, Guangdong, and Hainan. The 2020 census estimated about 11 million Miao in China. Miao communities also live in Thailand, Laos, Vietnam, Burma, the United States, French Guiana, France, and Australia as a result of migration. The designation includes groups with autonyms such as Hmong, Hmu, Xong (Qo-Xiong), and A-Hmao. Most Miao speak Hmongic languages, a branch of the Hmong–Mien language family, although the Mienic-speaking Kem Di Mun people in Hainan are also designated as Miao by the Chinese government. Miao languages and cultural practices are internally diverse, and the official category includes groups whose languages are mutually unintelligible.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Miao embroidery** — Miao women are described as highly skilled in embroidery, weaving, paper-cutting, batik, and intricate jewelry casting. Embroidered clothing includes vests, coats, hats, collars, cuffs, full skirts, and baby carriers. The designs are extremely complicated and colorful, with clean lines.

**Miao clothing** — Traditional clothing is described as highly elaborate and varies among major Miao groups. The Chinese classification historically associated groups with the characteristic colors of women’s clothing, including Red Miao, Black Miao, Big Flowery Miao, White Miao, Green/Blue Miao, and Small Flowery Miao.

Girls of around seven learn embroidery from their mothers and sisters and become skilled by their teenage years. Miao embroidery and silver jewelry are valued for their delicacy, beauty, design, style, and craftsmanship.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no individual embroidered motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe Miao women’s clothing as including vests, coats, hats, collars, cuffs, full skirts, and baby carriers. Clothing patterns are complicated and colorful, with clean lines. The sources distinguish color-associated categories including Red Miao, Black Miao, Big Flowery Miao, White Miao, Green/Blue Miao, and Small Flowery Miao.

Women’s clothing and adornment are especially important during weddings, funerals, and springtime celebration. Heavy and intricate silver headdresses and jewelry are worn on significant occasions. The sources state that silver jewelry is an essential element of Miao marriages, particularly for the bride, and that a mother’s silver jewelry collection can form part of bridal wealth or dowry.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do describe silver jewelry as completely handmade and carved with fine decorative patterns, but provide no separate account of metalworking beyond this jewelry production.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Miao silver jewelry is handmade, with distinctive design, style, and craftsmanship. It symbolizes the wealth of Miao women and is associated with social identity and marriage. A Miao saying recorded in the source states: “decorated with no silver or embroidery, a girl is not a girl.” Families may begin saving silver jewelry for girls at an early age, hoping that the accumulated jewelry will contribute to a favorable marriage.

Silver headdresses and jewelry are especially important during weddings, funerals, and springtime celebration. The sources also state that the youngest son receives his mother’s silver jewelry collection, which is used as bridal wealth or dowry. Tattoos, henna, hair practices, and other forms of body adornment are not covered.

## Music & performance

 They mention arts and culture among the activities to which Miao women contribute, but provide no further musical information.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources mention springtime celebration as an occasion when Miao women wear heavy and intricate silver headdresses and jewelry. Weddings and funerals are also identified as significant occasions for ceremonial silver adornment.

Marriage customs include clan exogamy: marrying within the same family clan name is described as taboo, even when the couple are not blood related or from the same community. A Miao woman has cultural freedom to marry a man of her choice, but marriage remains subject to these clan rules. The youngest son traditionally remains with and cares for aging parents after marriage and receives a larger share of the family inheritance.

The sources also describe women presiding over weddings in some Southeast Asian marriages.

## Foodways

 They mention swidden or slash-and-burn cultivation as a farming technique used on mountainous or marginal lands.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources record a Tang dynasty Chinese legend in which the Miao descended from the Jiuli tribe led by Chiyou. In the legend, they were defeated at the Battle of Zhuolu by the coalition of Huang Di and Yan Di. The San Miao are described in the same source tradition as descendants of the Jiuli Tribe, with a kingdom around Dongting Lake that was defeated by Yu the Great.

The sources also state that the Miao adopted one official ethno-name in modern China and that members use various self-designations. They mention Miao sayings, including the saying concerning silver and embroidery, but provide no broader collection of proverbs, riddles, epics, or folktales. The Hmongic languages have no universally accepted writing system. Some writing traditions and revival efforts are described in the language source.

## Language & religion

Hmongic languages, also known as Miao languages, include Hmong, Hmu, and Xong. Hmongic is one of the primary branches of the Hmong–Mien language family, the other being Mienic. The sources describe Hmongic as diverse, with perhaps twenty languages and probably over thirty when additional dialectal distinctions are considered. The languages of the four primary Miao groups—Hmong, Hmub, Xong, and A-Hmao—are mutually unintelligible.

The sources describe at least a dozen writing systems for Hmongic languages, none universally accepted among Hmong people. Samuel Pollard introduced the Pollard script around 1905 for A-Hmao. Shong Lue Yang’s Pahawh Hmong script originated in Laos. In the 1950s, the Chinese government devised pinyin-based Latin alphabets for Xong, Hmu, and Chuangqiandian, as well as a Latin alphabet for A-Hmao. The sources state that the religious landscape and spiritual practices of the Miao are not covered.
