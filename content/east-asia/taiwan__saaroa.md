## Overview

The Saaroa, also called the Hla'alua, are an Indigenous people of central southern Taiwan. They live in the villages of Taoyuan and Kaochung in Taoyuan District, Kaohsiung, and in Maya Village in Namasia District, Kaohsiung. The group received official recognition from the Taiwanese government on 26 June 2014 as Taiwan’s 15th Indigenous people; before that, it was considered a subgroup of the Tsou people. Saaroa is a Southern Tsouic language of the Austronesian family and is critically endangered, with fewer than 10 native speakers and an ethnic population of 400 people. The language has no longer an active speech community, while Mandarin and Bunun are widely used in daily life. Saaroa material culture documented in the sources includes leatherwork, clothing, hats, embroidery, and practical objects used in daily life, hunting, rituals, and children’s play.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

They describe clothing made from leather and fabric, including cross-stitch embroidery and tricolor stripes.

**Leather clothing** — The Hla'alua are described as excelling in hunting and tanning, developing leather crafts and products. Traditional men’s shirts, hats, and pants are made of goat or deer leather.

**Cross-stitch embroidery** — Women’s blue or white long-sleeved tops feature cross-stitch embroidery on the front panel.

**Tricolor stripes** — A red fabric top with yellow, green, white, green, and yellow stripes on the back symbolizes family and tribal commitment.

### Clothing & dress

Men’s formal dress includes a red long-sleeved top with a chest cover, a black short skirt, and a goat-skin hat. Traditional gentlemen’s attire also includes shirts, hats, and pants made of goat or deer leather. Leather hats are decorated with seashells on the front and five feathers: two eagle feathers on each side and a white tail feather from a Mikado pheasant in the center. Leather clothing and leather hats are associated with the group’s hunting and tanning skills.

Women braid their hair with traditional scarves and wear hats adorned with rooster feathers. Their clothing consists of a black skirt and a blue or white long-sleeved top with cross-stitch embroidery on the front panel. Women from Fliplara Village in Taoyuan often wear blue tops, while women from the communities of Paisiana, Talisia, and Bilangan in Gaozhong frequently choose white tops. According to the legend recorded in the source, rooster-feather hats commemorate the rooster and assist the tribe in negotiating with the sun.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that other handicrafts are prominent and are mostly practical items for daily life, hunting, rituals, and children’s toys.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources describe seashells sewn onto the front of men’s leather hats and feathers attached to those hats. They also describe rooster feathers adorning women’s hats and traditional scarves used in women’s braided hair. No other jewelry, tattooing, or body-adornment practices are documented.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that some practical handicrafts are used in rituals. The account of women’s rooster-feather hats records a legend in which the hats commemorate the rooster and help the tribe negotiate with the sun.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Saaroa language source records an oral tradition according to which the Tsouic group originated in Yushan. About 2,000 years ago, the group divided into Northern Tsou and Southern Tsou. Northern Tsou moved down along Nantzuhsien River to the west, while Southern Tsou moved down along Laonung River. The latter later divided into Kanakanabu and Saaroa about 800 years ago.

They do indicate that Saaroa has been documented through teaching and learning materials and through the Saaroa translation of President Tsai Ing-wen’s 2016 apology to Indigenous people.

## Language & religion

Saaroa, also called Hlaʼalua, is a Southern Tsouic language spoken by the Saaroa people. It is described as a Formosan or Tsouic language within the Austronesian family. Saaroa is critically endangered, with fewer than 10 native speakers, and there is no longer an active speech community. Even native speakers primarily use Mandarin or Bunun in daily life; many elders also speak Taiwanese Hokkien. The shift toward Bunun occurred after Bunun migration into the area inhabited by the Saaroa.

The language has 13 consonants and four vowels. Its basic syllable pattern is (C)V, and it has borrowed words from Japanese, Mandarin Chinese, Taiwanese southern Min, Bunun, and other neighboring Aboriginal languages. Many loan words were introduced during the Imperial Japanese Era (1895–1945). Roman script was used in earlier Saaroa writing materials.
