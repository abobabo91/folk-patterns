## Overview

The Nanai (Russian *nanaitsy*, from *nanai* / *nani*, "natives, locals, people of the land") are a Tungusic people of the Middle Amur Basin, living along the Amur (Heilongjiang), Songhua (Sunggari) and Ussuri (Wusuli) rivers. In Russia they are settled downstream of Khabarovsk on both sides of Komsomolsk-on-Amur, on the Ussuri and Girin rivers and toward the Sea of Okhotsk; the 2010 census counted 12,003. In China they are one of the 56 officially recognised ethnic groups, known as *Hezhe*, numbering 4,640 in the 2000 census, mostly in Heilongjiang. Their language belongs to the Manchu-Tungusic family. Their descent is traced to the Wild Jurchens of northernmost Manchuria. In folk-culture terms the Nanai are known above all for garments cut and sewn from tanned fish skin — a practice that earned them the exonym "Fish-skin Tartars" (*Yúpí Dázi*) — and for Amur shamanic practice.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Fish-skin garment work** — Clothing was made from fish skins: the skins were dried, struck repeatedly with a mallet until completely smooth, and sewn together, with fish weighing more than 50 kilograms selected for the purpose. A woman's costume in the Museum of Ethnography (Stockholm), acquired in 1926 from the Russian Museum of Ethnography in St Petersburg, is made of burbot skins.

**Fish-skin appliqué** — Cut-and-stitched appliqué in white fish skin over dyed fish-skin ground, as on a pair of fish-skin leggings (or sleeves) in the British Museum: tapered, with a semi-circular top, dyed blue with a darker lower third turning yellow toward the hem, carrying large circular scroll appliqués and much smaller motifs below, lined with cream tabby linen.

**Cloth appliqué on leather and fur** — A British Museum quiver of leather and fur, made in six horizontally stitched sections, carries a square cloth appliqué below the opening and on the flap, its scrolls picked out in cream tabby linen against a blue (indigo-dyed) ground, outlined in green, yellow and blue yarns, with fur appliqué across the remaining front.

**Embroidered collars** — A man's collar in the British Museum has a dark blue tabby ground worked with scroll patterning in buttonhole stitch and isolated scrolls in chain stitch, its hem carrying threaded glass beads and suspended pendants.

**Pieced cloth and embroidery samples** — An incomplete British Museum domestic cloth doubling as an embroidery sample is built from small triangles of cream and dark blue tabby cotton patched into a ten-by-ten grid of squares.

Ornament was not purely decorative: on the Stockholm costume the garment edges symbolised the life force of the river, and the patterning carried a protective power derived from the maker's creative energy. A woman who could make strong patterns held high status. Many such garments show Chinese influence in their patterning.

**Motif vocabulary.** Scrolls (the dominant element, both continuous and isolated); circular appliqué medallions; curved two-tone motifs on footwear; diamond-set bead grids on collar hems; and, on shaman's gear, serpents, lizards and frogs, with branching horns on the hat.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress was fish skin, dried and mallet-softened, and fur. Surviving pieces indicate a layered wardrobe: a woman's full fish-skin costume; tapered fish-skin leggings or sleeves; a splayed arc-shaped collar for men, beaded and hung with stone and pearl-like pendants; and cloth boots whose lower leg and sole are cream tabby cotton, the sole quilted in self-colour thread, with a pale blue band at mid-boot and, above it, yellow cloth carrying dark blue and cream curved appliqué outlined in cream, pink and blue thread. Hair was politically marked: Amur peoples already wore a queue at the back of the head, and the Qing ordered the front of all male heads shaved after conquest, so that the Ulch called the Nanai "shaved-head people". Shaman's dress stood apart from daily wear — a skirt and jacket, a leather belt hung with conical metal pendants, mittens figured with serpents, lizards or frogs, and a hat with branching horns or with bear, wolf or fox fur attached; fragments of Chinese mirrors were sometimes worked into the costume.

### Architecture

The sources used here describe settlement rather than building: the people on the Ussuri lived in villages along the river banks, and three ethnic Nanai villages remain in Russia — Dzhuen, Ulika and Dada — but they say nothing about house form, materials, roofing or architectural ornament.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Household and hunting equipment in the collections is of bark, skin, wood and leather rather than clay. Birch bark was worked into lidded boxes (a box and its separate cover are catalogued at the British Museum). Fish skin served containers as well as clothing, in the form of a sewn pouch. A knife survives as a wooden handle. The long rectangular quiver described above, with its top flap, side ties and fastening ties, is of leather and fur. The only worked metal the sources name is the set of conical pendants on the shaman's leather belt, alongside pieces of Chinese mirror incorporated into shamanic costume. Nothing in the sources describes Nanai pottery.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment appears in the sources as part of garment construction rather than as separate ornament. The British Museum collar carries cream, light blue and dark blue glass beads of different sizes threaded in a diamond pattern along the hem, and below the beadwork a row of suspended pendants made of semi-precious stones and pearl-like beads. Shamanic regalia adds conical metal pendants hung from a leather belt and inset fragments of Chinese mirror. One funerary body marking is documented: in preparing the rite for a dead infant it was common to mark the body with coal, for instance by drawing a bracelet around the wrist, so that a later child born with similar markings could be recognised as the same soul returned.

## Music & performance

Documented Nanai performance divides between shamanic ritual sound and twentieth-century professional music. Shamanic practice rests on the shaman's expulsion of bad spirits through prayers to the gods, and on the voice carrying the souls of the dead toward the underworld in the three-day *kasa tavori*. One such ritual voice was recorded commercially: the Nanai female shaman Tchotghtguerele Chalchin performed an incantation recorded in Siberia for the song "The Lighthouse" — an adaptation of Wilfrid Wilson Gibson's poem "Flannan Isle" — on the French producer Hector Zazou's 1994 album *Chansons des mers froides* (Songs from the Cold Seas), with lead vocals by Siouxsie Sioux and backing by the Sakharine Percussion Group and the Sissimut Dance Drummers. The best-known Nanai singer is Kola Beldy (1929–1993), popular across the Soviet Union and Russia and particularly identified with his rendition of "Увезу тебя я в тундру" ("I will take you to the tundra"). On the Chinese side, the Hezhen have a body of oral literature called *Yimakan*, which belongs as much to performance as to literature. The sources name no Nanai musical instruments and describe no wedding or seasonal song repertoire, so instrument names and song genres are left out here rather than supplied from elsewhere.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used for this profile name no Nanai dance or dramatic tradition; the only staged or choreographed material they mention is the dog-sledge procession of soul figures within the funerary ritual described below.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources document no annual festival calendar, but they describe the funerary cycle in unusual detail. The Nanai hold that each person has both a soul and a spirit, and that the body is only the soul's outer shell — a belief original to them rather than introduced by Christianity. At death the two part ways: the spirit turns malevolent and begins to harm living relatives, and these *amban* may in time be tamed and afterwards worshipped, or else driven off by a special ritual. The soul is first placed in a temporary cloth shelter called a *lachako*, where it stays seven days, then moved into a wooden doll, a *panyo*. The *panyo* is treated as a living person: the closest family member puts it to bed each night with a miniature pillow and blanket and wakes it in the morning, carries it along when travelling, and occasionally sets a pipe into the small hole carved for its mouth so the dead may smoke. The final rite, *kasa tavori*, lasts three days with much feasting; on the third day the souls pass from the *panyo* into large human-shaped wooden figures made to the height of the deceased, called *mugdeh*, which are loaded onto a dog sledge for the journey to the underworld, *Buni*. Before departure the shaman relays the dead's last wishes to the assembled family — in the anthropologist Gaer's account, one soul asked that a debt to a neighbour be repaid. The shaman then leads the sledges on the dangerous journey and must leave *Buni* before sunset or die. Afterwards relatives formerly neither visited the graves nor spoke of the dead. Children under a year are reckoned birds rather than people: an infant's soul flies off as a bird, the body is wrapped in birch-bark paper and placed high in a forest tree — a "wind burial" — and the soul is free to enter a woman again.

## Foodways

The river economy was the food economy. Early visitors, including the French Jesuit geographers who travelled the Ussuri and Amur in 1709, found a people whose subsistence was based on fishing: villagers spent whole summers at it, eating fish fresh in summer — sturgeon was especially prized — and drying the surplus for winter. Fish also fed the few domestic animals they kept, which made the flesh of a locally raised pig close to inedible for visitors with European tastes. Agriculture arrived slowly, and in 1709 practically the only crop grown by the villagers on the Ussuri shores was tobacco. Fire itself belonged to the food hearth as a person: it was personified as an elderly woman, *Fadzya Mama*, whom small children were not allowed to startle by running up to the fire and in whose presence men were always courteous. Beyond the three days of feasting at *kasa tavori*, the sources name no ceremonial dishes, drinks or dietary rules, and no vernacular dish names, so none are given.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Chinese Hezhen possess a rich oral literature known as *Yimakan*. Nanai cosmology carries its own narrative material: the land was once flat until great serpents gouged out the river valleys, and every thing in the universe holds a spirit that wanders the world independently. The Nanai share one myth with southern Chinese, and some Han Chinese are said to have founded clan subdivisions among them. The best-known Nanai figure in world literature is not an author but a subject — Dersu Uzala, the Nanai guide and friend of the Russian explorer Vladimir Arsenyev, who wrote about him in two books later adapted by Akira Kurosawa in his 1975 film *Dersu Uzala*. Written Nanai literature is a Soviet and post-Soviet project: folklore materials were published from the 1980s and 1990s, and Nanai-language books, including poetry and children's fairy tales, were issued at Khabarovsk by writers such as G. Bel'dy, E. Samar and A. Passar, alongside Valentin Avrorin's Nanai translation of a Russian children's play.

## Language & religion

Nanai (also called Gold, Goldi or Hezhen; *Hèzhéyǔ* in China) is Manchu-Tungusic, with about 1,400 speakers out of some 17,000 ethnic Nanai. Dialects fall into middle and lower Amur groups (Naykhin, Dzhuen, Bolon, Ekon, Gorin and others), plus Kur-Urmi near Khabarovsk, Bikin on the middle Ussuri and Sungari on the Chinese side; classifications by Sunik, Avrorin and Sem differ, and teaching based on the Naykhin dialect has levelled the distinctions. Writing passed through missionary Cyrillic in the late nineteenth century, a Unified Northern Alphabet Latin script approved in January 1932, and Cyrillic again from 1937, the current alphabet dating from 1993; in China Nanai write in Chinese, and a Hezhe schoolbook in pinyin transcription appeared in 2005. Russian teaching continues in 13 schools in Khabarovsk. The Nanai are mainly Shamanist, revering the bear (*Doonta*) and the tiger (*Amba*) and worshipping the spirits of sun, moon, mountains, water and trees; many are also Tibetan Buddhist.
