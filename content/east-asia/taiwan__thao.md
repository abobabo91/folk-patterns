## Overview

The Thao are a small group of Taiwanese indigenous peoples who have lived near Sun Moon Lake, also known as Zintun and Lake Candidius, in central Taiwan for at least a century and probably since the time of the Qing dynasty. Their population was around 800, making them one of the smallest recognized indigenous peoples in Taiwan. Most members work in the tourism industry at Sun Moon Lake. The Thao have retained customs, beliefs, traditional culture, and language while also becoming assimilated into mainstream culture. Their language belongs to the Austronesian family and is classified as a Western Plains Formosan language. The Thao are especially significant in folk culture for their clothing, wooden-pestle music, ritual practices, oral history, and efforts to maintain an endangered language.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Dagobun* is a famous Thao garment made from cloth knitted with flax yarn and dog fur. The Thao clothing style is known for geometric patterns and the use of brown, blue, gray, and black.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name geometric patterns.

### Clothing & dress

Women’s traditional clothing consists mainly of a head scarf, sleeved blouse, corset, waist skirt, belt, knee breeches, and a crown made of flowers and plants. The head scarf was formerly made of black cotton and is now more commonly made from red and black strips of cloth, with sequins and pearls sewn onto it. Small beaded tassels hang from the sideburns, and a belt is fastened to the back of the head.

The corset is a one-piece garment that functions as a skirt. It is made from two rectangular pieces of cotton reaching to the knees; the pieces are fastened diagonally over the left and right shoulders and tied with a belt. Men’s traditional clothing consists of headgear, headwear, chest wear, vest, skirt, breech-less trousers, and leather shoes. Men’s clothing is made from leather, linen, and bark. The sources do not distinguish separate everyday and ceremonial clothing beyond these descriptions.

### Architecture

They state that the Chi-Chi earthquake of 1999 damaged or destroyed 80% of Thao houses.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do describe the wooden pestle as an important object used for pounding millet, work, communication through sound, and collective ritual. Pestles are mainly made from timber or cypress wood. Their shape is tapering rather than cylindrical, and differences in wood density, length, and form produce different tones.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources describe sequins, pearls, and small beaded tassels as elements of women’s head scarves and head adornment. Women also wear a crown made of flowers and plants.

## Music & performance

The wooden pestle is central to Thao musical practice. Originally, women used pestles to pound millet, while men mainly worked in the fields. The instrument is closely connected with the division of labor in Thao society. Because wood is readily available in the Sun Moon Lake region, the pestle could be produced easily and used by a large part of the population. Its relatively simple construction emphasizes the group rather than individual ownership, and any community member can use an instrument for work or collective rituals.

Pestles are made from timber or cypress wood. The density of the wood affects the frequency, with denser wood producing a lower tone. The tapering shape creates a complex vibration, while greater length produces a lower tone. Each pestle therefore has a distinctive tone and sound profile.

According to Thao traditional knowledge, women strike stones with pestles to create rhythmic sounds. Before a ritual, they perform the pestle song to notify ancestral spirits and hunters in the mountains that a ceremony is about to take place. The song is therefore understood as communication across distance.

The myth “A Cai and Feng She” explains the origin of the pestle song. A young hunter named A Cai becomes exhausted in the mountains and cannot return. His fiancée, Feng She, follows instructions sent by the gods, pounds a long pestle in a stone, and sings a joyful song. The sound reaches A Cai, restores his strength, and guides him home.

Public performances are mostly performed by women but are led and guided by tribal leaders, elders, and the Shinshii, female ritual specialists who communicate with the ancestral spirits, called Apari. The rhythms and manner of playing follow rules and taboos set by the Shinshii.

During Japanese rule, Thao pestle music became connected with colonial tourism and state performances. Performers used wooden pestles in dances and musical presentations for members of the Japanese royal family, as well as at exhibitions and official celebrations in Taiwan and Japan. Pestle music and dance later continued under the KMT through films and other media, and today remain part of festivals and tourism activities.

## Dance & theatre

The sources describe Thao dances and musical presentations involving wooden pestles. During Japanese rule, Thao performers used wooden pestles in dances and presentations associated with tourism, including events for members of the Japanese royal family, exhibitions, and official celebrations in Taiwan and Japan. Pestle music and dance also continued under the KMT through movies and other media. Today, the sources state that these performances continue in festivals and tourism activities to bring awareness to Thao traditions and preserve cultural identity.

## Festivals & rituals

Pestle music is most commonly performed during Thao rituals. The Thao believe that Lalu Island is the home of their highest ancestral spirits. During major events such as Lus’an in the third lunar month and the New Year/Harvest Festival, the community pounds pestles together to communicate with the spirits and pray for the tribe’s safety and well-being.

These occasions also provide settings in which elders teach younger people how to choose suitable wood and practice complex rhythms. This transmission of knowledge is described as a means of passing down Thao culture. Before a ritual, the pestle song notifies ancestral spirits and hunters in the mountains that the ceremony is about to begin.

The sources describe ritual leadership by tribal leaders, elders, and the Shinshii. These female ritual specialists communicate with the ancestral spirits, or Apari, and establish the traditional rules and taboos governing performance.

The sources state that women originally used wooden pestles to pound millet, while men mainly worked in the fields.

## Oral tradition & literature

Thao oral history states that the people originally lived in Chiayi, later moved to the Alishan Range, and eventually settled around Sun Moon Lake and Lalu Island in the middle of the lake. According to the legend, they chased a deer because they wanted to eat it. The deer entered the water, leaving them at Sun Moon Lake. An elder later dreamed of a fairy in a white cloak, who identified himself as the deer and told them that the place was their promised land.

The myth “A Cai and Feng She” explains the origin of the Thao pestle song. A Cai, a young hunter, becomes exhausted in the mountains, while Feng She, his fiancée, uses a long pestle and a joyful song to restore his strength and guide him home.

They do mention Robert A. Blust’s Thao-English dictionary and teaching and learning materials published by the Council of Indigenous Peoples of Taiwan.

## Language & religion

Thao is an Austronesian language of the Formosan languages and is classified as a Western Plains Formosan language. It is nearly extinct and is spoken by only a few people, mostly elders. The language has been sinicized, and most Thao speakers are bilingual or trilingual, speaking Mandarin Chinese and/or Taiwanese as well. Barawbaw and Shtafari are identified as dialects.

Speaking Thao was criminalised under Japanese rule of Taiwan and later the Kuomintang regime. In 2014, four first-language speakers and one fluent second-language speaker lived in Ita Thaw, traditionally called Barawbaw. Four elderly first-language speakers and some semi-speakers were reported in 2021. The sources also state that Thao has loanwords from Bunun, the language of the Bunun ethnic group, with whom the Thao cooperated and intermarried.

Thao ritual life includes communication with ancestral spirits. Lalu Island is believed to be the home of the highest ancestral spirits, and the Shinshii communicate with spirits called Apari.
