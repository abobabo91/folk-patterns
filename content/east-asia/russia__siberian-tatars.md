## Overview

Siberian Tatars are an indigenous Turkic-speaking population of the forests and steppes of southern Western Siberia, in a territory extending from somewhat east of the Ural Mountains to the Yenisey River in Russia. They call themselves *Yerle Qalıq*, meaning “older inhabitants,” distinguishing themselves from more recent Volga Tatar immigrants. The 2010 census counted more than 500,000 people in Siberia defining their ethnicity as “Tatar,” while about 200,000 were considered indigenous Siberian Tatars; only 6,779 called themselves “Siberian Tatars.” The group comprises three autochthonous groups, all Sunni Muslims of the Hanafi madhhab, and includes Tobol-Irtysh, Baraba, and Tom Tatars. Their culture combines Turkic, Mongolic, Ket, Samoyedic, and Ugric historical influences.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The ancient outerwear of the Siberian Tatars is described as similar in cut and color to Central Asian and Sayan-Altai clothing, including a Uyghur-Chinese lapel. Women’s dresses are described as similar to Bashkir dresses, with several rows of frills along the hem. Costumes from the early 20th century and later were subject to Volga-Ural Tatar influence.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources state that the Siberian Tatars profess Sunni Islam and that, before conversion to Islam, they practiced shamanism. Shamanistic influences can still be found in certain funerary and spiritual customs. Islamization first occurred around the 14th century; adoption of Islam began by the early 15th century, and most Siberian Tatars were Muslim by the late 18th century. A *tugum*, a group of related families originating from one ancestor, regulated family, household, and domestic relations and performed religious and folk rituals.

## Foodways

Traditional Siberian Tatar cuisine includes barley, *kattama*, *boortsog*, noodles, and *peremech*, together with dairy products such as *kaymak* and *qurut*. Traditional occupations included hunting, raising horses, and porterage; beginning in the 19th century, some Siberian Tatars also sought work in tanneries and sawmills.

## Oral tradition & literature

N. F. Katanov studied legends of the Siberian Tatars. According to those legends, the army of Kuchum Khan was divided into four wings: Kordak, Turaly, Ayaly, and Baraba. In the 21st century, teams conducted scientific research on literary-language norms and published *Русско-сибирскотатарский словарь = Урысца-сыбырца сүслек* (2010), the Russian-Siberian Tatar Dictionary, and *Грамматика современного сибирскотатарского языка* (2014), the Grammar of Modern Siberian Tatar Language.

## Language & religion

Siberian Tatar is a Turkic language with several dialects. The Tobol-Irtysh Tatar dialect is generally classified in the Kipchak–Nogai group, while Baraba Tatar and Tom Tatar are classified in the Kipchak–Kyrgyz group of Kipchak languages. The language also contains elements that could be classified in the Upper Altaian language group, received Karluk influences beginning in the 12th century, and was influenced by Kipchak-Bulgar in ethnically mixed villages where Volga Tatars settled. Until the 1920s, Siberian Tatars used an alphabet based on Arabic script; they adopted Latin script in 1928 and Cyrillic script in 1939. They are Sunni Muslims of the Hanafi madhhab, with continuing shamanistic influences in certain funerary and spiritual customs. As of 2018, public education in Siberian Tatar was not available, and local schools taught only in Russian and Volga Tatar.
