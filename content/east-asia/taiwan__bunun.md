## Overview

The Bunun are a Taiwanese Indigenous people who speak the Bunun language, an Austronesian language of the Formosan group. They are widely dispersed across Taiwan’s Central Mountain Range rather than concentrated in one locality. In 2000, they numbered 41,038, approximately 8% of Taiwan’s total Indigenous population and the fourth-largest Indigenous group. The Bunun have five communities: Takbunuaz, Takituduh, Takibaka, Takivatan, and Isbukun. Their culture includes mountain settlement, hunting, slash-and-burn agriculture, millet cultivation, ritual clothing, polyphonic singing, the *latuk* bow-harp, lunar prescriptions, and the Malahtangia ear-shooting festival. In the Bunun language, *Bunun* means “human,” “human being,” “man,” or “people.”

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Ritual clothing** — Bunun clothing was originally made from animal skins and linen. Through integration with the Han people, cotton and wool also came into use. Men’s ritual clothing includes a white vest with multicolored geometrical patterns, worn with a short black skirt and a colorful abdominal bag.

**Embroidered men’s clothing** — A further men’s outfit consists of a knee-length black skirt and a black or blue long-sleeved coat. Colorful embroidery appears on the neckline, in the middle of the coat, and on the back.

**Women’s embroidered dress** — Women wear long black or blue dresses with extended sleeves, with embroidery at the sleeve ends and on the dress placket.

**Women’s shirt and skirt** — Another women’s outfit combines a long-sleeved shirt embroidered at the sleeve ends, collar, shirt placket, and bottom hem with a long skirt bearing a colorful pattern at the bottom hem.

**Apron ensemble** — The women’s skirt may be covered by an apron embroidered at the center, left side, and bottom. Trousers may be worn under the apron.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name geometrical patterns and colorful embroidery.

### Clothing & dress

Men’s clothing includes an open white vest with vibrant multicolored geometrical patterns at the opening and back. It is worn with a short black skirt and a colorful abdominal bag, and these clothes are usually worn during rituals. Another men’s outfit pairs a knee-length black skirt with a black or blue long-sleeved coat embroidered at the neckline, middle, and back.

Women wear long black or blue dresses with extended sleeves and embroidery at the sleeve ends and dress placket. They may instead wear an embroidered long-sleeved shirt with a long skirt patterned at the bottom hem, together with an apron embroidered at the center, left side, and bottom. Trousers may be worn beneath the apron. Men exclusively wear headbands; hats and necklaces are also mentioned among Bunun accessories.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do describe hunting equipment and agricultural practices, including rifles, bows, wooden frames used during the Malahtangia festival, and slash-and-burn agriculture.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Bunun people use ornaments and accessories including headbands, hats, and necklaces. The sources state that most of these accessories are made from glass beads or shell pieces. Men exclusively wear the headbands mentioned in the source.

## Music & performance

The Bunun musical tradition is strongly centered on vocals, with instruments serving more personal and supplementary roles. The *Pasibutbut* is a song of the Bunun Sowing Festival. It is sung polyphonically in four-part harmony, with the source also describing a common eight-voice heterophonic form and performances usually involving five to twelve heterophonic voices. The Taiwan composer Jin Fong Yang analyzed its structure, and the Japanese musicologist Takatomo Kurosawa recorded Bunun musicians in 1943.

The *latuk* is a traditional instrument constructed like a hunting bow and also called the Bunun bow-harp. Its body is made from Makino or Fargesia bamboo. Traditionally, its string was made from Peach Wood, Hemp, and Rattan; steel strings are used today. The player places the mouth on the end of the bow, plucks the string, and shapes the mouth and breath to produce overtones and melody, in a manner compared by the source to the Jew’s Harp. The name derives from *la*, “to pluck,” and *tuk*, an onomatopoeia for the instrument’s sound.

The Bunun traditionally play the *latuk* alone during leisure time, especially from dusk to midnight. It functions as self-entertainment and emotional expression, including relief from loneliness or grief, rather than as a public or ceremonial instrument. The source records the instrument in the Qing Imperial Illustrations of Tributary Peoples in 1776 and states that Takatomo Kurosawa recorded Bunun men using it in courtship in 1943.

## Festivals & rituals

The Malahtangia, or ear-shooting festival, is a male rite-of-passage ceremony in Bunun culture. It is usually held between March and April, and women are traditionally barred from attending. Before the festival, every adult male journeys into the mountains to hunt. After a successful hunt, the men return and hang the carcasses from wooden frames so that boys can shoot at the dead animals. A boy who can shoot a deer’s ear is regarded as especially talented because of the ear’s small size. After the ritual, participating boys are considered adults and may join their brothers and fathers in hunting.

Changes in Bunun society have made the festival more of a ritualized performance. The marksmanship and hunting skills taught through it are no longer as relevant, but the event continues to teach respect for elders and the wider community.

The Bunun lunar calendar traditionally governed many activities. The moon was considered one of the major spirits, and daily life was aligned with lunar cycles. The sources state that, in a certain lunar month, women were forbidden to wash themselves. The Bunun also developed a primitive form of writing for recording lunar cycles and their relation to events such as the harvest and the slaughter of pigs.

## Foodways

The Bunun traditionally practiced slash-and-burn agriculture, hunted game, and relied on millet and yam among their staple foods. Taiwan oil millet was one of their staple crops until the 1941 relocation, and its cultivation was revived in the 21st century. The sources also mention the slaughter of pigs as an event associated with lunar-cycle records.

## Oral tradition & literature

Bunun legends describe a time when two suns shone upon the Earth and made it unbearably hot. A father and son endured hardships and shot down one sun, which became the moon. The moon then instructed them to tell their people to observe three commandments: to follow the waxing and waning of the moon in rituals and work, to conduct rituals honoring the spirits of heaven and Earth throughout life, and to avoid forbidden behaviors in order to become orderly and peaceful.

A variant tells of parents who left their newly born son in a basket while working in a field. After a day beneath the two suns, the child had dried up and become a black lizard, after which the father shot down one of the suns. Another origin story describes the Vonum Group living in the plains before a destructive flood, a huge serpent, and a monster crab that killed the reptile.

The sources identify Bukun Ismahasan Islituan as a Bunun poet and writer.

## Language & religion

Bunun is a Formosan language within the Austronesian language family. It is subdivided into five dialects: Isbukun, Takbunuaz, Takivatan, Takibaka, and Takituduh. Isbukun is the dominant and prestige dialect and is mainly spoken in the south of Taiwan. Takbunuaz and Takivatan are mainly central dialects, while Takibaka and Takituduh are northern dialects. Takipulan, a sixth dialect, became extinct in the 1970s. The Bunun language is verb-initial and has an Austronesian alignment or focus system.

The Bunun traditionally understood the world as filled with supernatural beings called *qanitu*, associated with places such as trees and rocks. The sky, called *dihanin* or *diqanin*, was an important locus of supernatural power. Rituals, taboos, prophetic dreams, elders, and lunar observance structured traditional life. After missionary activity, most Bunun came to belong to the Catholic Church or the local Presbyterian Church.
