## Overview

Tibetans are an East Asian ethnic group native to the Tibetan Plateau, with major populations in the Tibet Autonomous Region and in Tibetan autonomous areas of Qinghai, Sichuan, Gansu, and Yunnan. Tibetan communities also live in Ladakh, Nepal, Bhutan, India, and a global diaspora. China’s 2020 census recorded approximately 7.06 million Tibetans nationwide. Important population centers include Lhasa and the Yarlung Tsangpo valley, the pastoral districts of Amdo around Qinghai Lake and the Yellow River, Khams communities along the upper Yangtze and Mekong, and oasis settlements such as Shigatse and Gyantse. Tibetic languages belong to the Tibeto-Burman branch of Sino-Tibetan. Tibetan folk culture is shaped by high-altitude pastoralism, barley cultivation, Tibetan Buddhism, Bön, regional trade, and distinctive adaptations in clothing, architecture, food, medicine, painting, music, and ritual life.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

***Chuba*** — The *chuba* is a long, wrap-style robe worn by men and women, made in wool, sheepskin, cotton, or silk according to region and status. Its broad sleeves, adjustable waist, and ability to fold over the body suit cold plateau conditions; rural and nomadic versions differ markedly from urban Lhasa dress.

***Shamthab*** — The *shamthab* is a wrapped, skirt-like lower robe worn in Tibetan Buddhist monastic dress.

***Pangden*** — The *pangden* is the striped woollen or wool-and-cotton apron worn by married Tibetan women, particularly in Central Tibetan dress. Its horizontal stripes distinguish it from ordinary skirts and encode marital and regional identity.

***Perak*** — The *perak* is an elaborate women’s headdress, often a long, rigid-backed form covered with turquoise, coral, amber, silver, and other stones. It is especially associated with Ladakh and western Tibetan cultural zones, where the arrangement and scale of stones communicate wealth and family position.

***Thangka*** — A *thangka* is a portable religious painting on cotton or linen, mounted in silk brocade and often rolled around a dowel. It uses mineral and organic pigments to depict Buddhas, bodhisattvas, mandalas, lineage teachers, protective deities, or astrological diagrams. Its composition follows iconometric rules rather than purely individual artistic invention.

***Khata*** — The *khata* is a ceremonial offering scarf, usually white or pale blue silk or synthetic fabric, presented during greetings, initiations, weddings, funerals, and visits to religious teachers. It is not a garment but a textile of blessing, respect, and exchange.

Additional indexed objects include *tsakli*—small devotional cards or initiation images—and the *dzi bead*, a polished agate bead marked with bands or “eyes,” often worn as a protective object. Tibetan carpets, brocades, and felt work vary between Central Tibetan, Amdo, Khams, Ladakhi, and Himalayan workshops.

**Motif vocabulary.** lotus (*padma*), endless knot (*dpal be’u*), victory banner, conch shell, wheel of dharma, paired fish, treasure vase, cloud scrolls, snow-lion, and the eight auspicious symbols.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday Tibetan clothing is adapted to cold, wind, altitude, and pastoral work. Men and women wear the *chuba*, often gathered at the waist so that the upper portion forms a warm pouch. Men commonly combine a shorter robe with trousers, boots, and a felt or fur-lined hat. Nomadic groups use heavy sheepskin robes, while urban residents in Lhasa and Shigatse often wear modern clothing except during festivals and ceremonies. Women may wear a blouse beneath the robe, a striped *pangden*, a broad sash, and regional jewelry. Married women’s dress is distinguished by the apron and, in some areas, by elaborate head ornaments. Footwear includes leather or felt boots, sometimes with brightly colored uppers. Ceremonial dress adds silk robes, brocaded sleeves, coral and turquoise ornaments, and headdresses such as the *perak*. Monastic robes differ by school and rank, with red, maroon, yellow, or saffron textiles used in ritual contexts.

### Architecture

Traditional Tibetan houses and monasteries are generally built on elevated, sunny sites, often facing south. Materials include stone, earth, timber, adobe, and whitewashed masonry. Flat roofs conserve heat and provide usable terraces, while inward-sloping walls help stabilize buildings in earthquake-prone mountain terrain. In rural settlements, thick walls and small upper windows reduce heat loss; in agricultural valleys, houses may include courtyards, grain stores, livestock areas, and roof terraces. Nomadic families traditionally use black yak-hair tents, while pastoral communities also employ felt-covered portable structures. The most prominent urban-monastic form is the fortified religious complex, exemplified by the Potala Palace in Lhasa, with the White Palace for administration and the Red Palace for chapels, shrines, scriptures, and ritual assembly. Smaller *gompas* and village temples combine painted interiors, carved timber, murals, prayer wheels, and rows of *mani* stones.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Tibetan household objects include wooden bowls, butter churns, grain measures, saddle equipment, woven bags, yak-hair ropes, and carved storage chests. Copper, brass, silver, and gilt-metal vessels are used for tea, butter lamps, offerings, and ritual water. Religious metalwork includes vajras, bells, offering bowls, incense burners, ritual knives, and decorated *ga'u* shrine boxes. Prayer wheels are made in wood, metal, or stone and contain written mantras or sacred texts. Portable shrines, carved printing blocks, and painted furniture often combine geometric borders with lotus, cloud, or auspicious-symbol designs.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Tibetan jewelry uses turquoise, coral, amber, dzi beads, silver, gold, and repoussé metalwork. Turquoise inlay and coral necklaces are especially prominent in women’s dress, while large pendants and amulets can indicate family wealth, regional affiliation, or religious protection. The *ga'u* is a small metal shrine box worn or carried as a protective reliquary, often containing an image, text, or sacred substance. Hair may be braided and decorated with beads, ribbons, and silver ornaments. The *perak* and related headdresses can cover much of the hair and extend down the back. Jewelry is also used in ritual exchange, marriage negotiations, pilgrimage, and the presentation of offerings.

## Music & performance

Tibetan music includes monastic chant, courtly and devotional repertories, pastoral songs, work songs, wedding music, and popular dance-song genres. The long-necked lute *dranyen* is one of the best-known instruments, with a waisted wooden body and several strings. Other instruments include the two-stringed *piwang* fiddle, the *lingbu* end-blown flute, the *gyaling* double-reed instrument, long trumpets such as the *dungchen*, the conch trumpet *dungkar*, cymbals, frame drums, and large ritual drums.

Monastic ensembles use deep sustained tones, layered chanting, cymbals, drums, and horns during tantric ceremonies, processions, initiations, and protector-deity rituals. Secular song traditions include the Central Tibetan *lu* and *shé*, performed at social gatherings, festivals, banquets, and courtly occasions. Amdo and Khams have distinct regional singing and dance styles associated with pastoral life, heroic narratives, and local celebrations. The *dranyen* also accompanies staged and popular repertories outside monasteries. Music is therefore divided between highly regulated liturgical sound and participatory song in which dancers, singers, and instrumentalists respond to one another.

## Dance & theatre

Tibetan opera, or *lhamo*, combines sung narrative, dance, dialogue, percussion, and brightly colored masks. Its repertory draws on Buddhist stories, legendary rulers, saints, and Tibetan history. Performances traditionally begin with purification and blessing rites, followed by a narrator’s introduction, dramatic scenes, songs, and a concluding blessing. The tradition is associated with the fourteenth-century lama Thang Tong Gyalpo and is performed during festivals including Shoton. UNESCO inscribed Tibetan opera on its Representative List in 2009.

Monastic masked dances, commonly known as *cham*, are ritual performances rather than secular theatre. Dancers wearing masks of deities, protectors, animals, skeletons, or demons enact the defeat of obstacles and the restoration of religious order in monastery courtyards. Movement, costume, music, and ritual timing are controlled by liturgical tradition. Secular circle and line dances accompany festivals, weddings, and regional celebrations, with dancers holding hands or moving in coordinated formations while singers perform verses.

## Festivals & rituals

The Tibetan year is marked by **Losar**, the New Year festival, usually falling in February or March according to the lunisolar calendar. Families clean houses, prepare special foods, make offerings, visit monasteries, and exchange greetings. Religious observances may include butter sculptures, incense, prayer flags, and renewed household shrines. **Shoton**, the “yogurt festival,” is associated with the unveiling of large thangka paintings, opera performances, picnics, and public celebrations, especially around Lhasa.

Pilgrimage is central to Tibetan ritual life. Pilgrims walk clockwise around temples, stupas, sacred mountains, lakes, and *mani* walls, turning prayer wheels and reciting *Om mani padme hum*. Prostration journeys and the placement of prayer flags mark sacred landscapes. Bön practitioners use distinctive ritual orientations and mantras, including *Om matri muye sale du*.

Lum medicinal bathing, part of Tibetan Sowa Rigpa practice, uses hot springs, herbal water, or steam to support health and prevent illness. UNESCO recognizes the practice as intangible cultural heritage. Birth rituals may involve naming, blessings, and protective amulets. Weddings combine family negotiation, exchange of textiles and jewelry, ceremonial scarves, songs, and communal feasting. Funerary practice varies by region and religious community; rites are directed by religious specialists and may include recitations, offerings, and the ritual treatment of the body according to local Buddhist custom.

## Foodways

Barley is the principal cultivated grain of the Tibetan Plateau. Roasted barley flour, *tsampa*, is mixed with tea, butter, and sometimes sugar or salt to form a dense staple food that requires little fuel and travels well. Butter tea, *po cha*, is churned with tea, salt, and yak butter and is served throughout the day in homes, tents, monasteries, and social visits. Sweet milk tea is also common in urban settings.

Momo, steamed dumplings filled with meat or vegetables, are widely eaten, as are hand-pulled or cut noodle dishes made from barley or wheat. Meat commonly includes yak, mutton, or goat, prepared fresh, dried, or stewed with potatoes and spices. Yak yogurt, butter, and cheese are important pastoral foods; well-made yogurt has also functioned as a prestige food. Mustard seed is cultivated and used as a seasoning and oil source. Religious ethics influence food choices, but Tibetan diets vary by region, livelihood, and access to meat. Monastic meals may emphasize vegetarian dishes, while pastoral households depend heavily on dairy and meat. Ritual foods include butter sculptures, offering cakes, and *torma*—shaped barley-flour offerings used in Buddhist and Bön ceremonies.

## Oral tradition & literature

The **Gesar Epic** is a major heroic narrative of Tibetan oral literature and is also known across Mongolia and Central Asia. It recounts the deeds of King Gesar of Ling through long cycles of sung and recited performance. Specialist bards preserve episodes through memorization, formulaic language, music, and improvisational expansion. Other oral forms include proverbs, riddles, origin legends, religious biographies, pilgrimage stories, and folktales about animals, kings, monks, and tricksters.

Classical Tibetan literature includes Buddhist scriptures, philosophical works, medical texts, biographies of religious teachers, chronicles, and poetry. Secular writing includes works such as *The Dispute Between Tea and Chang* and *Khache Phalu’s Advice*. The Gesar tradition, monastery libraries, woodblock printing, archival projects, and recordings of regional performers support contemporary preservation. Modern Tibetan writers also use Tibetan, Chinese, and English to address exile, urbanization, memory, religion, and changing pastoral life.

## Language & religion

Tibetic languages form a cluster within the Tibeto-Burman branch of Sino-Tibetan. Central Tibetan includes Ü-Tsang varieties such as Lhasa Tibetan; Khams and Amdo Tibetan are major regional forms. Ladakhi, Dzongkha, Sikkimese, Sherpa, and related languages are closely connected but are generally treated as distinct languages. Classical Tibetan is a major literary language, especially for Buddhist philosophy, ritual, medicine, and history. The Tibetan script was developed in the imperial period and remains central to religious and literary transmission.

Most Tibetans practice Tibetan Buddhism, including traditions associated with Nyingma, Kagyu, Sakya, and Gelug institutions. Indigenous Bön remains an important religious tradition and shares ritual vocabulary with Buddhism while maintaining its own liturgies and orientations. Mani stones, prayer flags, prayer wheels, circumambulation, pilgrimage, lamas, protective amulets, and household offerings connect formal religion with everyday folk practice. Smaller Tibetan Muslim and Christian communities also exist.
