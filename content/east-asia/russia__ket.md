## Overview

The Ket people are a Yeniseian-speaking Indigenous people of Siberia who live primarily in the Turukhansky district of Krasnoyarsk Krai, Russia, along the eastern middle stretch and middle and lower basin of the Yenisei River. They are descendants of fishing and hunter tribes of the Yenisei taiga; earlier groups also practiced reindeer breeding in northern areas. Kets were incorporated into the Russian state in the 17th century and later experienced deportation, collectivization, Russian-language education, and severe language loss. The 2010 census recorded 1,220 Kets in Russia, while the 2021 census recorded 1,088. Their importance for folk culture lies in their mythology, shamanic traditions, oral literature, distinctive language, and the material culture associated with household spirit images.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name shamanic skeleton symbolism, the loon, the bear, the eagle, the swan, the deer, the beaver, the burbot, the moon, the sun, the Big Dipper, and Orion.

### Clothing & dress

 They describe spirit images as an animal shoulder bone wrapped in a scrap of cloth simulating clothing. In Ket mythology, Alel is represented as a wooden figurine of a small man dressed in animal skins. These descriptions concern household and protective objects rather than documented human clothing.

### Architecture

 They state that Kets live in small villages along riversides and are no longer nomadic. The sources also report that Kets regard spirit images as household deities that sleep in the daytime and protect them at night.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do describe wooden protective figures and spirit images. Alel is a wooden figurine of a small man dressed in animal skins, while other spirit images are described as animal shoulder bones wrapped in cloth simulating clothing. These objects are associated with protection, household well-being, illness, and evil spirits.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 They describe the use of animal skins in the representation of the protective figure Alel, but do not present this as personal adornment.

## Music & performance

Ket musical culture is represented in the sources chiefly by shamanic songs and the work of Alexander Kotusov. Kotusov was a Ket folk singer, composer, writer of songs in the Ket language, poet, and a notable Ket figure who died in 2019. In Ket mythology, the words of the earlier great shaman Purtos are referenced in shamanic songs. The sources also identify shamanic ritual as an important performance context: shamans practiced healing and other local ritualistic spiritual activities, and different kinds of shamans were associated with sacral rites, curing, power, and animals such as the deer and bear. They state that Ket mythology includes legends, fairy tales, information connected with shamanic rituals, and other religious ideas. The sources also report that the practice of shamanism has largely been abandoned and that monotheism displaced shamanic ideas and practices.

## Dance & theatre

 They describe legends, fairy tales, shamanic rituals, oral traditions, and mythological narratives.

## Festivals & rituals

 The ritual material that the sources do cover concerns shamanic practice, healing, sacral rites, household protection, and mythological beliefs.

Ket shamans were identified as practitioners of healing and other local ritualistic spiritual practices. Different types of shamans were associated with sacral rites and curing, and with animals including deer and bear. Shamanism in Ket mythology is connected with the reincarnation of a shaman and with the inhabitation of spirits. A shaman becomes a shaman after spirits enter him, while a person becomes an object of the sixth mythological level after the main soul, uļwej, enters him and accompanies him throughout life.

The sources describe spirit images as household deities. These images were believed to sleep during the day and protect people at night. Alel was a protective assistant in the struggle against troubles, illnesses, and evil spirits. The sources state that contemporary shamanism has largely been abandoned and that monotheism displaced earlier shamanic ideas and practices.

## Foodways

 They state that Ket hunter and fishing communities lived in the Yenisei taiga and that, by the 19th century, Kets could no longer sustain themselves without food assistance from the Russian state. They also report that some people died of famine and diseases introduced from Europe. No specific food names or food-preparation traditions are provided.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ket oral culture includes legends, fairy tales, mythology, shamanic songs, and other religious ideas. Ket mythology is organized as a hierarchy of six levels, linked by stories in which characters from different levels participate. The highest level contains Eś, the upper god associated with the sky and natural phenomena such as thunder, lightning, clouds, and the northern lights. The second level includes Khosedem, the mistress of the underworld, and Tomam, associated with heat, the south, and the sun.

The third level contains epic heroes and cultural figures. Doh and Albe are described as the first cultural heroes of the Kets, while Balne, Belegene, and Torete are three brothers whose acts of bravery are especially well attested in Ket tales. Purtos and Vasily Lesovkin are named as great shamans of the past. The sources describe folklore concerning conflicts between mythological figures, the creation of land, and a waterfowl that dives under water to bring up earth. In the Ket variant, the creator and the earth-fetching being cooperate rather than compete.

Alexander Kotusov wrote songs and poetry in Ket. The sources also document preservation efforts through linguistic research, Ket teaching in some primary schools, special books for grades second through fourth, and the translation of *A Bit Lost* into Ket in 2013.

## Language & religion

Ket is the sole surviving language of the Yeniseian language family and is spoken along the middle Yenisei basin by the Ket people. It has been linked to the Na-Dené languages of North America, although this proposed relationship is not accepted by all linguists. Ket has Southern, Central, and Northern dialects, also described as Upper Inbatz and Lower Inbatz. Southern Ket is spoken in Kellog, Central Ket in Surgutikha, and Northern Ket in Maduyka. The sources state that Kellog, Russia, is the only place where Ket is still taught in schools.

The language is severely endangered. The number of ethnic Kets who were native speakers fell from 1,225 in 1926 to 537 in 1989. The latest reports cited in the sources give fewer than 30 native speakers, while another local source gives around 10 to 20. A Latin-based alphabet was developed in the 1930s, and a Cyrillic-based alphabet was created in the 1980s.

Ket religious traditions include mythology, shamanism, spirit images, healing, and oral ritual knowledge. The mythology contains six levels, from Eś and other higher beings through spirits, shamans, and human beings. The sources state that monotheism has displaced shamanic ideas and that many traditional practices have been abandoned.
