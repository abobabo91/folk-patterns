## Overview

The Khakas, also called Khakass, are a Turkic indigenous people of Siberia who live in the Republic of Khakassia, Russia. They speak the Khakas language, which belongs to the South Siberian subgroup of Turkic languages. The Khakas are associated with southern Siberian cultures including the Andronovo culture, Samoyedic peoples, the Tagar culture, and the Yenisei Kyrgyz culture. Their historical names include Kyrgyz, Tatar, *Tadar*, Khongorai, *Khoorai*, Abaka Tatars, Minusinsk Tatars, Abakan Tatars, and Yenisei Turks. Khakas traditional livelihoods included nomadic herding, agriculture, hunting, and fishing; the Beltir people also specialized in handicraft. Their folk culture is represented in the sources especially through language, shamanism, throat-singing, and named musical instruments.

## Music & performance

Khakass music has similarities to the music of Tuva, a neighboring region in Central Asia. Throat-singing is practiced in Khakassia and is often accompanied by a two-stringed instrument called the *khomys*. A bridge zither called *jadagan* is also used.

The city of Abakan has hosted the International Khakass Folk Music Competition since 1995. Musicians named in the source include Anna Burnakova, Sergey Charkov, Slava Kuchenov, Aleksandr Samozhikov, and Yevgeniy Ulugbashev. Folk music ensembles include Aylanys, Sabjilar, Ulgher, and Khyrkhaas.

The sources also identify Khakas music with the broader cultural setting of Khakassia, a region in Russia.

## Festivals & rituals

The religious information in the sources concerns Orthodox Christianity and traditional shamanism rather than a calendar of festivals. During the nineteenth century, many Khakas accepted Russian ways of life and most were converted to Russian Orthodox Christianity. Shamanism nevertheless remained common, and many Christians practiced shamanism together with Christianity.

The sources name traditional religious organizations including the Society of Traditional Religion of Khakas Shamanism *Ah-Chayan*, the Traditional religion of the Khakas society *Izykh*, and the Traditional religion society *Khan Tigir*.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Khakas language has a documented connection with oral literature. Matthias Castrén, who travelled through northern and Central Asia between 1845 and 1849, wrote a treatise on the Koybal dialect and recorded an epic. Wilhelm Radloff travelled extensively through the southern Siberian region between 1859 and 1870. His research included a four-volume dictionary and a ten-volume series of Turkic texts. The second volume contained Khakas materials with a German translation. The ninth volume, prepared by Radloff’s student Katanov, who was a Sagay, contained further Khakas materials with a Russian translation.

The Khakas literary language developed after the Russian Revolution of 1917 and was based on the central dialects Sagay and Kacha. The Beltir dialect was largely assimilated by Sagay, and the Koybal dialect by Kacha. In 2012, an Enduring Voices expedition documented the Xyzyl language from the Republic of Khakassia. It is officially considered a dialect of Khakas, while its speakers regard Xyzyl as a separate language.

## Language & religion

Khakas, also known as Xakas, is a Turkic language spoken mainly in the southwestern Siberian Republic of Khakassia, in Russia. The Khakas number 61,000, of whom 29,000 speak the Khakas language, and most Khakas speakers are bilingual in Russian. The language is part of the South Siberian subgroup of Turkic languages, together with Shor, Chulym, Tuvan, Tofa, and Northern Altai.

The language has dialects named Sagay, Kacha, Koybal, Beltir, and Kyzyl. The source states that these names represent former administrative units rather than tribal or linguistic groups. The Khakas language also had a dialect named Kamas Turk, which the UNESCO Atlas of the World’s Languages in Danger reported as extinct since the 1950s.

A Cyrillic alphabet was devised in 1924, replaced by a Latin alphabet in 1929, and replaced by a new Cyrillic alphabet in 1939. The Khakas literary language developed after the Russian Revolution of 1917.

At present, the Khakas predominantly are Orthodox Christians belonging to the Russian Orthodox Church. Traditional shamanism, identified also with Tengrism, remains part of the religious landscape. The sources name the religious movements *Ah-Chayan*, *Izykh*, and *Khan Tigir*.
