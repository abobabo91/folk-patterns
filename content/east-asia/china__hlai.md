## Overview

The Hlai, also known as Li or Lizu, are a Kra–Dai-speaking ethnic group officially recognized as one of the 56 ethnic groups of the People’s Republic of China. The vast majority live on Hainan Island, off the southern coast of China, where they are the largest minority ethnic group. They are divided into five branches: Qi (Gei), Ha, Run (Zwn), Sai (Tai, Jiamao), and Meifu (Moifau). The Hlai languages are spoken in the mountains of central and south-central Hainan. The Hlai are documented through distinctive customs, tattooing practices, animist traditions, oral legends, agricultural life, and the traditional wind instrument *kǒuxiāo*.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

 They do record that Hlai women tattooed their arms and backs after reaching a certain age, and that some Hlai people historically shaved their heads in connection with incorporation into regular citizenship.

### Architecture

 Historical accounts state that some Hlai villages had no walls and that people took refuge in mountains and narrow passes.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They describe bamboo objects used in transactions: the Hlai split bamboo into three parts and carved the price and amount of “hill” land onto it, with the two parties and a mediator each keeping one part as proof. The sources also describe grain tied up and hung over stoves, where smoke cured it for later consumption.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The principal documented form of Hlai body adornment is tattooing. Hlai women had a custom of tattooing their arms and backs after reaching a certain age.

## Music & performance

The Hlai play a traditional wind instrument called *kǒuxiāo* (口箫).

## Festivals & rituals

The Hlai were primarily animists, and their religious traditions included the worship of animals and beings connected with ancestral and protective powers. The most prominent animal in Hlai legends was the snake. One origin account states that Leigong, the God of Thunder, laid a snake on Li Mountain; from its egg hatched a woman named Limu, literally “mother of the Li.” She lived from wild fruits and nested in trees, and her descendants became the Hlai. Another account says that a woman arrived on a ship, married a dog, and gave birth to the Hlai.

The *Oxen’s Festival* was celebrated every year on the eighth day of the third lunar month. On that day oxen were not killed or worked. They stayed at home and were fed liquor believed to protect them and guarantee plentiful harvests. The ox was also represented in each house by a stone called the “soul of the ox.”

The *najiaxila* bird of legend was worshiped as a protector god because it cared for an ancestor woman of the Hlai. Dragons and cats were also worshiped because they were considered ancestors.

## Foodways

The sources describe Hlai cultivation, grain storage, rice lending, and the use of wild fruits. Historical accounts state that the Hlai plowed and planted, paid taxes, and performed corvée service. They did not store grain in ordinary granaries; after harvest, they tied up the grain and hung it over their stoves, using stove smoke to cure it before eating it after a number of days.

The sources state that the Hlai had no markets and no sellers of grain. Poor people who lacked food borrowed rice from those who had it, without calculating interest; repayment was not regarded as especially weighty. Later accounts describe lenders who lent in spring and expected repayment in fall with high interest. In the origin traditions, Limu lived from wild fruits.

## Oral tradition & literature

Hlai oral tradition includes origin legends concerning the descent of the people from a woman and an animal. Clans were said to have originated through the marriage of a woman and an animal, with the snake especially prominent. The stories of Limu, the “mother of the Li,” connect the Hlai to Leigong, Li Mountain, wild fruits, trees, and the descendants who became the Hlai. Other animal figures in Hlai tradition include the dog, ox, *najiaxila* bird, dragon, and cat.

 A Qing dynasty report dated 1756 stated that the Hlai did not have a writing system.

## Language & religion

The Hlai languages form a primary branch of the Kra–Dai language family and are spoken in the mountains of central and south-central Hainan in China. There are some 750,000 Hlai speakers. A quarter of Hlai speakers are monolingual. The languages include Cun, whose speakers are ethnically distinct. Jiāmào, spoken by the Sai subgroup, is a divergent Kra–Dai language with a Hlai superstratum and a non-Hlai substratum. Nadou is spoken in two villages in Dongfang, Hainan, and its speakers are officially classified by the Chinese government as ethnic Han Chinese.

None of the Hlai languages had a writing system until the 1950s, when the Latin script was adopted for Ha. The Hlai also speak or understand Hainanese and Standard Chinese. Hlai religion was primarily animist, with animal ancestors, protective beings, the “soul of the ox,” and the God of Thunder forming part of the documented religious landscape.
