## Overview

The Ulch people, also known as Olcha, Olchi or Ulchi, are an Indigenous people of the Russian Far East. They speak Ulch, a Tungusic language, and over 90% of Ulchis live in Ulchsky District of Khabarovsk Krai, Russia. According to the 2010 census, there were 2,765 Ulchs in Russia; the 2002 census recorded 2,913. The Ulch people descend from an autochthonous Paleolithic population of coastal Northeast Asia and are associated with the Amur region. Their traditional economy was centered on fishing, especially the capture and preservation of anadromous salmon, while hunting and the capture of sea animals also played important roles. Their material culture includes distinctive dwellings, clothing, fishing equipment and food-preservation practices.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The upper summer clothing of men and women consisted of kimono-cut cloth *capchum* robes, with the left hem fastened on the right side. Ornaments on men’s clothing were rare. Winter clothing was insulated with cotton wool. Fur coats were cut like robes and covered with cotton or silk fabric. Winter hats resembled hoods, with a top made from white dog kamuses and fox trim around the face. In severe frosts, devil’s-fur earmuffs were worn beneath the hat. Birch-bark hats were used in summer. Shoes were made from fish, deer and elk leather, known as *rovduga*, as well as seal and sea-lion leather. The sources do not distinguish ceremonial clothing from daily clothing or describe belts and other dress accessories.

### Architecture

The Ulchi led a sedentary life in small villages of two to five houses. Villages contained both winter and summer dwellings. The ancient winter dwelling, the *hagdu*, was a ground-frame structure made from pillars and logs, with a gable roof without a ceiling and an earthen or clay floor. It was heated by two *kanovye* hearths. In extreme cold, large metal braziers on three legs were also used. A characteristic feature was the “dog table,” or *uycheu*, a low platform where sled dogs were fed. Summer dwellings included quadrangular pole structures with gable roofs covered in bark, called *daura*, and pile *letniki*, called *gengga*. While fishing, the Ulchi built small cylindrical huts.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Everyday and economic objects included fishing gear, nets, rides, hook tackle and different types of stocks. Large metal braziers on three legs were used in severe cold. Sled-dog equipment was connected with the *uycheu*, a low feeding platform in the winter dwelling. Fish were preserved as *yukola*, made by drying fish in the wind and sun in the form of thin, long plates.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources state that ornaments on men’s clothing were rare.

## Festivals & rituals

The traditional religion of the Ulchi is expressed through animism and shamanism. A large role in Ulch social life is played by cults of family and ancestors, including ancestor worship.

## Foodways

Fishing was the main branch of the traditional economic complex. The most important food source was anadromous salmon, and the settlement pattern followed the right bank of the main channel of the Amur, along routes used by salmon returning to spawning grounds. Fish were caught both for daily consumption and for storage. The principal preservation method was making *yukola*: fish were dried in the wind and sun as thin, long plates. Hunting supplied Siberian rabbit, squirrel, otter, fox and sable, while sea-animal hunting included seals and sea lions.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that the Ulch language is endangered and that an ELAR archive concerns endangered Tungusic languages of Khabarovskij Kraj, including Ulcha. No contemporary literary revival or other preservation effort is described in the supplied material.

## Language & religion

Ulch, also called Olcha, is a Tungusic language spoken by the Ulch people in the Russian Far East. It belongs to the southern, or Amur, branch of the Tungusic languages. Along with the languages of the Nanai people and the Oroks, it contains relics of ancient pre-Tungusic Amuric vocabulary. The language is moribund, with only 150 speakers recorded in the 2010 census. The sources mention vowel length, a rare [f] in native words, and several consonant allophones. Traditional Ulch religion includes animism, shamanism and family and ancestor worship. It bears similarities to the religions of the Nivkh people and Ainu people.
