## Overview

The Khanty, known in older literature as Ostyaks, are an Ob-Ugric people of western Siberia, living in the Khanty–Mansi Autonomous Okrug — a region historically called Yugra — together with the Mansi. Their heartland is the Ob river basin: they call themselves *As Khoyat*, 'Obian people' or 'people from Ob'. In the 2021 census 31,467 people identified as Khanty, most of them in Tyumen Oblast (19,568 in Khanty-Mansi Autonomous Okrug, 9,985 in Yamalo-Nenets), with smaller numbers in Tomsk and Sverdlovsk Oblasts. Khanty belongs to the Ugric branch of the Uralic family, most closely related to Mansi and Hungarian; in the autonomous okrug Khanty and Mansi hold co-official status with Russian. In folk-culture terms they matter as a fishing, hunting and reindeer-herding people whose ceremonial life — above all the Bear Celebration — survived Christianization and Soviet collectivisation in recognisable form.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

What the sources do document, through the catalogued objects, is a consistent decorative repertoire built from **fur patchwork** and **applied cloth strips**: a man's coat assembled from many pieces of cream and mid-brown fur, its lower half banded with red, green and yellow patchwork, and narrow strips of red, dark blue and yellow tabby woollen cloth stitched to the front shoulders and the outer sleeves, applied most densely across the back of the shoulders. A pair of knee-length fur boots carries geometrically shaped patchwork on the mid-front and the lower sides, with the joins deliberately emphasised by added strips of red tabby wool.

Two further techniques recur. **Beadwork** appears as applied threaded glass beads worked in diamond patterning — on a woman's hair ornament or head-band of red tabby wool in white, red and green beads, and as a bead border on a domestic cloth. That embroidered cloth is the one piece with figural decoration recorded: alternating bands of blue and brown wool embroidery with stylized floral motifs either side of a horizontal line, zigzag patterning at one end in salmon pink, lilac and blue, and a bead band of white and green diamonds on all four edges, with beaded tassels at the corners. **Woven sashes** are represented by a woman's sash or girdle of hand-spun, hand-woven tabby woollen cloth with plied warp ends, dyed in the piece.

### Clothing & dress

Men's dress in the collection is a knee-length flared fur coat, lined with fur and finished with a fur collar, with mittens stitched directly to the sleeve ends and thin leather thongs as front fastenings — patchwork banding and cloth strips as described above. With it go knee-length fur boots whose top front is curved to cover the knee, on a brown sole, with short leather thongs inside the upper edge. A man's cap of seal skin is shaped like a coif: red napped tabby wool frames the face, bordered by a narrow strip of cream seal skin, the crown made of segments of pale fur, with an oval sealskin panel at the back of the head; it ties under the chin with a flat interlaced braid ending in cream, blue and green glass beads, and is trimmed with tassels made from recycled red and black woollen cloth. Also recorded are a leather belt with brass, a leather-and-bead shoe, and snow-spectacles or snow-goggles of metal, cloth, brass, beads, brass buttons and reindeer skin with the fur left on. Women's dress is represented chiefly by head and hair ornaments and by beaded necklaces, including holiday examples (below).

### Architecture

The sources describe a two-season settlement pattern rather than named building types. In winter the Khanty lived in stationary huts built of dirt and branches, in permanent villages. In spring they moved out to hunting and fishing grounds and put up temporary rectangular shelters of birch-bark and poles. The collection holds a **model tent** of fur, wood and skin, which is the only dwelling form represented as an object.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The everyday material recorded is overwhelmingly wood and birch bark. **Birch-bark vessels** include a fish basket with its own basketry-and-bark lid, a dish, a tumbler, and a bowl for eating fish; a wooden cup and a wooden spoon complete the tableware. Hunting and fishing gear dominates the rest: a bow trap for ermine, called *cherpak*, of wood; a fish trap called *morda*, of wood; further fishing tackle including a gauntlet and a trap. A man's knife combines ivory, white metal and wood. A smoking pipe has a hard-wood stem with a cylindrical mouthpiece and bowl of white metal, attached with inlaid metal pieces. Transport and child-rearing appear as models — dog reins with a model sledge of wood, cane and string, and a child's cradle of wood, cloth, straw, string and leather. A needle case and pin cushion is made of red and dark blue napped woollen patchwork, five blue squares set in a blue border, lined with brown warp-faced tabby wool, with pale blue beads and a notched wooden stick hanging from one corner.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Women's ornament is the best-documented adornment. There is a holiday **necklace with pendants** of beads and cloth, and a second necklace of beads, cloth, copper and string hung with a crucifix — a direct trace of Orthodox conversion in personal dress. Head ornaments and head-bands combine cloth, beads and metal: one is built on two joined strips of cream tabby linen faced with dark brown wool, with square silver-coloured metal plaques (each of four roundels) stitched in a vertical line down each strip, edged with couched black and white beads, and hung with larger blue and white beads finished in white and green beadwork, brass discs, crosses and coins. Another woman's hair ornament of red tabby wool carries white, red and green bead diamonds, with cotton-ribbon-wrapped hangings and a large beaten metal disc suspended on cream beads. Further recorded are a woman's beaded and metal hair ornament bearing an inscription, and an openwork metal disc or ring ornament with a linked rod loop ending in an openwork triangle.

## Music & performance

Two instrument types appear. The **"swan" harp**, of wood and string, is the stringed instrument recorded. The **shaman's tambourine** is represented three times over: one of wood and hide, one of wood, skin, brass and red cotton cloth fitted with bells, and a separate wood-and-hide beater. Song is best documented as part of the Bear Celebration, held after a successful bear hunt, where over 300 songs and performances take place across five or six days — the duration depending on the sex of the animal. The sources name three of its central songs: **Nukh Kiltatty Ar**, the Awakening Song; **Ily Vukhalty Ar**, the Coming Down From The Sky Song, which tells how the son of *Torum*, the sky god, was sent to rule the Earth, forgot his father's advice, lost his immortality, turned into a beast and was killed by hunters; and **Il Veltatty Ar**, the Lullaby.

## Festivals & rituals

Two calendar observances are named. The **Crow spring celebration** falls in April — now on 7 April, the same day as the Orthodox Annunciation. The **Bear Celebration** is not fixed to the calendar at all: it is held occasionally, after a bear has been successfully hunted, and runs five or six days with more than 300 songs and performances (see above). Ritual objects in the collection include a wooden **divination figure** — a cylindrical body with a roughly carved face, dressed in a full-length red cloak of napped tabby wool trimmed with yellow gold-coloured metal ribbon at hood, cuffs and hem, with cream, red and blue cotton ribbon hood ties — and the shaman's tambourines and beater. The historical Khanty shaman wore no special clothing except a cap.

Social organisation framed ritual life historically: in the 11th century Yugra denoted numerous tribes, each with its own centre and chief, and every tribe had two exogamic phratries, *mon't'* and *por*, whose members counted as blood relatives. Phratries were later replaced by clans, each led by a *knyazets* who negotiated with the Russian state and collected *yasaq* (tribute) from two *volosts*.

## Foodways

The sources establish the subsistence base — fishery, taiga hunting and reindeer herding, with gathering of major importance because the Khanty lived as trappers — and the eating equipment: a birch-bark bowl specifically for eating fish, birch-bark dishes and a tumbler, a wooden cup, a wooden spoon, and the fish traps *morda* and *cherpak* by which the food was taken.

## Oral tradition & literature

Khanty folklore comprises bear songs, fairy tales and other stories, and epic poetry, sharing themes with the mythical and heroic narratives of the Mansi. Written Khanty literature began in the first half of the 20th century; the first notable Khanty writer was **Grigori Lazarev** (1917–1979), best known for the novel *Sorneng tow*. Later Khanty authors include the writer and politician Yeremey Aypin (born 1948), the writer, journalist and professor Roman Rugin (1939–2016), the poets Mikul Shulgin (1940–2007) and Vladimir Voldin (1938–1971), and Maria Vagatova (1936–2026), the first Khanty poetess, also a storyteller, publicist, teacher, editor and journalist. Two Khanty-language newspapers carry the written language today: *Khanty yasang*, founded in 1957, and *Lukh avt*, founded in 2001; newspaper reporting and broadcasting are usually in the Kazym dialect.

## Language & religion

Khanty is a Ugric language of the Uralic family, closest to Mansi and Hungarian, and is really a set of largely mutually unintelligible dialect groups — Northern, Southern and Eastern — with individual dialects named after the rivers along which they are spoken. The western group includes Obdorian, Ob and Irtysh; the eastern includes Surgut and Vakh-Vasyugan. Southern Khanty has been extinct since the early 20th century. The 2020 census reported 13,900 speakers. Written Khanty was created after the October Revolution on a Latin basis in 1930 and switched to Cyrillic, with an added ⟨ң⟩ for /ŋ/, in 1937; literary work appears mainly in the Kazym, Shuryshkar and Middle Ob dialects. Most Khanty today are Orthodox Christians, mixed with traditional belief in shamans and reincarnation. Conversions after the Russian conquest of Siberia were largely superficial and economically motivated — despite destroyed idols, mass baptisms and children taken as hostages — so Native practice persisted inside Orthodoxy. Traditional cults are closely tied to nature.
