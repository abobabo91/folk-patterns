## Overview

The Nivkh are an indigenous people of Russia's Far East living on Sakhalin Island and along the Amur River. They traditionally lived as fishermen, hunters and dog breeders whose entire material world was built from fish skin, birch bark, wood and hide. Their population has declined sharply from 5,287 in 2002 to 3,842 in 2021, and their language is nearly extinct with fewer than 200 native speakers remaining. They are best known for the Bear Festival, an elaborate winter ceremony where a captured bear is treated as a sacred ancestor before being sacrificed.

## Material culture

Nivkh material culture was built almost entirely from natural local materials: fish skin, birch bark, wood, hide and fur, with some items made from imported Chinese and Manchurian metal and textiles.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Nivkh textiles relied on fish skin and embroidery rather than weaving, with decorative patterns coming mainly from traded Chinese silks.

- **Fish-skin garment** — Coats and jackets made from many pieces of salmon skin stitched together, often with decorative cut-outs and piping in dyed cotton cloth.
- **Embroidered women's robe** (*hukht*) — Light and multicoloured robe with intricate embroideries and ornaments sewn to sleeves, collar and hem.
- **Men's robe** (*skiy*) — Darker robe more sparingly ornamented on the sleeve and left lapel.
- **Birch-bark container** — Water buckets, boxes, vessels and tobacco boxes made of birch bark with wood and basketry bindings.
- **Summer hat** (*hiv hak*) — Conical hat made of birch bark with wood, vegetable fibre and rope.

### Clothing & dress

Nivkh robes fastened on the left side and used fish, seal, and fur; women wore embroidered light robes hung with ornaments while men wore darker, shorter robes with hunting accessories.

- **Winter fur coat** — Made from skins of fish, seal and sable and furs of otter, lynx, fox and dog.
- **Hunting kilt** (*kosk*) — Loose kilt worn by men when hunting or travelling by dog sledge.
- **Waterproof boots** — Made from fish, seal or deerskin constructed to be very watertight.
- **Winter fur hat** (*hak*) — Worn in winter, often with the animal's tail and ears left on the back and crown.
- **Headman's padded cap** — Built from several stitched fabrics including Chinese tapestry-woven sections with cloud patterns, silk damask, and printed cotton, falling below the chin with a wavy hemline.

### Architecture

Nivkh dwellings were small round dugouts in winter and semi-sedentary coastal summer sites, with villages of three to four households sharing resources from two to eight clans.

- **Round dugout dwelling** (*ryv or to*) — About 7.5 metres across, shored with wooden poles and covered with packed dirt and grass, with a central fireplace and smoke hole for light and smoke.
- **Gabled winter house** (*chad ryv*) — One-room structure with a gable roof heated by a Chinese furnace, modelled on Manchurian and Chinese dwellings of the Amur.
- **Work shed** — Stood beside the house and held sledges, skis, boats and dogs.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Nivkh everyday objects were overwhelmingly wood, bark, hide and imported iron rather than ceramic, with hunting and fishing gear dominating the material assemblage.

- **Seal spear** — Four-section spear made of wood, iron and leather for hunting sea mammals.
- **Salmon club** — Cut from a wood root and used on both salmon and seals.
- **Wooden bow** — Made of wood, bark, string, root, vegetable fibre and leather.
- **Snow-shoes** — Made of pine and leather for winter travel.
- **Netting needle** — Wooden tool used for making and repairing nets for fishing.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Nivkh adornment consisted mainly of coins, bells and beads sewn onto clothing rather than worn jewellery, with shamans wearing elaborate coats and metal belts.

- **Garment ornament** — Coins, bells and beads of wood, glass or metal of Manchurian and Chinese origin, sewn to the sleeves, collar and hem of women's robes.
- **Brass-mounted belt** — Set with horn, bone and ivory fittings, the closest item in the catalogue to worn metalwork.
- **Shaman's coat and belt** — Elaborate garments with metal belts worn by shamans who used talismans to prevent sickness.
- **Carved charm** — Wooden figures including human heads with fish tails, jointed human figures, seal or fish figures, and four-footed carvings with anthropomorphic heads.

## Music & performance

The Nivkh have extensive folklore and songs but no named instruments, song genres or performance occasions are recorded in the available sources.

## Dance & theatre

Dance and theatre practices are not covered in the available sources.

## Festivals & rituals

The Bear Festival, held between January and February, is the major ceremonial occasion, where a sacred bear is sacrificed and eaten to honour the dead and maintain clan ties.

- **Bear Festival** — A bear captured and raised in a corral for several years by women was dressed in ceremonial costume, offered a banquet, then sacrificed and eaten in an elaborate ceremony presided over by shamans, with the spirit thought to return to the mountain gods and repay the Nivkh with abundant forests.
- **Feeding ritual** — An open flame was fed a tobacco leaf, spices or vodka, and the sea was fed an object of value so that the sea god would protect travellers.
- **Marriage arrangement** — Controlled by the clan through a three-clan alliance group, with cross-cousin marriage as the older custom and bride price shared among the clan.

## Foodways

Nivkh subsistence depended on salmon fishing at winter spawning grounds inland and sea-mammal hunting on the coast, supplemented by forest gathering and dog breeding.

- **Winter salmon fishing** — Men caught salmon at the winter spawning grounds along rivers like the Tym' and Poronai, especially rich in fish.
- **Sea-mammal hunting** — Seals and other sea mammals were taken on the coast using specialized tools like seal spears.
- **Forest gathering** — Women gathered food in the forests while men hunted game in the surrounding grounds in late fall.

## Oral tradition & literature

Nivkh folklore is extensive with songs and myths about creation, heroes, spirits and beasts, recorded in folk tales, improvisational myths, and short epic tales.

- **Folk tale** (*tylgund*) — Individual tales first recorded by Bronisław Piłsudski and later collected by scholars including E. A. Kreinovich and V. Sangi.
- **Improvisational myth** (*nastund*) — A form of improvised mythical narrative.
- **Short epic tale** (*ker-aind*) — Brief epic narratives of mythological or legendary content.
- **Nivkh newspaper** (*Nivkh Dif*) — Monthly newspaper founded in 1990 and published in the West-Sakhalin dialect from Nekrasovka, carrying the written language today.

## Language & religion

Nivkh is a language isolate with four dialect clusters, agglutinating and SOV in structure, with native speakers declining from 1,079 in 1989 to 198 in 2010; religion is animist centred on shamanism and veneration of the ancestors and nature spirits.

- **Language isolate** (*Nivhgu dif*) — A language with eight to ten cases, two tenses, and consonant alternation at morpheme boundaries, written in a modified Cyrillic alphabet.
- **Deity** (*yz or yzng*) — Loosely defined gods over mountains, rivers, seas and sky in the animist pantheon.
- **Sakhalin as sacred being** — Imagined as a giant beast lying on its belly with trees as its hair and earthquakes as its trembling when angered.
- **Shamanic healer** (*ch'am*) — Shamans diagnosed and cured disease with plant and animal remedies and warded off death-causing spirits.
- **Sacred fire** — Venerated as the symbol of clan unity and a deity of the ancestors.
