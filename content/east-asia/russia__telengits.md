## Overview

The Telengits, also called Telengut, are a Turkic ethnic group native to Central Asia and recognized as one of the minor Indigenous peoples of Russia. They mainly live in the Kosh-Agachsky District of the Altai Republic and are an ethnic subgroup of the Altaians. Telengits live along the Chuya River in the western Altai and call themselves Chui-kizhi, meaning Chuya people. In the 2002 census, 2,398 people were recorded as Telengits, although 8,000 or 9,000 people might have identified themselves as Altaians instead. Their folk culture is documented through cattle herding, felt yurts, distinctive clothing, shamanism, Eastern Orthodox Christianity, and a strong connection to the land considered their sacred homeland.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Traditional Telengit dress was similar for men and women. It consisted of long-sleeved shirts, breeches, and robes. Double-breasted sheepskin coats, fur hats, and high boots were commonly worn. Married women additionally wore a sleeveless jacket over their coats. Modern clothing is not documented in the supplied sources.

### Architecture

Traditional Telengit dwellings included felt yurts. Modern Telengits live in wooden homes but commonly inhabit yurts during the summer months.

## Music & performance

 They state that shamanism continues to exert a strong influence in Telengit culture, but provide no description of the music, instruments, or ceremonies associated with it.

## Festivals & rituals

 They record that most Telengits practice shamanism and Eastern Orthodox Christianity, while smaller numbers practice Burkhanism. Shamanism continues to exert a strong influence in Telengit culture, and Orthodoxy has seen a recent revival among the Telengits.

The sources describe a strong connection to the land. The Altaians and Telengits feel connected to the land where they live and are supposed to worship their special homeland, which is considered sacred. Telengits say that when an Altaian leaves the Altai, he or she will become ill and die because of physical separation from the land. After living on the land, people become one with it, making separation from the homeland severe. No birth, coming-of-age, wedding, funeral, or other life-cycle rites are described.

## Foodways

Historically, most Telengits were nomadic or semi-nomadic cattle herders. They commonly raised sheep, cattle, goats, and horses. During Dzungar domination, the Telengits had to pay a fur tribute, or yasak, to the Dzungars.

## Oral tradition & literature

 Chinese chroniclers might have mentioned the Telengits as Middle Chinese: *tâ-lâm-kât*, 多覽葛, with Standard Mandarin Chinese: duōlǎngé. The supplied material does not describe a contemporary Telengit literary revival or preservation project.

## Language & religion

The Telengits are a Turkic ethnic group. The sources mention Altay, the Altay language, and the self-designation Chui-kizhi. Most Telengits practice shamanism and Eastern Orthodox Christianity; smaller numbers practice Burkhanism. Shamanism remains influential in Telengit culture, while Orthodoxy has experienced a recent revival.

Historically, the Telengits lived in a political and cultural landscape connected with the Dzungar Khanate and Russia. In the 14th century they created their own principality, known as the Ulus or Orda. Russian documents of the 16th–18th century called it the Teleut land, or Телеутская Землица, and modern historians call it the Telengit Ulus.
