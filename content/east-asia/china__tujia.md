## Overview

The Tujia are an officially recognized ethnic group in China whose population was recorded as 9,587,732 in the 2020 Census. They live mainly in the Wuling Mountains, across Hunan, Hubei and Guizhou Provinces and Chongqing Municipality. Their hereditary homelands include Xiangxi Tujia and Miao Autonomous Prefecture, Enshi Tujia and Miao Autonomous Prefecture, and several autonomous counties and districts. The Tujia language is a Sino-Tibetan language, usually treated as unclassified within that family, although most Tujia use varieties of Chinese, mainly Southwestern Mandarin. Tujia folk culture is represented in the sources by the Baishou dance, the hand-waving sacrifice, singing and song composing, richly patterned brocade known as *xilankapu*, handmade *ciba* cakes, and distinct lowland, mountain and plateau lifeways.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Xilankapu** — *Xilankapu* is a richly patterned Tujia brocade. The source describes it as a product that formerly figured regularly in tribute payments to the Chinese court and identifies exquisite Tujia brocade with the lowland Tujia tradition.

## Music & performance

The Tujia are described as renowned for singing and song composing. They state that lowland Tujia gather around the fire during their spring festival to sing folk songs and eat grilled *ciba*. The hand-waving sacrifice combines religion, sacrifice, dance, drama and other cultural forms, making performance part of a large-scale sacrificial ceremony. The sources call it the Tujia ethnic group’s most sacred traditional festival.

The available material also connects music and performance with the Tujia’s regional lifeways. Mountain Tujia historically maintained a strong hunting tradition, while their position between highlands and lowlands supported caravan transport and trade.

## Dance & theatre

**Baishou dance** is described as a 500-year-old collective dance using 70 ritual gestures to represent war, farming, hunting, courtship and other aspects of traditional life. The source identifies it by the Chinese name 摆手舞 and treats it as one of the best-known Tujia cultural forms.

**Hand-waving sacrifice** is a large-scale sacrificial ceremony integrating religion, sacrifice, dance, drama and other cultural forms. It is described as having been passed down from ancient times and as the most sacred traditional festival of the Tujia ethnic group.

## Festivals & rituals

The Tujia spring festival is associated in the sources with handmade glutinous rice cakes called *ciba cake*. People gather around the fire to sing folk songs and eat grilled *ciba*.

**Hand-waving sacrifice** is described as a large-scale sacrificial ceremony passed down from ancient times. It integrates religion, sacrifice, dance, drama and other cultural forms and is identified as the Tujia ethnic group’s most sacred traditional festival.

## Foodways

Lowland Tujia mainly inhabit hilly river-valley areas below 500 meters in elevation. Their staple food is rice, supplemented with wheat and other grains. They raise domestic fowl and livestock, and fishing is well developed. The sources associate the lowland Tujia with the greatest material abundance among the three Tujia subgroups.

Mountain Tujia live mainly in alpine gorge regions between 500 and 2,500 meters. Their diet consists of mixed grains, including rice and the highland crops wheat, barley and buckwheat. They raise livestock and poultry and sometimes graze cattle and sheep. Plateau Tujia mainly inhabit plateau regions above 1,000 meters, where staple foods are primarily highland crops such as buckwheat. They herd sheep, and some raise small numbers of chickens, pigs and other animals.

For the spring festival, Tujia prepare handmade glutinous rice cakes called *ciba cake*. They gather around the fire to sing folk songs and eat grilled *ciba*.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that the Tujia are renowned for singing and song composing and mention folk songs performed around the fire during the spring festival.

The sources describe ongoing language-preservation work. Tujia language enthusiasts work in Hunan and Hubei to preserve the language; two Tujia language instruction books have been published, and work continues on a Tujia dictionary. Tujia language scholar Chu Yongming works with children at Baifusi Ethnic Minorities School in Baifusi Town, Laifeng County, Hubei, to promote language use.

## Language & religion

Tujia is a Sino-Tibetan language and is usually considered an isolate or left unclassified within that family because of influence and borrowing from neighboring languages. It has Northern and Southern variants that are mutually unintelligible. The Northern variant has the vast majority of speakers, while the Southern variant is spoken in three villages of Tanxi Township in Luxi County. As of 2005, Northern Tujia was estimated to have 70,000 speakers, including about 100 monolingual speakers, and Southern Tujia 1,500 speakers.

The Tujia language has tonal contrasts, and its verbs distinguish active and passive voices. Its pronouns distinguish singular and plural numbers and basic and possessive cases. The sources state that most Tujia use varieties of Chinese, mainly Southwestern Mandarin; a few speak Hmongic languages, and nearly all fluent Tujia speakers are bilingual in some dialect of Chinese. The sources describe the hand-waving sacrifice as combining religion and sacrifice with dance and drama.
