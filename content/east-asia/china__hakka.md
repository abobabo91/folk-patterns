## Overview

The Hakka are a Han Chinese sub-ethnic group whose principal settlements and ancestral homes are dispersed across southern China. Their presence is especially prominent in Guangdong, Fujian, and Jiangxi, with Hakka communities also recorded in other provinces, Taiwan, Southeast Asia, and overseas Chinese communities. In mainland China, Hakka populations are found in 13 of the 27 provinces and autonomous regions. Western Fujian is described as having 3 million Hakka people scattered across villages in 10 counties. The Hakka speak a group of Chinese varieties closely related to Gan and have developed numerous regional dialects. Their folk-culture significance in these sources rests on their architecture, cuisine, music, language, migration history, and distinctive social practices.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Shanku* is a coat and trousers worn by both Hakka men and women. The preferred colours are usually black and blue.

### Clothing & dress

Hakka clothing described in the sources includes *shanku*, a coat and trousers worn by both men and women. Hakka people in Taiwan usually wear coats, trousers, and skirts. The sources state that the preferred colours of shanku are usually black and blue. *Liangmao* is identified as a traditional Hakka hat. Historically, Hakka women did not bind their feet when footbinding was commonplace in other parts of China. The sources connect this practice with women’s greater social freedom, travel, and participation in work.

### Architecture

The Hakka built several types of *tulou* and peasant fortified villages in mountainous rural areas of western Fujian, adjacent southern Jiangxi, and northern Guangdong. Tulou are earthen structures, generally round or square, designed as combined fortresses and multi-apartment complexes. They typically have one entrance and no windows at ground level. The first floor contains a well and livestock, the second is used for food storage, and the third and higher floors contain living spaces. Their construction was intended to withstand attacks by bandits and marauders.

A representative sample of Fujian tulou, consisting of 10 buildings or building groups, was inscribed in 2008 as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. In northern eastern Guangdong, especially Xingning and Meixian, another architectural form is the *weilongwu*, or Wrapped Dragon Village. Hakka architecture is contrasted in the sources with the courtyard houses known as *siheyuan*.

## Music & performance

Hakka hill songs are traditionally used by hillside farmers in parts of Taiwan and China, especially for entertainment in farming fields and courting practices. They have strong, resonating melodies and voices that echo around hills and can be heard for up to a mile. Participants may use them to communicate love songs or news.

*Hakkapop* is a genre of Hakka pop music made primarily in China, Taiwan, Malaysia, and Indonesia. The sources also describe Hakka Eight Sounds, an instrumental ensemble commonly performing at communal events. Its name refers to the eight materials in the classification system of the *Zhou Rites*: metal, stone, earth, leather, silk, wood, gourd, and bamboo. Taiwanese Eight Sounds ensembles tend to contain four musicians and four core elements: the *suona*, *erxian*, *panghu*, and percussion. The suona leads the ensemble and carries the main melody; the erxian and panghu are string fiddles forming the harmony, while drums, gongs, and clappers control rhythm and tempo.

Eight Sounds ensembles typically perform at Hakka weddings, religious and temple rituals, and funerals. Before the 1970s they played for joyous occasions, but westernization and new technology contributed to a decline in performances. Playing at funerals temporarily increased their use, while the performer base continued to age and shrink.

## Dance & theatre

They mention opera performance as one activity associated with the Liudui Sports Game festival in Taiwan.

## Festivals & rituals

The Liudui Sports Game is an annual event held by the local government in Taiwan and connected with Hakka culture. The event originated from early martial practice among Liudui immigrants and is described as the biggest and most unique Hakka contest in southern Taiwan, as well as the only Hakka sports event in the country. Before the competitions, the festival includes passing the Liudui torch, an opera performance, and a bicycling tour. After the competition, singers and bands hold an evening concert. The sources state that the first Liudui Sports Game was held in 1948 in Zhutian, Pingtung, and that the event returned in 1966 after an eighteen-year interruption during the martial-law period.

Hakka religious practice is described as including ancestor veneration and the worship of dragon deities. Eight Sounds ensembles perform at weddings, religious and temple rituals, and funerals.

## Foodways

Hakka cuisine is found primarily in Guangdong, Fujian, and Jiangxi, as well as parts of Taiwan and countries with significant overseas Hakka communities. It is associated with preserved meats, preserved vegetables, tofu, and stewed, braised, and roast dishes. The cuisine places emphasis on texture and on cooking meat thoroughly without hardening it. Preserved vegetables, including *mei cai*, are used in dishes such as steamed minced pork with preserved vegetables and braised pork with salted vegetables.

*Lei cha* is a traditional Southern Chinese tea-based beverage or rice gruel associated with Hakka cuisine. Its ingredients include green tea, basil, sawtooth coriander, mugwort, and an herb called “Fu Yip Sum.” The sources describe it as laborious to prepare and usually eaten with side dishes. Another account describes lei cha as a vegetable and bean dish whose preparation involves eight combinations and assistance from other family members.

*Yong tau foo* consists primarily of tofu filled with ground meat or fish paste. It may be eaten dry with sauce or served in soup. *Suanpanzi* consists mainly of yam or tapioca beaten into the shape of abacus beads and served with minced pork or chicken and light seasoning. Other foods mentioned include steamed buns, pork belly bao, tea fruit, tofu with preservatives, salt-baked chicken, and poon choi.

## Oral tradition & literature

Hakka hill songs function as entertainment, courtship, and communication among hillside farmers. Their participants use them for love songs and news, while the resonating vocal style allows sound to carry across hills. The sources also refer to the book *The Hakka Chinese: Their Origin, Folk Songs And Nursery Rhymes* by Chung Yoon-Ngan. They do record modern broadcasting and media in Hakka Chinese, including the Voice of Hakka, Hakka television, radio programmes, and automatic broadcasting added to the Xuexi Qiangguo platform in 2023.

## Language & religion

Hakka is a group of Chinese varieties spoken by the Hakka people in southern China, Taiwan, parts of Southeast Asia, and overseas Chinese communities. It is most closely related to Gan and is sometimes classified as a variety of Gan. Hakka varieties are spoken in Guangdong, Guangxi, Hainan, Fujian, Sichuan, Hunan, Jiangxi, Guizhou, Taiwan, Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, and Indonesia. Hakka is not mutually intelligible with Yue, Wu, Min, Mandarin, or other branches of Chinese, and some Hakka varieties are not mutually intelligible with one another.

Hakka is typically written with Chinese characters. Some varieties, including Taiwanese Hakka, are also written in the Latin script or *Pha̍k-fa-sṳ*. In Taiwan, Hakka is designated as one of the national languages. The Meixian dialect, also known as Moiyen, has been treated as the standard dialect by the government of mainland China.

Hakka religious practices are largely similar to those of other Han Chinese. Ancestor veneration is the primary form of religious expression, while worship of dragon deities is identified as a distinctively Hakka practice.
