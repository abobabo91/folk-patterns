## Overview

Pashtuns — also called Pakhtuns, and Pathans in India and the wider subcontinent — live primarily in southern and eastern Afghanistan and northwestern Pakistan, across the belt south of the Amu river and west of the Indus. Pashtun-majority cities include Kandahar, Jalalabad, Ghazni, Khost, Lashkar Gah, Peshawar, Quetta, Mardan, Kohat, Bannu, Mingora and Dera Ismail Khan, while Karachi holds the world's largest urban Pashtun community, larger than those of Kabul or Peshawar. Estimates put the group at 60–70 million people in between 350–400 tribes plus further sub-tribes — the world's largest tribal society — with about 15 million in Afghanistan, where they are the largest ethnic group at 37–47% of the population; in Pakistan they are the second-largest at around 15.4%. Most speak Pashto, an Eastern Iranian language. In this collection they are represented by dress: one densely ornamented woman's garment and sets of turned-up-toed leather shoes for women, men and children.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Bead-, shell- and coin-worked woman's dress** — A woman's dress in the atlas carries silk embroidery, glass beads, buttons, cowrie shells and coins, among them *gul-i peron* discs. It is built from a rust-coloured printed velvet skirt and green floral-printed cotton sleeves, the sleeves embroidered in pink, mustard, red and grey, their cuff edges set with metal stamped disks and blue glass beads. The bodice is the display field: multi-coloured glass beads, pearly buttons, cowrie shells, separately embroidered coloured panels, beaded tassels and bells, together with four different Afghan coins dated between 1968 and 1981, which date the garment's assembly rather than its pattern repertoire. A shallow pocket sits on the right-hand side.

**Patterned leather footwear** — The shoes in the collection are pattern-bearing as much as the dress: pale blue to turquoise leather uppers worked with floral ornament, sewn with yellow cotton thread together with metal thread, over red insoles and green or turquoise turned-up pointed soles.

A country-level UNESCO inscription on *sericulture*, silk production, lists Afghanistan among its states; the inscription is not specific to Pashtuns, but silk thread is the embroidery medium of the dress described above.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name only one: floral patterning, printed on the dress sleeves and worked onto the leather of every shoe in the group.

### Clothing & dress

The documented garments divide clearly by wearer. Women's shoes and men's shoes share the same construction — turquoise leather uppers with floral ornament, red insoles, green turned-up pointed soles, cotton and metal thread stitching — but the men's pairs have the leather extended upwards at the heel, and both show heavy wear to the sides. The child's pair repeats the uppers and red insole with a turquoise turned-up pointed sole and a leather strip rising from the inside of the heel; unlike the adults' shoes it is unworn. For women, the heavily ornamented velvet-and-cotton dress above represents ceremonial-weight dress rather than daily clothing, its bodice functioning as a mounted surface for beads, buttons, cowrie shells, *gul-i peron* discs and current coin. A nineteenth- or early twentieth-century albumen photograph in the collection shows the everyday end of the range among a village group: women in burkas, men bare-chested seated and standing, and children.

### Architecture

The only built form the sources record is in that same photograph: a small group of domed grass huts, photographed with the villagers, their sheep, a calf and a single very large dog, and described in the catalogue as closely resembling nomad groups still known today. Beyond this, a tenth-century notice in the Ḥudūd al-ʿĀlam of 982 locates Afghans at Saul, "a pleasant village on a mountain," probably near Gardez — a settlement site without a description of its houses.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The only metalwork they document is applied ornament on clothing: metal thread stitched through the leather shoe uppers, metal stamped disks along the dress cuffs, and the bells and Afghan coins of 1968–1981 fixed to the dress bodice.

### Jewelry & body adornment

No jewelry as such appears in these records. What they do show is that the materials of adornment — glass beads, pearly buttons, cowrie shells, metal stamped disks, *gul-i peron* discs, beaded tassels and struck coin — were sewn directly onto the garment rather than worn separately, concentrated on the bodice and sleeve cuffs of the woman's dress. The sources used say nothing about tattooing, henna or hair practices.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used carry no description of a Pashtun festival calendar or of birth, marriage and funeral rites. Two UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage elements list Afghanistan among their countries of origin and so form part of the national context: **Nowruz** (RL/02097), the traditional folk spring festival observed across several countries in Asia and the Middle East, and **sericulture** (RL/01890). Neither inscription is framed around Pashtuns specifically, and nothing in the material used here describes how either is kept by Pashtun communities. The one ritual-adjacent detail in the record is religious plurality rather than practice: the Ḥudūd al-ʿĀlam reports a king in Ninhar — present-day Nangarhar — who had Muslim, Afghan and Hindu wives.

## Foodways

Their only mentions of food production are incidental and ancient or colonial: fourth-century Bactrian letters addressed to and about "the clan of the Afghans" instruct a correspondent to look after the farming and hand over grain, and nineteenth-century records describe Rohillas recruited from British India as indentured labourers to the sugarcane fields of Trinidad, Surinam, Guyana and Fiji.

## Oral tradition & literature

The best-documented Pashtun oral tradition in these sources is genealogical. Tribal tradition holds that when the twelve tribes of Israel were dispersed, the tribe of Joseph and other Hebrew tribes settled in the Afghanistan region — hence the tribal name *Yusufzai*, "son of Joseph" in Pashto. The *Encyclopaedia of Islam* traces the written form of this descent claim to Nimat Allah al-Harawi, who compiled a history for Khan-e-Jehan Lodhi under the Mughal emperor Jahangir in the seventeenth century; the thirteenth-century *Tabaqat-i Nasiri* records the settlement of immigrant Bani Israel in the Ghor region at the end of the eighth century CE, and the *Maghzan-e-Afghani* supplies the version in which the exiles were permitted to travel east. Versions are repeated by Ibn Battuta in the fourteenth century and Ferishta in the sixteenth. The tradition is contested: Zaman Stanizai calls it a fabrication popularised in fourteenth-century India, and genetic work reports haplogroup R1a1a*-M198 in over 50% of Pathans against 15.3% among Ashkenazi Jews. A second strand of tradition attaches to the legendary progenitor Qais Abdur Rashid, said to have borne the title *batan* — a possible origin of "Pathan" from Arabic *batan*, "rudder." Mohan Lal wrote in 1846 that no one, even among the oldest and cleverest of the tribe, could give satisfactory information on Pashtun origins, and Mountstuart Elphinstone compared the Jewish-descent account to Roman, British and Irish origin legends.

## Language & religion

Pashto belongs to the Eastern Iranian branch of the Iranian family. It shares features with Munji — the closest surviving relative of the extinct Bactrian — and with Sogdian, Khwarezmian, Shughni, Sanglechi and Khotanese Saka; Classical Pashto carries strong Sanskrit influence. Georg Morgenstierne described Pashto as a Saka dialect, and Pashto shares vocabulary cognates with Ossetian that other Eastern Iranian languages lack, which Johnny Cheung explains through an undocumented Saka dialect spoken near reconstructed Old Pashto north of the Oxus. Not all Pashtuns speak it: those of the Hazara region speak Hindko, *Wanetsi* is spoken mainly within the Tareen tribe, Dari serves as a second language in Afghanistan and Urdu in Pakistan, and most Pashtun-descended communities in India now speak Hindi, Urdu or other regional languages. On religion the sources record Islam through historical notices — Afghans and Khiljis enlisting in Ghaznavid armies, tribes claiming Sayyid or Arab descent, the mixed-faith household at Ninhar — without describing sect or folk practice.
