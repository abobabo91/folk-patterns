## Overview

The Pamiris (also Badakhshanis) are an Eastern Iranian people of the high Pamir Mountains, concentrated in Tajikistan's Gorno-Badakhshan Autonomous Oblast (GBAO) around the town of Khorog and along the Panj, Ghund, Shakhdara, Bartang, and Wakhan river valleys. Communities also live in Afghan Badakhshan, Pakistan's Gilgit-Baltistan and Chitral, and China's Taxkorgan Tajik Autonomous County. Population estimates for the group as a whole range around 300,000. They speak a cluster of mutually unintelligible Eastern Iranian Pamir languages — most prominently the Shughni-Rushani group (Shughni, Rushani, Khufi, Bartangi, Roshorvi, Sarikoli), Wakhi, Ishkashimi, and Yazghulami — and are almost entirely Nizari Isma'ili Shi'a, a religious identity that shapes ritual, house form, poetry, and social life across the region.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Pamiri jurab** (*jūrāb*) — Thick, brightly patterned knitted woolen socks reaching mid-calf, made throughout Shughnan, Rushan, and Wakhan from hand-spun sheep wool dyed in reds, oranges, blues, and greens. Geometric bands stack from ankle to cuff, and the socks are worn inside soft leather boots in winter and given as wedding gifts.

**Wakhi textile** — In the upper Wakhan corridor, women weave narrow warp-faced bands and coarser twill lengths on ground looms from yak, sheep, and goat wool. The bands (used as belts, tent-ties, and cradle straps) carry hooked-lozenge and comb motifs unique to Wakhi weavers and distinct from lowland Tajik ikat.

**Pamiri chid textiles** — The *chid*, the Pamiri house, is dressed with layered flat-woven and pile rugs (*palas*, *gilem*) on the raised sleeping platforms, alongside embroidered cushion covers. Textiles are not incidental furnishing but a coded surface: colours and placements correspond to the five pillars and the Panj-tan.

**Embroidered *taqi* / *toqi*** — Small round or four-panel skullcaps worked in tight silk cross-stitch and chain-stitch on cotton or velvet grounds, embroidered by women for men, children, and brides. Pamiri caps favour dense floral rosettes and stepped diamond fields, often on a black or wine ground, and differ from the crisp black-and-white geometry of the Uzbek Chust *do'ppi*.

**Pamiri house pillar textiles** — The five wooden pillars of the chid (see Architecture) are individually named and, at weddings and Chirāgh-Rāwshan gatherings, wrapped or draped with embroidered cloths and hung with strings of dried mulberries and salt.

**Sūzanī-style wall embroideries** — Large embroidered hangings worked in silk floss on cotton, smaller and more restrained than Bukharan *sūzanī*, used to line the walls of the *chid* on festive days.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Panj-tan* five-part rosette (the five holy figures), *shamsa* (sun disk), *māh* (moon crescent), stepped *kūhī* (mountain) triangles, *bodom* (almond/paisley), *shākh-i buz* (ibex horn) hooks, *chashm* (eye) protective dot, *gul-i lola* (tulip), running *āb* (water) zigzag, and stacked *mehrāb* niche arches echoing the pillar shrine.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday women's dress is a long straight-cut cotton tunic (*kūrta*) over wide-legged trousers (*ezor* or *pojoma*) gathered at the ankle, with an embroidered yoke and cuffs. Married women bind their hair in two long plaits and cover the head with a large square shawl (*rūmol*) knotted at the nape; older women in Wakhan and Bartang wear a distinctive band-like forehead ornament under the shawl. Brides wear a red or crimson *kūrta* with heavy silver ornaments and a face-framing beaded fringe. Men wear a long shirt over trousers, a quilted or striped chapan-style coat (*chapan*), a woven or leather belt, and the embroidered *taqi* skullcap; in winter, a sheepskin coat (*pūstīn*) and felt-lined boots. Handmade woolen *jurab* socks are worn indoors year-round, including by guests, who remove their boots on entering the *chid*.

### Architecture

The **Pamiri house** — *chid* in Shughni, *khun* in Wakhi — is the most distinctive built form of the region: a single square or rectangular room of stone-and-timber walls, flat earthen roof, and a stepped skylight (*chorkhona* or *rūz*) of four concentric rotated wooden squares in the ceiling that admits light and vents smoke. The interior is organised around **five load-bearing wooden pillars**, each with a name and a theological identification: **Muhammad**, **Ali**, **Fatima**, **Hasan**, and **Husayn** — the Panj-tan of Din-i Panj-tani Isma'ilism. Additional named beams and platforms structure daily life: raised sleeping and sitting daises (*nekhs*) run along the sides, guests are seated according to precedence, and the hearth and the pillar of Fatima anchor women's activities. Ceilings are ornamented with painted rosettes and carved lattices, and the stepped skylight is read simultaneously as the four elements (earth, water, air, fire) and the Zoroastrian survival within an Isma'ili house. Villages cluster on alluvial fans above the rivers; fields are terraced and irrigated by long stone-lined channels of the kind attributed in local chronicles to Shah Wanji Khan's 18th-century Khust canal.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pamiri households historically made coarse low-fired earthenware for storage and cooking rather than the refined blue-and-white of lowland centres. More characteristic are wooden objects turned and carved from apricot, mulberry, and walnut: long-handled ladles, kneading troughs, low tea-tables, cradles (*gahvora*), and the carved capitals and brackets of the chid pillars themselves. Copper and tinned-copper vessels — teapots, ewers (*oftoba*), basins (*tosht*), and long-spouted samovars — are traded up from Bukhara, Samarkand, and Kabul but are treated as heirloom goods. The **Pamiri rubab**, a short-necked lute with a skin-covered belly, is both a musical instrument and a piece of household woodwork, hung on the wall between uses.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Women wear silver jewellery whose weight and quantity mark marital status: temple pendants and hair ornaments hung from the plaits, coin-and-bead necklaces layered across the chest of a bride, chased silver bracelets, and rings on multiple fingers. Amulet cases (*tumor*) — triangular or cylindrical silver containers holding Qur'anic verses or protective texts — are worn on cords around the neck or pinned to a child's cap against the evil eye. Carnelian, turquoise, and coral are the preferred stones, carnelian associated in Shi'a tradition with Imam Ali. Kohl (*surma*) is applied to the eyes of women and infants, and henna is used on the hands of brides and on the beards of older men returning from pilgrimage.

## Music & performance

Pamiri musical life is inseparable from Isma'ili devotion. The signature genre is **madoh** (*maddoh*, "praise") — sung poetry in Persian and Pamiri languages performed at gatherings and mourning assemblies, drawing on the verses of Nasir Khusraw, Rumi, Attar, Sanai, and Hafez. A madoh session opens slowly with recited quatrains and builds through increasing tempo and vocal intensity, often lasting several hours and culminating in ecstatic climax. The core instruments are the **Pamiri rubab** (a six-stringed short-necked lute with a goatskin belly, distinct from the Afghan rubab), the long-necked lute **tanbur**, the bowed **ghijak**, the transverse flute **nay**, and the frame drum **daf**, whose entry marks the shift into the faster sections. The **falak** genre — melancholic solo songs of longing and fate performed in the mountain valleys of GBAO and Kulob — is closely associated with Pamiri and Gharmi singers and is often accompanied by ghijak alone. Ritual song accompanies the **Chirāgh-Rāwshan** ("luminous lamp") ceremony, an Isma'ili rite performed after a death in which the *Chirāgh-Nāma* attributed to Nasir Khusraw is recited over a lit wick, believed by tradition to have been revealed to Muhammad by the angel Jibra'il at the death of his son Abd Allah. Pamiri music is stylistically distinct from the courtly **Shashmaqam** of Bukhara and Samarkand, though educated musicians in Khorog know both repertoires.

## Dance & theatre

The characteristic Pamiri dance is the **rapo** (or *raqs-i Pomiri*), performed by men and women — usually separately — at weddings, Nowruz, and Chirāgh-Rāwshan gatherings. It is a slow, floating solo or duet built on fluid arm and hand gestures and small circling steps, drawn from Sufi devotional movement; the wrists rotate and the hands trace arcs that dancers describe as flame-shapes. Faster, sharper male dances imitate the ibex and the eagle. Pamiri villages do not have a masked or puppet theatre tradition of their own; dramatic expression is carried instead by sung poetry, madoh performance, and the recitation of religious narrative in the *chid*.

## Festivals & rituals

The turning point of the year is **Nowruz** (locally **Navruz** or **Shogun-Bahor** in some Pamiri villages), the Iranian New Year at the vernal equinox, celebrated across Badakhshan with house-cleaning, sprouted-wheat *bāj* dishes, the rekindling of the hearth, communal meals, rapo dancing, and visits to relatives. Zoroastrian survivals are unusually visible: the sun and moon are acknowledged in ritual, and elements of *mehrparastī* (sun and moon veneration) persist in folk practice. **Chirāgh-Rāwshan**, the luminous-lamp rite, is performed on the third night after a death and again at the fortieth day and one-year anniversary, and functions as the central mourning and communal ritual. Life-cycle rites include the naming ceremony and cradle-laying (*gahvora-bandon*) for infants, first-haircut rites, circumcision feasts for boys, and multi-day weddings (*tūy*) in which the bride is escorted between houses and seated beneath decorated pillars. Pilgrimage to local shrines (*mazor*, *ostūn*) — often marked by ibex horns, prayer flags, and stones — punctuates the seasonal calendar, and the graves of the five *qalandar* Iranian *da'is* (Shah Khamush, Shah Malang, Shah Kashan, Shah Qambar Aftab, Shah Isam al-Din) remain active pilgrimage sites in Shughnan and Wakhan.

## Foodways

Pamiri cooking is shaped by high altitude, short growing seasons, and the products of mixed mountain farming: wheat, barley, millet, mulberries, apricots, walnuts, and the milk of yak, cow, sheep, and goat. The staple bread is a thick round flatbread (*kulcha*, *fatir*) baked on the hearth stone or in a *tanūr* clay oven. Signature Pamiri dishes include **osh-i Pomiri** or **bāj**, a thick porridge of wheat, peas, and mutton cooked slowly and served at Nowruz and funerals; **shirchoy**, salted milk-tea thickened with butter and crumbled bread eaten for breakfast; **noshkhūrak**, a ceremonial dish of hot melted butter, milk, and crumbled fatir served to a woman after childbirth and to honoured guests; and **kashk**, dried curd reconstituted into sauces and soups. Dried mulberries (*tūt*) are pounded into a sweet flour (*pikht*) and pressed into travel cakes — the classic high-valley winter food. Meat is preserved by drying and salting for winter, and *plov* (rice with mutton and carrots) marks weddings and major feasts, though rice is a lowland import and less central than in Uzbek cooking. As Nizari Isma'ilis, Pamiris observe halal but do not fast at Ramadan in the Sunni manner; alcohol (grape wine and mulberry spirit) is discreetly present in some households.

## Oral tradition & literature

Pamiri oral literature is built around sung Persian classical poetry — Nasir Khusraw above all, followed by Rumi, Attar, Sanai, and Hafez — memorised and performed in madoh sessions. Nasir Khusraw's *Safar-nāma* and philosophical treatises are read as sacred texts, and hagiographic cycles about him, about the five Iranian *da'i* brothers, and about Shah Wanji Khan of Shughnan circulate as prose narrative and pilgrimage tale. Local chronicles such as the *Ta'rikh-i Shughnan* and the *Ta'rikh-i Badakhshan* (with Fadl Ali-Beg Surkh-Afsar's appendix) preserve genealogies of the Shughnan pirs and mirs. Pamiri-language literature is a modern project: an alphabet for Shughni was drafted in the 1930s and the first Shughni adult reader was published in 1931 by Nadir Shambezoda (1908–1991), whose poetry collection was destroyed in the Great Purge and whose language was again suppressed when the Ferdowsi State Public Library in Dushanbe held a book-destruction campaign in 1972. Since the late 1980s a Pamiri-language revival has produced new poetry, newspapers, and scholarly editions, though state pressure in Tajikistan on Pamiri linguistic and cultural institutions intensified in the 2020s.

## Language & religion

The Pamiri languages are Eastern Iranian, forming a distinct sub-branch (or, per *Encyclopædia Iranica*, sharing a North-Eastern Iranian branch with Pashto). Shughni functions as a lingua franca among Pamiris; Tajik Persian (*forsi*) is the language of literature, religion, and inter-ethnic communication, historically written in Perso-Arabic script and now in Cyrillic in Tajikistan. Almost all Pamiris are Nizari Isma'ili Shi'a, followers of the Aga Khan, and articulate their faith locally as **Din-i Panj-tani** — the religion of the five (Muhammad, Ali, Fatima, Hasan, Husayn), with Chār-yārī used as a label for Sunnis. The Sarikolis of China and some Wakhi communities in Pakistan share this Isma'ili affiliation; a minority of Wakhis in Afghanistan are Sunni. Zoroastrian and Manichaean substrata survive in the architecture of the chid, in Nowruz practice, in fire and light symbolism, and in *mehrparastī*-derived veneration of sun and moon.
