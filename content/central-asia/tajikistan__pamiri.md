## Overview

The Pamiris are mountain people living in the high Pamirs and nearby ranges, speaking their own Eastern Iranian languages alongside Tajik Persian. They are almost entirely Nizari Isma'ili Muslims, and this faith shapes their homes, rituals, poetry, and daily life. Their culture blends mountain farming, Islamic tradition, and traces of older Zoroastrian practice.

## Material culture

Pamiri material culture centers on the five-pillared house, woven and embroidered textiles with sacred geometry, wooden household objects, and silver jewelry that marks life stages.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Pamiris make bright patterned textiles for wearing, living, and ritual, with designs drawn from Islamic and folk symbolism.

- **Pamiri socks** (*jurab*) — Thick knitted woolen socks in red, orange, blue, and green with geometric bands, made throughout the region and given as wedding gifts.
- **Wakhi woven bands** — Narrow warp-faced bands woven on ground looms from yak, sheep, and goat wool with hooked-lozenge and comb motifs.
- **Embroidered skullcaps** (*taqi / toqi*) — Small round or four-panel caps worked in silk cross-stitch with dense floral rosettes and stepped diamonds on black or wine grounds.
- **Wall hangings** (*sūzanī-style*) — Large embroidered hangings in silk floss used to line the walls of the chid on festive days.
- **House pillar textiles** — Embroidered cloths wrapped around the five wooden pillars at weddings and Chirāgh-Rāwshan gatherings, hung with strings of dried mulberries and salt.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday wear is a long embroidered tunic over wide-legged trousers, with men wearing a quilted coat and embroidered cap, and brides dressed in red with heavy silver ornaments.

- **Women's tunic** (*kūrta*) — A long straight-cut cotton tunic worn over wide-legged trousers gathered at the ankle, with embroidered yoke and cuffs.
- **Men's coat** (*chapan*) — A quilted or striped outer coat worn over a long shirt and trousers.
- **Head shawl** (*rūmol*) — A large square shawl worn by married women, knotted at the nape to cover the hair and head.
- **Winter coat** (*pūstīn*) — A sheepskin coat worn in winter along with felt-lined boots.
- **Bride's dress** — A red or crimson tunic with heavy silver ornaments and a face-framing beaded fringe.

### Architecture

The Pamiri house is a single stone-and-timber room centered on five named pillars and lit by a stepped wooden skylight, organizing family life and expressing Isma'ili theology through structure.

- **Pamiri house** (*chid (Shughni), khun (Wakhi)*) — A square or rectangular room with flat earthen roof, stone-and-timber walls, and a stepped skylight of four rotated squares that admits light and vents smoke.
- **Five pillars** — Load-bearing wooden pillars named Muhammad, Ali, Fatima, Hasan, and Husayn, embodying the five holy figures of Isma'ili faith and anchoring daily family activities.
- **Stepped skylight** (*chorkhona / rūz*) — A ceiling opening of four concentric rotated wooden squares that channels light and smoke while representing the four elements and Zoroastrian cosmology.
- **Sleeping platforms** (*nekhs*) — Raised daises running along the sides of the house where family members sit and sleep according to social rank.
- **Stone irrigation channels** — Long stone-lined channels that water terraced fields throughout Pamiri villages, attributed to Shah Wanji Khan's 18th-century Khust canal.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pamiris favor carved wooden household objects and treat copper vessels from lowland cities as treasured heirlooms, with little tradition of fine ceramics.

- **Wooden kitchen objects** — Long-handled ladles, kneading troughs, low tea-tables, and cradles turned and carved from apricot, mulberry, and walnut wood.
- **Copper vessels** — Teapots, ewers, basins, and samovars traded from Bukhara, Samarkand, and Kabul but kept as heirloom goods.
- **Pamiri lute** (*rubab*) — A short-necked lute with skin-covered belly used as both a musical instrument and a piece of household woodwork hung on the wall between uses.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Little is recorded.

- **Silver ornaments** — Temple pendants, hair ornaments, coin-and-bead necklaces layered across the chest of brides, chased bracelets, and rings worn in quantity to show marital status.
- **Amulet case** (*tumor*) — A triangular or cylindrical silver container holding Qur'anic verses or protective texts, worn on a cord around the neck or pinned to a child's cap against the evil eye.
- **Eye makeup** (*surma*) — Kohl applied to the eyes of women and infants for adornment and believed protection.
- **Henna** — Applied to the hands of brides and to the beards of older men returning from pilgrimage.

## Music & performance

Pamiri musical life centers on madoh, sung poetry in Persian and Pamiri languages performed at gatherings and mourning rituals, accompanied by traditional instruments.

- **Madoh** (*maddoh*) — Sung poetry in Persian and Pamiri languages drawn from classical poets like Nasir Khusraw and Rumi, opening slowly with recitations and building to ecstatic climax over several hours.
- **Pamiri lute** (*rubab*) — A six-stringed short-necked lute with goatskin belly, distinct from the Afghan rubab and central to madoh performance.
- **Long-necked lute** (*tanbur*) — A stringed instrument accompanying madoh and other traditional songs.
- **Mountain lament song** (*falak*) — A melancholic solo song of longing and fate performed in GBAO and Kulob valleys, often accompanied by the bowed ghijak alone.
- **Mourning lamp ritual** (*Chirāgh-Rāwshan*) — An Isma'ili rite performed after death in which verses from the Chirāgh-Nāma are recited over a lit wick, with ritual song and music continuing for three nights.

## Dance & theatre

The Pamiri rapo is a slow, fluid solo or duet with graceful hand gestures drawn from Sufi movement, performed at weddings and celebrations by men and women usually separately.

- **Rapo dance** (*raqs-i Pomiri*) — A slow floating dance built on fluid arm and hand gestures and small circling steps, with wrists rotating to trace flame-shapes, performed at weddings and Chirāgh-Rāwshan gatherings.
- **Animal dances** — Faster, sharper male dances that imitate the movement and posture of the ibex and the eagle.

## Festivals & rituals

The Pamiri year turns on Nowruz at spring and is marked by Chirāgh-Rāwshan mourning rituals, life-cycle ceremonies, and pilgrimage to mountain shrines.

- **Nowruz celebration** (*Navruz / Shogun-Bahor*) — The Iranian New Year at the vernal equinox celebrated with house-cleaning, sprouted-wheat dishes, rekindling the hearth, communal meals, rapo dancing, and family visits.
- **Mourning lamp rite** (*Chirāgh-Rāwshan*) — Performed on the third night after death and again at forty days and one year, with recitation of sacred verses and madoh singing, serving as the central communal ritual.
- **Cradle-laying ceremony** (*gahvora-bandon*) — A naming and blessing ritual for infants that marks their formal entry into household and community.
- **Wedding** (*tūy*) — A multi-day ceremony in which the bride is escorted between houses and seated beneath decorated pillars while madoh and rapo dancing occur.
- **Shrine pilgrimage** (*mazor / ostūn*) — Visits to local mountain shrines marked by ibex horns, prayer flags, and stones, and to the graves of the five Iranian da'is (Saint teachers), at seasonal turning points.

## Foodways

Pamiri cooking adapts to high altitude and short seasons, built on wheat, barley, mountain fruits, and dairy, with signature dishes served at Nowruz, weddings, and rituals.

- **Wheat porridge** (*osh-i Pomiri / bāj*) — A thick porridge of wheat, peas, and mutton cooked slowly and served at Nowruz and funerals.
- **Salted milk-tea** (*shirchoy*) — A breakfast dish of tea thickened with butter and crumbled bread.
- **Ceremonial butter dish** (*noshkhūrak*) — Hot melted butter, milk, and crumbled flatbread served to women after childbirth and to honored guests.
- **Dried curd** (*kashk*) — Reconstituted into sauces and soups, a preserved dairy product for winter eating.
- **Mulberry travel cake** (*pikht*) — Dried mulberries pounded into sweet flour and pressed into cakes, the classic high-valley winter food.

## Oral tradition & literature

Pamiri literature is built on memorized Persian classical poetry, hagiography of saints and leaders, and local chronicles, with a modern Pamiri-language revival since the late 1980s.

- **Classical poetry** — Verses from Nasir Khusraw, Rumi, Attar, Sanai, and Hafez memorized and performed in madoh sessions and considered sacred texts.
- **Sufi philosopher** (*Nasir Khusraw*) — An 11th-century Isma'ili poet and traveler whose Safar-nāma and philosophical works are read as scripture and whose hagiography circulates as pilgrimage narrative.
- **Local chronicle** (*Ta'rikh-i Shughnan / Ta'rikh-i Badakhshan*) — Prose histories preserving genealogies of Shughnan rulers and regional leaders.
- **Pamiri-language alphabet** — An alphabet for Shughni drafted in the 1930s with the first reader published in 1931, suppressed under Soviet rule but revived since the late 1980s.

## Language & religion

Pamiris speak Eastern Iranian Pamiri languages with Shughni as a lingua franca, use Tajik Persian for literature and religion, and are nearly all Nizari Isma'ili Shi'a followers of the Aga Khan.

- **Shughni language** — The most widespread Pamiri language, serving as a lingua franca among Pamiri communities.
- **Pamiri languages** — A distinct Eastern Iranian sub-branch including Shughni, Rushani, Khufi, Bartangi, Roshorvi, Sarikoli, Wakhi, Ishkashimi, and Yazghulami.
- **Isma'ili faith** (*Din-i Panj-tani*) — The religion of the five holy figures (Muhammad, Ali, Fatima, Hasan, Husayn), followed by nearly all Pamiris as Nizari Isma'ilis under the Aga Khan.
- **Sunni label** (*Chār-yārī*) — The local Pamiri term for Sunni Islam, contrasting with Isma'ili practice.
