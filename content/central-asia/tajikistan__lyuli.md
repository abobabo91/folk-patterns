## Overview

The Lyuli, a term commonly used for the Mugat Ghorbati, are a branch of the Ghorbati people inhabiting Central Asia. They live in Tajikistan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Afghanistan, and Russia. The Mugat community is closed to entry by non-Mugat. They are also called Mugat, Mughat, Gurbet, Multoni, Gurbath, or Central Asian Gypsies; “Lyuli” and “Jughi” are considered pejorative by the Mugat. Their native language is primarily Tajik, while many also speak Uzbek and/or Russian, and some use Persian Kowli as an argot. They practice Sunni Islam. In Tajikistan, 2,234 Mugat lived there in 2010.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Historically, Mugat women worked as tailors for non-Mugat women, including making hairnets for veils. In Russia, Mugat were sometimes mistakenly identified as Tajik refugees or ethnic Uzbeks because of their traditional Central Asian robes.

## Music & performance

Historically, Mugat worked as wandering musical entertainers, fortune-tellers, peddlers, and beggars. Modern Mugat are settled and work in diverse occupations, including education, factories, and business.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources mention local traditions held by the Mugat, according to which their community already existed in the region by the time of Timur, described in the article as the 14th Century. They also state that some words in Mugat argots can be traced to texts associated with the Banu Sassan and the Bani al Ghuraba.

## Language & religion

The native language of the Mugat is primarily Tajik, and most are bilingual in Uzbek and/or Russian. Persian Kowli is used by some as an argot. The Mugat practice Sunni Islam, although some traces of pre-Islamic beliefs have continued to endure. Their community is organized through historic regions, clans or “Tupar,” and extended family groups known as “Avdol.”
