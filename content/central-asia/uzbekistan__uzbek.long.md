## Overview

The Uzbek (*Oʻzbeklar*) are a Turkic people of Central Asia, the largest Turkic ethnic group in the region and the titular majority of Uzbekistan, with sizeable communities in northern Afghanistan, Tajikistan, Kyrgyzstan, Kazakhstan, Turkmenistan, and Xinjiang. They are concentrated in the oasis cities and irrigated valleys of Transoxiana — Samarkand, Bukhara, Tashkent, the Fergana Valley, and the Khwarazm delta near the Aral Sea — with a total population of roughly 35–40 million. Their language belongs to the Karluk branch of Turkic, descended from Chagatai, and their culture emerged from the long convergence of settled Iranian-speaking Sogdians, Bactrians and Khwarazmians with successive waves of Turkic and Mongol nomads. In folk-culture terms the Uzbek matter as heirs of the Silk Road oasis civilisation: the ikat weavers of Margilan, the tile masters of Samarkand, and the shashmaqam court repertoire of Bukhara all sit within their tradition.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Ikat / *abrbandi*** — the resist-dyed warp technique for which the Fergana Valley (especially Margilan) is famous. Silk warp threads are tied off in bundles and dyed in sequence before weaving, producing the characteristic blurred "cloud" edges (*abr* meaning cloud in Persian).

**Adras** — a mixed-fibre ikat with a silk warp and cotton weft, heavier and matte compared to pure-silk versions, used for chapans and women's dresses.

**Atlas** and **khan-atlas** — all-silk ikat with a lustrous satin face; *khan-atlas* ("royal atlas") is the finest grade, historically reserved for elite women's robes and now the signature textile of Uzbek weddings.

**Suzani** — large embroidered cotton or silk panels (*suzan* = needle in Persian) worked in chain, couching and *basma* stitches, made in Bukhara, Nurata, Shahrisabz, Samarkand and Tashkent as part of a bride's dowry. Regional schools are distinguishable by palette and by whether the ground is left visible.

**Bukhara gold embroidery** (*zardozi*) — couched metallic thread on velvet, historically worked by male embroiderers of the emir's workshops for chapans, saddle-cloths and skullcaps.

**Duppi / tubeteika** — the square or round skullcap; the black-and-white *chust duppi* of the Fergana Valley, embroidered with four stylised chilli-pepper (*qalampir*) motifs, is the most recognisable national form.

**Chapan** — the quilted, striped open coat worn by men over the shirt, often in *bekasab* (warp-striped silk-cotton) or ikat, tied with a folded sash.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Qalampir* (chilli pepper, protective), *bodom* (almond, fertility), *anor* (pomegranate, abundance), *chinor bargi* (plane-tree leaf), *islimi* (scrolling vine), *toj* (crown), *oy* (moon disc), *tumor* (triangular amulet), *ilon izi* (snake track), *guli sadbarg* (hundred-petal rose).

### Clothing & dress

Men's everyday dress is a long cotton shirt (*koʻylak*), wide trousers (*ishton*), a striped *chapan* over the shoulders, a folded sash (*belbogʻ* or *qiyiqcha*), soft leather boots (*mahsi*) worn inside galoshes (*kavush*), and a *duppi* skullcap; a turban (*salla*) wound over the cap marks age, piety, or a ceremonial role. Women wear an unfitted straight-cut dress (*koʻylak*) — in Fergana most brilliantly in khan-atlas — over narrow trousers (*lozim*) gathered at the ankle, with a velvet or embroidered waistcoat (*nimcha*). The bridal ensemble adds a heavily embroidered robe and, in Khwarazm and Bukhara traditions, tall silver-plated head ornaments. Until the Soviet *hujum* campaign of the 1920s urban women in Bukhara and Samarkand went out in the horsehair-veiled *paranji*, a long grey-brown robe with vestigial sleeves fastened at the back. Children's caps and cradle covers carry the same amuletic embroidery as adult dress, on the principle that pattern itself repels the evil eye.

### Architecture

The traditional urban house is a single-storey mud-brick and timber structure built around one or two interior courtyards (*hovli*), presenting a nearly blank wall to the street. The reception room (*mehmonxona*) opens onto a summer veranda (*ayvon*) supported on carved wooden columns with muqarnas capitals, facing a pool and a fruit garden. Interior walls carry carved and painted plaster (*ganch*), and ceilings are coffered and polychromed. Monumental architecture — the madrasas, mosques and mausolea of Samarkand's Registan, Bukhara's Poi-Kalyan complex, and Shahrisabz — is faced in the Timurid manner with carved terracotta, cuerda-seca tile, and full mosaic *kashin* in cobalt, turquoise and manganese, structured by *girih* strapwork and *islimi* vine. *Muqarnas* honeycomb vaulting fills half-domes and portal niches. Rural Uzbeks of the steppe fringe retained the felt *oʻtov* (yurt) into the twentieth century, particularly among Kipchak-speaking groups in Khwarazm and the Qashqadaryo. The pastoral *koʻcha* — high-walled lanes with occasional inset benches (*supa*) — remains the signature of the old *shahar*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**Rishtan** in the Fergana Valley is the surviving centre of Uzbek glazed earthenware, worked in an alkaline *ishkor* glaze coloured by local cobalt and copper into deep turquoise and ultramarine on a white slip, with painted almond, pomegranate and knife (*pichoq*) motifs on plates (*lagan*) and bowls (*kosa*). **Gijduvon** near Bukhara produces a warmer palette of yellows, browns and greens. Bukhara and Kokand were centres of engraved and chased copperware — ewers (*oftoba*), basins (*tog'ora*) and long-spouted teapots (*choynak*). Chust in Fergana is famous for its curved single-edged knives (*pichoq*) with bone or horn handles inlaid with wire. Carved and painted wooden cradles (*beshik*), low tables (*xontaxta*), Quran stands (*lauh*) cut from a single block of wood, and lacquered *sandiq* dowry chests are found in most households.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Uzbek jewellery, especially in the Bukhara and Khwarazm traditions, is silver, often gilded and set with turquoise, carnelian and coral. Characteristic forms include the *tumor* — a triangular or cylindrical amulet case holding a Quranic verse, worn on the chest — the *bibishak* and *tillakosh* forehead ornaments, and heavy braided temple pendants (*kaltapo'sht*). Bukharan gold-embroidered women's *peshonaband* headbands were paired with domed gilt crowns for weddings. Rings (*uzuk*), bracelets (*bilaguzuk*) with granulation, and coral bead necklaces are still made. Henna (*hina*) is applied to the bride's hands and feet at a dedicated pre-wedding gathering (*hinabandon*). Long unbraided hair for unmarried women, and braids reaching to the waist bound with silver-tipped cords, are the traditional signal of marital status.

## Music & performance

The classical core of Uzbek music is *shashmaqam* — "six maqams" — the courtly suite tradition shared with the Tajiks and centred historically on Bukhara, inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List. Each of the six *maqams* pairs an instrumental prelude with a long vocal cycle setting Persian and Chagatai poetry (Hafez, Navoi, Bedil). The Khwarazm school preserves a parallel *dutar maqomlari* repertoire. Regional song genres include *katta ashula* ("great song") of the Fergana Valley, an unaccompanied high-tessitura duet or trio sung with the singers holding a small plate (*lagan*) as a resonator, also on the UNESCO list, and *askiya*, a rapid-fire competitive verbal-humour genre performed in tea houses and bazaars. Instruments include the two-string long-necked lute *dutor*, the plucked *tanbur*, the bowed *g'ijjak*, the hammered dulcimer *chang*, the end-blown flute *nay*, the frame drum *doira* (played virtuosically by women in Bukhara), and outdoors the shawm *surnay* with the long trumpet *karnay* and kettledrums *nog'ora*, which announce weddings and the opening of bazaars. Sufi *zikr* singing survives in shrine communities. In Surxondaryo and among the Uzbek-Turkmen borderlands, *bakhshi* epic singers accompany themselves on the *dutor* or *qo'biz*.

## Dance & theatre

Three regional dance schools are recognised. The Bukhara school (*Bukhoro raqsi*) is stately, performed in heavy gold-embroidered robes, with restrained hand articulation and shoulder isolations. The Fergana school (*Fargʻona raqsi*) is lyrical, with flowing arms, wrist rotations and turning steps, danced in khan-atlas. The Khwarazm school is percussive and grounded, driven by the *doira*, and centres on *lazgi* — a virtuoso solo whose accelerating rhythm and step-by-step "waking of the body" from fingertips outward is inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List. Male dance survives in the *maskharaboz* and *qiziqchi* clown-comedians, who combine dance, mime and topical satire at weddings and festivals, and in the acrobatic tightrope walkers (*dorboz*) of the Fergana Valley. Puppet theatre (*qoʻgʻirchoq oʻyin*) uses both glove and marionette figures for satirical short plays.

## Festivals & rituals

The dominant seasonal festival is *Nowruz* (21 March), the pre-Islamic spring new year shared with Iranian and Turkic Central Asia, inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List; it is marked by public *sayil* fairs, horse games, *sumalak* cooking (a slow wheat-sprout pudding stirred through the night by women taking turns and reciting wishes), and household spring cleaning. *Hosil bayrami* marks the autumn harvest, and the two Islamic *hayit* — *Ramazon hayit* (Eid al-Fitr) and *Qurbon hayit* (Eid al-Adha) — anchor the religious calendar. The mountain oasis of Boysun in Surxondaryo preserves an unusually dense complex of pre-Islamic seasonal ritual, healing and epic-singing traditions inscribed by UNESCO as the "Cultural Space of Boysun District." Life-cycle rites are elaborate: *beshik toʻyi* (the placing of an infant in the carved cradle on the fortieth day), *sunnat toʻyi* (boys' circumcision, the largest family celebration of a boy's life), and the multi-day wedding sequence — *fotiha* (engagement), *non sindirish* (breaking of bread), *nikoh* (Islamic contract), *kelin salom* (the bride's ritual bowing to her new in-laws) — around which most textile, food and music traditions cluster. Funerals follow Hanafi Islamic form with additional commemorations on the third, seventh, twentieth and fortieth days and at one year.

## Foodways

The signature dish is *palov* (also *osh*, *plov*) — rice cooked with lamb or beef, onion, carrot and cumin in a heavy cast-iron *qozon* over an open fire, with regional variants (Fergana, Samarkand, Bukhara, Khiva) inscribed by UNESCO as an element of intangible heritage. It is the obligatory dish of weddings, funerals and the morning male gathering (*ertalabki osh*). Bread is *non*, a round wheat flatbread stamped with a *chekich* pattern and baked to the wall of a clay *tandir* oven; Samarkand *non* is particularly prized. Other staples include the pulled hand-cut noodle *lagʻmon*, the steamed dumplings *manti*, the pan-fried *chuchvara*, the samosa-like *somsa* baked in the tandir, and skewered *shashlik*. Yogurt appears as drained *suzma*, dried salted *qurt* balls, and the drink *ayron*. Melons (*qovun*) and grapes from Bukhara and Khwarazm are dried for winter, and quince and pomegranate feature in stews. Green tea (*koʻk choy*) is the constant of hospitality, poured back into the pot three times before serving in shallow *piyola* bowls; black tea (*qora choy*) predominates in Tashkent. Sericulture — *ipakchilik* — is a related UNESCO-inscribed tradition, and mulberry trees line most old canals.

## Oral tradition & literature

The great Uzbek epic is *Alpomish*, a Turkic *dastan* of the hero Alpomish, his abduction and return, transmitted by *bakhshi* singers of Surxondaryo and Qashqadaryo to the accompaniment of the *dumbira*. Other cycles include *Goʻroʻgʻli* (a huge oral epic shared with Turkmen and Azerbaijani tradition), *Kuntugmish*, and the romance *Tohir va Zuhra*. Classical written literature is anchored in the fifteenth-century Chagatai poet Alisher *Navoiy*, whose *Khamsa* of five long poems remains the canonical reference; the biographical *Boburnoma* of Zahiriddin Muhammad Bobur is the region's founding autobiographical text. Folk prose runs to trickster tales of Nasriddin Afandi (Nasreddin Hodja), animal fables, and the improvised humour of *askiya*. Proverbs (*maqol*) and four-line quatrains (*qoʻshiq*) are recited at weddings, and lament (*yigʻi*) is a specialised women's genre.

## Language & religion

Uzbek is a Karluk Turkic language descended from Chagatai, with three dialect groups: Karluk (the standard, urban Fergana–Tashkent–Samarkand–Bukhara), Kipchak (in the north and among former nomadic groups), and Oghuz (in southern Khwarazm, close to Turkmen). It has been written successively in Arabic script (until 1928), Latin (1928–1940), Cyrillic (1940–1992) and Latin again since Uzbek independence. The overwhelming majority of Uzbeks are Sunni Muslims of the Hanafi school, layered over pre-Islamic Zoroastrian and shamanic substrata visible in Nowruz, fire-jumping, tree-tying at saints' shrines (*mazar*), and the amuletic use of *tumor* and *isiriq* (wild rue smoke) against the evil eye. The Naqshbandi Sufi order, founded in fourteenth-century Bukhara by Bahauddin Naqshband, remains the most important spiritual current and shapes much shrine practice, music and poetry.
