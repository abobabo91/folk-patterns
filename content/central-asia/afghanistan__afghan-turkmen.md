## Overview

The Afghan Turkmen arrived in waves from the 17th century onward, with major movements caused by Russian conquest and Soviet collectivization. They inhabit irrigated oases and steppe margins along Afghanistan's northern rim, hemmed between larger Uzbek populations and the Turkmenistan border. They are best known as makers of world-class hand-knotted carpets and keepers of tribal gül medallions that function as visual lineage claims. Their material and oral culture reflects a long history as a mobile pastoral people now largely settled into mudbrick courtyard houses.

## Material culture

Turkmen material culture centers on textiles—especially carpets and woven storage bags—alongside silverwork, felt, and wood, with ceramics historically purchased from neighboring Tajik and Uzbek potters.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Turkmen weaving is organized around the gül, an octagonal medallion repeated across the carpet that marks tribal identity and lineage.

- **Ersari carpet** (*Ersari*) — Woven by the largest Turkmen tribe on wool with symmetric knots, warmer in color than other Turkmen carpets, with larger field guls and looser knotting.
- **Beshir carpet** (*Beshir*) — Named for a town on the Amu Darya, distinguished by long runners, prayer rugs with cloudband borders, and saturated yellows and aubergine.
- **Chuval and torba** (*chuval, torba*) — Storage bags for the yurt interior—the chuval a large flat-faced bag hung against the wall, the torba a narrower shelf-bag, both knotted like carpets.
- **Ok-bash and asmalyk** (*ok-bash, asmalyk*) — The ok-bash covers the yurt roof poles during migration; the asmalyk, a pentagonal trapping, was hung on the bride's camel in wedding processions.
- **Kilim and palas** (*kilim, palas*) — Flatweaves used as floor covers, tent bands, and grain sacks, with the narrow yurt-lashing bands among the most technically elaborate Turkmen weavings.

### Clothing & dress

Men wear quilted coats and tall sheepskin hats; women wear embroidered red silk shifts and distinctive headdresses that change with marital status.

- **Don or chapan** (*don, chapan*) — A long quilted coat of striped silk or cotton, belted with a folded sash, worn by men.
- **Telpek** — A shaggy sheepskin hat of karakul-related stock, worn by men even in summer, often over a smaller embroidered skullcap.
- **Köynek** — A women's straight-cut ankle-length shift of red or wine silk with a keyhole neck embroidered in silk chain stitch.
- **Börük** — A tall cylindrical headdress worn by married women, wrapped with a white or red scarf.
- **Çyrpy** — A false-sleeved mantle thrown over the bride's head as part of bridal costume.

### Architecture

The yurt, or gara öý, is the traditional Turkmen dwelling—a collapsible felt tent with a lattice frame that can be struck and re-erected in hours.

- **Gara öý or yurt** (*gara öý*) — A trellis-walled felt tent with collapsible lattice frame, bent-wood roof poles, and a central crown ring, lined with knotted bags and weavings.
- **Kerege** — The collapsible lattice frame of the yurt that forms its walls.
- **Mudbrick courtyard house** (*hawli*) — Modern sedentary dwellings built of mudbrick with flat earthen roofs, arranged around an enclosed courtyard with a tandyr bread oven.
- **Mazar** — The shrine of a local Sufi pir, a focal point in Turkmen villages.
- **Masjid** — A domed mudbrick mosque found in larger Turkmen settlements.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Turkmen metalwork, especially silver, is highly developed, while ceramics were historically purchased from neighboring potters; everyday objects center on iron, wood, felt, and leather.

- **Gazan** — A large iron cauldron used for cooking pilaf over open fire.
- **Tandyr** — A clay bread oven built into the courtyard wall, used to bake çörek flatbread.
- **Saljak** — A carved wooden cradle.
- **Kese** — A wooden bowl used in the kitchen.
- **Keçe** — A floor felt beaten, rolled, and dyed with madder-red hooked motifs.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Turkmen silverwork, hammered and fire-gilded with cabochon carnelian, is among the most distinctive in the Islamic world and worn to ward off the evil eye.

- **Tumar** — A hollow triangular or cylindrical amulet case worn on a chain across the chest to hold a Qur'anic verse.
- **Gülýaka** — A large round or oval brooch that closes the collar of the köynek shirt.
- **Asyk** — A heart-shaped pendant hung from a girl's back braid.
- **Bukav** — Hinged ankle cuffs worn by women.
- **Egme and tenechir** (*egme, tenechir*) — Silver forehead bands and temple pendants historically worn by married Ersari women.

## Music & performance

The dutar two-stringed lute and the professional bagşy singer are central to Turkmen classical music, performing the dessan epic at weddings and feasts.

- **Dutar** — A two-stringed long-necked lute with a mulberry-wood soundbox, played fingerstyle to accompany the bagşy.
- **Dessan** — A sung-narrated epic, the central genre of Turkmen classical music, performed by the bagşy at weddings and circumcisions.
- **Görogly** — The most politically charged dessan cycle, celebrating a heroic bandit-ruler resisting a foreign shah.
- **Gyjak** — A spike fiddle played on the knee, sometimes accompanying the dutar.
- **Bagşy** — A professional singer-reciter who performs dessans, alternating instrumental saz passages with declaimed verse in a forceful chest voice.

## Dance & theatre

Turkmen societies did not develop codified court dance or mask theatre; participatory wedding celebrations feature the küşt-depdi chant-and-step and horseback games.

- **Küşt-depdi** — A paired chant-and-step performed in a circle by young men and women at weddings, combining rhythmic clapping and a shuffling forward-and-back step.
- **Buzkashi** — A Turkmen variant of the goat-carcass mounted contest, played as wedding entertainment.

## Festivals & rituals

The major annual festival is Nowruz at the spring equinox; Islamic holidays and life-cycle rites—birth, circumcision, wedding, and funeral—structure the Turkmen year.

- **Nowruz** — The spring equinox New Year around 21 March, shared with Persians and other Central Asian peoples, marked by sprouted-wheat pudding, grave visits, and horse races.
- **Kyrky** — A forty-day ceremony after birth when the infant is bathed in salt water and dressed in tailored clothes.
- **Sünnet toýy** — A circumcision feast for boys aged three to seven, featuring dutar performance.
- **Wedding** (*toý, galyň*) — A multi-day celebration including bride-viewing, payment of bride wealth in livestock and carpets, procession under an asmalyk-draped camel, and ritual unveiling.
- **Eid al-Adha** (*Gurban Bayram*) — An Islamic holiday when a sheep is slaughtered and its meat divided in thirds among family, neighbors, and the poor.

## Foodways

Wheat flatbread and rice pilaf are the staples; palaw cooked in a communal cauldron is the signature dish, while dograma and çekdirme are considered distinctively Turkmen.

- **Çörek** — Round flatbread baked against the wall of the tandyr oven.
- **Palaw** — A one-pot rice cooked with lamb, yellow carrots, onion, and cumin in a gazan over open fire, required at every wedding.
- **Dograma** — A distinctively Turkmen bread-based dish in which torn çörek is soaked in mutton broth with hand-shredded meat and raw onion, eaten from a shared bowl at religious commemorations.
- **Çekdirme** — A lamb-and-potato stew considered a distinctive Turkmen preparation.
- **Gatyk and çal** (*gatyk, çal*) — Sour yoghurt and fermented camel milk, dairy products from sheep and camel milk.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Görogly epic cycle and romantic dessans are performed by bagşy; the classical poetry of Magtymguly Pyragy is recited from memory, alongside women's swing-songs, lullabies, and funeral laments.

- **Görogly** — A prose-and-verse epic cycle about the hero Göroglu, sung by bagşy; the Afghan Ersari repertoire preserves episodes lost in standardized versions.
- **Romantic dessans** (*dessan*) — Named romantic epics including Şasenem-Garyp, Zöhre-Tahyr, Leýli-Mejnun, and Hüýrlukga-Hemra, performed alongside the Görogly cycle.
- **Magtymguly Pyragy** — An 18th-century classical poet whose quatrains on Turkmen unity and Sufi morality are recited from memory and quoted in daily speech.
- **Läle** — Women's swing-songs sung by girls at spring gatherings.
- **Agy** — Ritual laments chanted at funerals.

## Language & religion

Afghan Turkmens speak the Ersari dialect of Turkmen, an Oghuz language; they practice Sunni Islam of the Hanafi school with a strong Sufi substratum centered on pir tomb veneration.

- **Ersari dialect** (*Ersari*) — The dialect of Turkmen spoken by Afghan Turkmens, closely related to Azerbaijani and Turkish.
- **Mazar** — The shrine or tomb of a pir, the focal point of folk-religious practice and votive cloth-tying.
- **Tumar amulet** (*tumar*) — A Qur'anic amulet worn to protect against the evil eye and harm.
- **Öwlat** — Lineage saints believed to descend from the Prophet's companions and to hold hereditary blessing.
- **Bereket** — Hereditary blessing held by lineage saints, invoked through appeals and shrine visits.

- *bagşy* — Professional singer-reciter who performs dessans at feasts
- *dessan* — Sung-narrated epic, central genre of Turkmen classical music
- *Görogly* — The most politically charged dessan cycle about a heroic bandit-ruler
- *gara öý* — The traditional Turkmen yurt or felt tent
- *kerege* — The collapsible lattice frame of the yurt
- *asmalyk* — Pentagonal trapping hung on bride's camel in wedding procession
- *chuval* — Large flat-faced storage bag for yurt interior
- *torba* — Narrow shelf-bag for yurt storage
- *köynek* — Women's embroidered red silk shift or dress
- *telpek* — Tall shaggy sheepskin hat worn by men
- *tumar* — Hollow amulet case worn on chest, holds Qur'anic verse
