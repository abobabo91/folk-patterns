## Overview

Yueban, also called Örpün, was an early Turkic tribe identified by Chinese historians as remnants of the Northern Xiongnu in Zhetysu, now part of modern-day Kazakhstan. The Yueban became visible after the disintegration of the Northern Xiongnu state because they remained closer to China than the main body of the Northern Xiongnu. Their principality was established in Zhetysu after they conquered neighboring Uar. The Örpün emerged after the disintegration of the Xiongnu confederation and split into four Chuy tribes during the 480s: Chuyue, Chumi, Chumukun, and Chuban. Their language and customs were described as the same as those of the Gaoche, who were Turkic speakers.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that Örpüns cut their hair and trimmed their ghee-smeared, sun-dried, glossy eyebrows evenly. They also state that Örpüns washed before meals three times every day. No other everyday or ceremonial clothing, head coverings, belts, footwear, or named garments are described.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention hair cutting and the grooming of ghee-smeared, sun-dried, glossy eyebrows.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources state that no records address Yueban religion, although Chinese annals describe manifestations of religious rites and magic. Chuban sorcerers were said to have incited a snowstorm during a war with the Rouran, causing frostbite and forcing the Rouran to retreat.

## Foodways

They state only that Örpüns washed before meals three times every day.

## Oral tradition & literature

Chinese annals preserve accounts of sorcerers who could cause frost and rainstorms, including a narration about Chuban sorcerers and their snowstorm against the Rouran.

## Language & religion

The Book of Wei described the Örpüns’ language and customs as the same as those of the Gaoche, who were Turkic speakers. No records address Yueban religion, although Chinese annals depict religious rites and magic. The sources also state that the Manichaean Chuyue tribe’s descendants, the Shatuo, later became active adherents and protectors of Buddhism and Taoism within China, where they initiated the construction of Buddhist temples. The Shatuo had a predominant Dragon cult and were recorded praying “old services following the custom of the North” at the Thunder-mountain, at the Gates of Dragon.
