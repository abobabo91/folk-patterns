## Overview

The Afghan Turkmen are an Oghuz Turkic people concentrated along Afghanistan's northern rim, from Herat Province in the west through Faryab, Jowzjan, and Balkh, with smaller settlements in Kunduz Province. They inhabit the irrigated oases and steppe margins of the Amu Darya (Oxus) basin and the Murghab River valley, hemmed in by the larger Uzbek population to their south and the Turkmenistan border to their north. Their number is estimated at roughly one million, or 2–3% of Afghanistan's population. Most arrived in successive waves — earlier trans-Caspian migrations from the 17th and 18th centuries, followed by refugees fleeing Russian conquest in the 1880s and, most decisively, Soviet collectivization and the crushing of the Basmachi revolts in the 1920s and 1930s. They matter to folk culture as the makers of some of the world's most recognizable hand-knotted carpets and as custodians of the *gül* — the tribal medallion that functions as a heraldic signature.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Ersari carpet** — Woven by the *Ersari*, the largest Turkmen tribe in Afghanistan, on wool warp and weft with a symmetric (Turkish) knot. Ersari work tends toward a warmer, more brick-red palette than the deep madder of Tekke pieces, with looser knotting and larger field guls, and includes prayer rugs (*ensi*) that once served as yurt door hangings.

**Beshir carpet** — Named for the town of Beshir on the middle Amu Darya, historically an Ersari sub-group production. *Beshir* pieces are distinguished by long runners and prayer rugs with cloudband and *herati* borders, and by the use of yellows and a saturated aubergine that neighboring Turkmen weaves largely avoid.

**Turkmen gül** — Not a textile but the organizing device of nearly all Turkmen weaving: an octagonal or quartered medallion repeated in rows across the carpet field. Each tribe carries a signature gül — the *Salor gül*, *Tekke gül*, *Saryk gül*, and the several *Ersari güls* (including the *gülli-göl* and *temirjin*) — used as a visual claim of lineage.

**Chuval and torba** — Storage bags for the interior of the yurt. The *chuval* is a large flat-faced bag that hung against the trellis wall; the *torba* is a narrower shelf-bag. Both are knotted in the same technique as carpets and often carry compressed gül variants.

**Ok-bash and asmalyk** — The *ok-bash* is a five-sided cover pulled over the exposed ends of the yurt's roof poles during migration. The *asmalyk*, a pentagonal trapping, was hung on either flank of the bride's camel in the wedding procession; Afghan Ersari asmalyks favor an ivory ground with tree-of-life or bird motifs.

**Kilim and palas** — Flatweaves in slitweave technique, used as floor covers, tent bands (*yolam*), and grain sacks. The narrow woven bands that lash the yurt's felt covering to its frame are among the most technically elaborate Turkmen weavings.

**Motif vocabulary.** *gül* (tribal medallion), *dyrnak* (hooked diamond, "fingernail"), *ayak* ("foot," border motif), *ashik* (knucklebone/amulet), *kotshak* (ram's horn, fertility), *gochak* (curled horn variant), *tumar* (triangular amulet), *el-gül* ("hand rose"), *chemche* (spoon or ladle), and the *ensi* cross-panel layout imitating a yurt door.

### Clothing & dress

Men wear the *don* or *chapan*, a long quilted coat of striped silk or cotton (often the ikat-patterned silk woven by neighboring Uzbeks), belted with a folded sash. Head covering is the *telpek*, a shaggy sheepskin hat of long-fleeced karakul-related stock, worn even in summer; under it a smaller embroidered skullcap (*tahya*) is common. Trousers are baggy and tucked into leather boots or wrapped in cloth. Women's dress centers on the *köynek*, a straight-cut ankle-length shift of red or wine silk with a keyhole neck opening embroidered in *keshde* silk chain stitch along the collar and cuffs; over it goes the *çabyt* or a sleeveless coat. Unmarried girls wear the *tahya* skullcap heavy with silver-gilt plaques and coral; married women adopt the *börük*, a tall cylindrical headdress wrapped with a white or red scarf. Bridal costume adds a heavy silver-and-carnelian pectoral and the *çyrpy*, a false-sleeved mantle thrown over the head.

### Architecture

The defining Turkmen dwelling is the *gara öý* ("black house"), the trellis-walled felt tent Russian ethnographers call the *yurt*. Its collapsible *kerege* lattice, bent-wood roof poles (*uk*), and central crown ring (*tüýnük*) can be struck and re-erected in hours. The felt covers are lashed down by the woven bands mentioned above, and the interior is lined with knotted bags, *asmalyks*, and a *germech* door-sill weaving. In Afghanistan, sedentarization since the mid-20th century has pushed most families into mudbrick courtyard houses with flat earthen roofs on wooden joists, arranged around an enclosed *hawli* yard with a *tandyr* bread oven; the yurt survives as a summer guest structure, a wedding tent, and a status object. Villages cluster along irrigation canals (*ariq*) fed from the Murghab and its tributaries, and larger settlements include a domed mudbrick *masjid* and the shrine (*mazar*) of a local Sufi pir.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Ceramics are a weak point in Turkmen material culture — historically a mobile people bought their glazed wares from settled Tajik and Uzbek potters (the blue-and-white tradition of Rishtan and the green-glaze of Istalif). What Turkmens made themselves was in leather, felt, and wood: carved wooden cradles (*saljak*), long-necked wooden mortars, the *kese* wooden bowl, and camel-leather water bags. Metalwork is dominated by silver — teapots, spoons, knife hilts, and above all jewelry. Kitchen equipment centers on the *gazan* iron cauldron for pilaf, the *tandyr* clay bread oven built into the courtyard wall, and the *choýnek* teapot. Felt itself is an object tradition: the *keçe* floor felt is beaten, rolled, and dyed with madder-red hooked motifs.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Turkmen silversmithing is among the most distinctive in the Islamic world. Pieces are hammered from high-grade silver, fire-gilded in patches, and set with cabochon carnelian (*hakyk*), which is understood to ward the evil eye. Signature forms include the *tumar*, a hollow triangular or cylindrical amulet case worn on a chain across the chest to hold a Qur'anic verse; the *gülýaka* (Russian *gulyaka*), a large round or oval brooch that closes the collar of the *köynek*; the *asyk*, a heart-shaped pendant hung from a girl's back braid; the *bukav*, hinged ankle cuffs; and the *çapraz*, articulated plaques sewn onto a bride's headdress or coat. Married Ersari women historically wore silver forehead bands (*egme*) and temple pendants (*tenechir*). Henna is applied to the hands and hair before weddings.

## Music & performance

The core instrument is the *dutar*, a two-stringed long-necked lute with a mulberry-wood soundbox and silk (now nylon) strings, played fingerstyle rather than with a plectrum. It accompanies the *bagşy* — the professional singer-reciter who performs the *dessan*, the sung-narrated epic that is the central genre of Turkmen classical music. Sessions traditionally take place in a *toý* (feast) at weddings and circumcisions, with the bagşy alternating instrumental *saz* passages with declaimed verse in a strained, forceful chest voice. A *gyjak*, a spike fiddle played on the knee, may join the dutar. The *dilli tüýdük*, a short reed pipe, and the *gopuz* jaw harp survive as pastoral instruments. Repertoire is organized around named *mukams* and specific *dessans* — *Görogly*, *Şasenem-Garyp*, *Zöhre-Tahyr*, *Leýli-Mejnun* — the *Görogly* cycle being both the longest and the most politically charged, celebrating a heroic bandit-ruler resisting a foreign shah. Afghan Ersari performance sits closer stylistically to the Bukharan sound than to Ashgabat's, and the shrine music at local pir tombs incorporates *zikr* chanting inherited from the Yasawi and Kubrawi Sufi lineages that converted the Turkmens between the 12th and 14th centuries.

## Dance & theatre

Turkmen societies did not develop a codified court dance tradition comparable to Uzbek *Khorezm* or *Ferghana* dance, and there is no indigenous mask or puppet theatre. What exists is participatory: the *küşt-depdi*, a paired chant-and-step performed in a circle by young men and women at weddings, combining rhythmic clapping, throat-voiced calls, and a shuffling forward-and-back step, historically associated with the western Yomut and Chowdur but performed among Afghan Turkmens as well. Male celebratory dance at *toý* involves shoulder-shaking and arm-raising figures shared with neighboring Uzbeks. Wedding entertainment more often takes the form of horseback games — a Turkmen variant of *buzkashi* (the goat-carcass contest) and mounted wrestling — than staged theatre.

## Festivals & rituals

The largest annual festival is **Nowruz**, the spring equinox New Year (around 21 March), shared with Persians, Kurds, and other Central Asian peoples and inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List as a multi-national element. Turkmen households prepare *semeni* (sprouted wheat pudding), visit family graves, and stage horse races. The Islamic calendar brings *Eid al-Fitr* (Ramadan-end) and *Eid al-Adha* (*Gurban Bayram*), when a sheep is slaughtered and its meat divided in thirds among family, neighbors, and the poor. Life-cycle rites cluster around three events. Birth is marked forty days later by the *kyrky*, when the infant is bathed in salt water and dressed for the first time in tailored clothes; a *tumar* amulet is pinned to the cradle. Circumcision (*sünnet toýy*) for boys aged three to seven is a major feast with dutar performance. Weddings unfold over several days: *gyz-görme* (the bride-viewing), the payment of *galyň* (bride wealth) in livestock and carpets, the bride's procession to her husband's household under an *asmalyk*-draped camel or (today) a car, and the ritual unveiling on the wedding night. Funerals follow Sunni Hanafi practice — same-day burial, forty-day mourning, and a graveside meal at seven days, forty days, and one year.

## Foodways

Wheat is the staple, eaten as *çörek* — round flatbread baked against the wall of the *tandyr*. The signature dish is *palaw* (*plov*), a one-pot rice cooked with lamb, yellow carrots, onion, and cumin in a *gazan* over open fire; every wedding requires a communal cauldron of it. *Çekdirme*, a lamb-and-potato stew, and *dograma*, a bread-based dish in which torn *çörek* is soaked in mutton broth with hand-shredded meat and raw onion, are considered the most distinctively Turkmen preparations, with *dograma* eaten from a shared bowl at religious commemorations. *Manty* (steamed meat dumplings) and *börek* pastries are shared with the wider Central Asian repertoire. Dairy from sheep and camel milk yields *gatyk* (sour yoghurt), *süzme* (drained yoghurt cheese), and *çal* (fermented camel milk). Green tea (*gök çaý*) is drunk continuously and offered first to any guest; in Afghanistan black tea (*gara çaý*) is common as well, taken with rock sugar. Melons from the Amu Darya oases — the *garry gyz* variety among others — are eaten fresh in summer and dried in strips for winter. Dietary rules are Sunni halal; alcohol is largely absent from village life.

## Oral tradition & literature

The central literary text is the **Görogly** epic, a cycle of prose-and-verse tales about the hero Göroglu, sung by *bagşy* to dutar accompaniment; the Afghan Ersari repertoire preserves episodes that have been reshaped or lost in the standardized Ashgabat versions. Alongside it stand the romantic *dessans* — *Şasenem-Garyp*, *Zöhre-Tahyr*, *Leýli-Mejnun*, *Hüýrlukga-Hemra* — and the 18th-century classical poetry of *Magtymguly Pyragy*, whose quatrains on Turkmen unity and Sufi morality are recited from memory and quoted in daily speech. Shorter genres include the *läle*, women's swing-songs sung by girls at spring gatherings; the *hüwdi* lullaby; ritual laments (*agy*) at funerals; and a vast corpus of proverbs (*nakyl*) and riddles (*matal*). Storytelling contexts in Afghanistan remain the wedding *toý*, the winter evening gathering (*gürrüňdeşlik*) in the guest room, and the shrine pilgrimage, where *bagşy* may still be hired for an all-night vigil.

## Language & religion

Afghan Turkmens speak the *Ersari* dialect of Turkmen, an Oghuz-branch Turkic language closely related to Azerbaijani and Turkish and mutually intelligible with them to a fair degree. Historical writing used the Perso-Arabic script and, in the Soviet-descended standard across the border, Cyrillic and now Latin; in Afghanistan the Perso-Arabic *türkmençe* orthography prevails, alongside Dari and Pashto as state languages. Religion is Sunni Islam of the Hanafi *madhhab*, with a strong Sufi substratum inherited from the Yasawi and Kubrawi orders that oversaw the conversion of the Turkmens between the 12th and 14th centuries. Folk-religious practice centers on the veneration of *pir* tombs (*mazar*), the tying of votive cloth strips to shrine trees, the wearing of Qur'anic *tumar* amulets, and appeals to lineage saints — the *öwlat* — believed to descend from the Prophet's companions and to hold hereditary blessing (*bereket*).
