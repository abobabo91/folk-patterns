## Overview

The Nuristanis are an Indo-Iranian ethnic group native to Nuristan Province, formerly Kafiristan, in northeastern Afghanistan and Chitral District in northwestern Pakistan. Their homeland lies in the southern Hindu Kush, with Nuristani-speaking areas drained by the Alingar River, Pech River, Landai Sin, and Kunar rivers. The Nuristani languages have approximately 214,000 speakers and form one of three branches of the Indo-Iranian language family. In Chitral, Nuristanis are also known as Bashgalis or Sheikhan; community estimates place their number there at least 12,000. Their folk culture is documented through clan traditions, seasonal festivals, sacred sites, oral literature, ritual specialists, agricultural practices, and the persistence of older religious customs after the conversion to Islam.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Siah-Posh robes** — The Siah-Posh were described as black-robed or black-clad Kafirs. The term designated the dominant group of Hindu Kush Kafirs inhabiting the Bashgal Valley.

**Safed-Posh robes** — Safed-Posh means white-robed or white-clad. The category included the Waigulis, Presungulis or Viron, and Ashkuns.

**Lal-Posh robes** — Lal-Posh means red-robed or red-clad and was used as another name for the Safed-Posh.

### Clothing & dress

Historical external writers classified the Kafirs into Siah-Posh, or black-robed people, and Safed-Posh or Lal-Posh, meaning white-robed or red-robed people. The Siah-Posh groups were described as sharing a common dress and customs and speaking closely related dialects of Kati. Woolen fabrics are mentioned among goods traded with neighbouring valleys.

### Architecture

They mention shrines called *astān*, which housed wooden effigies of gods or ancestors and could stand near sacred trees, springs, or elevated sites.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Nuristani and Kafir communities are described as accomplished ironworkers. They produced weapons, farming tools, and ritual items for local use or trade with neighbouring valleys. Trade goods included daggers, salt, woolen fabrics, and medicinal herbs. Religious shrines contained wooden effigies, including horned masks, stylized animals, and human figures carved from wood.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They mention carved wooden ritual figures and horned masks as objects associated with shrines.

## Music & performance

Nuristani religious and social life included hymns, epic poetry, oral tales, communal dancing, and ritual recitation. Bards and storytellers transmitted epic tales about gods and heroes through oral tradition. Oral literature included epic songs, genealogical recitations, hymns to deities, and moral tales performed communally. The sources also state that seasonal festivals involved the recitation of epic poetry.

Religious specialists included priests known as *bagisht*, hereditary male ritual specialists, shamans, bards, storytellers, and occasional female spirit mediums or oracles. Priests conducted sacrifices, rituals, and the interpretation of omens. Shamans advised people seeking help and participated in healing, divination, and protection rites. Sacred fires were maintained by priests and used for purification, oaths, and major ceremonies.

The available material connects performance mainly with communal festivals, religious ceremonies, oral transmission, and ritual life.

## Dance & theatre

The sources describe communal dancing and dancing in masks as elements of seasonal festivals. Masked dancing took place alongside sacrifices, communal feasting, wine-drinking, and epic recitation.

## Festivals & rituals

Nuristani religious life followed a ritual calendar based on seasonal changes. Festivals marked the summer and winter solstices, planting and harvest times, and the New Year, called *gósham* in some areas. These occasions involved animal sacrifices, communal feasting, dancing in masks, wine-drinking, and the recitation of epic poetry. They provided opportunities for social and spiritual renewal.

The cult of local deities centred on sacrifices, often performed before sacred fires. Fires maintained by *bagisht* priests were used for purification, oaths, and major ceremonies. Each village and clan had a guardian deity, while clan founders and revered ancestors were honoured at special shrines and remembered in feasts.

The sources also describe initiation rites for boys and girls before the conversion to Islam. These rites marked social maturity and spiritual engagement and could include communal dancing, animal sacrifice, and oaths to clan deities. After Islamization, some seasonal rituals continued privately or under Islamic labels, with Qur'anic recitations replacing older hymns in some contexts.

## Foodways

Before conversion to Islam, Kafir communities practiced terrace agriculture on steep mountain slopes. They cultivated wheat, barley, millet, and pulses. Goat and cattle herding supplemented their diet, while beekeeping and fruit orchards added to subsistence. Grapes and mulberries were used to produce wine, which was consumed at feasts and associated with hospitality.

The sources describe communal feasting as part of seasonal festivals and ancestor commemorations. Wine-drinking, sacrifices, and the recitation of epic poetry could occur together during festival observances. Occasional production of wine continued among parts of the rural population after Islamization.

## Oral tradition & literature

Nuristani oral literature included epic songs, genealogical recitations, mythic storytelling, hymns to deities, and moral tales. These narratives were transmitted across generations through communal performance and helped preserve history, identity, and cosmology. Bards and storytellers recited epic tales about gods and heroes, while oral traditions also remembered legendary ancestors and episodes of conflict.

The sources describe oral histories recounting defiance, martyrdom, and the clandestine preservation of sacred knowledge during and after the conquest of Kafiristan. They also state that epic poetry formed part of seasonal festivals. Richard Strand’s work on Nuristan and Jakob Halfmann’s study of Nuristani theonyms are identified in the supplied material as relevant further reading.

## Language & religion

Nuristani languages form a distinct branch of the Indo-Iranian family, alongside Indo-Aryan and Iranian. The main languages listed are Katë, also called Kati, Kamviri, or Kamkata-vari; Prasun, also called Vasi-vari; Ashkun, also called Ashkunu or Sanu-viri; Nuristani Kalasha, formerly known as Waigali; Tregami; and Zemiaki. Katë is described as the most-spoken Nuristani language. The languages use subject–object–verb word order in many cases, and their history includes contact with Indo-Aryan, Iranian, Bactrian, and neighbouring Dardic languages.

Before conversion, Nuristanis practiced a form of ancient Hinduism or an Indo-Iranian religion with local developments, animism, and ancestor worship. They recognized human-like deities living in an unseen Deity World. Imra or Mara was regarded as a creator and sky deity, while other named deities included Gish or Giwish, Mandi or Moni, Wushum or Shomde, Bagisht, Indr, Züzum, Disani, and Kshumai or Kime. After Emir Abdur Rahman Khan’s conquest of Kafiristan in 1895–96, the population was forcibly converted to Islam. Today the Nuristanis are predominantly Sunni Muslims, while older beliefs remain in folklore and cultural practices.
