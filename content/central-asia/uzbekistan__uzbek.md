## Overview

The Uzbek are the largest Turkic ethnic group in Central Asia, concentrated in oasis cities like Samarkand, Bukhara, and Tashkent. Their culture blends settled Iranian peoples with waves of Turkic and Mongol nomads over centuries. They are renowned as heirs of the Silk Road civilisation, known for fine textiles, metalwork, classical music, and regional cuisines.

## Material culture

Uzbek material culture centres on resist-dyed silks, elaborate embroidery, carved wood and tile work, and objects made for household, ceremonial and dowry use.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Silk ikat weaving, especially in the Fergana Valley, is the signature Uzbek textile tradition, paired with embroidered panels and gold-couched metalwork.

- **Ikat** (*abrbandi*) — Resist-dyed silk warp threads tied and dyed before weaving, creating characteristic blurred cloud-like edges, especially famous from Margilan.
- **Khan-atlas** — The finest all-silk lustrous ikat, historically reserved for elite women and now the signature textile of Uzbek weddings.
- **Suzani** — Large embroidered cotton or silk panels worked in chain, couching and basma stitches, made in multiple cities as part of a bride's dowry.
- **Bukhara gold embroidery** (*zardozi*) — Couched metallic thread on velvet, historically made by male embroiderers for chapans, saddle-cloths and skullcaps.
- **Chust skullcap** (*chust duppi*) — Black-and-white embroidered square cap from the Fergana Valley with four stylised chilli-pepper motifs, the most recognisable national form.

### Clothing & dress

Men wear quilted striped coats over shirts with sashes and skullcaps; women wear unfitted straight-cut dresses with embroidered waistcoats, with bridal dress heavily ornamented in silver.

- **Chapan** — Quilted striped open coat worn by men over the shirt, often in warp-striped silk-cotton or ikat, tied with a folded sash.
- **Skullcap** (*duppi or tubeteika*) — Square or round embroidered cap worn by men, with regional varieties; the chust duppi is most recognisable.
- **Waistcoat** (*nimcha*) — Velvet or embroidered waistcoat worn by women over their straight-cut dress.
- **Paranji veil** (*paranji*) — Long grey-brown horsehair-veiled robe with vestigial sleeves worn by urban women in Bukhara and Samarkand until the 1920s Soviet campaigns.
- **Henna ceremony** (*hinabandon*) — Pre-wedding gathering where henna is applied to the bride's hands and feet.

### Architecture

Traditional urban houses are single-storey mud-brick structures built around interior courtyards; monumental architecture features carved terracotta, tile mosaic and muqarnas vaulting.

- **Courtyard house** (*hovli*) — Single-storey mud-brick and timber structure with blank street walls, built around one or two interior courtyards and a reception room opening onto a carved veranda.
- **Summer veranda** (*ayvon*) — Open veranda with carved wooden columns and muqarnas capitals, facing a pool and fruit garden.
- **Carved plaster** (*ganch*) — Carved and painted plaster used on interior walls of traditional houses.
- **Yurt** (*oʻtov*) — Felt-covered portable dwelling retained by rural Uzbeks of the steppe fringe into the twentieth century.
- **Honeycomb vaulting** (*muqarnas*) — Three-dimensional geometric vaulting used in half-domes and portal niches of monumental mosques, madrasas and mausolea.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Uzbek ceramics centre on glazed earthenware from Rishtan and Gijduvon; metalwork includes engraved copperware and inlaid curved knives.

- **Rishtan pottery** (*Rishtan*) — Glazed earthenware from the Fergana Valley worked in alkaline glaze coloured with cobalt and copper into deep turquoise, with painted almond, pomegranate and knife motifs.
- **Gijduvon pottery** (*Gijduvon*) — Ceramics centre near Bukhara producing a warmer palette of yellows, browns and greens.
- **Chust knife** (*pichoq*) — Curved single-edged knife from Chust in Fergana with bone or horn handles inlaid with wire.
- **Engraved copperware** (*oftoba, tog'ora, choynak*) — Engraved and chased copperware from Bukhara and Kokand, including ewers, basins and long-spouted teapots.
- **Dowry chest** (*sandiq*) — Lacquered wooden chest used to store a bride's belongings and trousseau.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Uzbek jewellery is predominantly silver with turquoise, carnelian and coral; amulet cases and forehead ornaments are key ceremonial forms.

- **Amulet case** (*tumor*) — Triangular or cylindrical amulet case holding a Quranic verse, worn on the chest as protection.
- **Forehead ornament** (*bibishak or tillakosh*) — Silver ornaments worn on the forehead, especially during weddings and ceremonial occasions.
- **Temple pendants** (*kaltapo'sht*) — Heavy braided silver temple pendants worn as signs of marital status and adornment.
- **Wedding crown** (*peshonaband*) — Gold-embroidered headband paired with a domed gilt crown worn by Bukharan brides at weddings.
- **Braids and hair ornaments** — Long unbraided hair signals unmarried status; braids reaching to the waist bound with silver-tipped cords signal marriage.

## Music & performance

The classical courtly tradition shashmaqam dominates Uzbek music, paired with regional song genres and a rich ensemble of plucked, bowed, struck and wind instruments.

- **Shashmaqam** — Courtly suite of six maqams pairing instrumental preludes with long vocal cycles setting Persian and Chagatai poetry, centred on Bukhara and inscribed as UNESCO intangible heritage.
- **Katta ashula** — Unaccompanied high-tessitura duet or trio from the Fergana Valley sung while holding a small plate as a resonator, inscribed by UNESCO.
- **Competitive humour** (*askiya*) — Rapid-fire competitive verbal-humour genre performed in tea houses and bazaars, also an inscribed UNESCO tradition.
- **Dutar lute** (*dutor*) — Two-string long-necked lute used in both classical and epic singing traditions.
- **Frame drum** (*doira*) — Frame drum played virtuosically by women, especially in Bukhara, and central to Khwarazm dance traditions.

## Dance & theatre

Three regional dance schools are recognised—Bukhara, Fergana and Khwarazm—each with distinct movement vocabularies and costume.

- **Bukhara dance** (*Bukhoro raqsi*) — Stately dance performed in heavy gold-embroidered robes with restrained hand articulation and shoulder isolations.
- **Fergana dance** (*Fargʻona raqsi*) — Lyrical dance with flowing arms, wrist rotations and turning steps, danced in khan-atlas silk.
- **Lazgi solo** (*lazgi*) — Virtuoso percussive solo from Khwarazm with accelerating rhythm and step-by-step awakening of the body, inscribed by UNESCO.
- **Clown-comedians** (*maskharaboz or qiziqchi*) — Male performers who combine dance, mime and topical satire at weddings and festivals.
- **Puppet theatre** (*qoʻgʻirchoq oʻyin*) — Glove and marionette puppet theatre used for satirical short plays.

## Festivals & rituals

Nowruz (spring new year) is the dominant festival; life-cycle rites centre on marriage with elaborate multi-day ceremonies around which textile and food traditions cluster.

- **Nowruz** — Spring new year (21 March) marked by public fairs, horse games, wheat-sprout pudding stirring and household spring cleaning, inscribed by UNESCO.
- **Circumcision ceremony** (*sunnat toʻyi*) — Boys' circumcision celebration, the largest family celebration of a boy's life.
- **Wedding sequence** (*nikoh*) — Multi-day ceremony including engagement, bread-breaking, Islamic contract and the bride's ritual bowing to in-laws, around which most textile and music traditions centre.
- **Cradle ceremony** (*beshik toʻyi*) — Ritual placing of an infant in a carved cradle on the fortieth day of life.
- **Boysun cultural space** — Mountain oasis in Surxondaryo preserving a dense complex of pre-Islamic seasonal ritual, healing and epic-singing traditions, inscribed by UNESCO.

## Foodways

Palov (rice with meat, onion, carrot and cumin) is the signature dish, central to weddings and funerals; flatbread, noodles, dumplings and dairy products complete the staple diet.

- **Palov** (*palov or osh*) — Rice cooked with lamb or beef, onion, carrot and cumin in a cast-iron pot, with regional variants inscribed by UNESCO as intangible heritage.
- **Flatbread** (*non*) — Round wheat flatbread stamped with a pattern and baked to the wall of a clay tandir oven; Samarkand non is particularly prized.
- **Hand-cut noodles** (*lagʻmon*) — Pulled hand-cut noodles served in a meat and vegetable sauce.
- **Steamed dumplings** (*manti*) — Steamed dumplings filled with meat and onion, served with yogurt and meat sauce.
- **Yogurt forms** (*suzma or qurt*) — Drained yogurt or dried salted yogurt balls used as condiments and preserved for winter storage.

## Oral tradition & literature

The epic Alpomish transmitted by bakhshi singers and the classical poetry of Alisher Navoiy anchor Uzbek oral and written literary traditions.

- **Alpomish epic** (*Alpomish*) — Turkic dastan of the hero Alpomish, his abduction and return, transmitted by bakhshi singers of Surxondaryo and Qashqadaryo.
- **Goʻroʻgʻli epic** (*Goʻroʻgʻli*) — Huge oral epic shared with Turkmen and Azerbaijani tradition, transmitted by bakhshi singers.
- **Classical poet** (*Navoiy or Alisher Navoiy*) — Fifteenth-century Chagatai poet whose Khamsa of five long poems remains the canonical reference of Uzbek literature.
- **Trickster tales** (*Nasriddin Afandi*) — Folk prose tales of a clever trickster figure, widespread in Uzbek and broader Central Asian tradition.
- **Lament** (*yigʻi*) — Specialised women's genre of ritual lamentation performed at funerals and commemorations.

## Language & religion

Uzbek is a Karluk Turkic language with three dialect groups; Islam is practised in the Hanafi school with visible pre-Islamic Zoroastrian and shamanic substrata.

- **Karluk Turkic** (*Karluk*) — Standard urban dialect of Uzbek spoken in Fergana, Tashkent, Samarkand and Bukhara, descended from Chagatai.
- **Naqshbandi order** (*Naqshbandi*) — Sufi order founded in fourteenth-century Bukhara by Bahauddin Naqshband, the most important spiritual current shaping shrine practice and poetry.
- **Evil eye protection** (*tumor or isiriq*) — Amulet cases and wild rue smoke used to protect against the evil eye, a pre-Islamic practice.
- **Saint shrine** (*mazar*) — Shrine to a saint where tree-tying and veneration practices continue as part of Sufi devotion.
- **Fire-jumping** — Pre-Islamic seasonal ritual still practiced as part of Nowruz celebrations in some regions.
