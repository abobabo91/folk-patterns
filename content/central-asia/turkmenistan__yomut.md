## Overview

The Yomut (Turkmen: *Ýomut*, Persian: یَمود/یُمود) are a large ethnographic group within the Turkmen people and were historically one of the five major tribes of Turkmenistan. They maintain a distinct cultural identity and live primarily in Turkmenistan, Iran, and Uzbekistan; smaller subgroups are also found in Afghanistan and Karakalpakstan. Their main regional groupings are the Gurgan Yomut, living in the Gorgan Plain of Iran and along the southern bank of the Caspian Sea in Turkmenistan, and the Khiva Yomut, living immediately west of Khiva across the border in Turkmenistan. They describe Yomut society, livelihood, dress, fishing, wedding customs, and internal tribal divisions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Felt* and woven carpets were among the products traded by the Chomur with neighbouring Persian settlements, particularly Asterabad.

*Khasava* was a traditional Yomut Turkmen women’s wedding headdress rather than a textile. It was constructed on a widening frame, first made from wheat stalks and later from cardboard, then covered with fabric and a *purenjek* or *don*. It was decorated with gilded silver plaques, pendants, beads, and chains with bells.

*Uramak* was a local name for a similar headdress among northern Yomut women, while *aladaŋy* was the name used among western Yomut women.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The *khasava* was worn by young brides on their wedding day. It reached 30 cm or more in height and was richly decorated with gilded silver plaques, pendants, beads, and chains with bells. Its jingling was compared with the rattle of a snake. The bride removed it during the *gaytarma* period, the temporary return to her family’s home after the wedding, and then wore it again until the birth of her first or second child. By the early 20th century, variants were widespread among young and middle-aged women regardless of childbirth status. The tall form became rare and was gradually replaced by the shorter *alym dany*, also called *beruk*, a style borrowed from Iranian women. Other local names for comparable headdresses included *borik*, *topby*, *topba*, *bagmak*, *chanie*, *kars*, *dastar*, and *pechek*.

### Architecture

They mention settlements in the Gorgan Plain, along the Gorgan and Atrek rivers, near Khiva, and on the edge of the desert toward Astrabad, but provide no architectural account.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do describe gilded silver plaques, pendants, beads, and bell-bearing chains used on the *khasava*. In fishing communities, modern tools included galvanized fishing nets and motorboats. The nets were locally called “Namardi nets”; *Narmardi* literally meant “invisible” and was also used colloquially for something considered unmanly.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Yomut women’s adornment included the gilded silver plaques, pendants, beads, and chains with bells attached to the *khasava*. The headdress was associated with ancient ritual headgear connected with fertility cults. G. P. Vasilyeva identified similarities between Yomut jewelry and North Caucasian Alan ornaments from the 6th–8th centuries in Ossetia, as well as finds from medieval burial grounds in mountainous Ingushetia dating to the 15th–16th centuries.

## Music & performance

The sources identify *Kushtdepdi* as a traditional rite of singing and dancing combining improvised poetic verses with rhythmic group movement. In 2017, Wikipedia states that it was inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity under the title “Kushtdepdi rite of singing and dancing.” The practice has been performed across various Turkmen communities. Contemporary cultural accounts often note the contribution of coastal groups, including some Yomut communities, to its preservation and transmission, although the sources state that UNESCO and other major heritage sources do not attribute it exclusively to one Turkmen tribe.

The Yomut material instead emphasizes the combination of improvised verse and collective movement in *Kushtdepdi*.

## Dance & theatre

**Kushtdepdi** is the only named Yomut-associated performance tradition in the sources. It combines singing, improvised poetic verses, and rhythmic group movement. The sources describe it as a traditional rite and identify its preservation among coastal communities, including some Yomut groups.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do describe wedding-related practice. The *khasava* was traditionally worn by a young bride on her wedding day. It was removed during the *gaytarma* period, when the bride temporarily returned to her family home after the wedding before fully settling in her husband’s house, and was then worn again until the birth of the first or second child. The association of the headdress with fertility cults is also noted.

## Foodways

The Chomur practised agriculture along the Gorgan River and toward the Karasu. They cultivated wheat, barley, rice, and vegetables, and maintained gardens and fields in river valleys. Their commercial contacts with Persian settlements included trade in grain, butter, sheep, horses, felt, and woven carpets. The Charwa, by contrast, were pastoral nomads whose economy centred on herding sheep, camels, and horses.

The available material concerns agricultural production, pastoralism, and trade rather than cuisine.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources record several explanations and legends concerning the ethnonym Yomut. One folk legend connects the name with *ýowm it*, “dog’s lair”: a woman gave birth while travelling, wrapped the child, and placed it in a dog’s den. The people descended from that child were said to become known as *ýowmit–ýowmut–ýomut*. A second legend derives the word from *ýow*, “enemy,” and *mut*, “defeated, subdued,” meaning “one who defeats the enemy.”

The sources also mention Abylgazy’s *Genealogy of the Turkmens*, in which the Yomut name is not used for the tribe but for a smaller lineage or clan. The genealogy gives the descent sequence Ögürjik Alp → Berdi → Gulmy → Yomut.

## Language & religion

The sources give Turkmen and Persian forms of the name Yomut and mention dialectal and minor cultural differences among Yomut groups. They discuss several proposed etymologies, including links to *ýowm it*, *ýow* and *mut*, and the Old Turkic word *yom*, interpreted as “people,” “tribe,” or “clan.”

They only associate the *khasava* with ancient ritual headgear and fertility cults.
