## Overview

The Pashayi or Pashai are an Indo-Aryan ethnolinguistic group living primarily in eastern Afghanistan. They are concentrated in Laghman, Nangarhar, Kunar, Kapisa, Parwan, Nuristan, and Panjshir provinces, as well as Khyber Pakhtunkhwa in Pakistan. Many Pashayi speak Pashto in addition to Pashayi, and some also speak Persian. Some Pashayi have been assimilated by Pashtuns, while Pashayi in Panjshir and Parwan have been assimilated by Tajiks. Their folk culture, as described in the source, includes agriculture and herding, communal meetings, wedding customs, oral traditions, descent-group myths, and a circle dance performed at weddings and celebrations.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do record that, in the thirteenth century, Marco Polo described local men as wearing brooches and earrings decorated with gemstones.

### Architecture

They do mention valleys, villages, districts, and permanent land settlements in connection with Pashayi history, but provide no architectural description.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The only specifically described objects are the brooches and earrings decorated with gemstones that Marco Polo reportedly observed among local men in the thirteenth century.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used mention brooches and earrings decorated with gemstones in Marco Polo’s thirteenth-century description of local men.

## Music & performance

They do describe a communal performance associated with Pashayi weddings and celebrations: young people of all genders gather in a circle, hold one another by the shoulders, and sing songs together. The performance lasts for 20–25 minutes. No name is supplied for the songs, the dance-song form, or any accompanying instruments.

The source also describes *marat*, meetings in which Pashayi elders lead discussions and resolve issues. This is compared with the Pashtun *jirga*. No information is provided about funeral music, court performance, religious recitation, instrumental traditions, or music-making in domestic settings.

## Dance & theatre

The source describes a Pashayi dance performed at weddings and during celebrations. Young people of all genders form a circle, hold each other by the shoulders, and sing songs while performing the dance. It lasts for 20–25 minutes.

## Festivals & rituals

The available description concerns weddings and celebrations, during which the communal circle dance and singing are performed.

Pashayi marriage customs are described as differing from those of other regions. An engaged couple may spend time together and interact freely from the beginning of their engagement. Restrictions commonly imposed on engaged couples elsewhere do not apply, and wedding ceremonies follow distinctive traditions.

The Pashayi also hold *marat* meetings to resolve issues. Pashayi elders are present and lead the discussions.

## Foodways

The Pashayi practice a mixed economy of agriculture and herding. Common crops include rice, wheat, and corn. They raise goats, cattle, and sheep. Marco Polo’s thirteenth-century account stated that the local diet consisted mainly of rice and meat.

The source states that, when the Mughal ruler Babur visited Darai Nur in the sixteenth century, eating pork had only recently been forbidden and the town was famous for its wine. By the early twentieth century, Pashayi were described as Sunni Muslims, with a minority of Nizari Isma'ili Muslims.

In the account of settlement in Darra-i Nur, the Kafir Kalautar was required to pay Deishamir tribute of a *kharwar* of cheese annually.

## Oral tradition & literature

Pashayi oral tradition includes an account of the conversion of Darra-i Nur. According to the tradition, in the mid-eighteenth century Deishamir Baba conquered the valley and converted its inhabitants to Islam. The story names Deishamir Baba’s sons, Ranga and Japar, and identifies his six grandsons as ancestors of the Pashai in Sotan. It also names the Kafir rulers Bhim Raja of Bambakot, Shultan Raja of Sotan, Sher Raja, Laṇā Raja of Shemul, and Muturu Raja of Utran.

Pashayi mythology explains the origins of two important descent groups in Darra-i-Nur. An abandoned newborn was discovered and raised by a goat, which scraped away leaves with its hoof; this origin story gives the descent group Soom its name, meaning hoof. A second version involves a goat discovering a child with its horns, giving rise to Shenganek, with *sheng* meaning horn. The source also names the Kolman or Kulman tribe and Surat Baig.

The source mentions the *Sifat Nima*, a book written by Darvish Mohammad Khan, and the *Tabakat-i-Akbari* of Nizamuddin Ahmad.

## Language & religion

Pashayi is associated with an Indo-Aryan ethnolinguistic group. Many Pashayi are bilingual in Pashto, and some are trilingual in Pashto, Pashayi, and Persian.

Before the arrival of Islam, religions traditionally practised by Pashayi included variant forms of Buddhism and Hinduism, animism, local beliefs, combinations of these, and Shamanism. Goats were sacrificed as part of their beliefs. According to the *Sifat Nima*, the Pashayi worshipped gods named Pandad, Sharway, and Lamanday. G.J. Daushvili described a syncretic religion among Pashayi and other Western Dards that included elements of hunting cults, the Nuristani Kafir religion, and Shaivism.

The sources describe conversion to Islam as occurring through several historical processes. By the early twentieth century, Pashayi and Parachi were collectively referred to as Kohistani and were Sunni Muslims, while a minority were Nizari Isma'ili Muslims. Pashayi-speaking Sikhs have also existed, although Muslim Pashayi do not consider them part of the ethnic group.
