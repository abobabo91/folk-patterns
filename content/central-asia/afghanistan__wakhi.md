## Overview

The Wakhi people, also locally referred to as the Wokhik, are an Iranic ethnic group native to Central and South Asia. They primarily inhabit the Wakhan Corridor in Afghanistan, as well as parts of Gilgit-Baltistan and Chitral in Pakistan, Gorno-Badakhshan in Tajikistan, and southwestern Xinjiang in China. In Afghanistan, they primarily live in Wakhan District of Badakhshan Province. Their population was estimated at approximately 50,000–60,000 in 2015; a 2018 estimate counted about 72,000 Wakhi-speakers across four countries. Wakhi is an Eastern Iranian language within the Indo-Iranian branch of the Indo-European family.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Wakhi houses are built of stone and sod. The sources state that many Wakhi people have two residences, one for winter and one for summer, in connection with their primarily nomadic way of life.

## Music & performance

The sources identify music as part of cultural preservation and public cultural activity. In Pakistan, the Wakhi Cultural Association Pakistan works to preserve Wakhi language and culture and to document Wakhi poetry and music. The organization has arranged more than twenty programmes since 1984, including cultural shows, musical nights, and large-scale musical festivals, in collaboration with Lok Virsa Pakistan, the Aga Khan Cultural Service Pakistan, and Pakistan television.

Radio Pakistan’s Radio Gilgit has broadcast Wakhi-language programming. One programme is named *Bam-e Dunya*, translated in the source as “Roof of the World,” while another is called “Sadoyah Boomy Dunyo,” translated as “Voice of the Roof of the World.”

In 2000, the Wakhi Cultural Association won a “Best Programme” organizer award in the Silk Road Festival from the President of Pakistan, Pervez Musharraf.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources mention cultural festivals organized by the Wakhi Tajik Cultural Association and musical festivals arranged by the Wakhi Cultural Association Pakistan. In 1990, the Gojali Wakhis of Pakistan established the Wakhi Tajik Cultural Association to preserve, document, and publish local culture. The association organized cultural festivals and introduced a script used in linguistic and literary textbooks.

The Wakhi Cultural Association Pakistan has arranged cultural shows, musical nights, and large-scale musical festivals since 1984.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe efforts to preserve and publish Wakhi language, poetry, literature, and history. The Wakhi Tajik Cultural Association, established in 1990 by Gojali Wakhis of Pakistan, introduced a script used in linguistic and literary textbooks and worked to document local culture. The Wakhi Cultural Association Pakistan also works to document Wakhi poetry and music.

The Wakhi language article lists several publications. *X̌ikwor zik* is attributed to Master Haqiqat; religious hymns are attributed to Riaz Ahmed Riaz; *Qaida e Wakhi Zaban* is attributed to Ahmed Jami Sakhi; and *Magazines and Parlon Wakhi* is associated with Karim Khan Saka. *Beyoz-e-Bulbul* is described as a collection of old and new poems and folklores compiled by Bulbulik Heritage Center, Gulmit.

 They do, however, identify oral literature as a field of documentation, including a work titled *La langue wakhi*, described as a corpus of oral literature.

## Language & religion

Wakhi is an Eastern Iranic language within the Indo-Iranian branch of the Indo-European language family. It belongs to the areal Pamir language group and is spoken in the Wakhan region of Afghanistan, parts of Pakistan and China, and Gorno-Badakhshan in Tajikistan. Wakhi speakers refer to themselves as Khik and to their language as Khik zik; in the language article, the self-appellation is written as *X̌ik*, with *X̌ik-wor* denoting the language.

Wakhi has historically been unwritten. Writing systems have since been developed using Arabic, Cyrillic, and Latin scripts, but no single orthographic standard has become a unifying writing system. The sources describe separate Afghan and Pakistani Arabic-script conventions, as well as Cyrillic and Latin developments. A Latin alphabet was developed in 1953 by I.I. Zaroubine and V. S. Sokolova and further developed by A.L Grünberg and I.M Stéblinn-Kamensky in the early 1960s.

The Wakhi predominantly adhere to Nizari Isma'ili Sh'ia Islam and are followers of the Aga Khan. In Tajikistan, Wakhi are recognized by the state as Tajiks but self-identify as Pamiri; in Afghanistan, they are officially called Pamiri.
