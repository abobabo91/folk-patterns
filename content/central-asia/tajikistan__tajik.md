## Overview

The Tajik are a Persianate people of Central Asia, the largest ethnicity of Tajikistan and a substantial population in Afghanistan and Uzbekistan, with historic urban centers at Samarkand, Bukhara, Khujand, and Termez. They inhabit the Oxus (Amu Darya) basin, the Zarafshan and Fergana valleys, and the high Pamir Mountains of Gorno-Badakhshan, where the distinct Pamiri (Shughni, Wakhi, Bartangi, Yazgulami) and Yaghnobi groups are officially counted as Tajik in Tajikistan. They speak Tajiki — an eastern variant of Persian written in Cyrillic in Tajikistan and Perso-Arabic in Afghanistan — descended from the New Persian that displaced Sogdian and Bactrian after the Islamic conquest. Descendants of Sogdians, Bactrians, and Scythians with Persian, Turkic, and Greek admixture, the Tajik carry a folk culture that fuses Iranian oasis urbanism, mountain pastoralism, and pre-Islamic Zoroastrian residues under a dominant Sunni-Hanafi frame.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Chakan** — *chakan* is the signature suzani-adjacent embroidery of the Kulob region and southern Tajikistan, worked in bright silk chain-stitch (*yurma*) on cotton or silk ground, producing dresses, shawls, curtains, and pillow covers. Unlike Bukharan suzani, chakan compositions foreground cosmological registers — sun, moon, tree of life — rather than radial floral medallions.

**Pamir chakan** — a related but distinct highland embroidery from Badakhshan, worked on wool and cotton with denser geometric fill, used to ornament caps, cushions, and the collars and cuffs of women's dresses in the Wakhan and Bartang corridors.

**Atlas silk** (*atlas*) — a warp-ikat silk woven along the upper Zarafshan (Khujand, Istaravshan) and shared with Uzbek Fergana workshops, dyed by tie-resist before weaving to produce blurred vertical bands of magenta, ochre, and indigo. Tajik atlas favors narrower repeats and darker grounds than Margilan pieces.

**Adras** (*adras*) — a mixed silk-warp / cotton-weft ikat, heavier and matter than pure atlas, used for coats (*chapan*), women's dresses, and cradle covers; the cotton weft dulls the color and makes the cloth wearable for daily labor.

**Kurta** (*kurta*) — the long straight-cut woman's dress, traditionally cut from a single width of atlas, adras, or hand-woven cotton (*karbos*), with a narrow standing collar and embroidered neck slit; the dress is the primary display surface for both ikat and chakan work.

**Toqi** (*toqi*, also *tupi*) — the small embroidered skullcap worn by men, women, and children, with strong regional signatures: Chust-style black-and-white almond motif in the Fergana Tajik towns, densely couched gold-thread *zardozi* caps in Bukhara, and beaded, mirrored Pamiri caps in Badakhshan.

**Pamir house painting** — although painted rather than woven, the decorated wooden ceiling panels and lintels of the Pamiri *chid* share the same repertoire of solar disks, ram's-horn spirals, and tulip motifs as chakan embroidery, and are treated by weavers as a common design vocabulary.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Oftob* (sun disk), *mohtob* (moon), *sitora* (star), *daraxti hayot* (tree of life), *lola* (tulip), *anor* (pomegranate — fertility), *bodom* (almond — the paisley/boteh element on toqi), *shokhi buzghola* (goat's horn spiral), *muhri Sulaymon* (Solomon's seal, eight-pointed star), *chorbogh* (four-garden quartered field), *tumor* (triangular amulet).

### Clothing & dress

Women wear the *kurta* dress over loose ankle-gathered trousers (*ezor* or *poyjoma*), with a headscarf (*rumol*) knotted at the nape for everyday work and a longer embroidered shawl for visiting. Bridal and festive dress in Kulob and Khatlon is a full chakan-embroidered *kurta* with a matching headdress and heavy silver pectoral. In the Pamirs, women wear a distinct forehead band and a fringed white cotton headscarf. Men's daily dress is a plain cotton *kurta* shirt, wide trousers, and the *toqi* skullcap; over this goes the *chapan*, a quilted, striped ikat or wool coat, tied at the waist with a folded sash (*miyonband*) that doubles as pocket and prayer mat. Elders and religious figures wrap a *salla* turban over the toqi. Traditional footwear is soft leather boots (*mahsi*) worn inside galoshes (*kaush*) that are shed at the threshold. Silver-mounted knives and, in the mountains, felt cloaks (*chakman*) remain in occasional use.

### Architecture

Lowland Tajik urban houses in Bukhara, Samarkand, and Khujand follow the Central Asian courtyard type: a blank mud-brick street facade opens onto an interior courtyard (*hovli*) with a pool, a *mehmonkhona* guest room, and an *ayvan* — a deep summer porch supported by carved wooden columns with muqarnas capitals. Interiors are ganch-plastered, with carved and painted niches (*tokcha*) for ceramics and folded bedding. The signature highland dwelling of the Pamirs is the *chid* (also *pamiri khona*): a square, flat-roofed stone-and-timber house whose central skylight (*roshandon*) is built of five concentric wooden squares symbolizing the Ismaili five holy figures, and whose interior is organized around five load-bearing pillars named for those figures, with a raised platform (*nakhchir-poya*) for elders. Rural Zarafshan and Hisar houses use pakhsa (rammed earth) and mud-brick walls with flat earthen roofs rolled after each rain. Timber mosques and mausolea, notably the tenth-century Samanid mausoleum in Bukhara, anchor the built repertoire.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The main living Tajik ceramic center is **Istaravshan** (Ura-Tyube), producing lead-glazed earthenware in oxide green, manganese brown, and cobalt blue with painted floral scrolls; related workshops operate at **Kanibadam** and **Kulob**. Bukharan and Samarkand Tajik potters historically supplied the blue-and-white *lyagan* platters and *piyola* teabowls used across the region. Copperwork centers on **Istaravshan** and Bukhara, producing hammered and tinned *aftoba* ewers, *dastshuy* handwashing basins, samovars, and engraved trays. Wooden objects include carved *lauh* Quran stands, low tea tables (*khontakht*), painted cradles (*gahvora*), and the columns and doors of houses and mosques, worked in walnut and elm with deep floral relief. Knife-making at **Chorku** near Isfara produces the *kordi chusti* with a bone or bull-horn handle and stamped blade.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Tajik women's jewelry is silver-based, often gilded and set with carnelian, turquoise, coral, and colored glass. Characteristic forms include the *tumor* — a hinged triangular or cylindrical amulet case worn on the chest, holding a Quranic verse; the *haykal*, a large pectoral plaque; forehead pieces (*silsila* or *tillakosh*) worn under a headscarf, sometimes hung with coin fringes; heavy earrings (*gushvor*), nose rings (*arabak*) in the south, and stacked silver bracelets (*dastpona*). Bridal parures combine all of these with a beaded headdress. Hair is worn in many thin braids (as many as forty for brides) weighted with tassels (*kokul*). Kohl (*surma*) darkens the eyes, henna colors palms and hair, and eyebrows are joined with a painted line (*usma*) using indigo-plant juice as a beauty and protective mark.

## Music & performance

The classical apex of Tajik-Uzbek urban music is **shashmaqam** (UNESCO 2003/2008), the "six maqams" — Buzruk, Rost, Navo, Dugoh, Segoh, Iroq — a suite tradition developed in the courts and Sufi lodges of Bukhara that alternates instrumental preludes, metered songs (*saraxbor*, *talqin*, *nasr*), and settings of Persian and Chagatai ghazals by Hafez, Bedil, and Navoi. The instruments are the long-necked lutes *tanbur* and *dutar*, the plucked *rubab*, the spike fiddle *ghijak*, the hammered dulcimer *chang*, the frame drum *doira*, and the reed flute *nay*. In the mountainous south and in Badakhshan, **falak** (UNESCO 2021) is the dominant genre — a solo lament sung to *dutar* or *setor* accompaniment, expressing separation, exile, and complaint against fate, with subgenres *falaki dashti* (steppe falak) and *falaki kuhi* (mountain falak). Pamiri devotional music centers on the *maddoh*, sung Ismaili poetry accompanied by the *rubobi pomiri* and the goblet drum *tablak*, performed in the *chid* around a lamp during vigils. Wedding and circumcision music is carried by the *surnay* shawm and *nagora* kettledrums played from the rooftop. Bardic *hofiz* singers and, in villages, women's *doira*-led song-circles preserve local repertoires.

## Dance & theatre

Tajik dance splits into two main streams. The lowland urban tradition, associated with Bukhara, Samarkand, and Khujand, features soft, wrist-and-shoulder-centered women's dances such as *raqsi Bukhoro* and *raqsi Samarqandi*, historically performed by professional *sozanda* female entertainer troupes at weddings; expressive hand articulation, spins, and shoulder shimmies are accompanied by *doira*. The mountain tradition — Kulobi, Hisori, and Pamiri — is more grounded and percussive, with the Pamiri *rapo* danced in pairs with slow, mirror-image arm gestures during *maddoh* gatherings. Men's dances include *raqsi kordi* (knife dance) and the acrobatic rope-walking *dorbozi*, historically performed at Nowruz and bazaar festivals. Full theatrical forms are thin — professional stage theatre is a Soviet-era import — but comic itinerant *maskharaboz* clowns and *qiziqchi* jesters continued older bazaar improvisation, and Ismaili communities perform devotional dramatic recitations during *chirogh-rawshan*, the lamp-lighting mourning rite.

## Festivals & rituals

The pivot of the calendar is **Nowruz** (UNESCO 2009/2016), the Persian New Year at the spring equinox, celebrated with the *haft sin* / *haft shin* table, sprouted wheat (*sabzi*), bonfires, wrestling (*gushtingiri*), buzkashi in the countryside, and above all the cooking of *sumanak* — a sweet paste boiled overnight from germinated wheat by women who sing *sumanak* songs around the cauldron. **Sada**, the pre-Islamic midwinter fire festival, has been revived as a state holiday in Tajikistan. **Mehrgon** marks the autumn harvest. Islamic feasts *Iydi Ramazon* (Eid al-Fitr) and *Iydi Qurbon* (Eid al-Adha) structure the religious year, and Shia and Ismaili Pamiris additionally observe Ashura and the Aga Khan's birthday. In Badakhshan, *chirogh-rawshan* — the "lamp-lighting" — is a three-day mourning rite in which sung Ismaili poetry accompanies the kindling of a wick in a bowl of flour to guide the soul. Life-cycle rites include the *gahvorabandon* cradling of a newborn on the fortieth day, the *sunnat-tuy* boys' circumcision feast, and the multi-day wedding cycle of *fotiha* (engagement), *non-shikanon* (bread-breaking), *nikoh* (contract), and the bride's ritual crossing of the threshold over fire.

## Foodways

The Tajik table is anchored by wheat bread — round *non* baked in a *tanur* clay oven and stamped with a *chekich* pricker — and by rice and mutton. The national dish is *osh* or *palov* (UNESCO 2016 for Tajik *osh palav* and separately for Uzbek *palov*), a one-pot rice cooked in a cast-iron *deg* with mutton, yellow carrots, onion, cumin, and often chickpeas or quince, served on a communal platter and eaten by hand from the right. Signature dishes include *qurutob* — the Tajik "national" dish of flaky *fatir* bread torn into a bowl and drenched in melted *qurut* (dried yogurt) with onions, tomatoes, and herbs; *mastova* rice-and-meat soup; *shurbo* mutton broth with whole vegetables; *manti* steamed dumplings; *sambusa* baked meat pastries; *laghmon* hand-pulled noodles; and *tushbera* small boiled dumplings in broth. Skewered *shashlik* is the bazaar staple. Dairy centers on *qurut*, *chakka* strained yogurt, and sheep's-milk butter. Melons, apricots, mulberries, and pomegranates from the Zarafshan and Vakhsh valleys are dried for winter. Green tea (*choi kabud*) in the north and black tea (*choi siyoh*) in the south are drunk continuously from *piyola* bowls, always poured for guests by the youngest present, with the first cup returned to the pot three times (*qaytarma*) to blend the brew.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tajik oral tradition sits on the Persian classical bedrock — Ferdowsi's *Shahnameh*, Rumi's *Masnavi*, and the ghazals of Hafez, Saadi, and the Bukhara-born Rudaki (regarded as the father of New Persian poetry and a founding Tajik cultural hero) are recited, sung, and quoted proverbially. The living epic tradition is carried by *gūrūghlī-khon* bards who sing the **Gurughli** cycle — hundreds of episodes about the hero Gurughli and his adopted son Avaz, ruling the utopian city of Chambuli Maston — to *dumbrak* accompaniment, especially in Kulob and Qarotegin. Shorter genres include *ruboi* quatrains, *bayt* couplets improvised in contests, tongue-twisters, riddles (*chiston*), and cycles of trickster tales around **Mullah Nasruddin** (*Afandi*), shared with the wider Turko-Persian world. Pamiri communities preserve their own sung devotional poetry (*maddoh* and *qasoid*) in Persian and in the Pamiri languages. Twentieth-century literary Tajik was defined by Sadriddin Ayni, whose memoirs and novels codified modern Tajik prose, and by the poet Mirzo Tursunzoda; post-independence writers have led a conscious revival of classical Persian vocabulary and of the Perso-Arabic script.

## Language & religion

Tajiki is a Western Iranian language of the Indo-European family, the eastern branch of New Persian, mutually intelligible with Iranian Farsi and Afghan Dari but written since the 1930s in a modified Cyrillic alphabet in Tajikistan and in Perso-Arabic in Afghanistan; a brief Latin script phase preceded Cyrillic. Alongside it, the Pamiri population speaks the Eastern Iranian Shughni, Rushani, Wakhi, Yazgulami, and Bartangi languages, and the Yaghnobi of the upper Zarafshan preserve a direct descendant of Sogdian. The great majority of Tajiks are Sunni Muslims of the Hanafi school, with the Pamiris following Nizari Ismaili Shia Islam under the Aga Khan and small Twelver pockets in Herat and elsewhere. Folk religion retains visible pre-Islamic Zoroastrian layers — Nowruz, Sada, fire-jumping, veneration of springs and sacred trees (*mazor*), the ritual role of the *bibi seshanbe* Tuesday women's rite — alongside Sufi shrine visitation to figures such as Bahauddin Naqshband near Bukhara.
