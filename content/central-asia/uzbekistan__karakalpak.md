## Overview

The Karakalpak are a Turkic people living in the delta wetlands of Uzbekistan, numbering about 800,000. They developed a distinctive culture blending steppe-nomadic yurt life with settled oasis crafts. Their material heritage—particularly women's dress, silver jewelry, and woven textiles—is among the most completely documented Central Asian traditions. They speak a Kipchak Turkic language and practice Sunni Islam mixed with older folk beliefs.

## Material culture

Karakalpak material culture integrates nomadic yurt traditions with oasis craftsmanship, centered on women's costume, silver jewelry, and patterned textiles.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Karakalpak weavers create patterned bands for yurts and dense embroidery for clothing using pile techniques and colorful silk thread.

- **yurt tension bands** (*aq baskur, qyzyl baskur*) — Wide woven bands in supplementary-weft pile that wrap the interior junction of the yurt's roof poles and wall, white or red with geometric motifs.
- **embroidery** (*shanshu*) — Dense silk chain-stitch and couched-cord work applied to dresses and ceremonial covers in carmine red, indigo, and yellow on dark wool.
- **Karakalpak yurt** (*aq oy*) — The 'white house,' whose felt covers, reed screens, and interior textiles form an integrated decorative program including individually wrapped reed patterns.
- **embroidered wall hanging** (*kupbet*) — A large hanging suspended behind the bride's bed, worked with radiating floral and horn motifs, inherited through the female line as part of the dowry.
- **floor spreads** (*aq jayma*) — Flat-woven cotton spreads with wide red and black stripes and small pile inserts at the ends.

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress is elaborate and heavily embroidered, with distinct garments marking marital status and life events, while men wear quilted coats and fur-trimmed hats.

- **everyday shift** (*koylek*) — A long straight-cut shift of red or dark cotton worn over trousers, embroidered at the cuffs and neckline.
- **married woman's headdress** (*kimeshek*) — A close-fitting white or dark cloth framing the face and falling to the shoulders, densely embroidered on the front panel.
- **unmarried girl's cap** (*taqiya*) — A small round cap worn by unmarried girls.
- **bridal coat-veil** (*jegde*) — A false-sleeved coat-veil worn over the head on the wedding day, shoulders loaded with silver plaques and coral.
- **men's quilted coat** (*shapan*) — A long quilted coat worn over a shirt and trousers, belted with leather and paired with a fur-trimmed or sheepskin winter hat.

### Architecture

The dominant dwelling is the Karakalpak yurt with a distinctive steep dome and interior reed screens, alongside mud-brick settled houses in delta villages.

- **Karakalpak yurt** (*aq oy*) — A trellis-tent with a wooden lattice wall of willow, roof poles meeting at a central smoke-ring, covered with felts and patterned bands, distinguished by a steeper dome than Kazakh yurts.
- **interior wainscot** (*shiy*) — Reed screens made of desert reeds individually bound in colored wool to build geometric patterns, used as an interior lining between lattice and felt.
- **settled house** (*tam, jay*) — A flat-roofed mud-brick house with a walled courtyard, raised sleeping platform, and summer kitchen built in delta villages.
- **carved yurt door** (*esik*) — Two-leafed wooden door painted red and incised with ram's-horn medallions.
- **medieval necropolis** (*Mizdakhkan*) — A surviving cemetery with domed brick tombs from the twelfth through nineteenth centuries.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Karakalpak crafts emphasize silver metalwork, carved woodwork, and practical ceramics, with the decorated wooden chest as a central household piece.

- **water vessels** (*ko'za*) — Unglazed pottery jars used for storing and carrying water.
- **fermentation churn** (*gubi*) — A tall unglazed churn for making fermented mare's-milk drink.
- **decorated storage chest** (*sandyq*) — A wooden chest with iron strapwork standing beside the bed in the yurt, whose decoration echoes embroidery motifs.
- **butter churn** (*chapchaq*) — A carved wooden tool used for churning dairy products.
- **saddle blanket** (*terlik*) — A felted blanket placed under saddles for riders.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Karakalpak silver jewelry is internationally recognized for high-purity silver, mercury gilding, and carnelian stones, made by hereditary jeweler families.

- **pectoral amulet** (*haykel*) — A large hollow rectangular ornament suspended on the chest, containing prayer papers.
- **triangular amulet-case** (*tumar*) — A small triangular pendant worn at the neck.
- **temple pendants** (*onshaki*) — A set of ten coin-like pendants attached to the kimeshek headdress.
- **hinged cuff bracelet** (*bilezik*) — A bracelet with granulated bosses, worn on the wrist.
- **forehead ornament** (*arebek*) — A decoration of coin-like disks worn on the forehead.

## Music & performance

The central musical tradition is the dastan epic sung by hereditary bards accompanying themselves on traditional stringed instruments.

- **heroic epic bard** (*jyrau*) — A hereditary bard who chants heroic epics to the accompaniment of the qobyz fiddle in an archaic, guttural style.
- **lyrical epic bard** (*baqsy*) — A hereditary singer who performs love epics accompanying himself on the dutar lute and traditionally served as a shaman-healer.
- **two-stringed fiddle** (*qobyz*) — A horsehair fiddle bowed vertically, used to accompany jyrau epic performance.
- **two-stringed lute** (*dutar*) — A long-necked lute used to accompany baqsy singers.
- **wedding antiphonal song** (*jar-jar*) — A song exchanged between the bride's and groom's parties during wedding celebrations.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is a minor tradition compared to music, with women's circle dances at weddings and men's mimetic solo dances, rather than court or masked theatre.

- **women's circle dances** — Circle dances performed by women at wedding celebrations.
- **mimetic solo dances** — Short solo dances by men imitating horsemanship or hunting movements.
- **mounted goat-carcass game** (*ulaq tartys*) — An athletic contest played on horseback with a goat carcass, cognate with Kyrgyz kok-boru.
- **wrestling** (*gures*) — A wrestling competition held at large gatherings and during Nowruz celebrations.

## Festivals & rituals

The calendar is anchored by Nowruz spring festival and Islamic holidays, with elaborate multi-day wedding rituals and Islamic-modified funeral rites.

- **spring new year** (*Nowruz, Nawryz*) — Celebrated on 21 March with the cooked-wheat dish sumelek, wrestling matches, and jyrau epic performances.
- **first plowing** (*Amal*) — A seasonal marker celebrating the beginning of spring agricultural work.
- **first mare's milking** (*Qymyz-muryndyq*) — A spring feast marking the start of fermented mare's-milk production.
- **cradle-placing ceremony** (*besikke salu*) — A ritual on the fortieth day after birth, placing the infant in the cradle.
- **cutting the tether** (*tusau kesu*) — A ceremony when an infant takes its first steps, symbolically cutting away a ritual tether.

## Foodways

Karakalpak food reflects the delta economy, featuring fish and mutton served over bread or noodles, fermented dairy products, and flatbread baked in clay ovens.

- **signature mutton dish** (*turama*) — Boiled mutton finely chopped over torn flatbread and moistened with broth, eaten communally from a shared platter.
- **shared meat-and-noodle dish** (*beshbarmaq*) — Boiled meat served over wide noodles, eaten at weddings and funerals.
- **sun-dried fish** (*qaqlangan baliq*) — Fish preserved by sun-drying, a delta specialty.
- **fermented mare's milk** (*qymyz*) — A prized dairy drink made by fermenting mare's milk, central to steppe diet and ritual.
- **clay oven** (*tandyr*) — A clay oven set into the courtyard, used for baking flatbread daily.

## Oral tradition & literature

Karakalpak literature is dominated by the dastan epic corpus, with the national epic Qyryq Qyz featuring a female warrior protagonist, and a literary canon anchored by the eighteenth-century poet Berdaq.

- **warrior epic with female hero** (*Qyryq Qyz*) — The national epic telling the story of the warrior Gulaim and her forty women defending the delta against Kalmyk invaders.
- **hero epic** (*Alpamys*) — A core epic shared with Uzbek and Kazakh traditions, with a distinct Karakalpak version.
- **genealogical verse** (*Shejire*) — A didactic poem by the poet Berdaq tracing Karakalpak genealogy and remaining the touchstone of the national literary canon.
- **proverb collection** (*maqal*) — Traditional sayings and wisdom collected during Soviet folklore campaigns.
- **riddle collection** (*jumbaq*) — Traditional riddles assembled during Soviet folklore campaigns.

## Language & religion

Karakalpak is a Kipchak Turkic language with northeastern and southwestern dialects; religion is Sunni Islam mixed with pre-Islamic veneration of ancestors and sacred places.

- **language** (*Karakalpak*) — A Kipchak Turkic language mutually intelligible with Kazakh and Nogai, written in Arabic script until the 1920s, then Latin and Cyrillic, now transitioning back to Latin.
- **Hanafi Islamic school** (*madhhab*) — The branch of Sunni Islam practiced by Karakalpak, adopted gradually from the tenth century.
- **ancestral spirit veneration** (*aruaq*) — A pre-Islamic folk practice honoring the spirits of ancestors, surviving in contemporary religious practice.
- **pilgrimage to holy place** (*ziyarat*) — Pilgrimage to sacred shrines like the Sultan Uwais Baba shrine, blending Islamic and folk practice.
- **protective amulet** (*tumar*) — A charm containing Qur'anic verses worn for protection, combining Islamic and folk belief.

- *kimeshek* — married woman's close-fitting embroidered headdress
