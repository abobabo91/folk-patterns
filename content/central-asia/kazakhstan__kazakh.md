## Overview

The Kazakhs are a Turkic people whose homeland stretches across the vast Kazakh Steppe from the Ural River in the west to the Tien Shan and Altai mountains in the east, and from the southern Siberian forest-steppe down to the Syr Darya and the Aral basin. Roughly 15 million ethnic Kazakhs live in the Republic of Kazakhstan, with substantial diaspora communities in Xinjiang (China), Bayan-Ölgii (western Mongolia), Russia, Uzbekistan, and Golestan (northern Iran). Their language, Kazakh, belongs to the Kipchak branch of the Turkic family. The Kazakhs consolidated as a distinct people in the 15th–16th centuries out of the fragmenting Ulus of Jochi, and their folk culture — felt textiles, mounted pastoralism, epic song, kumis-drinking, and the yurt — is the fullest surviving expression of Inner Asian steppe nomadism.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Kazakh textile world is built on wool, felt, and the mobile household of the yurt. Almost every documented pattern-bearing textile is either a felt floor covering, a wall hanging, or a woven band that structures the yurt itself.

**_Syrmak_** — a mosaic felt rug built by cutting contrasting sheets of colored felt and stitching the pieces edge-to-edge so that positive and negative shapes interlock; the joins are then couched over with cord. Kazakh *syrmak* tends toward large, sweeping horn-motif fields on a single ground color, distinguishing it from denser Kyrgyz work.

**_Shyrdak_** — the inlaid/appliqued felt floor covering shared with the Kyrgyz and inscribed on UNESCO's Urgent Safeguarding list; Kazakh *shyrdak* uses the same cut-and-fit technique as *syrmak* but with bolder, fewer motifs per panel.

**_Tuskiiz_** (*tus kiiz*) — an embroidered wall hanging placed on the lattice wall of the yurt behind the seat of honor; typically a red or black velvet or wool ground with a U-shaped border of chain-stitch and couched-cord floral and horn motifs framing a plain central field.

**_Koshma_** — plain pressed felt, the base material of the entire yurt covering and of the rugs above; made by rolling wet, carded wool inside a reed mat (*chiy*) with rhythmic pressure, a communal women's task.

**Yurt bands (*bau, basqur, aq baskur*)** — long tablet-woven or warp-faced woven bands of dyed wool that tie the wooden lattice (*kerege*) to the roof poles (*uyq*) and finish the interior seam where wall meets roof; the *aq baskur* is the wide white patterned band that runs around the entire interior.

**_Alasha_** — narrow strip-woven wool rugs sewn edge-to-edge into a larger floor covering, patterned in bands of geometric ornament; distinctively steppe-Kazakh rather than urban Central Asian.

**Kazakh applique and felt** — beyond *syrmak*, Kazakh women work reverse-appliqued *tekemet* (pressed-in-pattern felt, where dyed wool motifs are laid on the *chiy* mat before rolling so the design becomes integral to the felt itself).

**Motif vocabulary.** *Qoshqar müyiz* (ram's horns, the master motif), *qos müyiz* (double horns), *ömirjaq* (life-vertebra), *qusmurun* (bird's beak), *it izi* (dog's trace), *tumarsha* (small amulet/triangle), *balдaq* (crutch/T-form), *aidar* (crown), *sunaq* (running-water meander), *tört qulaq* (four-eared cross).

### Clothing & dress

Everyday Kazakh dress is built around the *koilek* (a long tunic-shirt worn by both sexes, women's versions gathered and long-sleeved), loose trousers (*shalbar*), and the *chapan* — a quilted or padded long coat, often of striped silk or wool broadcloth, belted at the waist. Men wear the *chapan* over the *koilek* with a leather or embroidered belt (*kemer*) hung with knife and pouch, and boots (*etik*) with a high heel and soft, upturned toe suited to the stirrup. Winter dress adds sheepskin *ton* and the *malakhai*, a fur-trimmed hat with long ear flaps and a back tail. Men's summer headgear includes the felt *kalpak* and the embroidered skullcap *takiya*. Married women wear the *kimeshek*, a white cotton hood-and-bib that covers the head, neck, and shoulders, with regional variations in the shape of the crown and the length of the back panel. The great ceremonial garment is the bridal *saukele*: a tall conical headdress on a felt or leather frame, mounted with silver plaques, coral, turquoise, and pearl, and trailing long side pendants of gold thread and feathers — the most expensive single item in a traditional trousseau.

### Architecture

The Kazakh vernacular building is the *kiiz üy* ("felt house"), the fully collapsible steppe yurt. Its skeleton is a lattice of bent willow (*kerege*) tied into a cylindrical wall, topped by radiating roof poles (*uyq*) that plug into a wooden crown-wheel (*shanyraq*) — the smoke hole, and the most sacred object of the household, passed from father to youngest son. The frame is covered in layers of *koshma* felt and lashed with the patterned *bau* and *baskur* bands. Interior space is strictly ordered: the door faces south or east, the honored seat (*tör*) is opposite the door, men's gear (saddles, weapons) is on the right, women's (chests, cradle, kitchen) on the left. The *shanyraq* appears on Kazakhstan's state emblem. Beyond the yurt, the steppe is dotted with masonry funerary architecture — the *mazar* and *kesene*, domed or pyramid-roofed tomb-shrines of fired brick or stone, often the only permanent structures for hundreds of kilometers. Sedentary Kazakh villages in the south (around Turkestan and the Syr Darya) use mudbrick courtyard houses under the strong influence of settled Central Asian architecture.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Because the Kazakh household was mobile, its objects favor leather, wood, and metal over pottery. The *torsyk* and *saba* are stitched-leather vessels for fermenting and carrying *kumis*, decorated with stamped and embossed ornament. Wooden bowls (*tostagan*), ladles (*ozhau*), and the great mare-milk churning stick (*pispek*) are turned or carved from birch and juniper. Painted and carved wooden chests (*sandyq*) and low tables (*dastarkhan*) carry the same horn-motif vocabulary as the felts. Metalwork centers on horse gear — silver-mounted bridles, stamped and gilded saddle plates, iron stirrups — and on weapons: the curved sabre (*qylysh*), the whip (*qamshy*), and the eagle-hunter's leather glove and hood used with the golden eagle (*berkut*). Kazakh silversmiths (*zerger*) work in filigree, granulation, and niello, often with turquoise, carnelian, and coral cabochons.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Kazakh women's jewelry is worn in massive, matched sets that both display wealth and function as amulets. The *tumar* is a triangular or cylindrical silver amulet-case worn on the chest, containing a Qur'anic verse or protective substance. The *bilezik* is a wide silver cuff bracelet, often worn in pairs and joined by chains to finger-rings (*saqina*). Long chest ornaments (*oniryk*), temple pendants (*shekelik*) that hang from the *saukele* or *kimeshek*, and heavy earrings (*syrga*) complete the ensemble. Girls wear a single braid, married women two; hair ornaments (*shashbau*) of silver plaques and coral hang from the braid ends. Belts (*kise belbeu*) worn by men are studded with silver plaques and carry a knife, flint, and tobacco pouch. Body tattoos are not part of Kazakh tradition, and henna use is minimal compared to sedentary Central Asian neighbors.

## Music & performance

Kazakh music divides between instrumental composition and sung poetry. The signature instrument is the *dombra*, a two-stringed long-necked lute plucked with the fingers; solo instrumental pieces for the *dombra* are called ***kui***, a genre inscribed by UNESCO, whose short programmatic compositions each carry a narrative title and legend (of a horse, a battle, a lost love). The other great instrument is the ***kobyz***, an archaic two-stringed horsehair fiddle held upright between the knees, historically the instrument of the shaman (*baqsy*) and of the epic reciter; it too is on the UNESCO list. Additional instruments include the *sybyzgy* (end-blown flute), the *sherter* (smaller plucked lute), and the *jetigen* (seven-string zither). Vocal traditions are dominated by the ***akyn*** — the improvising bard-poet, whose competitive song-duel *aitys* pits two *akyns* against each other in extemporized verse to *dombra* accompaniment; *akyn* art is on the UNESCO Representative List. Long narrative singers are called *jyrau* (composers of *tolgau*, philosophical-political verse) and *zhyrshy* (reciters of epic). Wedding song cycles include the bride's lament *sıŋsu* and the groom's-side welcome *bet ashar* ("face-opening").

## Dance & theatre

Kazakhs have no tradition of masked or scripted theatre comparable to the sedentary Central Asian *maskharaboz* or the Uyghur *meshrep*; performance culture is centered on the *aitys* song-duel and on solo instrumental *kui* recital, both of which are theatrical in delivery. Dance in the strict sense is a modern stage tradition: pieces such as *Kara Zhorga* (a horse-imitating shoulder-and-neck dance) and *Kamazhai* (a women's shoulder-shrug dance) circulate today as choreographed folk numbers, developed largely in Soviet-era ensembles from steppe movement vocabulary. Ceremonial performance is instead embedded in life-cycle rites — the wedding *bet ashar*, the *baqsy*'s trance ritual with the *kobyz*, and the mounted games (see below), which function as public spectacle.

## Festivals & rituals

The great annual festival is ***Nauryz*** (**Nowruz**, 21–22 March), the pre-Islamic Iranian-Turkic spring new year inscribed by UNESCO, marked by the ritual dish *nauryz köje*, house cleaning, reconciliation between neighbors, and public games. Spring is also the season of the horse-breeder's calendar rites — **traditional spring festive rites of the Kazakh horse breeders**, a UNESCO-listed complex covering the first milking of mares, the branding of foals, and the tying of the first *kumis*-churn — which mark the transition from winter camp to summer pasture. Life-cycle rites are elaborate: the fortieth-day *qyrqynan shygaru* bath for a newborn, the first-steps *tusau kesu* ceremony where a child's symbolic hobble is cut, the coming-of-age *sundet toi* (circumcision feast), the multi-stage wedding (*qyz uzatu* at the bride's home, *kelin tüsiru* at the groom's, closed by the *bet ashar*), and the funeral cycle with its seventh- and fortieth-day and one-year commemorations (*jetisi, qyrqy, jyldyq*). Steppe games at weddings and festivals include ***Kazakh kuresi*** (belt wrestling, UNESCO-listed), the mounted goat-carcass tug-of-war *kokpar*, the courtship race *kyz kuu* ("chase the girl"), and the traditional children's astragalus game ***Kazakh assyk*** (UNESCO-listed). The board game ***togyz kumalak*** (nine-pebbles mancala, UNESCO-listed) is played year-round.

## Foodways

Kazakh cuisine is a meat-and-dairy cuisine of the pastoralist, with horse and mutton at its center and grain products in supporting roles. The national dish is *beshbarmak* ("five fingers") — boiled horsemeat or mutton served over wide flat noodles (*kespe*) with onion broth, eaten by hand from a communal platter; the honored guest receives the boiled sheep's head (*bas*) and distributes cuts to others by rank. Cured horse products are prestige foods: *qazy* (horse-rib sausage in the intestine), *shuzhyq*, *jal* (neck fat), and *jaya* (rump). Dairy centers on ***kumis*** (*qymyz*), the lightly alcoholic fermented mare's milk churned in a leather *saba*, inscribed by UNESCO; also *shubat* (fermented camel's milk), *ayran*, *qatyq*, and the dried curd balls *kurt* and *irimshik* that travel with herders. Breads are baked flat: *shelpek*, *taba nan*, and the fried-dough *baursaq* served in a heap at every celebration; Kazakh participation in the multinational UNESCO inscription **flatbread making and sharing (Lavash, Katyrma, Jupka, Yufka)** covers the *katyrma* tradition. Tea — black, strong, and drunk with milk and salt or with cream — is served at the low *dastarkhan* table throughout the day and opens every meal.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Kazakh oral canon rests on the *jyr*, the long sung epic performed by a *zhyrshy* to *dombra* or *kobyz* accompaniment. Core heroic epics include ***Alpamys Batyr***, ***Qobylandy Batyr***, and ***Er Targyn***; the lyric-romantic cycle includes ***Qyz Zhibek*** and ***Kozy Korpesh — Bayan Sulu***. The **Book of Dede Korkut**, the shared Oghuz epic inscribed by UNESCO, is claimed within the broader Turkic heritage though it belongs more directly to the Oghuz line. Alongside epic sits the philosophical *tolgau* verse of the *jyrau* — Asan Qaigy, Bukhar-jyrau — and the improvised *aitys* duels of the *akyns*. Prose genres include the trickster tales of Aldar Kose, the wise-fool anecdotes of Zhirenshe Sheshen, and a dense body of proverbs (*maqal-mätel*). The 19th-century poet, composer, and philosopher **Abai Qunanbayuly** (1845–1904) — whose *Book of Words* (*Qara Sözder*) crystallized modern literary Kazakh — is the foundational figure of the written tradition, followed by the ethnographer-scholar **Shoqan Walikhanov**. Post-independence Kazakhstan has invested heavily in re-recording *akyn* and *jyrau* repertoire and in staging *aitys* on national television.

## Language & religion

Kazakh is a Kipchak (Northwestern) Turkic language, closely related to Kyrgyz, Karakalpak, and Nogai. It has been written in Arabic script (into the 1920s), Latin (1929–1940), Cyrillic (1940–present in Kazakhstan), and is currently transitioning back to a reformed Latin alphabet. Kazakhs are overwhelmingly Sunni Muslims of the Hanafi school, converted gradually from the 14th century onward under the influence of Sufi missionaries — the shrine of Khoja Ahmed Yasawi at Turkestan, a 14th-century Timurid mausoleum, remains the most important pilgrimage site. Folk Islam is strongly interwoven with pre-Islamic steppe practice: veneration of ancestral spirits (*aruaq*), the sacred status of the yurt's *shanyraq* and the hearth, healing and divination by the *baqsy* shaman with the *kobyz*, and the marking of graves with *mazar* and horse-tail standards.
