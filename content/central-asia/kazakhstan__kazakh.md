## Overview

The Kazakhs are a Turkic people of the Central Asian steppe who consolidated as a distinct group in the 15th–16th centuries. Their folk culture is built on the yurt, felt textiles, mounted pastoralism, epic song, and kumis-drinking. The Kazakh household was mobile, shaped by the horse and sheep; their arts emphasize decorative metalwork, intricate felts with horn-motif patterns, and a rich tradition of sung poetry and instrumental performance. Folk Islam blends Qur'anic practice with older steppe veneration of ancestors and sacred objects.

## Material culture

Kazakh material culture favors leather, wood, metal, and felt over pottery, built around the mobile yurt household and its decorated furnishings.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Kazakh textiles are felt and wool, nearly all patterned, designed for the yurt and its furnishings.

- **Syrmak** — A mosaic felt rug made by cutting contrasting colored felts and stitching the pieces edge-to-edge with horn-motif fields, then couching the seams with cord.
- **Shyrdak** — An inlaid felt floor covering with bold, few motifs per panel, using the same cut-and-fit technique as syrmak.
- **Tuskiiz** — An embroidered wall hanging hung on the yurt's lattice wall behind the seat of honor, typically red or black velvet with a U-shaped border of floral and horn motifs framing a plain central field.
- **Koshma** — Plain pressed felt made by rolling wet carded wool inside a reed mat with rhythmic pressure, the base material of the entire yurt covering.
- **Yurt bands** (*bau, basqur, aq baskur*) — Long tablet-woven or warp-faced bands of dyed wool that tie the wooden lattice to the roof poles and finish interior seams, with the aq baskur running around the entire interior as a wide white patterned band.

### Clothing & dress

Kazakh dress centers on the long tunic-shirt, loose trousers, and quilted coat, with distinct items for men, women, married status, and ceremony.

- **Koilek** — A long tunic-shirt worn by both men and women, with women's versions gathered and long-sleeved.
- **Chapan** — A quilted or padded long coat of striped silk or wool broadcloth, belted at the waist and worn by men over the koilek.
- **Kimeshek** — A white cotton hood-and-bib worn by married women, covering the head, neck, and shoulders, with regional variations in crown shape and back-panel length.
- **Saukele** — A tall conical ceremonial headdress on a felt or leather frame, mounted with silver plaques, coral, turquoise, and pearl, with long side pendants of gold thread and feathers—the most expensive item in a traditional trousseau.
- **Kalpak** — A felt hat worn by men in summer, or the malakhai—a fur-trimmed hat with long ear flaps and a back tail worn in winter.

### Architecture

The Kazakh vernacular building is the kiiz üy, a fully collapsible steppe yurt with a sacred wooden crown at its center.

- **Yurt** (*kiiz üy*) — A felt house with a lattice willow skeleton topped by radiating roof poles plugged into a wooden crown-wheel, the frame covered in layers of felt and lashed with patterned bands.
- **Shanyraq** — The wooden crown-wheel at the yurt's peak that serves as the smoke hole and the most sacred object of the household, passed from father to youngest son.
- **Kerege** — The lattice of bent willow that forms the yurt's cylindrical wall.
- **Mazar** — A domed or pyramid-roofed tomb-shrine of fired brick or stone, often the only permanent structure for hundreds of kilometers on the steppe.
- **Kesene** — A masonry funerary structure, similar to the mazar, dotting the steppe as tomb-shrines.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Because the Kazakh household was mobile, objects favor leather, wood, and metal; decorated leather vessels for kumis, turned wooden bowls and chests, and fine metalwork for horse gear.

- **Torsyk** — A stitched-leather vessel for fermenting and carrying kumis, decorated with stamped and embossed ornament.
- **Saba** — A leather vessel for fermenting and carrying kumis, decorated with stamped and embossed patterns.
- **Tostagan** — A wooden bowl turned or carved from birch and juniper wood.
- **Sandyq** — A painted and carved wooden chest carrying the same horn-motif vocabulary as the felts.
- **Qylysh** — A curved sabre, part of the metalwork tradition that also includes silver-mounted bridles and stamped saddle plates.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Kazakh women wear massive matched sets of jewelry in silver and semi-precious stone, serving both as wealth display and as protective amulets.

- **Tumar** — A triangular or cylindrical silver amulet-case worn on the chest, containing a Qur'anic verse or protective substance.
- **Bilezik** — A wide silver cuff bracelet, often worn in pairs and joined by chains to finger-rings.
- **Syrga** — Heavy earrings worn as part of the jewelry ensemble.
- **Shekelik** — Temple pendants that hang from the saukele or kimeshek, part of a woman's ceremonial jewelry set.
- **Shashbau** — Silver plaques and coral ornaments that hang from the braid ends of married women's hair.

## Music & performance

Kazakh music divides between instrumental compositions and sung poetry, centered on the dombra and the improvising bard.

- **Kui** — A solo instrumental piece for the dombra, a short programmatic composition with a narrative title and legend (of a horse, battle, or lost love).
- **Dombra** — A two-stringed long-necked lute plucked with the fingers, the signature Kazakh instrument.
- **Kobyz** — An archaic two-stringed horsehair fiddle held upright between the knees, historically the instrument of shamans and epic reciters.
- **Akyn** — An improvising bard-poet who performs in the aitys song-duel, extemporizing verse to dombra accompaniment.
- **Aitys** — A competitive song-duel where two akyns pit extemporized verse against each other to dombra accompaniment.

## Dance & theatre

Kazakhs have no masked theatre tradition; performance is centered on the aitys song-duel and solo instrumental recital, with modern choreographed dances developed from steppe movement.

- **Kara Zhorga** — A horse-imitating shoulder-and-neck dance, choreographed as a folk number in modern stage traditions.
- **Kamazhai** — A women's shoulder-shrug dance, developed in Soviet-era ensembles as a choreographed folk number.

## Festivals & rituals

Kazakhs mark the spring new year Nauryz, life-cycle ceremonies from birth to death, and competitive games at weddings and festivals.

- **Nauryz** — The pre-Islamic Iranian-Turkic spring new year (21–22 March), marked by the ritual dish nauryz köje, house cleaning, reconciliation, and public games.
- **Kazakh kuresi** (*kuresi*) — Belt wrestling, a traditional steppe game performed at weddings and festivals.
- **Kokpar** — A mounted goat-carcass tug-of-war, a traditional steppe game.
- **Kyz kuu** — A courtship race meaning 'chase the girl,' a traditional steppe game at weddings and festivals.
- **Kazakh assyk** (*assyk*) — A traditional children's game using astragalus bones, played year-round.

## Foodways

Kazakh cuisine is a meat-and-dairy pastoralist's food, centered on horse and mutton, with fermented mare's milk as the national beverage.

- **Beshbarmak** — The national dish—boiled horsemeat or mutton served over wide flat noodles with onion broth, eaten by hand from a communal platter.
- **Kumis** — Lightly alcoholic fermented mare's milk churned in a leather saba, a prestige dairy product and the national beverage.
- **Qazy** — Horse-rib sausage in the intestine, a cured horse product eaten as a prestige food.
- **Kurt** — Dried curd balls that travel with herders as portable nutrition.
- **Baursaq** — Fried dough served in a heap at every celebration.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Kazakh oral canon rests on the jyr, the long sung epic, and the philosophical verse and improvised duels of the akyns and jyrau.

- **Alpamys Batyr** — A core Kazakh heroic epic performed by a zhyrshy to dombra or kobyz accompaniment.
- **Qyz Zhibek** — A lyric-romantic epic cycle in the Kazakh oral canon.
- **Kozy Korpesh — Bayan Sulu** — A lyric-romantic epic cycle in the Kazakh oral canon.
- **Abai Qunanbayuly** (*Abai*) — A 19th-century poet, composer, and philosopher (1845–1904) whose Book of Words crystallized modern literary Kazakh.
- **Tolgau** — Philosophical-political verse composed by the jyrau, a branch of the oral literary tradition.

## Language & religion

Kazakh is a Kipchak Turkic language written today in Cyrillic and transitioning to Latin; Kazakhs are Sunni Muslims who blend Islamic practice with steppe veneration of ancestors.

- **Khoja Ahmed Yasawi** — A 14th-century Timurid shrine at Turkestan, the most important pilgrimage site in Kazakh Islam.
- **Baqsy** — A shaman who performs healing and divination with the kobyz, blending Islamic and pre-Islamic steppe practice.
- **Aruaq** — Ancestral spirits venerated in folk practice, a pre-Islamic steppe tradition blended with Sunni Islam.
