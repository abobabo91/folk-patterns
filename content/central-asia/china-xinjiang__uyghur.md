## Overview

The Uyghurs are a Turkic-speaking, predominantly Sunni Muslim people of the Tarim Basin and Dzungaria in the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region of northwest China, with substantial diaspora communities in Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan, and Turkey. Approximately 12 million live in Xinjiang, concentrated in the oasis cities that ring the Taklamakan Desert — Kashgar, Hotan (Khotan), Yarkand, Aksu, Turpan, and Kucha — and along the Ili valley in the north. They speak Uyghur, a Karluk Turkic language written today in an Arabic-derived script. Their folk culture is a distinctive Silk Road synthesis: sedentary oasis agriculture and long-distance caravan trade produced a material vocabulary — ikat silk, knotted-pile carpets, muqam suites, embroidered caps — that draws on Persian, Central Asian, Chinese, and Indic threads while remaining unmistakably its own. The old identifier *yerlik* ("local") captures how communities long defined themselves by oasis before "Uyghur" was adopted as a common ethnonym at the 1921 Tashkent conference.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Atlas silk (*atlas*)** — A warp-ikat silk woven principally in Hotan and around Yarkand, in which the warp threads are bundled, tied, and dyed before weaving so the pattern emerges as the loom is dressed. Uyghur atlas is distinguished from Uzbek Fergana ikat by its softer, more liquid edges and its preference for asymmetric, splashy compositions built from a limited palette of black, red, yellow, blue, and green.

**Khan-atlas (*khan-atlas*)** — The "royal" or highest grade of atlas, historically reserved for wealthier households and bridal wear; it uses finer silk, more colours per bolt, and more elaborate pattern repeats than everyday atlas.

**Uyghur ikat** — Beyond atlas proper, Uyghur weavers produce warp-ikat cottons and mixed cotton-silk *adras*-type cloth for lining, lightweight robes, and cushion covers.

**Hotan carpet (*Khotan gilam*)** — Hand-knotted wool pile carpets from Hotan and its satellite villages, using asymmetric knots on a cotton foundation. The classic Hotan design has a central medallion, pomegranate-and-vine borders, and a distinctive palette of madder red, indigo, and undyed camel-wool ivory. Nineteenth-century Hotan carpets often carry Chinese-influenced fret borders that mark them off from Persian or Turkmen work.

**Uyghur *doppa*** — The four-panelled square skullcap, embroidered in silk or metal thread on a black cotton or velvet ground. The Kashgar *badam doppa* ("almond cap"), covered in white almond (paisley-shaped) motifs on black, is the most widely recognised form; Hotan, Turpan, Ghulja, and Kucha each have their own colour codes and stitch traditions.

**Uyghur embroidery (*kashta*)** — Domestic embroidery on pillow ends, wall hangings (*chapan* and *bashliq* linings), tablecloths, and prayer mats, worked in chain stitch, satin stitch, and couched metal thread. Motifs cluster around fruit, blossom, and geometric medallions.

**Felted rugs and saddle covers** — Pressed-wool *kigiz* felts, less pile-heavy than carpet work, used for floor covering, sleeping surfaces, and horse gear.

**Motif vocabulary.** *badam* (almond/paisley), *anar* (pomegranate — fertility), *gül* (rose/generic flower), *chinar* (plane-tree leaf), *tumarcha* (small amulet-triangle), *ay* (crescent moon), *yultuz* (star), *qoshqar müngüz* (ram's horn), *daraxt* (tree-of-life), *su* (running-water wave border).

### Clothing & dress

Women's daily dress centres on a long-sleeved dress (*köynek*) worn over trousers, often cut from atlas silk for festive occasions and cotton print for daily wear; older women add a long velvet or wool *chapan* coat. Married women historically wore a head covering — a light scarf (*rumal*) in the south, a heavier shawl in Ili — and in the past a horsehair or dark cotton face veil in some Kashgar and Yarkand households. Men wear a long belted *chapan* over a shirt and loose trousers tucked into leather boots (*ötük*), with a leather or embroidered belt (*bäl-baghliq*). The *doppa* skullcap is worn year-round by men and boys and by many women, either alone or under a scarf; its regional variant identifies the wearer's oasis. Bridal dress layers a bright khan-atlas dress with a heavily embroidered coat, silver jewellery, and a red or gold headscarf; the groom wears a fine *chapan*, white shirt, and an embroidered doppa. Felt boots and quilted overcoats (*postin*) handle Taklamakan winters.

### Architecture

The classic Uyghur oasis house is a single-storey mud-brick or rammed-earth structure organised around an interior courtyard, with a flat roof used for drying apricots, mulberries, and grain. Rooms open onto a covered verandah (*aywan*) supported by carved wooden posts, and living rooms are dominated by a raised platform (*supa*) covered in felts and carpets on which the household eats, sits, and sleeps. Interior walls carry plaster niches (*taqcha*) for lamps, teapots, and Qur'ans, and the best rooms have carved and painted wooden ceilings. In Kashgar's old town and in Turpan, houses cluster into dense lanes with overhanging upper storeys; in Turpan the courtyards are shaded by grape trellises fed by the *karez* underground irrigation channels. Religious architecture is anchored by the mosque and shrine (*mazar*): the **Id Kah Mosque** in Kashgar (yellow-tiled portal, timber hypostyle hall), the **Emin Minaret** at Turpan (a tapered brick tower patterned with brickwork lozenges and rhomboids), and the tomb complex of the Afaq Khoja outside Kashgar exemplify the local idiom — glazed tilework in turquoise and green, muqarnas-style plaster, and brick geometry rather than the full Persian iwan.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Kashgar and Yarkand potters produce glazed earthenware — bowls, plates, and *aptawa* ewers — with green, yellow, and turquoise lead glazes and incised or slip-painted floral motifs, distinct in palette from Uzbek Rishtan blue-and-white. Coppersmithing survives in Kashgar's coppersmith bazaar, turning out hammered basins, long-spouted water ewers, samovars, and trays. Woodworkers in Yarkand and Kucha carve doors, Qur'an stands (*lauh*), musical-instrument bodies, and cradles (*böshük*); poplar and mulberry are the standard timbers. Leather workers produce riding boots, saddle skirts, and knife sheaths, and the Yengisar knife (*pichaq*) — a slender single-edged blade with a horn, brass, or bone handle inlaid with coloured mastic — is a signature male accessory. Household staples include the *choghan* (cast-iron cooking pot), the wood-fired *tonur* (clay oven) for naan, and lacquered wooden bowls for tea.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver dominates: married women wear hoop or pendant earrings (*halqa*), multiple silver bangles (*bilähzük*), and long silver-and-coral necklaces. Bridal sets add a silver forehead ornament and temple pendants worked with turquoise, carnelian, and freshwater pearl, often filigreed. Amulet cases (*tumar*), triangular or cylindrical and hung on a cord or belt, hold folded prayers or Qur'anic verses; their triangular form recurs across the textile motif vocabulary. Henna (*hena*) is applied to bride's palms and fingertips the night before a wedding. Uyghur women historically pencilled the brows into a single line joined over the nose using the juice of the *osma* plant (a mark of adult female beauty), and hair is worn in multiple long braids (up to forty for a bride), sometimes weighted with silver terminal ornaments.

## Music & performance

The core Uyghur classical tradition is the **On Ikki Muqam** ("Twelve Muqams") — twelve extended suites of sung poetry, instrumental interludes, and dance movements, each named for its mode (Rak, Chäbbayat, Mushawräk, Nawa, and so on). A full suite unfolds in three large sections — *chong näghmä* (the great song), *dastan* (narrative songs), and *mäshräp* (fast dance songs) — and can run several hours; UNESCO inscribed **Uyghur Muqam of Xinjiang** on the Representative List in 2008. The lead melodic instruments are the long-necked lutes *dutar* (two-stringed, soft and domestic) and *tämbür* (long, wire-strung, brilliant), the short lute *rawap*, the spike fiddle *ghijäk*, the plucked/hammered zither *qalun*, the hammered dulcimer *chang*, and the transverse flute *nay*. Rhythm is carried by the frame drum *dap* and paired kettledrums *naghra* with the shawm *sunay* — the *naghra-sunay* pair announces weddings, circumcisions, and outdoor festivals from rooftops and gate towers. Song genres beyond muqam include the narrative *dastan* (long sung tales such as *Gherip-Sänäm* and *Tahir-Zöhrä*), lyric *qoshaq*, and the sociable songs of the *meshrep* gathering. Regional muqam traditions — Dolan muqam of the Yarkand river reaches, Turpan muqam, Qumul muqam — coexist with the classical Twelve.

## Dance & theatre

Uyghur dance is soloistic, virtuosic, and centred on head, wrist, and shoulder articulation over rapid footwork. **Sanam** is the everyday social dance, opening most gatherings, with couples and soloists entering and leaving the circle. **Dolan dance**, from the Dolan communities of the Mäkit and Awat districts, is more ecstatic — paired hunters mime the stalking of a deer to Dolan muqam accompaniment. Set-piece dances include the **plate dance** (*chinä ussuli*, balancing porcelain bowls on the head and hands), the **lamp dance** with oil lamps on the palms and crown, and the humorous **Näzirköm** couple dance of the Turpan basin. Court-derived solo dances survive in staged muqam performance. The **Meshrep** (UNESCO Urgent Safeguarding List, 2010) is not a dance in itself but the framing men's social gathering — food, music, sung poetry, dance, moral adjudication, and comic sketches — inside which much of the folk repertoire is transmitted.

## Festivals & rituals

The festival year follows the Islamic lunar calendar for **Roza Héyt** (Eid al-Fitr, ending Ramadan fasting) and **Qurban Héyt** (Eid al-Adha, the sacrifice feast, the largest gathering of the year, marked by mass prayer at the Id Kah and household mutton feasts). The solar new year **Noruz** on 21 March is celebrated with spring foods, outdoor meshrep, and shrine visits in some districts, though its prominence varies. Harvest gatherings cluster around the grape and melon crop in Turpan in late summer. Pilgrimage to Sufi shrines (*mazar*) — most famously the Ordam Padishah shrine in the desert south of Yengisar and the Imam Asim shrine near Hotan — combined devotional visiting, storytelling, and trade, though state restrictions have curtailed the largest gatherings. Life-cycle rites: at birth, the child is placed in a carved cradle (*böshük toy*) in a ceremony forty days on; boys are circumcised (*khätnä toy*) with a large feast between roughly five and seven years; weddings (*toy*) span multiple days with the *nikah* religious ceremony, the bride's procession in atlas silk, and closing meshrep; funerals follow Sunni Muslim practice with three-day, seven-day, and fortieth-day (*qiriq*) commemorations.

## Foodways

Wheat is the staple: **naan** (*nan*) is baked daily against the walls of the *tonur* clay oven, ranging from thin *toqach* to the thick, seed-crusted *girdä*. Hand-pulled noodles (**laghman**) are served with a stir-fried mutton, tomato, pepper, and celery sauce; fried noodles are **suoman**. **Polo** (*polu*), the Uyghur pilaf, layers rice with mutton, carrot batons, onion, and cumin, and appears at every wedding and Eid. **Kawap** (skewered mutton kebab, cumin- and chilli-dusted) is the market street food; **samsa** are lamb-and-onion pastries baked stuck to the tonur wall. **Manta** are steamed lamb dumplings; **chüchürä** are small boiled dumplings in broth. **Göshnan** is a large meat-stuffed griddle bread. Melons and grapes of Turpan and Hami, mulberries, apricots, pomegranates, and walnuts figure heavily; fruit is dried in flat trays and in the pierced brick drying towers (*chunche*) of the Turpan basin. Tea is drunk constantly — brick tea boiled with milk and salt in the north, plain green or spiced *dora chay* with cardamom, saffron, rose, and pepper in the south, always served with naan and dried fruit. All food follows halal rules; pork is absent, alcohol restricted though a fermented mulberry drink (*müsälläs*) is traditional in some districts.

## Oral tradition & literature

The great narrative songs — *dastan* — form the backbone of oral literature: **Gherip-Sänäm**, **Tahir-Zöhrä**, **Yüsüp-Ehmäd**, and **Chin Tömür Batur** are performed both by professional bards accompanying themselves on dutar or rawap and within meshrep gatherings. The classical Chagatai literary heritage supplies the shared canon: the eleventh-century **Kutadgu Bilig** ("Wisdom of Royal Glory") of Yusuf Khass Hajib of Balasaghun and the roughly contemporary **Divan-i Lughat-it-Türk** of Mahmud al-Kashgari — the first comparative dictionary of the Turkic languages — are both associated with the Kara-Khanid cultural world of Kashgar. Sufi poetry, proverbs (*maqal*), riddles (*tepishmaq*), and children's counting rhymes circulate domestically. Twentieth-century writers such as Abduxaliq Uyghur and Lutpulla Mutellip reworked folk forms into modern verse. The muqam suites carry classical *ghazal* poetry — Nawa'i, Fuzuli, Meshrep — into sung performance, so the boundary between "literature" and "music" is porous.

## Language & religion

Uyghur is a Karluk Turkic language, closely related to Uzbek, with major dialect zones in the central Tarim (Kashgar–Yarkand–Hotan), the east (Turpan–Kumul), and the north (Ili). It has been written in Old Uyghur script (Manichaean/Buddhist period), then in the Perso-Arabic script from the Islamic conversion onward; a Cyrillic script and a Latin "New Script" (Yengi Yeziq) were tried in the twentieth century, and today an Arabic-script orthography (Uyghur Ereb Yëziqi) is standard in Xinjiang alongside a Latin form used online. Religion is overwhelmingly Sunni Islam of the Hanafi school, layered over pre-Islamic Buddhist, Manichaean, and Nestorian Christian pasts still visible in shrine geography and cave-temple sites near Turpan and Kucha. Sufi lodges (Naqshbandi, Qadiri) shaped shrine pilgrimage, healing practice, and the ecstatic *zikr* traditions embedded in some meshrep and muqam performance.
