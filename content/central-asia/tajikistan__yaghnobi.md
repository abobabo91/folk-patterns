## Overview

The Yaghnobi are an Eastern Iranian people residing in Tajikistan’s Sughd region, especially in the valleys of the Yaghnob, Qul, and Varzob rivers. They also live in and around the Amu Darya River, the Yaghnob River, the Yaghnob Valley, the Qul River, the Varzob rivers, and the town of Anzob. Their principal language is Yaghnobi, an Eastern Iranian language considered a direct descendant of Sogdian and sometimes called Neo-Sogdian. The estimated number of Yaghnobis is approximately 25,000, while the Yaghnobi language article gives some 12,500 speakers divided among several communities. The Yaghnobi are regarded as descendants of Sogdian-speaking peoples who once inhabited much of Central Asia beyond the Amu Darya River in ancient Sogdia. Their folk-cultural record in the supplied sources concerns agriculture, animal breeding, weaving, earthenware, language, settlement history, and religious continuity.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Weaving* is named among the traditional handicrafts of the Yaghnobi. The sources state that men mostly did this work.

No named Yaghnobi pattern vocabulary is given in the supplied sources.

### Clothing & dress

They provide no named garments, head coverings, belts, footwear, dress distinctions between men and women, or ceremonial costume.

### Architecture

The sources describe a remote settlement environment rather than specific building forms. The Yaghnob Valley comprises approximately ten settlements, each housing between three and eight families, with other small settlements elsewhere. The upper Yaghnob River Valley was protected by an almost impenetrable gorge until recently. They state that some of the area remains remote from roads and power transmission lines and that the majority of returnees remain without roads and electricity.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Women traditionally worked on moulding earthenware crockery.

## Music & performance

No Yaghnobi musical tradition is named in the material provided.

## Festivals & rituals

The Yaghnobi are Sunni Muslims, and many elements of the pre-Islamic Iranian religion of Zoroastrianism are still preserved.

The historical sources describe major disruptions to Yaghnobi social life. Contact with the Soviet Union in the 1930s during the Great Purge led to the exile of many Yaghnobis. Forced resettlements took place in 1957 and 1970, moving people from the Yaghnob mountains to the semi-desert lowlands of Tajikistan. In the 1970s, Red Army helicopters evacuated inhabitants from valleys regarded as being at risk from avalanches. Some Yaghnobis reportedly died of shock during the movement, while many were forced to work at cotton plantations. Since 1983, families have begun returning to the Yaghnob Valley.

## Foodways

The sources identify agriculture as a traditional occupation and name barley, wheat, and legumes among the produce grown by the Yaghnobi. They also mention the breeding of cattle, oxen, and asses. The Yaghnobi historically lived through a natural economy, and returnees to the Yaghnob Valley continue to do so. These references indicate the agricultural and pastoral basis of subsistence.

## Oral tradition & literature

They mention an “anecdote about Nasreddin” as a section heading in the Yaghnobi language article, but the supplied material does not include the text or describe its performance context.

The sources do document preservation and study of the language. Yaghnobi was mostly unwritten until recent times. Some Yaghnobi scholars used the Persian script before 1928, mainly when they needed to hide information from Tajiks. In the 1990s, Sayfiddin Mirzozoda of the Tajik Academy of Sciences introduced a modified Tajik alphabet and prepared textbooks for elementary-school students. Since 2006, the Tajik government has stopped providing funding for printing Yaghnobi textbooks and hiring Yaghnobi teachers, and these efforts have largely stagnated.

## Language & religion

Yaghnobi is an Eastern Iranian language spoken in the upper valley of the Yaghnob River in the Zarafshan area of Tajikistan. It is considered a direct descendant of Sogdian or closely related to it and has sometimes been called Neo-Sogdian. Most Yaghnobi speakers are bilingual in Tajik, a dialect of Persian. Yaghnobi is mostly used for daily family communication, while Tajik is used for business and formal transactions. The language is taught in elementary school within the ethnic community, and Tajikistan has enacted legislation supporting education in minority languages, including Yaghnobi.

There are western and eastern dialects, together with a transitional dialect sharing features of both. Yaghnobi was written by some scholars in the Persian script before 1928. It is now largely transcribed by scholars using a modified Latin alphabet, and a modified Tajik alphabet was introduced in the 1990s. A Cyrillic alphabet is also described, with the letter Ԝ. The Yaghnobi people are Sunni Muslims, while many elements of Zoroastrianism remain preserved.
