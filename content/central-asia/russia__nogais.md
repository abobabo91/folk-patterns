## Overview

The Nogais are a Turkic people whose language belongs to the Kipchak branch of the Turkic languages. They live in Eastern Europe, including the North Caucasus and the Volga region, as well as Central Asia and Turkey. Most are found in northern Dagestan and Stavropol Krai, with further communities in Karachay-Cherkessia, Chechnya, and Astrakhan Oblast. Nogais also live in Dobruja, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Ukraine, and elsewhere. In the 1990s, 65,000 were still living in the Northern Caucasus. Their ancestors included Mongolic and Turkic tribes associated with the Nogai Horde, and their history includes nomadic pastoralism, seasonal migration, cavalry service, and repeated movement across the Black Sea region, the Caucasus, Central Asia, and Anatolia.

## Music & performance

The sources name Arslanbek Sultanbekov, born 1965, as a Nogai musician. No other Nogai instruments, musical forms, or performance contexts are documented in the supplied material.

The sources do describe the historical importance of cavalry and military organization. Many Nogais migrated to the Crimean peninsula to serve as the Crimean khans’ cavalry, while the Nogai Horde maintained political and military relationships with the Astrakhan Khanate, the Crimean Khanate, Russia, and the Ottoman Empire. These references concern military and political history rather than documented music or performance.

## Festivals & rituals

They do state that Nogais raised herds and migrated seasonally in search of better pastures, and that they valued nomadic traditions and independence, which they considered superior to settled agricultural life. These statements describe social and economic values rather than named festivals or ritual practices.

The historical record includes major episodes of migration and resettlement. The Kalmyks reached the Volga about 1630, and the Nogais were subsequently expelled from parts of the Lower Volga region. After the Russian annexation of Crimea in 1783, approximately 120,000 Nogais were resettled from Bessarabia and areas northeast of the Sea of Azov to the Kuban and the Caucasus during the 1770s and 1780s. The great exodus to the Ottoman Empire took place in 1860, while 50,000 Nogais disappeared from the northern Black Sea region by 1861. These are historical movements, not festivals.

## Foodways

The sources document a Nogai cuisine maintained in Turkey. Named foods include *üken börek*, *kaşık börek*, *tabak börek*, *şır börek*, *köbete*, and *Nogay şay*. The source describes *Nogay şay* as Nogai tea, a drink prepared by boiling milk and tea together with butter, salt, and pepper. These are the only named Nogai dishes and drinks in the supplied material.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do state that Nogai identity is associated with clan stratification, clan-group ethnonyms, genealogies, and the customary identification of clan-family signs called *tamga*.

Nogai historical memory in the supplied material includes the names of Nogai Khan, Yusuf Mirza, Ismail Mirza, and Mirza Kazy, as well as the formation and division of the Nogai Horde. These accounts concern political and dynastic history rather than a documented oral genre.

## Language & religion

Nogai is a Turkic language belonging to the Kipchak branch of the Turkic languages. The sources state that it remains spoken in some villages of Central Anatolia, mainly around Salt Lake, Eskişehir, and Ceyhan. They also record that the language is spoken by Nogais living in Russia, Central Asia, Turkey, and other regions. The name is also spelled Nogay or Noghay, and the people are also known as the Nogalar or Mangkyt.

They mention the Ottoman Empire, the Crimean Khanate, and Russian rule in historical contexts.
