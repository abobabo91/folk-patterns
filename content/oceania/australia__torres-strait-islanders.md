## Overview

Torres Strait Islanders are the seafaring Indigenous people of 133 islands between Australia and Papua New Guinea, with a culture rooted in Papuo-Austronesian tradition. Their material culture centers on worked shell, wood, turtleshell and plaited fibre rather than pottery or metal. Dance, song and ceremony are the living heart of their tradition, preserving oral history and island identity. Christianity arrived in 1871 and integrated with earlier beliefs centered on the spiritual system of Tagai.

## Material culture

Torres Strait Islander material culture is made from marine and plant materials—shell, turtleshell, wood, bamboo, coconut, fibre and seed—worked with engraving, plaiting and carving into functional and ceremonial objects.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Plaited and twined fibre dominates, with engraved shell and wood carrying a small, marine-based design vocabulary.

- **Plaited fibre girdles and aprons** — Flat plaited bands of dark brown fibre folded with a plaited cord, worn with front-and-back aprons as structural everyday garment.
- **Grass skirts and aprons** — Skirts with tassels and double aprons made of grass, worn in both daily and dance contexts.
- **Plaited mats** — Large roughly rectangular fibre mats in diagonal weave with red stripes interwoven at intervals.
- **String figures** (*wame, wameya*) — Looped cord figures prominent among the artforms and performed rather than kept.
- **Patterned shoulder straps** — Grass straps worked with black pattern and zigzags, with fringe on one edge where zigzags fall.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress ranged from cloth wraps to layered fibre and grass garments; ceremonial dress is elaborate and additive, built from shell, feather, seed and fibre ornaments.

- **Shell penis sheath** (*cymbium*) — Cymbium shell ornamented with dots and lines, hung from a plaited leaf band around the waist.
- **Ceremony and war headdress** (*samera*) — Cassowary feathers bound into a woven fibre band whitened and tied with plaited sinnet.
- **Elaborate headdress** (*Samu, Dhoeri*) — Named headdresses worn by dancers in ceremonial dress on Mer.
- **Male coronet** — Kangaroo teeth bound into fine woven fibre.
- **Initiation cloth wraps** — Paired cloth wraps worn with a large cassowary tail feather tucked in at the back.

### Architecture

Buildings are thatched-roof structures; house-boards are wooden panels engraved and painted in red, white and black with human and animal designs.

- **Thatched-roof structures** — Repeated in photographs across the islands as the main building form.
- **House-boards** — Wooden panels engraved and painted in red, white and black carrying human figures, zigzag lines and animal designs including lizards.
- **Bamboo fences** — Long bamboo fences enclose ground where ceremonial events like initiation are watched.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

No pottery or metalworking; everyday kit is entirely of shell, wood, bamboo, coconut, turtleshell and stone, with marine hunting equipment the most elaborate.

- **Coconut water vessels** — Coconut bottles closed with leaf stoppers, singly or joined in pairs by vegetable-fibre cord for carrying water.
- **Engraved turtleshell fish-hooks** — Fish-hooks with perforated shank, worked from turtleshell.
- **Harpooning rope** (*amu*) — Rope plaited from vine stems and attached to the harpoon head when hunting dugong.
- **Spear** (*wap*) — Marine hunting spear carried by island chiefs in photographs with dugong catches.
- **Warfare headtaker's loop** (*singi*) — Cane loop bound with sinnet to a double head of carved wood, used to carry heads taken in warfare.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ornament is cut from pearl shell and turtleshell and strung from shell and seed, with elaborate feathered headdresses as the most conspicuous form.

- **Fish-shaped turtleshell pendant** (*sabagorar*) — Flat turtleshell pendant in the shape of a fish with incised fins, gills, mouth and eye, drilled for suspension.
- **Pearl-shell frog pendant** (*mai goai*) — Cut from thin pearl shell in the form of a tree frog with incised lines around the edge.
- **Elaborate feathered headdress** (*dhari, dari, dhoeri*) — Triangular canvas plate whitened with red and black edges, framed in openwork rattan, with white feathers radiating out and a black feather at centre.
- **Pearl-shell bird ear ornaments** — Polished pearl shell shaped as a bird, drilled with a single hole.
- **Shell necklaces** — Strung from oliva shells.

## Music & performance

Singing and dancing are the literature of Torres Strait Islander people; instruments are struck, shaken and plucked, including drums, rattles, whips and jaw harps.

- **Carved wooden drum** — Carved wooden vessel with open mouth cut with a crocodile and two men in feather headdresses, one side dressed with cassowary feathers and goa nuts.
- **Bamboo cracking whip** (*lolo*) — Flexible bamboo strip with thin sinnet cord and fine coconut root tied at the end, its handle finely incised.
- **Bamboo jaw harp** — Bamboo jaw harp with tongue cut longitudinally, inner face painted with vermilion and a lozenge carved at one end.
- **Turtleshell rattle** — Spiral band of engraved turtleshell with wooden head and plumes of cassowary feathers.
- **Bean pod castanet** — Bean pod holding five beans used as a castanet in night dances.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is creative and competitive expression where the dancer is storyteller; each island group has its own performances carrying island tradition and clan identity.

- **Hand-held dance ornament** (*getau-za*) — Built from a central wooden disc engraved with the sucker fish gapu, with bamboo spokes radiating to a cane ring, feathers tied along the outer edge and painting in red, black and white.
- **Turtleshell masks** — Fish-shaped and human-face masks worn in dance, unique to Torres Strait Islander culture.
- **Elaborately sculptured headdress** (*dari*) — Elaborate feathered headdresses often with moving parts, made by artists like Ken Thaiday Snr, incorporating powerful totems like the hammerhead shark.
- **Model dugong harpoon** — Made of wood, feathers and cloth for dancing rather than hunting.
- **Dance machines** — Hand-held mechanical moving objects used as performance props.

## Festivals & rituals

The central annual observance is Coming of the Light on 1 July, commemorating the 1871 arrival of Christian mission; charm objects protect crops and canoes; adoption practice kupai omasker has legal recognition.

- **Coming of the Light festival** (*Zulai Wan, Bi Akarida*) — Celebrated 1 July by all Island communities, commemorating the 1871 arrival of London Missionary Society mission on Erub with eight Loyalty Island teachers.
- **Sea snake taboo charm** — Naturally S-shaped wood carved as a sea snake and painted red, placed in plantations to ward off thieves.
- **Canoe figure-head** (*dogai*) — Curved wood with pearl-shell eyes, red-and-white painted face and projecting cassowary feathers, fixed to the prow to bring good luck.
- **Adoption practice** (*kupai omasker*) — Allows a child to be adopted by a relative or community member, given legal recognition in Queensland by the Meriba Omasker Kaziw Kazipa Act 2020.
- **Island of Origin rugby league tournament** — Annual sporting event between island teams that brings people together across the Strait.

## Foodways

Islanders are agriculturalists and hunters; dugong, turtle, crayfish, reef fish and wild fruits were traditionally hunted and collected, with specialized marine hunting technology.

- **Dugong and turtle hunting** — Understood as continuing the Islander tradition of close association with the sea, using specialized harpoons and rigging.
- **Harpoon with plaited rope** (*amu*) — Plaited vine rope attached to harpoon head when hunting dugong.
- **Engraved turtleshell fish-hooks** — Specialized fishing equipment with perforated shank.
- **Tobacco cultivation** — Plantations on Aureed Island evidence agricultural cultivation alongside hunting and gathering.
- **Red fruit** (*sorbi*) — Named fruit held in the right hand of Tagai in spiritual narratives.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tradition is oral, with stories handed down through song, dance and ceremony; Tagai is the central spiritual system and framing narrative.

- **Tagai spiritual system** (*Tagai*) — Spiritual system and framing narrative; Tagai stands in a canoe holding a fishing spear (the Southern Cross) and red fruit, his crew of twelve cast into the sea as star patterns.
- **Four brothers cycle** (*bala*) — Cycle of four brothers—Malo, Sagai, Kulka and Siu—who paddled from Cape York and each established a following, setting rules for living.
- **Myths and Legends of the Torres Strait** — Published by Margaret Lawrie in 1970, reviving narratives that had fallen out of use.
- **Haddon Collection** — About 2,000 objects and film of ceremonial dance collected by Alfred Cort Haddon on 1898–1899 expedition, held at Cambridge University.
- **Tales from the Torres Strait** — Published by Margaret Lawrie in 1972, continuing the revival of island narratives.

## Language & religion

Two Indigenous languages are spoken: Kalaw Lagaw Ya (Pama-Nyungan) on western and central islands, and Meriam Mir (Eastern Trans-Fly) on the east; Christianity spread from 1870s and integrated with earlier Tagai-centered beliefs.

- **Kalaw Lagaw Ya language** (*Kalaw Lagaw Ya*) — Western-Central Torres Strait Language spoken on southwestern, western, northern and central islands, belonging to Pama-Nyungan family.
- **Meriam Mir language** (*Meriam Mir*) — Spoken on eastern islands, one of four Eastern Trans-Fly languages.
- **Torres Strait Creole** (*Yumplatok*) — English-based creole serving as the language of trade and commerce.
- **Christianity arrival** — Spread from 1870s; in 2016 census, 20,658 of 32,345 Torres Strait Islander people reported Christian affiliation.
- **Integration of belief systems** — Islanders continued connection to land, sea and sky and followed Tagai's instructions while integrating Christianity into everyday life.
