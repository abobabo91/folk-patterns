## Overview

The Pitjantjatjara are an Aboriginal Australian people originating from the Central Australian desert near Uluru, Australia. They live mostly in the northwest of South Australia, extending across the border into the Northern Territory to just south of Lake Amadeus, and west a short distance into Western Australia. They refer to themselves as Aṉangu people and are closely related to the Yankunytjatjara and Ngaanyatjarra people. Their languages are varieties of the Western Desert language and are, to a large extent, mutually intelligible. The land is an inseparable part of their identity, with every part described as rich with stories and meaning to the Aṉangu.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do describe sacred places and Tjukurpa tracks as possessing important spiritual and ceremonial significance for the Aṉangu. Uluru / Ayers Rock and Kata Tjuṯa / Mount Olga are separated from Pitjantjatjara lands by the border between the Northern Territory and South Australia and have become a major tourist attraction and a national park.

From 1950 onwards, many aṉangu were forced to leave their traditional lands because of British nuclear tests at Maralinga, and some were subsequently contaminated by nuclear fallout from the atomic tests. After four years of campaigning and negotiations with government and mining groups, the Pitjantjatjara Land Rights Act 1981 was passed on 19 March 1981, granting freehold title over 103,000 km2 of land in the northwestern corner of South Australia.

 The language article states that *ngangkari* was defined in the Macquarie Dictionary as an Indigenous practitioner of bush medicine.

## Oral tradition & literature

The supplied sources connect Pitjantjatjara identity and place to stories, meaning, Tjukurpa, and “Dreaming” tracks. Storytelling is described as virtually always taking place in Pitjantjatjara, while schoolchildren and other speakers may use English in classrooms, playgrounds, communities, business, and holidays.

The sources also document a substantial translation and literacy history. The Ernabella Mission was established by Charles Duguid and the Presbyterian Church of Australia in 1937 at the location now known as Pukatja. Missionaries learned Pitjantjatjara and taught it in the school, delivered sermons in it, and helped make it a written language. The first draft of the New Testament’s Gospel of Mark, *Tjukurpa Palja Markaku*, was completed in 1945 by Reverend Bob Love and Ronald Trudinger and published in 1949. A new translation of the New Testament and about 15% of the Old Testament was first published in 2002.

## Language & religion

Pitjantjatjara is a dialect of the Western Desert language traditionally spoken by the Pitjantjatjara people of Central Australia. It is mutually intelligible with other varieties of the Western Desert language and particularly closely related to the Yankunytjatjara dialect. Pitjantjatjara shares an 80% overlap in vocabulary with Yankunytjatjara. It is described as a relatively healthy Aboriginal language, with children learning it, and it is taught in some Aboriginal schools. The literacy rate for first-language speakers is 50–70%, and for second-language learners it is 10–15%.

The name Pitjantjatjara derives from *pitjantja*, a nominalised form of the verb “go,” combined with the comitative suffix *-tjara*. The language has a dictionary, and the New Testament of the Bible has been translated into it.
