## Overview

Native Hawaiians are Indigenous Polynesians who settled the Hawaiian Islands at least 800 years ago, sailing from the Society Islands. At Captain Cook's arrival in 1778, the population numbered 250,000 to 800,000; introduced disease reduced it drastically to fewer than 100,000 by 1900. They developed distinctive arts including intricate featherwork, barkcloth textiles, hula dance, and chanted poetry. After an 1893 overthrow, hula and the Hawaiian language were suppressed until cultural revival efforts began in the late 20th century.

## Material culture

Hawaiian craftspeople worked in natural materials—feathers, fiber, bone, wood, stone, and shell—creating textiles, clothing, tools, and ornaments of high technical refinement.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Kapa barkcloth is the principal Hawaiian textile, stamped and painted in distinctive patterns of stripes, checks, triangles, and dots.

- **Kapa** — Barkcloth stamped or painted with narrow red lines in bands, checks, cross-hatching, or stripes in red, black, and yellow.
- **Featherwork panels** — Rectangular textiles built from olonā fiber strips faced with red, yellow, and black feathers set in bands or plaid patterns.
- **ʻieʻie basketry** (*ʻieʻie*) — Aerial rootlets twined into basket lids, helmet foundations, and other woven goods.
- **Pandanus fans** — Plaited pandanus leaf stiffened with a cane rib along each side.
- **Pāpale hat** (*pāpale*) — Woven in triple-ply from split rattan reed in black, brown, and natural colors.

### Clothing & dress

Prestige garments are made of feathers; everyday dress includes the malo loincloth and kapa skirts.

- **Malo** — Loincloth of kapa worn by men, often printed with large triangles and geometric designs.
- **ʻahu ʻula cloak** — Prestigious cloak made from honeyeater and honeycreeper feathers bound together and knotted into olonā netting.
- **Kīpuka cape** (*kīpuka*) — Trapezoidal cape layering white, black, and golden-brown chicken feathers with red ʻapapane and yellow ʻōʻō feathers.
- **Mahiole helmet** (*mahiole*) — Crested helmet built of ʻieʻie basketry with high crest, covered in red, yellow, and scarlet feathers in transverse rows.

### Architecture

Land organization, not building, is the primary architectural structure in Hawaiian society.

- **Moku** — Land divisions held by people of high standing and kept within the family on each island.
- **Ahupuaʻa** — Land zones running from sea to mountain top, holding all resources needed for survival.
- **ʻili** — Major subdivisions of ahupuaʻa, each paying tribute to the chief of the ahupuaʻa and the island chief.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Hawaiian households use wood, gourd, stone, shell, and fiber; no pottery or metalwork is made.

- **Kou bowls** (*kou*) — Vessels turned from hard brown kou wood, including shallow kava bowls, squared vessels, and carved bowls with figures and shell inlays.
- **Poi containers** — Coconut shells cut lengthways and polished to store poi.
- **Gourd water bottles** — Gourds banded with black straight and zigzag lines and fitted with twisted coconut-fiber suspension loops.
- **Basalt adzes** — Tools with polished basalt blades lashed with twisted cord to oblique T-shaped handles.
- **Makau hook** (*makau*) — Composite fishing hook of bone shank and point tied with fine fiber over a wooden wedge and coiled to thick twisted line.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Rank is marked by whale-tooth ornaments and feather garlands; other adornments use teeth, shell, and fiber.

- **Lei niho palaoa** — Whale-tooth ornament suspended on many strands of plaited human hair cord, marking high rank.
- **Lei hulu** — Feather garlands for head or neck made chiefly of red feathers with narrow yellow bands bordered in black.
- **Boars' tusk bracelet** — Bracelet of twenty-three boars' tusks cut square at the base and threaded on double plaited olonā.
- **Kūpeʻe niho ʻīlio** — Dog-tooth leg ornament worn in hula, with teeth fixed at regular intervals to olonā netting.

## Music & performance

Mele and oli, songs and chants, are central to ritual; percussion instruments accompany hula dancing.

- **Pahu hula** — Drum made of half a pale coconut split on one side, resting on a hollow palm-wood cylinder with a fish-skin membrane.
- **Kilu** — Thigh drum of coconut shell with a fish-skin head worn on a plaited leg band.
- **Ipu** — Percussion instrument that accompanies chanted hula.
- **Hano** — Nose flute, also called ʻohe hano ihu, played with the nose.
- *ʻūkēkē* — Bamboo mouth-resonated string instrument with pointed ends, held in the teeth.

## Dance & theatre

Hula is Hawaii's best-known Indigenous art, originally a ritual to honor gods and goddesses, now taught worldwide in two distinct styles.

- **Hula kahiko** — Traditional hula style developed before European contact, interpretive and accompanied by percussion and chanting.
- **Hula ʻauana** — Modern hula style shaped by Western influence, danced to non-traditional instruments in colorful outfits.
- **Merrie Monarch Festival** — Annual festival gathering hula hālau from around the world to compete and share knowledge, honoring King David Kalākaua.
- **Hālau** — Hula schools and groups that compete and perform at major festivals.

## Festivals & rituals

Major celebrations include feasts and ceremonies honoring Hawaiian history; warfare had specialized equipment and ritual.

- **Lūʻau** — Banquet celebration, the most popular form of celebration in Hawaiʻi, often featuring poi, poke, kālua pig, and hula.
- **Prince Kūhiō Day** — Celebrated March 26 since 1949, marking the birthday of Prince Jonah Kūhiō Kalanianaʻole and featuring canoe races and feasts.
- **King Kamehameha Day** — Celebrated June 11, honoring Kamehameha I who unified the islands, marked by parades and lei-draping ceremonies.
- **Kapu system** (*kapu*) — Traditional system of social stratification governing ritual life, overthrown after the introduction of Christianity.
- **Pikoi** — Throwing club with four-pointed stone head lashed to wooden handle, launched at opponents' lower legs.

## Foodways

Poi, made from taro, is the principal food; fishing and feasts with poi, poke, and kālua pig remain central to Hawaiian culture.

- **Poi** — Staple food made from taro, the plant believed to be the manifestation of Hāloa, ancestor of all Hawaiians.
- **Poke** — Raw fish dish featured at lūʻau feasts.
- **Kālua pig** — Roasted pork, a traditional feast dish.
- **Haupia** — Coconut-milk dessert served at feasts.
- **Kava** — Ritual beverage drunk from coconut cups and carved wooden bowls.

## Oral tradition & literature

Chants and songs carry knowledge across generations; legends tell of origins, arrival, and the gods.

- **Oli** — Chants, one of the carriers of Hawaiian knowledge and cultural tradition.
- **Mele** — Poems and songs, one of the carriers of Hawaiian knowledge and central to ritual.
- **Hawaiʻiloa** — Legendary figure in Hawaiian origin stories.
- **Paʻao** — Navigator-priest said to have voyaged between Hawaiʻi and Tahiti and introduced many customs.
- **Menehune** — Little people in Hawaiian legend said to have built heiau and fishponds, cited as evidence of an earlier population.

## Language & religion

Hawaiian is a Polynesian language now an official state language; traditional religion is polytheistic and animistic, centered on four gods plus Pele.

- **ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi** — Hawaiian language, Polynesian family, now taught in immersion schools and remaining the primary language of Niʻihau.
- **Kāne** — God of creation.
- **Kanaloa** — God of the ocean.
- **Kū** — God of war and male pursuits.
- **Lono** — God of peace, rain, and fertility.

## Glossary

- *kapa* — Barkcloth, the principal Hawaiian textile
- *olonā* — Fiber used to face featherwork and bind garments
- *ʻieʻie* — Aerial rootlets twined into baskets and helmets
- *malo* — Loincloth of kapa worn by men
- *kīpuka* — Trapezoidal feathered cape
- *mahiole* — Crested helmet covered in feathers
- *moku* — Land division held by high-ranking family
- *ahupuaʻa* — Land zone running from sea to mountain, containing all survival resources
- *ʻili* — Subdivision of ahupuaʻa paying tribute to chiefs
- *kou* — Hard brown wood used for bowls and other vessels
- *makau* — Composite fishing hook of bone and wood
- *lei niho palaoa* — Whale-tooth ornament on plaited hair cord, marking rank
- *pahu hula* — Percussion drum accompanying hula
- *kilu* — Thigh drum with fish-skin head
- *hula kahiko* — Traditional hula style with percussion and chanting
- *hula ʻauana* — Modern hula style with Western instruments and costumes
- *lūʻau* — Banquet celebration with traditional foods and entertainment
- *kapu* — Traditional system of social stratification and ritual prohibition
- *pikoi* — Throwing club with stone head
