## Overview

The Fore are an ethnic group inhabiting Okapa District in the Eastern Highlands Province of Papua New Guinea. Approximately 20,000 Fore live in the North Fore and South Fore regions, separated by the Wanevinti Mountains. Their territory is a steep mountainous region of about 400 square miles (1,000 km2), with settlements commonly located between 4,500 and 7,000 feet above sea level. The Lamari and Yani rivers are the two largest rivers in the Fore region. The main form of subsistence is slash-and-burn farming. The Fore language belongs to the East Central Family, East New Guinea Highlands Stock, and Trans-New Guinea phylum of Papuan languages, and has Northern, Central, and Southern dialects. Fore culture is documented through kinship, gardening, ritual, warfare, peacemaking, folklore, and mortuary practices.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

One account describes a Nokoti stealing a woman’s skirt, shells, and beads, then dressing a tree with the stolen clothes.

### Architecture

Fore settlements generally consist of clusters of larger buildings near land cleared for cultivation. A typical kinship-group settlement includes a house for men and boys and several smaller surrounding houses for women and children. These hamlets are established when gardening land around an existing settlement is exhausted, after which residents move outward and clear more woodland for crops. Settlements are temporary and are frequently demolished and rebuilt elsewhere as new land is acquired. More isolated villages retained separate men’s houses, while larger villages contained rest houses.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention wooden shields, bows and arrows, bamboo vessels used in a ritual for determining responsibility in sorcery accusations, and small axes associated with the forest spirit Nokoti.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention shells and beads as traditional valuables in a Nokoti story. They also state that boys undergoing a manhood initiation ceremony receive piercings, and that a groom is often pierced in the nose because piercings are thought to increase a man’s strength.

## Music & performance

They state that boys undergoing the manhood initiation ceremony live isolated in the forest for a month, receive a new name, undergo piercings, and play music with sacred flutes. This ceremony provides the only named musical context in the sources.

Music and vocal performance also appear in warfare and peacemaking. During open-field battles, Fore fighters used chanting, singing, dancing, sudden advances, and yelling profane insults as intimidation tactics. After peace terms had been agreed, allied groups held a battlefield ceremony. Following the exchange of daka leaves and sugarcane, singing and cheering could take place, and speeches praising warriors and flattering new allies were delivered by both sides.

## Dance & theatre

They state that dancing formed part of the intimidation practices used during open-field warfare, alongside chanting, singing, sudden advances, and profane insults. No further description of the dance movements, participants, or occasions is provided.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document rituals connected with initiation, marriage, conflict, peace, death, and sorcery.

Boys could become *nagaiya*, or age mates, by undergoing the manhood initiation ceremony together. The ceremony involved a month of isolation in the forest, receiving a new name, piercings, and playing music with sacred flutes. A Fore girl became eligible for marriage when she first menstruated. During the marriage ceremony, the husband’s family paid the bride’s family a marriage fee, which was thrown on the bride’s head as “head pay”; the bride and groom then attended separate feasts.

Peacemaking rituals followed agreements brokered by a *pako*, a peaceful envoy. Pigs served as remuneration for fatalities, damaged crops, and damaged property. At the battlefield ceremony, elderly women from both sides placed daka leaves and sugarcane at the center of the field. Elderly men then chewed the sugarcane, symbolizing acceptance of the gifts.

Mortuary rituals were intended to direct the deceased’s five souls to their proper destinations. Family members left food with the body, which could be buried, entombed, or, historically, cooked and consumed two to three days after death. A deceased man’s *nagaiya* attended the funeral, received *anonkaiyambu*, or “head pay,” from the family, and was then expected to avenge his age mate’s death.

## Foodways

Fore subsistence is based mainly on slash-and-burn farming. The diet consists of pigs, small animals, insects, wild plants, and root vegetables such as taro. Gardens produce sweet potato, *ebia*, sugarcane, and *pitpit*. Root vegetables are cultivated on land cleared through slash-and-burn farming. Sweet potato became a staple food and was also used as feed for domesticated pigs. The sources state that its introduction, roughly 150 years ago, increased food production because it could grow in harsher conditions than existing root vegetables. Around the same time, the Fore began domesticating pigs, reducing the need to hunt wild ones.

The Fore supplement cultivated foods with pigs, rodents, and adult insects. The sources describe the diet as unusually rich and varied and state that a 1957 study found no evidence of clinical malnutrition in the general population. Pork also has ceremonial and social importance. Pigs are used as remuneration for fatalities, damaged crops, and damaged property in peacemaking. Allied clans invite one another to extravagant pork feasts, helping maintain relations after warfare.

Dietary restrictions are associated with menstruation and marriage. When a man’s wife first menstruates, he stops eating pork, rats, possums, and spinach-like vegetables until she gives birth to her first child. His *nagaiya* share these restrictions in order to support him.

## Oral tradition & literature

Fore folklore includes stories about *Nokoti*, a forest spirit associated with isolated and sacred places such as unusual rock formations or misshaped trees. Nokoti was originally portrayed as a tall masculine figure carrying a small axe or a small bag containing magical power. A person entering a Nokoti’s *ples masalai*, or sacred place, could suffer illness caused by the Nokoti *kionei*, or poison. Nokoti could also bless people with superior hunting skills or increased fertility.

One tale concerns a hunter who encounters a Nokoti named Walusubo on a unique rock formation. Walusubo promises a magical bag in the future, tells the hunter to eat sugar cane leaves, and gives him instructions that improve his hunting abilities. After the hunter dies, Walusubo has also died because the two were *nagaiya*. The rock becomes known as Walusubo Yaba, or Walusubo’s stone. Later stories portray Nokoti as a thief who steals clothing, shells, beads, or money, attacks patrol huts, or is sent to Australia after robbing a store.

## Language & religion

Fore is spoken in Okapa District in the Eastern Highlands Province of Papua New Guinea. It belongs to the East New Guinea Highlands Stock and the Trans-New Guinea Phylum; the source also places it within the East Central Family. Fore has three dialects: Northern, the prestige dialect, Central, and Southern. A separate linguistic description identifies Fore or Foré as a Kainantu-Goroka language spoken in the Goroka District of Eastern Highlands Province.

Traditional Fore beliefs describe the land, or *bagina*, as alive and as the creator of the world. The *bagina* created the ancestor guardians, *amani*, from whom the Fore are descended. A person is believed to have five souls: the *auma*, *ama*, *aona*, *yesegi*, and *kwela*. Mortuary rituals help these souls reach their proper destinations. The sources also describe Nokoti folklore and belief in sorcery. Many Fore converted to Christianity after contact with missionaries from 1957 onwards and denied the efficacy of sorcery.
