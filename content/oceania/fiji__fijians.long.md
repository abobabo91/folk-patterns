## Overview

Fijians, who call themselves *iTaukei* — "owners of the land" — are the indigenous Austronesian people of the Fiji Islands in Melanesia, indigenous to every part of the group except the island of Rotuma. They speak Fijian, a language of the Central Pacific (Fijian–Polynesian) branch of the Austronesian family, alongside English. Their ancestors reached Fiji roughly 3,500 years ago as descendants of the Lapita people, a seafaring population originally from what is now Taiwan, whose distinctive pottery appears in Fiji from 800 BCE onward; Melanesian arrivals followed around 500 BCE, and long commerce with Tonga and Samoa added further layers. As of 2005 indigenous Fijians were slightly more than half of Fiji's population, predominantly of Melanesian extraction with some Polynesian admixture. In folk-culture terms they are remembered across the Pacific as its canoe-building experts, and their barkcloth, whale-ivory ornaments and kava ceremonial gear are among the most recognisable object classes in Oceanic museum collections.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Barkcloth** — The dominant patterned textile in the collection, beaten from bark and decorated with brown, red and black pigment. One large piece is divided into squared design fields, each filled with geometric patterning; another is a long narrow panel with elaborate red-and-black work on one face, a border pattern along the edges, and a slight fringe. The technique is partly stencilled and partly painted, and the two are combined on the same cloth. Thin smoked brown barkcloth with fringed edges was also made up into headgear, and oblong lengths served as loincloths, patterned in black with brown touches and a band of zigzags along one edge.

**Masi kesa** — A named barkcloth panel type in the Metropolitan Museum's collection, of barkcloth and pigment, dated mid- to late nineteenth century.

**Plaited mats** — Large square mats of closely interwoven vegetable fibre, and lighter mats of woven grass, straw-coloured, finished at one end with a short cut fringe and at the other with a longer fringe knotted into lengths. Colour is used sparingly: one mat carries a single line of short dark-brown zigzags across one end. Mats are the surface people sit on for ceremony — a photograph in the collection shows five men, two standing and three seated on a mat, outside a thatched building with stone walls at its base.

**Motif vocabulary.** Geometric fields divided into squares; flowers; bands of crosses; lines; diamonds; zigzags; dot-and-groove bands around a vessel shoulder; openwork zigzags carved into a bowl stand; angular patterns in sunk relief on a wooden handle.

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress is represented by the *liku*, a skirt of vegetable fibre whose strands hang from a plaited waistband; one example uses both dyed and natural fibre in the band, and other skirts are made of root fibre strung with glass beads, or of two hemp cords through which tufts of plant material are passed to form a fringe. A light plaited waist garment of hibiscus bark carries long fibre tassels. Girdles are made of fibre cord — one strung with glass beads, another hung with fish vertebrae. Men in the collection's photographs and drawings wear a cloth wrap at the waist, with arm, ear and neck ornaments; one drawing shows a man in a turban with a neck ornament and a cloth wrap, a club carried from his belt. The most striking headdress is the wig of human hair built on a base of interwoven leaf strips and secured with a fine twisted fibre cord; two portrait photographs from Viti Levu show chiefs and men wearing these "big-head" wigs, one with a headband. Another drawing records a man from Suva wearing two hibiscus flowers in his hair. Ceremonial dance dress, photographed in rows of women, layered barkcloth skirts with personal ornaments.

### Architecture

The sources describe Fijian building only obliquely. A miniature spirit house in the British Museum is made of coconut-fibre cord worked over a foundation of probable reeds, with cane — a model of a religious structure rather than a dwelling, and the clearest indication in this collection of how a sacred building was framed and bound rather than nailed. A photograph taken at Nagarawai shows a full-size building with a thatched roof rising from stone walls at its base.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Fijian pottery here is unglazed, fired earthenware in compound forms. The *saqa moli* is a vessel of four small containers joined by four large handles that meet at an apex, the top half patterned and the bottom plain, with a small spout on one container and a hole in another. A related piece joins five water-bottles at the centre by a bar. The *saga ni wai* water carrier is flat-bottomed, semi-circular, red, with a single opening and dot-and-groove decoration around the shoulders; another vessel has a heavily zigzagged upper half, a plain lower half blackened at the base, and a small nozzle halfway up. Woodwork runs to kava bowls cut from a single piece and stained with kava patina, one with an openwork zigzag stand threaded with coconut cord; a kava ladle of coconut shell with a wooden handle and cachalot tooth, riveted with copper; drinking cups of coconut shell with a fibre mouth-wiper attached; a leaf-shaped oil dish; carved platters; a headrest; combs; basalt adze heads; plaited leaf baskets in beige and black; a plaited pandanus fan; a fly whisk (*i roi*); and the carved wooden fork catalogued as a "cannibal" fork. Weapons are a major class: the *totokia* club with a pineapple-shaped carved head and coconut-fibre binding, the *gata* club, the *iula drisia* throwing club with carved grip and bulbous head, and notched or barbed wooden spears.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Whale ivory is the prestige material. The *tabua* is a revered whale's tooth used in public and private ceremony and considered sacred; one in the collection is a presentation tooth incised with two sailing ships and hung on coconut-fibre cord. Breast ornaments are roughly circular plates assembled from five to eight irregular pieces of cachalot ivory pegged together through drilled holes and suspended on fibre ties. Neck ornaments string six or fifteen carved and polished whale-tooth pendants at their roots on two-ply twisted coconut cord; one necklace combines five differently shaped teeth — whole, split in half, and one cut as a disc — with alternating coconut-shell and white shell discs. A boar's-tusk neck pendant, the *bati ni vuaka*, is worn by a chief in one photograph. Ornaments of plant stems with red and white glass beads show imported material entering the same forms. Tattooing equipment survives: the flat heart-shaped wooden mallet called *tata*, and an instrument of bone, cane and vegetable fibre. Two long needles of human bone are linked with string.

## Music & performance

Two wind instruments are documented. The *davui*, a conch-shell trumpet made from the shell of *Ranella lampas* with fibre, is in the Metropolitan Museum and dated to the late nineteenth century, as is a reed pipe of bamboo, shell and wood. Percussion is represented by a slit instrument hollowed from a solid log of heavy brown wood into a trough of semi-elliptical section whose floor rises towards each end, and by a related wooden gong or drum with a square hole cut into the top.

## Dance & theatre

One photograph records a dance in progress: women in rows wearing ceremonial layered barkcloth skirts with western-style tops and personal ornaments, watched from behind by a group of men in uniform, with a western-style building in the background. The sources name no individual dances and describe no dramatic tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources centre Fijian ceremonial life on two things. The first is *yaqona*, also known as kava, an infusion prepared from the root of *Piper methysticum*, a pepper plant; in the era of the older religion it was prepared and drunk ceremonially by chiefs and priests alone. Today it belongs to daily life in villages and towns and across all classes — used to welcome and bond with visitors, to frame storytelling sessions, or simply to pass time, and colloquially called "having a grog". The collection's kava bowls, coconut-shell cups with fibre mouth-wipers, and ivory-handled ladle are the material side of that institution. The second is the *tabua*, the sacred whale's tooth presented on both public and private occasions. Fijian traditions generally are described as centred on ceremonies and events that bond the community. Above the level of the village stood the *Bose Levu Vakaturaga*, the Great Council of Chiefs, which met yearly to discuss indigenous Fijian concerns and was composed of 55 chiefs drawn from the fourteen provinces, with three appointees from Rotuma and six named by the Minister of Fijian Affairs; it was disbanded by the military after the 2006 coup.

## Foodways

Roughly 70 percent of indigenous Fijians farm, many at subsistence level, growing sugar cane, cassava, rice, sweet potatoes and bananas. The one prepared consumable the sources treat at length is *yaqona*, infused from the pounded root of the pepper plant and drunk from coconut-shell cups; the wooden bowls in the collection carry a patina from long use with it.

## Oral tradition & literature

Storytelling is documented in the sources only as a setting for kava: sessions of *yaqona* drinking are one of the recognised occasions for it. No epic, tale cycle, proverb collection or recitation genre is named.

## Language & religion

Fijian belongs to the Central Pacific (Fijian–Polynesian) branch of the Austronesian family; the indigenous word for a Fijian is *Kaiviti*, and since the Fijian Affairs (Amendment) Decree of 2010 official usage replaces "Fijian" and "indigenous Fijian" with *iTaukei* when referring to the original settlers, while all citizens of Fiji are now called Fijians. Indigenous Fijians are overwhelmingly Christian: the Methodist Church of Fiji and Rotuma claimed 64.5 percent in the 2007 census, with Roman Catholics at 9.1 percent, the Assemblies of God at 5.7 percent and Seventh-day Adventists at 3.9 percent, and 10.4 percent in other denominations. Islam is Fiji's third-largest religion at 6.3 percent. Only about 0.8 percent report a non-Christian religion or none. Earlier practice is referred to as the "old religion", under which kava was restricted to chiefs and priests; the miniature spirit house in the collection belongs to that order.
