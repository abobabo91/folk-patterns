## Overview

Māori are the indigenous Polynesian people of Aotearoa New Zealand, descended from East Polynesian voyagers who arrived in ocean-going *waka* between roughly 1250 and 1350 CE. The 2023 census counted 978,246 people of Māori descent (19.6% of the national population), with 84.9% living in the North Island — concentrated in Northland, the Waikato basin, the Bay of Plenty, Tairāwhiti (Gisborne) and Te Urewera, plus heavy urban populations in Tāmaki Makaurau (Auckland). Five districts are majority Māori, including Wairoa (68.5%) and the Chatham Islands (68.6%). Te reo Māori belongs to the Eastern Polynesian branch of Austronesian, an ancestry reaching back some 5,000 years to the indigenous peoples of Taiwan. Māori matter to folk-culture study because centuries of isolation produced a carving, weaving and oratorical system with no close parallel elsewhere in Polynesia: curvilinear *whakairo* on a monumental architectural scale, finger-twined cloaks made without a loom, and a chiselled facial tattoo used as a legible statement of descent.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Raranga** — plaiting in *harakeke* (New Zealand flax, *Phormium tenax*), the base craft for baskets (*kete*), floor mats (*whāriki*) and wall panels. Leaf blades are stripped, softened and worked over-and-under in checkwork and twill; unlike Southeast Asian pandanus plaiting, harakeke is used both as a flat blade and as extracted fibre (*muka*).

**Whatu** — the finger-twining (downward-working, off a suspension cord, with no loom or shuttle) by which all Māori cloaks are made. Two-pair and four-pair twining variants (*whatu aho pātahi*, *whatu aho rua*) control density. That Polynesia's finest garment-weaving tradition is loomless is its defining technical fact.

**Kākahu** — the cloak class as a whole: prestige garments of scraped, washed and dressed *muka*, held on the shoulders and worn as the visible sign of rank and occasion. They remain the object handed down through families and worn at graduations, weddings and *tangihanga*.

**Korowai** — a cloak with a muka ground hung with rows of black-dyed rolled flax tassels (*hukahuka*), dyed in *paru* (iron-rich swamp mud) after a tannin bath from *hīnau* or *tānekaha* bark. The tassel field gives korowai a mobile, rattling surface distinct from feather cloaks.

**Kahu huruhuru** — feather cloak, the muka foundation covered in overlapping feathers worked in during twining. Kiwi, kererū, kākā, tūī and (historically) huia feathers are arranged into blocks, chevrons and bands; *kahu kiwi* of solid kiwi plumage is the highest-status form.

**Tāniko** — the weft-twined geometric border technique, worked in dyed muka in black, red-brown and yellow, applied to cloak hems, headbands (*tīpare*) and belts. Tāniko is strictly rectilinear — triangles, chevrons, diamonds, stepped lozenges — in deliberate contrast to the curvilinear carving idiom.

**Piupiu** — the waist garment of suspended flax tubes, scraped in alternating bands so the exposed muka takes dye black while the unscraped sections stay pale, producing the horizontal barring that flashes when the wearer moves in *haka*.

**Tukutuku** — lattice panels set between the carved *poupou* of a *wharenui*, made by stitching *kiekie* or dyed harakeke across a framework of *kākaho* (toetoe culms) to build named geometric patterns — the counterpart in flat pattern to the carving beside it.

**Kōwhaiwhai** — painted scroll ornament in red, black and white on the ridgepole (*tāhuhu*) and rafters (*heke*) of a wharenui, historically also on canoe hulls and paddles. The koru-based curve is continuous, bilaterally reflected and enumerated by name rather than freely improvised.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Koru* (unfurling fern frond, growth); *manaia* (beaked profile figure, guardian); *pākati* (dogtooth notch ridge, the commonest carving fill); *rauponga* (parallel ridges flanking a pākati band); *unaunahi* (fish-scale fill); *tarataratū* and *haehae* (ridge-and-groove carving); *pūhoro* (streaming curvilinear motif, also a thigh tattoo pattern); *patikitiki* (flounder, a tāniko/tukutuku diamond); *poutama* (stepped "stairway to heaven" tukutuku pattern); *niho taniwha* (monster's teeth, triangular).

### Clothing & dress

Pre-contact dress was built from flax, not from bark cloth: New Zealand is too cold for the *tapa* of tropical Polynesia, and Māori substituted dressed muka. The base was a waist garment — *rāpaki* or the fringed *piupiu* — worn by both sexes, with a cloak over the shoulders when needed. Rain cloaks (*pākē*, *hieke*) were shaggy with undressed flax or *tī kōuka* leaf tags shedding water; the finest were the ornamented *kaitaka* with wide tāniko borders (*tāniko taniko* at hem and sides), *korowai*, *kahu huruhuru*, and dog-skin *kahu kurī*, the rarest of all. Cloaks were fastened with a bone, wood or *pounamu* pin (*aurei*). Feet were usually bare; plaited flax sandals (*pāraerae*) were made for rough travel. Women wore a plaited or tāniko headband (*tīpare*); chiefly men bound the hair in a topknot (*tikitiki*) dressed with feathers and a *heru* (comb), often with the *huia* tail feather as the mark of highest rank. Today cloaks function as regalia: a *korowai* over academic dress, over a bride, or laid on a coffin.

### Architecture

The *marae* complex is the built centre of Māori life. Its principal structure is the *wharenui* (great house, also *whare whakairo*, carved house), understood as the body of an ancestor: the *tāhuhu* ridgepole is the spine, the *heke* rafters the ribs, the *maihi* bargeboards the outstretched arms ending in *raparapa* fingers, the *koruru* mask at the gable apex the head, and the *amo* the legs supporting it. Inside, carved *poupou* ancestor slabs alternate with *tukutuku* lattice, beneath painted *kōwhaiwhai* rafters. The doorway lintel (*pare*, *korupe*) and window lintel carry protective figures. Construction used adzed tōtara and kauri timber, a *raupō* reed and bark roof, and no metal fastening before contact. Alongside it stand the *pātaka*, a raised and richly carved storehouse whose elevation on posts guarded food from rats and displayed the community's surplus; the *wharekai* (dining house, deliberately *noa*, unrestricted, and kept apart from the tapu wharenui); and the *whare puni*, the low, earth-banked sleeping house. Villages at strategic sites were fortified as *pā*, with ditches, banks, palisades and terraced platforms — most densely in the north.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Māori made no pottery and worked no metal before European contact: the clay-firing tradition was lost in the eastward Polynesian migrations, and the islands had no smelted metals. The technology that carries identity is instead *whakairo rākau* (wood carving) and stone- and bone-working. *Waka huia* are lidded treasure boxes for storing head-combs and feathers, carved on every surface including the underside because they were hung from rafters and seen from below. *Waka taua* war canoes, up to 30 metres long, carried a pierced openwork prow (*tauihu*) and towering sternpost (*taurapa*). Everyday objects include *hue* (gourd) containers, *ipu* and *tahā* for water and preserved birds, *kō* digging spades, *patu muka* stone beaters for pounding flax fibre, bone and shell fishhooks (*matau*), and *toki* adzes hafted with plaited lashing. Weapons double as regalia: *mere pounamu* (greenstone hand club), *patu paraoa* of whalebone, and the two-handed *taiaha* with its carved tongue-and-face upper blade.

### Jewelry & body adornment

*Pounamu* (nephrite jade, from the rivers of Te Tai Poutini on the South Island's west coast) is the material of highest value, worked by abrasive sawing and drilling into *hei tiki* — the human-form pendant, head tilted, worn on the chest and inherited through generations, gaining mana with each wearer. Other named forms include the *hei matau* (fishhook), *pekapeka* (bat-like double figure), *koropepe* (coiled form), *kuru* and *kapeu* ear pendants, and the *pōria* bird-leg ring. Whale ivory, whale tooth (*rei puta*), bone and shell supply further pendants. Body marking is *tā moko*, cut with an *uhi* chisel of albatross bone, which grooves the skin rather than puncturing it, leaving a ridged and legible record of *whakapapa* (descent). Men's full-face *mataora* is laid out in named zones — *ngākaipikirau*, *rerepehi*, *uirere*, *pūhoro* on the thighs; women wear *moko kauae* on chin and lips. Suppressed under colonial pressure, *tā moko* and the needle-applied *kirituhi* have returned strongly since the 1990s, and *moko kauae* is now worn by Māori women in public office.

## Music & performance

*Taonga pūoro* — "treasured sound instruments" — were nearly silenced in the twentieth century and were reconstructed from museum specimens and oral fragments from the 1980s onward by Hirini Melbourne, Richard Nunns and carver Brian Flintoff. The class covers flutes: the *kōauau* (short cross-blown flute of wood, bone or albatross wing), the *nguru* (a curved nose-flute of wood, stone or sperm-whale tooth), the *pūtōrino* — unique to Māori — a bugle-shaped, two-voiced instrument with a central figure-mouth, giving a low male trumpet voice and a high female flute voice; the *pūkāea* (long wooden war trumpet), *pūtātara* (conch with wooden mouthpiece, used to announce arrivals), and *pōrotiti* (whirled disc). *Pūrerehua*, the bullroarer, was used in healing and rain rites.

Vocal genres carry the greater weight. *Mōteatea* are traditional sung poetry — laments (*waiata tangi*), love songs (*waiata aroha*), lullabies (*oriori* composed to instruct a high-born child in its genealogy) — performed in a narrow melodic range with continuous breath overlap so the line never breaks. *Karanga* is the high ceremonial call of women that opens a *pōwhiri*; *whaikōrero* is formal male oratory, opened by a *tauparapara* chant; *karakia* are incantations. *Pātere* and *kaioraro* are fast recited retorts to insult. From the 1910s Āpirana Ngata and Paraire Tomoana shaped *waiata-ā-ringa* (action songs) to European harmony — "Pōkarekare Ana" and "E Pari Rā" among them — and the guitar-backed Māori showband tradition, then reggae and hip-hop in te reo, followed.

## Dance & theatre

*Kapa haka* — the grouped performance of *haka*, *waiata-ā-ringa*, *poi*, *mōteatea* and *whakaeke* entrance — is the central performing form, held by tradition to originate with Tinirau. *Haka* are not solely war dances: *peruperu* is the leaping war haka with weapons, *tūtū ngārahu* is performed from a standing position, *haka taparahi* is ceremonial and weaponless, and *ngeri* is short and unchoreographed. "Ka Mate", composed by Te Rauparaha of Ngāti Toa, is the best-known example. *Wero*, the ritual challenge by an armed warrior, and *haka pōwhiri* open formal welcomes. *Poi* — light balls on cords, historically of raupō and flax — is danced chiefly by women in single and double long-and-short forms, struck against hand and body in rhythmic counterpoint. *Whare tapere*, pre-European entertainment houses, hosted storytelling, games, string figures (*whai*) and puppetry (*karetao*, jointed figures worked by string). Since 1972 *Te Matatini* has been held biennially as the national kapa haka competition, and Māori theatre has an established repertoire through Taki Rua and playwrights Hone Kouka, Briar Grace-Smith and Riwia Brown.

## Festivals & rituals

*Matariki*, the heliacal rising of the Pleiades in midwinter (June–July), opens the Māori new year; it marks remembrance of the dead, the reading of the cluster's stars for the coming season's harvest, and feasting, and became a public holiday in 2022. The lunar-agricultural calendar (*maramataka*) governs planting, fishing and bird-snaring nights. *Puanga* (Rigel) serves the same new-year function for western and southern iwi where Matariki sits low.

All formal gathering opens with *pōwhiri*: *wero* challenge, *karanga* call, *whaikōrero* speeches, *waiata* in support of each speaker, *koha* gift, and the *hongi* pressing of noses that makes the visitor *tangata whenua*. *Tangihanga*, the three-day funeral wake on the marae with the open coffin attended day and night and the deceased addressed directly in oratory, is the most tenacious of all Māori institutions; *kawe mate* later carries the memory of the dead to other marae, and in some traditions *hahunga* exhumed and reinterred the bones. *Tapu* and *noa* structure everything: a new wharenui is opened by *whakawātea*, and *tā moko*, burial and warfare all require lifting tapu afterwards with water and cooked food. *Rāhui* closes a place or a species to use — after a drowning, or to let a fishery recover. Naming (*tohi*) was performed over infants with water and karakia; *pūrākau* of the child's descent were sung to them as *oriori*.

## Foodways

The founding crops were Polynesian: *kūmara* (sweet potato), *taro*, *hue* (gourd) and *aruhe* (bracken fernroot), the last a staple carbohydrate pounded and chewed. Kūmara demanded elaborate management in a temperate climate — raised, gravel-mulched gardens and semi-subterranean storage pits (*rua*) — and its cultivation was ritually bound to the god Rongo. Protein came from birds (*kererū*, *tītī* muttonbird, historically moa), fish, shellfish, eel (*tuna*), crayfish (*kōura*) and the dog (*kurī*). The signature cooking method is the *hāngī*, an earth oven: stones fire-heated in a pit, food layered over damp leaves and sacking, covered with earth and steamed for hours; in the Rotorua geothermal district Ngāti Whakaue and Tūhourangi cook directly in boiling pools and steam boxes. *Rēwena parāoa* is a sourdough potato-bread developed after wheat arrived, still made from a kept potato "bug". *Kānga pirau* (or *kānga wai*) is maize steeped in running water until fermented, then boiled — a deliberately sour, strong-smelling delicacy. *Karengo* (a native laver seaweed), *pikopiko* (young fern shoots), *kawakawa* leaf and *pūhā* (sow thistle, boiled with pork bones as *pūhā me te kapana*) are gathered greens. Preservation was by drying, and by potting birds in their own rendered fat in *tahā huahua* gourds. Food is emphatically *noa*: contact with cooked food removes tapu, which is why food never enters the wharenui and why the *wharekai* stands separately.

## Oral tradition & literature

*Whakapapa* — recited genealogy, traceable from the *waka* of migration through named ancestors to the living — is the master framework of Māori knowledge, and the reason oratory carries such authority. *Pūrākau* narrate Ranginui (sky) and Papatūānuku (earth) prised apart by their son Tāne; the demigod *Māui* slowing the sun, fishing up the North Island (Te Ika-a-Māui) and dying in his attempt on Hine-nui-te-pō; *Tāwhaki* climbing to the heavens; and the *taniwha* guardians of rivers and harbours. Local traditions include Hinemoa's swim to Tūtānekai on Mokoia Island and Tinirau's dealings with the whale Tutunui. *Whakataukī* (proverbs) are the compressed form quoted in speeches — *He aha te mea nui o te ao? He tangata, he tangata, he tangata* ("What is the greatest thing? It is people") and *Ka pū te ruha, ka hao te rangatahi*. Māori adopted literacy fast in the 1830s, and iwi newspapers and the manuscript books of scribes such as Te Rangikāheke preserved a great body of narrative. The Tohunga Suppression Act 1907 and school punishment of te reo broke transmission; *kōhanga reo* from 1982, *kura kaupapa Māori*, Māori Television and the Waitangi Tribunal record have rebuilt it, alongside writers Witi Ihimaera, Patricia Grace, Keri Hulme and Hone Tuwhare.

## Language & religion

Te reo Māori is an Eastern Polynesian language of the Austronesian family, closest to Cook Islands Māori and Tahitian, with regional dialects (Ngāpuhi, Waikato-Tainui, Tūhoe, Ngāti Porou, and southern Kāi Tahu with its *k* for *ng*) differing in vocabulary and pronunciation but mutually intelligible. There was no pre-contact script: knowledge was carried in recitation, carving and *tā moko*, and the Latin orthography was fixed by missionaries in the 1820s. About a fifth of Māori speak the language to some degree. At the 2018 census, 53.5% of Māori reported no religion, 29.9% a Christian denomination, and 7.7% Māori religions and philosophies — including the prophetic movements *Rātana* and *Ringatū* (founded by Te Kooti), and historically *Pai Mārire*. Older practice persists independently of affiliation: *tapu* and *noa*, *mana*, *wairua*, *mauri* (life force), *karakia* at the start of gatherings, *rāhui*, and *tohunga* as ritual specialists.
