## Overview

The Māori are the indigenous people of Aotearoa New Zealand, descendants of Eastern Polynesian voyagers who arrived by double-hulled sailing canoe in the late thirteenth century — the last major landmass on earth to be settled by humans. Their language, te reo Māori, belongs to the Eastern Polynesian branch of Austronesian and is closest to Cook Islands Māori, Tahitian and Hawaiian, a relationship still audible in shared vocabulary for canoe, house and ancestor. Arriving in a temperate land far larger and colder than the tropical islands they had left, the settlers rebuilt their material culture around what Aotearoa offered: no pottery clay traditions survived the voyage and no loom was brought, but the country gave enormous podocarp timber, the fibrous leaf of *harakeke* (New Zealand flax), the feathers of forest birds, and the hard nephrite jade of the South Island rivers. From these came the traditions for which Māori are best known — monumental figurative wood carving, finger-woven cloaks of unmatched refinement, and greenstone ornaments and weapons. Society is organised by descent into *whānau* (extended family), *hapū* (sub-tribe) and *iwi* (tribe), each traced to a founding ancestor and to one of the ancestral canoes; the tribal homeland of Te Arawa around Rotorua, with its geothermal terraces and lakes, remains one of the great centres of carving, performance and hospitality. Roughly 900,000 people identify as Māori in New Zealand today, about a sixth of the population, with several hundred thousand more in Australia. After more than a century of land loss and language decline following the 1840 Treaty of Waitangi, a sustained renaissance since the 1970s has restored te reo Māori to official status, rebuilt *marae* across the country, and returned carving, weaving, tattooing and navigation to full living practice rather than museum memory.

## Material culture

Māori material culture is dominated by two great crafts — the carving of wood, bone and stone, and the finger-weaving of flax fibre — both governed by *tapu* (sacred restriction) and both understood as the work of ancestors made visible. Objects are not merely decorative: a carved post, a cloak or a greenstone pendant carries *mana* and a genealogy, accumulating meaning with each owner.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Without a loom, Māori developed *whatu*, a downward finger-twining technique in which weft pairs twist around hanging warps, producing cloaks of extraordinary fineness; *raranga* is the separate plaiting tradition used for mats, baskets and wall panels. The principal fibre is *muka*, the silky inner fibre stripped from *harakeke* with a mussel shell and softened by pounding.

- **Flax fibre** (*muka*) — Inner fibre of the *harakeke* leaf, scraped free with a shell, washed, pounded and rolled on the thigh into two-ply cord; the basis of all fine weaving.
- **Finger-twining** (*whatu*) — Downward twining worked on a pair of upright pegs without a loom, the defining structural technique of cloak-making.
- **Plaiting** (*raranga*) — Checkerwork and twill plaiting of whole or split leaf used for floor mats (*whāriki*), food baskets (*kete*) and platters.
- **Coloured border weaving** (*tāniko*) — Intricate geometric borders worked in dyed *muka* by a modified twining that brings colour to the surface, producing triangles, chevrons and diamonds in black, red-brown and yellow.
- **Lattice panels** (*tukutuku*) — Ornamental wall panels of horizontal laths and vertical rods stitched with dyed *kiekie* and *pīngao*, worked by two people from opposite sides in named patterns such as *poutama*, the stepped stairway of ascending knowledge.
- **Painted rafter patterns** (*kōwhaiwhai*) — Curvilinear scroll painting in red, black and white on meeting-house rafters, built from the unfurling fern-frond *koru* in named sequences.

### Clothing & dress

Pre-contact dress was based on rectangular garments worn around the waist and over the shoulders, fastened at the shoulder with a pin. The cloak is the pinnacle: a *taonga* handed down as an heirloom and still worn at graduations, weddings and funerals.

- **Cloak** (*kākahu*) — General term for the woven shoulder garment, of which many named types exist according to surface treatment.
- **Tag cloak** (*korowai*) — Cloak whose ground is covered in hanging dyed black rolled cords (*hukahuka*), one of the most widely recognised forms.
- **Feather cloak** (*kahu huruhuru*) — Cloak densely covered with feathers — historically *kiwi*, *kererū*, *kākā* and formerly *huia* — worked into the twining row by row; the highest-status garment.
- **Dog-skin cloak** (*kahu kurī*) — Prestige cloak faced with strips of the skin of the now-extinct Polynesian dog, worn by chiefs and now extremely rare.
- **Flax skirt** (*piupiu*) — Waist garment of rolled and scraped flax tubes that rattle in movement, dyed in bands with *paru* mud and today standard performance dress.
- **Cloak pin** (*aurei*) — Whale-bone or greenstone pin used to fasten the cloak at the shoulder, also worn as an ornament.

### Architecture

Māori building centres on the *marae*, the enclosed ceremonial courtyard and its buildings, which remains the living institution of tribal life. The great carved meeting house is conceived as the body of an ancestor: the ridgepole is the spine, the rafters the ribs, the facing boards the arms, and those inside are gathered in the ancestor's embrace.

- **Meeting house** (*wharenui*) — Large gabled communal house with a deep porch, carved and painted throughout, used for gatherings, debate, sleeping and funerary rites; most standing examples date from the nineteenth century onward.
- **Carved wall figures** (*poupou*) — Upright ancestral figures set along the interior walls between *tukutuku* panels, each representing a named forebear in the house's genealogy.
- **Bargeboards and arms** (*maihi*, *amo*) — The sloping facing boards of the gable and the vertical support boards at their ends, read as the outstretched arms and fingers of the ancestor.
- **Door lintel** (*pare*) — Carved lintel above the doorway, often with a central female figure and flanking *manaia*, marking the threshold between the sacred and the everyday.
- **Storehouse** (*pātaka*) — Raised storehouse on posts for preserved food and valuables, often the most elaborately carved building on a settlement.
- **Fortified settlement** (*pā*) — Terraced and palisaded hilltop settlements with ditches and banks, thousands of which remain visible as earthworks in the landscape.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Māori made neither pottery nor metal before European contact; both clay firing and metallurgy were absent from the Polynesian tradition that reached Aotearoa, and cooking was done instead in the earth oven. The equivalent skill went into carving wood, bone and stone, and into the shaping of the ocean-going canoe.

- **War canoe** (*waka taua*) — Long plank-built canoe with a towering openwork sternpost (*taurapa*) and carved prow (*tauihu*), paddled by scores of men and the supreme communal artwork of a tribe.
- **Wood carving** (*whakairo rākau*) — The overarching carving tradition, worked historically with stone adzes and later steel, in *tōtara* and other podocarps.
- **Feather box** (*waka huia*) — Small lidded box carved on all surfaces including the underside, since it hung from the rafters and was seen from below, holding *huia* feathers and combs.
- **Greenstone club** (*mere pounamu*) — Flat, spatulate short club of nephrite used in close combat with a thrusting strike, the pre-eminent chiefly heirloom.
- **Earth oven** (*hāngī*) — Pit oven of heated stones in which food is steamed under cloth and earth; in Rotorua geothermal steam and boiling pools are used directly.
- **Fish hooks** (*matau*) — Composite and one-piece hooks of bone, shell and wood, both functional and, in later ornament form, worn at the neck.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Personal adornment concentrates on *pounamu* — the nephrite and bowenite of the South Island, traded the length of the country — and on tattoo, which is less an ornament than a permanent statement of identity and rank.

- **Greenstone** (*pounamu*) — Nephrite jade, worked by laborious sawing and abrasion with sandstone; several named varieties are distinguished by colour and translucency.
- **Neck pendant** (*hei tiki*) — Stylised human figure with tilted head and hands on thighs, worn at the neck, passed between generations and accruing the *mana* of each wearer.
- **Tattoo** (*tā moko*) — Chiselled tattoo applied with bone *uhi* into grooved skin, worn full-face by men and on the chin and lips (*moko kauae*) by women, recording descent and standing; widely revived since the 1990s.
- **Ear pendant** (*kuru*, *kapeu*) — Straight or curve-tipped greenstone drops worn in the ear, often paired with a shark-tooth pendant (*mako*).
- **Cloak pin and comb** (*aurei*, *heru*) — Whale-bone pins and carved combs worn in the topknot, the head being the most *tapu* part of the body.
- **Carving motifs** (*koru*, *manaia*, *pākati*) — The unfurling frond, the profile beaked figure often read as a guardian, and the dog-tooth notching that fills surface bands; the shared grammar of carved and painted ornament.

## Music & performance

Traditional Māori music is overwhelmingly vocal and text-bearing: melody serves the words, and the repertoire is a library of genealogy, grievance, lament and welcome. Instruments — *taonga pūoro* — were largely silent for much of the twentieth century and have been reconstructed since the 1980s from museum specimens and oral memory.

- **Chant** (*waiata*) — Sung poetry in named genres including *waiata tangi* (lament), *waiata aroha* (love song) and *oriori* (lullaby composed to teach a child their descent), typically narrow in range and sung in unison.
- **Rhythmic chant** (*karakia*, *pātere*) — Incantations and fast recited chants, the former ritual and protective, the latter often composed by women in reply to slander.
- **Flutes** (*kōauau*, *nguru*) — Short cross-blown and nose-blown flutes of wood, bone or stone, played with intimate breathy tone.
- **Two-voiced flute** (*pūtōrino*) — Bugle-shaped instrument unique to Māori, with a central figured mouth, capable of both a flute voice and a trumpeted voice.
- **Shell and wood trumpets** (*pūtātara*, *pūkāea*) — Conch with wooden mouthpiece and long wooden war trumpet, used to signal and announce.
- **Swung instruments** (*pūrerehua*) — Bullroarers of wood or bone whirled on a cord, associated with weather, healing and ritual.
- **Guitar-band song** (*waiata-ā-ringa*) — Twentieth-century action song set to Western harmony and guitar, now the popular backbone of concert repertoire.

## Dance & theatre

Performance is communal and disciplined rather than theatrical in the European sense; the *kapa haka* group, standing in ranks, is the standard performing unit and competition between groups is intense.

- **Posture dance** (*haka*) — Vigorous chanted dance with stamping, thigh-slapping, *pūkana* (dilated eyes) and *whetero* (protruded tongue), performed by men and in some forms by women; the *peruperu* war form was danced with weapons, while *haka pōwhiri* welcomes guests.
- **Ka Mate** — The best-known *haka*, composed by the Ngāti Toa chief Te Rauparaha in the 1820s and made globally familiar by the national rugby team, with its authorship legally acknowledged to the tribe.
- **Action song** (*waiata-ā-ringa*) — Melodic song with synchronised hand movements illustrating the text, developed in the early twentieth century and now central to competition.
- **Poi** (*poi*) — Light balls on cords swung in rhythmic patterns against the body and hands by women, historically also used in wrist training for men.
- **Stick game** (*tītī tōrea*) — Rhythmic game in which short sticks are tapped and thrown between seated partners at increasing speed.
- **Challenge** (*wero*) — Formal challenge in which an armed warrior advances on arriving visitors and lays down a token to be picked up, testing intent before welcome.
- **Te Matatini** — The biennial national *kapa haka* festival, the largest gathering of Māori performing arts and a major driver of composition.

## Festivals & rituals

Ritual life is anchored in the *marae* and in the protocols (*tikanga*) governing encounter, death and the management of *tapu*. Rather than a calendar of saints' days, the year turns on the stars and the tribal cycle of gathering.

- **Welcome ceremony** (*pōwhiri*) — The sequence of challenge, calling (*karanga*) by women, speeches (*whaikōrero*), song and the pressing of noses (*hongi*) by which visitors are brought from the status of stranger to that of host.
- **Funeral gathering** (*tangihanga*) — Multi-day funeral on the *marae* at which the deceased lies in an open coffin surrounded by cloaks and photographs, addressed directly in speech, with kin keeping vigil; the most important of all Māori gatherings.
- **Māori New Year** (*Matariki*) — Midwinter rising of the Pleiades marking the new year, revived from the late twentieth century and made a New Zealand public holiday in 2022, observed with remembrance of the year's dead, feasting and planning.
- **Unveiling** (*hura kōhatu*) — Unveiling of the headstone roughly a year after death, drawing the family together and closing the mourning period.
- **Waitangi Day** — 6 February, marking the 1840 signing of the Treaty of Waitangi; a national holiday and an occasion for both commemoration and political protest.
- **Lifting of tapu** (*whakanoa*) — Rites, commonly involving water and cooked food, that return a person or place from sacred restriction to ordinary use, performed after funerals, building openings and illness.

## Foodways

Traditional Māori subsistence combined cultivation of tropical crops adapted to a temperate climate with intensive fishing, fowling and gathering, all regulated by seasonal prohibitions (*rāhui*) that protected stocks.

The staple cultivated crop was *kūmara*, the sweet potato, grown in raised gravel-mulched plots and stored in underground pits over winter; *taro*, *hue* (gourd) and *aruhe* (bracken fernroot) supplemented it, the last serving as the bulk starch where *kūmara* would not grow. Aotearoa's forests and coasts supplied the protein: *kererū* (wood pigeon) and *tītī* (muttonbird), the latter still harvested from southern islands under customary right by Rakiura Māori; freshwater *tuna* (eel) taken in weirs; *kaimoana* of every kind — *pāua*, *kina*, mussels, crayfish and fish — gathered under strict tribal custody of the shore. Food is steamed in the *hāngī*, and in the geothermal districts around Rotorua cooked directly in steam boxes and boiling pools, a distinctive practice of Te Arawa. Introduced foods were absorbed quickly and thoroughly: the potato transformed nineteenth-century agriculture, and pork, watercress and wheat entered the repertoire, giving the modern *marae* table its characteristic mix of *hāngī* meats, boil-up with *pūhā* (sow thistle), fried bread (*parāoa parai*) and steamed pudding. Cooked food is *noa*, the opposite of *tapu*, and is therefore used deliberately to neutralise sacredness — a reason food may not be carried into a carved house or taken near the head.

## Oral tradition & literature

Before the introduction of writing in the 1810s, Māori knowledge was carried entirely in memorised speech and song, and the prestige genres remain oral even now. *Whakapapa*, genealogy recited from the primal parents down to the living, is the organising framework of all knowledge: land claims, marriage, precedence in speech and the identity of a carved figure all rest on it. *Whaikōrero*, formal oratory on the *marae*, is judged on the skilful embedding of proverb (*whakataukī*), song and genealogical allusion, and remains a highly competitive art. The cosmological narratives — the separation of Ranginui the sky father and Papatūānuku the earth mother by their children, the exploits of the trickster-hero Māui who fished up the North Island and sought immortality, and the voyaging traditions of each ancestral canoe — are held in tribally distinct versions, and the differences between iwi accounts are themselves meaningful rather than errors. Nineteenth-century Māori were rapid adopters of literacy, producing an extensive Māori-language newspaper press (*niupepa*) between the 1840s and the 1930s, now digitised and a major historical source. A modern literature in English and te reo has followed, with Witi Ihimaera, Patricia Grace, Hone Tuwhare and Keri Hulme among its best-known figures, and film adaptations such as *Whale Rider* and *Once Were Warriors* carrying Māori narrative to international audiences.

## Language & religion

Te reo Māori is an Eastern Polynesian language with a small phoneme inventory — five vowels distinguished by length, ten consonants including the digraphs *wh* and *ng* — and dialectal variation across iwi, most audibly in the northern *wh* and the Southern substitution of *k* for *ng*. Fluency collapsed through the twentieth century under schooling policies that punished its use, falling to a small and largely elderly minority by the 1970s; the response was the *kōhanga reo* language-nest preschool movement from 1982 and *kura kaupapa* immersion schools thereafter, and te reo became an official language of New Zealand in 1987. It remains classified as vulnerable, though numbers of speakers and learners have risen substantially. Traditional religion recognises a pantheon of departmental *atua* — Tāne of the forest, Tangaroa of the sea, Rongo of cultivated food, Tūmatauenga of war and humankind — and regulates conduct through the interlocking concepts of *tapu* (restriction, sacredness), *noa* (ordinariness), *mana* (inherited and earned authority) and *mauri* (life force). Most Māori today identify as Christian, and two indigenous churches founded in Aotearoa are significant: Ringatū, founded by the prophet Te Kooti in the 1860s, and Rātana, founded by Tahupōtiki Wiremu Rātana in 1918, which combines faith healing with a continuing political alliance. In practice the older framework has not been displaced so much as layered: *karakia* open meetings of every kind, *tapu* still governs the *marae*, and rivers and mountains are addressed as ancestors — a view given legal form when the Whanganui River was recognised as a legal person in 2017.
