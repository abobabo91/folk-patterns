## Overview

Cook Islands Māori are the indigenous Polynesian people of the Cook Islands, a group composed of 15 islands and atolls in Polynesia in the Pacific Ocean. They originated from Tahitian settlers in the sixth century and share cultural affinities with New Zealand Māori and Tahitian Mā'ohi while maintaining a unique culture and language. Only 15–17% of Cook Islands Māori people now live in the Cook Islands; New Zealand and Australia each have larger populations. Cook Islands Māori is an Eastern Polynesian language and one of the Cook Islands’ two official languages. The sources document language, grammar, vocabulary, population distribution, ethnic affiliations and religious denomination.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state only that Cook Islands Māori has an “o” possessive category used for clothes.

### Architecture

They state that buildings and transport, including horses, belong to the “o” possessive category.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The language source gives *ʻapinga* as a word occurring in possessive examples and translates it as “possession(s),”.

## Music & performance

The Cook Islands Māori language source records *ʻīmene*, meaning “to sing” or “song,” and *Tārekareka*, meaning “entertain, amuse, match, game, play game,”.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that the religious distribution in the 2021 official census included Protestant groups, the Cook Islands Christian Church, Seventh-Day Adventist, Assemblies of God, Apostolic Church, the Catholic Church and the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints.

## Foodways

The language source records *Pia*, “Polynesian arrowroot,” and *Tātāpaka*, “a kind of breadfruit pudding,” as vocabulary items, but provides no further information about their preparation, use or cultural context. It also lists food and drink among the things associated with the “a” possessive category.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Cook Islands Māori language source includes a sample translation of Article 1 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in Cook Islands Māori, also called Rarotongan, but this is not described as an oral tradition or literary genre.

## Language & religion

Cook Islands Māori is an Eastern Polynesian language closely related to, but distinct from, New Zealand Māori. It is also known as Māori Kūki ʻĀirani, Maori Kuki Airani, Rarotongan and, among many Cook Islanders, *Te Reo Ipukarea*, translated as “the language of the ancestral homeland.” English and Cook Islands Māori are the official languages of the Cook Islands, and Cook Islands Māori became an official language in 2003 under the Te Reo Maori Act 2003. The language has dialect differences, an isolating grammatical structure, predicate-initial unmarked constituent order and “a” and “o” possessive categories. The sources state that the 2021 official census recorded Protestant groups as 63.2% of believers, the Cook Islands Christian Church as 43.1%, the Catholic Church as 16.6% and the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints as 3.9%.
