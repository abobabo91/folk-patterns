## Overview

Koori is a demonym for Aboriginal Australians from a region approximately corresponding to southern New South Wales and Victoria. The term derives from the Indigenous language Awabakal and has been described by some people and groups as a reclaiming of Indigenous language and culture. Indigenous subgroups in the region include the Eora nation of modern-day Sydney, Ngunnawal nation of Canberra and Woiwurrung nation of Melbourne. The Koori region contains the largest proportion of Australia's Indigenous population, although Koori-identifying people form only a small proportion of the overall populations of New South Wales and Victoria. Koori culture is characterised by commitment to the Dreaming and by an understanding of interconnectedness between land and community.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Possum-skin cloaks* were traditionally gifted to Koori newborns. They were embellished with the markings of the newborn's clan and family and added to as the child grew, representing a kind of Koori “autobiography.” The craft has declined but is being revived by contemporary Koori artists such as Kelly Koumalatsos.

**Shell craft** uses shells found in the coastal environment to decorate ornamental pieces. Koori women were documented selling shell craft baskets and decorative shoes to settler women at markets in La Perouse and Circular Quay; the practice appears unique to the Sydney area.

**Koori painting** is based largely on dot work and uses “earthy colours” such as blacks, whites, reds and browns. Some Koori elders identify this style as a means of reconnecting with traditional Indigenous culture and ancestry.

**Motif vocabulary.** Clan and family markings on possum-skin cloaks; dot work; blacks, whites, reds and browns.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe possum-skin cloaks as a distinctive Koori garment traditionally given to newborns. Their markings recorded the child's clan and family, and further markings were added as the child grew. Shell craft included decorative shoes, which Koori women sold at markets in La Perouse and Circular Quay.

### Architecture

They do record ancient structures in Western Victoria, including stone-walled fishing traps measuring up to six metres in height and three kilometres in length, and similarly aged “village” sites in South-Eastern Victoria with wooden structures, garden areas and agricultural wetlands.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention ancient human remains, tools, small tools used for processing plants and hunting, fishhooks, watercraft, weaponry, and tools.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources describe shell craft used to decorate ornamental pieces, baskets, and shoes.

## Music & performance

Historical records describe Koori birthing rituals involving song, dance and ceremonial practices.

The NSW Koori Rugby League Knockout is described as a modern-day corroboree for the Koori people of New South Wales. It has been held annually over the October long weekend since 1971 and is one of the largest gatherings of Indigenous people in Australia.

Koori Radio is a community radio station based in Redfern that broadcasts to Sydney on a citywide licence. It forms part of the Gadigal Information Service and provides full-time broadcasting to the Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander community.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention dance as part of historical Koori birthing rituals and describe the NSW Koori Rugby League Knockout as a modern-day corroboree.

## Festivals & rituals

Historical Koori birthing rituals involved song, dance and ceremonial practices. Gunditjmara Kooris of south west Victoria recorded the ritualistic use of sand heated by fire, both to warm the infant and to welcome it to country. Historical “birthing trees” were sites where mothers, families and communities gathered to deliver the baby and welcome it to country. Sometimes the placenta was buried under the tree to symbolise the newborn's connection to country.

The NSW Koori Rugby League Knockout has been held annually over the October long weekend since 1971 and is described as a modern-day corroboree.

## Foodways

They mention natural pain management and the use of heated sand in birthing practices, but these are not presented as foodways.

## Oral tradition & literature

Koori culture is characterised by commitment to the Dreaming, described as an overall worldview that values interconnectedness between land and community.

Koori art and literature continue to be produced in contemporary Australia, often with reference to traditional Indigenous artistic techniques. The sources also describe attempts to revive Koori languages, including an increase in the number of New South Wales Indigenous language speakers between 2006 and 2016 and the enactment of the New South Wales Government's Aboriginal Languages Act in 2017.

## Language & religion

The word “Koori” comes from *gurri*, meaning “man” or “people” in Awabakal, an Indigenous language spoken on the mid-north coast of New South Wales. The term may also be spelt “goori” or “goorie” on the far north coast of New South Wales. Its first documented usage occurred in 1834 as “Ko-re,” translated to mean man or mankind.

The Koori region includes many traditional Indigenous languages. Victoria has speakers of 38 Aboriginal languages, while New South Wales has historically been home to more than 70. Kooris may also speak “Koori English,” a dialect that developed from pidgin English used to communicate with settlers.
