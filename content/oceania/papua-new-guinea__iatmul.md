## Overview

The Iatmul are an ethnic group of about 10,000 people living in some two-dozen politically autonomous villages along the middle Sepik River in northern Papua New Guinea. Their communities are grouped partly by dialect of the Iatmul language and partly by sociocultural affinities. Iatmul culture is associated with art, men’s houses, male initiation, elaborate totemic systems, and the ritual called *naven*, which Gregory Bateson first studied in the 1930s. The Iatmul language is spoken around the Sepik River in East Sepik Province and belongs to the Ndu language family. The villages are traditionally organized, although a total of 46,000 speakers is estimated. The Iatmul are not a centralized tribe: villages are autonomous, and people commonly identify through their clan, lineage, village, or the regional term Sepik.

## Music & performance

The principal named Iatmul performance tradition in the sources is *naven*, a famous ritual first studied by Gregory Bateson in the 1930s.

Ritual speech is documented more clearly. Iatmul ancestral histories are encoded in long chains of complex polysyllabic names called *tsagi*, known only to ritual specialists. When *tsagi* are chanted during rituals, their names evoke ancestral migrations and places and landscape features created by mythic ancestors.

The Iatmul language itself has features relevant to spoken performance. A phonological utterance ends with falling intonation, questions are marked through variations in pitch, and focus is marked by initial high intonation followed by a fall. These linguistic features are described in the language source, but no separate musical tradition is named.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do describe a ritual called *naven*, male initiation, and ritual performances involving *tsagi*. No dates, festival seasons, or annual observances are supplied.

Iatmul origin traditions begin with a primal sea. A wind stirred waves, land surfaced, and a large pit opened from which emerged the first generation of ancestral spirits and culture-heroes. The ancestors then undertook mythic-historic migrations. Where they travelled, land appeared, and through naming they created the features of the world, including trees, mountains, stars, winds, rains, tributaries, villages, and actions.

The primal pit is said to be near the Sawos-speaking village of Gaikarobi. After emerging, most ancestors travelled to Shotmeri before dispersing throughout the region and arriving in the villages inhabited today. These accounts are connected to the organization of Iatmul society into clans, lineages, and branches. Membership is conferred at birth through the father, and each group preserves its own ancestral history of migration.

## Oral tradition & literature

Iatmul oral tradition includes legends of world creation, ancestral migrations, and the naming of the landscape. In the origin account, ancestral spirits and culture-heroes emerged from a primal pit and created the world through naming. These names are called totemic names, are claimed by specific patrilineal groups, and form the basis of the religious system.

Each clan, lineage, or branch tells its own ancestral history of migrations through the region. These histories are encoded in *tsagi*, long chains of complex polysyllabic names known only to ritual specialists. Chanted during rituals, the names recall ancestral journeys and places created by mythic ancestors. They do state that Iatmul culture was studied by Gregory Bateson, whose work included the ritual *naven*.

## Language & religion

Iatmul is spoken around the Sepik River in East Sepik Province, northern Papua New Guinea, and belongs to the Ndu language family, which consists of at least six languages. The Iatmul do not usually call their language Iatmul; they call it *gepmakudi*, meaning “village language,” from *gepma*, “village,” and *kudi*, “speech.” The inhabitants of the villages are trilingual in Tok Pisin, Iatmul, and some English, while Tok Pisin is the first language of the youngest children despite efforts to reverse this trend.

Iatmul communities are associated with dialect differences and sociocultural affinities. The language has masculine and feminine gender marking and singular, dual, and plural numbers. Its counting system combines quinary, decimal, and vigesimal elements, although many speakers cannot count correctly beyond “twenty” in Iatmul because other counting systems are increasingly used.

The religious system is based on totemic names, which are connected to ancestral spirits, culture-heroes, migrations, and the creation of the world through naming.
