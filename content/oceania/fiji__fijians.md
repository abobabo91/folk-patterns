## Overview

Fijians are the indigenous Austronesian people of the Fiji Islands, descended from Lapita seafarers who arrived around 3,500 years ago. Their ancestors built the distinctive pottery found in Fiji from 800 BCE, with later Melanesian arrivals and trade contact with Tonga and Samoa adding cultural layers. They speak Fijian and English, and are known across the Pacific as expert canoe builders and master craftspeople in barkcloth, whale ivory, and kava ceremonial gear. Today about 70 percent farm at subsistence level, growing sugar cane, cassava, rice, sweet potatoes and bananas.

## Material culture

Fijian material culture centers on textiles (barkcloth and plaited mats), whale-ivory prestige goods, and wooden vessels and weapons, with significant imported materials like glass beads appearing in later pieces.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Barkcloth beaten from bark and decorated with pigment dominates the textile collection, alongside plaited mats of woven vegetable fiber.

- **Barkcloth** (*masi*) — Beaten from bark and decorated with brown, red and black pigment in geometric fields, sometimes combined with painted and stencilled work.
- **Masi kesa** — A named barkcloth panel type from the mid- to late nineteenth century.
- **Plaited mats** — Large square mats of closely interwoven vegetable fiber, straw-colored with fringed edges, used as sitting surfaces for ceremony.

### Clothing & dress

Women wear fiber skirts and waist garments; men wear cloth wraps with ornaments, wigs of human hair, and ceremonial dress.

- **Liku** — A skirt of vegetable fiber strands hanging from a plaited waistband, sometimes using dyed fibers or strung with glass beads.
- **Big-head wig** — A headdress of human hair built on a base of interwoven leaf strips and secured with twisted fiber cord, worn by chiefs and men.
- **Cloth wrap** — A waist wrap worn by men, often paired with arm, ear and neck ornaments.
- **Ceremonial dance dress** — Layered barkcloth skirts worn by women in rows with personal ornaments.

### Architecture

Buildings feature thatched roofs rising from stone-wall bases, with structures framed and bound by cord rather than nailed together.

- **Spirit house** — A small religious structure made of coconut-fiber cord worked over a reed foundation, framed and bound rather than nailed.
- **Thatched dwelling** — A full-size building with thatched roof and stone walls at its base, documented in photographs from Nagarawai.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Fijians crafted unglazed earthenware vessels with compound forms, kava bowls and ladles, and wooden weapons and utensils.

- **Saqa moli** — A vessel of four small containers joined by four large handles meeting at an apex, with patterned top half and plain bottom.
- **Saga ni wai** — A flat-bottomed, semi-circular water carrier with dot-and-groove decoration around the shoulders.
- **Kava bowl** — A wooden bowl cut from a single piece and stained with kava patina, sometimes with an openwork stand threaded with cord.
- **Totokia club** (*totokia*) — A club with a pineapple-shaped carved head and coconut-fiber binding.
- **Fly whisk** (*i roi*) — A wooden implement for waving away flies.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Whale ivory is the prestige material, fashioned into revered teeth, breast plates, and necklaces; boar's tusk and beaded ornaments also appear.

- **Tabua** — A revered whale's tooth used in public and private ceremony, considered sacred and sometimes incised with designs.
- **Whale-tooth breast plate** — A roughly circular ornament assembled from five to eight pieces of cachalot ivory pegged together and suspended on fiber ties.
- **Whale-tooth necklace** — A necklace stringing six to fifteen carved and polished whale-tooth pendants on twisted coconut cord with shell disc spacers.
- **Bati ni vuaka** — A boar's-tusk neck pendant worn by chiefs.
- **Tattooing equipment** (*tata*) — A flat heart-shaped wooden mallet and bone instruments used for tattooing.

## Music & performance

Wind and percussion instruments include conch-shell trumpets, reed pipes, and wooden slit instruments and drums.

- **Davui** — A conch-shell trumpet made from the shell of Ranella lampas with fiber attachments.
- **Reed pipe** — A bamboo, shell and wood pipe used as a wind instrument.
- **Slit instrument** — A wooden drum hollowed from a solid log into a semi-elliptical trough, used for percussion.

## Dance & theatre

Documented dances feature women in rows wearing ceremonial layered barkcloth skirts with personal ornaments.

- **Ceremonial dance** — A dance with women in rows wearing layered barkcloth skirts and western-style tops with personal ornaments.

## Festivals & rituals

Ceremonial life centers on yaqona (kava) drinking and the tabua (sacred whale's tooth) presented on public and private occasions.

- **Yaqona** — An infusion prepared from the root of Piper methysticum, drunk ceremonially and now used daily to welcome visitors and bond community.
- **Kava ceremony** (*grog*) — Preparation and drinking of yaqona as a ritual framing storytelling sessions and social time.
- **Tabua ceremony** (*tabua*) — Presentation of the sacred whale's tooth on public and private occasions to bond the community.
- **Bose Levu Vakaturaga** — The Great Council of Chiefs, composed of 55 chiefs from fourteen provinces, which met yearly to discuss indigenous Fijian concerns until disbanded after the 2006 coup.

## Foodways

About 70 percent of indigenous Fijians farm at subsistence level, growing sugar cane, cassava, rice, sweet potatoes and bananas; yaqona is the documented prepared consumable.

- **Yaqona** — An infusion from the pounded root of the pepper plant, drunk from coconut-shell cups in ceremonial and daily settings.

## Oral tradition & literature

Storytelling is documented only as an occasion for kava drinking; no epics, tale cycles or named recitation genres appear in the sources.

## Language & religion

Fijians speak a Central Pacific Austronesian language; they are overwhelmingly Christian, with the Methodist Church, Roman Catholicism and other denominations predominating.

- **Fijian language** (*Kaiviti*) — The indigenous word for a Fijian person; Fijian belongs to the Central Pacific branch of Austronesian languages.
- **ITaukei** — Meaning 'owners of the land,' the official term for indigenous Fijians since the 2010 Fijian Affairs Decree.
- **Methodist Church of Fiji and Rotuma** — The largest Christian denomination, claimed by 64.5 percent in the 2007 census.
- **Old religion** — The earlier indigenous practice under which kava was restricted to chiefs and priests, now largely replaced by Christianity.
