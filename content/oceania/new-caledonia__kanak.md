## Overview

The Kanak are the indigenous people of New Caledonia, making up 41.2% of the territory's population. They speak a diverse set of Austronesian languages and maintain a rich material and ceremonial culture centered on ancestral connections. Their traditions include distinctive architectural forms, jade working, decorated textiles, and ceremonial practices tied to clans and life events.

## Material culture

Kanak material culture combines worked stone, jade, vegetable fibre, flying-fox fur and engraved bamboo into objects that mark status, record village life, and connect the living to ancestors.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Kanak fibre work and beaten-bark traditions carry geometric patterns and images tied to daily life and ancestry.

- **Tapa** — A bark cloth often made from banyan, worked into small pieces and used to wrap Kanak bead money.
- **Fibre and fur garment work** — Women's garments built from vegetable fibre and vegetable-fibre cord, sometimes combined with flying-fox fur.
- **Engraved bamboo** — Bamboo canes fired to blacken engraved designs, showing scenes of the pilou dance, agricultural motifs, and village life such as fishing or the building of a great house.

### Clothing & dress

Kanak dress combines plant-material ornaments, vegetable fibre garments, and missionary-period cotton dresses, with dancers painting themselves and wearing wooden masks.

- **Headbands and armbands** — Men wore headbands of plant material, armbands, and feathers in their hair.
- **Grass skirts** — Women wore grass skirts and necklaces in the nineteenth century.
- **Penis sheath** — Men wore penis sheaths as part of ceremonial dress.
- **Missionary-period dresses** — Long sewn dresses of imported printed cotton with yokes, frills, lace trim and cross-over strips at the back.
- **Wooden masks** — Dancers wear wooden masks made of bark, feathers and leaves to cement relationships with the ancestors.

### Architecture

The chief's great house is a conical structure defined by its carved spire, which represents ancestral spirits and marks the boundary between the worlds of the living and the dead.

- **Grande case** — The great house, the chief's hut, is a conical structure standing in forest clearings.
- **Flèche faîtière** — A spear-like carved spire representing ancestral spirits, with three parts: a flat crowned face for the ancestor, a rounded pole run through by mounted conch shells for the ancestor's voice, and a base symbolising the link between clan and chief.
- **Carved door-posts and door boards** — Doorways were framed with carved wooden door-posts carrying a face and carved door boards.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Stone and wood supply the cutting edge and decorative forms in Kanak material culture, from polished stone axes to carved wooden weapons and fired bamboo.

- **Lapita pottery** (*Lapita*) — Pottery dating to about 1000 BC, made from clay deposits and decorated with geometric patterns and stylised human faces, with pinhole-incised designs made with tooth combs.
- **Stone ceremonial axes** (*pilou*) — Axes of jade or serpentinite, polished smooth like a disc, which stood for a clan's strength and were used in warfare and at ancestor celebrations called pilou.
- **Wooden clubs** — Heavy brown wood clubs with mushroom-shaped heads, half-ovoid knobbed heads, phallic heads, or bird's-beak forms, used as weapons.
- **Engraved bamboo** — Metre-length canes dated between 1850 and 1920, fired to give engraved parts a black patina, showing scenes of village life and stuffed with magic herbs to ward off evil spirits.
- **Pirogues** — Large double-hulled outriggers with triangular sails used for fishing.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Jade and nephrite beadwork is the most striking ornament, strung with flying-fox fur tassels and worn with shell armlets, bamboo combs and feathered headbands.

- **Meciwe** — A jade bead necklace strung from 155 drilled jade beads with tassels of plaited flying-fox fur.
- **Jade and nephrite necklaces** — Necklaces with carved and polished jade or nephrite beads drilled and strung on twisted vegetable fibre.
- **Shell armlets** — Armlets cut from cone shell.
- **Bamboo combs** — Combs with blackened teeth and incised upper parts, or with a convex, partially carved face.
- **Mourning mask** — A mask combining a human face carved in wood with human hair bound on with vegetable fibres, bamboo and fibre plaited into the hair, and feathers fixed at the sides and back.

## Music & performance

Kanak music uses conch shells, rhythm instruments like the bwanjep and jew's harp, and flutes, with modern kaneka blending reggae with traditional Kanak languages.

- **Conch shells** — Blown by an appointed person to announce a clan chief's arrival or to stand for the voice of an ancestor.
- **Bwanjep** — A rhythm instrument played by a group of men during ceremonies.
- **Wadohnu** — The jew's harp, made from a dried piece of coconut palm leaf held between the teeth with an attached segment of soft nerve leaf.
- **Kaneka** — A modern genre associated with the Kanak, a form of reggae with added flutes, percussion and harmonies, often with political lyrics.

## Dance & theatre

The pilou-pilou is the distinctive Kanak dance form recounting clan stories, involving stomping, bark-clappers, and hundreds of dancers singing, though it was banned and the last performance was in 1951.

- **Pilou-pilou** — The distinctive Kanak dance form recounting the stories of the clans, involving stomping with bamboo tubes and the beating of bark-clappers, with hundreds of dancers singing in duets punctuated by shrieks and whistles.
- **Welcome dances** — Dances performed by groups that are popular today, with dancers trained in traditional forms in special workshops.

## Festivals & rituals

Kanak life-cycle events and ancestor celebrations bring clans together through dance, music and singing, with the death of a chief removing and relocating the carved spire.

- **Pilou** — Ancestor celebrations where ceremonial stone axes of jade or serpentinite were used.
- **Life-cycle events** — Birth, marriage, circumcision and the death of a chief are occasions for dance, music and singing.
- **Festival des Arts du Pacifique** — The Festival of Pacific Arts, held every four years.

## Foodways

Bougna is the Kanak casserole treated as a national dish, made from root vegetables and coconut milk wrapped in banana leaves and cooked over hot stones.

- **Bougna** — A casserole made from sliced root vegetables such as taro, manioc and yams with coconut milk, pork, chicken or seafood, wrapped in banana leaves and cooked over hot stones in an earthen oven.
- **Flying foxes and local deer** — Traditional Kanak cooking ingredients alongside marine foods.
- **Lagoon and coral-reef fish** — Marine staples including dawa, along with crabs and lobsters.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kanak knowledge comes from an oral tradition passed through poems, legends and stories, with modern authors and institutions supporting Kanak language and culture.

- **La Présence Kanak** — A work by Jean-Marie Tjibaou, the leader of the independence movement.
- **Écoles populaires kanak** — Schools established by Tjibaou that taught spiritual and practical knowledge through local Kanak languages alongside French and English.
- **Jean-Marie Tjibaou Cultural Centre** — A cultural centre in Nouméa formally established in May 1998 to support Kanak culture and language.

## Language & religion

Kanak languages belong to the New Caledonian subgroup of Oceanic, diversified into 33 mutually unintelligible languages, with most Kanak following Christianity and some traditional beliefs.

- **Drehu** — The most widely spoken of the Kanak languages.
- **Cèmuhî and Paicî** — Northern Kanak languages that have developed lexical tones.
- **Kanak Language Academy** — A provision of the Nouméa Accord supporting the use and preservation of Kanak languages.
