## Overview

The Huli live in the highlands of Hela Province, around 1,600 meters above sea level. Their homeland includes forests, marshes, grasslands, rivers, and mounded gardens. Their material culture includes tools, weapons, garments, wigs, belts, ornaments, and ceremonial costumes. Their intangible culture includes oral histories, genealogies, traditions, and a special language used during bush activities.

## Material culture

Huli material culture uses plant fibre, bark, wood, cane, shells, feathers, fur, bone, pigment, and stone.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources do not document named Huli textile traditions, but they record fibre work and several decorative techniques.

- **Carved chevrons** — An adze has carved chevrons on its wooden upper section, with the cuts filled with red pigment.
- **Burned bamboo designs** — A bamboo tobacco-pipe has designs made by burning the bamboo.
- **Red-pigment decoration** — Red pigment appears on an adze, belts, arm-bands, a hornbill-beak neck ornament, and a tobacco-pipe.

### Clothing & dress

Huli clothing includes plant-fibre skirts, bark loin cloths, aprons, belts, wigs, and ceremonial costumes.

- **Fibre aprons** — Apron-like garments are made from vegetable-fibre cord or woven brown vegetable-fibre string.
- **Fibre-cord fringe** — One apron has a fibre-cord fringe ending in short pieces of yellow cane.
- **Bark and fibre belts** — Belts may use bark wrapped with brown fibre strips or curved rattan strips bound with fibre cord.
- **Plant-fibre skirts and loin cloths** — Photographs show Huli men wearing plant-fibre skirts and bark loin cloths.
- **Ceremonial costumes** — Ceremonial costumes include black plant-fibre wigs, feather headdresses, skirts or loin cloths, arm ornaments, necklaces, and decorated poles or weapons.

### Architecture

The sources do not cover Huli houses, settlements, roofs, or named buildings.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Huli tools and weapons are mainly made from wood, cane, fibre, claws, pigment, and polished stone.

- **Axes** — Axes have wooden hafts, cane bindings, and small polished stone blades.
- **Adzes** — Adzes combine wooden pieces bound with cane, and some have cassowary claws as cutting points.
- **Bows** — Bows are made of wood and cane.
- **Arrows** — Arrows are made from wood, cane, vegetable fibre, claw, and pigment.
- **Bamboo tobacco-pipe** — A bamboo tobacco-pipe has a closed node and burned designs.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Huli adornment combines shells, pig tusks, feathers, fibre, fur, paint, and other natural materials.

- **Pearl-shell breast ornament** — One object is a polished pearl-shell breast ornament.
- **Cowrie-shell necklaces** — Cowrie shells are mounted on curved wooden strips to make necklaces.
- **Hornbill-beak neck ornament** — A neck ornament is made with a hornbill beak and red pigment.
- **Pig-tusk necklaces** — Photographs and ceremonial costumes show necklaces made with pig tusks.
- **Composite wigs** — Wigs combine vegetable fibre, human hair, feathers, leaves, snake skin, cuscus fur, and other materials.

## Music & performance

The sources do not document Huli musical instruments, song genres, performance settings, or named music traditions.

## Dance & theatre

The sources do not document named Huli dances or theatre, but photographs show ceremonial dress and decorated ceremonial poles or weapons.

- **Ceremonial poles and weapons** — Ceremonial poles or weapons are decorated with feathers.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do not describe a festival calendar or named rites, but they identify some objects and photographs as ceremonial.

- **Ceremonial costume** — Ceremonial clothing includes wigs, feather headdresses, plant-fibre skirts or loin cloths, arm ornaments, necklaces, and decorated poles or weapons.

## Foodways

The sources do not describe Huli foods or cooking, but they mention collecting karuka nuts during a bush taboo practice.

- **Bush divide taboo** (*tayenda tu ha illili*) — This special language is used while collecting karuka nuts, called anga, and while hunting or travelling.
- **Karuka nuts** (*anga*) — The Huli collect karuka nuts while using the bush divide taboo language.

## Oral tradition & literature

Huli people preserve history and folklore through family genealogies, traditions, and long oral histories.

- **Family genealogies** — Knowledge of family genealogy helps Huli people understand their history and folklore.
- **Clan oral histories** — Huli people recount lengthy oral histories about individuals and clans.

## Language & religion

Huli is a Tari language with a special pandanus vocabulary used during bush activities, while the sources do not describe the current religious landscape.

- **Huli language** (*Huli*) — Huli is a Tari language spoken by the Huli people of Hela Province.
- **Pandanus language** (*tayenda tu ha illili*) — This specialized language is used for collecting anga, hunting, and travelling, partly to evade malevolent bush spirits.
- **Pentadecimal counting** (*ngui*) — Huli uses a base-fifteen number system in which ngui means 15.
- **Thirty** (*ngui ki*) — In the Huli number system, ngui ki means 30.
- **Two hundred twenty-five** (*ngui ngui*) — In the Huli number system, ngui ngui means 225.
