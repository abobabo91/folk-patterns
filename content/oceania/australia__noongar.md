## Overview

The Noongar are Aboriginal Australian people who live in the south-west corner of Western Australia, from Geraldton on the west coast to Esperance on the south coast. The Noongar cultural bloc comprises 14 groups: Amangu, Ballardong, Yued, Kaneang, Koreng, Mineng, Njakinjaki, Njunga, Pibelmen, Pindjarup, Wadandi, Whadjuk, Wiilman and Wudjari. Noongar people refer to their land as Noongar boodja. Their languages belong to the Pama–Nyungan language family, and contemporary Noongar speech includes Australian Aboriginal English with Noongar vocabulary. In the 2011 Australian census, 10,549 people identified as Indigenous in south-western Western Australia; by 2021, this number had increased to 14,355. Noongar culture includes seasonal ecological knowledge, hunting and gathering, language revival, oral traditions, visual arts, theatre and contemporary opera.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources name one specific textile tradition:

**Buka** — *Buka* is a traditional cloak made of kangaroo skin.

### Clothing & dress

The *buka* is a traditional Noongar cloak made of kangaroo skin. The sources mention that a monumental canvas titled “Ngallak Koort Boodja – Our Heart Land Canvas” was unveiled at the 2006 Perth International Arts Festival, but this is a work of visual art rather than a clothing tradition. The canvas was commissioned by representatives of united elders and families from across the Noongar nation and painted by Shane Pickett, Tjyllyungoo, Yvonne Kickett, Alice Warrell and Sharyn Egan.

### Architecture

They do state that many Noongar people live in country towns throughout the south-west and in Perth, Mandurah, Bunbury, Geraldton, Albany and Esperance. The Western Australian government dedicated areas for communities specifically for Noongar people, including the now closed Swan Valley Noongar Community, but the sources provide no architectural description of these places.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The *kodj*, also called *kodja*, is a Noongar hafted axe. The word *kodj* is glossed as “to be hit on the head,” and Kojonup and The Kodja Place visitor centre in Kojonup are named after it. Hunting and food-gathering equipment is mentioned only in general terms: people hunted and trapped game, spearfished, and culled fish in traps.

## Music & performance

The sources provide limited information about Noongar music and performance. In October 2021, the first Noongar opera, *Koolbardi wer Wardong*, opened. It was written by Gina Williams and Guy Ghouse and performed at His Majesty’s Theatre by members of West Australian Opera, West Australian Young Voices, Noongar Children’s Choir and the Western Australian Youth Orchestra.

Noongar language has also been used in contemporary song. Singer-songwriter Gina Williams promoted the language through songs, including lullabies for children and a translation of “Moon River.” In July 2022, King Gizzard & the Lizard Wizard released a special edition of *Butterfly 3000* with all its 10 songs translated into Noongar by Merinda Hansen and Lois Spehn-Jackson; the proceeds from this edition were donated to the Langford Aboriginal Association.

## Dance & theatre

The Noongar theatre company Yirra Yaakin describes itself as responding to the Aboriginal community’s need for positive self-enhancement through artistic expression. It seeks community development and aims to create “exciting, authentic and culturally appropriate indigenous theatre.”

Noongar culture is associated with a substantial contemporary theatrical repertoire. Jack Davis’s plays are included in the school syllabus in several Australian states. His first full-length play, *Kullark*, a documentary about the history of Aboriginal people in Western Australia, was first produced in 1979. Other plays named by the sources are *No Sugar*, *The Dreamers*, *Barungin: Smell the Wind*, *In Our Town*, *Honey Spot* and *Moorli and the Leprechaun*. An adaptation and translation of Shakespeare’s *Macbeth* into Noongar was performed at the 2020 Perth Festival. Named *Hecate*, it was produced by Yirra Yaakin Theatre Company with Bell Shakespeare and performed by an all-Noongar cast.

## Festivals & rituals

The Noongar seasonal calendar has six seasons: *Birak*, *Bunuru*, *Djeran*, *Makuru*, *Djilba* and *Kambarang*. Their timing corresponds to observable environmental changes and to movement between habitats and seasonal feeding patterns.

*Birak* occurs in December and January and is dry and hot; sections of scrubland were burned to force animals into the open for easier hunting. *Bunuru*, in February and March, is the hottest part of the year and has sparse rainfall. *Djeran*, in April and May, brings cooler weather; fishing continued and bulbs and seeds were collected. *Makuru*, in June and July, is associated with cold fronts and is usually the wettest part of the year. *Djilba*, in August and September, is often the coldest season, with clear cold nights and days or warmer, rainy and windy periods. *Kambarang*, in October and November, brings a definite warming trend, longer dry periods, fewer cold fronts and the height of the wildflower season.

The sources also describe Noongar rituals connected with early contact. Noongar rituals celebrated the reception of European newcomers in a ceremonial form. The arrival of Europeans was understood by many Noongar as the return of deceased people, sometimes imagined as relatives deserving accommodation. The newcomers were called *djaanga* or *djanak*, meaning “white spirits.”

They do mention *Welcome to Country* ceremonies, for which Elders are increasingly asked to provide formal welcomes.

## Foodways

Noongar subsistence historically combined hunting, trapping, fishing and the gathering of wild plants. Game included kangaroos, possums and wallabies. People living near the coast or river systems spearfished and culled fish in traps. Edible wild plants included yams and wattle seeds. Nuts of the zamia palm were eaten during *Djeran*, in April and May, but required extensive treatment to remove their toxicity.

The sources describe the seasonal importance of food and fire. During *Birak*, in December and January, Noongar people burned sections of scrubland to force animals into the open for easier hunting. During *Djeran*, fishing continued while bulbs and seeds were collected. In modern times, rabbit became an important part of the Noongar diet in the early 20th century.

Contemporary bush-tucker activities may include kangaroo, emu, quandong jam or relish, bush tomatoes, witchetty grub pâté and bush honey. In some areas of south-western Western Australia, visitors can join bush-tucker walks. Many country Noongar people continue to hunt kangaroo and gather bush tucker.

## Oral tradition & literature

Noongar oral culture includes stories about the land. Many country Noongar people teach their children stories connected with the country. In Perth, the Darling Scarp is said to represent the body of *Wagyl*, a snakelike Dreamtime creature and common deity in Noongar culture. *Wagyl* is thought to have created the Swan River while moving across the land and creating rivers, waterways and lakes. Mount Eliza was an important hunting site where kangaroos were herded and driven over the edge for meat. At its base is a sacred site where *Wagyl* is said to have rested during its journeys.

Noongar culture is particularly strong in written literature. Jack Davis’s plays include *Kullark*, *No Sugar*, *The Dreamers*, *Barungin: Smell the Wind*, *In Our Town*, *Honey Spot* and *Moorli and the Leprechaun*. Kim Scott won the 2000 Miles Franklin Award for *Benang* and the 2011 award for *That Deadman Dance*.

Language preservation and literary work continue through projects such as Noongarpedia, a bilingual wiki intended as a teaching aid in schools. The project began under the leadership of Len Collard and was continuing in April 2021.

## Language & religion

Noongar, also called Nyungar, is an Australian Aboriginal language or dialect continuum spoken by some members of the Noongar community and others. It belongs to the Pama–Nyungan language family. Before European settlement, there was no single standard Noongar language; the region contained closely related languages and varieties differentiated by geography and, in some cases, cultural practices. The modern Noongar language developed after colonisation as dialects merged.

The sources name varieties and related forms including Wudjari, Minang, Bibelman or Pibelman, Kaneang or Kaniyang, Wardandi, Balardung or Ballardong, Yuat or Juat, Wiilman, Whadjuk or Wajuk, Pinjarup, Koreng or Goreng, Njakinjaki or Nyakinyaki and Njunga or Nunga. Noongar was first recorded in 1801 by Matthew Flinders. On the 2016 census, 443 people identified themselves as speaking Noongar at home.
