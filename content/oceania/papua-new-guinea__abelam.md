## Overview

The Abelam live in East Sepik Province of Papua New Guinea. Farming is central, especially growing giant yams. Their crops include yams, taro, bananas, and sweet potatoes, with gathered foods, domestic animals, and hunted animals adding to the diet. Museum collections show objects linked to adornment, music, ceremonies, and daily use.

## Material culture

Abelam material culture includes fibre bags and masks, shell and feather adornments, carved wood and bone objects, stone tools, and coconut-shell vessels.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Fibre work includes patterned net bags, braided masks, and ornaments used in ceremonies and dancing.

- **Net bag** (*Nätväska*) — A Wosera net bag uses natural fibre with brown patterned sections.
- **Colored-thread net bag** — This Wosera bag uses brown, faint red, and blue threads and has a band that can be worn as a headband.
- **Rattan mask** — A Maprik facial mask is braided from rattan with spiral work and traces of red, brown, and grey-white colour.
- **Yam mask** — Yam masks use rattan, palm-leaf ribs, grass, hair, and chalk, with some black and red paint.
- **Karawut** — This plaited-fibre ornament shows a stylised human figure and is coloured red, black, and yellow.

### Clothing & dress

The sources show adornments and carrying equipment.

- **Headband bag** — A Wosera net bag has a carrying band that serves as a headband.
- **Feather hair ornament** — A Malmba hair ornament is a long, narrow light-wood plate with feathers inserted into it.
- **Feathered comb** — A Niamingum comb has five pointed wooden pins, plaiting, and feathers.
- **Shell necklace** — A Malmba necklace has kauri shells strung on a cord.
- **Karawut dance ornament** (*karawut*) — The karawut can be worn on the back or carried in the mouth when dancing.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Everyday objects include carved bone, stone, coconut shell, and wood items.

- **Bone dagger** — Bone daggers have incised ornament.
- **Cross-axe blade** — A Maprik cross-axe blade is polished light-grey stone with a pointed neck and convex sections.
- **Coconut-shell bowl** — A bowl from Maundu or Malmba has low-relief cutwork underneath and a toothed edge.
- **Wooden stool** — A Maundu stool is cut from one piece of light wood and has two plates joined by five outward-curving legs.
- **Bird-shaped clamp** — A Maundu wooden clamp is carved in one piece and has a bird form at the top.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment uses kauri shells, feathers, wood, fibre, and boar’s tusks.

- **Kauri-shell necklace** — A Malmba necklace is made from kauri shells on a cord.
- **Feather hair plate** — A narrow light-wood plate holds inserted feathers.
- **Feathered comb** — The Niamingum comb has five pointed pins, feathers, and a braided upper part.
- **Headband net bag** — A Wosera net bag has a carrying band used as a headband.
- **Shell-and-tusk karawut** (*karawut*) — This karawut has rows of cut nassa shells and boar’s tusks, with red, black, and yellow pigment.

## Music & performance

Performance objects include a coconut-shell flute and ornaments and masks used in dancing and yam ceremonies.

- **Coconut-shell flute** — A Maundu vessel flute has two round holes, incised decoration, and a zigzag pattern.
- **Karawut dance ornament** (*karawut*) — The karawut is worn on the back or carried in the mouth during dancing.
- **Men’s yam ceremony mask** — Basketry masks for men’s yam ceremonies use dark brown, yellow, red, and white colours.
- **Yam cult mask** — A yam cult mask is made from vegetable fibre with chain-like fibre appendages and traces of red pigment.

## Dance & theatre

Dancing is linked with the karawut and masks used in yam ceremonies.

- **Karawut** — The karawut is worn on the back or carried in the mouth when dancing.
- **Rattan dance mask** — A rattan mask is a braided facial mask with a loop marking the nose.
- **Yam ceremony mask** — Basketry masks are connected with men’s yam ceremonies.
- **Yam cult mask** — A vegetable-fibre mask is identified as a yam cult mask.

## Festivals & rituals

Yam festivals connect farming, village status, rivalry, food giving, and rules about emotion and behaviour.

- **Yam festival** — At yam festivals, villages compete through the size of their yams and the food they provide.
- **Enemy yam exchange** — A man gives his largest yam to his worst enemy, who must grow an even larger one or lose status.
- **Giant yam cultivation** — Large yams can reach 80–90 inches, or 2.3 metres, and help determine the status of people and villages.
- **Yam-growing taboo** — During the growing season, fighting and sexual activity are taboo because strong emotions are thought to hinder yam growth.
- **Yam ceremony masks** — Museum records identify several masks as men’s yam ceremony masks or yam cult masks.

## Foodways

Abelam food comes from crops, rainforest gathering, domestic animals, and hunting.

- **Yams** — Yams are a main crop and have major social importance.
- **Taro** — Taro is one of the main Abelam crops.
- **Bananas** — Bananas are one of the main Abelam crops.
- **Sweet potatoes** — Sweet potatoes are one of the main Abelam crops.
- **Hunted animals** — People hunt small marsupials and cassowaries and raise pigs and chickens.

## Language & religion

Ambulas is a Sepik language with several named dialects, while the sources give only limited information about religion.

- **Ambulas language** (*Ambulas*) — Ambulas, also called Abelam or Abulas, belongs to the Ndu languages of the Sepik River region.
- **Maprik dialect** (*Maprik*) — Maprik is one of the named Ambulas dialects.
- **Wingei dialect** (*Wingei*) — Wingei is one of the named Ambulas dialects.
- **Wosera-Kamu dialect** (*Wosera-Kamu*) — Wosera-Kamu is one of the named Ambulas dialects.
- **Wosera-Mamu dialect** (*Wosera-Mamu*) — Wosera-Mamu is one of the named Ambulas dialects.
