## Overview

The Tolai live in East New Britain and nearby islands of Papua New Guinea. Their main language is Kuanua, and it is widely used in Kokopo and Rabaul. Museum objects include fishing gear, tools, drums, masks, dance-wands, and a carved figure. Christian belief is widespread, but Tubuan traditions, Duk-Duk ceremonies, and sorcery beliefs continue.

## Material culture

Tolai material culture is known through fibre fishing gear, carved and painted wood objects, drums, masks, dance-wands, and tools.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources do not describe a Tolai textile tradition, but they show fibre nets and richly decorated wooden dance objects.

- **Fishing net** — This long rectangular seine net is made from vegetable fibre, with stone sinkers and orange wood floats.
- **Decorated end floats** — The large wooden end floats have cane plaiting and painted designs of a human figure, turtles, fish, and plant-like shapes.
- **Lor dance mask** (*lor*) — This composite mask has a painted wooden face, fibre beard and hair, a feather-edged disk, and a peacock figure.
- **Dance-wand** — This flat wooden wand shows a human face with a tall fretwork headdress, feathers, beads, pearl shell, and fibre hair.
- **Dance-wand fragment** — This triangular wooden fretwork piece is painted red, yellow, green, and white and has feathers at the top.

### Clothing & dress

The sources do not describe everyday Tolai clothing or ceremonial dress, but some dance objects use fibre, feathers, and European textile.

- **Fibre beard and hair** — Masks and dance objects have beards and hair made from vegetable fibre.
- **Feather headdress** — Dance objects include headdresses and other ornaments made with white, yellowish, black, and red feathers.
- **Textile-covered hat** — One carved mask has a hat covered with red European textile.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Documented objects include fishing gear, wood tools, drums, masks, dance-wands, and a stone figure; ceramics and metalwork are not described.

- **Elbow-shaped adze** — This wood adze has a shell blade fixed with coconut-fibre lashing.
- **Fish-hook** — This wooden hook for Ruvettus? has coconut-fibre binding and is attached to a wooden rod by a fibre snood.
- **Stone figure** (*ingut*) — A stone ingut figure is among the documented wooden and stone objects.
- **Slit-drum** — This small wooden drum has a central tongue and cylindrical handles at both ends.
- **Drum** — This hour-glass-shaped wooden drum has a lizard-skin (?) membrane, a handle, carved designs, and European paint.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources do not explain Tolai jewelry or body-adornment practices, but museum objects include beads, pearl shell, and nose-pins.

- **Glass beads** — Glass beads appear on a dance-mask.
- **Pearl-shell eyes** — A dance-wand has eyes made from pearl shell.
- **Nose-pin** — A stick nose-pin appears on one face of a dance-wand and on a carved mask.

## Music & performance

Tolai performance objects include drums and slit-drums.

- **Dance drum** — A wooden drum is recorded as being used in dance.
- **Slit-drum** — A small wooden slit-drum has a central tongue and handles.
- **Kai.Tupi drum** (*Kai.Tupi*) — One drum bears the inscription “Kai.Tupi” near its handle.

## Dance & theatre

Dance equipment and Duk-Duk ceremonies are documented, but named dances and theatrical plots are not.

- **Duk-Duk society** (*Duk-Duk*) — The Duk-Duk society holds secret ceremonies performed by initiates.
- **Lor dance mask** (*lor*) — The lor mask has a painted face, fibre beard and hair, a feather-edged disk, and a peacock figure.
- **Face dance-wand** — One dance-wand represents a human face with a fretwork headdress, feathers, beads, and fibre beards.
- **Fretwork dance-wand** — Another dance object is a painted wooden fretwork fragment with feathers.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe secret Duk-Duk ceremonies, Tubuan spirits, sorcery beliefs, and the later spread of Christianity.

- **Duk-Duk ceremonies** (*Duk-Duk*) — Initiates perform secret ceremonies connected with the Duk-Duk society.
- **Tubuan spirits** (*Tubuans*) — Tolai tradition includes belief in female spirits called Tubuans.
- **Sorcery** — Sorcery is believed to be used to gain someone’s love or punish an enemy.
- **Christian mission arrival** — Methodist ministers and teachers from Fiji arrived in 1875, and Christianity later became widespread.
- **Tolai apology to Fiji** — In 2007, descendants publicly apologized for the killing and eating of four Fijian missionaries in 1878.

## Foodways

The sources give only a few Tolai terms connected with chewing betelnut.

- **Small supply of betelnuts** (*minamai*) — Minamai means a small supply of betelnuts for chewing.
- **Betelnut** (*buai*) — Buai is a Tok Pisin word likely derived from Tolai and means betelnut.
- **Chew betelnut** (*mamai*) — Mamai is the Tolai verb meaning to chew betelnut.

## Language & religion

Kuanua is a prestigious Oceanic language, and most Tolai are Christian while older traditions remain important.

- **The real language** (*tinata tuna*) — Tolai people call their language tinata tuna, meaning “the real language.”
- **Kuanua** — Kuanua is the main Tolai language and is widely used in Kokopo and Rabaul.
- **Place over there** (*Kuanua*) — Kuanua is apparently a Ramoaaina word meaning “the place over there.”
- **Oceanic language** — Tolai belongs to the Oceanic branch of the Austronesian language family.
- **Patpatar–Tolai group** (*Patpatar–Tolai*) — Tolai’s closest language subgroup also includes Lungalunga and Patpatar.
