## Overview

Samoans, or Samoan people, are the Indigenous Polynesian people of the Samoan Islands, an archipelago in Polynesia whose inhabitants speak the Samoan language. Their home islands are politically and geographically divided between the Independent State of Samoa and American Samoa, an unincorporated territory of the United States of America. Despite the national border, Samoan culture and language are described as the same on both sides. Samoan society is organized through extended families, village councils, chiefly titles, communal labor, land allocation, ceremonies, oral histories, music, tattooing, and dance. Samoan culture is also described as an important link in the formation and spread of Polynesian culture, language, and religion throughout Eastern Polynesia.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Ie'toga** — The sources identify the *ie'toga* as a finely woven mat used in the festive attire of performers in the *Taualuga*. It may be decorated with feathers of the “sega,” identified in the source as the collared lory or blue crowned lorikeet; modern performances primarily use dyed chicken feathers.

**Woven mats** — Woven mats are used in funerary practice. The deceased is bathed, dressed in white, and placed on woven mats before the funeral, which takes place less than 24-hours later.

**Motif vocabulary.** Geometric tattoo designs, va'a (canoe), kava bowl, Samoan house or fale, shells, fish, birds, waves, centipedes, geometric lines and angles.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe ceremonial clothing more fully than everyday dress. In the *Taualuga*, a *taupou* or *manaia* wears full festive attire, traditionally including a finely woven *ie'toga* mat decorated with feathers. Performers may wear anklets and armbands made of *ti* leaves, sea turtle shells (*uga laumei*), coconut shells, or boar’s tusk. The crowning headdress is called a *tuiga*. The performer is also finished with a drenching of coconut oil for cosmetic purposes. Modern performances primarily use dyed chicken feathers rather than the feathers identified as “sega.”

### Architecture

Samoan village house lots contain clusters of houses or huts, with each house lot including a main sleeping house, a guest house, and a latrine. Yards with trees and gardens surround the buildings, and some lots contain the extended family. The sources name the *fale* as the Samoan house; in tattoo symbolism, its characterization signifies kinship. They do describe the wider settlement as organized around family land, village land, cultivation, gardens, and household work.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do describe wooden and plant-based objects connected with work and music, including the *pate*, a hollowed-out log drum, and the *fala*, a rolled-up mat beaten with sticks. The sources also mention tools used in tattooing: a short piece of bamboo or light wood, tortoiseshell, a small bone comb, and a small mallet. The *pate* may be carved with tribal references or designs.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Samoan tattooing is called *tatau*. The *pe'a* is the male *tatau*, while the *malu* is the female *tatau*. The sources state that Samoans have practiced tattooing men and women for over 2,000 years. A man’s tattoo traditionally extends from the mid-back down the sides and flanks to the knees; a woman’s tattoo is less extensive or heavy. Designs use geometric patterns associated with rank and status, together with symbols such as the *va'a*, or canoe. The tattoo master applies black ink made from the soot of burnt candlenut shells and punctures the skin with a cutting tool. Tattooing takes days and can be interrupted by periods of recuperation. Ceremonial adornment also includes *ti*-leaf anklets and armbands, sea turtle shells, coconut shells, boar’s tusk, feathers, the *tuiga*, and coconut oil.

## Music & performance

Music has an important place in Samoan life. The sources state that early Samoan music used two principal instruments, the *pate*, a hollowed-out log drum in various sizes, and the *fala*, a rolled-up mat beaten with sticks, together with the human voice. Because there was no written language, songs transmitted stories and legends. The complex rhythms of the *pate* are important in many Samoan dances, and dancers may add rhythm by clapping their hands, producing different sounds according to how the hands are held.

Two further instruments are the *selo* and the ukulele. The *selo* is made from a broomstick or similar object attached to a box, bucket, or other sounding board. A single string joins the top of the stick to the box and is plucked to produce a sound similar to a bass. The ukulele is described as a small guitar-like instrument with four strings. One form resembles a miniature guitar, while another has a body made from half a coconut shell.

Western string instruments, including guitars, have been widely available across the Pacific Islands. Bands in Samoa have performed and recorded acoustic and amplified music since the 1970s. Younger generations perform in string bands and also engage with reggae, hip hop, rhythm and blues, gospel, and soul. The sources describe a resurgence of old Samoan songs remixed in a reggae style, retaining elements such as the *pate* and older chord structures. Modern Samoan music also includes influences from reggae and Hawaiian music. The guitar style known in the islands as “Le Igi” is identified with fingerpicking and linked in the source to Hawaii and “Slack Key Guitar.”

## Dance & theatre

The *Siva Afi*, or fire knife dance, is described as the most popular Samoan dance among tourists in Samoa. The *Fa'ataupati*, or slap dance, is performed by men, who slap their bodies in fierce rhythmic motion to drum beats. The *Maulu'ulu* is an all-female dance described as more elegant. The *Sasa* can be performed by men and women, either seated or standing. The *Siva Tau* is a war dance performed by Samoan sporting teams before each match.

The *Taualuga* is a celebratory *siva* and a center of Samoan culture. Traditionally it is performed by the virgin highborn son or daughter of a Samoan chief: a *taupou* if female or a *manaia* if male. The performer wears festive attire, including the *ie'toga*, feathers, anklets, armbands, and the *tuiga*. Performances are accompanied by upbeat but simple drum beats and take place at a variety of cultural celebrations.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do describe ceremonies and life-cycle events. Marriage is a village event involving two ceremonies and a feast. The bride and groom first go through the village to a district judge, who conducts a civil ceremony. They then gather in a church for a religious ceremony. Families provide food from throughout the village, and after the wedding the newlyweds choose which side of the family they will live with.

Funerals begin almost immediately after a member of the extended family dies. Choirs are directed to the mourner’s land. The deceased is bathed, dressed in white, and placed on woven mats. The funeral takes place less than 24-hours later, and at least one family member remains with the deceased during those 24-hours. A feast concludes the event, with food served to mourners and those who helped with the burial.

Samoan chiefly rituals include the election and installation of a *matai*. After a new *matai* is chosen, a feast is held for the family and later a larger feast for the village. At the larger feast, the new *matai* gives a traditional inaugural speech, retelling Samoan myths. The newly elected *matai* then hosts a village-wide feast, provides food, and gives gifts to other *matai*. Kava ceremonies and chiefly or royal ceremonies are also mentioned as contexts for high-chief speeches and oral histories.

## Foodways

The sources describe food as central to household, village, chiefly, wedding, and funeral life. The *matai* manages the amount of food brought into the family and stores some for difficult times. The *aumaga*, the untitled labor group, plants and harvests gardens, fishes, cuts and sells coconut meat, and helps the chief with ritual cooking. In village land, hunting for food such as wild pig and birds is allowed, and fishing is also permitted.

Family reserves are used for cultivation. The sources identify taro leaves and yams as the largest amount of crops grown within Samoan culture. Family-reserve crops can be shared with other villages and families, although the land remains separately classified from the crops. Village land is community property rather than family-owned land, and planting there requires permission from the village council.

Feasts accompany marriages, the election of a *matai*, and funerals.

## Oral tradition & literature

Samoan stories and legends have traditionally been propagated through song. The sources also describe oral high chiefs recounting voyages, histories, and contacts within the Polynesian archipelago in speeches and decrees during kava ceremonies and chiefly or royal ceremonies. The voyages remembered in ancient Polynesian chieftain oratory poetics are called *taeao*. These accounts recall past histories and contacts and include familial or tribal contacts with neighboring islands and places such as Tokelau, Tuamotu, Huahine, Tahiti, Ha'apai, Vava'u, Rarotonga, Pukapuka, Tuvalu, Havai'i, Futuna, Uvea, Rotuma, and Vanua Levu.

At chiefly feasts, the new *matai* gives a traditional inaugural speech that displays public speaking ability, wisdom, and knowledge of Samoan myths. The sources also mention Margaret Mead’s ethnography *Coming of Age in Samoa*, which contains observations about Samoan villages, households, social structures, childhood, women’s status, and female sexuality. No named Samoan epic, proverb collection, riddle tradition, or contemporary literary preservation project is covered by the sources.

## Language & religion

Samoans speak the Samoan language. The sources describe Samoan as part of Polynesian culture. They state that there was no written language in the earlier musical context, so songs helped transmit stories and legends.

The sources describe Christianity as formally introduced with the arrival of L.M.S. Christian missionaries in August 1830. Marriage includes both a civil ceremony and a religious ceremony in a church. Earlier Samoan religious and ritual life is associated in the sources with myths, kava ceremonies, chiefly or royal ceremonies, and ritual cooking.
