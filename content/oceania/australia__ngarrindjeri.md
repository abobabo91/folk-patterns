## Overview

The Ngarrindjeri are traditional Aboriginal Australian people of the lower Murray River, the eastern Fleurieu Peninsula, and the Coorong in southern-central South Australia. Their lands include Lake Alexandrina and Lake Albert, extending from Victor Harbor to Cape Jaffa and inland toward Murray Bridge and Coonawarra. The Ngarrindjeri comprise several related groups, including the Jarildekald, Tanganekald, Meintangk and Ramindjeri. Ngarrindjeri is a Pama–Nyungan language with dialects including Warki, Tanganekald, Ramindjeri, Portaulun and Yaraldi. The 2016 Australian census recorded 312 speakers of Ngarrindjerri. Ngarrindjeri culture is documented through its relationships with the River Murray, Dreaming narratives, clan organisation, fishing, basketry, nets, seasonal camps, ceremony and language-revival work.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Basketry, matting and nets** — Bulrushes, reeds and sedges were used for basket-weaving and rope-making. The Ngarrindjeri were known as “outstanding craftsmen” specialising in basketry, matting and nets; fishing nets were made from bulrush roots, whose fibres were spun by women and woven into nets by men.

**Emu-catching nets** — Records describe nets more than 100 metres long used to catch emus. Colonists regarded Ngarrindjeri fishing nets as superior to those used by Europeans.

### Clothing & dress

They state that trees provided wood for spears and that a traditional trading camp at Tagalang was used to exchange ochre, weapons and clothing. The sources also describe funeral customs in which bodies were smoked, placed in trees, on platforms or in rock shelters, or buried according to local custom. Some groups removed the skull for use as a drinking vessel, while others collected bones from bodies placed in trees.

### Architecture

They do document permanent summer and winter camps, reflecting a semi-sedentary way of life supported by fertile land. Pomberuk, on the banks of the Murray in Murray Bridge, was the most significant Ngarrindjeri site and served as a meeting place for all 18 lakinyeri during corroborees. Tagalang, also known as Tailem Bend, was a traditional trading camp. Raukkan was established as a mission in 1859 and later became a settlement of Ngarrindjeri people from the Coorong region.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They describe stone tools, wooden spears, fishing nets, rope and camp ovens. The people used bulrushes, reeds and sedges for cordage and nets, while stones were fashioned into tools. Their cooking was associated with efficient camp ovens, remains of which occur throughout the River Murray area. At Raukkan, people also practised saddlery, blacksmithing, carpentry, stonemasonry and baking.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ochre is mentioned as an item exchanged at Tagalang.

## Music & performance

Ngarrindjeri groups regularly met with the Walkandi-woni, the collective name for groups living along the River as far as Wentworth in New South Wales, to exchange songs and conduct ceremonies. Meetings took place at Wellington, Tailem Bend, Murray Bridge, Mannum and Swan Reach. Pomberuk was a major gathering place where all 18 lakinyeri met for corroborees.

Songs from the period before colonisation describe smallpox as coming from the Southern Cross in the east with a loud noise like a bright flash. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, the musical group The Deadly Nannas, also called Nragi Muthar, has written and sung songs in Ngarrindjerri and English, using them to help teach the language in schools and other venues.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used name corroborees and ceremonies. Ngarrindjeri groups met for ceremonies and exchanged songs with the Walkandi-woni. Moogy Sumner is identified as an Ngarrindjeri elder, dancer and environmental activist.

## Festivals & rituals

They document gatherings for corroborees and ceremonies at Pomberuk, Wellington, Tailem Bend, Murray Bridge, Mannum and Swan Reach. In 1849, George Taplin observed a mustering of 500 Ngarrindjeri warriors and was told that as many as 800 had gathered seven years earlier.

Funeral practices varied among the 18 lakinyeri. Bodies could be smoked before being placed in trees, on platforms, in rock shelters or in the ground. Some groups placed bodies in trees and later collected the fallen bones for burial. Other practices included removing the skull for use as a drinking vessel and peeling the skin from the dead. They do state that members of the same lakinyeri could not marry, and that marriage was also forbidden between members of different lakinyeri who shared a great-grandparent or closer relation.

## Foodways

Ngarrindjeri foodways drew on the flora and fauna of the Fleurieu Peninsula, the River Murray and surrounding wetlands. Food sources included birds, fish, snake-necked turtles, yabbies, rakali, ducks and black swans. Flora included the leek orchid, guinea flower and swamp wattle, also called Wirilda. The people were known to Europeans for their cooking skills and the efficiency of their camp ovens.

Food restrictions were organised through ngarambi, or taboo prohibitions, and ngaitji, or family-group totems. A ngaitji was described as a “friend” and “spiritual advisor” to the family group. Different family groups had different attitudes toward eating their ngaitji: some prohibited it, some allowed it only when caught by another family group, and some imposed no restriction. Male dogs were not eaten because they were friends of the Ngarrindjeri, while female dogs were considered unclean. Snakes were not eaten because of the feel of their skin. Some birds were ngarambi because they were associated with the spirits of the dead or because they behaved cruelly toward other animals.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ngarrindjeri Dreaming narratives are closely connected to the River Murray and its lakes. Ngurunderi, an ancestral hero, chased the enormous Murray cod Pondi. Pondi’s movements created the River Murray and adjoining lagoons, while Ngurunderi’s actions shaped places including Kauwira, Peindjalong and Raukkan. At Raukkan, Ngurunderi cut up Pondi and threw the pieces into the water, where they became different species of fish.

Near the junction of the Murray River and Lake Alexandrina, Murungun, also called Mason’s Hill, is associated with the water spirit Muldjewangk. The Muldjewangk or Mulyawonk punished people who took more than their fair share of fish or allowed children to approach the water. These stories conveyed practical messages about caring for country and its people. Ngarrindjeri stories also include Thukabi, a turtle. Contemporary preservation efforts include dictionaries, language teaching and songs performed in Ngarrindjerri and English.

## Language & religion

Ngarrindjeri, also written Narrinyeri and Ngarinyeri, is a Pama–Nyungan language of southern South Australia. A 2002 study distinguished the dialects Warki, Tanganekald, Ramindjeri, Portaulun and Yaraldi or Yaralde Tingar. Tanganekald, also known as Thangal, is extinct. The last fluent speaker of Ngarrindjerri died in the 1960s, while revival efforts have included dictionaries, adult teaching and community education.

A Ngarrindjerri Bible was published in 1864, the first time portions of the Bible were translated into an Aboriginal language. The sources describe Christian conversion among people settled at Raukkan, but also document Ngarrindjeri Dreaming, ancestral beings, water spirits, family-group totems and ritual practices.
