## Overview

The Mundugumor, also known as Biwat, are an indigenous people of Papua New Guinea living on the Yuat River in East Sepik Province. They speak the Mundugumor language and Melanesian Pidgin. The Mundugumor language is also called Munduguma and Mundukomo and is spoken in Biwat village of Yuat Rural LLG, East Sepik Province. Margaret Mead studied the Mundugumor during fieldwork in Papua New Guinea from 1931 to 1933, and later studies described changes among people then called the Biwat. The sources present marriage, kinship, motherhood, agriculture, exchange, wooden ancestral figures, masks, drums, and slit-drums as parts of the documented cultural record.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Braided vegetable fibre strap** — A British Museum drum is described as having a braided vegetable fibre strap.

**Wicker frame** — A British Museum mask has a wicker frame behind the wooden mask. The catalogue does not give a vernacular name or describe a broader basketry tradition.

**Vegetable fibre tassel** — One British Museum mask has an ear threaded with a vegetable fibre tassel.

### Clothing & dress

The sources mention vegetable fibre in the construction of a drum strap, a mask tassel, and rigid strips of bamboo threaded through mask perforations, but these catalogue descriptions concern objects rather than clothing.

### Architecture

The sources state that the Mundugumor lived in a village on the Yuat River and that a local mission's airstrip was closed after a land dispute.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The catalogue records document wooden drums, a wooden slit-drum, wooden masks, and carved wooden ancestral figures. One drum has incised decoration and handles carved in the form of heads, with a braided vegetable fibre strap. A slit-drum has a handle at each end in the form of a quadruped and is made of wood with cowrie shells and feathers.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The museum records describe cowrie shells, feathers, pearl shell, pigment, vegetable fibre, and bamboo as materials or decorative elements on objects. One mask has perforated pearl shell eyes, red, black, and white pigment, conical and flattish bosses, perforated ears, a vegetable fibre tassel, and rigid bamboo strips threaded through perforations.

## Music & performance

The sources identify drums and slit-drums in museum catalogue records. One wooden drum has incised decoration and handles carved in the form of heads; another object is a wooden slit-drum with handles in the form of quadrupeds, cowrie shells, and feathers. The Mundugumor language article mentions recordings of Biwat language in the Paradisec collection and recordings from Papua New Guinea, Australia, and the Pacific from Don Laycock that include Biwat materials.

## Dance & theatre

The sources record wooden masks in museum collections.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources document life-cycle and social practices concerning marriage, pregnancy, childbirth, and childhood. Marriage commonly involved brother-and-sister exchange, and a valuable flute could sometimes be used in an exchange. Affairs and elopements could lead to marriage, with relatives sometimes pursuing the woman and fighting. Around one third of Mundugumor marriages are said to have begun in this violent fashion. During pregnancy, intercourse was subject to a taboo associated with fear of one child turning into twins. When a child was not preserved, the infant could be thrown into the Yuat River or strangled before disposal. The sources also describe children being given as hostages to temporary trade allies before the Mundugumor village came under Australian governmental control.

## Foodways

The sources state that most food was produced through slash-and-burn agriculture. In later accounts, the main cash crops were tobacco and betel nut, while the Biwat also kept pigs and other livestock, including cattle. They travelled by motorized canoe along the Yuat River to buy and sell produce in nearby Angoram, Wewak, and Madang. The sources describe mothers feeding and weaning children, including breastfeeding.

## Oral tradition & literature

Margaret Mead's field notes and Reo Fortune's field notes were later revisited by Nancy McDowell. McDowell completed Mead's book after Mead's death in 1978, and the book was published in 1991 as *The Mundugumor: From the Field Notes of Margaret Mead and Reo Fortune*. The sources also mention a collection of recordings of Biwat language held by Paradisec and Biwat materials in recordings from Don Laycock.

## Language & religion

Mundugumor, also called Munduguma and Mundukomo, is a Yuat language of Papua New Guinea. It is spoken in Biwat village of Yuat Rural LLG, East Sepik Province. The people also speak Melanesian Pidgin. Later studies state that most of the Biwat were Catholic, that many no longer understood the indigenous kinship terminology described by Mead, and that education was widely available.
