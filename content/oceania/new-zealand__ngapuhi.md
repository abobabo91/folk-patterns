## Overview

Ngāpuhi, also known as Ngāpuhi-Nui-Tonu or Ngā Puhi, is a Māori iwi associated with the Northland regions of New Zealand, centred in the Hokianga, the Bay of Islands, and Whangārei. According to the 2023 New Zealand census, its estimated population was 184,470. Ngāpuhi is formed from 150 hapū and has 55 marae. Te Rūnanga ā Iwi o Ngāpuhi, based in Kaikohe, administers the iwi and represents it in consultations with the New Zealand government. The sources describe shared histories, ancestral traditions, missionary encounters, warfare, political declarations, treaty debates, migration, marae, broadcasting, and religious affiliation.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources mention *marae*, including Te Tii Marae and Ōkorihi marae, and identify a *whārenui* at Ōkorihi marae. The whārenui burned down in 2003. The sources also mention pā, including Whiria pā and Ruapekapeka Pa.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do mention muskets, European plants, technology, knowledge, fishing equipment, anchors, and waka in historical narratives.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They mention that Kūrāmarotini anointed her body with *taramea* oil.

## Music & performance

They mention *karakia*, including the incantations associated with Kupe, Hoturapa, and Nukutawhiti, and name the chant *E kau ki te tai e*, translated as “Swim upon the sea.” Tautoko FM broadcasts to the people of Ngāpuhi-nui-tonu and began operating on 28 November 1988; it broadcasts on 99.5 FM in Mangamuka. Its building burned to the ground on 18 May 2015.

## Festivals & rituals

They document historical and political gatherings at Waitangi, including the meeting of various Northland chiefs with British resident James Busby on 28 October 1835, when the Declaration of the Independence of New Zealand was signed. They also describe the signing of the Treaty of Waitangi in 1840, the Flagstaff War from 1845 to 1846, and the re-erection of the flagstaff at Kororāreka in January 1858. In 2010, the Waitangi Tribunal began hearings concerning Ngāpuhi’s claim that sovereignty was not given up through the signing of the Treaty of Waitangi.

## Foodways

They state that Kareroariki craved and ate a human heart in an ancestral story, and that Kupe and Hoturapa went out in a waka to fish and set traps. These accounts are presented within origin narratives rather than as descriptions of Ngāpuhi foodways.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ngāpuhi oral tradition includes *kōrero*, described as legends or stories. The sources relate several versions of the origins of the name Ngāpuhi, including the story of Kareroariki and her three children, Puhikaiariki, Puhimoanaariki, and Puhitaniwharau. They also describe Ngāpuhi accounts of Kupe’s arrival in Aotearoa, his relationship with Kūrāmarotini, the journey on Matawhaorua, and the naming of Te Hokianga-Nui-a-Kupe, commonly shortened to Hokianga. Another narrative concerns Nukutawhiti, the renaming of the waka to Ngātokimatawhaorua, the taniwha Niniwa and Āraiteuru, and their transformation into guardians of the Hokianga harbour.

## Language & religion

They record Māori terms and expressions, including *kōrero*, *karakia*, *taniwha*, *hapū*, *marae*, *pā*, *ariki*, *rangatira*, and *whārenui*. Historically, Ngāpuhi hosted Christian missionaries: the first Christian mission in New Zealand was established on Ngāpuhi land in 1814, and mission stations, farms, and schools followed at Oihi Bay and Paihia. Rawiri Taiwhanga was baptised in 1830, while Hōne Heke and his wife Ono were baptised in 1835. According to 2018 Census-based estimates, 49.6% of Ngāpuhi identified as irreligious and 42.2% specified a religious affiliation.
