## Overview

The Mundugumor live in and around Biwat village on the Yuat River. Their documented culture includes marriage, kinship, motherhood, farming, trade, wooden objects, masks, and drums. The profile gives little information about clothing, houses, dances, festivals, stories, or personal adornment. Later studies describe major changes, including Catholicism, education, and reduced knowledge of older kinship terms.

## Material culture

The documented material culture centers on carved wooden drums, slit-drums, masks, and ancestral figures, with shells, feathers, pigment, bamboo, and vegetable fibre used as details or fittings.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources do not describe a wider textile tradition, but they record vegetable fibre and wicker on museum objects.

- **Braided vegetable fibre strap** — A British Museum drum has a braided vegetable fibre strap.
- **Wicker mask frame** — A British Museum mask has a wicker frame behind the wooden mask.
- **Vegetable fibre tassel** — One British Museum mask has an ear threaded with a vegetable fibre tassel.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The documented objects are mainly carved wooden drums, slit-drums, masks, and ancestral figures.

- **Wooden drum** — One drum has incised decoration and handles carved as heads, with a braided vegetable fibre strap.
- **Wooden slit-drum** — A slit-drum has handles shaped like quadrupeds and is decorated with cowrie shells and feathers.
- **Wooden mask** — Museum records document wooden masks.
- **Carved wooden ancestral figure** — Museum records document carved wooden ancestral figures.

### Jewelry & body adornment

- **Pearl shell eyes** — One mask has perforated pearl shell eyes.
- **Pigment** — One mask uses red, black, and white pigment.
- **Bamboo strips** — Rigid strips of bamboo are threaded through mask perforations.

## Music & performance

Museum records identify drums and slit-drums, but give no musical names or performance settings.

- **Drum** — A wooden drum has incised decoration and handles carved as heads.
- **Slit-drum** — A wooden slit-drum has quadruped-shaped handles, cowrie shells, and feathers.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do not describe annual festivals or named ritual occasions, but they record marriage, pregnancy, childbirth, and childhood practices.

- **Brother-and-sister marriage exchange** — Marriage commonly involved an exchange between brothers and sisters.
- **Flute exchange** — A valuable flute could sometimes be used in a marriage exchange.
- **Violent marriage beginning** — Affairs and elopements could lead to marriage, sometimes followed by relatives pursuing the woman and fighting.
- **Pregnancy taboo** — During pregnancy, intercourse was subject to a taboo linked to fear that one child could become twins.
- **Child hostage exchange** — Children were sometimes given as hostages to temporary trade allies before Australian government control.

## Foodways

Most food came from slash-and-burn agriculture, with later cash crops, livestock, and river trade.

- **Slash-and-burn agriculture** — Most food was produced through slash-and-burn agriculture.
- **Tobacco** — Tobacco was a main cash crop in later accounts.
- **Betel nut** — Betel nut was another main cash crop in later accounts.
- **Pig keeping** — The Biwat kept pigs and other livestock.
- **Motorized canoe trade** — They travelled by motorized canoe along the Yuat River to buy and sell produce.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources do not record Mundugumor stories or oral genres, but they preserve field notes and language recordings.

- **The Mundugumor** — Nancy McDowell completed Mead's book after Mead's death, and it was published in 1991 as The Mundugumor: From the Field Notes of Margaret Mead and Reo Fortune.
- **Biwat language recordings** (*Biwat*) — The Paradisec collection holds Biwat language recordings, including materials from Don Laycock.

## Language & religion

Mundugumor is a Yuat language, and the people also speak Melanesian Pidgin; later studies say most Biwat were Catholic.

- **Mundugumor language** (*Mundugumor*) — Mundugumor is also called Munduguma and Mundukomo and is spoken in Biwat village.
- **Munduguma** — Munduguma is another name for the Mundugumor language.
- **Mundukomo** — Mundukomo is another name for the Mundugumor language.
- **Melanesian Pidgin** — The Mundugumor also speak Melanesian Pidgin.
- **Catholicism** — Later studies state that most of the Biwat were Catholic.
