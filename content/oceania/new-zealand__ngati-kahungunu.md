## Overview

Ngāti Kahungunu are a Māori iwi located along the eastern coast of the North Island of New Zealand, traditionally centred in the Hawke's Bay and Wairārapa regions. The iwi comprises 86 hapū and 90 marae and is organised into six geographical and administrative divisions: Wairoa, Te Whanganui-ā-Orotū, Heretaunga, Tamatea, Tāmaki-nui-a Rua and Wairarapa. In the 2018 census, 82,239 people identified as Ngāti Kahungunu, making it the 4th largest iwi in New Zealand by population. Ngāti Kahungunu trace their origins to the Tākitimu waka, which traditions describe as arriving on New Zealand's North Island around 1100–1200 AD. The sources identify the people as Māori.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They mention *tapu* in accounts of the Tākitimu voyage.

### Architecture

Marae are identified as Ngāti Kahungunu meeting grounds, and 90 marae are associated with the iwi. Pāpāwai Marae in Wairārapa and Waipatu in Heretaunga hosted parliamentary-style meetings of the Kotahitanga movement.

## Music & performance

Music is documented through the compositions of Paraire Tomoana, a Ngāti Kahungunu leader and the son of Henare Tomoana. In January 1918, Tomoana published the words of **E Pari Ra**, a piece written for soldiers lost in battle. After the First World War, its tune was adopted by the Royal New Zealand Navy as its official slow march.

Other songs composed by Tomoana include **Tahi nei taru kino**, **I runga o ngā puke**, **Hoki hoki tonu mai**, **Hoea rā te waka nei**, and **Pōkarekare ana**. He also composed the haka **Tika tonu**. The sources state that these songs later became treasured anthems of Ngāti Kahungunu, and that some were adopted by other iwi because of their wartime popularity.

Many Ngāti Kahungunu men enlisted during the First World War and were organised into the New Zealand (Māori) Pioneer Battalion. The battalion participated in the Gallipoli campaign in 1915 and on the Western Front between 1916 and 1918. During the Second World War, many men again enlisted and served primarily with the 28th (Māori) Battalion.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name the haka **Tika tonu**, composed by Paraire Tomoana.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record several historical and political gatherings. The Kotahitanga movement held parliamentary-style meetings at Pāpāwai Marae in Wairārapa and at Waipatu in Heretaunga during the 1890s. In 1840, a number of Ngāti Kahungunu chiefs were signatories to the Treaty of Waitangi.

The sources also describe practices associated with political alliance and leadership. Kahungunu’s marriages were often arranged for diplomatic purposes, uniting iwi against enemies, forming bonds, and securing peace. Te Huki’s series of diplomatic marriages was called “setting the net of Te Huki.” In 1988, Te Rūnanganui o Ngāti Kahungunu Incorporated was established as a centralised organisation for iwi development; after entering receivership in 1994, it re-emerged under a new constitution in 1996 as Ngāti Kahungunu Iwi Incorporated. Elections for its board are held every three years.

## Foodways

In the account of the Tākitimu voyage, the crew is described as comprising men only, and no cooked food was eaten before or during the voyage.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ngāti Kahungunu traditions trace the iwi’s origins to the Tākitimu waka, which arrived on New Zealand's North Island around 1100–1200 AD. According to local legend, the waka and its crew were completely *tapu*. The captain was Tamatea Arikinui, who left the waka at Tauranga in the Bay of Plenty or at Tūranga, near modern-day Gisborne, and travelled overland to Ahuriri, now part of Napier, in the Hawke's Bay Region.

Oral history states that Kahungunu was the great-grandson of Tamatea Arikinui and was born in present-day Kaitaia. Another tradition recounts his courtship and marriage to Rongomaiwahine, a chief from Nukutaurua on the Māhia Peninsula. The iwi Ngāti Kahungunu and Ngāti Rongomaiwahine both descend from this marriage.

The sources also preserve accounts of battles, migrations, genealogical descent, diplomatic marriages, and the origins of place and group names. These include the Battle of Te Paepae o Rarotonga, the Battle of Whāwhāpō, and the migration from Nukutaurua to Heretaunga.

## Language & religion

The sources identify Ngāti Kahungunu as a Māori iwi and use the term *reo* for language in describing qualifications for representatives of the iwi board. They also refer to *tikanga* and *kawa*, respectively described as Ngāti Kahungunu customary practice and traditional legal protocols.

The Tākitimu account describes the waka and its crew as completely *tapu*, and states that its crew consisted of high chiefs, chiefs, *tohunga*, and elite warriors.
