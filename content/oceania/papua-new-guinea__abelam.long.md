## Overview

The Abelam are a people of East Sepik Province in Papua New Guinea. They live in the Prince Alexander Mountains near the island’s north coast, including the Maprik district and places recorded in museum catalogues as Wosera, Maundu, Malmba, and Niamingum. They are a farming society in which giant yams play a significant role. Their main crops are yams, taro, bananas, and sweet potatoes, supplemented by gathered rainforest foods, domestically raised pigs and chickens, and hunted small marsupials and cassowaries. Their language belongs to the Sepik language family. The sources present Abelam culture through farming, yam cultivation and festivals, language, and material objects associated with adornment, music, ceremony, and everyday use.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Nätväska** — Museum catalogues record net bags from Wosera in the Maprik district. One is made in the natural colour of the fibre, with sections patterned in brown; brown threads are joined to natural-coloured threads, and additional brown threads are knotted into the upper part.

**Nätväska med färgade trådar** — Another Wosera net bag is patterned with brown, faint red, and blue threads that are joined or braided into the fibre. It is convex and wider at the bottom and concave and narrower at the top; its carrying band serves as a headband.

**Rattan mask** — A facial mask from the Maprik district is braided from strips of rattan, including spiral technique. Its nose is marked by a loop, and its exterior retains traces of red, brown, and grey-white colour.

**Yam mask** — British Museum records describe yam masks made from rattan, palm-leaf ribs, grass, hair, and chalk. Some have grass fixed to the top and hair at the back, with a thin chalk coating and black and red painted portions.

**Men’s yam ceremony mask** — Basketry masks associated with men’s yam ceremonies are coloured dark brown, yellow, red, and white. One has a red panel around the eyes; another is partly openwork and has a yellow eye panel.

**Yam cult mask** — A yam cult mask is made of vegetable fibre with chain appendages formed from plaited vegetable-fibre links. The object retains traces of red pigment.

**Karawut** — The British Museum records a *karawut* as a plaited-fibre ornament worn on the back or carried in the mouth when dancing. It is coloured with red, black, and yellow pigment and takes the form of a stylised human figure with a raised nose.

**Motif vocabulary.** Raised nose, human figure, bird, eye panels, spiral technique, zigzag pattern, red, black, yellow, brown, and grey-white.

### Clothing & dress

Museum catalogues do record adornments and carrying equipment. A net bag from Wosera has a carrying band that serves as a headband. A hair ornament from Malmba consists of a long, narrow plate of light wood with feathers inserted into it. A comb from Niamingum has five pointed wooden pins held together at the top by plaiting, with feathers inserted on one side. A necklace from Malmba consists of kauri shells strung on a cord. The *karawut* is associated with dancing and can be worn on the back or carried in the mouth. A yam mask from the Maprik district is described as having adorned large yam tubers during harvest ceremonies, according to information supplied by its seller.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The catalogued objects include a dark-brown bone dagger with incised ornament and other bone daggers with incised ornament. A cross-axe blade from the Maprik district is made of light-grey stone, polished, and shaped with a pointed neck and convex sections. A small bowl from Maundu or Malmba is made from coconut shell; much of its underside is decorated with low-relief cutwork, and its edge is toothed at the pointed end. A wooden stool from Maundu is cut from one piece of light wood and consists of two plates joined by five outward-curving legs. A wooden clamp from Maundu is carved in one piece and has the form of a bird at the top.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The catalogues record a necklace from Malmba made of kauri shells strung on a cord. A hair ornament from probably Malmba is a narrow light-wood plate into which feathers are inserted. The Niamingum comb has five pointed wooden pins, feather decoration, and a braided upper part. A net bag from Wosera has a carrying band used as a headband. British Museum records also describe a *karawut* decorated with rows of cut nassa shells and boar’s tusks, with red, black, and yellow pigment.

## Music & performance

The museum catalogue records a flute from Maundu made from a coconut shell with two round holes: one near a pointed end and another on the side. The shell is decorated on the outside with incisions, including a zigzag pattern. It is a vessel flute and can also be used as an edge flute by blowing against either opening.

Performance is represented through objects connected with dancing and yam ceremonies. The *karawut* is an ornament worn on the back or carried in the mouth when dancing. Its stylised human form, attached shells, and boar’s tusks are part of its described appearance. British Museum records also identify basketry masks as men’s yam ceremony masks and identify another fibre mask as a yam cult mask.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention dancing through the *karawut*, an ornament worn on the back or carried in the mouth when dancing. They also document masks connected with yam ceremonies, including basketry masks, a rattan mask, and a yam cult mask made of vegetable fibre. The catalogue descriptions record their materials, pigments, forms, and attachments.

## Festivals & rituals

Yam growing forms a large part of Abelam society. Large yams can reach 80-90 inches, or 2.3 m, in length, and the growing of large yams determines the status of individuals and whole villages. At yam festivals, an individual gives his largest yam to his worst enemy, who is then obligated to grow an even larger yam or experience a fall in status in each year when this is not achieved. Separate villages gather at yam festivals, where the host village’s status is determined by the size of its yams and by its ability to provide more food than the rival village can eat and carry away.

During the yam-growing season, strong emotions are kept to a minimum because they are thought to impede yam growth. Fighting and sexual activity are taboo, and the yams are thought to have a spirit capable of sensing strong emotions. Museum records identify several masks as men’s yam ceremony masks or yam cult masks.

## Foodways

The main Abelam food crops are yams, taro, bananas, and sweet potatoes. These crops are supplemented by food gathered from the tropical rainforest, pigs and chickens raised domestically, and hunted small marsupials and cassowaries. The sources describe villages at yam festivals providing more food than a rival village could eat and carry away.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Abelam language source gives information about word formation, including reduplication, compound stems, and derivational stems, but it does not present oral narratives or literary genres.

## Language & religion

Ambulas, also called Abelam or Abulas, is a member of the Ndu languages of the Sepik River region of northern Papua New Guinea. The source identifies the dialects as Maprik, Wingei, Wosera-Kamu, and Wosera-Mamu. Most words in Ambulas are roots, although some word-stems are reduplicated, compounded, or derived. Compound stems occur in nouns, including temporal nouns and quantifiers, and in verbs. Adjective stems can be formed by adding suffixes such as *-mama*, meaning “possessing much,” to noun roots or noun stems.

The sources state that yams are thought to have a spirit and can sense strong emotions during the yam-growing season. Museum records also use the terms yam cult mask and yam ceremony mask.
