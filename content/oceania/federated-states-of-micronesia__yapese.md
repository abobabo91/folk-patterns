## Overview

The Yapese are a Micronesian ethnic group indigenous to the main island of Yap, in the Federated States of Micronesia. Yapese culture emphasizes respect and responsibility, and traditional culture remains important in modern life. A 1994 census found that 48.1% of people on Yap identified as Yapese; the figure was 49.1% in 2000. A 2010 survey reported that Yapese formed 5.7% of the population of the Federated States of Micronesia, while a 2012 survey counted Yapese outside the FSM in the Northern Mariana Islands, Guam, Hawaii, and the contiguous United States. The Yapese language is an Austronesian language in the Oceanic branch. Traditional knowledge is taught in schools through “Practical Art/Culture.”

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Traditional clothing on the main island of Yap includes loincloths and hibiscus fiber for men and grass skirts for women. This clothing remains popular in remote villages and during festivals, while modern clothing is slowly replacing it. Traditional Yapese clothing is also worn during dances.

### Architecture

A *tabinaw* is a Yapese household, generally composed of a nuclear family. Each nuclear family generally has its own house and land. A long log divides the house into two sections: the back side, *tabgul*, is reserved for the father, while the front side, *to’or*, is open to any member of the family. Traditional households have cookhouses for different family members, whereas modern households have a shared family cookhouse. Yapese houses are made of lumber, corrugated metal, and concrete. An estate includes several households living on land owned by a single house site.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum catalogue records supplied for this atlas contain no objects.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They mention shell money in connection with death customs: after a death, the deceased’s siblings and clan members exchange shell money.

## Music & performance

Yap traditionally has no instrumental music. Local dances are human-made and feature no instruments; they are described as the “writing” of Yapese culture and are passed from generation to generation. A traditional Yapese dance, *churu’*, is formal and ritualistic. Dances are performed in groups and accompanied by chanting. The movements and chanting generally tell a story. Dances are performed on special occasions such as Yap Day and during ritual exchanges called *guyuwol*. Traditional clothing is often worn during these performances.

They state that everything in traditional Yapese music is human-made and that traditional dance performance uses chanting rather than instrumental accompaniment.

## Dance & theatre

*Churu’* is a traditional Yapese dance described as formal and ritualistic. It is performed by a group, with movements and chanting that generally tell a story. Performances take place during special occasions such as Yap Day and during ritual exchanges, *guyuwol*. Traditional Yapese clothing is often worn while dancing.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources mention Yap Day as a special occasion for dance. Ritual exchanges called *guyuwol* also provide a setting for traditional dance.

The ritual calendar connected with eating ranks in the district of Rull begins with the lunar month *Monyibwuo*. During the following month, *Wagaeygaey*, Yapese prepare by planting yam gardens. In *Tafgif*, people from Rull clean their yards and graves and place food offerings on graves; people of Gitam later eat these offerings. *Makan* is a sacred month that begins with the disappearance of the constellation Pleiades. Initiates fast, remain secluded, and learn during this period, while priests of Pemgoy and Alog gather at a shrine of Rull. During the last ten days, a magician divines for the eating class and its initiates by burning bundles of material. In the final month, *Rir*, initiates are welcomed as members of the eating class and prepare a feast for visitors from southern Yap.

Death customs include cleaning the body, rubbing it with turmeric, and plugging its orifices. The next-of-kin remain with the body for three days. After burial, kinfolk stay near the gravesite and mourn for three days, then continue mourning in temporary huts for almost a month. A special *tamaarong* performs a ritual believed to send the deceased spirit to Gatsam.

## Foodways

Local foods express cultural values and bind the land and people together. Foods grown or obtained in Yap include taro, yams, breadfruit, bananas, and reef fish. Traditional agricultural systems include gardens, shifting cultivation, taro systems, and animal husbandry. Men generally work as fishermen, while women generally work as farmers in taro patches. The protein from men’s work, *thumag*, and the starch from women’s work, *ggaan*, complement each other in the Yapese meal. Imported foods are adapted to this classification: canned meats are considered *thumag*, while rice is considered *ggaan*.

Chewing betel nut, drinking palm wine, and consuming commercial alcohol are popular social activities. Imported foods such as white rice, canned meat, and frozen meat became more common after United States subsidies to Micronesia contributed to a decline in local food production.

Eating classes consist of male non-serfs who eat together. New members undergo an initiation ritual called *dowach*. Food is separately prepared for men, women and children, and menstruating daughters. Boys over the age of ten live and eat in the village’s young men’s house, while menstruating girls eat in a separate house. In the main house, older males eat separately from women and younger children.

## Oral tradition & literature

Yapese clans or sibs, *genung*, claim mythical ancestry and a mythical place of origin. Yapese mythology includes creation accounts in which Gavur li yel yel creates the cosmos and Yanolop creates other sky deities. Yälfaath the Elder is regarded as the creator of the human race and creates the first man and woman, Ganan and Nik.

A traditional belief holds that each living person has two souls: *yaan ni fos*, the soul that lives, and *yaan ni yam*, the soul that dies. After death, a ritual prayer and incantation called *ma’log* induces the soul toward Gatsam, from which it can remain or proceed to heaven. A Yapese Talking Dictionary was produced by the Living Tongues Institute for Endangered Languages.

## Language & religion

Yapese is an Austronesian language in the Oceanic branch, spoken on the islands of Yap in the Federated States of Micronesia. It is distinguished from the Chuukic languages spoken in the Yap Neighboring Islands, including Ulithian, Woleaian, Satawalese, and, to an extent, Nguluwan.

Written Yapese uses the Latin script. A newer orthography created in the 1970s uses double vowel letters for long vowels and the letter *q* for the glottal stop. The indigenous name of the Yap Main Islands is given as Waab in the earlier spelling and Waqaab in the newer orthography; Waqab and Waʼab are also used. Spelling remains difficult to standardize across generations.

Religion in Yap is predominantly Roman Catholic. Traditional practices concerned gods, spirits, taboos, and death, and most traditional religious ceremonies have been forgotten, although most people still hold animistic beliefs concerning spirits and magic. Protestantism and other Christian sects also have small congregations. Catholicism first arrived in Yap in the late 1880s.
