## Overview

The Pintupi are part of the Western Desert cultural group. Their Country includes Lake Mackay, Lake Macdonald, Mount Russell, the Ehrenberg and Kintore ranges, and Warman Rocks. Many Pintupi people moved to Papunya and Haasts Bluff from the 1940s to the 1980s, and later some returned to their traditional Country. They established Kintore, Kiwirrkura and Jupiter Well.

## Material culture

The profile mainly documents canvas painting and simple shelters at Papunya, with little information about other material traditions.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources do not document a separate textile tradition, but they show Pintupi men painting on canvas at Papunya.

- **Canvas painting** (*Yumari*) — Several men painted sections of the 1981 canvas titled Yumari.
- **Painting materials** — Uta Uta Tjangala used a small stick and paint pots to paint on the canvas.
- **Papunya painting activity** (*Papunya*) — The paintings were made on canvas laid over cloth on the ground at Papunya.

### Clothing & dress

The sources give almost no information about Pintupi clothing.

- **Head band** — Timmy Payungka Tjapangati wore a head band in one photograph taken outdoors at Papunya.

### Architecture

The museum records show wooden and metal shelters at Papunya.

- **Metal shelter** — Timmy Payungka Tjapangati and family members stood in front of a metal shelter.
- **Wooden shelter** — A wooden shelter stood beside a metal shelter, with people seated around it.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The photographs show everyday objects used around painting and shelters, but the sources give no details about their making or ownership.

- **Paint pots** — Paint pots were placed around the canvas during painting.
- **Large buckets** — Large buckets stood at the entrance of a metal shelter.
- **Canvas** — Canvas was laid on cloth on the ground for painting.
- **Small painting stick** — A small stick was used to apply paint.
- **Dogs** — Dogs appeared beside the shelters and the canvas.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The only named personal appearance item is a head band.

- **Head band** — Timmy Payungka Tjapangati wore a head band in one museum photograph.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do not describe festivals or named rites, but they explain how place stories are linked to skin names.

- **Place and dreaming stories** (*Dreamtime*) — Pintupi refer to places and their attached dreaming stories by the skin names of their owners or ancestral heroes.
- **Ancestral heroes** — Stories about ancestral heroes help preserve the complex Pintupi kinship structure.

## Oral tradition & literature

Pintupi oral tradition connects places with dreaming stories, while written work includes translations, dictionaries and school literature.

- **Dreaming stories** (*Dreamtime*) — Pintupi places are linked with stories about Dreamtime figures and ancestral heroes.
- **Universal Declaration of Human Rights translation** (*Pintupi-Luritja*) — Elders and linguists translated the Universal Declaration of Human Rights into Pintupi-Luritja in 2015.
- **Hansen orthography** — An orthography developed by K. C. and L. E. Hansen is used in a dictionary, grammar sketch, bible portions and bilingual school materials.

## Language & religion

Pintupi is a Wati language in the Pama–Nyungan family, while most Pintupi people today speak Pintupi-Luritja.

- **Pintupi language** (*Pintupi*) — Pintupi is a variety of the Western Desert Language.
- **Pintupi-Luritja** — Pintupi-Luritja developed among children who grew up in Papunya and Haasts Bluff through contact with Arrernte, Warlpiri and other Western Desert varieties.
- **Wati language group** (*Wati*) — Pintupi belongs to the Wati languages of the Pama–Nyungan family.
- **Western Desert Language** — Pintupi is one of the varieties of the Western Desert Language.
