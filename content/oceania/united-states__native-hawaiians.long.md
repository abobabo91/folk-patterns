## Overview

Native Hawaiians — *kānaka*, *kānaka ʻōiwi*, *Kānaka Maoli*, *Hawaiʻi maoli* — are the Indigenous Polynesian people of the Hawaiian Islands, an archipelago settled by Polynesian voyagers who sailed from the Society Islands at least 800 years ago. The islands they inhabit include Hawaiʻi, Oʻahu, Maui, Molokaʻi, Lānaʻi, Kauaʻi and Niʻihau, the last of which still has Hawaiian as its primary language. At the time of Captain Cook's arrival in 1778 the population is estimated at between 250,000 and 800,000; introduced disease reduced it to fewer than 100,000 by 1900, and the 2000 U.S. census counted 283,430 residents of Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander ancestry. The Hawaiian language belongs to the Polynesian family and shares many similarities with its relatives across the Pacific. In folk-culture terms Hawaiians matter for hula, for chanted poetry, for featherwork of a technical order found nowhere else, and for the navigational knowledge that brought them to the islands in the first place.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Kapa** — barkcloth, the principal Hawaiian textile. Museum pieces are printed and painted rather than woven: one is stamped longitudinally and transversely with narrow red lines grouped into broad transverse bands of unequal length; another carries an orange-red chequer pattern and served as the outside sheet of a woman's skirt; a third bears yellow stripes with bands of black cross-hatching; a fourth is painted with stripes in black and red pigment and groups of three black dots. A heavy strip shows very large opposing black longitudinal triangles on a sienna background. The wooden beater used to make it is circular in section with longitudinal grooves along one side and a handle tapering slightly toward the end, and a drawing in the British Museum records a wooden tapa-making implement alongside two neck ornaments.

**Featherwork panels** — rectangular and broad-band textiles built from strips of *olonā* fibre fastened together and faced with feathers. One is divided into five vertical bands with grounds of red, yellow, black, red and yellow again, carrying lozenges and triangles; another sets red, black and yellow feathers in bands of varying width that read as a kind of plaid check. Both have loose plaited cord tags at the ends.

**Plaiting and basketry** — *ʻieʻie* (*Freycinetia arborea*) aerial rootlets are twined into basket lids and into the foundation of helmets; pandanus leaf is plaited into fans stiffened with a cane rib along each side. A later hat, *pāpale*, is woven in a triple-ply weave from split round rattan reed in black, brown and natural.

**Motif vocabulary.** Longitudinal and transverse stripes; broad banded groupings of narrow red lines; chequer; cross-hatching; opposing large triangles; dots in threes; lozenges; chevron and zigzag plaited into helmet crests; concentric circles of feathers; zig-zag patterning in human hair on fan handles.

### Clothing & dress

Men wore the *malo*, a loincloth of *kapa*; women's skirts were sheeted in printed barkcloth. A ceremonial *maro* survives in orange and red with two very large triangles and short black lines, showing that loincloths could be as elaborately printed as anything else in the wardrobe. The prestige garments are the feathered ones. The cloak *ʻahu ʻula* is made of honeyeater and honeycreeper feathers — *ʻiʻiwi*, *ʻōʻō*, *mamo* — bound together and knotted into a dense netting of *olonā* vine fibre. The *kīpuka*, a trapezoidal cape, layers white, black and golden-brown chicken feathers with red *ʻapapane*, some red *ʻiʻiwi* and yellow *ʻōʻō* feathers, several still attached to the bird skins stitched onto an *olonā* mat. The crested helmet *mahiole*, also called *mahiole hulu manu*, is built of *ʻieʻie* basketry with a high overarching crest, netted with *olonā* and covered in feathers: red and yellow in alternate transverse rows along the crest, scarlet on the sides, a black and yellow binding at the front edge. A massive horseshoe-shaped collar of *olonā* fibre with red, yellow and black feathers served as a helmet band.

### Architecture

The sources used here do not describe Hawaiian house building directly. Two things appear at the edges of the record: a photograph of women and men seated on a mat outside a grass building, and another of a man standing before a thatch-roofed building on Oʻahu. Land, rather than architecture, is the organising structure in the written sources — each island was divided into *moku*, held by people of high standing and kept within the family, and each *moku* into *ahupuaʻa* running from the sea to the top of the nearest mountain so that every one held all the resources needed for survival, hardwoods and food alike. Managers appointed by the island chief collected tribute, and the level of tribute determined how an *ahupuaʻa* was split; its major subdivisions were *ʻili*, each paying one tribute to the chief of the *ahupuaʻa* and another to the island chief. Unlike European feudal peasants, Hawaiian commoners were never bound to the land and could move as they chose.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

There is no pottery or metal here: Hawaiian household goods are wood, gourd, stone, shell and fibre. Bowls are turned from *kou*, a hard brown wood — a shallow circular kava bowl, a squared vessel with its angles cut off and a projecting handle on one side, and a double bowl carved with a figure whose joints and eyes are marked with small pearl-shell discs, its hair of red feathers and fur, a scrap of barkcloth tied at the hips as a loincloth. One spitting bowl of *kou* is studded all over with human teeth, base included. Poi was kept in a coconut cut lengthways and highly polished; kava was drunk from a coconut cup. Gourds served as water bottles, one globular with a short neck, its body banded with alternating black straight and zigzag lines and a twisted coconut-fibre suspension loop. Adzes have polished basalt blades lashed with stout twisted cord to oblique T-shaped handles. Fishing gear includes grooved stone sinkers and a composite *makau*, a hook of two bone pieces — shank and point — tied with fine fibre over a wooden tightening wedge and coiled to a thick twisted line. Discs of smoothly ground black or brown-black lava, thin or thick, flat or slightly convex, served as mirrors, *kilo pohaku*.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The emblem of rank is the *lei niho palaoa*, a whale-tooth ornament suspended on many strands of plaited human hair cord; a related pendant carves a turtle from sperm-whale tooth with turtle-shell inlaid eyes. Feather garlands, *lei hulu*, are made for head or neck, chiefly of red feathers, some with three narrow yellow bands bordered in black set at equal intervals. Other ornaments use teeth and tusks: a bracelet of twenty-three boars' tusks cut square at the base and threaded on a double band of plaited *olonā*; an armlet of tortoise-shell sections alternating with sperm-whale tooth, each piece twice pierced for the strings. Leg ornaments include one of small white shells closely set on fibre string, a pair made of berries on a netted foundation, and the dog-tooth *kūpeʻe niho ʻīlio* worn in hula, its teeth fixed at regular intervals to *olonā* netting. A hook of sperm-whale ivory, pierced halfway down its shaft, hangs on fibre strings strung with black seeds.

## Music & performance

*Mele* are the poems and songs, *oli* the chants, and both are central to ritual. The percussion that accompanies chanted hula includes the *pahu hula*, the *kilu* or *puniu*, the *ipu*, the nose flute *hano* or *ʻohe hano ihu*, and the *ka*, *pū*, *oeoe*, *pahupahu kaʻekeʻeke*, *hokio* and *wī*. Collected instruments show what these were: a drum of half a pale coconut split on one side, resting on a hollow cylinder of palm wood pierced with six oblong apertures, its membrane strained by twisted sinnet run through them; a thigh drum of coconut shell with a fish-skin head bound to a ring of barkcloth and worn on a plaited leg band; paired bamboo percussion sticks closed at one end and split comb-like down the other, beaten together to mark time at hula; a single closed bamboo length dropped on stone for the same purpose; and the *ʻūkēkē*, a bamboo mouth-resonated string instrument with pointed ends, held in the teeth. Dancers add the *ʻuli*, *puʻili*, *ʻiliʻili*, *papahehi* and wooden sticks — a gourd rattle of the *uliuli* type carries seeds inside and a bouquet-shaped framework of sticks and dried palm leaves above, its concave top ringed with two circles of cocks' feathers alternating with two of red *ʻiʻiwi* feathers around a central shell disc. The ukulele and guitar came with Portuguese, Mexican and Spanish arrivals; Hawaiians went on to invent the slack-key and steel guitars, and Hawaiian music using these instruments found worldwide popularity from the 1920s.

## Dance & theatre

Hula is Hawaii's best-known Indigenous art form, danced originally as ritual to honour the gods and goddesses, each dance telling a story through movement and gesture. It divides into *hula kahiko*, the style developed before European contact, interpretive and accompanied by percussion and traditional chanting; and *hula ʻauana*, shaped by later Western influence, danced to non-traditional instruments in colourful outfits, popular with tourists and the form most practised outside the islands. After the 1893 overthrow hula was frowned upon along with the Hawaiian language, paddling and music. The annual Merrie Monarch Festival now gathers hula *hālau* from around the world to compete and share their knowledge, and crowns winners in each category including an individual female winner, Miss Aloha Hula; it honours King David Kalākaua, the last reigning king of Hawaii, who restored and elevated hula after the arrival of United States missionaries. Much of the world treats hula as a fitness trend and a cultural dance, but it carries the stories of Hawaiian politics, culture and tradition across generations. The sources describe no theatre tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The *lūʻau*, a banquet, is the most popular form of celebration in Hawaiʻi. Prince Kūhiō Day, held every March 26 since 1949, marks the birthday of Prince Jonah Kūhiō Kalanianaʻole, the Congressman who helped Native Hawaiian families become landowners; canoe races and *lūʻau* are held across the islands. King Kamehameha Day falls on June 11 and honours Kamehameha I, who unified the islands and founded the Kingdom of Hawaiʻi and who was remembered as a fearless warrior and wise diplomat; it is celebrated with parades and with lei-draping ceremonies in which *lei* are brought to the King Kamehameha statues on the islands and hung from the cast bronze arms and neck. Ritual life was governed by the *kapu* system of social stratification, overthrown after the introduction of Christianity. Volcanic eruption is understood as the earth being reborn, a time to pray, sing and give offerings to Pele. Warfare had its own equipment, the weapons themselves a record of practice: the throwing club *pikoi*, whose four-pointed stone head was lashed by plaited coir to a wooden handle and launched at the lower legs of oncoming opponents to ground them; the missile club *newa* of heavy brown *kou* with a three-sided head; the barbed spear *ihe*, one example headed with five triple-barbed tiers on a slender uncarved shaft; the sling *maʻa* of plaited *olonā* with a lozenge-shaped pouch of cord stained reddish brown; daggers called *pāhoa*, one of *koa* wood and swordfish bill, another *pāhoa aʻu* made from a blue marlin bill and coconut fibre; shark-tooth weapons, one a single large tooth lashed to a right-angled handle, another three teeth strung on plaited fibre; and a strangling cord of plaited *olonā* with a whale-ivory toggle.

## Foodways

*Poi* is the food the sources name first, kept in polished half-coconut vessels; it comes from taro, the plant said to be the manifestation of Hāloa, from whom all Hawaiians originate, whose cultivation reinforces the belief that Hawaiians take their origin from the land itself. A *lūʻau* commonly features *poi*, *poke*, lomi-lomi salmon, kālua pig and *haupia*, with ʻukulele music and hula as entertainment. Kava was drunk from coconut cups and from shallow carved bowls of hard brown wood, one of the latter recorded as a priest's cup. Fishing supplied much of the rest: the composite bone hook and its coiled line, grooved stone sinkers, and — in a photograph from Oʻahu — a man holding an octopus. Another photograph shows women and men on a mat outside a grass house preparing for a feast, with pots of different sizes, a water gourd and food-preparation tools laid out, and two young men in a canoe with a fishing hoop behind them. Changes to the Hawaiian diet after contact, together with introduced disease, contributed to the collapse of the population.

## Oral tradition & literature

Hawaiians created new religious and cultural structures in response to their new circumstances and to pass knowledge from one generation to the next, and the chants and songs — *oli* and *mele* — are the carriers of that knowledge. The legends the sources name concern origins and arrival: Hawaiʻiloa, and the navigator-priest Paʻao, said to have voyaged between Hawaiʻi and "Kahiki" — Tahiti — and to have introduced many customs, though King Kalākaua claimed Paʻao came from Sāmoa. Stories of the *Menehune*, little people who built *heiau* and fishponds, are cited as evidence of an earlier population, but similar stories are told throughout Polynesia. The gods Wākea and Papahānaumoku are the father and mother of the islands: their stillborn child formed the deep roots of Hawaii and their second child was Hāloa. Preservation work is institutional as well as oral — the Bernice Pauahi Bishop Museum, founded in 1889 and designated the Hawaiʻi State Museum of Natural and Cultural History, holds the largest collection of Native Hawaiian artifacts and documents.

## Language & religion

*ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi*, the Hawaiian language, belongs to the Polynesian family. An 1896 law made English the only medium of instruction in all public and private schools, and Hawaiians today predominantly speak English, or the creole known locally as Pidgin that developed during the plantation era. Hawaiian is now an official language of the State of Hawaiʻi alongside English, taught in immersion schools since the 1978 cultural-preservation programme and studied to doctoral level at the University of Hawaiʻi at Hilo; it remains the primary language of Niʻihau. Some Hawaiians also used the little-studied Hawaiʻi Sign Language. The traditional religion is polytheistic and animistic, holding that spirits are present in objects such as the waves and the sky, and centring on the four gods Kāne (creation), Kanaloa (the ocean), Kū (war and male pursuits) and Lono (peace, rain and fertility), alongside Pele, goddess of volcanoes and fire. Respect for the *ʻāina*, the land, and for *ʻohana*, family, follows from it. The religion is protected under the American Indian Religious Freedom Act.
