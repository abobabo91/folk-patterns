## Overview

The Huli are an indigenous Melanesian ethnic group living in Hela Province of Papua New Guinea, especially in the Tagari River basin and on the slopes of surrounding mountain ranges. Their homeland lies at an altitude of about 1,600 meters above sea level and includes primary forests, reed-covered marshes, kunai grasslands, scrub brush, rivers, streams, drainage ditches, and mounded gardens. They number over 250,000 people, based on the population of Hela at the time of the 2011 national census. They speak mainly Huli and Tok Pisin; many also speak surrounding languages, and some speak English. The sources present Huli material culture through tools, weapons, garments, wigs, belts, ornaments, and ceremonial costume, while their intangible culture includes oral histories, genealogies, traditions, and a specialized language used in bush activities.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Carved chevrons** — One British Museum adze has carved chevrons on its quadrangular wooden upper section, with the incisions infilled with red pigment.

**Burned bamboo designs** — A bamboo tobacco-pipe has designs formed by burning.

**Red-pigment decoration** — Red pigment appears on several objects, including an adze, belts, arm-bands, a hornbill-beak neck ornament, and a tobacco-pipe.

### Clothing & dress

The catalogue records describe both garments and ceremonial costumes. Apron-type garments are made from vegetable fibre cord or woven brown vegetable fibre string. One has a fibre-cord fringe ending in short lengths of yellow cane, while another is roughly rectangular; another garment has a central woven panel. A belt may consist of bark overwrapped with brown fibre strips and decorated with brown and yellow fibre, or of curved rattan strips bound with fibre cord. Photographs describe Huli(?) men wearing plant-fibre skirts, bark loin cloths, plant-fibre arm ornaments, belts, and wigs. Women are shown wearing plant-fibre wigs, feather headdresses, shell ornaments, necklaces, and fur chest ornaments. Ceremonial costumes include black plant-fibre wigs, feather headdresses, plant-fibre skirts or loin cloths, arm ornaments, shell and pig-tusk necklaces, and decorated poles or weapons.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The catalogue records document wooden and cane tools and weapons, including axes, adzes, bows, and arrows. Axes have wooden hafts, cane bindings, and small polished stone blades. Adzes combine wooden pieces bound with cane; one has a cassowary(?) claw cutting point, and another has a claw secured with fibre string. Arrows are made of wood, cane, vegetable fibre, claw, and pigment. Bows are made of wood and cane. A bamboo tobacco-pipe has a closed node and burned designs.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The objects include a polished pearl-shell breast ornament, cowrie-shell necklaces mounted on curved wooden strips, a hornbill-beak neck ornament, and ornaments incorporating pig tusks. Photographs show shell necklaces, pig-tusk necklaces, shell-chain nose ornaments, nose ornaments, beaded necklaces, fur chest ornaments, and arm ornaments. A photographed Huli(?) woman has yellow face paint, while a photographed Huli(?) man has a red-painted nose. Wigs combine vegetable fibre, human hair, feathers, cassowary feathers, bird of paradise feathers, leaves, snake skin(?), cuscus fur, and other materials. Some catalogue records also mention red ochre, bone, jaw(?), and metal springs.

## Dance & theatre

The museum photographs do document ceremonial costumes and ceremonial poles or weapons with feather decorations.

## Festivals & rituals

The Huli are described as maintaining genealogies, traditions, oral histories, and cultural expressions, and the catalogue records include objects and photographs identified as ceremonial.

## Foodways

The Huli language article states that *tayenda tu ha illili*, glossed as “bush divide taboo,” is used while collecting karuka nuts, called *anga*, as well as while hunting or travelling.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Huli are described as being keenly aware of their history and folklore through knowledge of family genealogy and traditions. They recount lengthy oral histories relating to individuals and clans.

## Language & religion

Huli is a Tari language spoken by the Huli people of Hela Province of Papua New Guinea. The sources state that Huli speakers mainly use Huli and Tok Pisin; many also speak surrounding languages, and some speak English. Huli has a specialized pandanus language called *tayenda tu ha illili*, used for collecting *anga*, hunting, and travelling, partly to evade malevolent bush spirits. Its grammar is nearly identical to ordinary Huli, but its vocabulary changes and may borrow words from Duna with altered meanings. Huli has a pentadecimal numeral system: *ngui* means 15, *ngui ki* means 30, and *ngui ngui* means 225.
