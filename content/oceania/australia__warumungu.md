## Overview

The Warumungu, also known as Warramunga, are Aboriginal Australians of the Northern Territory. They are mainly concentrated in the region of Tennant Creek and Alice Springs, and Tennant Creek is the urban centre of Warumungu country. In Warumungu, Alice Springs is called Warm Springs, described as its original name. Norman Tindale estimated that Warumungu lands once extended over some 21,300 square miles (55,000 km2), from Mount Grayling (Renner Springs) southwards to the headwaters of the Gosse River, with eastern boundaries around Alroy and Rockhampton Downs and western limits 50 miles west of Tennant Creek. Warumungu is a living language, with 320 speakers recorded by 2016, while the Warumungu estimate their speaker population to be 700 and increasing. The group’s documented significance in this source set lies especially in language, country, history, land rights, and mythology.

## Festivals & rituals

 They state that *Wollunqua* is the Warumungu people’s version of the Rainbow Serpent, a creator being common to a number of Aboriginal creation stories.

## Foodways

 They state that many Warumungu people traditionally used perennial waterholes along the creek during drought years. The sources also record that, by 1915, invasion and reprisal had brought the Warumungu to the brink of starvation, and that payment for station work was generally in rations only, under generally very poor conditions. No specific foods or food names are given.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources name *Wollunqua* as the Warumungu people’s version of the Rainbow Serpent and describe it as a creator being shared by a number of Aboriginal creation stories.

## Language & religion

Warumungu is the language of the Warumungu people in Australia’s Northern Territory. It is similar to Warlpiri and is classified as a living language. It is a suffixing language: verbs are formed by adding a tense suffix, although some verbs are formed by compounding a preverb. The language is undergoing rapid change, and the morphology used by younger speakers differs significantly from that used by older speakers. An example given in the source is *apurtu im deya o warraku taun kana*, meaning “Father’s mother, is she there, in town, or not?”. Speakers have been shifting to Kriol since 2007. They identify *Wollunqua* as a creator being in Warumungu mythology.
