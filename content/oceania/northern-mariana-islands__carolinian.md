## Overview

The Carolinian people, also known as *Refaluwasch*, are a Micronesian ethnic group originating in the Caroline Islands and now living chiefly in the Northern Mariana Islands, especially Saipan. They are also known as *Remathau* in Yap’s outer islands; *Refaluwasch* means “People of the Deep Sea.” The Carolinian language is an Austronesian, specifically Chuukic, language and is an official language in the Northern Mariana Islands. The Carolinians have a history of inter-island travel, navigation, fishing, agriculture, and settlement extending across the Caroline Islands, Guam, Saipan, Tinian, and other islands. Their folk culture is particularly documented through canoe travel, celestial navigation, matriarchal land tenure, gendered dances, fishing technologies, adoption practices, tattoos, burial customs, and respect for elders.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name painted symbols on sails.

### Clothing & dress

The sources provide limited information about everyday clothing. During the American administration of Guam, Carolinian women and men were subjected to rules banning nudity; many men went to Saipan to avoid these restrictions. Carolinian women and men have distinct tattoo practices.

The sources describe ceremonial elements connected with the men’s dance. Men may wear large leaves called *ubwuut* around their necks, a traditional bead called *Lighatuttur* or *Usos*, and coconut leaves fashioned into a crown.

### Architecture

Carolinian settlements were established near sandy beaches, swamp-like areas, reef passages, and sheltered lagoons. The swampy areas east of the beach supported taro cultivation. The settlements included villages called *Arabwal* and *Ppiyal Oolang* on Saipan; another village, Tanapag, was established by Refaluwasch who left Tinian in 1879.

The people built canoes with a small roof covering less than half of the canoe, and the sides could be different colors. A boat called *Waa* was used for transporting cargo and passengers over long distances. During the battle of Saipan in 1944, Carolinians built underground bunkers reinforced with coconut logs.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do describe several practical objects and tools. Pre-contact Carolinian-designed pestles, fishing hooks, and shell rings were found in Guam. The *poiu* was a fishing stone consisting of a limestone spherical sinker smoothed into an egg shape and pierced with holes. It was connected by cordage to an inverted half coconut shell containing mashed or ground meat from a young coconut, forming a device used to attract fish.

The sources also describe the *uumw*, a traditional underground oven in which food was roasted. During the battle of Saipan, coconut logs were used to reinforce underground bunkers.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Carolinian women and men have their own tattoos. Women have tattoos on their calves, while men have tattoos on their thighs, buttocks, arms, backs of the necks, and lower backs.

The men’s dance costume includes a traditional bead called *Lighatuttur* or *Usos*. Men may also wear large leaves called *ubwuut* around their necks and crowns made from coconut leaves.

## Music & performance

They do document dance performance and the social contexts surrounding it.

The men’s dance is called *Maas*, and men also perform a stick dance. The women’s dance is called *Bwaay* and is described as slow. Certain men’s dances include leaves, beads, and coconut-leaf crowns. The dance was originally associated with warriors and later spread to other men after the Refaluwasch came to the Mariana Islands.

The sources describe fishing as connected with annual village parties, baptisms and confirmations, marriages, and other special celebrations. Most Refaluwasch are described as belonging to the Roman Catholic faith.

## Dance & theatre

Carolinian dance traditions include two gendered forms. The men’s dance is *Maas*, while the women’s dance is *Bwaay*, a slow dance. Men also perform a stick dance. In certain men’s dances, performers wear large leaves called *ubwuut* around their necks, a traditional bead called *Lighatuttur* or *Usos*, and crowns made from coconut leaves.

The sources state that the dance was originally for warriors and later spread to other men after the Refaluwasch came to the Mariana Islands.

## Festivals & rituals

In 2018, former Acting Governor of the Northern Mariana Islands Victor Hocog signed a proclamation establishing September as Chamorro and Carolinian Heritage Month and establishing Chief Aghurubw Day.

Life-cycle and family practices are more fully documented. *Mwei-mwei* is a Refaluwasch form of adoption, usually initiated and carried out between familial wives. Children are generally adopted as infants but may be as old as eleven. The biological parents must give permission, and adoptive parents treat the children as their own.

Fishing trips support village parties, baptisms and confirmations, marriages, and other special celebrations. The sources describe strong respect for elders and gendered customs concerning speech, movement, rooms, dishes, and family obligations. When a girl experiences her first menstrual period, older women may care for her in a special house; she may receive orange face-paint so the community knows that she has come of age. The sources state that these practices largely ceased in Saipan during the Second World War, although many Carolinians still practice them.

Death rituals include covering the body with sweet-smelling flowers and vines, rolling it in a pandanus mat, and placing it on the water at the edge of a particular reef. Weights are attached and the current carries the body out to the ocean. *Fiirourow* are ancestral villages and reef places where belongings of loved ones are burned.

## Foodways

Subsistence agriculture was a way of life in the Northern Mariana Islands from 1815 to 1914. In 1840, visitors observed people on Saipan living from fish, turtles, taro, and yam. Seafood forms a major part of Carolinian meat dishes, and fishing has been practiced for thousands of years. Fish may be distributed to the fisher’s wife’s family members and to others.

In the past, *palu*, or navigators, brought food from the sea and were respected. *Payúr* is the Refaluwasch term for mackerel scad. It is caught with a *poiu*, a limestone fishing stone connected to a coconut shell bait container by cordage. The device is lowered to the seabed and moved toward the surface over the course of a month; fish reaching the surface are gathered with a scoop net.

Food may be roasted in an *uumw*, a traditional underground oven.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do describe knowledge transmitted between generations. For centuries, Carolinians used sea-lanes based on memorized information passed down from the previous generation. Their navigation traditions included celestial navigation and a triangulation method called *etak*, glossed as “moving islands.”

The sources also describe a traditional political and exchange system called *sawei*, in which Carolinians gathered in Yap every two or three years. The word is given as meaning Yapese chief, conqueror, or tyrant.

The Carolinian language has been affected by English, modern media, and global culture. The sources record efforts to preserve native languages, including the Saipan Carolinian Orthography Committee, whose conference took place from July 26 to August 4, 1976. The committee established standard systems for representing the pronunciations of three dialects without selecting one dialect as the official form for school and government documents.

## Language & religion

Carolinian is an Austronesian language originating in the Caroline Islands and spoken in the Northern Mariana Islands. It is specifically a Chuukic language and is an official language alongside English. It is closely related to Satawalese, Woleaian, and Puluwatese, and also shares lexical similarities with Mortlockese, Chuukese, and Ulithian. Carolinian has several versions and is also known as Saipan Carolinian.

The language developed through migration and continuing inter-island travel. Its writing has been influenced by Chamorro, German, English, and Japanese orthographic practices. The alphabet had 28 letters in 1977 and 33 letters in 2004. Carolinian simple sentences follow Subject-Verb-Object order.

Most Refaluwasch are Roman Catholic. The sources also describe older beliefs concerning ghosts. Two types are named: *alü luwal* and *alü leim*. Ghosts were thought capable of causing illness and other harm, while medicine men could limit the damage caused by these spirits.
