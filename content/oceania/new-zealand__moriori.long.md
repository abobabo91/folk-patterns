## Overview

The Moriori are Polynesians and the first settlers of the Chatham Islands, known in Moriori as *Rēkohu* and in Māori as Wharekauri. They came from the New Zealand mainland by at least about 1500 AD, with traditions and archaeological evidence also connecting some arrivals to East Polynesia. Their culture diverged from mainland Māori during isolation, producing a distinct language, mythology, artistic expression and way of life. The population was recorded as 1,806 in 2023, most of whom did not live on the Chatham Islands. Moriori culture is especially documented through its pacifist law, marine-based subsistence, tree carvings, language revival and surviving material objects such as schist clubs, bird-hunting equipment and whale-tooth pendants.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used do not document Moriori textile or patterned-cloth traditions.

**Dendroglyphs** — Moriori carved designs called *rakau momori* into tree trunks. Most depict human forms, while others represent fish and birds; some are protected by the Hāpūpū / J M Barker Historic Reserve.

**Albatross feathers** — The albatross remains important in Moriori culture. Its feathers are worn in the hair of some Moriori as a sign of peace, and the bird appears in the design of the Kōpinga marae.

**Motif vocabulary.** Human forms, fish, birds, albatross.

### Clothing & dress

The sources do not provide a systematic account of everyday or ceremonial Moriori clothing. They state that seals supplied food and clothing before European contact, and that European men intermarried with Moriori. In the account of the first European landing, the Moriori interpreted the clothing of the crew as possibly indicating that the men were female. Albatross feathers are worn in the hair of some Moriori as a sign of peace. The museum records document whale-tooth and whalebone pendants, but do not explain whether these were daily, ceremonial or gender-specific items.

### Architecture

The Moriori built environment is represented in the sources by marae and meeting houses rather than by a detailed description of domestic house forms. The new Kōpinga marae on Chatham Island became associated with the renewal of the Covenant of Peace in January 2005. The Kopinga meeting place and Hokomenetai meeting house are in Waitangi, on Chatham Island. The albatross appears in the design of Kōpinga marae. The sources do not describe Moriori house plans, roofing, construction materials or village layouts.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources do not document Moriori ceramics or metalwork. They describe a material culture adapted to the Chatham Islands, where the population lacked culturally significant resources such as greenstone and plentiful timber. Tree trunks therefore became an important surface for ritual carving. The museum catalogue records two short schist clubs: *pohatu taharua*, with an oblong blade, convex sides, concave edges, strongly marked shoulders and a rounded butt; and *okewa*, with a bill-hook-shaped blade, curved handle and pointed butt. A bird-spear point called *uata* is made of bone, possibly albatross wing-bone, and has five sharp barbs on one side.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The museum records three whale-related pendants. Two are whale-tooth pendants called *rei*: one is perforated, deeply undercut on its convex side near the hole, and strongly curved; the other has a large hole, abrasions near the curved point and a worn or broken tip. A third pendant, also called *rei* or a cloak pin, is made of whalebone, perforated, curved and blunt-tipped. The sources do not document tattooing, henna, hair-cutting customs or gender-specific systems of adornment. They do record the wearing of albatross feathers in the hair as a sign of peace.

## Music & performance

The sources provide little information about Moriori music or named instrumental traditions. In 2001, work began on preserving the vocabulary and songs of the Moriori people. The sources do not name instruments, song genres, performance occasions or musical structures. Moriori oral tradition is mentioned in connection with the first European landing in 1791: the crew of HMS Chatham was remembered as containing the god of fire because of the pipes they were smoking, while their clothing contributed to the interpretation that they were female. The sources also describe the preservation of Moriori songs as part of a wider cultural and language revival. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription concerns this ethnic group.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used do not document Moriori dances, dramatic genres, masked performances, theatrical forms or named entertainment traditions. They mention artistic expression in general and describe dendroglyphs, or *rakau momori*, as a distinctive form of carving, but do not connect these carvings with dance or theatre.

## Festivals & rituals

The central documented Moriori ritual principle is the law of Nunuku, associated with the sixteenth-century Moriori leader Nunuku-whenua. This pacifist culture prohibited warfare and cannibalism and replaced armed conflict with ritual fighting and conciliation. In the described form of ritual fighting, men could strike one another with a rod, but the contest ended when the skin was abraded or blood first appeared, at which point honour was considered satisfied.

The renewal of the Covenant of Peace at Kōpinga marae in January 2005 symbolised the continuing importance of this principle. The sources do not provide an annual festival calendar or detailed accounts of birth, coming-of-age, marriage or funeral rites. They do record a Moriori council, or *hui*, at Te Awapatiki in 1835, where two chiefs, Tapata and Torea, declared that the law of Nunuku was a moral imperative rather than a strategy to be altered according to circumstances.

## Foodways

Moriori foodways developed in response to the colder and less hospitable conditions of the Chatham Islands. The islands were unsuitable for cultivating most crops known to Polynesians, so the Moriori adopted a hunter-gatherer lifestyle. Food was almost entirely marine-sourced, with fish, fur seals and the fatty young of seabirds providing protein and fat. Stranded whales, grampuses and porpoises were described as special delicacies, while seals and whale blubber were also valued. The islands supported about 2,000 people under this way of life.

The sources state that pigs and potatoes were introduced after European contact. Seals had religious significance and supplied food and clothing, but were almost entirely wiped out after European sealing activity expanded. The museum records identify an *uata* as equipment for hunting birds and describe its bone point and barbs. The sources do not document named dishes, cooking methods, beverages, dietary rules or ceremonial meals.

## Oral tradition & literature

Moriori knowledge was transmitted through oral tradition, including accounts of the first European landing and traditions concerning genealogy and settlement. Moriori oral tradition remembered the crew of HMS Chatham as containing the god of fire because of the pipes they smoked. Genealogical traditions and artefact evidence also form part of discussions of migration to the Chatham Islands.

The sources state that much knowledge of pre-contact Moriori culture and language is conjectural because evidence was lost. A manuscript petition written in 1862 by surviving Moriori elders, a vocabulary collected by Samuel Deighton and published in 1887, and texts collected by Alexander Shand and published in 1911 are principal documents for the language. Work began in 2001 to preserve Moriori vocabulary and songs. The language was reconstructed for Barry Barclay’s 2000 documentary The Feathers of Peace, and the Ta Rē Moriori app was launched in 2021 to teach the language.

## Language & religion

Moriori is an Eastern Polynesian language closely related to Māori and Cook Island Māori, with which it was mutually intelligible. It shared about 70% of its vocabulary with Māori but differed in grammar and pronunciation. Moriori had a guttural diction and regularly suppressed terminal vowels, so words could end in consonants. It became extinct as a first language at the turn of the 20th century, although revitalisation efforts continue. The last man who could speak it was Johann Friedrich Wilhelm Baucke, who lived from 1848 to 1931.

Moriori religious and ethical life is documented through the law of Nunuku, the ban on warfare and cannibalism, the religious significance of seals and the ritual importance of tree carvings. The sources do not describe a complete Moriori pantheon, conversion history or current religious landscape.
