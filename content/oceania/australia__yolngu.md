## Overview

The Yolŋu or Yolngu are an aggregation of Aboriginal Australian people inhabiting north-eastern Arnhem Land in the Northern Territory of Australia. *Yolŋu* means “person” in the Yolŋu languages. Yolŋu society comprises several distinct groups differentiated by the languages and dialects they speak, while sharing similarities in ritual life and hunter-gathering economic and cultural practices. Their languages are generally grouped under Yolŋu Matha, a linguistic family including Dhangu-Djangu, Nhangu, Dhuwal, Ritharngu, Djinang and Djinba. Yolŋu social life is organised through the Dhuwa and Yirritja moieties and the complex kinship system known as *gurruṯu*. Their customary law, artistic practices, music, dance, land-rights history and continuing cultural institutions make them significant to the study of Aboriginal Australian culture.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Baskets** — Yolŋu weavers make baskets from dyed pandanus leaves.

**Bead necklaces** — Necklaces are made from beads of seeds, fish vertebrae or shells.

**Bark painting** — Yolŋu art is associated with fine cross-hatching paintings on bark. The sources identify this as a well-known Yolŋu style.

**Larrakitj** — *Larrakitj* are hollow logs used in Arnhem Land burial practices. They serve an important spiritual purpose and also function as canvases for Yolŋu art.

Colours can help identify where an artwork comes from and which clan or family group created it. Some designs are described as insignia of particular families and clans.

### Architecture

They do refer to sacred and restricted places called *dhuyu ṉuŋgat wäŋa*, which are used in the maintenance, education and development of law.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Yolŋu sacred objects or emblems used in ceremony are called *Raŋga*. *Maḏayin girri* refers to the instruments and objects that encode and symbolise Yolŋu law. The sources also describe *yiḏaki*, traditionally made and played by Yolŋu men, and spears, which were vital to Yolŋu livelihood and took several days to make and forge. Yolŋu obtained boomerangs through trade from Central Australian clans rather than manufacturing them themselves.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention necklaces made from beads of seeds, fish vertebrae or shells.

## Music & performance

The *yiḏaki*, named by Europeans as the didgeridoo, is associated with Arnhem Land. Yolŋu are both players and craftsmen of the instrument. It can be played only by certain men, and strict protocols traditionally surround its use. The sources distinguish Yirritja and Dhuwa varieties: Yirritja *yiḏaki* are shorter and higher-pitched than Dhuwa *yiḏaki*.

The Yothu Yindi band became Australia’s most successful contemporary Indigenous music group and performed throughout the world. Its song “Treaty”, released in 1989, became an international hit and expressed a protest concerning Aboriginal land rights and respect for Yolŋu culture, territory and Law. The band’s name refers to *yothu-yindi*, a term literally meaning “child-big (one)” and describing the special relationship between a person and their mother’s moiety.

Geoffrey Gurrumul Yunupingu (1971–2017) was a famous Yolŋu singer. Yolŋu traditional dancers and musicians have performed widely throughout the world.

## Dance & theatre

Yolŋu traditional dancers and musicians have performed widely throughout the world. Yolŋu ceremony includes songs and dances, and Wangarr beings are described as having given clans their language, law, paintings, songs, dances, ceremonies and creation stories.

The sources also mention *Waŋa*, a work performed in 2022. Rirratjŋu lore man Banula Marika advised choreographer Gary Lang and his NT Dance Company on the work, which was performed with MIKU Performing Arts and Darwin Symphony Orchestra and depicts a spirit’s journey after death.

## Festivals & rituals

Every year, Yolŋu come together to celebrate their culture at the Garma Festival of Traditional Cultures. Non-Yolŋu are welcome to attend and learn about Yolŋu traditions and Law. The Yothu Yindi Foundation oversees the festival.

Yolŋu customary law is called *rom*, and particular ceremonies associated with it are known as Rom ceremony. The complete system of customary law is known as *Ngarra*, or *Maḏayin*. Maḏayin includes law, instruments and objects, oral dictates, names and song cycles, and holy restricted places. It regulates land and waters, resources, production and trade, and moral, social and religious law.

Yolŋu kinship governs responsibilities for ceremony and marriage rules. A Yirritja person must marry a Dhuwa person, and children take their father’s moiety. Brother–sister avoidance, called *mirriri*, normally begins after initiation.

## Foodways

The sources describe Yolŋu as having hunter-gathering economic and cultural lifestyles. Yolŋu Wangarr ancestor-beings are said to have hunted and gathered food, held ceremonies and created plants and geographical features. The sources also mention conservation and farming of plants and aquatic life as matters regulated by Yolŋu law.

Yolŋu engaged in extensive annual trade with Makassan fishermen, who visited to harvest trepang and pearls. Yolŋu received goods including knives, metal, canoes, tobacco and pipes.

## Oral tradition & literature

Yolŋu oral histories and the Djanggawul myths preserve accounts of a Baijini people said to have preceded the Makassan. The sources describe several interpretations of the Baijini accounts, including earlier Southeast Asian visitors, mythological reflections of Yolŋu journeys to Sulawesi with the Macassans, or traders from China.

The story of Barnumbirr, the Morning Star, depicts the first death in the Dreamtime and is described as the beginning of Maḏayin, the cycle of life and death. Wangarr ancestor-beings created plants and geographical features, named species of plants and animals, made them sacred to local clans, and gave clans their languages, laws, paintings, songs, dances, ceremonies and creation stories.

They do identify Yolŋu oral histories and records made by Donald Thomson between 1935 and 1937 as important historical documents.

## Language & religion

Yolŋu Matha means “the Yolŋu tongue” and is a linguistic family associated with the languages of Yolŋu clans. It consists of about six languages, about thirty clan varieties and perhaps twelve different dialects. In 2002, there were about 4600 speakers of Yolŋu Matha languages. Exogamy has often meant that mothers and fathers speak different languages, so children traditionally grew up at least bilingual and often polylingual.

The 1963 Yirrkala bark petitions were written in a standardised Yolŋu script developed by the missionary Beulah Lowe, based on Yolŋu Matha languages, together with an English translation. Yolŋu Matha is used in films, music and broadcasting, including the Gumatj dialect sung by Dr. G. Yunupingu and the music of Yothu Yindi.

Yolŋu law and spiritual life are expressed through *rom*, *Ngarra*, *Maḏayin*, Wangarr ancestor-beings and *Raŋga* sacred objects.
