## Overview

The Tiwi are an Aboriginal people of the Tiwi Islands off northern Australia, thought to have been inhabited for some 40,000 years in relative isolation. They are known for an abstract and geometric art tradition, carved pukumani poles, and a body of song centred on ceremony. The Tiwi language is one of the most polysynthetic of all Australian languages and remains spoken by around 2,500 people.

## Material culture

Tiwi material culture is built from wood, bark, fibre and natural ochres, with no recorded ceramics or metalwork.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Tiwi textiles feature painted bark and fibre containers with distinctive diagonal lines and circles of colour.

- **Painted bark basketry** (*tunga*) — Containers folded and sewn from bark or cabbage palm leaf and painted in natural ochres with diagonal lines infilled with repetitive smaller lines, and circles of colour set around the rim.
- **Fibre string bags** (*dilly bags*) — Bags worked entirely in plant-fibre string, some with a string handle attached, carrying no applied pigment.
- **Plaited cane and fibre bands** — Head-bands made of plaited cane fibre alone or of fibre string decorated with pigment, or combining vegetable fibre, paint, beeswax, gum and feathers.
- **Lorraine Cross** — A motif taken from the missionary Francis Xavier Gsell, used on pukumani poles and in Tiwi printing.

### Clothing & dress

Tiwi dress centres on body painting and ornaments rather than woven cloth.

- **Body painting** — Designs painted onto the bodies of both men and women for dance and ceremony, and explicitly part of mourning as an expression of love for the dead.
- **Mourning armlet** — An armlet of cane bound with animal-hair string and coloured yellow and white, with a pendant of the same string.
- **Mourning neck ornament** — A neck ornament of pliant wood whose ends are drawn together and tied with string and brown gum, the wooden part carrying a powdery white pigment.
- **Headbands** — Worn with neck ornaments and armlets for ceremony, decorated with natural ochre pigments.

### Architecture

The sources say almost nothing about Tiwi building.

- **Mission at Nguiu** — Built after 1911 under Bishop Francis Xavier Gsell with the help of two local Tiwi men, now called Wurrumiyanga.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Tiwi everyday objects are made from wood, bark and fibre, with no recorded ceramics or metalwork.

- **Barbed spears** — Wooden spears decorated with earth pigments.
- **Clapping sticks** — The only instrument recorded in the museum collection.
- **Ceremonial paddle** — A paddle with a decorated blade and a pronged grip, its shaft bound with fibre cord at the top.
- **Game club** — A club with a bulbous head, fluted lines, a coat of red pigment and a carved band below the head picked out in yellow ochre.
- **Whisk** — Made of black cockatoo feathers with its handle coated in wax and ochre.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Tiwi adornment uses plaited cane, fibre, ochre, seeds and introduced glass beads.

- **Head ornament** — Built up from plaited cane covered with gum and formed into a flat ring, painted in ochre bands of red, white and yellow, with a tassel of two-ply twisted vegetable-fibre cord.
- **Arm ornaments** — A circular woven vegetable-fibre arm-band; a band bound with string and coated in mud-like substance, with string pendants; or a bangle of fibre, cabbage palm bark and beeswax.
- **Necklet** — Made of coloured glass beads with twisted fibre ties, an introduced material.
- **Chest scarification** — Visible on men photographed for ceremony.
- **Grass rings** — Worn in bundles.

## Music & performance

Music runs through every part of Tiwi life and is centred on the kulama initiation ceremony.

- **Kulama ceremony** — The yam initiation ceremony around which Tiwi music is organised and songs are centred.
- **Tiwi songs** — Songs about the land, hunting, cooking, family, animals, plants and the Australian outback, handed down across generations.
- **Clapping stick** — The only instrument recorded for music.
- **Mourning songs** — Songs sung in mourning, where music joins painting and dance as the expression of grief.

## Dance & theatre

Dance called yoi is part of everyday life and defines spiritual identity through totemic forms.

- **Yoi** — Dance that is part of everyday life and inherited from the father, evocative of the Dreamtime belief system.
- **Narrative dances** — Dances performed to depict everyday life or historical events.
- **The First Funeral Dance** — A published Tiwi text recording two parts of a traditional ceremony initiated by Alikampwaɹni and T̪aŋkənaŋki, and the ceremony Purukupaɹli dances to mourn his son.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual year turns on kulama, the yam initiation ceremony, and on funeral and mourning observances marked by pukumani poles.

- **Kulama** — The yam initiation ceremony around which Tiwi music is organised and the ritual year centres.
- **Pukumani poles** (*pukumani*) — Carved poles that carry an abstract and geometric art tradition and mark funeral and mourning observances.
- **Mourning observances** — The body is painted, and music, art and dance express love for the person who has died, with the name of the dead becoming taboo for many years.
- **Puberty ceremonies** — Ceremonies for women documented in photographs from Melville Island.
- **Widow's remarriage** — Traditionally took place at the gravesite of the previous husband, immediately after his death.

## Foodways

Hunting and gathering remain important, with a conservation rule that mothers and juveniles are never taken out of respect for the land.

- **Land hunting** — The Tiwi hunt wallaby, lizards, possums, carpet snakes, pig, buffalo, flying foxes, bandicoot, turtle and seagull eggs, and magpie geese.
- **Sea hunting** — From the sea, the Tiwi take turtle, crocodiles, dugong and fish.
- **Bush foods** — Fish, turtle eggs, mussels, yams and mangrove worms gathered by men and women in relatively equal division of labour.
- **Yam** (*kulama*) — Carries a medical belief: the concept of tarni, the general state of sickness, derives from the belief that if a pregnant woman ate a yam the sharp pointed tip would pierce the womb and kill the baby.
- **Plant remedies** — Pandanus leaf for diarrhea and leaves from the centre of plants laid on the forehead for headache.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tiwi art forms an integral part of the oral tradition, passing history and wisdom between generations.

- **Creation account** — An old blind woman named Mudangkala made the Tiwi Islands: she rose from the sea cradling three infants, and as she crawled through the dark the sea and land filled the imprints of her body.
- **Purukupaɹli narrative** — A funeral-dance narrative published with Tiwi text and English translation by C. R. Osborne in 1974, the fullest recorded narrative in the sources.
- **Ratuwati Yinjara** — The original name for the two large islands, meaning two islands.

## Language & religion

Tiwi is the most polysynthetic of all Australian languages with around one hundred nominals that can be incorporated into the verb, and religion is dual: Dreamtime belief alongside Roman Catholicism.

- **Tiwi language** (*Nimara*) — The most polysynthetic of all Australian languages, one of about 10% of Australian languages still frequently learned by children, with around 2,500 speakers.
- **Traditional Tiwi** — Polysynthetic, by 1993 spoken only by people over 55.
- **Modern Tiwi** — Relatively isolating with some inflection and carrying many English loans, spoken by everyone up to about 30.
- **Tunuvivi** — Another name for the Tiwi language meaning people, or we the only people.
- **Dreamtime belief** — A traditional belief system held alongside Roman Catholicism, established after Bishop Francis Xavier Gsell's arrival in 1911.

## Glossary

- *Tunuvivi* — The Tiwi people; we the only people
- *tunga* — Painted bark basketry containers
- *pukumani* — Carved poles that mark funeral and mourning observances
- *yoi* — Dance that is part of everyday life
- *kulama* — Yam initiation ceremony; also the yam itself
- *tarni* — The general state of sickness
- *Ratuwati Yinjara* — Two islands; original name for the two large Tiwi Islands
- *Nimara* — To talk; language
- *Mudangkala* — Old blind woman who created the Tiwi Islands in creation account
- *Purukupaɹli* — Figure in funeral-dance narrative who mourns his son
- *Alikampwaɹni* — Initiator of traditional funeral dance
- *T̪aŋkənaŋki* — Brother-in-law of Alikampwaɹni who co-initiated traditional funeral dance
