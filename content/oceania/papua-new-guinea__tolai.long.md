## Overview

The Tolai are the indigenous people of the Gazelle Peninsula and the Duke of York Islands of East New Britain, in the New Guinea Islands region of Papua New Guinea. They are ethnically close kin to peoples of adjacent New Ireland, including the Tanga people, and are thought to have migrated to the Gazelle Peninsula relatively recently, displacing the Baining people westwards. Approximately 100,000 Tolai speak Kuanua as their first language, while Lungalunga and Bilur each have approximately 2,000 first-language speakers. Tolai belongs to the Oceanic branch of the Austronesian language family. The sources document Tolai material culture through fishing equipment, tools, masks, dance-wands, drums, a figure and other museum objects, and describe continuing ceremonial beliefs associated with Tubuans and the Duk-Duk society.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used do not document a Tolai textile or patterned-cloth tradition.

**Fishing net** — A British Museum fishing net is a long rectangular seine net made from vegetable fibre. It has twenty stone sinkers, two end sinkers encased in cane plaiting, and eighteen flat, oval-shaped orange wood floats.

**Decorated end floats** — The large spatulate wood floats at the ends of the net are attached with cane plaiting. Their two sides carry punctuated and painted designs representing a human figure, two turtles, one of two fish, and numerous fish and plant-like shapes.

**Dance-mask (‘lor’)** — A composite *lor* dance mask has a narrow, roughly triangular light-wood face with cut-out eyes, painted curved horizontal bands, a vegetable-fibre beard and hair, and a domed head. Above the face is a vegetable-fibre disk edged with white feathers and bearing a standing peacock figure with outstretched wings and displayed tail.

**Dance-wand** — One flat wooden polychrome dance-wand is carved and painted on both sides in the form of a human face with a tall fretwork headdress ending in a plume of white, yellowish and black feathers. It has pearl-shell, rubber and white-bead details, fibre beards and feather “hair.”

**Dance-wand fragment** — Another British Museum object is a triangular-shaped polychrome piece of wooden fretwork with feathers attached at the top. The wood is painted red, yellow, green and white, and the feathers are white, red, yellow and black.

**Carved mask** — A carved mask has a white-painted face made with natural pigments, a nose-pin and a fibre beard. Its hat is covered with red European textile, and a textile-covered snake emerges from the crown.

**Motif vocabulary.** Human figure, turtles, fish, plant-like shapes, peacock, concentric circles, tear-drop motifs, chevrons, fretwork headdress.

## Music & performance

The Tolai sources document drums and slit-drums but do not provide names for them in a Tolai language. One British Museum object is a wood drum used in dance. It has an hour-glass shape, a lizard-skin (?) membrane, a handle and a detached cane coil that originally held the membrane in place. Its lower third is decorated with low-relief carving and European paint: three motifs of concentric circles, three green tear-drop motifs, a band of chevrons, and a green band at the base. The object is inscribed near the handle “Kai.Tupi.”

Another object is a small wood slit-drum with a circular cross-section, a central tongue and cylindrical handles at each end. The sources do not state how it is played, who plays it, or whether it accompanies particular songs, dances, ceremonies or life-cycle events. They also do not document song genres, vocal styles, musical scales, performance ensembles, weddings, funerals, courts, festivals or recording and preservation projects.

The Tolai language is described as the primary language of communication in the two major centres of East New Britain, Kokopo and Rabaul. The sources do not connect these centres with specific musical traditions.

## Dance & theatre

The sources document a Duk-Duk society and secret ceremonies performed by initiates. They also describe a continuing belief in the female spirits of the Tubuans. The sources do not explain the precise sequence, participants, meanings or calendar of these ceremonies.

Museum records identify a wood drum as used in dance, and several objects as dance equipment: a composite *lor* dance mask and two dance-wands. The *lor* mask includes a painted face, fibre beard and hair, feather-edged disk and peacock figure. One dance-wand represents a human face with a fretwork headdress, feathers, beads and fibre beards; the other is a polychrome wooden fretwork fragment with feathers.

The sources do not document named dances, dramatic plots, theatrical genres, audience arrangements, or distinctions between ceremonial and entertainment performances beyond the association of these objects with dance and the Duk-Duk society.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not provide an annual Tolai festival calendar, named seasonal festivals, or dates for recurring ceremonies. They do describe secret ceremonies performed by initiates of the Duk-Duk society and a belief in the female spirits of the Tubuans. They also record a belief in sorcery used either to gain someone’s love or to punish an enemy.

Christianity was introduced to the New Guinea islands region in 1875, when Methodist ministers and teachers from Fiji arrived. In 1878, some tribespeople ate four of the missionaries. George Brown, the Englishman who led the missionaries, directed and took part in a punitive expedition that resulted in a number of Tolais being killed and several villages being burnt down.

In August 2007, descendants of Tolai tribespeople who had eaten a Fijian minister and three Fijian teachers in 1878 publicly apologized to Fiji’s High Commissioner, Ratu Isoa Tikoca. The apology was accepted. At the event, Papua New Guinea’s Governor-General Paulias Matane spoke about the work of the early Fijian missionaries in spreading Christianity in the islands region.

The sources do not describe Tolai birth, coming-of-age, wedding or funeral rites.

## Foodways

The sources used do not describe Tolai staple foods, dishes, cooking methods, food taboos, ceremonial meals, beverages, dietary rules, or food production. The Tolai language source gives *minamai* as meaning “a small supply of betelnuts for chewing,” and lists *buai* as a Tok Pisin vocabulary item likely derived from Tolai, meaning “betelnut.” It also gives *mamai* as the Tolai verb “to chew betelnut.” No further foodways are documented.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used do not document Tolai folktales, epic poetry, proverbs, riddles, written literature, storytelling contexts, or contemporary literary preservation efforts. The Tolai language source provides example sentences and vocabulary, including *tinata*, “the language,” but does not identify an oral-literary genre. It states that Tolai is one of the major substratum languages of Tok Pisin and lists vocabulary items likely to come from Tolai or a closely related language, including *aibika*, *buai*, *diwai*, *guria*, *kawawar*, *kiau*, *lapun*, *liklik* and *umben*.

## Language & religion

The Tolai language, or Kuanua, is spoken by the Tolai people in the Gazelle Peninsula of East New Britain Province. The language is also referred to in literature as Tolai, although Tolai is the name of the cultural group. The Tolais themselves call their language *tinata tuna*, translated as “the real language.” Kuanua is apparently a word in Ramoaaina meaning “the place over there.”

Tolai belongs to the Oceanic branch of the Austronesian language family. Its most immediate subgroup is the Patpatar–Tolai group, which also includes Lungalunga and Patpatar. Tolai is described as a healthy language and as a prestigious primary language of communication in Kokopo and Rabaul. It has many Tok Pisin loanwords and has lost the phoneme /s/, although /s/ has been reintroduced through English and Tok Pisin loanwords.

The majority of Tolais speak Kuanua as their first language. The sources say that Tolais almost universally define themselves as Christian and are predominantly Roman Catholic and United Church, while older beliefs and traditions, including Tubuans, Duk-Duk ceremonies and sorcery, still persist.
