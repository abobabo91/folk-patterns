## Overview

The Wiradjuri are an Aboriginal Australian people from central New South Wales, united by common descent through kinship and shared traditions. Their country extended across the plains west of the Blue Mountains and was known as “the land of the three rivers”: the Wambuul (Macquarie), the Kalare, later known as the Lachlan, and the Murrumbidgee, or Murrumbidjeri. Major Wiradjuri groups live in Condobolin, Peak Hill, Narrandera and Griffith, with significant populations at Wagga Wagga and Leeton and smaller groups at West Wyalong, Parkes, Dubbo, Forbes, Cootamundra, Darlington Point, Cowra and Young. They were skilled hunter-fisher-gatherers organized in family groups, clans and bands. Their language belongs to the Pama–Nyungan family and the Wiradhuric branch.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Possum-skin cloaks* were stitched together from several possum furs. The Wiradjuri were known for these cloaks, and a Wiradjuri man presented one to Governor Macquarie during his visit to Bathurst in 1815.

*Carved trees* functioned as taphoglyphs marking the burial site of a notable medicine-man, ceremonial leader, warrior or orator. Initiated men stripped bark from a tree and incised symbols on the side facing the burial mound. Four examples still stand near Molong at the Grave of Yuranigh.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name incised symbols on carved trees.

### Clothing & dress

The documented Wiradjuri garment is the possum-skin cloak, made by stitching several possum furs together.

### Architecture

They do record places of historical and cultural significance, including Koonadan Historic Site, the Wellington Convict and Mission Site, Mungabareena Reserve, and sites at Yathong Nature Reserve and Nombinnie Nature Reserve.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

## Music & performance

The sources record corroborees as a Wiradjuri performance tradition. In the 1850s there were still corroborees around Mudgee, although there were fewer clashes between British settlers and Wiradjuri people.

The sources also identify Wiradjuri people involved in music and the arts, including Alan Dargin, a didgeridoo player; Melanie Horsnell, a singer-songwriter; Mo’Ju, a musician; and other artists, dancers and performers. Ella Havelka is identified as a dancer and the first Indigenous person to join The Australian Ballet. These entries do not provide descriptions of specifically Wiradjuri musical genres or performance contexts.

## Dance & theatre

They record corroborees around Mudgee in the 1850s and identify Ella Havelka as a Wiradjuri dancer.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe burial practices involving carved trees, or taphoglyphs. When a distinguished Wiradjuri person died, initiated men stripped bark from a tree and incised symbols into the trunk facing the burial mound. Such trees marked the burial sites of notable medicine-men, ceremonial leaders, warriors or orators. The sources state that these trees were generally located near rivers, where softer earth made burial easier.

Alfred William Howitt described the incised trees as transit points through which mythological cultural heroes could ascend to and descend from the firmament, as well as means for the deceased to return to the sky. Four carved trees remain near Molong at the Grave of Yuranigh.

The sources also mention corroborees around Mudgee in the 1850s.

## Foodways

Wiradjuri people lived as hunter-fisher-gatherers, and their diet included yabbies and fish such as Murray cod from the rivers. During dry seasons they ate kangaroos, emus and food gathered from the land, including fruit, nuts, yam daisies (*Microseris lanceolata*), wattle seeds and orchid tubers. In summer they travelled into Alpine areas to feast on bogong moths.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that Wiradjuri people shared traditions and that carved trees were associated with mythological cultural heroes and the deceased’s return to the sky.

The Wiradjuri language is undergoing reclamation. The process was supported by a reconstructed grammar, earlier ethnographic materials and wordlists, and the memories of Wiradjuri families. A First Wiradjuri Dictionary was published in 2005 by Stan Grant Senior and John Rudder; a revised edition containing over 8,000 words was published in 2010. A Grammar of Wiradjuri language was published in 2014. The sources also mention a mobile app and web-based version based on the dictionary.

## Language & religion

Wiradjuri is a Pama–Nyungan language of the Wiradhuric subgroup and the traditional language of the Wiradjuri people of New South Wales. Wiraiari and Jeithi may have been dialects. The language is effectively extinct, but revival work is underway. It is taught in primary and secondary schools, at TAFE and at Charles Sturt University, including in Parkes, Forbes, Condobolin, Peak Hill, Dubbo, Narromine, Wellington, Gilgandra, Trangie, Geurie and Young.

The sources describe a reconstructed grammar based on earlier ethnographic materials, wordlists and Wiradjuri family memories. They also record vocabulary such as *guwandhaang*, “native peach”; *wagga*, “crow”; and *wagan*, “crow”. The meaning of Wagga Wagga is disputed in the sources: one account connects it with “many crows”, while Stan Grant Sr states that it means “dance celebrations”.
