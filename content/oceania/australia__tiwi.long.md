## Overview

The Tiwi, who call themselves *Tunuvivi*, are an Aboriginal people of the Tiwi Islands off the northern coast of Australia — chiefly Melville and Bathurst Islands, together some 8,344 square kilometres of heavily forested land lying roughly 48 to 80 kilometres from Darwin. Nearly 2,000 Tiwi live there today, and the Tiwi language is spoken by around 2,500 people. That language has long been treated as an isolate, sharing no evident genetic link with the languages of Arnhem Land on the mainland, though recent historical-linguistic work suggests a possible place within the Gunwinyguan family. The islands are thought to have been inhabited for some 40,000 years, and in relative isolation. In folk-culture terms the Tiwi matter for an art tradition that reads as abstract and geometric beside Arnhem Land work, for the carved *pukumani* poles that carry it, and for a body of song centred on ceremony.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Painted bark basketry (*tunga*)** — Containers folded and sewn from bark or cabbage palm leaf and painted in natural ochres: white, yellow, red, grey and brown. The characteristic surface is a field of diagonal lines infilled with repetitive smaller lines, with circles of colour set around the rim; a simpler version carries white painted rings over an all-over red ochre ground. Cabbage palm bags of the same construction take a single cane handle and white designs over red.

**Fibre string bags** — Bags worked entirely in plant-fibre string, some with a string handle attached, of the kind catalogued as dilly bags. They carry no applied pigment and stand apart from the painted bark containers by technique rather than ornament.

**Plaited cane and fibre bands** — Head-bands are made either of plaited cane fibre alone or of fibre string decorated with pigment and closed with fibre ties. A more elaborate version combines vegetable fibre, paint, beeswax, gum and feathers in one band.

**Motif vocabulary.** Diagonal lines infilled with repetitive smaller lines; circles and rings of colour ranged around a container's edge; hatch patterns, which stand for friendships within the community; and the Lorraine Cross, taken from the Alsace–Lorraine of the missionary Francis Xavier Gsell and since used on *pukumani* poles and in Tiwi printing.

### Clothing & dress

Photographs in the museum record show Tiwi men and boys in loin cloths for ceremony, and women at a puberty ceremony in cloth wraps — one in a dress — with headbands, neck ornaments and armlets. Body painting is the substantial element of ceremonial dress: designs are painted onto the bodies of both men and women for dance, and men photographed prepared for ceremony have decorated hair and chest scarification. Mourning has its own dress. Mourning ornaments in the collection include an armlet of cane bound with animal-hair string and coloured yellow and white, with a pendant of the same string, and a neck ornament of pliant wood whose ends are drawn together and tied with string and brown gum, the wooden part carrying a powdery white pigment. Painting the body is explicitly part of mourning, alongside music and dance, as an expression of love for the person who has died. Natural ochre pigments gathered locally supply the colour throughout.

### Architecture

The sources used here say almost nothing about Tiwi building. The only structures they record are two small shelters made from plant fibres, visible in the background of a photograph of a ceremony on Bathurst Island, and the first mission at Nguiu — present-day Wurrumiyanga — built after 1911 under Bishop Francis Xavier Gsell with the help of two local Tiwi men. The one standing rule of the built environment reported for the contemporary islands is religious rather than structural: there remains in effect a moratorium on building any religious structure that is not Catholic.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The catalogued objects are wood, bark and fibre; the sources record no Tiwi ceramics or metalwork. Wooden weapons and implements dominate: barbed spears decorated with earth pigments, throwing sticks, and clapping sticks. A distinct class of club is described as used in a game — one has a bulbous head, fluted lines, a coat of red pigment and a carved band below the head picked out in yellow ochre, with white pigment on the head itself. A ceremonial paddle has a decorated blade and a pronged grip, its shaft bound with fibre cord at the top. Bark containers served for water: one photograph shows a man holding a bark container filled with water. A whisk is made of black cockatoo feathers with its handle coated in wax and ochre.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Head ornaments are built up from plaited cane covered with gum and formed into a flat ring, painted in ochre bands of red, white and yellow, with a tassel of two-ply twisted vegetable-fibre cord ending in a ball of gum. A man's head ornament combines bark string, abrus seeds and red ochre. Tassels are made of cockatoo feathers, ochre and human-hair cord. Arm ornaments take several forms: a circular woven vegetable-fibre arm-band; a band bound with string and coated in a mud-like substance, with string pendants attached; and a bangle of fibre, cabbage palm bark and beeswax. Grass rings occur in bundles. Introduced materials appear in a necklet of coloured glass beads with twisted fibre ties. Scarification on the chest is visible on men photographed for ceremony.

## Music & performance

Music runs through every part of life on the Tiwi Islands and is centred on the *kulama* initiation ceremony, named for the yam. The innovative use of song on such occasions is now at risk as the customary rituals that generated it weaken. Tiwi people sing songs about the land, handed down across generations, and about hunting, cooking, family, animals, plants and the Australian outback. Some have been recorded and archived, and performances by Tiwi women have been given at a number of important venues in Australia. The Tiwi "strong women's group" takes part in a collaborative project to conserve the music of Bathurst and Melville Islands, and research projects have brought some of the oldest Tiwi songs back into use — work that, given songs sung for thousands of years, must be carried out with the strictest sensitivity. The only instrument in the museum record is the wooden clapping stick. Songs are also sung in mourning, where music joins painting and dance as the expression of grief.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is called *yoi*, and it is part of everyday life. Tiwi inherit their totemic dance — evocative of the Dreamtime belief system, and defining of their spiritual identity — from their father. Narrative dances are performed to depict everyday life or historical events. Museum photographs show men dancing in a circle beside several carved *pukumani* poles, wearing loin cloths, neck ornaments and armlets; a half-circle of men and boys watching several men perform, with painted body designs and chest scarification; and women dancing in a semi-circle as part of a puberty ceremony. A published Tiwi text, "The First Funeral Dance", records two parts of a traditional ceremony: the first a dance initiated by Alikampwaɹni and his brother-in-law T̪aŋkənaŋki, which other members of the islands would perform after their deaths; the second the ceremony Purukupaɹli dances to mourn his own son, joined by his wives Waijai and Pamatikimi.

## Festivals & rituals

There are no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions for Australia among the sources used here. The ritual year as the sources describe it turns on *kulama*, the yam initiation ceremony around which Tiwi music is organised, and on the funeral and mourning observances marked by the *pukumani* poles. Death carries a heavy set of obligations: the body is painted, and music, art and dance express love for the person who has died. The name of the dead becomes taboo — for the many years during which the spirit returns to the land, that name cannot be spoken. Mourning has its own ornaments, the cane armlet and the wooden neck ornament described above. A widow's remarriage traditionally took place at the gravesite of the previous husband, immediately after his death. Puberty ceremonies for women are documented in photographs from Melville Island. Birth is understood through spirits: the Tiwi hold that unborn spirits come from bodies of water, that a man has no physical role in conception, and that his part is to find a spirit and uncover it so that it is sent to his wife — the spirit's kin clan must match the wife's. Marriage, bestowal of young wives, and remarriage govern economic, social and political standing.

## Foodways

Hunting remains an important part of Tiwi life on both Bathurst and Melville Islands. On land the Tiwi hunt wallaby, lizards, possums, carpet snakes, pig, buffalo, flying foxes, bandicoot, turtle and seagull eggs, and magpie geese; from the sea, turtle, crocodiles, dugong and fish. A conservation rule governs the taking: when locating food in the bush the Tiwi never take the mothers or the juveniles, out of respect for the land. Men and women shared a relatively equal division of labour in hunting and gathering "bush foods" — fish, turtle eggs, mussels, yams and mangrove worms. Older women held great importance in the society through their gathering skills, which secured the household's food supply; younger women learned those skills from them as apprentices. Yams carry a medical belief as well as a nutritional role: the concept of *tarni*, the general state of sickness, derives from the belief that the sharp pointed tip of a yam carries *tarni*, and that if a pregnant woman ate one the tip would pierce the womb, release the sickness and kill the baby. Plants also serve as remedies — pandanus leaf, cut and the middle chewed and swallowed, for diarrhea; leaves from the centre of the plant laid on the forehead for two or three days against headache.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tiwi art forms an integral part of the oral tradition, passing history and wisdom between generations, and the painted designs are used to tell stories. The creation account holds that an old blind woman named Mudangkala made the Tiwi Islands: she rose from the sea cradling three infants, and as she crawled through the dark the sea and land filled the imprints of her body; she then populated the islands with plants and animals to make a beautiful home for her children, who became the Tiwi. The two large islands were originally called *Ratuwati Yinjara*, "two islands". The funeral-dance narrative of Purukupaɹli, published with the Tiwi text and an English translation by C. R. Osborne in 1974, is the fullest recorded narrative in the sources, and comes with notes on lexical meanings, background to items of Tiwi culture, and the non-verbal gestures performed in the ceremony.

## Language & religion

Tiwi is the most polysynthetic of all Australian languages and has around one hundred nominals that can be incorporated into the verb. It is one of about 10% of Australian languages still frequently learned by children. Traditional Tiwi, polysynthetic, was by 1993 spoken only by people over 55; Modern Tiwi, relatively isolating with some inflection and carrying many English loans, is spoken by everyone up to about 30, alongside Children's Tiwi and Tiwi-English. Other names for the language include *Tunuvivi* ("people", or "we the only people"), *Wongak* and *Woranguwe* from Iwaidja, and *Nimara* ("to talk", "language"), coined by the writer Bill Harney; the name Tiwi itself was established by the anthropologist C. W. M. Hart in 1930 and subsequently adopted by the islanders. Religion is dual: a Dreamtime belief system alongside Roman Catholicism, established after Gsell's arrival in 1911.
