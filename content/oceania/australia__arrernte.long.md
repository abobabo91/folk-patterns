## Overview

The Arrernte are a group of Aboriginal Australian peoples who live in the Arrernte lands at Mparntwe (Alice Springs) and surrounding areas of the Central Australia region of the Northern Territory. The Arrernte name includes Central Arrernte, Eastern Arrernte and Western Arrarnta, whose lands extend east and west of Alice Springs. Norman Tindale estimated their territory at some 47,000 square miles (120,000 km2), including the Finke River, the eastern and western MacDonnell Ranges and parts of the Arunta Desert. Arrernte belongs to the Arandic language group. Eastern/Central Arrernte is spoken by about 4,100 people, while several other dialects are threatened or extinct. Arrernte cultural life includes oral history, ceremonies, songs, religious objects and a highly developed sign language.

### Clothing & dress

The museum records document several forms of dress and bodily equipment. A men’s waist girdle is made of human hair string. Another waist girdle consists of reddish woolly strings, described as possum fur strings, slightly crimped and secured at two places by short cross-bindings. A woman’s necklace is made of red seeds from the bean tree, strung on vegetable fibre string. Men’s nose ornaments are made from bone: one is probably the fibula of a kangaroo, rubbed with ochre and worn through a hole in the nasal septum; another is a sharpened bone splinter with a knob of gum, greased and ochred. The sources do not document footwear, head coverings or a separate named ceremonial dress.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum catalogue records wooden, stone and resin objects associated with Arrernte or Aranda catalogue entries. A boomerang is made of hard polished wood, roughly plano-convex in section, longitudinally grooved on the convex side and ochred. Another boomerang is made of hard wood and similarly grooved and ochred. A shield is made of wood identified as bean tree, with an oval form, a handle cut into the solid and red ochre covering. A knife is made of stone and resin identified as porcupine grass. An axe consists of stone and wood. A throwing stick, identified as *weet-weet*, is a thin flexible wooden stick that enlarges at one end into an elongated pointed bulb and is coated with red ochre.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources document necklaces, nose ornaments and ochre application. The woman’s necklace uses red bean-tree seeds and vegetable fibre string. Men’s nose ornaments are made from sharpened bone and may include gum knobs; the catalogue describes them as greased and ochred or rubbed with ochre. One is worn through a hole in the nasal septum. The museum records also include an ovoid ball of dark brown human hair string. The sources do not document tattoos, henna, hair-cutting practices or named body-painting traditions.

## Music & performance

Arrernte cultural life and religion were documented from the late nineteenth century by Carl Strehlow, Walter Baldwin Spencer, Francis Gillen and later T. G. H. Strehlow. Arrernte men worked with Strehlow to document their songs and ceremonies between 1932 and 1974. The sources do not identify named musical instruments, song genres or performance venues. They do identify songs and ceremonies as important subjects of documentation.

Peter Sculthorpe’s music theatre work *Rites of Passage* (1972–1973) is written partly in Arrernte and partly in Latin. Western and Southern Arrernte were used in parts of the libretto for Andrew Schultz’ and Gordon Williams’ *Journey to Horseshoe Bend*, based on the novel by Ted Strehlow. The Arrernte language is also used in media and local government, and is taught in schools and universities in the Alice Springs area. The sources do not describe musical scales, dance accompaniment, ceremonial instruments or contemporary performance groups.

## Festivals & rituals

Arrernte spirituality focuses on the landscape and The Dreaming, for which the Arrernte name is *Altyerre*. *Altjira* is described as the creator being of the *Inapertwa* that became all living creatures. *Tjurunga* are objects of religious significance. Arrernte oral history describes the region of Alice Springs and its environs as shaped by primordial caterpillar-beings known as *Ayepe-arenye*, *Ntyarlke* and *Utnerrengatye*, which were ancestral to the Arrernte people. The eastern MacDonnell Ranges were formed by *Ayepe-arenye*, while the western portion was formed by *Ntyarlke*.

The sources state that Arrernte men documented songs and ceremonies between 1932 and 1974, but they do not describe an annual festival calendar, seasonal festivals, initiation ceremonies, weddings, funerals or other life-cycle rites. They also do not provide dates for ritual observances.

## Oral tradition & literature

Arrernte oral history discusses the region of Alice Springs and its environs as a landscape shaped by ancestral caterpillar-beings. These include *Ayepe-arenye* (*Hyles livornicoides*), *Ntyarlke* (*Hippotion celerio*) and *Utnerrengatye* (*Coenotes eremophilae*). The eastern MacDonnell Ranges are associated with *Ayepe-arenye*, and the western portion of the ranges with *Ntyarlke*.

Arrernte cultural life was documented by Carl Strehlow, Walter Baldwin Spencer, Francis Gillen and T. G. H. Strehlow. Arrernte men worked with Strehlow to document songs and ceremonies between 1932 and 1974. The sources do not provide named folktales, epics, proverbs, riddles or a distinct literary canon. They do record language-preservation work, including the Living Archive of Aboriginal Languages, projects to revive dying dialects such as Southern Arrernte/Pertame, and the teaching of Arrernte in schools, universities and language courses.

## Language & religion

Arrernte or Aranda is a dialect cluster in the Arandic language group. It is spoken in parts of the Northern Territory by the Arrernte people. Eastern/Central Arrernte has about 4,100 speakers and is taught in schools and universities, used in media and local government, and spoken in the Alice Springs area. Eastern Arrernte, also known as Central Arrernte, includes Akarre, Antekerrepenh, Ikngerripenhe and Mparntwe Arrernte. Alyawarre is the second biggest dialect in the group. Other dialects are threatened or extinct, including Andegerebinha and Ayerrerenge; Western Arrarnta is described as nearly extinct.

The Arrernte also have a highly developed sign language, known as *Iltyeme-iltyeme*. Arrernte spirituality focuses on the landscape and *Altyerre*. *Altjira*, *Inapertwa* and *Tjurunga* are named in the sources in connection with Arrernte religious life. The sources do not describe historical scripts or a current religious denomination.
