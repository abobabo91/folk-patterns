## Overview

The Baining are an ethnic group indigenous to the Gazelle Peninsula of East New Britain, Papua New Guinea, and currently inhabit the Baining Mountains. They are considered a genetic isolate and speak the Baining languages, a distinct language family whose members correspond to clan groups. The Baining are swidden cultivators whose main traditional crop is taro, although singapu or kong-kong taro replaced most taro crops after the 1957 taro blight. Their best-known cultural expression is the fire dance, a night ceremony involving masked dancers, percussion, and a central bonfire.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

 For the fire dance, male dancers wear large masks made from bark cloth, bamboo, and leaves. These masks are used once for the ceremony before being discarded or destroyed. In Kairak areas, the masks are known as *kavat* and *vungvung*. The *vungvung* is the larger type and features an axial bamboo pole up to 13 feet (4.0 m) in length.

### Architecture

 They state that garden plots are often separated from villages and supplied with shelters where people can temporarily stay. Colonial administrators forced the Baining into settled villages, changing their earlier semi-nomadic lifestyle.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention bamboo as a material in fire-dance masks and percussion instruments as part of the accompanying male orchestra.

## Music & performance

The Baining fire dance is accompanied by an all-male orchestra of percussion instruments. During the ceremony, dancers take turns parading through the dance ground while the tempo increases. The dancers then briefly pass through a large central bonfire. The performance continues until daybreak, when members of the accompanying orchestra chase the masks from the dance grounds.

The fire dance originated in Qaqet villages, where it was called *atut*, and was later adopted by central and southern Baining communities. It was associated with the birth of new children, the commencement of harvests, and remembrance of the dead. It also functions as a rite of passage initiating boys into adulthood. During the ceremony, dancers are considered possessed by anthropomorphised animal spirits called *masalai*, which enhance the dancer’s masculinity. In recent times, the fire dance has become part of local and international cultural festivals and a major tourist attraction. The Baining have adopted it as a symbol of cultural identity and use its iconic status to emphasize their differences from the Tolai.

## Dance & theatre

The best-known Baining performance is the night dance commonly called the fire dance. Dancers run through, leap over, and kick the embers of a fire while wearing large masks. The masks represent various male-associated animals and plants of the forest, particularly in the *vungvung* form.

The fire dance is performed by men. Baining women and children traditionally act primarily as spectators, although women often perform secret nocturnal rituals and dances beforehand to “cool” the main ritual site.

## Festivals & rituals

The fire dance was traditionally connected with the birth of new children, the commencement of harvests, and remembrance of the dead. It is also a rite of passage for boys entering adulthood. Dancers are regarded as being possessed by anthropomorphised animal spirits called *masalai*. The dance lasts until daybreak, after which the orchestra members chase the masks out of the dance ground.

Women perform secret nocturnal rituals and dances to “cool” the principal ritual site before the main ceremony. In the late 20th century, tourism encouraged the revival of the fire dance. In recent times, it has become part of local and international cultural festivals.

The Baining have a history of localistic millenarian cults, notably Pomio Kivung, whose main leaders have been Mengen speakers. Several Baining villages formed “proudly local” secessionist movements that criticized Mengen cultural hegemony while continuing to follow the structure of the main Kivung movement.

## Foodways

The Baining are swidden, or slash-and-burn, cultivators. Taro was their main traditional crop, but the 1957 taro blight caused considerable disruption to agriculture and led to the replacement of most taro crops by singapu or kong-kong taro. Attempts to introduce bananas, beans, and other staple crops were generally resisted, although various other vegetables are grown alongside taro.

Garden plots are often separated from villages and contain shelters for temporary stays.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They describe a history of localistic millenarian cults, particularly Pomio Kivung, and mention several local secessionist movements connected with it. The sources also state that the fire dance involves anthropomorphised animal spirits called *masalai*, but provide no narrative corpus or literary account associated with them.

Anthropological studies have described Baining cultural life, while earlier studies by Gregory Bateson and Jeremy Pool were characterized as “failed ethnographic projects” because the researchers did not communicate effectively with the people they studied. Jane Fajans’s study notes that existing anthropological theoretical frameworks are poor tools for analysing Baining culture.

## Language & religion

The Baining languages form a distinct language family spoken by the Baining. The languages correspond to clan groups and include Kairak, Simbali, Mali, Qaqet, and Ura; Makolkol neighbored the other Baining languages to the southwest and is probably extinct. The family is usually classified as non-Austronesian and non-Oceanic. It is grouped geographically with other non-Austronesian languages in the East Papuan Languages, although East Papuan has not been established as a genetic unit.

Qaqet, also called Kakat, Makakat, or Maqaqet, is a non-Austronesian language of the Baining family. It is spoken in the Gazelle Peninsula in East New Britain Province. The sources describe villages with major Qaqet-speaking populations, including Raunsepna, Kamanakam, and Walmetki. Qaqet has a noun-class system, distinguishes singular, dual, and plural number, and generally uses subject–verb–object word order. They document Pomio Kivung, localistic millenarian cults, and the *masalai* spirits associated with the fire dance.
