## Overview

Ngāi Tahu, also called Kāi Tahu, is the principal Māori iwi of the South Island of New Zealand. Its takiwā is the largest in New Zealand, extending from the White Bluffs / *Te Parinui o Whiti*, Mount Māhanga and Kahurangi Point in the north to Stewart Island / *Rakiura* in the south. The territory comprises 18 rūnanga corresponding to traditional settlements. According to the 2023 census, an estimated 84,000 people affiliated with the Kāi Tahu iwi. Ngāi Tahu translates as “People of Tahu,” referring to the ancestor Tahupōtiki. Its traditional history connects the iwi with the Tākitimu waka, the Arahura River, the Waiau River near Manapōuri, and migrations from the North Island through Wellington into the South Island. The sources identify five primary hapū: Ngāti Kurī, Ngāti Irakehu, Kāti Huirapa, Ngāi Tūāhuriri, and Ngāi Te Ruahikihiki.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources mention a dogskin cloak belonging to the paramount chief Tamaiharanui. When a woman named Murihaka wore this cloak, the act caused an insult and helped spark the Kaihuānga feud. The history also describes a headdress as a sign of status: during the episode called *Pikitūroa* (“The Long Standing Feather Plumes”), Rokopaekawa took Marukore’s headdress so that Marukore would not be identified and sacrificed.

### Architecture

Ngāi Tahu history repeatedly refers to the *pā*, a fortified settlement, and the *whare*, a house or building. Named pā include Taumatahīnaki, Kaihinu, Takahanga, Kaiapoi, Te Kōhaka-a-kaikai-a-waro, Ōhiri, Te Mata-ki-kaipoinga, Waikakahi, Rīpapa, and Onawe. Te Kōhaka-a-kaikai-a-waro is identified with the Kaiapoi pā, established at the Taerutu Lagoon near Woodend. The sources describe buildings used in conflict, including the whare in which Hikaororoa trapped Marukore’s party and the whare associated with the incident known as *Kārara Kōpae* (“The Laying Down of Fighting Chiefs”).

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do mention the *Tākitimu waka* and other canoes, including Makawhiu, as well as carved wooden objects. At Pariwhakatau pā, Te Hineumutahi is described as a guardian represented by a wooden figure or carving suspended in the air. The history also records pounamu in Te Tai Poutini and the discovery of Nōti Raureka, identified with Browning Pass, in connection with Ngāti Wairangi territory.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They do mention Rākaitekura preparing her hair beside a stream later called Koukourārata.

## Music & performance

They do record oral performances and named episodes connected with warfare and history, including *Te Whataroa*, *Tāwiri-o-Te Makō*, and Oraumoanui or Tauanui. These names belong to historical narratives rather than documented musical traditions. The sources also mention children playing games during the events leading to Te Whataroa, when the hosts used the activity as a distraction while preparing an attack.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention children’s games during the events associated with Te Whataroa.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document ritual concepts and life-cycle-related practices involving tapu, status, marriage, death, and burial. Tamaiharanui’s spiritual status was so great that people of lower rank avoided looking directly at him; if his shadow fell on food, that food became tapu and had to be destroyed. Sacred customs also appear in the account of Tūrakautahi and Te-ake, whose companions were killed in Ngāti Wairangi territory after breaking such customs.

Marriage could establish or restore political relationships. Tūhaitara was married to Marukore, and later Tūteurutira and Hinerongo married after an alliance between Ngāti Kurī and Ngāti Māmoe. In the 18th century, marriages between Ngāi Tahu and Ngāti Māmoe helped cement peace, including the marriages of Raki-ihia and Hinehākiri, and of Honekai and Kohuwai.

Funeral and death practices appear in several accounts. Hinehou laid Tamaraeroa and Huirapa on the floor of her whare for their grandchildren to see before burning the building in the incident called *Kārara Kōpae*. In another telling, Hinehou and Pahirua built Kārara Kōpae and burned the bodies of the slain there. The sources also describe cannibalism in warfare and the destruction or abandonment of settlements during the Kaihuānga feud and the wars with Ngāti Toa.

## Foodways

They do, however, describe food in several historical episodes. At a feast organized by Chief Tūtekohi, fish and birds had been cleverly carved from wood to give the impression that food was being prepared in the storehouse. In another episode, Tūtekohi fed a prepared feast to his *kurī* named Kauerehuanui, an insult that contributed to conflict.

Food was also governed by tapu. Because Tamaiharanui’s shadow could make food tapu, such food had to be destroyed. The sources describe the cooking and disposal of human bodies in accounts of warfare, including the failure to cook Rokopaekawa properly in the episode called *Pikitūroa*. The plume of the headdress remained visible in the dirt, which was treated as a bad omen, and the body was discarded.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ngāi Tahu traditional history traces descent from Tahupōtiki, also known as Te Tuhi-māreikura-o-Oho-a-tama-wahine and Tahumatua, and connects him with Porourangi, Paikea, Chief Uenuku, and Ruatapu. Tahupōtiki was said to have been born in Whāngārā around 1450CE and to have commanded the Tākitimu waka. His descendants and migrations form the basis of extensive genealogical narratives.

The sources record named stories, battles, place-name explanations, and ancestral traditions. These include *Te Whataroa*, *Kārara Kōpae*, *Te Pakiaka* (“The Roots”), *Pikitūroa* (“The Long Standing Feather Plumes”), *Tāwiri-o-Te Makō*, and the stories associated with the Takitimu Mountains and Āraiteuru. Ngāi Tahu also learned the genealogies and traditions of Ngāti Wairangi, Ngāti Tūmatakōkiri, and Waitaha.

## Language & religion

They do describe spiritual concepts including tapu, mana, ariki status, tohunga, sacred customs, and guardian figures. Tūāhuriri is explained through *tūāhu*, “sacred altar,” and *riri*, “to be angry.” The history also refers to tohunga named Iriraki and Tautini, who cursed Moki after he insulted two women.
