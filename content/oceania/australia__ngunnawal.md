## Overview

The Ngunnawal, also spelt Ngunawal, are an Aboriginal people of southern New South Wales and the Australian Capital Territory in Australia. Their country has been described as extending from Goulburn to Yass and Boorowa, southwards towards Lake George, and westwards towards Goodradigbee. More recent research places their primary country around the Yass River, between Lake George and the Murrumbidgee, with a southern boundary north of Canberra. The Ngunnawal are associated with Ngunnawal, an Australian Aboriginal language of the Pama–Nyungan family. Their history includes changing interpretations of traditional ownership in the Canberra area, displacement following pastoral occupation in the 1820s, and ongoing work to revive their language.

## Festivals & rituals

The Ngunnawal article records an incident in 1826 in which many Aboriginal people at Lake George protested an incident involving a shepherd and an Aboriginal woman; the protesters then moved away peacefully.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record language-preservation work: the Ngunnawal community has been engaged in efforts to revive the language and bring it back into daily use within the community. This work includes collaboration with AIATSIS linguists and the identification of historical records that can be used in language revival.

## Language & religion

Ngunnawal and Gundungurra are described as Australian Aboriginal languages associated with the Ngunnawal and Gandangara peoples. They are very closely related and can be considered dialects of a single unnamed language in the technical linguistic sense, although Ngunnawal and Gundungurra people prefer to describe their varieties as separate languages. Ngunnawal is generally classified within the tentative Yuin–Kuric group of the Pama–Nyungan family. One classification groups it with Ngarigo among the Southern Tableland languages of New South Wales.

The traditional language area has been associated with country extending near Goulburn, north to Boorowa, south through Canberra and perhaps Queanbeyan, and west towards the Goodradigbee River. The language was extinct by 1994, although recordings of the last native speakers exist. The Ngunnawal community has worked towards language revival with AIATSIS linguists.

The sources also record disagreement about identity and traditional ownership in the Canberra area. Groups identified in the dispute include the Ngambri, the Ngarigo, and the Walgalu-speaking Ngambri-Guumaal. The 2012 report *Our Kin, Our Country* stated that the evidence was too scant to support any family’s claims to be original owners and described the language spoken in the Canberra region as a dialect of Ngarigu, related to but distinguishable from varieties spoken at Tumut and Monaro.
