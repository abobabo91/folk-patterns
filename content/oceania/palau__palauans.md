## Overview

The Palauans are an oceanic people whose material culture is documented primarily through British Museum holdings. Their objects reveal a maritime society that works with reef materials — shark teeth, swordfish jaw, turtle shell, and pearlshell — shaped into weapons, vessels, and ceremonial items. Taro feasts, carved narrative boards, and gendered dance traditions emerge as central to their cultural life.

## Material culture

Palauan material culture draws entirely from the reef, shore and forest — wood, turtle-shell, pearlshell, and plaited plant fibres, often combined with inlaid shell and red pigment.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Palauan textiles use plant fibres — banana, hibiscus, and pandanus — either woven on looms or plaited by hand.

- **Loom-woven bast cloth** — A loincloth woven from banana leaf fibre and hibiscus fibre on a loom.
- **Dyed hibiscus-fibre dance dress** — A woman's dance skirt of hibiscus fibre dyed yellow with turmeric and red, hung from a waistband.
- **Plaited leaf work** — Leaf strips plaited or woven into hat crowns, dance-brassieres, wrist-ornaments and baskets.

### Clothing & dress

Dress is almost entirely ceremonial and mostly for women, made from dyed and plaited plant fibres with no woven cloth.

- **Women's dance costume** — A complete ensemble of dyed hibiscus skirt, woven leaf-strip brassiere, vegetable-fibre belt, and plaited leaf wrist-ornaments.
- **Men's loincloth** — A loom-woven garment of banana and hibiscus fibre, shown in red on carved storyboards.
- **Plaited leaf hat** — A hat with a crown of plaited leaf strips, the only recorded head covering.

### Architecture

The *bai* is the recognisable traditional house, appearing on storyboards and lending its form to carved ceremonial objects.

- **Bai** — The traditional Palauan house, shown on storyboards and used as the shape for a carved ceremonial board.
- **Waterside platforms** — Platforms at the water's edge shown on storyboards alongside houses and trees.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Palauan makers work in wood, turtle-shell and shell inlay, with no metalwork recorded and only one ceramic piece.

- **Turtle-shell vessels** — Polished plates, dishes, bowls, spoons and fish-hooks carved from turtle-shell, some with lugs or pointed ends.
- **Shell-inlaid containers** — Wooden vessels and bowls inlaid with white clam shell and pearlshell, often coloured with red pigment.
- **Taro feast table** — A red six-legged wooden table inlaid with white clam shell and pearlshell, made to display taro at feasts.
- **Taro pounder** — A carved and polished hardwood tool for processing taro.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Personal ornament uses reef materials — carved turtle-shell, sea urchin spines, and shell beads — along with tattooing.

- **Turtle-shell bangle** — A carved and polished bracelet made from turtle-shell.
- **Sea urchin spine necklace** — Nine brown-and-white striped sea urchin spines pierced and strung on twisted fibre cords.
- **Money-pendant** — A single reddish-brown bead hung on cord, used as both currency and ornament.
- **Tattooing** — A practice attested by a tattooing-instrument on a wooden haft and a storyboard depicting the process.

## Music & performance

No instruments, song genres or musical occasions are named in the sources, though dance had purpose-made equipment.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is sharply gendered, with men using display weapons and women wearing elaborate plaited-fibre costumes.

- **Dance-spear** — A wooden spear with a flattened barbed head and shaft painted with black crosses and red spots.
- **Dance-sword** — A wooden sword tapering to a point, painted in black, yellow and red across both faces of the blade.
- **Dance-sticks** — Paired wooden sticks with leaf strips attached near one end.
- **Women's dance costume** — A complete ensemble of dyed skirt, woven brassiere, belt and wrist-ornaments of plaited plant fibres.

## Festivals & rituals

Feasting and ritual are documented through objects, with taro displays and ceremonial swords marking public and ritual occasions.

- **Taro feast** — A public celebration centred on displaying taro on an inlaid feast table.
- **Brotech** — A ceremonial sword of wood inlaid with white and cream-coloured shell, classified as religious or ritual equipment.

## Foodways

Taro is the named staple, celebrated in feast tables and pounder tools; fishing and turtling are evident from material culture.

- **Taro** — The staple crop processed with a wooden pounder, displayed on feast tables and shown in storyboard scenes.
- **Fishing and turtling** — Subsistence practices evident from turtle-shell vessels, fish-hooks and representations of boats with catch on storyboards.

## Oral tradition & literature

Narrative is carried on carved storyboards depicting origin stories, historical battles, currency and social practices.

- **Llochukl el chesbocheb** — A carved storyboard shaped as a *bai* that depicts the discovery of turtles' egg-laying and natural childbirth on its two faces, with the 'lady of lost virtue' at each end.
- **Battle storyboard** — A carved board depicting a historical fight between canoes, with a central warrior surrounded by red-hulled vessels of spear-wielders, shore buildings and trees.
- **Currency storyboard** — A carved board showing a boat with men and women in conversation before three large stone discs of currency and a background of buildings and trees.
- **Tattooing storyboard** — A carved board depicting male and female figures engaged in tattooing against trees and a *bai*.

## Language & religion

Three Palauan words are recorded — *bai*, *brotech* and *llochukl el chesbocheb* — but no information on language family or religion appears in the sources.

## Glossary

- *bai* — A traditional Palauan house.
- *brotech* — A ceremonial sword of wood inlaid with shell, used in ritual.
- *llochukl el chesbocheb* — A carved storyboard shaped as a house, depicting origin narratives.
