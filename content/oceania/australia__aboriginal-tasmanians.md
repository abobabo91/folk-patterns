## Overview

Aboriginal Tasmanians are the Aboriginal people of Tasmania, a large island south of mainland Australia. Aboriginal people lived there for tens of thousands of years before European settlement, and archaeological evidence indicates occupation from approximately 35,000 years ago. Rising sea levels formed Bass Strait and separated Tasmania from mainland Australia for approximately 8,000 years. Before British colonisation in 1803, communities maintained distinct languages, cultural traditions, and regional identities, with family and clan groups connected to particular territories and seasonal movement patterns. Estimates of the population before colonisation range from 3,000 to 15,000 people. Contemporary Palawa communities continue cultural revival projects, including the reconstruction and use of *palawa kani*, a composite Tasmanian Aboriginal language.

### Architecture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources describe bone tools and sharpened stone tools. Aboriginal Tasmanians appear to have changed from worked bone tools to more efficient sharpened stone tools approximately 4,000 years ago; bone tools are believed to have been used primarily for fishing. The sources also mention spears, stones, musket use during resistance, hunting dogs, and traded goods including flour, tea, tobacco, and kangaroo skins. No ceramics, metalwork, or further household-object traditions are documented.

## Music & performance

The best-known surviving spoken recordings of an original Tasmanian Aboriginal language are recordings of Fanny Cochrane Smith singing traditional songs in 1899 and 1903. Smith is often described as the last fluent speaker of a Tasmanian Aboriginal language.

The original Tasmanian languages survive mainly through fragmentary wordlists recorded by European visitors, explorers, and colonists between the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. More than twenty individuals documented words and phrases during the colonial period, including English, Scottish, Danish, and French recorders. Spoken recordings are extremely rare. Contemporary cultural revival includes the reconstruction and use of *palawa kani*, created from surviving historical wordlists and linguistic records.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do describe social and political organization through family and clan groups connected to particular territories and seasonal movement patterns. They also record that some Aboriginal women may have been given to incorporate newcomers into Aboriginal society through marriage. In the colonial period, Aboriginal women taken to the Bass Strait islands sometimes returned to their native tribe, taking gifts, while others were held by sealers. These accounts concern historical contact and exploitation rather than a documented ritual system.

## Foodways

Aboriginal Tasmanians were primarily nomadic people who moved through adjoining territories according to seasonal changes in food supplies. Their foods included seafood, land mammals, native vegetables, berries, shellfish, seals, possums, kangaroos, and wallabies. Fish were never a large part of the diet, ranking behind shellfish and seals.

Approximately 4,000 years ago, Aboriginal Tasmanians largely dropped scaled fish from their diet and began eating more land mammals. The significance of this change is debated. Some interpretations connect it with changing scrub and grassland, which provided increased food resources; another interpretation treats it as evidence of a maladaptive society. The sources state that the cost/benefit ratio of fishing may have become too high as other resources became available.

Aboriginal women were highly skilled in hunting seals and obtaining other foods such as seabirds. Aboriginal people traded kangaroo skins for goods including flour, tea, and tobacco.

## Oral tradition & literature

 Oral traditions have been studied alongside archaeological evidence in research into the environmental history of the Bassian Plain and the formation of Bass Strait.

Oral traditions also appear in debates about Aboriginal identity and descent. The sources record claims by the Huon and Channel Aboriginal people of descent from two Aboriginal women, although research found that both women were non-Aboriginal convict women. Contemporary cultural preservation includes the reconstruction and revival of *palawa kani* from historical wordlists and linguistic records.

## Language & religion

The archaeological, geographic, and linguistic record suggests successive waves of occupation and the coalescence of three language groups into a broad group. Colonial settlers identified the western Nara and eastern Mara language and ethnic groups. The North Midlands language was classified as *mairremenner* and was spoken by the Ben Lomond and North-east nations and by the Luggermairrenerpairer clan of the Central Highlands. The sources also name Pleistocene Palawa, Furneaux, Nara, and Mara language groups.

There are no living speakers of the original Tasmanian languages. Many languages disappeared before systematic documentation, and surviving records use inconsistent spellings and transcription methods. Contemporary *palawa kani* is a reconstructed language created from surviving historical wordlists and linguistic records.
