## Overview

The Tahitians are Indigenous Polynesian people of Tahiti and thirteen other Society Islands. Their material culture includes barkcloth, feathers, shellwork, fibre work, quilts, and wooden and stone tools. The sources describe major population loss after European contact and later anticolonial writing. They also mention Protestantism, traditional gods, healing, and French citizenship.

## Material culture

Tahitian material culture uses barkcloth, feathers, shells, pearlshell, wood, coconut, and plaited fibre in clothing, adornment, tools, and quilts.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Tahitian textiles include barkcloth, cotton quilts, fibre matting, and striped ponchos.

- **Barkcloth** — Barkcloth was made from paper mulberry bark and could be dyed and decorated with pressed leaf designs, fingerprints, and handprints.
- **Tifaifai quilt** (*tifaifai*) — A tifaifai is a cotton quilt, including examples with a blue fan design or an orange Tiare Tahiti flower.
- **Fibre matting** — Matting was used for garments, including a dress covered with fibre tufts.
- **Striped poncho** (*tiputa*) — A tiputa is a barkcloth poncho decorated with stripes.

### Clothing & dress

Tahitian clothing ranged from simple barkcloth and fibre garments to an elaborate mourner’s costume.

- **Mourner’s costume** — The Tahitian Mourner’s costume included a feather cloak, pearlshell mask, wooden breast-plate, barkcloth apron, fibre head-dress, and other pieces.
- **Barkcloth apron** — A barkcloth apron was one of the recorded Tahitian garments.
- **Fibre-matting cap** — A cap made from fibre matting is recorded among Tahitian clothing.
- **Feather cloak** — The mourner’s cloak was made of feathers bound to a fibre netted base.
- **Pearlshell mask** — The mourner’s mask combined pearlshell and tropic bird feathers.

### Architecture

The sources give little information about Tahitian architecture.

- **Cultivated volcanic land** — The original Tahitians cleared fertile volcanic soils for cultivation.
- **Fishing canoe** — The original Tahitians built fishing canoes, but the sources do not describe their construction.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources do not describe Tahitian pottery or metalwork, but they mention tools, canoes, and a whale-ivory handle.

- **Stone, bone, shell, and wood tools** — When first discovered, Tahitian tools were made of stone, bone, shell, or wood.
- **Fly-whisk handle** (*Tahiri*) — A Tahiri is an 18th-century handle for a fly whisk made of whale ivory and coconut fibre.
- **Fishing canoe** — Fishing canoes are mentioned, but their construction is not described.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Tahitian adornment used shells, coconut shells, flowers, and garlands.

- **Shell necklace** — A shell necklace used young coconut leaves, spiral shells, pearl shells, and a cowrie clasp.
- **Dance bra** — A dance bra was made from two drilled coconut shells joined with synthetic cord.
- **Flower hair adornment** (*tiare*) — A woman is recorded wearing flowers in her hair and a garland around her chest, while a white tiare behind the left ear signified readiness to take a lover in one painting.

## Music & performance

The sources name Tahitian poets and anticolonial writing but do not describe music or instruments.

- **Anticolonial poetry and writing** — Henri Hiro, Charles Manutahi, Vaitiare, and Turo Raapoto led anticolonial writing in Tahiti in the late 1960s and early 1970s.
- **God of Culture** — Henri Hiro’s God of Culture invokes Oihanu, the Tahitian god of culture and husbandry.

## Dance & theatre

The sources provide little information about Tahitian dance or theatre.

- **Coconut-shell dance bra** — A museum catalogue records a dance bra made from two coconut shells.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do not describe an annual festival calendar or named life-cycle ceremonies, but they record mourner’s clothing, fear, and healing.

- **Mourner’s costume** — The Tahitian Mourner’s costume used feather, barkcloth, fibre, wood, coconut-shell, pearlshell, and tropic-bird-feather elements.
- **Irrational fear** (*mehameha*) — Mehameha is a Tahitian concept of irrational fear.
- **Healers** (*taʼata rāʼau; taʼata rapaʼau*) — Tahitian healers were called taʼata rāʼau or taʼata rapaʼau.
- **Tiurai** — Tiurai was described as the most famous Tahitian healer and died at age 83 during the 1918 influenza outbreak on Tahiti.

## Foodways

The sources do not describe Tahitian foods, cooking, or cuisine.

- **Cultivation** — The original Tahitians cleared fertile volcanic soils for cultivation.
- **Fishing canoes** — The original Tahitians built fishing canoes, but the sources give no further account of fishing practices.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe modern anticolonial writing and questions of Tahitian identity rather than older oral genres.

- **Anticolonial writing** — Henri Hiro, Charles Manutahi, Vaitiare, and Turo Raapoto spearheaded anticolonial writing in Tahiti in the late 1960s and early 1970s.
- **God of Culture** — Henri Hiro’s God of Culture calls on Oihanu to empower the new generation.
- **Tahitian identification writing** — Michou Chaze, Chantal Spitz, and Vaitiare explored problems of Tahitian identification in contemporary French Polynesia.

## Language & religion

Tahitian identity is expressed through the names Māʼohi and Tahitiens, while Protestantism became the main religion in the 1820s.

- **Tahitian name for the people** (*Māʼohi*) — Māʼohi is the Tahitian name for the people.
- **French name for the people** (*Tahitiens*) — Tahitiens is the French term for the people.
- **Protestantism** — Protestantism became the main religion on Tahiti in the 1820s.
- **Oihanu** — Oihanu is described as the Tahitian god of culture and husbandry.
- **Herbal and European medicine** — Tahitian healers used herbal remedies, and in the 19th century Tahitians added European medicine.
