## Overview

The Wurundjeri are an Aboriginal people of the Woiwurrung language group within the Kulin nation. Together with the Boonwurrung people, they are the traditional owners of the Yarra River Valley, including much of the present location of Melbourne, known in their language as Naarm. Wurundjeri people continue to live in this area and throughout Australia. Early European colonists called them the Yarra tribe. Their Country included the Yarra and Saltwater rivers around Melbourne and extended toward Mount Disappointment, Macedon, Woodend, Lancefield, Mount Baw Baw and Healesville. The Wurundjeri population probably fluctuated around 400 individuals before colonisation. Their cultural record includes Woiwurrung language, seasonal classifications, initiation practices, kinship divisions, creation stories, ceremonial gatherings and material practices connected with hunting, exchange and clothing.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Possum rug** — A possum rug was given to a boy after his hair had grown back following initiation.

**Woven bag** — During initiation, the boy kept a plucked possum in a woven bag and did not leave the *jibbauk* stockade without it and a firestick.

**Baskets** — In 1838, Wurundjeri people sold baskets, possum skins and lyrebird feathers to settlers.

**Possum-skin cloak** — The sources identify the possum-skin cloak as a related cultural object.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe everyday and ceremonial clothing mainly through initiation. Before coming of age, the boy was naked; during the ceremony he was hooded with a rug and later received one or more kilts called *branjep*. His head was close-shaven except for a crested ridge of hair, and mud and pipeclay were applied to his head, shoulders, face and trunk. He carried a firestick and a plucked possum in a woven bag. After his hair had grown back two inches, he was given a possum rug and reintroduced to the camp. The sources also record the sale of possum skins and lyrebird feathers to settlers.

### Architecture

They refer to camps, an encampment called *wilam*, fenced initiation enclosures made from interwoven boughs, and Aboriginal reserves and stations at Nerre Nerre Warren, Merri Creek and Coranderrk. The initiation enclosure stood some two to three hundred yards from the main encampment and was screened by interwoven boughs. Bolin Bolin lagoon was an important habitation, ceremonial and food-resource site.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They describe firesticks, woven bags, baskets, possum skins, lyrebird feathers and stone axe production. The Wurundjeri-willam and Bulug-willam clans mined diorite at the Mount William stone axe quarry, producing valued greenstone hatchet heads that were traded across a wide area as far as New South Wales and Adelaide. The quarry covered 18 hectares, included underground pits of several metres and had been used for more than 1,500 years.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They do describe initiation body treatments: the initiate’s head and shoulders were covered with mud, while his face was smeared ear to ear with pipeclay and his trunk was marked from the kilted waist to the back of the head. His head was close-shaven, leaving a crested ridge of hair.

## Music & performance

The sources mention ceremonial rites, dance and song. Melbourne was a gathering place for men, women and children from Kulin groups for ceremonial rites, trading, marriage-making and dispute settlements. Following initiation, a boy’s manhood was celebrated for several days in dance and song after he had been reintroduced to the camp.

Wurundjeri elders attend events with visitors and give a traditional welcome to country in the Woiwurrung language:

*Wominjeka yearmenn koondee-bik Wurundjeri-Ballak*

The source translates this as “Welcome to the land of the Wurundjeri people.”

The Wurundjeri cosmological narratives also involve spoken names and performances of ancestral events. Bunjil, the creator spirit and celestial headman, is associated with the creation of the landscape, the making of people from clay and the ordering of social relations.

## Dance & theatre

They record that initiation concluded with several days of dance and song celebrating the initiate’s manhood. Ceremonial gatherings among Kulin groups included rites, trading, marriage-making and dispute settlements.

## Festivals & rituals

The Wurundjeri shared a Woiwurrung division of the year into seven seasons:

- *Biderap*: the dry season from January to February.
- *Iuk*: eel season in March.
- *Waring*: wombat season from April to July.
- *Guling*: the season of orchids in August.
- *Poorneet*: tadpole season from September to October.
- *Buarth Gurru*: the season of grass flowering in November.
- *Garrawang*: kangaroo apple season in December.

A youth’s coming-of-age ceremony was called *Jiba-gop*, also pronounced *Jibauk*, a term that could also refer to the initiate. A 10–11-year-old boy was taken to an enclosure by his *guritch*, his sister’s husband, or his *kangun*, his maternal uncle. He was hooded, painted, given a kilt and kept apart from the main encampment. He was taught which foods were forbidden, including emu, black duck, musk duck, flying tuan, iguana and echidna. As his hair grew back, he received a possum rug and returned to the camp. His manhood was then celebrated in dance and song. A *wirrarap* supervised him, and he gradually gained permission to eat the forbidden foods, with the right to eat emu concluding at around 30–35 years of age.

## Foodways

The sources describe food procurement, seasonal resources and dietary restrictions. Firestick farming created extensive grassy plains where yam daisies, called *murnong*, and various tuber lilies grew. These roots formed a major source of starch and carbohydrates. Camps were located according to seasonal changes, food availability and other conditions, often along the Yarra River, called *Birrarung*, and its tributaries.

Bolin Bolin lagoon was an important site where eels, fish and possum were procured. The seasonal cycle included *Iuk*, the eel season, and *Waring*, the wombat season. The season of kangaroo apple was called *Garrawang*. During initiation, boys were taught that emu, black duck, musk duck, flying tuan, iguana and echidna were forbidden foods. They were gradually permitted to eat these foods under supervision, with permission to eat emu granted at around 30–35 years of age.

## Oral tradition & literature

Wurundjeri dreamtime legends connect the physical landscape with the actions of Bunjil. The sky realm was known as *Tharangalk-bek*, described as “gumtree/manna gum country,” and Bunjil, usually called *mami-ngata*, “Our Father,” was said to dwell there. Bunjil created the landscape of earth, *beek-narreen*, fashioned men from clay and was associated with the eagle-hawk and the star Altair. His brother, the bat Pallina, secured wives for them by fishing them out of the water.

Bunjil’s wives included Ganaivarra, a black swan, and his son Binbeal was the rainbow. Bunjil established the exogamous kinship system and eventually ascended to the sky after Bellin-bellin, the Musk-crow, released a whirlwind from his skinbag. Another story explains the creation of the Pleiades through the women called Karat-goruk, their yam-sticks and the crow Waang.

A landscape story concerned *bukkertillibe*, the “bottomless pit,” a 120-foot-deep hollow near Brushy Creek, also called Narneian, which was said to have been made when Bunjil caused a star to fall. The site was later identified with a quarry at Cave Hill in Lilydale and is now destroyed.

## Language & religion

Wurundjeri people spoke a variety of Woiwurrung, an eastern Kulin dialect. The sources record Woiwurrung terms including *biik* for Country, *wilam* for camping ground or dwelling, *gurrborra* for koala, *wirrarap* for medicine man, *mainmeet* for outsiders or foreigners, *dhanguth* or *dhangidj* for food, *burndap* for good and *nyilam* for bad.

Wurundjeri cosmology includes a skyworld inhabited by creator spirits and the souls of the dead. This realm, *Tharangalk-bek*, was connected with Bunjil, who shaped the land and established social relations. The dead were called *murup*, and the other land was accessible through bridging pathways associated with the setting sun’s rays, called *karalk*. Bunjil was also identified with Altair and imagined as the celestial *ngurungaeta*, or headman, of the tribe.

They state that Wurundjeri culture and language were undermined when people were forbidden to “talk culture” and language, and that contemporary preservation work includes collecting knowledge from documents and maintaining a cultural “keeping place.”
