## Overview

The only sources available for this profile are the catalogue records of the Palauan objects shown here, all of them British Museum holdings; no Wikipedia article and no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription was supplied. The records therefore say nothing about where Palauans live, how many there are, or what language they speak. What they do document is a maritime material culture built from what the reef, the shore and the forest supply: swords edged with shark teeth, weapons made from the upper jaw of a swordfish, turtle-shell plates and bowls, pearlshell and clam-shell inlay set into red-pigmented wood, war canoes with matching paddles, and taro as the food of feasts. Three vernacular words survive in the catalogue text — *bai* for a house, *brotech* for a ceremonial sword, and *llochukl el chesbocheb* for a carved storyboard — and the storyboards themselves carry the narrative culture into the object collection.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources name no textile tradition by a vernacular term, and record no dyed, resist-patterned or figured cloth. They document two fibre technologies:

**Loom-woven bast cloth** — a loin-cloth garment woven on a loom from banana leaf fibre and, the record suggests, hibiscus fibre.

**Dyed hibiscus-fibre dance dress** — a woman's dance-skirt of hibiscus fibre, its two groupings of fibre dyed yellow with turmeric and red, suspended from a waist-band.

**Plaited leaf work** — leaf strips, plaited or woven, make a hat crown, a dance-brassiere with plaited leaf straps, a pair of women's dance wrist-ornaments, and an oval basket in which the plait forming the centre of the base runs up the sides and over the rim. A dance-belt is of vegetable fibre, queried in the record as pandanus.

**Motif vocabulary.** Turtle (in inlaid shell on a container lid); human heads and birds (as alternating decorative schemes on the hulls of canoes shown on a storyboard); a bird, as the whole form of a lidded bowl; a Janus-type double head terminating a dagger handle, its eyes and mouth inlaid with shell; and painted black crosses and red spots on a dance-spear shaft.

### Clothing & dress

Dress in these records is almost entirely ceremonial and almost entirely women's. A single matched dance costume, catalogued as numbers 21 to 30, comprises a hibiscus-fibre skirt dyed yellow and red on a waist-band, a brassiere of woven leaf strips with plaited leaf straps, a belt of vegetable fibre, and a pair of wrist-ornaments of leaf strips — a full ensemble of plaited and dyed plant material with no cloth in it. For men the only garment recorded is a loom-woven loin-cloth of banana and hibiscus fibre. A hat with a crown of plaited leaf strips is the one head covering. The carved storyboards corroborate this division and add colour: male figures appear in red loincloths, female figures in red skirts with black waistbands. No footwear is recorded, and the sources describe no everyday dress as distinct from dance dress.

### Architecture

The catalogue names one building type, the *bai*, and treats it as the recognisable house of Palau: storyboards show trees and houses identified as *bai*, and platforms at the water's edge. One carved board, *llochukl el chesbocheb*, is not merely a picture of a *bai* but is itself shaped as a stylised traditional house, with a female figure described as the 'lady of lost virtue' at either end — so the building form governs the shape of the carving that decorates it. Beyond this the records are silent: they give no construction materials, no roof type, no plan, no information on settlement layout, and nothing about who built or occupied a *bai*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

One ceramic object appears: a lamp of earthenware combined with cloth and coconut fibre cord. No metalwork of any kind is recorded, and the only modern industrial material noted is commercial paint on a dance-sword. Everything else is wood, turtle-shell, shell and cord. Turtle-shell is worked into a run of shallow vessels — polished plates, circular and pointed dishes, one with a lug on the underside, an oval vessel, a spoon and a fish-hook. Wooden objects are frequently inlaid with shell: a lidded container with a fibre cord suspension loop and a turtle on its lid, a bird-shaped bowl and lid coloured with red pigment. A rectangular six-legged table, red and inlaid with white clam shell and pearlshell in its sides and legs, was used to display taro at feasts. Tools include a taro pounder of carved and polished wood, possibly mahogany, an incomplete pump drill, and a tattooing-instrument on a wooden haft.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Personal ornament in the records draws on the same reef materials as the vessels. A bangle is of carved and polished turtle shell. A necklace strings nine brown-and-white striped sea urchin spines, each pierced near one end, from twisted fibre cords. A money-pendant — catalogued as currency as well as ornament — hangs a single reddish-brown bead, queried as synthetic, on a cord; a separate storyboard shows three large stone discs identified as currency. The leaf-strip wrist-ornaments belong to the women's dance costume rather than to daily wear. Tattooing is attested twice over: by a tattooing-instrument mounted on a wooden haft, and by a storyboard whose subject is male and female figures engaged in tattooing, set against trees and a *bai*. The records do not describe tattoo designs, placement or occasion.

## Music & performance

The sources name no instrument, no song genre and no musical occasion. What they establish is that dance was equipped with its own purpose-made implements — see below — but the music that accompanied it is not documented in the material used here.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is the best-attested performance context in the collection, and it is sharply gendered. Men's dance equipment consists of weapons and staffs made for display rather than use: a **dance-spear** whose head ends in a flattened point and carries three pairs of barbs outlined in black paint and marked with red spots, its shaft painted with black crosses and red spots identically on both sides; a **dance-sword** of wood tapering to a point, painted in black, yellow and red commercial paint across both faces of the blade and the adjacent part of its short handle; and paired **dance-sticks** of wood, each with two leaf strips attached near one end. Women's dance is represented by the costume set described above. A war canoe is inlaid in pearlshell with the figure of a man holding, in each hand, an object the cataloguer reads as a dance-club or a paddle — suggesting the two were visually close enough to be confused. No dance is named, and the sources record no theatrical or masked tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The records document ritual and feasting through objects rather than through a calendar. The clearest is the six-legged red table inlaid with clam shell and pearlshell, made specifically for displaying taro at feasts, which puts a named crop at the centre of public celebration. The ceremonial sword *brotech*, of wood inlaid with white and cream-coloured shell and classified in the catalogue as religious or ritual equipment, is the one object explicitly assigned a ceremonial function. The storyboard *llochukl el chesbocheb* carries on its two faces the discovery of the turtles' egg-laying cycle and the discovery of natural childbirth — origin narratives touching reproduction in both the human and the animal world — and sets a figure called the 'lady of lost virtue' at each end of the house-shaped board. Beyond these, the sources describe no annual festival cycle and no birth, initiation, marriage or funeral rite.

## Foodways

Taro is the only foodstuff the records name, and they name it three times over: a pounder of carved and polished hardwood made for it, the feast table built to display it, and a storyboard on which four female figures in red skirts hold red bowls of taro while a man approaches carrying a fish. The same board places five fish and one turtle in the water beneath the canoes, and turtle-shell supplies the material for plates, dishes, a spoon and a fish-hook — so fishing and turtling are legible in the objects even where the catalogue does not discuss diet. No cooking method, prepared dish, beverage or dietary rule appears in the sources.

## Oral tradition & literature

The carved storyboard is the form in which Palauan narrative enters this collection, and the records treat each board as the telling of a specific story. *Llochukl el chesbocheb* renders two discoveries — the turtles' egg-laying cycle and natural childbirth — one on each face of a board shaped as a *bai*, with the 'lady of lost virtue' at either end. A battle board depicts a fight between canoes on the water, understood as a historical event rather than a fable: a central vessel with a warrior in the foreground, surrounded by red-hulled canoes of spear-wielding warriors, spears shown in flight with a central pair crossing, the hulls on the left and centre patterned with human heads and those on the right with birds, and trees, *bai* and platforms along the shore. A third board, catalogued as depicting "yap money", shows a boat holding two men, four women in conversation, two crouching men, two men facing away, and three large stone discs of currency before a background of trees and a *bai*. A fourth narrates tattooing. The sources name no epic, no proverb or riddle tradition, and no storyteller or performance setting.

## Language & religion

The sources yield three Palauan words — *bai*, *brotech* and *llochukl el chesbocheb* — but say nothing about the language they belong to, its family, its dialects or its writing. They likewise record no religion, deity, sect or spiritual practice; the single object called ritual equipment, the *brotech* sword, is given no ceremonial context in its record.
