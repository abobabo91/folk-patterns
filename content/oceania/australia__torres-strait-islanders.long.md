## Overview

Torres Strait Islanders are the Indigenous Melanesian peoples of the Torres Strait Islands, which form part of the Australian state of Queensland and lie between Cape York and Papua New Guinea. Of the 133 islands, 38 are inhabited; the 2021 census counted 4,124 people living on them, while 39,538 people across Australia identified as being of Torres Strait Islander descent, most of them now on the mainland, including five communities at Bamaga, Seisia, Injinoo, Umagico and New Mapoon. Two Indigenous languages are spoken — *Kalaw Lagaw Ya*, a Pama-Nyungan language of the western and central islands, and *Meriam Mir*, an Eastern Trans-Fly language of the east — alongside the creole *Yumplatok*. Archaeological, linguistic and folk-history evidence places the core of Island culture as Papuo-Austronesian, and the people are traditionally seafarers. They are the only culture in the world to make turtleshell masks.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The surviving museum record for this people is dominated by plaited and twined fibre rather than loom cloth, and by engraved shell, turtleshell and wood rather than printed pattern.

**Plaited fibre girdles and aprons** — flat plaited bands of dark brown fibre, folded and finished with a plaited cord, worn with front-and-back aprons of brown fibre; the girdle is the structural element to which the fibre garment attaches.

**Grass skirts and aprons** — skirts with tassels and double aprons made of grass, worn in both daily and dance contexts and visible in nineteenth-century photographs of dancers from Mer and Nagi.

**Patterned shoulder straps** — grass straps worked with a black pattern and occasional zigzags, with a short fringe on one edge wherever a zigzag falls, so the ornament and the fringe are generated by the same weave.

**Plaited mats** — large roughly rectangular fibre mats in diagonal weave, with a red stripe interwoven at intervals.

**Plaited armour for archery** — an archer's bracer made as a plaited tube of five strips of palm leaf, and a hollow cylindrical rattan gauntlet; bamboo and rattan wrist-guards belong to the same set.

**String figures (*wame*, also *wameya*)** — many different figures made in looped cord, prominent among the artforms and performed rather than kept.

**Motif vocabulary.** Engraved and painted designs name a small, marine repertoire: *gapu*, the sucker fish, engraved on the central disc of a dance ornament; the fish, with fins, gills, mouth and eye incised, on the pendant *sabagorar*; the tree frog outlined on the pearl-shell pendant *mai goai*; the crocodile, carved at the open mouth of a drum with two men in feather headdresses; the dugong, the turtle, the ray fish; the human face in profile or relief; fishtails flanking a carved board; and zigzag lines on painted boards. Turtles, fish, dugongs, sharks, seabirds and saltwater crocodiles are totemic beings, and totems attach to specific figures — the crocodile to Sagai, the trevally *gai gai* to Kulka, the tiger shark *baidam* to Siu.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress in the earliest photographs is the cloth wrap, worn by men on the beaches of Mabuiag and Mer, with women photographed at Mabuiag in dresses. Beneath and before that lies the fibre and grass wardrobe: a plaited fibre girdle carrying front and back aprons, grass skirts, double grass aprons, and a shell penis sheath of *cymbium* ornamented with dots and lines, hung from a plaited leaf band around the waist. Ceremonial dress is layered and additive. Photographs from Mer show dancers wearing leg ornaments, plant-fibre skirts, neck, arm and nose ornaments, and headdresses — a *Samu* headdress in the centre of one group, *Dhoeri* headdresses at either end. Head coverings include the ceremony and war headdress *samera*, cassowary feathers bound into a woven fibre band whitened and tied with plaited sinnet; a male coronet of kangaroo teeth bound into fine woven fibre; and a wig a foot high, built of whitish and brown fibrous tufts on a frame of coconut sinnet. Shoulder belts of *coix* seeds, knee ornaments of vegetable fibre and seed arm-bands complete the dance costume. At initiation ceremonies on Mer, men wore paired cloth wraps with a large cassowary tail feather tucked in at the back.

### Architecture

The sources describe the built environment only through photographs and through a class of object catalogued as house-boards. Thatched-roof structures appear repeatedly — three men seated on rocks before one at an unnamed island, three thatched buildings behind men holding spears and spear-throwers at Mabuiag, a thatched building behind Nomoa, chief of Mabuiag, with two dead dugongs at his feet — and long bamboo fences enclose the ground where an initiation ceremony is being watched. The house-boards themselves are wooden panels engraved and painted in red, white and black: one carries human and other figures, one is cut with zigzag lines, one bears animal designs identified as lizards. They establish that domestic or ceremonial structures carried engraved and polychrome ornament in the same three-colour scheme as masks, dance ornaments and canoe fittings, but the sources used do not describe house form, plan or construction.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The catalogued household kit is entirely of shell, wood, bamboo, coconut, turtleshell and stone; the sources record no pottery and no metalworking. Water is carried in coconut bottles, each closed with a leaf stopper and originally joined in pairs by vegetable-fibre cord, one blackened and slung on plaited grass cord; connected pairs of coconut vessels serve the same purpose. Fire is made with a pair of sticks, one of wood, one of wood and gum. Turtleshell is worked into engraved fish-hooks with a perforated shank and into needles with light incised ornament at the head. Other everyday forms include a bamboo comb, a bamboo tobacco pipe, and a basket of palm leaf and cotton cloth. Marine hunting equipment is the most elaborate: a stone axe-head, a bamboo bow with reed arrows of wood, string and bone, a club with a circular nephrite disc lashed to a long wooden shaft with rattan, and *amu*, harpooning rope plaited from vine stems and attached to the harpoon head when hunting dugong. *Singi*, a cane loop bound with sinnet to a double head of carved wood, was used to carry heads taken in warfare.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ornament is cut from pearl shell and turtleshell and strung from shell and seed. *Sabagorar* is a flat turtleshell pendant in the shape of a fish, its fins, gills, mouth and eye incised and its nose drilled for suspension; *mai goai* is cut from thin pearl shell in the form of a tree frog, with incised lines around the edge. Ear ornaments are polished pearl shell in the shape of a bird, drilled with a single hole. A head-band or fillet is built from perforated pearl-shell discs carrying a double string. Necklaces are strung from *oliva* shells; nose ornaments are thin shell tapering to a point at both ends. Arm-bands combine vegetable fibre, seeds, cloth and cord, and knee ornaments are of plain vegetable fibre. The most conspicuous ornament is *dhari* (also *dari*, *dhoeri*), the elaborate headdress that appears on the Torres Strait Islander flag: one example is a triangular canvas plate whitened with red and black edges, framed in openwork rattan, with cut white feathers radiating out and a single black feather at the centre.

## Music & performance

Singing and dancing are, in Ephraim Bani's formulation of 1979, the literature of Torres Strait Islander people and the most important aspect of Island life — the medium through which oral history is preserved and presented. Song subjects are specific: the weather, the myths and legends, life in the sea and the totemic gods, and important events. The instruments in the collections are struck, shaken and plucked. The drum is a carved wooden vessel, its open mouth cut with a crocodile and two men in feather headdresses, one side dressed with cassowary feathers and goa nuts tied through drilled holes. Rattles are made of wood, seeds, shell, cloth and fibre, or built as a spiral band of engraved turtleshell with a wooden head and plumes of cassowary feathers. *Lolo* is a bamboo cracking whip: a flexible bamboo strip with a length of thin sinnet cord and a fine coconut root tied at the end, its handle finely incised, used to make a cracking noise. A bamboo Jew's harp has its tongue cut longitudinally, the inner face painted with vermilion and a lozenge carved at one end. A bean pod holding five beans serves as a castanet in night dances, and clappers accompany dance. Christine Anu, an ARIA Award-winning singer-songwriter of Torres Strait Islander heritage, became widely known for her version of "My Island Home".

## Dance & theatre

Dance is a form of both creative and competitive expression, and the dancer is the storyteller: the dances act as illustrative material for the songs, and each island group has its own performances, whose artefacts carry Island tradition and clan identity. Performance objects include "dance machines" — hand-held mechanical moving objects — clappers, headdresses, and *getau-za*, a dance ornament held in the hand, built from a central wooden disc engraved with the sucker fish *gapu*, with bamboo spokes radiating to a cane ring, feathers tied along the outer edge and painting in red, black and white. A model dugong harpoon of wood, feathers and cloth was made for dancing rather than hunting. Masks are worn in dance: watercolour portraits made in the 1890s show Genai, a 26-year-old Kulkalgal man from Nagi, in a fibre skirt with a fish-shaped mask, holding a spear and club; Tetera, also of Nagi, in a bark-cloth mask with bow and arrows; and a man from Mori in a human-face mask. Initiation dancing is documented photographically on Mer. Ken Thaiday Snr is renowned for elaborately sculptured *dari*, often with moving parts, incorporating the hammerhead shark as a powerful totem.

## Festivals & rituals

The central annual observance is the **Coming of the Light**, also called *Zulai Wan* or *Bi Akarida*, celebrated by all Island communities on 1 July. It commemorates the arrival of the London Missionary Society mission of Rev. Samuel Macfarlane and Rev. Archibald Wright Murray on Erub (Darnley Island) on 1 July 1871, aboard the chartered schooner *Surprise*, with eight teachers and their wives from the Loyalty Islands; the clan elder and warrior Dabad walked to the water's edge ready to defend his people, and accepted the Bible offered to him, an act interpreted as the receiving of the Light. Traditional foods retain a ceremonial role even among Islanders living away from the islands. Ritual protection belongs to charm objects: a taboo charm of naturally S-shaped wood carved as a sea snake and painted red was placed in plantations to ward off thieves; *sukuba madub*, a carved wooden human face in profile cut from ship's weatherboard, served as a canoe ornament and protected the tobacco crop; *dogai*, a canoe figure-head of curved wood with pearl-shell eyes, a red-and-white painted face and projecting cassowary feathers, was fixed to the prow to bring good luck; and a wooden ray-fish model served as a house luck charm. Among life-cycle practices, *kupai omasker* allows a child to be adopted by a relative or community member — for instance where a family needs an heir to carry on the role of looking after land — and was given legal recognition in Queensland by the Meriba Omasker Kaziw Kazipa Act 2020. Sporting events, notably the annual 'Island of Origin' rugby league tournament between island teams, now bring people together across the Strait.

## Foodways

Islanders have long been agriculturalists as well as hunters and gatherers; tobacco plantations on Aureed Island evidence the cultivation side, and a bamboo tobacco pipe survives in the collections. Dugong, turtle, crayfish, crabs, shellfish, reef fish and wild fruits and vegetables were traditionally hunted and collected and remain an important part of the subsistence diet. Dugong and turtle hunting and fishing are understood as ways of continuing the Islander tradition of close association with the sea, and the technology is correspondingly specialised: harpoons rigged with the plaited vine rope *amu*, engraved turtleshell fish-hooks, and the spear called *wap*, carried by Nomoa, chief of Mabuiag, in a photograph taken with two dead dugongs at his feet. Water is stored and carried in stoppered coconut bottles, singly or in cord-joined pairs. One named fruit appears in the sources: *sorbi*, the red fruit held in the right hand of Tagai. The marine economy later drew Asian and Pacific Island traders to the beche-de-mer, mother-of-pearl and trochus-shell industries, making the islands multicultural. The sources used do not name prepared dishes or describe cooking methods.

## Oral tradition & literature

Historically the tradition is oral, with stories handed down and communicated through song, dance and ceremonial performance, and sea, sky and land figure strongly in them. *Tagai* is the spiritual system and the framing narrative: in one story Tagai stands in a canoe holding a fishing spear in his left hand — the Southern Cross — and the red fruit *sorbi* in his right. His crew of twelve consumed all the food and drink meant for a journey, so Tagai strung them together in two groups of six and cast them into the sea, where they became the star patterns seen in the Pleiades and Orion. Another cycle tells of four brothers (*bala*) — Malo, Sagai, Kulka and Siu — who paddled from Cape York, called *Kay Daol Dai*, "big land", and each established a following: Sagai at Iama, Kulka at Aureed as god of hunting, Siu at Masig as god of dancing, and the eldest, Malo, at Mer, where he set out rules for living combining religion and law, presented by Eddie Mabo in the native title case of 1992. In 1898–1899 Alfred Cort Haddon collected about 2,000 objects and filmed ceremonial dances, believing the material had to be saved from destruction by missionaries; the Haddon Collection at Cambridge University is the most comprehensive in the world. Margaret Lawrie, working for the Queensland State Library, recorded stories across the islands and published *Myths and Legends of the Torres Strait* (1970) and *Tales from the Torres Strait* (1972), reviving narratives that had fallen out of use and shaping the work of a generation of artists.

## Language & religion

*Kalaw Lagaw Ya*, the Western-Central Torres Strait Language, is spoken on the southwestern, western, northern and central islands, with *Kala Kawa Ya* distinguished as a further Top Western dialect; it belongs to the Pama-Nyungan family. *Meriam Mir*, spoken on the eastern islands, is one of four Eastern Trans-Fly languages, the other three spoken in Papua New Guinea. Torres Strait Creole, an English-based creole also called *Yumplatok*, serves as the language of trade and commerce. Christianity spread from the 1870s and remains strong: in the 2016 census 20,658 of 32,345 Torres Strait Islander people reported a Christian affiliation. Its arrival did not end earlier belief — Islanders continued to uphold their connection to land, sea and sky and to follow the instructions of Tagai, and the new religion was integrated into everyday life. After the Coming of the Light, objects central to earlier ceremony lost their role, were replaced by crucifixes, and in some cases were prohibited outright, so their production ceased.
