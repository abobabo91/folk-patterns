## Overview

The Tariana or Taliaseri are an Indigenous people of the Vaupés or Uaupés River in the Amazon region of Brazil and Colombia. The largest numbers live along the middle and upper reaches of the Uaupés River, with the largest concentration in and around Iauaretê. Other Tariana communities are located along the Vaupés River and the lower Papurí River in Colombia, and Tariana also live in the Brazilian Indigenous territories of Alto Rio Negro, Médio Rio Negro I, Médio Rio Negro II, Balaio and Cué-Cué/Marabitanas. In 2010, DAI/AMTB reported 1,914 Tariana in Brazil and 205 in Colombia; in 2014, Siasi/Sesai reported 2,684 Tariana in Amazonas. The Tariana language belongs to the Arawakan linguistic family but is endangered, with approximately 100 speakers in Brazil. Their folk-cultural significance in the sources lies especially in patrilineal social organization, linguistic exogamy, mythology, initiation rites, ceremonial objects and inherited ritual status.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do state that the Tariana have specialists in dances and ornaments as part of an inherited social hierarchy. The sources also mention ceremonial objects that missionaries demanded the Indians surrender as a condition of receiving the sacrament and participating in trade.

### Architecture

Tariana communities are described in relation to villages and riverside settlements, including Iauaretê and its old neighborhoods of São Miguel, Don Bosco, Santa Maria and São Pedro. Other named communities include Campo Alto, Itaiaçu, Miriti, Japurá, Aracapá, Sabiá, Santa Rosa, Periquito, Ipanoré, Urubuquara, Piu-Pinu and Nova Esperança. The sources mention traditional malocas, which missionaries tried to replace with individual houses grouped around a chapel. At the Salesian mission, construction included housing for boys and girls, a church, hospital, lodgings, a sawmill and a pottery.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Tariana ceramic fragments found in Jurupari date back 600 years. At the Salesian mission, a pottery was constructed using local labor. The sources also mention tools offered by Capuchin missionaries to induce Indians to move to riverside settlements, and refer to ceremonial objects without identifying their forms or materials. No Tariana metalwork tradition or named household object is documented.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources state that inherited social status included specialists in ornaments. They also mention a mask of Jurupari, which women were not allowed to see because it was used in male initiation rites.

## Music & performance

They do state that inherited social status included specialists in dances and ornaments, and that Jurupari masks were associated with male initiation rites. The sources also describe the importance of ceremonial objects.

## Dance & theatre

The Tariana social hierarchy includes inherited specialists in dances and ornaments. A mask of Jurupari was used in male initiation rites; three Franciscan missionaries were expelled by the Tariana in 1883 after displaying one on a church pulpit, because women were not allowed to see it.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe male initiation rites connected with the mask of Jurupari, which women were not allowed to see. The Tariana are traditionally patrilineal, exogamous and patrilocal: lineage follows the father, marriage is with women from different ethnic groups, and the wife moves to the husband’s community. Tariana women may marry men of the Tucano or Piratapuyo groups. Tariana tradition says that the people originated around the Uapuí waterfall on the upper Aiari River, a tributary of the Içana River, and their mythology describes places where they halted and where hierarchy was established among the ancestors of different sibs of the exogamic Tariana ethnicity.

Missionaries attempted to replace traditional practices, demanded the surrender of ceremonial objects, and encouraged movement from traditional malocas into individual houses grouped around chapels. Starting in the 19th century, missionaries tried to persuade the Tariana to abandon traditional beliefs and practices.

## Foodways

They state that boarding schools in the Salesian mission depended partly on contributions by parents of about 25 kilograms of flour per pupil per year. The sources also mention plantations and ranches.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tariana tradition says that the people originated around the Uapuí waterfall on the upper Aiari River, from which the upper Uaupés can be reached by land. Their mythology describes several places where the ancestors halted and explains the establishment of hierarchy among the ancestors of different sibs of the exogamic Tariana ethnicity. The Tariana understand themselves as *bipó diroá masí*, “children of the blood of the thunder,” a view accepted by the ethnic groups of the Uaupés River.

Research on the Tariana language includes a grammar book and a Tariana-Portuguese dictionary by Alexandra Aikhenvald of La Trobe University. “Telling the Truth in Tariana” is identified as an ABC radio documentary transcript. These materials document the language and its use but are not described in the sources as a distinct Tariana literary tradition.

## Language & religion

Tariana is an endangered Maipurean, also known as Arawak, language spoken along the Vaupés River in Amazonas, Brazil. The Tariana language is closely related to Baniwa and belongs to the Arawakan linguistic family. Approximately 100 people speak it, while approximately 1,500 people in the upper and middle Vaupés River area identify as ethnic Tariana but do not speak the language fluently. Almost all Tariana can speak Tucano, the lingua franca of the Uaupés. In 1996, there were no Tariana speakers in Colombia and just 100 in Brazil.

The Indigenous peoples of the Vaupés region are linguistically exogamous: languages and tribal identities are passed down through patrilineal descent, and people traditionally spoke between three and ten other languages, including their mother’s and father’s tongues, Spanish and Portuguese. Tariana speakers have been switching to the unrelated Tucano language. Salesian missionaries promoted exclusive use of Tucano from the 1920s in an effort to convert the Indians. Efforts were made to teach Tariana as a second language at the secondary school in Iauaretê in 1999, and regular classes have been offered there since 2003.

Starting in the 19th century, missionaries tried to convert the Tariana and abandon their traditional beliefs and practices.
