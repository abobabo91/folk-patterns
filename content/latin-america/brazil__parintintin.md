## Overview

The Parintintin are an Indigenous people of Brazil who live in the Madeira River basin. They refer to themselves as *Cabahyba*, *Kagwahiva’nga*, or *Kagwahiva*, translating as “our people.” As of 2010, their population was around 418, living in three villages on two Indigenous territories: TI Ipixuna and TI Nove de Janeiro. Their language is a dialect of Tenharim within the Tupi-Guarani language family and is written in the Latin script. Parintintin life includes agriculture, fishing, and gathering. Their social organization is based on two exogamous, patrilineal moieties named for different types of birds. Their history also reflects the effects of Brazilian contact, disease, colonization, rubber extraction, and highway construction.

## Foodways

The sources identify the Parintintin as agriculturalists, fishermen, and gatherers.

## Language & religion

Parintintin is a dialect of the Tenharim language, which belongs to the Tupi-Guarani language family, and it is written in the Latin script. The broader Kawahíva cluster is described as a Tupi–Guarani dialect cluster of Brazil. The Tenharim, Parintintín, Jiahúi, Amondawa, Karipúna, Uru-eu-wau-wau, Piripkúra, Júma, and Capivarí call themselves Kawahíva; their speech is mutually intelligible, and it is similar to other languages or dialects that are now extinct.
