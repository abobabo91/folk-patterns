## Overview

The Shipibo-Conibo are an Indigenous people living along the Ucayali River in the Amazon rainforest in Peru. Formerly two groups, the Shipibo and Conibo became one tribe through intermarriage and communal rituals. Their population is estimated at over 20,000, although census data is unreliable; figures cited for recent decades range from approximately 11,000 to 25,000 individuals. Shipibo, also called Shipibo-Conibo or Shipibo-Konibo, is a Panoan language spoken in Peru and Brazil by approximately 26,000 speakers. The group is especially represented in folk culture through medicine songs, geometric designs, pottery, textiles, beadwork, and a cosmology connected to the art and artifacts they produce.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Shipibo-Conibo textiles* are made by women and are associated with decorative designs inspired by medicine songs. The sources describe clothing and textiles as among the objects carrying these designs.

*Shipibo-Conibo beadwork* is made by women. The sources identify beadwork as a recognized form of production.

**Geometric decorative designs** also appear on textiles, clothing, pottery, and tools. The sources describe the pottery designs as maze-like red and black geometric patterns.

**Motif vocabulary.** Maze-like red and black geometric patterns.

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that Shipibo-Conibo women make textiles and that decorative designs inspired by medicine songs appear in clothing.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Shipibo-Conibo women are known for pottery decorated with maze-like red and black geometric patterns. These ceramics were traditionally made for use in the home, while an expanding tourist market has enabled households to earn additional income through the sale of pots and other craft items. Decorative designs associated with medicine songs are also found on tools.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention women’s beadwork.

## Music & performance

The sources describe medicine songs as an important influence on Shipibo-Conibo artistic tradition and decorative designs. They also state that ayahuasca medicine work remains a practiced tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources state that the Shipibo and Conibo became one tribe through intermarriage and communal rituals. They also describe ayahuasca medicine work and a rich, complex cosmology connected directly to the art and artifacts the group produces. Christian missionaries, particularly Franciscans, worked to convert them from the late 17th century, while Franciscans missionized the speakers of Shipibo until their expulsion in the 1820s.

## Foodways

The Shipibo-Conibo prepare *chapo*, described as a sweet plantain beverage. The Shipibo of the village of Paoyhan formerly had a diet of fish, yuca, and fruits. The sources state that drought followed by flooding has killed most mature fruit trees and damaged some banana trees and plantains. They also connect deforestation and erosion along the Ucayali River with increases in energy and food prices. No further cooking methods, ceremonial foods, dietary rules, or tea and coffee traditions are described.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They do state that medicine songs have influenced the group’s artistic tradition and decorative designs. The language’s historical documentation includes a Konibo grammar sketch and vocabulary produced in 1800; these works remained unpublished until a century later. No contemporary literary revival or preservation project is identified.

## Language & religion

Shipibo, also called Shipibo-Conibo or Shipibo-Konibo, is a Panoan language spoken in Peru and Brazil by approximately 26,000 speakers. Three attested dialects are identified: Shipibo and Konibo, which have merged; Kapanawa of the Tapiche River, which is obsolescent; and extinct Xipináwa, also called Shipinawa, for which there is no linguistic data. The sources connect Shipibo-Conibo cosmology with their art and artifacts and mention ayahuasca medicine work. Christian missionaries, especially Franciscans, worked to convert them from the late 17th century.
