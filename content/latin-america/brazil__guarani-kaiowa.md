## Overview

The Guarani-Kaiowás are an Indigenous people of Paraguay, the Brazilian state of Mato Grosso do Sul, and northeastern Argentina. They mainly live in Mato Grosso do Sul, where they inhabit Ñande Ru Marangatu, an area of tropical rainforest declared a reservation in October 2004. In Brazil, more than 30,000 Guaranis are estimated to live; in Paraguay, they number about 40,000. The Guarani-Kaiowá are one of three Guaraní sub-groups, alongside Ñandeva and Mbya. Their language belongs to the Tupi-Guarani family, Subgroup I. Their folk-culture significance in the sources lies especially in land-based religious life, village organization, prayer houses, farming, fishing, hunting, and continuing struggles over ancestral land.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The Guarani-Kaiowá villages have prayer houses, and the cacique, or shaman, is of great importance in the community. Their villages also have schools and government assistance. The sources describe many contemporary settlements as camp sites beside busy motorways or temporary settlements in occupied farmland in Mato Grosso do Sul. They also describe a Guarani-Kaiowá settlement around the Indian museum in Rio de Janeiro, next to Maracana football stadium; the building had been abandoned in 1977 and occupied by indigenous communities since that moment.

## Music & performance

The sources do identify the prayer house as a central village institution and describe the cacique, or shaman, as highly important in community life.

## Festivals & rituals

The Guarani-Kaiowá share a religion in which the land is very important. They believe that the god Ñande Ru created the Guarani as the first peoples, and they are described as deeply spiritual. Every village has a prayer house, while the cacique, or shaman, has great importance in the community. In their mythology, “Terra sem Mal,” meaning land without evil, is the land of the dead people. It is important that every soul can go to Terra sem Mal.

The sources also describe collective responses to threats against land and settlement. In October 2012, a group of 170 Kaiowás—50 men, 50 women, and 70 children—camping for almost a year at the Cambará farm near the Joguico River in Iguatemi, Mato Grosso do Sul, declared that they were ready to accept their extinction rather than leave the territory. The decision was suspended by a court order on October 30, 2012. In Rio de Janeiro, on the twelve of January 2012, a police unit arrived at an indigenous settlement without a judicial order; the siege lasted over 12 hours and ended when the police left without taking action.

## Foodways

The Guarani-Kaiowá traditional life is described as based on fishing, hunting, and farming. Their lifestyle also consists of living on their own land and growing small crops to support their livelihood. The sources connect these practices to the loss of land: when intruders occupy Guarani territory, the community has too little land to sustain its traditional life. The sources also report that Guarani people have denounced the poisoning of their water resources by farmers.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record the mythological concept of Terra sem Mal, “land without evil,” described as the land of the dead people, and the belief that every soul should be able to go there. The sources also reproduce a letter sent by Guarani-Kaiowá people to the Conselho Indigenista Missionário, or Cimi, and to the national management of Fundação Nacional do Índio, or Funai, during the 2012 land conflict. The letter asks the Government and the Federal Justice not to decree eviction, but instead to decree mass death and bury the people with their ancestors where they were living.

## Language & religion

The Guarani-Kaiowá speak Kaiwá, a Tupi-Guarani language of Subgroup I. Kaiwá is written in the Latin script, but literacy is described as extremely low, from 5% to 10%. The Guarani language is one of the official languages of Paraguay alongside Spanish. The people are also known as the Kaiwá, Caingua, Caiua, Caiwa, Cayua, Kaiova, and Kayova. The name Kaiowá means “the people” in their own language.

The Guarani sub-groups have different forms of social organization but share a religion that places great importance on land. They believe that Ñande Ru created the Guarani as the first peoples. Religious life is connected to the prayer house in every village, the authority of the cacique or shaman, and the mythology of Terra sem Mal. The sources describe the loss and occupation of land as an offence against Guarani religion.
