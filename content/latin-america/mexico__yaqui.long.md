## Overview

The Yaqui — who call themselves *Hiaki* or *Yoeme*, from *yoemem* ("people") — are an indigenous people whose homeland, *Hiakim*, lies along the lower Río Yaqui valley in the northwestern Mexican state of Sonora, where eight Yaqui pueblos stand: Bácum, Benem, Cócorit, Huiribis, Pótam, Rahum, Tórim and Vícam. Others live in Sinaloa and in city neighbourhoods across Mexico, and a population that fled state violence settled in southern Arizona, forming the Pascua Yaqui Tribe of Arizona at Tucson, the only federally recognized Yaqui tribe in the United States; in 2008 it counted 11,324 voting members. Their language, *Yoem Noki*, belongs to the Uto-Aztecan family and has roughly 16,000 speakers. In folk-culture terms the Yaqui are known above all for a ceremonial life — deer songs, masked clowns, Lenten and Easter observance — that fused Jesuit Christianity with older belief without surrendering either.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Matachin belt** — A woollen belt worn by every *matachin* dancer during the matachines dance, woven by the men of the community; the museum record describing a Sonoran example notes that the dancers who wear it belong to their society for life.

**Chal (shawl)** — A woven shawl collected in Sonora, black with white flecks and ribbed across its width, its warp threads densely plaited at both ends into a broad band that finishes in long tasselled fringes; at about 292 cm long and 80 cm wide it is a wrapping garment rather than a scarf.

Beyond these, the sources record only that the Yaqui made cotton products, and that house walls were built of woven reed.

### Clothing & dress

The sources used here say little about everyday Yaqui dress; what they document in detail is ceremonial regalia. Men who dance *matachines* wear the woollen belt described above and carry a feather wand. Dancers in the *pascola* complex wear a carved wooden mask — one British Museum example is painted black with red and white decoration, with tufts of white horsehair pegged into the eyebrows, cheeks and chin. Around their ankles deer dancers and *pahkolam* tie *tenabaris*, rattles made of cut cocoons sewn together in pairs along two cords, each cocoon holding small stones that strike its hard walls at every movement; the cords end in bright tassels used to bind them to the dancers' shins, and the practice honours the insect world. Rattles made from deer hooves are worn at the waist, honouring the many deer that have died. A head ornament recorded simply as a *corona* (crown) belongs to the same dance equipment. Since the adoption of Christianity many Yaqui households set a wooden cross before the house, its placement and condition given particular attention during *Waresma* (Lent).

### Architecture

A Yaqui house traditionally consisted of three rectangular sections: a bedroom, a kitchen, and a living room called the *portal*. Floors rested on wooden supports; walls were of woven reeds; the roof was reed covered with thick layers of mud for insulation. Branches might be worked into the living-room structure to let air circulate, since much of the day — especially in the hot months — was spent there. A patio belonged to the house as well. The ceremonial geography of the settlement is older than the buildings: the Jesuits persuaded a dispersed river population to concentrate into eight towns, and those towns remain the frame of Yaqui public life. In the Arizona barrios the same forms were improvised from whatever came to hand, with houses built of scrap lumber and railroad ties in Barrio Libre and Pascua in Tucson, and in Guadalupe, established in 1904 and incorporated in 1975; New Pascua was laid out west of the community of Hu'upa in the late 1960s.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The most fully documented Yaqui objects are instruments assembled from pottery, gourd and wood. A water drum comes in three parts: a glazed earthenware pot with two handles, a half-gourd bowl floated in it, and a wooden stick to strike with. A rasp is likewise three parts — a half gourd serving as a sounding board, a grooved stick laid against it, and a round stick drawn across the grooves. Flutes, hand rattles and sistrum-type dance rattles complete the set in Swedish collections gathered on the Second Swedish Mexico Expedition of 1934–1935. Subsistence equipment stands behind this: field agriculture in beans, maize and squash, fishing along the river mouth and the Sonora and Sinaloa coasts, and hunting and gathering in the deserts and mountains.

### Jewelry & body adornment

A Sonoran necklace in the Museum of World Culture is strung in five rows of mostly white glass beads shot through with black and red, carrying six mother-of-pearl ornaments — four triangular with rounded bases, two smaller and rectangular — every one of them notched at the edges so the notches form an X. The ornaments hang from shorter strands of glass beads in many colours; the beadwork does not close around the neck but is tied at the nape with a red cotton band finished in tassels. A simpler necklace of pink glass beads threaded on white cotton runs about 80 cm. The same X appears on a wooden amulet: a rectangular plaque incised with an X cut partly through the wood, a polished blue stone set at the centre flanked by sunken fields, with smaller polished white stones above and below it and a pierced lug at the top carrying a blue cotton thread.

## Music & performance

Yaqui music is dominated by the deer songs, *maso bwikam*, which accompany the deer dance and are often noted for mixing Native American and Catholic religious thought. The ensemble combines native and Spanish instruments: harp, violin or fiddle, rasp (*hirukiam*, also *kuta*), drum and rattles. In the deer dance the water drum represents the deer's heartbeat and the rasp its breathing, the gourd rattles held by the dancers honour the plant world, and flute, fiddle and frame harp join the frame drum. Deer songs are only one genre; the sources also name messenger songs (*suru bwikam*), corn wine songs (*vachi vino bwikam*), fly songs (*nahi bwikam*) and coyote songs (*wo'i bwikam*). The deer singers, *masobwikamem*, sing of things from nature that the deer might see, in a register that differs slightly from casual Yaqui and resembles elders' speech — syllable reduplication, *yeyewe* for *yewe* ("play"), the substitution of /l/ for another sound, and archaic words such as *seyewailo*, the flower world. A song falls into two stanza-like parts, *u vat weeme* and the concluding *u tonua*: the first is sung many times over, and the conclusion often turns on antithesis. Frances Densmore made the first recordings of Yaqui music, including thirteen deer songs, in 1922; among the ritual societies, the *Kantoras* are the women singers.

## Dance & theatre

The deer dance, usually held all night, thanks and honours the deer — *maso*, little brother deer, *saila maso* — for coming from its home, the flower world (*seyewailo*), and letting itself be killed so that people may live. It is danced by a *pascola*, the word taken from Spanish *pascua* (Easter), who is also called a deer dancer. Alongside them the *pahkolam* act as ritual clowns: they dance, give sermons, host the gathering by providing water, joke, and perform comedic skits such as pretending to be coyotes. The **matachines** dance is a separate institution, held with strong European influence — the Swedish collectors were told the word *matachin* means a bully or tyrant, that the dance is usually performed inside the churches, that it is accompanied by gourd rattles and violins, and that its leader is called the *monarca*. Men enter the matachin society for life. The collecting notes also place these dances outside Sonora: a pascola mask and a pair of cocoon ankle rattles were bought from Yaqui people at a pascola dance in Tlaxcala in the mid-1930s.

## Festivals & rituals

Yaqui ceremonial time turns on *Waresma* (Lent) and Easter. Many Yaqui hold that the existence of the world depends on their annual performance of the Lenten and Easter rituals, and that much ritual exists to perfect the overlapping worlds, the *aniam*, and to undo the harm people have done to them. Pascolas perform at religio-social functions many times of the year, but above all during Lent and Easter. The labour is distributed among named societies: the prayer leaders, the *Kiyohteis* (female church assistants), the *Vanteareaom* (female flag bearers), the *Anheiltom* (angels), the *Kohtumvre Ya'ura* or fariseo society, the *Kantoras* (female singers), the *Officios*, the *Pahko'ola* and deer dance societies, the *Wiko Yau'ra* society, and the *Matachinim*, the matachin dancers. The Easter ceremonies mattered enough that Yaqui families in the Arizona barrios, living on migrant farm wages, took great pains to continue them. Flowers run through all of it: Yaqui teaching holds that flowers sprang from the drops of blood shed at the crucifixion, flowers are the manifestation of souls, and a man may greet a close male friend with *Haisa sewa?* — "How is the flower?" The sources used here do not describe birth, coming-of-age, marriage or funeral rites.

## Foodways

The old agricultural base was beans, maize and squash, grown on land the river inundated every year; Yaqui living near the river mouth and along the Sonora and Sinaloa coasts fished as well as farmed, while those in the deserts and mountains depended on hunting and gathering. The Jesuits, during the 120 years of their mission, introduced wheat, cattle and horses, and the four crops have been grown together since. The scale of that farming is recorded for the Cárdenas years, when the state reserved ancestral land on the north bank of the Yaqui River, built a dam for irrigation and supplied equipment and water pumps: in 1935 the Yaqui produced 250 tons of wheat and no maize or beans, and by 1939 they produced 3,500 tons of wheat, 500 tons of maize and 750 tons of beans. One ceremonial drink is attested only through its music, the corn wine songs, *vachi vino bwikam*. The sources used here name no prepared dishes, no cooking methods and no dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

The deer song corpus is the literary tradition the sources document, with its own conventions — a fixed two-part form, an elevated register distinct from everyday speech, and a vocabulary of archaic words. Verbal etiquette is formal: a four-phrase greeting formula is used even among close friends — *Lios em chania* ("Greetings!", literally "God preserves you!", *Lios* an early borrowing of Spanish *Dios*), answered by *Lios em chiokoe* ("God pardons you!"), then *Empo allea* ("May you rejoice!", with *allea* said to come from Spanish *alegre*) and *Kettu'i* ("How kind!"). The language itself carries expressive resources that shade a speaker's meaning: a word containing /l/ may be said as written to signal approval, or with /r/ in place of /l/ to signal disapproval, and the word *laute* means both "quickly" and "slowly", disambiguated by a fast or slow open-handed gesture. Revitalization is active: in 2009 the Pascua Yaqui Tribal Council and the University of Arizona began a programme in which tribal elders teach the language to families, and a university project has worked from thirty-year-old audio tapes recorded by the tribal member Maria Leyva.

## Language & religion

*Yoem Noki* is Uto-Aztecan, one of the Cahitan languages — a group of about ten mutually intelligible tongues once spoken across much of Sonora and Sinaloa, of which only Yaqui and Mayo survive. It is an agglutinative, "noun-heavy" language with generally subject–object–verb order, object nouns suffixed in *-ta*, plurals in *-im*, past in *-k* and future in *-ne*, and adjectives that behave as verbs. Whether it is tonal is genuinely disputed in the sources: one describes a pitch accent on the first or second syllable, another finds no widespread use of tonemes in the modern language. Two orthographic traditions exist, the Pascua Yaqui spelling and Mexican systems following Spanish conventions ("rohikte" against "rojicte"). The Yaqui Religion, dated from 1500 to the present, is a syncretism of older belief with Jesuit teaching and is considered the earliest revitalization reform movement within Native American religions; it works through song, music, prayer and dance. Its cosmology recognizes nine or more overlapping worlds, the *aniam*: *sea ania* the flower world, *yo ania* the enchanted world, *tenku ania* a dream world, *tuka ania* the night world, *huya ania* the wilderness, *nao ania* the corncob world, *kawi ania* the mountains, *vawe ania* the world under the water and *teeka ania* the world from the sky upward. The deer emerges from *yo ania* into *huya ania* and dances in *sea ania*.
