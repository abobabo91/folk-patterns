## Overview

The Igneri were an Indigenous Arawakan-speaking people of the southern Lesser Antilles in the Caribbean. Their historical relationship to the Kalinago remains debated: older accounts described an invasion in which the Kalinago conquered and displaced them, while later linguistic and archaeological studies proposed cultural transition, limited conquest without displacement, or the gradual adoption of a “Kalinago” identity through economic and political relations with mainland Kalina. The Igneri language developed into the Kalinago language, also known as Island Carib and Igneri. Archaeologist Irving Rouse associated the Igneri with the Suazoid culture, which emerged around AD 1000 as a continuation of the Saladoid culture. The Suazoid culture lasted until around 1450.

## Music & performance

They do record a linguistic distinction associated with Kalinago men: during the period of French colonization in the 17th century, and possibly earlier, men used a Cariban-based pidgin in addition to the Arawakan Kalinago language. This pidgin was evidently similar to one used by Kalina people when communicating with their Lokono neighbors.

## Festivals & rituals

The available accounts concern historical traditions about the relationship between the Igneri, Kalinago, and Kalina rather than named ritual practices. According to Kalinago traditions, the Igneri were the original inhabitants of the islands, while the Kalinago were invaders originating in South America.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Igneri are known through traditions of the Kalinago concerning the settlement and political history of the Lesser Antilles. These traditions represented the Igneri as the islands’ original inhabitants and the Kalinago as invaders originating in South America, and they were described as widespread and internally consistent. European observers consequently accepted the invasion account as historical. Later scholars proposed different interpretations of the same tradition, including conquest without displacement and the gradual adoption of a “Kalinago” identity through relations with the mainland Kalina polity.

## Language & religion

The Igneri spoke an Arawakan language. The language historically spoken by the Kalinago of the Lesser Antilles was known as Kalinago, Island Carib, and Igneri, with forms including Iñeri and Inyeri. Linguistic studies determined that the Antillean Carib language was Arawakan rather than Cariban, related to the Lokono language of the South American mainland and more distantly to Taíno in the Greater Antilles. Kalinago proper became extinct by about 1920 through population decline and colonial-period deportations, while an offshoot survives as Garifuna in Central America.
