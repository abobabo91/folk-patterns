## Overview

The Tlapanec, or *Meꞌphaa*, are an Indigenous people of Mexico native to the state of Guerrero. Today, Tlapanecs live primarily in Guerrero and number more than 98,000. Their language belongs to the Oto-Manguean language family and is closely related to the extinct Subtiaba language of Nicaragua. In pre-Columbian times, they lived in the isolated mountain area along Guerrero’s Costa Chica region, just east of present-day Acapulco. Their main city was Tlapan, and the name Tlapanec is Nahuatl for “Inhabitant of Tlapan.” The sources document language, myth, nagualism, and historical relations with the Yopi.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state that Yopi culture differed from Tlapanec culture in part through the Yopi practice of not wearing clothing until marriage, after which palm leaves and deerskins could be worn.

### Architecture

They identify Tlapan as the main Tlapanec city in pre-Columbian times and Tehuacalco as an archaeological site inhabited by the Yopi people.

## Music & performance

Tlapanec-language programming is carried by the CDI’s radio station XEZV-AM, broadcasting from Tlapa de Comonfort, Guerrero.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe religious explanations of natural phenomena through myth. In the creation myth, the sun, *Akha'*, the moon, *Gon'*, and the fire god, *Akuun mbatsuun'*, were born on the bank of the river and raised by *Akuun ñee*, goddess of the temazcal sweatbath and patron of the hot/cold duality.

The sources also describe nagualism as an important element of Tlapanec culture. When a baby is born, an animal is said to be born at the same time; that animal is the child’s nahual. The child alone knows which animal is its nahual, because the nahual reveals itself to the child in dreams.

The sources describe additional practices among the Yopi, a people seemingly related to the Tlapanec proper. The Yopi were invited to the coronations of Mexica rulers in Tenochtitlan, where they received gifts. The sources state that divorce was permitted among the Yopi and that adultery was punished by the offended spouse biting off the offender’s nose upon the first infraction and by stoning to death on the second.

## Foodways

They mention the temazcal sweatbath in connection with *Akuun ñee*, goddess of the temazcal and patron of the hot/cold duality, but provide no further description of foodways.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources document Tlapanec myths concerning the creation of the sun, the moon, and fire. *Akha'*, *Gon'*, and *Akuun mbatsuun'* were born on the bank of the river and raised by *Akuun ñee*.

The sources also describe the belief that every child has a nahual, an animal born at the same time as the child. The nahual appears to the child in dreams, and no one except the child knows which animal it is. This belief is presented as part of Tlapanec religious culture and oral explanation of human and animal relations.

## Language & religion

Tlapanec, or *Meꞌphaa*, is an Indigenous Mexican language spoken by more than 98,000 Tlapanec people in Guerrero. The ethnic group refers to its ethnic identity and language as *Me̱ꞌpha̱a̱* [meʔpʰaː]. Tlapanec is part of the Oto-Manguean language family and forms its own branch with the extinct and closely related Subtiaba language of Nicaragua.

Ethnologue distinguishes four Tlapanec languages: Acatepec, Azoyú, Malinaltepec, and Tlacoapa. Acatepec includes the dialects Acatepec proper, Huitzapula, Nanzintla, Teocuitlapa, and Zapotitlán Tablas; Malinaltepec includes Huehuetepec/Zilacayotitlán; and Tlacoapa includes Tlacoapa proper and Tenamazapa. Other sources, including native speakers and the Instituto Nacional de Lenguas Indígenas, identify eight or nine varieties with official status.

Tlapanec is tonal and has complex inflectional morphology. It is an ergative–absolutive language and is described as a marked absolutive language. The sources state that tones differ by variety and that the glottal stop is written with a saltillo, ⟨Ꞌ ꞌ⟩.

Religiously, Tlapanecs explain natural phenomena through myth and practice nagualism.
