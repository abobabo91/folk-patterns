## Overview

The Mixtec are an Indigenous people of southern Mexico whose homeland spans three regions: the mountainous Mixteca Alta, the hot Mixteca Baja, and the humid Mixteca Costa. They are celebrated in anthropology for their surviving pictorial codices painted on deerskin and their mastery of textiles dyed with shellfish purple and cochineal red. Their cities were laid out around multiple small plazas rather than a single grand plaza, and they later built at Monte Albán, Tilantongo, and other major Mesoamerican sites. Today they number about 800,000 in Mexico with significant diaspora communities in the United States.

## Material culture

Mixtec material culture centers on backstrap-loom textiles with costly dyes, refined ceramics, and masterwork in gold and turquoise.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Mixtec weavers created three-panel textiles using hand-spun cotton and silk dyed with shellfish, cochineal, and indigo, featuring woven or brocaded motifs.

- **Brocaded wedding huipil** (*huipil*) — A three-panel ceremonial shirt woven in white cotton, shellfish-dyed lilac, and burgundy cochineal silk, with a double-headed bird brocaded in four bands across the stripes and purple satin trim at the neck.
- **Embroidered wedding huipil** (*huipil*) — A second wedding form with three cotton panels joined by green silk ribbon, thicker weft threads raising horizontal bands, silk embroidered neck with animals and flowers, and double-headed birds on front, back, and shoulders.
- **Wrap-around skirt** (*posahuanco*) — Built from three webs of cloth sewn together in horizontal bands of blue, mauve, and maroon, combining cotton dyed with indigo and shellfish dye with cochineal-dyed silk.
- **Plain brown and white cloth** (*servilleta in coyuche cotton*) — A backstrap-loom cloth of hand-spun naturally brown cotton with warp stripes alternating brown and white and a twisted fringe at both ends.
- **Plaited palm baskets** — Lidded baskets plaited from palm fiber with fuchsia-dyed fiber set against undyed ground in zigzag and geometric patterns, finished with thin cross stitch.

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress consists of two sewn loom-woven pieces: the huipil and wrapped posahuanco, with ceremonial garments distinguished by costly dyes and elaborate trim.

- **Ceremonial wedding huipil** (*huipil*) — Made for marriage ceremonies in shellfish purple and cochineal silk with satin or silk ribbon trim and double-headed bird motifs, marking expense and status.
- **Everyday huipil** (*huipil*) — Plain undyed browns and whites of coyuche cotton, in contrast to the elaborate wedding form.
- **Dance-belt horse** — A carved wooden horse painted white with a brown saddle blanket trimmed in red, gold and green, fitted with leather ears, horsehair mane, and twisted-cord reins and stirrups, tied to the waist for masquerade.

### Architecture

Mixtec cities were laid out around multiple small plazas rather than single great plazas, and hillsides were terraced with stairways to accommodate the population.

- **Cerro de las Minas** — A Baja city built around several small plazas with the population distributed around them, featuring terraced hillsides with stairways and walls inscribed in ñuiñe script.
- **Monte Negro** — One of the first substantial towns in the Mixteca Alta, appearing towards the end of the Middle Preclassic period.
- **Hillside terrace system** (*coo yuu (lama-bordo)*) — Terraces that reshaped hillsides to allow settlement and were threaded with stairways throughout the city.
- **Classic period fortified cities** — Cities in the Baja such as Cerro de las Minas and Diquiyú with administrative and religious buildings on hill slopes and dwellings on lower ground.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Mixtec potters made burnished and slipped wares in orange or white, from water-jugs with geometric and animal designs to elaborate candelabra shaped as trees of life.

- **Water-jug** — Ovoid or round-bottomed vessels tapering to a long flaring neck with two or three looped handles evenly spaced around the shoulder.
- **Painted pottery candelabrum** — A tree-of-life form built on four legs with an angel at the trunk base, a figure holding a cross and skull, birds and dogs on wire, five candle tubes, and flowers and leaves mounted on wire.
- **Carved gourd vessels** — Gourds carved on the outside with geometric fields and animals and fitted with lids tied on by twisted cord.
- **Gold jewelry and ornaments** — Works by the Mixtec, regarded as the foremost goldsmiths of Mesoamerica, created by lost-wax casting of gold and its alloys.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Mixtec jewelers and mosaicists created turquoise mosaic masks and gold work that held political and religious weight throughout ancient Mesoamerica.

- **Turquoise mosaic masks** — Masks given as gifts to seal political alliances, worn in ceremonies impersonating gods, and attached to funerary bundles treated as oracles.
- **Gold jewelry** — Mixtec goldsmiths' output formed an important part of the tribute paid to the Aztecs and was highly valued across Mesoamerica.

## Music & performance

Mixtec music was transmitted orally through five centuries after the Conquest, surviving largely through ritual and community practice.

- **Gourd rattle** — A round gourd mounted on a wooden dowel, filled with pebbles or seeds, its surface cut away to show brown designs of two birds, a rabbit and a fox on a beige ground, with a fuchsia and green striped handle.
- **Contemporary Mixtec music** — The poem 'Yunu Yukuninu' by Juan de Dios Ortiz Cruz was set to music by Lila Downs, one of the leading figures in contemporary Mixtec music.

## Dance & theatre

Dance traditions are documented only through a carved wooden horse dance-belt worn at the waist for masquerade.

- **Horse dance-belt masquerade** — A carved wooden horse worn at the waist on fiber cords, painted white with a brown saddle blanket trimmed in red, gold, and green, with leather ears and horsehair mane.

## Festivals & rituals

Mixtec ritual practice is tied to sacred places and objects, with the most documented life-cycle rite being marriage ceremonies marked by distinctive textiles.

- **Colossal Natural Bridge** — A cave site in the Coixtlahuaca Basin that is an important sacred place for the Mixtec.
- **Colossal head sculptures** (*ñuiñe*) — Small stone sculptures of anthropomorphic heads found in the Baja, some of which are objects of worship in Mixteca guerrerense communities today.
- **Wedding ceremony** — Marked by a three-panel huipil woven in shellfish purple and cochineal silk with double-headed bird motifs, distinct from everyday cloth.
- **Divine impersonation ceremony** — Pre-Hispanic ceremonies in which turquoise mosaic masks were worn by the wearer impersonating a god.

## Foodways

The Mixtec economy rested on four basic Mesoamerican crops: corn, chili, beans, and squash, with corn holding cosmological significance.

- **Corn milpa** (*milpa*) — The field first Mixtecs planted overnight to hold the land won from the Sun; corn is also central to the Mixtec creation account where four gods make humanity from it.
- **Four basic Mesoamerican crops** — Corn, chili, beans, and squash formed the foundation of settlements from around the third millennium BC.
- **Lemon** (*tzikua Iya (sour orange) or limun*) — A newly introduced Spanish crop recorded in San Martín Duraznos, Oaxaca, with both Spanish and indigenous names.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Mixtec are known for their surviving pictorial codices painted on deerskin in screenfold form, narrating the exploits of Lord Eight Deer.

- **Codex Bodley** — One of four surviving codices narrating the exploits of Lord Eight Deer Jaguar Claw, housed in a European collection.
- **Codex Colombino** — A surviving codex narrating the exploits of Lord Eight Deer, held in the National Museum of Anthropology in Mexico City.
- **Genealogical screenfold** — A pictorial record on sixteen double-sided leather pages showing twenty-six generations of a noble family with subsidiary lines and native language commentary.
- **Lienzo Seler II** (*lienzo*) — A post-Conquest pictorial cloth glossed in the Latin alphabet from Coixtlahuaca, representing the transition from codices to lienzos.
- **Modern Mixtec writers** — Writers of the Mixteca Alta such as Raúl Gatica, whose work appears in contemporary collections like 'Asalto a la palabra' gathering Mixtec poets.

## Language & religion

Mixtec belongs to the Oto-Manguean language family and is tone-based; pre-Hispanic worship of Dzahui the rain god was supplemented by Roman Catholicism after the 16th century.

- **Mixtec language** (*tu'un savi (or sahan sau, dahan davi)*) — Over fifty varieties forming a dialect continuum with abrupt boundaries, spoken by over half a million people; means 'word of the rain' in local forms.
- **Tone system** — Commonly three tonal levels, with nine in Yoloxóchitl Mixtec, alongside contrastive nasalization and glottalization.
- **Dzahui** — Chief pre-Hispanic deity, god of rain and patron of the Mixtec nation, whose cult was consolidated during the Classic period.
- **Nine Wind-Coo Dzahui** — The civilising hero who brought agriculture to the Mixtec people.

## Glossary

- *Huipil* — A sleeveless shirt woven on the backstrap loom in three panels
- *Posahuanco* — A wrap-around skirt made from three webs of cloth sewn together
- *Coyuche* — Naturally brown hand-spun cotton
- *Coo yuu* — Hillside terraces (lama-bordo) used to reshape settlement areas
- *Ñuiñe* — Mixtec script used to inscribe reliefs on walls; also refers to colossal head sculptures
- *Milpa* — A field planted with corn and other basic crops
- *Tu'un savi* — Mixtec self-name for the language meaning 'word of the rain'
- *Dzahui* — Pre-Hispanic Mixtec god of rain and nation's patron
- *Lienzo* — A large post-Conquest pictorial cloth glossed in the Latin alphabet
- *Servilleta* — A small backstrap-loom cloth
- *Codex* — A pictorial screenfold narrative painted on deerskin in pre-Hispanic form
