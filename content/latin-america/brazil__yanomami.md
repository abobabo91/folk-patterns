## Overview

The Yanomami, also spelled Yąnomamö or Yanomama, are approximately 35,000 Indigenous people living in some 200–250 villages in the Amazon rainforest on the border between Venezuela and Brazil. Their territory includes the States of Roraima and Amazonas, and their historical connections extend toward the Orinoco river and the highlands of Brazil and Venezuela. Yanomami communities are politically autonomous villages rather than a single united group. They are associated with four main language varieties—Ninam, Sanumá, Waiká, and Yanomamö—within the Yanomaman languages, although many local variations and dialects exist. Their folk culture is organized around communal village life, rainforest foraging, horticulture, hunting, fishing, body adornment, ritual feasts, shamanic healing, and distinctive forms of domestic architecture.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Basket weaving** — Yanomami women weave baskets from small strings of bark and roots. The baskets carry plants, crops, and food back to the *shabono*; women dye them with the red berry known as *onoto* or *urucu* and decorate them with masticated charcoal pigment.

**Body and loincloth dyeing** — *Onoto* or *urucu* is also used to paint the body and dye loincloths.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe women’s use of loincloths. During a girl’s first menstrual period, her old cotton garments are replaced with new ones symbolizing womanhood and availability for marriage. After menstruation begins, a girl must keep herself covered with a loincloth and is forbidden from showing her genitalia. Yanomami women also use *onoto* or *urucu* to dye loincloths. During ritual feasts, women decorate their bodies with feathers and flowers.

### Architecture

Yanomami villages are usually formed by children and extended families and generally contain between 50 and 400 people. The entire village commonly lives under a shared roof called a *shabono*, a structure with a characteristic oval shape and open ground in the center measuring an average of 100 yards (91 m). The *shabono* forms the perimeter of the village unless it has been fortified with palisades. Support posts divide the interior into individual houses and spaces, although these divisions are not marked by permanent walls. The structures are made from rainforest materials including leaves, vines, and tree trunks. Heavy rain, wind, and insects damage them, so new *shabonos* are constructed every 4 to 6 years.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do describe bark straps, woven baskets, cotton garments, gourds, and the use of leaves, vines, and tree trunks in village construction. A gourd may preserve the ashes of a deceased person during the ritual cycle of remembrance.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They state that Yanomami people decorate their bodies with feathers and flowers during harvest feasts. Women also paint their bodies with *onoto* or *urucu*, the red berry used for dyeing baskets and loincloths. Body adornment is therefore documented in connection with feasting and bodily decoration.

## Music & performance

Music and performance are described mainly in relation to ritual and communal feasting. The Yanomami celebrate a good harvest with a large feast to which nearby villages are invited. Village members gather substantial quantities of food, and during the feast women dance and sing late into the night. The event also includes body decoration with feathers and flowers and helps maintain good relations with neighboring villages.

Shamans use hallucinogens or entheogens known as *yakoana* or *ebene* as part of healing rituals for ill members of the community. *Yakoana* also denotes the tree from which the substance is derived, *Virola theiodora*. *Yopo*, derived from *Anadenanthera peregrina*, is usually cultivated in the shaman’s garden. The Xamatari mix powdered bark of *Virola theiodora* with powdered yopo seeds to create *ebene*. These substances facilitate communication with the *hekura*, spirits believed to govern many aspects of the physical world. The practice is called *shapuri*, and women do not engage in it.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name no dramatic or theatrical tradition. They state that women dance and sing late into the night during the large harvest feast, when nearby villages are invited and the hosts decorate their bodies with feathers and flowers. No further dance names, performance forms, or distinctions between ceremonial and entertainment traditions are provided.

## Festivals & rituals

They describe a harvest feast held to celebrate a good harvest. Nearby villages are invited, food is gathered in large quantities, and the gathering helps maintain good relations between communities. Women decorate their bodies with feathers and flowers, dance, and sing late into the night.

Healing rituals are conducted by Yanomami shamans using *yakoana* or *ebene*. These substances facilitate communication with the *hekura*, spirits believed to govern aspects of the physical world. The sources also describe female puberty as a significant life-cycle transition. Girls typically begin menstruation around the ages of 12 to 13; they may be betrothed before menarche, but marriage may be consummated only once menstruation begins. During the first menstrual period, a girl is confined behind a screen of leaves, fed with a stick, required to whisper, and permitted to speak only to close female kin.

The Yanomami practice ritual endocannibalism after the death of a kinsman. The body is wrapped in leaves and placed in the forest some distance from the *shabono*. After insects consume the soft tissue, usually in about 30 to 45 days, the bones are collected and cremated. The ashes are mixed with a banana soup and consumed by the community. The ashes may be preserved in a gourd and the ritual repeated annually until they are gone. Personal belongings are burned, and the dead person is discussed only on the annual “day of remembrance,” when the ashes are consumed and relatives recall the deceased person’s life.

## Foodways

Yanomami foodways combine rainforest gathering, hunting, fishing, and slash-and-burn horticulture. Bananas and cassava are important crops, and crops compose up to 75% of the calories in the Yanomami diet. Women cultivate cooking plantains and cassava, while men clear forest for gardens. Garden plots may also contain sugarcane, mangoes, sweet potatoes, papayas, maize, and other crops. When soil becomes exhausted, the Yanomami move away from overused areas in a practice known as shifting cultivation.

Protein comes from wild resources obtained through gathering, hunting, and fishing. Food sources include fruit, nuts, tubers, grubs, frogs, terrestrial crabs, caterpillars, and fish. Yanomami people sometimes cut down palms to facilitate the growth of grubs. Women gather termite nests and other grubs, which are roasted at family hearths. They also prepare cassava by shredding the roots, expressing the toxic juice, and roasting the flour into flat cakes known in Spanish as *casabe*. Women carry harvested crops with bark straps and woven baskets.

The traditional diet is very low in edible salt, and the sources state that Yanomami blood pressure is characteristically among the lowest of any demographic group. During the harvest feast, villagers gather large amounts of food for collective consumption. In the endocannibalistic remembrance ritual, cremated human ashes are mixed into a soup made from bananas and consumed by the community.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do preserve quoted historical and anthropological accounts, including statements attributed to Apolinar Diaz de la Fuente, Jacques Lizot, Napoleon Chagnon, R. Brian Ferguson, and Helena Valero, but these are presented as historical or scholarly testimony rather than as Yanomami oral literature.

## Language & religion

Yanomaman languages comprise four main varieties: Ninam, Sanumá, Waiká, and Yanomamö. Many local variations and dialects exist, and people from different villages cannot always understand one another. Many linguists consider the Yanomaman family a language isolate unrelated to other South American Indigenous languages, while others place the Yanomami language within the Macro-Jê group. The origins of the language are described as obscure.

The ethnonym Yanomami derives from *yanõmami*, which in the expression *yanõmami thëpë* means “human beings.” The expression is contrasted with *yaro* (“game animals”), *yai* (“invisible or nameless beings”), and *napë* (“enemy, stranger, non-Indigenous”). Yanomami shamans use *yakoana* or *ebene* in healing rituals to communicate with the *hekura*, spirits believed to govern many aspects of the physical world. The sources also describe ritual endocannibalism and the belief that a dead person’s belongings may harbor evil spirits.
