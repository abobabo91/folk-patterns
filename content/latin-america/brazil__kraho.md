## Overview

The Krahô are a Timbira people who live on an Indigenous territory in Tocantins, Brazil. They were seminomadic hunters and gatherers who also farmed, and were pushed westward by settlement. They are known for beautifully plaited and painted palm-leaf work, feathered regalia, and masked dance traditions. Most of what we know comes from objects collected in the mid-twentieth century.

## Material culture

Krahô material culture is built from plaited palm leaf, with decoration in feathers, paint, beads and cotton, reflecting a portable way of life.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Palm-leaf plaiting is the main technique, with pattern created by the weave itself or by painting with natural pigments.

- **Twill-plaited palm leaf** — Sleeping mats, baskets, bags and headbands are all made by plaiting palm leaf in twill, with the weave pattern itself creating the ornament.
- **Urucu-painted plaitwork** (*urucu*) — Plaited surfaces are painted with urucu pigment to add color and decoration on top of the woven pattern.
- **Log-racing headbands** — Palm-leaf headbands made for log-racing are sewn with fiber thread and painted in yellow-ochre and purple.
- **Woven cotton belts and armlets** — Women's singing-belts are woven in red cotton with bead tassels; composite armlets combine plaited cotton with beads and urucu paint.

### Clothing & dress

Krahô dress centers on head-gear, belts and footwear, with feathered regalia for ceremonies and simpler versions for children and racing.

- **Feathered head-dress** (*arara*) — Ceremonial head-dresses mount macaw (arara) or falcon feathers on a plaited or bast headband, finished with red cotton tassels.
- **Boy's head-dress** — Children wear the same head-dress form as adults but in smaller scale, with plaited palm leaf stained with urucu and arara feathers.
- **Singing-belt** — A woman's red cotton belt hung with boiled-bead tassels that marks a vocal performance role.
- **Plaited sandals** — Palm-leaf sandals with the plait twisting outward from the toe-strap.

### Architecture

No information is documented about Krahô house forms or villages.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Krahô households use plaited palm leaf, gourd, bamboo, horn and animal bone rather than ceramics or metal.

- **Fire fans** — Plaited palm-leaf fans for managing fire.
- **Storage basket** — A square-based plaited palm-leaf basket with a separate lid, made to be carried with a cord.
- **Tobacco pouch** — A twill-plaited pouch with two loops for carrying or hanging.
- **Sleeping mat** — Plaited palm-leaf mats, some plain and some painted with urucu pigment.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Krahô adornment combines beads, feathers, gourds and animal materials, with urucu pigment used across multiple forms.

- **Lip ornaments** — Two lip ornaments made from yellowish stone are the only documented stone objects.
- **Boiled beads** — Beads strung as tassels on the singing-belt, attached to whistles and woven into cotton armlets.
- **Necklet whistle** — A gourd whistle hung on a cord necklet and ornamented with yellow feathers and deer hooves that sound with movement.
- **Urucu pigment** (*urucu*) — A red-orange natural pigment used for coloring beads, plaitwork, masks and body decoration.

## Music & performance

Krahô instruments are whistles, rattles and a singing-belt, made from gourds, bone, bamboo and other natural materials.

- **Gourd whistles** — Whistles built from gourds, often ornamented with feathers, deer hooves, beads or cotton tassels.
- **Bone whistles** — Whistles made from the nail of a giant armadillo or capybara, often decorated with yellow feathers.
- **Bamboo whistle** — A bamboo whistle with a fiber loop, tuned with a plug of bee's-wax for pitch control.
- **Double whistle** — A composite instrument combining gourd, horn, boiled beads, cotton tassel and string.
- **Dance rattle** — A gourd mounted on a stick, brown in color, used in dance performance.

## Dance & theatre

The documented dramatic tradition centers on masked water monster performers, with both adult and child versions.

- **Water monster dance mask** — A plaited palm-leaf mask with two wooden horns, decorated with urucu-painted bands across the top.
- **Child water monster mask** — A smaller version of the adult mask with horns decorated with deer hooves and red feathers, showing children perform the same figure.

## Festivals & rituals

Log-racing and masked water monster performances are the documented Krahô ceremonies, with children participating in both.

- **Log-racing** — A ceremonial competition evidenced by specially made palm-leaf headbands painted yellow-ochre and purple.
- **Water monster performance** — A masked dance with a plaited palm-leaf mask, wooden horns and gourd rattle, performed by adults and children.

## Foodways

The Krahô historically combined hunting and gathering with shifting cultivation, but cooking methods and dishes are not documented.

- **Hunting** — Game animals including giant armadillo, capybara, deer and macaw provided materials used in whistles, masks and ornaments.
- **Gathered plants** — Gourds, palm leaf, bast, fiber and cotton were used as raw materials for tools and textiles.
- **Tobacco** — The only plant product explicitly mentioned as consumed, stored in a plaited pouch.

## Oral tradition & literature

The water monster is the only figure named in documented Krahô narrative, with no other folktales or storytelling recorded.

- **Water monster** — A figure enacted in masked dance, though its story and meaning are not documented.

## Language & religion

The Krahô speak Krahô (Mehĩ jarkwa), a Timbira dialect; their spiritual beliefs are not documented beyond what masked performance implies.

- **Mehĩ jarkwa** — The Krahô language, a Timbira dialect of the Northern Jê group, spoken in Tocantins.
- **Timbira** — The language family to which Krahô belongs, part of the larger Northern Jê and Macro-Jê groups.

## Glossary

- *urucu* — Red-orange natural pigment used for painting and coloring
- *arara* — Macaw; feathers used in ceremonial head-dresses
- *Mehĩ jarkwa* — The Krahô language
- *Timbira* — A language family of northern Brazil; the Krahô speak a Timbira dialect
- *twill* — A weaving technique creating diagonal patterns in fabric
- *plaiting* — Interlacing strips of material to create textile or basketwork
- *bast* — Fiber from the inner bark of plants, used for textiles and binding
- *boiled beads* — Beads used as ornaments, strung on singing-belts and whistles
- *log-racing* — A Krahô ceremonial competition
- *singing-belt* — A woman's red cotton belt with bead tassels marking a vocal performance role
- *fire fan* — A plaited palm-leaf fan for managing fire
- *gourd* — A dried fruit shell used to make whistles, rattles and containers
- *water monster* — A masked dance figure performed by Krahô adults and children
- *shifting cultivation* — Farming practice where land is cleared, used, then left to recover
- *seminomadic* — Moving camp seasonally while maintaining some permanent settlements
- *Northern Jê* — A language group of indigenous Brazil
