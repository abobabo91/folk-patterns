## Overview

The Warao — also written Waroa, Guarauno, Guarao and Warrau — are an Indigenous Amerindian people of northeastern Venezuela, with smaller communities in Guyana, Trinidad and Tobago, and Suriname. Most live in the Orinoco Delta, a landscape of hundreds of streams, rivulets and marshes where overland movement is impractical and the canoe is the ordinary means of getting anywhere; the name Warao itself translates as "the boat people," after that lifelong connection to the water. Venezuela's 2011 census counted 49,271 Warao, making them the country's second largest Indigenous group after the Wayuu. They speak Warao, an agglutinative language isolate. In folk-culture terms they matter as a delta-adapted people whose material repertoire — dugout canoes, plaited cane and palm-leaf containers, bark-cloth dress, houses raised over water — is built almost entirely from the vegetation of the delta itself.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The museum record for the Warao in this atlas is dominated by plaiting rather than loom weaving, and the catalogue descriptions are the only documentation the sources provide.

**Twilled cane box** — A lidded basketry box in twilled plaited cane with reinforced rims, its lid carrying a decorated snake design named *orolli*. The reinforced rim and the patterned lid distinguish it from the plain utility containers in the same collection.

**Cokeriti-leaf box** — A box with a telescoping lid (a separate over-sleeve that slides down over the body), plaited from *cokeriti* leaf. The catalogue records several of these and notes in each case that they were made by boys, which makes the form a documented instance of basketry learned and practised by children rather than by adult specialists.

**Plaited palm-leaf scoop** — A scoop, catalogued with a query as a strainer, made of plaited palm leaf: an open-work food-processing implement rather than a storage container.

**Bark dress** — An entire dress for a woman made from the bark of a tree, finished with strings of fibre. It is bark-cloth clothing, not woven textile, and is the only garment the sources describe.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Orolli* — a snake design, worked on the lid of a twilled cane box.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe exactly one Warao garment: a complete woman's dress made from tree bark and hung with strings of fibre, collected by the British Museum. Nothing in the material used here distinguishes men's from women's dress beyond that single attribution, names any other garment type, or records head coverings, belts, footwear or a ceremonial costume separate from daily wear.

### Architecture

Warao houses are built on stilts over the water. This was the first thing Europeans recorded about them: soon after Christopher Columbus reached the delta, Alonso de Ojeda navigated the Orinoco upstream and saw the distinctively stilted Warao huts balanced above the river. Similar architecture at Sinamaica, far to the west, had already been likened to Venice with its canals below and buildings above, and that comparison gave the whole country the name Venezuela — "little Venice." The settlement pattern the sources describe is dispersed and riverine: roughly 364 villages in the Venezuelan delta, with Curiapo as the main town and Tucupita as the state capital; about 5,000 Warao in the Guyanese border area, where the main villages are Assakata, Kamwatta Hill, Koriabo and Whitewater; and a minority presence in the Surinamese villages of Cupido and Post Utrecht. Delta infrastructure is minimal, with no sanitation. One further built form is documented visually rather than textually: a platform burial among the Guarauno of the Orinoco Delta, recorded in a woodcut made after a photograph taken in 1881 and now held by the Museum of World Culture.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The signature Warao object is the canoe, and the sources describe two kinds. The *bongo* carries up to five people and is made through a long, deliberate sequence: the heads of each household reach a consensus on which large tree is best, the tree is found and killed at the start of the dry season, felled at the end of it, then hollowed out and flattened using fire together with stone tools traded from the mountains or local shell tools. The heavy work is done by young men, under instructions from the eldest woman of the household, the knowledge passing from her to her daughters and through them to their husbands. The second type seats only three and serves daily travel to and from food sources. Household goods documented in the collections are the plaited containers and the palm-leaf scoop described above. The sources record no Warao ceramics and no metalwork.

## Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used here say nothing about Warao jewelry, body painting, tattooing or hair practices.

## Music & performance

What the sources establish is narrow but specific: the Warao have shamans, and those shamans perform music, including rain dances and songs. No instrument is named, no song genre is named, and no performance occasion beyond shamanic practice is described. The one extended audiovisual document of Warao performance and daily life named in the sources is a feature film rather than a music recording: the 2015 Venezuelan film *Gone with the River*, directed by the Cuban-Venezuelan director Mario Crespo with a cast of nonprofessional actors, which was spoken in Warao and which the sources credit with a detailed look at Warao culture and life along the river. The Museum of Modern Art in New York presented it in April 2017.

## Dance & theatre

The only dance the sources name is the rain dance performed by Warao shamans, together with their songs; the sources describe no masked, dramatic or theatrical tradition. The film by Mario Crespo, whose subject is the choice between remaining with Warao traditions and leaving to be educated among the "Creoles," is the nearest thing to a documented dramatic treatment, and it is cinema rather than an indigenous performance genre.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources give a seasonal rhythm rather than a festival calendar. The clearest communal food event is the crab season: in July and August the Warao feast on crabs as these come into the delta from the beach. The making of a *bongo* is also a seasonal ritual sequence, opening with the household heads' consensus on a tree, the killing of that tree at the start of the dry season, and its felling at the close of the same dry season. Hunting is generally avoided because of cultural taboos — a standing dietary prohibition rather than a calendar observance. For life-cycle rites, the sources document the treatment of the dead only indirectly, through the 1881 image of a platform burial in the delta, and say nothing about birth, naming, coming-of-age or marriage rites. One piece of childhood enculturation is recorded: Warao babies, toddlers and small children are famed for holding tight to their mothers' necks and for paddling, and often learn to swim before they learn to walk. Basketry, as the catalogue of the *cokeriti*-leaf boxes shows, was likewise something boys made.

## Foodways

The Warao diet is varied and weighted toward the products of the delta, above all fish. By 1500 the Warao had acquired basic horticulture, and many of the fruits and vegetables eaten daily come from the domesticated and cultivated palm orchards of the delta. The *moriche* palm supplies grubs, which are eaten occasionally, and the pith of the palm was formerly eaten in its own right; contemporary practice is more likely to add it as an extra ingredient to cassava bread. Crabs are the seasonal delicacy of July and August, taken as they move into the delta from the beach. Against this, hunting is generally avoided on account of cultural taboos, so terrestrial game plays little part in the diet even though the founding narrative of the people is a hunter's story. The sources name no beverage tradition, no ceremonial dish and no cooking technique beyond the preparation of cassava bread with palm pith.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Warao account of their own origin, as they report it, begins in a sky world inhabited by men and birds but no other animals. The primordial hunter, shooting at these heavenly birds with bow and arrow, struck one in mid-air; the bird fell, hit the heavenly floor and burst through it, continuing down through the clouds toward the terrestrial land below. The hunter looked through the hole it had made, saw lush and fertile land with abundant game and fruit, and resolved to descend: he tied a long rope of heavenly cotton to a tree, threw it through the hole, and lowered himself to what is now Earth, forsaking his sky world. The Warao descend from him. Beyond this narrative, the sources note that Warao oral traditions concerning Trinidad were among the evidence Arie Boomert used in arguing for a formerly wider Waroid distribution, and that Johannes Wilbert published collections of Warao oral material. Warao-language cinema, in the 2015 film, is the one contemporary medium the sources record.

## Language & religion

Warao — also called Guarauno, Guarao and Warrau — is an agglutinative language isolate spoken by about 33,000 people, with an estimated 28,100 speakers in Venezuela as of 2007, and UNESCO considers it endangered. It is unrelated to any recorded language in the region or elsewhere; Terrence Kaufman placed it in a hypothetical Macro-Paezan family in 1994, but the supporting work was never done, and a proposed wider "Waroid" family remains contested. Its most unusual feature is object–subject–verb basic word order, very rare among nominative–accusative languages. The consonant inventory is small, and there are five oral and five nasal vowels. Religious life as documented here rests on the primordial-hunter descent narrative and on shamans, who perform rain dances and songs. The sources record no church affiliation or syncretic cult.
