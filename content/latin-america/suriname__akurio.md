## Overview

The Akurio, also called Akuriyo, are an Indigenous hunter-gatherer people living in Suriname. They were nomadic and had a predilection for honey-gathering, using stone tools for this activity. The group was first contacted by outsiders in 1937 during Conrad Carel Käyser’s expedition to the Oelemari River, and Willem Ahlbrinck contacted them again in 1938. They were rediscovered in 1969 by Ivan Schoen, a Protestant missionary. In 1975, American missionaries persuaded the group to live in Pelelu Tepu. The population was estimated at 50 in 2000 and 40 in 2012. Their language, Akurio, is an extinct Cariban language belonging to the Taranoan subgroup and was replaced by Tiriyó.

The sources identify stone tools among the objects held by the Akurio, stating that these were typically employed for honey-gathering.

## Foodways

The sources state that the Akurio had a predilection for honey-gathering and that their stone tools were typically used for this endeavor.

## Language & religion

Akurio, also known as Akuriyó or Awayaikuletongo, was a Cariban language belonging to the Taranoan subgroup. Tiriyó was its closest language among the languages named in the sources, and Carijona was another Taranoan language spoken in Colombia. The Akurio people used Akurio until the late twentieth century, after which they switched to Tiriyó. The last native speakers were reported to have died in 2002; only three people could remember the language in 2012, while another account states that by 2012 only two semi-speakers remained.
