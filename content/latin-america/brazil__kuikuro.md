## Overview

The Kuikuro are an Indigenous people from the Mato Grosso region of Brazil, associated with the Xingu Indigenous Park and villages along the Culuene River. Their language belongs to the Cariban language family and is closely related to the Kalapalo, Nahukuá, and Matipu languages. The Kuikuro population was 592 in 2010, compared with 450 in 2002. They have many similarities with other Xingu tribes. Archaeological research connects them with the settlements known as Kuhikugu, located at the headwaters of the Xingu River and inhabited from around 1,500 years ago to about 400 years ago. Their folk-culture significance in the sources lies especially in their language, oral narratives, shamanic beliefs, and connection with the archaeological history of the Xingu region.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Archaeological studies cited in the sources state that, by AD 1400, Indigenous villages in the Xingu region had reached great proportions. These settlements included buildings, palisades, bridges, entry gates, and roads whose bridges and roads had congruent angles to one another. The Kuikuro are likely descendants of the people who built the settlements known to archaeologists as Kuhikugu, situated at the headwaters of the Xingu River.

## Music & performance

They do record an extensive audio-preservation project associated with linguist Bruna Franchetto. Franchetto collected recordings of stories told in Kuikuro with transcriptions, and a library of Kuikuro recordings was created in collaboration with the Kuikuro community. These materials are permanently archived at the Archive of the Indigenous Languages of Latin America. The Kuikuro language article also mentions an Archive of Kalapalo Recordings by Ellen Basso, containing audio recordings of narratives, instrumental music, and text transcriptions from AILLA, as well as the Kuikuro Collection by Bruna Franchetto, consisting of audio recordings in Kuikuro with text transcriptions.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe Kuikuro religious beliefs and shamanic initiation rituals. Kuikuro religion combines Shamanist and healing beliefs. Traditional narratives explain how and why things exist, including the belief that Giti, identified as the Sun, and Alukuma, identified as the Moon, created the world. The sources also describe beliefs concerning the ancestors of the sun and moon and their creation.

Itseke are beings believed to live in the waters and in the forest and to bring illness and death. Shamans serve as religious leaders and healers and can connect with the Itseke, whereas ordinary people cannot. Becoming a shaman involves many rituals and initiations, during which the initiate must remain in a state of seclusion. Shamans can diagnose diseases and identify causes of natural disasters and theft in the village.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral tradition is central to the sources’ account of Kuikuro history and belief. Kuikuro narratives remember Karl von den Steinen’s 1884 expedition; Steinen is known in Kuikuro narratives as Kalusi, described as “the first white man to come in peace.” Kuikuro oral history extends further back than Steinen, to accounts of an earlier European man who visited the Xingu. These people were described as unlike Steinen because they captured and killed Indigenous people and were known as bandeirantes.

The sources also describe stories of Kuhikugu, the settlements associated with the ancestors of the Kuikuro. These stories may have inspired the British explorer Percy Fawcett during his ill-fated expedition in the 1920s in search of the “Lost City of Z.” In contemporary documentation, Bruna Franchetto recorded Kuikuro stories with transcriptions. The recordings are archived at the Archive of the Indigenous Languages of Latin America, and some educational materials have been produced by Kuikuro teachers.

## Language & religion

Kuikúro, also known as Amonap, Upper Xingu Cariban, and Apalakiri, is a Cariban language spoken by the Kuikuro and Kalapalo peoples of Brazil. It is spoken in seven villages along the Culuene River in the Xingu Indigenous Park of Mato Grosso. The Kuikuro language is one of four dialects of southern Karib, alongside Kalapalo, Nahukuá, and Matipu. As of 2006, the sources estimate 1,100 native speakers, including 600 Kuikúro and 500 Kalapálo, who speak the same language but are ethnically distinct.

Kuikuro remains in use across all aspects of Kuikuro life except communication with other tribes. Portuguese has become more necessary through intermarriage, increased access to television, and trade with the outside world. Men and tribal leaders most often know Portuguese, while Portuguese is rarer among women, although this has been changing. An orthography has been developed for Kuikuro and other Upper Xingu languages to train Indigenous teachers and create educational materials. Kuikuro is an agglutinative language with complex morphology, head-final structure, dependent marking, and an ergative case system. Its religion combines Shamanist and healing beliefs.
