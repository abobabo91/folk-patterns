## Overview

The Ofayé, also spelled Opaié or Ofaié, are an Indigenous people of Central Brazil. They live along the Paraná River, near the mouth of the Sucuriú River and the headwaters of the Ivinhema and Vacaria Rivers. Most Ofayé live in an Indigenous territory near Brasilândia, in Mato Grosso do Sul. Their traditional lands extended between the Paraná River, the Serra de Maracaju, the Sucuriú River, and the Verde River. Historically, they were hunter-gatherers and fishers who moved in small bands when hunting or fishing became scarce. Their social organization was matrilineal and uxorilocal. The Ofayé language belongs to the Macro-Jê family and is severely endangered. The sources describe approximately sixty Ofayé today, although many belong to other Indigenous ethnicities or no longer speak the language.

## Material culture

### Architecture

They do record that the Ofayé traditionally lived in mobile bands rather than permanent settlements and that, after displacement, communities occupied camps and settlements in several locations, including along the Verde River and the Paraná River.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The historical record does mention tape recorders in an account of an Ofayé woman shown speaking while believing that the device played the voices of her deceased parents, but this is not presented as an Ofayé cultural object.

## Music & performance

One account states that an Ofayé woman in São Paulo was shown speaking to a tape recorder during a television broadcast, but the source presents this as a case of misidentification and media representation rather than as a musical tradition.

The sources do record a political movement called *Ofaié: Nós ainda estamos vivos*, translated in the source as “Ofayé: We Are Still Alive.” The movement developed after another hydroelectric plant was built on the Paraná River. The community organized protests in Campo Grande and gained national media coverage. This is the only named public performance or collective political expression described in the supplied material.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do mention Ofayé burial grounds. During the period when the Hoffig family consolidated landholdings around the Verde River, those burial grounds were destroyed with heavy machinery. No further details about funerary practice are supplied.

The sources describe political protest as an important form of collective action in the modern period. Following the development of another hydroelectric plant on the Paraná River, the Ofayé organized *Ofaié: Nós ainda estamos vivos* and staged protests in Campo Grande. International pressure was followed by formal recognition of Ofayé land rights and ethnic survival by INCRA, FUNAI, and the Mato Grosso do Sul state government.

## Foodways

They do state that the Ofayé historically lived as hunter-gatherers and fishers, moving when hunting or fishing became scarce. In the present reserve in Brasilândia, hunting and fishing are described as not feasible.

The supplied material also records food-related hardship after displacement. Ofayé who migrated to the Serra de Maracaju faced starvation, epidemics, inter-tribal clashes, and violence from ranchers and corrupt SPI agents. The community that temporarily lived along the Paraná River suffered from tuberculosis, malnutrition, and river pollution caused by neighboring estates. No named food tradition is given.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources do document efforts to preserve and study the Ofayé language. Grammatical descriptions were made by the Pankararú linguist Maria das Dores de Oliveira, as well as by Sarah C. Gudschinsky and Jennifer E. da Silva of the Universidade Federal do Mato Grosso do Sul. The language is described as severely endangered, and language revitalization efforts are underway.

## Language & religion

Ofayé, also called Opaye, Ofaié-Xavante, or Opaié-Shavante, is a Macro-Jê language spoken in Mato Grosso do Sul, Brazil. It forms an independent branch of the language family. The supplied sources report that only two people were recorded speaking it in 2005, while the language article states that it is spoken by only three Ofayé people. The language was spoken on the Ivinhema River, Pardo River, and Nhandú River. Guachi, spoken on the Vacaria River, is identified as a dialect. The sources mention lexical similarities with Macro-Mataguayo-Guaykuru languages resulting from language contact.

They do record that Capuchin missionaries petitioned the São Paulo State Congress in 1912 for land in the Ribeirão das Marrecas valley to proselytize the Ofayé and Kaiowá.
