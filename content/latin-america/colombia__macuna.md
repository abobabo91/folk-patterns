## Overview

The Macuna, also called Buhágana, are a Tucanoan-speaking group of the eastern Amazon basin. They live around the confluence of the Pira Paraná River and Apaporis river, in Colombia’s Vaupés Department and Brazil’s state of Amazonas. There are no reliable census data; the entire population was estimated at some 600 individuals in 1991, compared with 400 in 1973, including 450 living in Colombia. Macuna is also known as Wahana. The sources describe a society whose subsistence, economic history and territorial life are connected to swidden-cultivation, hunting, fishing, gathering forest products, trade and contact with rubber patrons, coca growers and gold miners.

## Material culture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that contact with coca growers and gold miners brought substantial quantities of trade goods and money to indigenous people in the region.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources mention a first Catholic mission established in the area in the 1960s and intermittent contact with missionaries since at least the 18th century, but they provide no further description of religious ceremonies or ritual practice.

## Foodways

Macuna subsistence is based essentially on swidden-cultivation, hunting, fishing and gathering forest products. Manioc is the staple, while plantains, sweet potatoes, bananas and sugarcane are also cultivated. Meat comes from game including pacas, tapir, peccaries, large birds, monkeys and caimans.

## Oral tradition & literature

Little is known about the early history of the Macuna beyond spoken accounts of a violent past with southern neighbors, especially the Yauna and Tanimuka Indians. The sources mention the first Macuna references in Portuguese accounts of the 18th century.

## Language & religion

Macuna, also known as Buhagana and Wahana, is a Tucanoan language of Colombia and Brazil. They record intermittent missionary contact since at least the 18th century and the establishment of the first Catholic mission in the area in the 1960s.
