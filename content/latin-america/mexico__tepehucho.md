## Overview

The source identifies the people as the Tepehuán, an Indigenous people of Mexico living in Northwestern, Western, and parts of North-Central Mexico. Their territory includes the Sierra Madre Occidental, the Valley of Guadiana in Durango, southern Chihuahua, eastern Sinaloa, and northern Jalisco, Nayarit, and Zacatecas. The Tepehuán language has three branches: Northern Tepehuan, Southeastern Tepehuan, and Southwestern Tepehuan. The people are divided into the Ódami, Audam, and O'dam, who have distinct languages, cultures, and beliefs. The northern Tepehuán numbered 18,249 in 2005, the southeastern 10,600, and the southwestern 8,700. Their folk culture includes weaving, basketry, wooden carving, music, ritual dancing, mitotes, shaman-curers, and ceremonies combining Catholic and older Tepehuan practices.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Kutum* and *sawira* are Northern Tepehuán names for blankets. The source describes them as having simple decoration in colorful thread, especially along hems and folds.

Domestic wool is woven into blankets and other household items. Purchased cloth is also sewn into clothing and household goods.

Women's satin-like fabrics are decorated with lace and colored ribbons.

Basket and mat weaving are practiced among the Ódami.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday men's clothing commonly consists of jeans, a shirt, a cowboy hat, and sandals. Traditional men's clothing consists of a shirt, pants, and blankets. In Northern Tepehuán, the blankets are called *kutum* and *sawira*. In some communities, colorful thread decorates hems and folds, and in San Francisco de Ocotán multicolored tissues may be attached from the hem of the pants to the knee.

The traditional hat, called *bonam*, is made of soyate fabric and has a circular shape. Leather three-hole *Susak* huaraches are now uncommon, although their use is mandatory in ceremonies such as the mitote in some communities.

Women's dress consists of a skirt or springcity, a long-sleeved blouse, and an apron around the waist. Satin-like fabrics, lace, and colored ribbons are used. Brightly colored long socks and plastic shoes are widespread. Women also wear long hair combs, beaded necklaces, earrings, and other accessories. Men and women use traditional bags.

### Architecture

Among the Ódami, settlements include small named communities called *rancherías*, surrounding *pueblos* or small towns that serve as social and political centers. A ranchería consists of separate dwellings of four or five families. In the *tierra templada*, houses are constructed of timber in small clusters on mesas. In the *tierra caliente*, houses are made of stone-and-mud mortar and are usually located along streams leading into canyons.

Among the O'dam and Audam, each *Comunidad* is a territorial and political unit. Its central town serves as a religious and political center for surrounding *anexos* and rancherías. These towns contain public and administrative buildings, a church or chapel, a school, and a community kitchen.

A typical Southern Tepehuan dwelling is rectangular, has two rooms, and stands on a platform of prepared earth. Its walls are made of stone and adobe, and its roof is thatched with grass. One room is used for cooking and the other for sleeping.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Ódami crafts include baskets, mats, ropes, hats, small violins, bowls, utensils, bows and arrows, sandals, sleeping mats, carrying baskets, canteens, bowls, and dippers. Common gourds are used for canteens, bowls, and dippers. Cooking pots are made from clay.

Skins of various animals are used for sandals, sleeping mats, carrying baskets, and other useful objects. Wooden plows and dibble sticks are associated with farming. Among the O'dam and Audam, pottery is still made but is described as mostly functional and undecorated.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Women wear beaded necklaces, earrings, long hair combs, and other accessories. Clothing is enriched with these forms of adornment, while colored ribbons and lace decorate women's garments.

## Music & performance

Music is important in Tepehuan life. Among the Ódami, old Spanish matachines tunes, songs with Tepehuan themes sung in Tepehuan, and popular Spanish-Mexican songs are performed at dances and fiestas. Instruments include homemade violins, gourd rattles, ankle rattles, reed flutes, rasping sticks, and drums.

Jesuit accounts describe rasping sticks, rattles, and reed or ceramic flutes as precolonial instruments used during dances and ceremonies. These instruments remain part of ceremonial music for the mitote. The musical bow is played on a gourd sounder. Drums and violins, the latter described as an instrument of Spanish origin, accompany corridos and other popular Mexican songs at fiestas.

Clay pipes and incense burners similar to pre-Spanish objects are sometimes used by curers in healing rituals. Oral tradition is performed by adult members of communities through folklore. The source mentions animal tales of regional origin and local versions of familiar tales of Old World derivation.

## Dance & theatre

The source describes Catholic pastoral dramas performed during Christmas, Holy Week, and the October fiestas of San Francisco. These dramas were introduced by the Jesuits in colonial times. The fiestas have an urban, mestizo phase and a Tepehuan phase, with the two groups sometimes working together.

Ritual activities include the defense and destruction of a figure of Judas. Groups called *fariseos* engage in sham battles. Among the Southern Tepehuan, the standard *matachines* are danced during Christian holy days, including Easter, the Feast of the Virgin of Guadalupe, Christmas, and village saints' days.

The mitote includes a grand display of ritual dancing on its fifth night.

## Festivals & rituals

Northern Tepehuán communities hold fiestas in which stewards are elected a year in advance. They gather funds for decorations and for the slaughter of beef offered to the commemorated saint.

Ódami Catholic pastoral dramas take place during Christmas, Holy Week, and the October fiestas of San Francisco. Tepehuan and mestizo participants may work together. Ceremonies led by the shaman ask for good crops, show reverence for the dead, and petition for the physical well-being of people and animals. Festivities include dancing, offerings of food before a cross, and tesguino.

The Southern Tepehuan celebrate Easter, the Feast of the Virgin of Guadalupe on 12 December, Christmas, and village saints' days. The *elote* First Fruits Fiesta occurs in early October. Fresh maize cannot be eaten until the festival has taken place. It is a thanksgiving ceremony and is one of the practices distinguishing Tepehuan culture from mestizo culture in Durango.

Fertility and thanksgiving ceremonies are called *Mitotes* in Spanish and *Xiotahl* in Tepehuan. They include five days of fasting and prayer, followed on the fifth night by ritual dancing. At sunrise, celebrants break their fast with food placed as offerings at the east end of the dance platform, on an altar dedicated to the rising sun. Mitotes are held on average three times a year according to the agricultural cycle: to seek protection from the dry winter, bless spring sowing, and give thanks for the fall harvest. A special Mitote may ask for rain during drought. The *jefe del patio* organizes and leads the ceremonies. In May and October, *xiotahl* festivals can ritually induct newborn children into an *apellido* group and recognize young adults of 15 years of age as adults of the group.

Among the Ódami, ceremonies may include a Sacred Pipe ritual. People form a circle around a fire and provide tobacco for the pipe. The source says the Sacred Pipe was or is used to make treaties with other tribes, prepare for war, or please the gods.

## Foodways

Ódami households grow maize, squashes, and beans as staple crops. Wheat, barley, potatoes, oats, and peas are also grown, while tobacco and chilies are cultivated in the lowlands. Gathering remains important: wild fruits, piñon nuts, walnuts, edible acorns, crude honey, insects, reptiles, grubs, and occasional rattlesnake are among the gathered foods. Deer and wild turkeys are prized game.

Chickens, turkeys, and pigs provide additional food. Sheep and goats are valued for their wool and as food during fiestas. In the highlands, figs, pomegranates, peaches, and apples are grown; orange and lemon trees occur in the hot canyon lands.

Among the O'dam and Audam, maize, beans, and two kinds of squash are traditional crops and dietary staples. The diet includes tortillas, beans, cheese, and other farm products requiring no irrigation, supplemented by gathered foods.

*Tesguino* is maize beer. It is used in curing and blessing and has communal functions. Fiestas include an ample supply of tesguino. Mestizo-influenced fiestas are described as occasions for revelry and mescal drinking.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ódami oral tradition is carried on by adult members of communities through spirited performances of folklore. The stories include animal tales of regional origin and local versions of familiar tales derived from the Old World.

Ódami mythology includes the Cocotyomes, a group of giants who ate children. The source also records supernatural figures and beings connected with hunting, death, wind, and the mountains.

## Language & religion

The Tepehuán language has three branches: Northern Tepehuan, Southeastern Tepehuan, and Southwestern Tepehuan. Ódami, O'dam, and Audam are endonyms associated with the Northern, Southeastern, and Southwestern groups. O'dam and Audam belong to the Tepiman branch of the Uto-Aztecan language family, as does Ódami.

Tepehuan religious life combines Catholic beliefs with older ceremonies, practices, myths, and deities. Among the Ódami, a creator called “God Our Father” is accompanied by other ancient deities. The Lord of the Deer is named *Kukúduli*, and *Úgai* is a spirit appearing as a light in the sky after death. A mountain god takes the form of an owl, and another spirit is master of the wind.

The shaman-curer is called *bajadios*, “he who brings God down.” The shaman diagnoses and heals illness, sees the unseen, and communicates with the supernatural through séances and dreams. O'dam and Audam religious life separately maintains Catholic and older practices. Dios Padre is associated with the sun, Jesús Nazareno with the moon, and Madre María with figures including the Virgin of Guadalupe.
