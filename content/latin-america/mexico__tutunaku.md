## Overview

The Totonac are an Indigenous people of Mexico who reside in the states of Veracruz, Puebla, and Hidalgo. Approximately 90,000 Totonac speakers reside in the region. Their traditional language, Totonac, belongs with Tepehua to a small language family and is not related to the Mayan, Oto-Manguean, or Uto-Aztecan families. Totonac has several local varieties that are not mutually intelligible, including Papantla Totonac, North-Central Totonac, South-Central Totonac, and Misantla Totonac. The Totonacs are associated with the pre-Columbian city of El Tajín and with Cempoala and Teayo, which are identified in one proposed interpretation of *toto-nacu* as “three hearts.” Their cultivation and production of vanilla, especially in Papantla, played a major role in their society and economy.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no Totonac motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Totonac women were expert weavers and embroiderers. Women wore skirts, with embroidered skirts associated with noble women, and a small triangular poncho covering the breasts. Noble women wore shell and jade necklaces and earrings, and they often tattooed their faces with red ink. Married women wore their hair in the Nahuatl fashion, while peasant women wore their hair long. Women also braided their hair with feathers, and a contemporary description characterized their appearance as “quite elegant.”

Noble men dressed in multicolored cloaks and loin cloths. They wore necklaces, arm bands, lip plugs, and devices made of prized quetzal feathers. Their hair was kept long, with a thick tuft on top tied with a ribbon.

### Architecture

Totonac houses were generally rectangular, thatched, and built with an overhang.

In the lowlands, Totonac society was mostly based on semi-isolated familial units rather than urban spaces or villages, with exceptions including El Tajín and Cempoala. At the time of Independence, only 11 Totonac villages existed, primarily serving as “important locuses of communal culture.”

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do state that there was a total absence of comals, metates, and manos, and connect this absence with the claim that Totonacs did not eat tortillas.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Noble Totonac women wore shell and jade necklaces and earrings, and often tattooed their faces with red ink. Women braided their hair with feathers. Noble men wore necklaces, arm bands, lip plugs, and devices made of prized quetzal feathers. Men also kept their hair long, with a thick tuft tied with a ribbon.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name **Danza de los Voladores de Papantla**. No other Totonac dance, theatre, mask, puppet, or dramatic tradition is covered.

## Festivals & rituals

Most present-day Totonacs are Roman Catholic, but Christian practice is often mixed with vestiges of traditional religion. One notable example is *la Costumbre*, described as the survival of an old rite of sacrifice. In this practice, various seeds are mixed with earth and the blood of fowls and dispersed over planting fields.

Mother goddesses played an important role in Totonac belief because each person’s soul was understood to be made by them. When a newly born child died, its soul was said not to go west, the place of the dead, but east with the Mothers. The sources also preserve a myth concerning a maize deity, described as a culture hero with counterparts among most other cultures of the Gulf Coast and possibly represented by the Classic Maya maize god.

Traditional curers were believed to be born during a storm under the protection of thunder. According to the account, a lightning bolt could strike the house of a newborn baby and place the child under its possession. Other named deities include Chichiní, identified as the sun, and Aktzin.

## Foodways

Totonac agriculture included maize, manioc, squash, beans, pumpkin, and chili peppers. The region was also noted for cotton production. Totonacs cultivated vanilla before the arrival of Columbus in the New World, and until the mid-19th century they were described as the world’s main producers of vanilla. In the twentieth century, Totonacs were associated with growing the highest-quality vanilla, and most Mexican vanilla was produced by Totonacs.

Vanilla became an important export good in the early-eighteenth century. Totonacs traded it with other Totonacs and with people in northern Oaxaca. In 1743, the mayor of Papantla attempted to regulate the harvesting of Mexican vanilla for personal profit. Another law was promulgated in 1767 after Totonac vanilla growers in Colipa complained about the theft of immature vanilla pods. During Humboldt’s travels in Mexico, Totonacs in the Misantla region harvested about 700,000 vanilla beans per year.

The sources state that Totonacs ate fruit, especially zapotes, guavas, papayas, plantains, and avocados. Men hunted and fished shark, turtle, deer, armadillo, opossums, and frogs. Women raised turkeys and dogs. The morning meal was corn porridge. Lunch was the main meal and consisted of manioc, bean stew, or, for nobles, a rich meat sauce. Fish, seafood, and game were eaten by nobles and farmers. The agave provided liquor.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe a Totonac myth concerning a maize deity, understood as a culture hero with counterparts among most other cultures of the Gulf Coast and possibly represented by the Classic Maya maize god. The sources also record beliefs concerning Mother goddesses, the destination of the souls of deceased children, and traditional curers protected by thunder.

The traditional religion was described in the early 1960s by the French ethnographer Alain Ichon.

## Language & religion

The Totonac people traditionally speak Totonac, which forms a small language family with Tepehua. Totonacan languages are not related to the Mayan, Oto-Manguean, or Uto-Aztecan families. Several local varieties are not mutually intelligible. Papantla Totonac is spoken by some 80,000 speakers in El Escolín, Papantla, Cazones, Tajín, Espinal, and other towns along the Gulf Coast of Veracruz. North-Central Totonac is spoken between Poza Rica in Veracruz and Mecapalapa, Pantepec, and Xicotepec de Juárez in Puebla. South-Central Totonac is spoken mostly in the Sierra Norte de Puebla, including Zapotitlán de Méndez, Coatepec, and Huehuetla. Misantla Totonac is spoken by fewer than 500 people in Yecuatla and other communities outside Misantla, Veracruz.

Most present-day Totonacs are Roman Catholic. Their Christian practice includes vestiges of traditional religion, including *la Costumbre*.
