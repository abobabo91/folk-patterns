## Overview

The Rarámuri, called Tarahumara by the Spanish from the 1500s onward, are an Indigenous people of the state of Chihuahua in northern Mexico. Once spread across much of Chihuahua, they withdrew on the arrival of Spanish colonizers in the 16th century into the high sierras and canyons of the Sierra Madre Occidental — including the Copper Canyon — and the part of that range they now occupy is often called the Sierra Rarámuri. Estimates put their number in 2006 at between 50,000 and 70,000. Their language belongs to the Uto-Aztecan family and is the second most widely spoken member of it after Nahuatl. In folk-culture terms they are known above all for prayer and communication through long-distance running, for a religion that reworked Catholic figures into Indigenous ones with little outside supervision, and for the brewing and sharing of maize beer that organizes nearly every collective occasion.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Rarámuri weaving in the collections shown here is overwhelmingly narrow-band work: sashes and belts whose patterning is carried entirely in the warp, so the design appears as lengthwise bands, lozenges and waves rather than as a woven-in picture.

**Natépare** — A broad woven wool sash. Documented examples set geometric figures, including zigzags, in dark brown or black against undyed white, and frame them with a border that runs the whole length of the band in red, black, orange and blue; both ends finish in a fringe of interlaced warp threads. The wool is handspun and the belts are long enough to wrap the body several times.

**Faja** — The wool belt type in its wider form, with parallel bands of brown, orange, blue, red, yellow or green flanking a central field in brown and white that carries an undulating wave pattern, sometimes alternating straight and wavy stripes toward the ends. Some examples combine undyed handspun wool warps with commercial synthetic plied yarns in blue, red, pink and green — a sign of how industrial thread entered an otherwise domestic technique.

**Ceñidor** — The narrow cotton band, woven in mercerized cotton rather than wool: a single coloured stripe on each side of a centre field of alternating transverse blocks (white and green, yellow and green, red and green, blue and orange). The warps at both ends are gathered into two plaited fringes that end in a tassel.

**Tira** — The museum term used for these woven bands as a class, recorded alongside *faja* and *ceñidor* for the same objects, indicating that one narrow-band technique served several belt widths and functions.

**Telar** — The band loom itself was collected as an object. A British Museum man's sash is described as ring-woven on a rigid horizontal loom with handspun wool, with the end of the weaving above the fringe reinforced by a line of twining.

**Motif vocabulary.** Warp-patterned diamond or lozenge in the centre field, usually brown and white; zigzag; a central wave or undulating line; alternating transverse blocks read as a checkerboard; parallel lengthwise stripes framing the centre field; a continuous border band running the full length; plaited or interlaced warp fringes finished with tassels.

### Clothing & dress

The dress represented in collections is built around the loose cotton blouse, *blusa*, made for men, women and children alike, and the woven belt. A man's blouse of red cotton printed with small yellow, blue and purple flowers has a round neck without a collar, a vertical band down the centre of the chest from neck to below the waist with the fabric turned reverse-side out, pleats on either side of that band, balloon sleeves, and white buttons at the neck opening and cuffs. Another is cut from unbleached sheeting with a shoulder yoke, cuffs and hem binding of the same red floral cotton, pleats below the yoke front and back, and a purple button moulded with a flower. Children's blouses follow the adult pattern in orange or pink cotton with contrasting yokes, cuffs, short standing collars and hem bindings, fastened with mismatched buttons and snaps. Over these go the *natépare* and *faja* sashes. Footwear is the *huarache*, the sandal worn for running, which scientific and journalistic writing on Rarámuri endurance has made a byword in minimalist-running circles. Ceremonial dress is distinct: *matachín* dancers appear lavishly dressed in colorful attire that observers have compared to North African garments.

### Architecture

The sources are thin on building form. Most Rarámuri still practice a traditional lifestyle that includes inhabiting natural shelters — caves and cliff overhangs — in the canyon country, and almost all households move from one place to another over the course of a year, many of them practising transhumance with cattle, sheep and goats. The structures the sources do name are built collectively: granaries, houses and corrals raised by neighbours working together, the work party itself being one of the occasions for which maize beer is brewed. The other built landscape in Rarámuri territory is the mission: Juan Fonte's San Pablo Balleza at the southern edge of the territory, later foundations at Las Bocas, Huejotitán, San Felipe and Satevó, the mission of San Francisco de Borja destroyed in the 1648 war, and the Spanish Villa de Aguilar planted in the heart of the upper country afterward. Nothing in the sources used describes roof types, wall construction or architectural ornament.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Rarámuri pottery is lightly fired and fire-stained earthenware. The recurring form is a round pot with a flat bottom tapering to a short vertical neck, sometimes with a looped handle on either side and a separate lid shaped like an onion, pierced through the top; decoration is painted directly on the body — white flowers around the neck and white dots on the lid, or orange flowers with an orange band and zigzag line at the rim — and one example has large incised lines across the base. Museum records keep the Spanish names for the vessel classes: *jarro* for the bellied jar, *vasija* for a clay vessel generally, and *jícara* for a wide fired-clay bowl with traces of red painted decoration, heavily sooted on the outside, which its collector reported was used for roasting maize. Basketry is equally characteristic: double-walled baskets plaited from pine needles with a square base flaring out to a rounded rim, double-walled basketry mugs with serrated lips and a looped handle, and a lidded double-walled basket plaited from yucca or sotol fibre.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment in these collections is glass-bead work, recorded under Spanish names. *Collar de chaquira* describes a bundle of eight bead necklaces, each in several colours, bound together with a brown-red cotton band. A single *collar* is strung as a long repeating sequence: a run of white beads, then five red and five blue, then white again. *Aretes de chaquira*, bead earrings, combine red, blue and white beads terminating in a larger angular white bead, hung from white-metal fittings. Beyond beadwork the sources mention metal only through an individual: Xemexe Tierrablanca is listed among notable Rarámuri as a silversmith, photographer and musician. The sources used say nothing about tattooing, body painting or hair practices.

## Music & performance

Music is not an occasional art among the Rarámuri but a frame for ordinary conduct; the classical pianist Romayne Wheeler, who has written about them, puts it as "Music sanctifies the moment in the life of all the Tarahumaras" and "All of our actions have musical meaning." The instrument at the centre of it is the violin, played through the end-of-year cycle and masterfully carved but left unvarnished. Collected examples are carved of wood with an hour-glass body, f-holes cut on either side of the strings, a bridge and two wire strings fastened to pegs at the head; the Metropolitan Museum records one under the Rarámuri name *raberi*, made of yellow pine with horsehair. The pieces played in this season are known as *matachín* tunes and accompany dancing, with rattles called *sáuraka*. During Lent the instruments change: three-holed flutes of river cane are played together with drums. Also collected are *matraca* rattles — a stick handle with a cog near the end and a perpendicular piece of wood whose flap rests on the cog, the wood decorated with burnt S-shapes or burnt circles around the edge — classed in the museum record as religious and ritual equipment. At the drinking gatherings described below, the music is fiddles, flutes, drums and guitars. Rarámuri-language radio is broadcast by station XETAR from Guachochi, Chihuahua.

## Dance & theatre

Music and dance are described as highly integrated into Rarámuri social life, and the named dance tradition is the ***matachín***: dancers in elaborate, brightly coloured costume moving to violin tunes during the end-of-year cycle, accompanied by *sáuraka* rattles. Dancing also belongs to the beer-centred gatherings, where it goes on alongside the fiddles, flutes, drums and guitars. The other patterned public performances are competitive rather than theatrical. In *rarajipari* — translated as 'foot throwing' — teams of men kick a wooden ball as they run, relaying it to the next runner while teammates move ahead to the following relay point; races run from a few hours to a couple of days without a break. Women's races use a stick and hoop. Collections hold the equipment: wooden balls carved for use in a throwing game, catalogued as game-balls and sporting equipment. Footraces appear in the sources under the names *rarajípare* and *ariwete*. The sources used do not describe a masked or puppet theatre.

## Festivals & rituals

The Rarámuri ceremonial year is organized around the fermented maize drink *batári* (Spanish *tesgüino*) and the gatherings called *tesgüinadas*. These include rain fiestas, harvest ceremonies, curing fiestas, the Guadalupe Fiesta, Holy Week, races and Sunday gatherings. Many fall in the winter post-harvest season, when sharing beer between neighbouring communities renews social ties; rain and harvest ceremonies fall within the farming months to secure the crop. Others arise directly out of collective labour — the work parties called *nateba*, when neighbours help one family with its fields or raise a granary, house or corral. Each event requires a shaman, a *curandero* or a chanter: the *curandero* diagnoses and heals the sick, while chanters lead the community in the chants and rhythms that carry the ceremony. The religious obligations around the drink are precise. An *olla* of *batári* must be dedicated to Onorúame before anyone drinks from it, and during a curing ceremony the *olla* rests in front of a cross until the rite ends; some rituals run as long as 48 hours. The one life-cycle rite the sources name is a coming-of-age: at fourteen a boy is allowed to drink *batári* for the first time, after a short sermon about his responsibilities as a man. The anthropologist John Kennedy called the whole institution the "tesgüino network" and reckoned that the average Tarahumara spends at least 100 days a year directly concerned with the drink or under its influence and aftereffects; because the Rarámuri are shy and private, these occasions also serve as social lubricant between husbands and wives.

## Foodways

The staples are maize, beans, greens, squash and tobacco, with chilli, potatoes, tomatoes and sweet potatoes in more Mexicanized districts. Corn is planted in February and March with oxen, often borrowed since not every household owns one; it flowers in August and is harvested by November, then cooked or stored. The standard corn dishes are *pinole*, tortillas, *atole*, *tamales*, and ears boiled or roasted — and a wide fired-clay bowl, *jícara*, in the collection was used specifically for roasting maize. Beans, essential for protein, are boiled and then fried; beans and tamales are the food carried on journeys. Wheat and fruit — apples, apricots, figs, oranges — came in with the missionaries and remain minor. Meat is less than a twentieth of the diet, mostly fish, chicken and squirrel, with cattle, sheep and goats killed on ceremonial occasions; deer and wild turkey are taken by persistence hunting, followed at a steady pace for a day or two until the animal drops. *Batári* is brewed year-round from sprouted corn — malted, spread in a shallow basket under pine needles and kept moist for four or five days until the starch has turned to sugar, mashed and boiled for eight hours, then combined with a fire-fermented herb paste and left three to four days more. It is drunk soon after, since it can spoil within a day. When corn is scarce the same drink is made from still-green stalks, in which case it is called *paciki*, or from cactus fruit, shrubs, wheat and trees. The dietary researcher William Connors linked the traditional diet to a low incidence of diseases such as Type 2 diabetes; where processed goods have replaced the staples, that advantage is eroding.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used say little about Rarámuri narrative art, and nothing about an epic cycle. What they document is the ceremonial speech register: chanters who lead a community in chants and rhythms through a fiesta, and the sermon delivered to a boy at his first drink. One stylistic convention of Rarámuri storytelling is recorded in linguistic description — narrators sometimes drop the demonstrative before an animal's name, treating the animal as a proper noun and ranking it higher on the animacy scale, and narratives favour an object-verb-subject word order uncommon in ordinary speech. The oldest written document about the people from inside the colonial encounter is the Jesuit report of 1691 on Rarámuri resistance to evangelization, *Historia de la tercera rebelión tarahumara*. Present-day documentation includes a Rarámuri translation of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the Tarahumara Books project, which publishes work by, for and about the Ralámuli of Chihuahua in Ralámuli, Spanish and English.

## Language & religion

Rarámuri — endonym *Rarámuri* or *Ralámuli ra'ícha*, 'people language' — is Uto-Aztecan, grouped with its closest relative Guarijío. Within the language *rarámuri* refers specifically to men; a woman is *mukí*, and women collectively *omugí* or *igómale*. Specialists disagree on the dialect count: proposals of two (Western and Eastern), four, and five circulate, and the five tentatively recognized by the Mexican government are not those recognized by Ethnologue; the historian Manuel Orozco y Berra listed Varohio, Guazapare, Pachera and Tubar. The language is used in primary schools, local administration, traditional religious practice and local commerce, but only about 1% of speakers read it, against 20% literate in Spanish. Religion is a syncretism worked out largely without outside supervision after the Jesuits were expelled. God, *Riosi*, is identified with the Indigenous *Onoruame*, 'Great Father'; the Virgin Mary with *Iyeruame*, 'Great Mother'; their sons are the *sukristo* and their daughters the *Santi*. The devil, *Riablo*, is not wholly evil but tainted by its links with the *Chabochi*, the non-Rarámuri, and can be appeased or even enlisted. Many practices carry the sense of *konema*, feeding God — returning a little of what he has given. Like other Uto-Aztecan peoples, the Rarámuri venerate peyote.
