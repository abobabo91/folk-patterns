## Overview

The Toba — who call themselves *Qom* — are one of the largest Indigenous peoples of Argentina, numbering 126,967 self-identified people in the 2010 national census. Their homeland is the Gran Chaco: the flat, thorn-forest and savanna country drained by the Pilcomayo and Bermejo rivers, spanning present-day Formosa, Chaco, eastern Salta and northern Santa Fe, with smaller populations in the Bolivian Gran Chaco and the Chaco Boreal of Paraguay. Since the mid-twentieth century large numbers have moved to city peripheries — Resistencia, Formosa, Rosario (where the Barrio Toba is a named neighbourhood), and Greater Buenos Aires. The language, Qom l'aqtaqa, belongs to the Guaycuruan family alongside Pilagá, Mocoví and the extinct Abipón. In folk-culture terms the Qom matter as one of the world's most sophisticated users of a single wild plant fibre — *chaguar* — and as carriers of a shamanic, hunter-gatherer aesthetic that survived both Jesuit reduction and the military Conquest of the Gran Chaco.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Chaguar** — the defining Qom (and wider Chaco) fibre, taken from the leaves of wild bromeliads of the genus *Bromelia*, locally *caraguatá*. Women cut the leaves from the thorn forest, scrape them against a wooden board or bone to strip the pulp, wash and sun-bleach the fibre, then roll it thigh-to-palm into two-ply cord. No spindle is used: the thread is rolled on the leg, which makes Chaco *chaguar* work technically distinct from every Andean spun-yarn tradition to the west.

**Llica** — the large carrying net, worked in looping or knotless netting from *chaguar* cord and slung from forehead or shoulder. It stretches to hold firewood, palm fruit, algarroba pods or a child, and contracts when empty. The mesh structure itself carries the design, with colour changes producing banded and lozenge fields.

**Yica** — the smaller shoulder bag in the same looping technique, used by men and women for personal goods, tobacco, hunting gear and, latterly, as the emblematic craft object sold at fairs and Indigenous-craft outlets. *Yica* patterning is denser than *llica* and more often dyed.

**Chaguar netting** — the looping family itself (*malla*, worked with a single needle-like implement rather than a heddle) covers fishing nets, hunting nets set across animal runs, hammocks and sacks. Dyes are all forest-sourced: rust-brown and black from *algarrobo* and *guayacán* bark, ash and mud mordants, and the deep tan of *palo santo*.

**Chaguar faja** and **tejido en telar de cintura** — narrow sashes and bands woven in *chaguar*, and later in acquired sheep's wool and commercial cotton, on a backstrap or staked horizontal frame. These are the one Qom textile format that is genuinely woven rather than looped, and they carry the crispest geometric registers.

**Cestería de palma** — coiled and plaited basketry from *palma caranday* leaf and *totora*, made for winnowing, storage and market sale; the plaiting rhythm generates its own chevron and step patterning without added dye.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Guaycurú* motif — the family of stepped-diamond and interlocking-fret designs shared across Guaycuruan peoples; *ojo de pescado* (fish eye) — concentric lozenge; *caparazón de tortuga* (tortoise shell) — hexagonal field; *huella de ñandú* (rhea track) — three-toed arrow form; *espinazo* (spine) — central zigzag with lateral barbs; *víbora* / snake-skin lattice; *panal* (honeycomb); *cruz del sur* — four-point star; banded *listado* colour registers dividing a net or sash into horizontal fields.

### Clothing & dress

Pre-conquest dress was minimal and hide-based: men wore a hide breechclout and women a wrapped skirt or apron of deer or capybara skin, with a *manta* or blanket of woven *chaguar* thrown over the shoulders in the Chaco winter. Bare feet were normal; the *ojota*-type sandal came in with Creole contact. The equestrian period added the most distinctive item of Qom gear — pieces of hardened leather strapped to the head and shoulders when riding through thorn forest, a working armour against both *quebracho* spines and jaguars dropping from branches.

Ceremonial and leadership dress is built around feathers. The *vincha de plumas* is a headband mounting rhea, parrot, macaw and toucan feathers; the full *penacho* is a radiating crest worn by shamans (*pi'oxonaq*) and by *caciques* at assemblies and, today, at political mobilisations. Seed-and-feather chest ornaments accompany it. *Pintura corporal qom* — body paint in red *urucú* (annatto), white clay and charcoal black — was applied in geometric registers across face, chest and arms for war, curing ceremony and initiation. Everyday dress is now Argentine working clothing, with *yica*, *vincha* and *penacho* retained as markers of Qom identity.

### Architecture

The classic Chaco dwelling is a low, dome-to-beehive hut of bent branches lashed into a frame and thatched with grass, palm leaf and *totora*, entered by a single low opening and built directly on the ground — a form that suited a band of up to sixty families moving seasonally between fishing, hunting and *algarroba*-gathering camps. Frames used *palo santo* and *quebracho*, the two hardwoods that resist the Chaco's termites. Huts were grouped in a loose arc or circle around an open plaza used for dancing and assembly, with no interior partitioning and a hearth outside the entrance.

Settlement under the Franciscan reductions of 1901 — San Francisco Solano de Tacaaglé on the Pilcomayo and San Francisco de Laishí in southeastern Formosa — imposed gridded rows of mud-and-stick houses. Today's rural Qom communities build in *adobe* and *chorizo* mud-and-wattle walling with corrugated-iron or thatch roofs, and communal ramadas — open-sided post-and-thatch shelters — serve as the real centre of social life, where *chaguar* is processed and stories told. In the *villas miserias* of Rosario and Buenos Aires, self-built brick and sheet-metal housing has replaced all of this.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**Alfarería qom** — coil-built, unglazed earthenware, worked without a wheel from *greda* (river clay) tempered with ash or crushed sherd, smoothed with a pebble or maize cob and open-fired in a pit with wood and dung. Forms are globular water jars, wide cooking bowls and small anthropomorphic and zoomorphic vessels; surfaces carry incised and combed geometric bands and red slip from iron-rich earth. **Palo santo carving** turns the aromatic, resinous heartwood of *Bulnesia sarmientoi* into bowls, spoons, mortars, animal figures and mortar-and-pestle sets — the wood's green-brown oiliness and scent are the point, and the tradition is now a major cash craft.

The hunting kit — self bow, cane arrows with hardwood and bone points, fish spear, throwing club (*macana*), net trap — remained in use into the twentieth century alongside firearms acquired for resistance. Gourds (*porongos*) serve as containers and rattles; deer-hoof and bone tools do the *chaguar* scraping.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The **collar de semillas** is the characteristic Qom ornament: multi-strand necklaces strung from forest seeds — *chañar*, *tala*, black and scarlet *porotos de monte*, coloured *achira* — alternated with snail shell, bone discs, small feathers and, since contact, glass trade beads. Colour is the seed's own; the sequence is read as pattern. Feather ear ornaments and feather-and-seed pectorals accompany the *penacho* in ceremony. Shamans wore bone and nut pendants, jaguar and puma claws, and carried gourd rattles as part of their visible equipment.

Body decoration went further than jewelry: *pintura corporal* in annatto red and charcoal, and facial tattooing and scarification, historically marked girls' puberty rites and male status among Guaycuruan peoples. Long hair, worn loose or bound under a *vincha*, was cut in mourning. Today seed-work is both a ritual ornament and a substantial part of the craft economy run through Qom women's cooperatives.

## Music & performance

The signature Qom instrument is the ***nviké*** (also *n'vike*, *nvique*) — a one-string fiddle with a body of *palo borracho* or tin can, a horsehair string and a small bow, played with a keening, speech-like melodic line and closely associated with courtship and with solo lament. Alongside it stand the ***pim pim*** — the double-headed drum that names the dance it accompanies — the *sonajero* gourd rattle, deer-hoof rattles, notched scrapers, cane flutes and whistles, and the friction and reed sound-makers used in curing. Shamanic song is the oldest layer: the *pi'oxonaq* sings his acquired power songs over a patient, rattle in hand, and the song is understood as the vehicle of the helper spirit rather than as performance.

Since the mid-twentieth century Qom music has run on two tracks. **Pim pim** and *nviké* repertoire persists at community gatherings in Formosa and Chaco; in parallel, Pentecostal and Anglican mission churches generated a large body of Qom-language hymnody, sung in chorus with guitar, bass drum and handclap, which is now the most widely practised Qom vocal music. Urban Qom groups in Rosario and Resistencia perform both on stage, and the Qom-language radio station FM 89.3 in Formosa — attacked and wrecked in 2015 during the land conflict — has been central to circulating it.

## Dance & theatre

The **pim pim** is the principal Qom dance, named for its drum and danced in a line or open circle, men and women moving with short stamping steps to drum and voice at night-long gatherings; it is the dance that marks collective celebration — a good harvest of *algarroba*, a successful hunt, a visit between communities, and now Indigenous political assemblies. Shamanic curing sessions involve rhythmic, rattle-led movement by the *pi'oxonaq* around and over the patient rather than choreographed dance.

The Qom have no masked or puppet theatre tradition. Dramatic performance is instead embedded in narration — a storyteller enacting the voices of *Fitzcalxaic* (the trickster fox), animals and monsters before a night audience. Bilingual intercultural schools in Chaco and Formosa now stage Qom-language dramatisations of these narrative cycles, and urban Qom dance troupes present *pim pim* at provincial Indigenous-culture festivals.

## Festivals & rituals

The Qom ritual year is keyed to the Chaco's wild harvests rather than to an agricultural calendar. The great gathering was the ***algarroba*** season of late spring and early summer, when carob pods ripen and bands converged to harvest, brew *aloja* — fermented *algarroba* beer — and hold nights of *pim pim*; this, not a fixed date, was the Qom festival. Fish runs on the Pilcomayo and Bermejo at the start of the rains drew comparable assemblies. Since Christianisation, mission calendars overlay this: the founding dates of the 1901 Franciscan reductions (21 and 25 March) are still marked in Misión Tacaaglé and San Francisco de Laishí, and Pentecostal congregations hold multi-day *cultos* and baptisms in the rivers.

The most important political-ritual date is now **19 July**, the anniversary of the 1924 **Massacre of Napalpí**, in which some 200 Qom and Mocoví were killed by Chaco police and *estancia* men. It is commemorated annually, and gained new weight when Argentina held a "truth trial" over the killings in 2022. Argentina's **Día del Respeto a la Diversidad Cultural** (12 October) is likewise a Qom mobilisation day.

Life-cycle rites: birth involved seclusion and food restriction for both parents; girls' first menstruation brought seclusion in a screened corner of the hut with dietary prohibition and, on emergence, painting and ornament; boys' passage was marked by first hunting and, for a few, by the shamanic vocation, entered through dream-encounter with a helper spirit and apprenticeship to an established *pi'oxonaq*. Death required abandonment or destruction of the deceased's dwelling and goods, hair-cutting by mourners, and avoidance of the dead person's name — practices still partially observed.

## Foodways

Qom subsistence was hunting, fishing and gathering, not farming, and the food vocabulary reflects it. The staple wild carbohydrate is the pod of the *algarrobo* (carob, *Prosopis*), ground in a wooden mortar into *harina de algarroba* and eaten as *patay* — a pressed, dried flour cake — or fermented into ***aloja***, the ceremonial *algarroba* beer central to gatherings. *Chañar* fruit is boiled down into the syrup *arrope de chañar*; *mistol*, *doca*, prickly-pear and palm hearts round out the gathered diet, together with wild honey taken from *lechiguana* and other nests, the most prized food of all.

Protein came from river fish — *sábalo*, *surubí*, *dorado*, *bagre* — caught with nets, weirs and spears and roasted on coals or in ash; from *ñandú* (rhea), *carpincho* (capybara), peccary, armadillo, iguana and deer; and from rhea eggs. Meat and fish are typically cooked *a las brasas* or buried in embers rather than boiled. Post-contact staples added wild and then rustled cattle, maize, cassava, squash and *mate*, which is drunk communally from a gourd as elsewhere in the Chaco and Litoral. *Locro*-type maize-and-meat stews and fry-bread *tortas fritas* entered from Creole neighbourhoods. Food sharing across households is an obligation rather than a courtesy, and the collapse of access to hunting and fishing territory is the direct cause of the malnutrition and tuberculosis crises documented in Chaco and Formosa communities from the 2000s onward.

## Oral tradition & literature

Qom narrative is a large, well-collected corpus of etiological and trickster tales told at night by older men and women. Its central figure is the fox trickster, *Fitzcalxaic* (Spanish *el Zorro*), whose greed and cleverness explain features of the world; alongside him stand *Nesoxsot* and other animal protagonists, the celestial woman-of-the-sky cycle, the origin of fire and of *algarroba*, and the tales of monstrous beings of the thorn forest. Cosmology places a forest-and-sky world governed by owner-spirits of each species and place — animals, water, trees each have a *dueño* who must be respected by hunters — and it is these beings who become a shaman's helpers.

There is no epic poem tradition. What has grown instead is a documentary and literary revival: Qom-language readers and story collections produced through Chaco and Formosa's intercultural bilingual schools, the *Encuentro Nacional de Escritores en Lenguas Indígenas* circuit, and the writing and public testimony of Qom leaders — above all Félix Díaz, whose account of the 2010 Route 86 conflict and the Buenos Aires *Qopiwini* encampment has become a widely circulated Indigenous political text in Argentina.

## Language & religion

Qom l'aqtaqa (Toba) is a Guaycuruan language, closely related to Pilagá and Mocoví, and was reported spoken by roughly 59,800 people in Argentina in the 2000s — high retention by Argentine standards, but declining in urban settings. There is no indigenous script; writing uses a Latin orthography developed by missionary linguists and now standardised for bilingual schooling, with Qom-language Bible translation, radio broadcasting and school materials. Regional variation distinguishes western Formosa speech, where Qom and Pilagá have intermixed as the Toba-Pilagá, from eastern Chaco speech.

Religion is layered rather than replaced. The *pi'oxonaq* shaman, dream-acquired power songs, and the owner-spirits of animals and places persist alongside overwhelming affiliation to Pentecostal and Evangelical congregations — many Qom-led — and, historically, to Franciscan Catholicism and the Anglican mission. The Chaco *Iglesia Evangélica Unida*, founded and run by Indigenous pastors, is the institutional heart of this synthesis, with curing by prayer standing in continuity with shamanic curing rather than in opposition to it.
