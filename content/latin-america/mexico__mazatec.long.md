## Overview

The Mazatec are an Indigenous people of Mexico who live in the Sierra Mazateca, in the northern part of the state of Oaxaca, with further communities in the adjacent states of Puebla and Veracruz. Researchers have proposed that the Mazatec, together with speakers of Popoloca, once occupied the lowlands of the Papaloapan basin and were pushed up into the neighbouring highlands by the expansion of the Nahuas. Their languages form the Mazatecan group within the Popolocan subgroup of the Oto-Manguean family; INEGI counted 237,000 speakers in 2020. Two features give the Mazatec a particular place in the study of folk culture: a whistle-speech register that carries entire conversations on the tonal contours of the language, and a ritual tradition built on cultivated entheogens, associated internationally with the Huautla de Jiménez healer María Sabina.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The museum objects in this atlas are almost entirely cotton garments and cloths, and they document two separate ornamental systems — loom-figured weave and counted-thread embroidery — sometimes on the same garment.

**Gauze-weave and supplementary-weft brocade** — Hand-spun cotton is woven so that open gauze bands alternate with denser ground; coloured or white supplementary wefts are floated across the surface to make geometric figures. Head cloths in the collection are squares of white cotton in which red brocaded stripes alternate with bands of gauze weaving, fringed on two sides; girdles and bags carry the same alternation concentrated at the ends.

**Cross-stitch on *cuadrillé*** — One huipil is built not from a figured weave but from a rectangle of white open-mesh embroidery cloth, called *cuadrillé* in Spanish, folded in half with a rounded neck opening cut into it and then covered in cross-stitch in cotton thread.

**Two-panel *huipil*** — The woven type is made of two gauze-woven panels of hand-spun cotton joined lengthwise, folded and sewn up the sides, with a square neck hole cut; neck, shoulder and lower borders carry a geometric indigo design, and the body is embroidered with figures.

**Tubular wrapped skirt** — Two rectangles of off-white cotton are seamed along their long sides, folded and seamed again into a tube. One example brocades the bottom edge with a red geometric pattern over blue horizontal stripes and binds the hem in blue cotton; another, combining a commercially woven upper panel with a hand-woven lower one, replaces the brocade with cross-stitch bands and finishes the hem with a blue cotton strip.

**Men's plaid shoulder cloth** — A rectangle of cotton in a white and brown plaid, hemmed on two opposing sides, with no brocade or embroidery at all — the plainest patterned textile in the set.

**Motif vocabulary.** Red and purple flowers along a hem; multicoloured birds and flowers across the chest; red flowers at the shoulders; trees, birds and rabbits scattered over a huipil body; horses and human figures ranked above a flower band; geometric figures in indigo or red; red, blue and white stripes, in single lines and in sets of three; white-on-white brocaded bands; white and brown plaid.

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress in these records consists of the *huipil* and a tubular skirt. The huipil appears in both of its construction types — the woven two-panel garment with a cut square neck, and the embroidered *cuadrillé* rectangle with a rounded neck — and in both cases the ornament is concentrated on the chest, shoulders and lower border, that is, on the parts that read at a distance. The skirt is a sewn tube decorated only along the bottom edge, where brocade or cross-stitch sits above a contrasting blue hem binding. A square white head cloth, brocaded in red and fringed on two sides, completes the women's set; a 1952 photograph of inhabitants of San Bartolomé Ayautla, Oaxaca, taken by Bodil Christensen, is catalogued for its headwear. Men's dress is represented by a girdle — an off-white cotton rectangle with sets of three red stripes and brocaded geometric bands at each end, finished with four white tassels per end — and by the plaid shoulder cloth or scarf. A striped and brocaded cotton bag with braided carrying loops sewn into its top corners is the one accessory in the group.

## Music & performance

What the sources do document is a non-musical use of pitch: most Mazatec communities employ whistle speech, in which utterances are produced by whistling the tonal contours of words and phrases, a practice the language supports because tone carries so much grammatical and semantic load. Whistling is above all the register of young men, who hold complex conversations entirely in it; small boys learn to whistle as they learn to talk, older men use it more rarely, and women generally do not use it. It serves to communicate across distance, to catch the attention of a passer-by, or to avoid cutting across a spoken conversation, and even economic transactions can be concluded in it. Because the whistle encodes nothing precise about vowels or consonants, an utterance is often ambiguous, but the narrow range of topics normally lets context settle the meaning. Mazatecan-language radio programming is broadcast by the CDI station XEOJN at San Lucas Ojitlán, Oaxaca.

## Festivals & rituals

Mazatec ritual practice as documented here centres on the cultivation of entheogens for spiritual and ceremonial use. Three are named: psilocybin mushrooms; the psychoactive seeds of morning glories, including the species *Ipomoea tricolor* and *Ipomoea corymbosa*; and *Salvia divinorum*, which Mazatec shamans call *ska María Pastora* — a name that invokes the Virgin Mary and so marks the overlay of Catholic devotion on the practice. The two Mazatec figures the sources name, María Sabina and Julieta Casimiro, both belong to this tradition.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used name no Mazatec folktale cycle, epic or proverb collection. They do, however, describe an unusual verbal art and a substantial body of written material. Whistle speech, described above, is the living oral register that distinguishes Mazatec communities; it is learned in childhood alongside speech and sustains full conversation without words being voiced. On the written side, the Mazatec language is vigorous in many of the smaller communities and spoken by almost everyone in many towns, though Spanish is gaining ground in larger centres such as Huautla de Jiménez and Jalapa de Díaz. Literacy work has had to reckon with the fact that the varieties are only partly mutually intelligible, so programmes must recognise local standards rather than a single norm. The entire New Testament exists in several varieties of Mazatec, and Jehovah's Witnesses publish a range of Bible-based literature and video content in the language.

## Language & religion

The Mazatecan languages belong to the Eastern branch of Oto-Manguean, within the Popolocan subgroup alongside Popoloca, Ixcatec and Chocho; under the General Law of Linguistic Rights of the Indigenous Peoples they are recognised as national languages of Mexico. ISO 639-3 enumerates nine, named for the villages where they are spoken except for Puebla Mazatec: Chiquihuitlán, Huautla (the prestige variety, about 50,000 speakers), Ayautla, Mazatlán, Eloxochitlán, Ixcatlán, Jalapa, Soyaltepec, and Puebla and Northeastern Mazatec. They divide into Highland and Lowland groups, a split that corresponds to the political division of highland and lowland territories between 1300 and 1519; under Aztec dominance from 1456 to 1519 the highlands were ruled from Teotitlán del Camino and the lowlands from Tuxtepec, and that division persists. All the varieties are tonal, with complex tone inventories and phonation contrasts including creaky and breathy voice. On religion the sources record only the entheogenic shamanic tradition and the Marian name *ska María Pastora*.
