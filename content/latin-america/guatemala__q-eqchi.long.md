## Overview

The Qʼeqchiʼ are a Mayan people of northern Guatemala whose language belongs to the Quichean branch of the Mayan family. The core of the Qʼeqchiʼ-speaking area in northern Guatemala extends over some 24,662 square kilometres (about 9,522 square miles), and includes the mountainous departments of Alta Verapaz and Baja Verapaz — territory the Spaniards renamed *Verapaz*, "true peace", after Dominican priests led by Fray Bartolomé de las Casas were admitted to proselytize while lay Spaniards were kept out. Qʼeqchiʼ communities also live in southern Belize, principally the Toledo District, where the language is the first language of many villages, and in the Mexican states of Campeche, Quintana Roo and Chiapas. Qʼeqchiʼ is the third largest Mayan language in Guatemala and the fourth across the Mayan region. The sources used here document the language in detail and a body of museum-held costume, mask and dance material; they do not offer a general ethnography of Qʼeqchiʼ life.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

What the sources do document is a set of finished cloth objects held by the British Museum, all of them cotton or synthetic, and most of them belonging to dance costume rather than daily wear.

**Embroidered blouse** — a short-sleeved blouse of lemon-coloured synthetic fabric, gathered with elastic at the cuffs, with an embroidered yoke and a scalloped-edged neck. The yoke carries a blue-and-white border and, at its centre, a blue dove edged in pink, with flowers in pinks and reds.

**Dance costume in red cotton** — a matching jacket, breeches and skirt, all of red cotton worked with a black-and-white floral design and lined in white cotton. The jacket has lace at the cuffs; the skirt carries an apron flap at front and back.

**Kerchief** — a red cotton square with a black-and-white curvilinear band running around the edge and a repeating black-and-white ring design at the centre, recorded as probably part of a dance costume.

**Embroidered calendar cloth** — a white cotton cloth, machine-hemmed, embroidered in multi-coloured acrylic wool with the symbols and names of the Mayan calendar.

**Dance head-dresses** — one is a rectangle of red cotton cloth with a tie at each short end and bundles of blue and purple wool hanging from the lower edge to represent hair; another, a man's head-dress from a devil dancer's costume, is made of vegetable fibre and cotton.

The sources name no motif vocabulary beyond the descriptive terms above: the dove, the floral sprays, the curvilinear edge band, the ring, and the calendar signs.

### Clothing & dress

The collected garments divide into two groups. The embroidered blouse — synthetic fabric, elasticated cuffs, a machine-friendly construction — represents women's wear of a kind made for ordinary use, and it is the only everyday garment the catalogue records describe. Everything else belongs to performance: the red cotton jacket, breeches and skirt with their black-and-white floral field and white linings, the red kerchief with its ringed centre, and the two head-dresses for devil dancers, one a cloth band trailing bundles of blue and purple wool in place of hair, the other built from vegetable fibre and cotton. A further piece is catalogued simply as a devil dancer's cloth. The sources say nothing about men's or women's daily dress more broadly, nor about belts, footwear, or hair coverings outside the dance, so the distinction between ceremonial and quotidian costume can be drawn here only as far as the accession notes allow.

### Architecture

What the sources do note about the built landscape is indirect: the difficult mountainous terrain of the Qʼeqchiʼ homeland is identified as one of the factors that allowed the language to survive where neighbouring Itzaʼ, Mopan and Chʼoltiʼ became moribund or extinct.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Two object classes stand out in the collection. The first is the *incensario*, a hand-modelled pottery censer with a flat base and a conical body opening into a large aperture, pierced with multiple perforations at the back and fitted with a wire suspension loop; the recorded example still holds a burned layer of copal incense, evidence of use rather than display. The second is a netted-fibre carrying-bag — a rounded bag with a loop at one upper corner and a handle running from the opposite corner and tied to that loop. Metal appears in the collection chiefly as fittings rather than as vessels or ornament: tin ears nailed to the temples of dance masks, a cast bell made for a mask, and a devil-dance rattle assembled from tin, wood and cloth.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The only adornment they document is worn as part of masked costume: the bells hung from masks, the tin and leather ears nailed to their temples, and the wool hair-bundles of the dance head-dress.

## Music & performance

Music enters the sources through two objects and one text. A gourd instrument is recorded as an oblong gourd with ridges carved along the top, two holes cut on each side and one at the end, a length of curved wood inserted into the open end, and a painted surface of red and green stripes. Alongside it is a devil-dance rattle of tin, wood and cloth — an instrument tied explicitly to masked performance rather than to independent music-making, which suggests that in the material recorded here sound and dance are not separable categories.

Sung repertoire appears in the written record instead. The Catholic songbook *Qanimaaq Xloqʼal li Qaawaʼ*, "We praise the Lord", is described as very popular among Qʼeqchiʼ Catholics, in print for many years and regularly updated with new songs — the sources' clearest indication of an active, expanding vocal tradition, and a religious rather than secular one. The Roman Catholic Church has been one of the primary proponents of written Qʼeqchiʼ over the past two decades, and this hymnal is part of that effort.

## Dance & theatre

The single dance tradition the sources document is a masked one, recorded throughout the British Museum material as the **devil dance**, and it is documented by its equipment rather than by any description of the performance itself. Its masks are carved of wood and crudely worked, and they fall into recognisable types. Several are monstrous heads painted in combinations of black, red, white, yellow and green, carrying three detachable horns set into holes cut across the forehead; one has a large moustache and two points extending from the chin, with a metal bell hanging from the central horn and a leather ear nailed to each temple. The most elaborate has each of its three horns surmounted by a squatting bird, with heads of snakes, animals and a toad issuing from the mouth and intertwined over the whole surface, the snakes given bodies of red and white cloth and tin ears nailed to the temples. Others are animal faces: a monkey with a pink face and black hair, eyebrows and eyes and white teeth; a boar's head with three horns and a cotton-cloth snake in its mouth; a pig painted red, green, yellow and white with tin ears. Separate from the masks are carved serpent figures with green-painted wooden heads and stuffed cotton bodies, some coiled into bundles — dance properties rather than costume.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not set out a Qʼeqchiʼ festival calendar or describe life-cycle rites. Three ritual traces are nonetheless recorded. Copal incense survives as a burned layer inside the pottery *incensario*, fixing that vessel to actual ceremonial use. The devil-dance masks, head-dresses, rattle, cloth and serpent figures indicate a masked performance with a ritual or calendrical occasion the accession notes do not name. And the embroidered calendar cloth, worked with the symbols and names of the Mayan calendar in acrylic wool, shows the calendar itself treated as a subject for textile display.

## Oral tradition & literature

Qʼeqchiʼ is, like most Mayan languages, still in the process of becoming a written and literary language, and the sources divide the existing texts into three kinds. Educational texts include dictionaries, grammars and workbooks for rural Guatemalan schools in communities where most people are native speakers; *Aprendamos Kekchí* is a widely used Spanish-language learning workbook. Religious texts are the most widely available: the Protestant Bible published by the Summer Institute of Linguistics, based on the work of William Sedat and of Francis Eachus and Ruth Carlson; a Catholic New Testament, Genesis and Exodus, and instructional pamphlets; the hymnal *Qanimaaq Xloqʼal li Qaawaʼ*; and the Book of Mormon with other LDS texts. Secular non-instructional writing has begun to appear only in the last decade or so and remains few in number. Its most ambitious works are a free translation of the Kʼicheʼ *Popol Wuj* by the Qʼeqchiʼ language teacher and translator Rigoberto Baq Qaal, and a collection of Qʼeqchiʼ folk tales. The Guatemalan Constitution has also been translated into Qʼeqchiʼ.

## Language & religion

Qʼeqchiʼ — also spelled Kekchi, Kʼekchiʼ or Kekchí — is a Quichean-branch Mayan language with 29 consonants (three loaned from Spanish) and 10 vowels distinguished by quality and length; stress falls on the final syllable, with few exceptions. It is ergative–absolutive, uses two sets of pronominal affixes, and has a basic verb–object–subject word order. Terrence Kaufman identified two dialect groups: eastern, covering Lanquín, Chahal, Chahabón and Senahú, and western everywhere else. Recent postcolonial expansion has made Qʼeqchiʼ perhaps the most homogeneous of the larger Mayan languages. Roman Catholic friars first transcribed it in the 16th century, Francisco de la Parra devising extra letters for unfamiliar Mayan consonants; the SIL/IIN orthography of the 1950s–60s remains in circulation, but the ALMG orthography — the Academia de Lenguas Mayas de Guatemala's revision of the Proyecto Lingüístico Francisco Marroquín system, replacing its "7" with the apostrophe — has been the Guatemalan standard since 1990. The language is taught in public schools through Guatemala's intercultural bilingual education programmes. Both Catholic and Protestant institutions are active, and the sources record no further detail on religious practice.
