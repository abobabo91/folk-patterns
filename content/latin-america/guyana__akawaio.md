## Overview

The Akawaio are Indigenous people of the Guiana highlands in Guyana, Brazil, and Venezuela who speak Kapóng, a language meaning 'Sky People.' They were prominent regional traders from the seventeenth century onward. Their material culture centers on glass beadwork and plaited fibre patterns, while their spiritual life is dominated by alleluia, a syncretic religion blending Christian and traditional Kapóng belief.

## Material culture

Akawaio material culture is made from natural materials—plaited fibre, gourds, wood, feathers, and glass trade beads—with no pottery or metalworking documented.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Pattern-bearing work combines glass beads and plaited fibres with woven-in black designs.

- **Beaded women's skirt** — A rectangular panel of white, violet, and blue glass beads with tassel trim of white beads and mambetuna.
- **Beaded girl's skirt** — A smaller version with a black bead ground carrying blue, red, and white patterning.
- **Man's beaded belt** (*livrem*) — Half plaited kamoa and half white glass beads on cotton thread, joining two techniques in a single band.
- **Plaited mana work** (*mana*) — Flatwork including a rectangular fan, shoulder bag, and serving fan, all with black designs worked in during plaiting.
- **Cotton binding and tassels** — Hand-spun cotton finishes edges and makes hanging bands and tassels on skin bags, war clubs, and arrows.

### Clothing & dress

Dress is built from beaded panels, plaited bands, and fibre footwear rather than cut cloth.

- **Women's beaded skirt** — The patterned glass-bead panel worn as the main garment, wound twice at the waist.
- **Sandals** (*otatsa*) — Palm-stem footwear with bast cords.
- **Feather crown** — A head-dress of feathers, reed, cotton, and cord held in the British Museum.
- **Dance girdle** — Hung with fruit-shell rattles and worn so that movement produces sound.
- **Rattle band** (*kévei*) — Cotton band with fruits attached, mounted on a staff and used in dance.

### Architecture

The vernacular architecture of the Akawaio is not documented in these sources, but settlement locations are well recorded.

- **Upper Mazaruni settlements** — Villages at Kamarang, Jawalla, Waramadong, and Kako clustered along the river.
- **Ireng River villages** — Settlements including Waipa, Marituaka, Kurikuparu, Sapritik, and Sukoabi.
- **Isseneru** — A village founded in the 1970s after people returned from upriver to avoid conflict with miners and a proposed dam.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Household inventory is plaited, gourd, and wood; no pottery or metalworking is documented.

- **Calabash bowls** — Gourds serving as drinking and serving vessels, one holding ulali poison for blowpipe darts.
- **Fruit-shell bowl** (*komeli*) — A storage box fitted with a cord.
- **Plaited serving fan** (*wo?woy*) — A deep fan 29 cm across used to carry food.
- **Paddle-shaped spoon** (*pare*) — Used for serving and eating.
- **Hunting and fishing gear** — Including the bow urába, blowpipe quivers, war clubs, fish-arrows, game arrows, scoop net pómoe, and wooden dugout canoe model.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment relies on glass beadwork and feather work rather than metal.

- **Glass trade beads** — White, violet, blue, red, and black beads worked into skirts, tassels, and the man's belt.
- **Feather crown** — The one documented head ornament, made of feather, reed, cotton, and cord.
- **Skin bags** — Otter and jaguar bags with cotton edging and tassels, functioning as display pieces.
- **Armadillo-shell bag** — A container and display piece.
- **Pepper-water nasal tube** (*kasiuok*) — A calabash tube used to draw pepper water into the nose.

## Music & performance

Three instruments are documented: a bone flute, a cow-horn trumpet, and rattles worn during dance.

- **Bone flute** (*sarákoa*) — A three-holed instrument made from jaguar or deer bone, bound with cotton and ornamented with incised crosses.
- **Cow-horn trumpet** (*paiaka létik*) — A single-holed horn blown as a trumpet.
- **Rattle band** (*kévei*) — Cotton band hung with kévei fruits and fastened to a staff for dance performance.
- **Shamanic ritual** — The shaman meets with the god through hallucinogenic ritual, tobacco, and prescribed diet.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is present in the collections only as equipment; no choreography, named dances, or theatrical traditions are documented.

- **Dance girdle** — A girdle of fruit-shell rattles catalogued explicitly as dance gear.
- **Staff-mounted rattle** (*kévei*) — A rattle-bearing staff used in dance performance.

## Festivals & rituals

No annual festival calendar is documented; religious practice centers on shamanism and the syncretic alleluia religion.

- **Alleluia religion** (*hallelujah*) — A blend of Christianity and older Kapóng belief created by the prophet Bichiwung, a Macushi who was killed and resurrected with white medicine.
- **Chief god** (*Makunaima*) — The creator god who made the Akawaio people and the most important figure in their polytheistic belief.
- **Flood god** (*Iwarrika*) — The deity blamed for flooding the earth.
- **Mythological figures** (*Kanaima, Sigu*) — Additional important figures in Akawaio mythology.
- **Sky-centered cosmology** — Belief in sun worship, sun spirits, and a higher being in the sky traced back to the sixteenth century among Carib-speaking peoples.

## Foodways

Cassava is the documented staple, with protein from hunting, fishing, and insect gathering.

- **Cassava bread** — The staple food, prepared with a smoother tool now obsolete.
- **Plaited food containers** — Including the wo?woy fan, palm-leaf bowls, and palm-leaf baskets for carrying prepared food.
- **Pepper water** — A condiment carried in palm-leaf baskets and drawn into the nose through the kasiuok tube.
- **Blowpipe poison** (*ulali*) — A poison applied to darts used in hunting game.
- **Insect delicacy** (*kokong*) — A gathered insect regarded as a great delicacy, collected by torchlight.

## Oral tradition & literature

Mythology is named but not narrated; the founding of alleluia is transmitted as a narrative of death, dismemberment, and restoration.

- **Mythological cycle** — Includes the figures Makunaima, Iwarrika, Kanaima, and Sigu, described as important to Akawaio culture.
- **Bichiwung narrative** — The founding story of alleluia, telling of the prophet's death, dismemberment, and resurrection by white medicine.
- **Simile formation** — The Kapóng language forms similes freely using the suffixes -kasa 'like' and -walai 'similar to'.
- **Alleluia study** — Audrey Butt's 1960 study 'The Birth of a Religion' in the Journal of the Royal Anthropological Institute documents the religion's practice.

## Language & religion

Kapóng is a Cariban language whose vocabulary embeds a sky-centered cosmology; it retains strong transmission among Akawaio speakers.

- **Language family** (*Kapóng*) — A Cariban language with three dialects: Akawaio, Ingarikó, and Patamona, spoken across Guyana, Brazil, and Venezuela.
- **Word order** — Preferentially subject–object–verb but flexible, with no gender distinctions in pronouns or affixes.
- **Pluralization marker** (*yamok*) — A word that pluralises a noun.
- **Possession marker** (*-da*) — An affix marking possession.
- **Sky vocabulary** — Key terms include Kapóng 'Sky People', kapo 'in the sky', akwa 'God's place', akwalo 'the spirit', Iopotari akuru 'chief spirit', taemogoli 'grandfather', and waica 'warrior'.
