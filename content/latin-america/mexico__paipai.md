## Overview

The Paipai, also called Pai pai, Pa'ipai, Akwa'ala, and Yakakwal, are an Indigenous people of Mexico living in northern Baja California. Their traditional territory extended between the Kiliwa to the south and the Kumeyaay and Cocopa to the north, from San Vicente near the Pacific coast nearly to the Colorado River’s delta in the east. Today they are concentrated primarily at the multi-ethnic community of Santa Catarina in Baja California’s Sierra de Juárez, although Paipai is also spoken in settlements of the Ensenada municipality. Historical population estimates range from 780 and 1,000 people associated with the San Vicente and Santa Catarina missions to 1,800 for the aboriginal Paipai population, while more recent accounts describe fewer than 200 speakers or around 231 speakers of the language. Paipai belongs to the Yuman language family and is closely related to Upland Yuman.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Paipai material culture included rabbitskin robes, fiber sandals, buckskin aprons, and basketry caps for women. The sources identify these garments and footwear.

### Architecture

Traditional Paipai structures included rectangular houses with thatched roofs, ramadas, and probably sweathouses. The Paipai lived historically across a territory extending from the Pacific coast toward the Colorado River’s delta, while the principal modern settlement identified in the sources is Santa Catarina, a community shared with Kumeyaay and Kiliwa residents.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Paipai material culture included pottery, basketry, manos and metates, mortars and pestles, cordage, stone knives, awls, cradles, and equipment used for hunting and warfare. Hunting and warfare equipment included bows, cane arrows, war clubs, and nets. The sources also describe Paipai processing equipment. They identify pottery and basketry as elements of Paipai material culture and mention a living pottery tradition in the title of a work by Michael Wilken-Robertson.

## Music & performance

Paipai music was produced by singing and by instruments that included flutes, gourd rattles, and jinglers.

Social recreation included games such as shinny, kickball races, the ring-and-pin game, dice, peon, archery, spinning tops, juggling, and cat’s cradle. These activities are presented as forms of social recreation rather than as musical or theatrical genres. Pets were also kept. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription concerns the Paipai, and no museum catalogue records were supplied for Paipai musical objects.

## Dance & theatre

They mention social games and musical instruments.

## Festivals & rituals

They mention structures that were probably sweathouses and discuss concepts of disease and curing in the title of a cited dissertation, but the supplied material does not describe Paipai ritual practice in detail.

The historical record includes the founding of the Dominican mission of San Vicente near the coast in Paipai territory in 1780 and the establishment of an inland mission at Santa Catarina in 1797 near the boundary between Paipai and Kumeyaay territories. Mission Santa Catarina was destroyed in 1840 by hostile Indian forces, apparently including Paipai. These events belong to the history of contact and mission administration rather than to a documented Paipai festival cycle.

## Foodways

Aboriginal Paipai subsistence was based on hunting and gathering natural animals and plants rather than on agriculture. Important plant foods included agave, yucca, mesquite, prickly pear, acorns, pine nuts, and juniper berries. Other plants were used as medicine or as materials for construction and craft products. Animal foods included deer, pronghorn, bighorn sheep, rabbits, woodrats, other medium and small mammals, quail, fish, and shellfish.

Crop growing and stock raising were introduced during the historic period. Judith Joël published *Some Paipai Accounts of Food Gathering*, but the supplied material does not reproduce the food-gathering accounts themselves.

## Oral tradition & literature

Paipai traditional narratives are conventionally classified as myths, legends, tales, and oral histories. The recorded oral literature is described as rather limited but includes narratives belonging to each of these categories. Paipai narratives such as the creation myth have their closest affinities with narratives of the Kumeyaay to the north.

The Paipai language was documented by Judith Joël, who published texts and studies of phonology, morphology, and syntax. Mauricio J. Mixco published transcriptions of stories and works concerning Paipai literature and the language’s historical and linguistic relationships. The supplied sources mention a creation myth narrated by Jackrabbit in 1921–1922, a creation myth recorded in 1929, a narrative collected from Rufino Ochurte, and *The Earthquake of ’57: A Paipai Text*.

## Language & religion

Paipai is the native language of the Paipai and is spoken in the Baja California municipality of Ensenada. It belongs to the Yuman language family and, within that family, to the Pai branch, which also includes Upland Yuman, whose dialects are spoken by the Yavapai, Walapai, and Havasupai of western Arizona. Paipai and Upland Yuman are described as very closely related, although observers have disagreed about whether they are mutually intelligible.

The language was traditionally spoken between the Pacific coast and the opening of the Colorado River Delta at the Gulf of California; its present distribution has been reduced to Ensenada. The sources list many settlements where it is spoken, including Comunidad Indígena de Santa Catarina, San Isidoro, El Pinacate, El Ranchito, La Vinata, Sauce Largo, Sauce Solo, and Yokakiul. In the oral tradition of most Yuman tribes, people descended from Avikwame, also known as Newberry Mt., and went where the spirit Kumat directed them.
