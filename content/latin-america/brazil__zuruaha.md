## Overview

The Zuruahã, also called Suruahá, Indios do Coxodoá and Suruwahá, are an Indigenous people of Brazil who live along the Purus River in the state of Amazonas. They are hunter-gatherers, and about 130 people speak the Zuruahã language. The language belongs to the Arawá language family and is spoken in Brazil. The Zuruahã are described as an amalgamation of other tribes that fled disease and violence, especially during the rubber boom. After influenza caused a high death rate from 1922 to 1924, survivors withdrew from non-Native settlements. Sustained contact with the outside world began in 1980.

## Festivals & rituals

They report that some Zuruahá began to commit suicide by drinking curare after first contact with the outside world, including cases in which eight people died in a day, and that pressure on their territories resulted in increased suicide. This information concerns a documented historical and social crisis rather than a festival or ritual tradition.

## Foodways

The sources mention curare in connection with suicide and identify “aluminum cauldron” as the meaning of a recent Portuguese loanword in the language.

## Language & religion

Zuruahá, also called Suruaha, Suruwaha, Suruwahá, Zuruwahã, Zuruaha and Índios do Coxodoá, is an Arawan language spoken in Brazil by about 130 people. It had not been studied as of 1994 and was described as seeming most similar to Deni. Dan Everett made first contact with the community in 1980, during a 3-day hike from Dení territory in Amazonas state. The sources describe vowels and consonants, including a vowel that appears to have been introduced more recently and consonants whose pronunciation varies between colloquial and careful speech.
