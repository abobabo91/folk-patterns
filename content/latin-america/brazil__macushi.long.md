## Overview

The Macushi (*Makuusi*; Portuguese *Macuxi*, also written Macusi, Macussi, Makushi, Makusi, Teueia and Teweya) are an Indigenous people of the borderlands where northern Brazil, southern Guyana and eastern Venezuela meet. In Brazil they live in northeastern Roraima, along the Rio Branco and the Contingo, Quino, Pium and Mau rivers, and they are the largest Indigenous group in that state and a part of the population of Boa Vista; in Guyana they occupy the northern and southern Rupununi Savannah. Instituto Socioambiental puts the population at about 43,192 — 33,603 in Brazil, 9,500 in Guyana, 89 in Venezuela. Their language belongs to the Macushi-Kapon branch of the Cariban family, and they are counted, with the Arecuna, Kamarakoto and Taurepan, as a sub-group of the Pemon. For folk culture they matter above all as cassava cultivators and as makers of cotton hammocks, featherwork and curare — goods that circulated widely in the region.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Cotton hammock** — Woven cotton hammocks are the best-documented Macushi textile. In the nineteenth century the Macushi "specialized in producing hammocks, various crafts, and a potent form of curare," trading them to other Amerindian groups, often for cassava graters and blow-pipes. A hammock registered in 1947 is coloured blue, red and yellow with synthetic dyes and finished with a tasselled fringe; a second, registered in 1950, is plain cotton.

**Shoulder cloak (dance ornament)** — A man's dance ornament built from a band of woven cotton, from which hang five loose twisted cords, each ending in a ball of down. The woven band plus pendant cords distinguishes it from the solidly feathered capes below.

**Featherwork capes and tippets** — Capes of egret feathers on twine and of macaw feathers on twine are catalogued as medicine men's garments; a further cape or collar of feathers is fastened with cotton cord. A tippet of red macaw feathers and cotton is recorded as worn by a chief or by a dancer at a feast.

**Plaited palm matting and trays** — A serving tray plaited from *ite*-palm leaf was collected in 1912 at the village of Aynaymong, south of Roraima; fire-fans are plaited from palm leaves in the same technique.

**Cane and bamboo casing** — The *matape*, a cassava squeezer, is plaited from cane; a double-case quiver is made of split bamboo lined with reed and coated outside with a thick gum or resinous pitch, with a fibre cord twisted around the middle.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no motif names, but they describe the ornament carried on specific objects: incised designs picked out in red and white pigment on the triangular blade of a chief's club; four bird-headed figures carved at the broad end of a war club; bands of black lines on the top and bottom of a manioc grater; zigzag lines on a dance rattle-stick; and glass beads strung in alternating red and white on a nose ornament.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe Macushi dress almost entirely through ceremonial and dance pieces rather than everyday wear. Headgear is the most varied category: head-dresses of feathers mounted on wood with twine and cotton, a crown of feathers and reed, and a basketry frame made to carry a head-dress. Over the shoulders go feather capes — egret and macaw — attributed to medicine men, a feather cape or collar closed with cotton cord, and the cotton-and-down shoulder cloak worn by men in dance. The red macaw-feather tippet is explicitly a chief's or dancer's garment for a feast, so featherwork marks both office and performance. For the feet there is a pair of sandals cut from a split palm-branch, the foot held to the convex side by lengths of two-ply twisted fibre twine. A cotton anklet survives as one of a pair. Body paint belongs to dress as well: a small cane-and-cotton container is catalogued as a container for body paint.

### Architecture

Macushi villages are linked to one another by tracks and paths, and within a village the houses are built around a central courtyard. Residence after marriage is uxorilocal — the couple lives in the wife's family's village — and the father-in-law holds great importance in Macushi kinship, so the built cluster reflects the kin group that assembled it. Before European colonization the Macushi were semi-nomadic; permanent settlements formed later, usually around Catholic or Anglican missions or government-built schools, and the Brazilian government has since added schools and hospitals. Named Macushi settlements in Guyana include Annai, Kwatamang, Surama, Rewa, Crash Water, Karasabai and Yupukari in the northern Rupununi, and St. Ignatius and Moco-Moco in the south. In Brazil the territory consists of three blocks: the Raposa Serra do Sol Indigenous Territory, the largest and most populated, the San Marcos Indigenous Territory, and small areas around isolated villages in the extreme northwest.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The one documented pot is a cooking vessel of red-fired clay, built up from rolls of clay — coil-built, not wheel-thrown — collected in 1912 by Otto Thulin at Aynaymong on the Rio Wailang, a tributary of the Rio Mau (Rio Ireng) southeast of Mount Roraima, during an expedition to Guyana with Olof Liljewalch. Cassava processing accounts for most of the household kit: a wooden manioc grater set with small stones and striped with black lines, the cane *matape* squeezer, the plaited *ite*-palm tray, a palm-leaf fire-fan, and a calabash bowl with a blackened interior. Wooden clubs carry the clearest carving: a chief's club used as a badge of office, knobbed at one end and cut to a triangle at the other with red and white incised work, and a war club bound with cotton cord and carved with four bird-headed figures. Hunting gear includes reed-and-cane poisoned arrows, iron-tipped fish arrows, a bundle of about fifty darts poisoned with curare, and a cotton wrapping for blowpipe arrows.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver crescent-shaped nose ornaments are the signature piece: each has two pierced projections above for suspension and a row of teeth cut along the lower edge — one example with eight teeth, another with six. A third nose ornament strings fourteen glass beads, alternating red and white, above a semicircular silver plate, combining imported beads with worked silver. Necklaces use animal and plant materials: a man's necklace of seeds on a double cotton string, and a plaited fibre cord tied with numerous deliberate knots and threaded with about 150 acouri teeth. A cotton anklet completes the body ornament recorded here, along with the cane container for body paint.

## Music & performance

Macushi instruments in the collections fall into three families. Flutes are made of bone — one with three finger holes and cotton-cord wrappings at both ends, another finished with cotton tassels on loosely twisted cord, a third of deer or possibly large-cat bone — and there is a double flute of reed in which the longer tube is stopped at the upper end with one large and one small hole above and two below, while the shorter tube is open with two oblong facing openings and four small holes along the side between them. Bamboo pan-pipes are also recorded. Membranophones include the *sampura'*, a drum hollowed from a section of palm trunk with skin stretched taut over each end and a cord strung across one head with a small piece of wood wedged beneath it to yield a different note, and a cylindrical wooden drum headed with deer skin, laced with cotton string and struck with a wooden beater. Rattles are tied to dancing: sticks of wood or cane, one ornamented with zigzag lines, carry plaited cotton bands hung with *Thevetia peruviana* seeds and are used to beat time in dancing. A longer conjuring wand, a stick about four feet long with cords of roughly 2 ft 8 in and 3 ft 6 in at each end strung with large halved *sibeni* seeds, was rattled by *piai*-men at their dances.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name no individual Macushi dances, but they document the apparatus and the personnel of dancing clearly. Rattle-sticks of wood and cane, hung with thevetia seeds on plaited cotton bands, were used to beat time in dancing; the *piaiman* — the medicine-man and spiritual leader, called *piai*-man or conjurer in the older catalogue language — rattled a long seed-hung wand at his dances; and the red macaw-feather tippet was worn by a chief or by a dancer at a feast, as was the cotton-and-down shoulder cloak listed as a man's dance ornament. Costume therefore distinguishes ritual performance by a specialist from festive dancing by chiefs and others. No dramatic or puppet tradition appears in the sources.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources give no annual festival calendar. What they do document is the ritual world that the objects serve. Feasts are attested indirectly, through the chief's or dancer's feather tippet worn at one. Ritual authority rests with the *piaiman*, the medicine-man and spiritual leader, whose feather capes, conjuring wand and dances the collections record; the role is shared with neighbouring peoples such as the Patamona and Akawaio. Against him stands belief in *keinaimi* (*kanaima*), an evil spirit personified as an "outsider" who brings death and misfortune, associated with shape-shifting into animals — jaguars, bats, armadillos — and with attacks on people caught alone, who are assaulted and die some days later; the same term also covers stealthy, assassin-like action used as protection, which can rebound on those it was meant to shield. Life-cycle practice is documented at marriage: the couple settles in the wife's family's village, and the father-in-law becomes a figure of great importance. Village standing is measured by success in cassava farming. Since the eighteenth century Catholic and Anglican missions have anchored many permanent settlements; the Jesuit Cuthbert Cary-Elwes settled among the Rupununi Macushi in 1909, learned the language, and stayed more than twenty-three years.

## Foodways

Macushi subsistence combines hunting, fishing and shifting cultivation, with fish-poisoning used to take fish. Hunting gear is well represented: reed and cane arrows poisoned at the tip, iron-headed fish arrows, and bundles of some fifty darts poisoned with curare — a potent form of which the Macushi themselves produced and traded in the nineteenth century, when they also acquired blow-pipes and cassava graters by exchange. The staple is bitter cassava, and traditional life relies on it heavily. Cultivation is divided by gender: men clear the land, women tend and harvest. Women are also the main processors, and the recorded products are cassava bread, *farine*, *parakari*, *wo* (a drink), starch tapioca and *casereep*. The processing chain survives in the objects: a wooden grater set with small stones, the cane *matape* squeezer for expressing the poisonous juice, the plaited *ite*-palm tray, a palm-leaf fire-fan for the hearth, the coil-built red clay cooking pot from Aynaymong, and a calabash bowl with blackened interior. In Macushi lore cassava was created to be cultivated and is overseen by a Cassava Mother, *kisera yan*. Village status is correlated with success in cassava farming.

## Oral tradition & literature

Macushi oral history makes the people descendants of the sun's children: the benevolent *Insikiran* (*Inshkirung*) and his malevolent brother *Makunaima*, also called *Negi*, who created fire and also diseases; the brothers are likewise credited with the discovery of *Washacá*, the Tree of Life. The Macushi hold to a life principle, *stkaton*, which comes from the sun. Narrative also carries history: the memories of villages abandoned and escape plans laid as Portuguese settlers, rubber extractivists, mineral developers and later *grileiros* — forgers of land titles — pressed into the territory are still preserved in Macushi oral histories, documented for Instituto Socioambiental by the anthropologist Paulo Santilli. Written literature in the language began with the New Testament, translated into Macushi in 1996 and printed in Latin script. Among contemporary Macushi authors and public figures are the Brazilian artist, activist and writer Jaider Esbell (1979–2021) and the activist and tribal leader Bernaldina José Pedro (1945–2020).

## Language & religion

Macushi is a Cariban language of the Macushi-Kapon group and the most populous language in the family, written in the Latin script. Abbott (1991) describes its basic word order as OVS, with SOV used to foreground the subject; vowels may be nasal, marked [ã], and the word carries a high or low (unmarked) pitch — *átí* 'you go' against *àtí* 'he goes' — the high pitch falling on the final, penultimate or antepenultimate syllable. Numerals precede the noun they modify; Carson notes the native numeral system cannot express large numbers, so quantifiers are used instead. Macushi communities live in contact with Portuguese in Brazil, English in Guyana, Spanish in Venezuela, and Wapixana. Crevels (2012) lists the language as "potentially endangered" and the UNESCO Atlas of the World's Languages in Danger as "vulnerable," status 6b, Threatened; in the 1950s Macushi were hesitant to teach it to outsiders, and it was disparaged as "slang" beside official Portuguese. Religious life combines the *piaiman* and *kanaima* complex described above with long-established Catholic and Anglican mission presence.
