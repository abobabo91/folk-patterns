## Overview

The Coahuiltecans were a diverse grouping of Indigenous peoples of Aridoamerica who lived in present-day northeastern Mexico and southern Texas, on both sides of the Rio Grande. Their territory extended from the Gulf Coast near the mouth of the Guadalupe River toward San Antonio, Del Rio, Monclova, Monterrey, Ciudad Victoria, the Sierra de Tamaulipas, and the Tropic of Cancer. They were originally nomadic hunter-gatherers living in small autonomous bands without political unity above the band and extended family. More than 1000 bands or ethnic groups were recorded during Spanish colonial history. Their languages are now extinct, although revival efforts concern Pajalate and other languages. Coahuiltecan culture documents adaptation to the dry country of northern Mexico and southern Texas.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Woven mats** — At campsites, Coahuiltecan bands covered the frames of small circular huts with woven mats.

**Palm fronds** — Along the Rio Grande, some Coahuiltecan used palm fronds as a building material for more substantial dwellings.

### Clothing & dress

Coahuiltecan people adapted their clothing to the warm climate and wore minimal clothing. The sources do not distinguish men's and women's dress, identify named garments, or describe head coverings, belts, footwear, or ceremonial clothing.

### Architecture

Coahuiltecan camps contained small circular huts made from frames of four bent poles covered with woven mats. These dwellings were associated with mobile camps, where bands carried few possessions and moved seasonally to acquire food without depleting local resources. Encampments were generally small, consisting of a few homes and a few dozen people, although bands sometimes gathered in groups of hundreds. Along the Rio Grande, some Coahuiltecan lived more sedentary lives and constructed more substantial dwellings, using palm fronds as a building material.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that Coahuiltecan hunter-gatherers carried few possessions on their backs as they moved between seasonal food sources. Woven mats, bent poles, palm fronds, open fires, fire pits, and pits used to cook plant foods are the only material practices described.

## Music & performance

The sources provide little information about Coahuiltecan music. They record all-night dances called *mitotes*, which took place when Coahuiltecan people came together in large numbers. During these occasions, participants danced and took peyote as medicine.

## Dance & theatre

The named Coahuiltecan dance tradition in the sources is the *mitote*, an all-night dance held when people gathered in large numbers. The dance was connected with the taking of peyote as medicine.

## Festivals & rituals

They describe occasional large gatherings for all-night *mitotes*, during which people danced and took peyote as medicine. No named seasonal festival, harvest festival, wedding rite, coming-of-age ceremony, birth rite, or funeral practice is recorded.

## Foodways

Coahuiltecan foodways were based chiefly on hunting, fishing, shellfish gathering, and the collection of wild plants. Animals hunted for meat included deer, bison, peccary, armadillos, rabbits, rats, mice, snakes, lizards, frogs, salamanders, and snails. Fish was probably most important for groups living near the Rio Grande delta, and people also caught shellfish.

Most foods could be eaten raw. When cooking, Coahuiltecan people used open fires or fire pits. Pecans were an important protein source; they were gathered in the fall and stored for future use. The bulbs and root crowns of maguey, sotol, and lechuguilla were cooked in pits. Mesquite beans were ground into flour. Prickly pear, including its paddles and fruits, was an important summer food and could provide water when that resource was scarce. In winter, plant roots supplied important sustenance.

The Payaya band near San Antonio maintained ten different summer campsites within a 30 square-mile area, reflecting a regular seasonal movement through food-gathering areas.

## Oral tradition & literature

Catholic missionaries compiled vocabularies of several Coahuiltecan languages in the 18th and 19th centuries, but the language samples were too small to establish relationships among the languages. Modern revival efforts include work by the Tap Pilam Coahuiltecan Nation language director to revive Pajalate and work by Jessica L. Sánchez Flores.

## Language & religion

Coahuiltecan was proposed as a language family in John Wesley Powell’s 1891 classification, but most modern linguists reject the view that the peoples of southern Texas and adjacent Mexico spoke a single related group of languages. At least seven languages were spoken, including Comecrudo, Cotoname, and Coahuilteco or Pakawa. Other recorded names include Amotomanco, Aranama, Solano, Mamulique, and Garza. Comecrudo and Cotoname were spoken in the delta of the Rio Grande. Coahuilteco or Pakawa appears to have served as a lingua franca near the Catholic Missions at San Antonio in the 18th century.

The Coahuiltecan languages and cultures are now extinct, although language revival efforts continue. Little is known about original Coahuiltecan religions. The sources connect *mitotes* with all-night dancing and the taking of peyote as medicine. Catholic missionaries and Spanish missions later became significant forces in the region, including Mission San Antonio de Valero, established in 1718.
