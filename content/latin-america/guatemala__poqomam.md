## Overview

The Poqomam are a Maya people in Guatemala and El Salvador. Their indigenous language is also called Poqomam and is closely related to Poqomchiʼ. In Guatemala, Poqomam settlements are located in Chinautla, Palín, and San Luis Jilotepeque. Poqomam is spoken by 50,000 or so people in several small pockets in Guatemala, the largest of which is in Jalapa department. Before the Spanish Conquest, the Poqomam had their capital at Chinautla Viejo. Poqomam communities in Guatemala and El Salvador form the geographic and historical focus of the available sources.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources identify Chinautla Viejo as the pre-Conquest capital of the Poqomam.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do describe language-preservation and language-promotion efforts connected with Mayan languages in Guatemala. The establishment of the Academia de Lenguas Mayas de Guatemala is identified as one such effort; it is an autonomous state institution directed by Mayas. The sources also mention linguistic research by Mayas, foundations for language promotion, bilingual education programs, private “Maya schools,” nongovernmental associations supporting those schools, and Maya presses publishing in and about Mayan languages.

## Language & religion

Poqomam is a Mayan language closely related to Poqomchiʼ. It is spoken in municipalities of Escuintla, Jalapa, and Guatemala departments, including Chinautla, Mixco, San Luis Jilotepeque, San Pedro Pinula, San Carlos Alzatate, and Palín. The sources also state that the few Poqomam who remained in El Salvador live near the Guatemala border, in the departments of Santa Ana and Ahuachapan.

The Poqomam language has been documented through everyday greetings. These include “Nqakʼul ta awach” for “Hello,” “Saqʼwa” for “Good Morning,” “Sanaqʼiij” for “Good Afternoon,” “Qawaqʼaabʼ” for “Good Night,” “Qanke jat wilkaat” for “How are you?,” “Qanke xtisaqʼwa” for “How did you wake up?,” “Tiyoox tii” for “Thank you,” and “Xahʼoo” for “Bye.” The sources state that Poqomam’s agent focus is optional in all relevant contexts. They also describe language revitalization as including the 1996 Peace Accords, the 1999 referendum of the constitutional changes, and the May 2003 Guatemala congress passage of the “Law of National Languages.” The law states that Spanish is the official language of Guatemala while recognizing indigenous languages as essential parts of the national identity that must be promoted.
