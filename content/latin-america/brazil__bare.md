## Overview

The Baré, also called Hanera, are an Indigenous people related to the Werekena of northwest Brazil and Venezuela. In Brazil, they live mainly along the Xié River and the upper reaches of the Rio Negro, including the Cué-Cué/Marabitanas Indigenous Territory. In 2014, the Siasi/Sesai recorded 11,472 Baré people in Amazonas, Brazil, while Venezuela’s 2011 national census reported 5,044. The Baré originally spoke an Arawakan language, but most now speak Nheengatu, a lingua franca spread by the Carmelites during the colonial period. Their material and subsistence culture includes agriculture, hunting, fishing, gathering, piassava-fiber extraction, boatbuilding, weaving, basketry and pottery. Their history has also been shaped by violent exploitation and forced labor by Portuguese and Spanish merchants.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Cumare, curagua and moriche fiber weaving** — The Baré used the fibers of cumare, curagua and moriche for weaving. The fibers were dried in the sun and dyed red, purple and yellow.

**Chiquichique fiber** — Chiquichique fiber was used for fishing lines.

**Textiles and hammocks** — The Baré manufactured textiles and hammocks as part of their household material culture.

### Architecture

Most Baré and Werekena people live in small communities of log houses built around a wide area of clean sand. Some settlements include a Catholic or Protestant chapel, a small school and perhaps a medical station, while others consist only of houses.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Baré manufactured their own tools, wooden boats and paddles, baskets and pottery. Their traditional fishing equipment included harpoons, arrows, hooks, traps and nets.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that both Catholic and Protestant communities continue to use shamans who follow traditional practices for curing.

## Foodways

They state that most Baré communities are engaged during much of the year in agriculture, hunting, fishing and gathering. The sources also mention forest products and extracted products including cocoa, salsaparilha, piaçaba, puxuri, balata, rubber and sorva.

## Oral tradition & literature

A historical account says that the Jesuit Ignácio Szentmatonyi wrote in 1753 that the “Verikenas” inhabited the River “Issié” and spoke their own language. Later writers reported that they used knotted cords to communicate messages. The sources also say that much of Baré culture was forgotten during the long struggle with European colonialists and that most precolonial artifacts were lost.

## Language & religion

Baré is an extinct Arawakan language of Venezuela and Brazil. The Baré and Werekena originally spoke Baré and Warekena, respectively, but most Baré people now speak Nheengatu, a Tupian language and regional lingua franca spread by the Carmelites in the colonial period. Some communities of the Upper Xié still speak Warekena. Baré is closely related to the extinct languages Guinau and Mandawaca. Its last fluent speaker, Candelário da Silva, died on 2 February 1993; six Baré people still remembered something of the language in 2001. The language had two recognized subdivisions, Ihini, “the ones from there,” and Arihini, “the ones from here.” Baré communities downstream from the Cumati waterfall are mostly Protestant, while those upstream are mostly Catholic. Both groups continue to use shamans for traditional curing.
