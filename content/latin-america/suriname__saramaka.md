## Overview

The Saramaka, also called Saamaka or Saramacca, are one of six Maroon peoples in the Republic of Suriname and are also present in French Guiana. About 90,000 Saramaka live in Suriname, with about one-third in French Guiana after migration connected with warfare in Suriname. Their principal homeland lies along the Upper Suriname River and its tributaries, the Gaánlío and Pikílío; others live along the Lower Suriname River, in Paramaribo, and in French Guiana. The Saramaka language, Saramaccan, is an Atlantic creole historically related to Sranan Tongo and drawing vocabulary from West and Central African languages, English, Portuguese, Dutch, and Amerindian languages. Saramaka folk culture includes matrilineal village life, forest-based subsistence, woodcarving, decorative sewing, oral tradition, dance, drumming, divination, and elaborate funerary ceremonies.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Decorative sewing** — Saramaka women sew patchwork and embroidered clothing. The sources identify this sewing as an important form of women’s material production and as one of the gifts exchanged in courtship.

**Woven palm-frond construction** — Palm fronds are woven into house walls. The sources describe this as part of the construction of traditional houses rather than as a named textile tradition.

**Hammock production** — Hammocks are among the Western goods considered essential in Saramaka villages.

**Patchwork clothing** — Patchwork is used in women’s clothing. The sources give no vernacular name, named pattern, or specific technique beyond patchwork and embroidery.

**Embroidered clothing** — Women produce embroidered clothing. No particular garment, motif, or regional contrast is named.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no specific textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that Saramaka women sew patchwork and embroidered clothing, while an increasing number of clothing items are imported from the coast. Men’s and women’s clothing are not described in detail. Decorative sewing is also associated with courtship, since women’s sewing and men’s woodcarving may be exchanged as gifts.

### Architecture

Traditional villages average 100 to 200 residents and are located near rivers for water, transportation, and fishing. They contain an irregular arrangement of small houses, open-sided structures, domesticated trees, occasional chicken houses, shrines, and scattered patches of bush. Traditional Saramaka houses are compact, wide enough for a hammock and not much longer from front to back. Their walls are made of planks and woven palm fronds; roofs are traditionally thatched or increasingly made of corrugated metal. Houses generally lack windows but often have elaborately carved façades.

Horticultural camps contain permanent houses and shrines and are located several hours by canoe from villages. Since the Suriname civil war, increasing numbers of houses have followed a coastal, Western style, using concrete as well as wood, with windows and more expansive floor plans. Transmigration villages constructed in the 1960s were arranged in a European-style grid.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources describe pottery made by some Saramaka women, but give no named ceramic forms, glazes, or decorative conventions. Men carve wooden objects for domestic use, including stools, paddles, winnowing trays, cooking utensils, combs, and canoes. Women carve calabash bowls, and parts of plants are used to make needed goods. Baskets are made by some men. Imported goods include tools, pots, shotguns, powder, cloth, soap, kerosene, rum, outboard motors, transistor radios, tape recorders, and cell phones.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They state that body cicatrization was practiced by virtually all Saramaka women as late as the 1970s and 1980s, but had become relatively uncommon by the start of the 21st century. No design vocabulary, technique, or ritual meaning is provided.

## Music & performance

Saramaka culture includes numerous genres of singing, dance, drumming, and tale telling. Music and performance are especially prominent in funerary rites. Initial funeral rites include all-night drum, song, and dance performances, together with feasts for the ancestors and the telling of folktales. Several months later, a “second funeral” marks the end of mourning and includes the largest public gatherings in Saramaka society; it also features all-night drum, song, and dance performances.

Music and dance are likewise part of religious life. Gods and spirits are honored through prayers, libations, feasts, and dances. Ceremonial life includes public curing rites, rituals honoring kúnus, including snake gods and forest spirits, and ceremonies connected with the installation of political officials.

## Dance & theatre

Dance forms part of Saramaka ceremonial and religious life. It appears in all-night drum, song, and dance performances during initial funerary rites and the later “second funeral,” and in dances honoring gods and spirits. The sources also mention dance in connection with public curing rites, rituals for kúnus, and other ceremonies.

## Festivals & rituals

Saramaka ceremonial life is not organized by a fixed calendar. It is regulated by particular misfortunes and by interpretations reached through divination. The most important ceremonies include funerals and the appeasement of ancestors, public curing rites, rituals honoring kúnus—especially snake gods and forest spirits—and the installation of political officials.

Birth, death, and other life passages are surrounded by extensive rituals. A death initiates a complex series of rites lasting about a year and ending with the deceased’s transition to ancestor status. Initial rites last from one week to three months, depending on the importance of the deceased, and end with burial in an elaborately constructed coffin filled with personal belongings. Divination with the coffin, feasts for ancestors, all-night performances, and folktales form part of these rites. Months later, the second funeral ends the mourning period and symbolically drives the deceased’s ghost from the village.

Women enter seclusion during menstruation, a period regarded as transgressive and destructive of village order. The Saramaka expression for menstrual seclusion is also the expression for mourning.

## Foodways

Saramaka subsistence is based on shifting or swidden horticulture, hunting, fishing, and the gathering of wild forest products. Women carry out most horticulture and process crops for meals and storage. Rice is the most cultivated crop, using a dry hillside technique. Other crops include cassava, taro, okra, maize, plantains, bananas, sugarcane, and peanuts. Domesticated trees in villages include coconut, orange, breadfruit, papaya, and calabash.

Men hunt game with shotguns and conduct most fishing. Women gather products such as palm nuts. The sources state that the Saramaka historically imported a few key items, including salt, and later obtained pots, cloth, soap, kerosene, and rum through work on the coast and through village stores. Feasts for ancestors are part of funerary rites, but their foods are not specified.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Saramaka preserve a very rich oral tradition, and tale telling remains part of cultural life. Folktales are told during funerary ceremonies, including the initial rites and the second funeral.

Saramaccan has a history of published dictionaries, wordlists, literacy books, collections of folktales written by Saramaccans, and a complete translation of the New Testament. The sources name Christian Schumann’s 1778 *Saramaccanisch Deutsches Worter-Buch*, Johannes Riemer’s 1779 *Wörterbuch zur Erlernung der Saramakka-Neger-Sprache*, Jan Voorhoeve and Antoon Donicie’s 1963 wordlist *De Saramakaanse Woordenschat*, and later Dutch–Saramakaans and Saramakaans–Dutch works. Two books by Richard Price have been published in Saamakatongo: *Fesiten* and *Boo go a Kontukonde*. The Saramaccan orthography created by the Summer Institute of Linguistics is not in universal use.

## Language & religion

Saramaccan, also called Saamakatöngö or Saamakatongo, is a creole language spoken by people of West African descent near the Saramacca and Upper Suriname River, in Paramaribo, French Guiana, and the Netherlands. Its vocabulary derives from English, Portuguese, West and Central African languages, Dutch, and, in the broader description, Amerindian languages. Its African component includes Fon and other Gbe languages, Akan, Kikongo, and related Central African languages. Saramaccan has two main dialects described as the Upper Suriname River and Lower Suriname River dialects.

Saramaka religious life includes village deities, ancestors, forest spirits, snake gods, river gods, warrior spirits, and kúnus, the avenging spirits of wronged people or gods. Communication with supernatural powers takes place through spirit possession, oracle-bundles, dreams, and other forms of divination. About 25 per cent of Saramaka are described as nominal Christians, mainly Moravian, with others Roman Catholic; increasing numbers have converted to Evangelicalism.
