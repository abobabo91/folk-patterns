## Overview

The Wauja, also called Waura, are an Indigenous people of Brazil who live near the Upper Xingu River, in the Xingu Indigenous Park in Mato Grosso. Their language, Waurá, is an Arawakan language, and the entire population speaks it. The Wauja had a population of 487 in 2010. Their folk-culture traditions documented in the sources include pottery, basket weaving, village architecture, initiation rites, festivals, flute performance, singing, shamanic healing, and distinctive forms of leadership. The Wauja and Mehinako are Arawakan-speaking peoples native to the Upper Xingu River. Archaeological records indicate that communities in the region were mostly sedentary between 1000 and 1600, with villages organized around central plazas.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Basket weaving** — Men are traditionally responsible for weaving baskets.

### Clothing & dress

During *lamurikuma*, women wear headdresses, armbands, and body paint while singing together. In the female initiation ritual *kajatapá*, older women place a cord called *uluri* on the initiated girls.

### Architecture

The largest Wauja settlement is the village of *Piyulaga*. It is centred on a square containing the *kuwakuho*, described as the “house of the flutes.” Residential homes surrounding the square are oval-shaped and made from *imperata brasiliensis*, a type of tropical grass. Political meetings, rituals, and festivals occur on the square. Traditionally, extended families consisting of blood relations lived in the same homes; in modern times, most residences house one or two couples and their children.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Wauja pottery is made from clay. Men traditionally collect clay for ceramic construction, while women grind shells to mix with the clay, manually shape the pieces, and paint them with pigments obtained from plants and fruits. Wauja pottery is sold in cities and used as a bargaining chip with other villages. Men also collect wood to make the *matapu*, an instrument stored in the *kuwakuho*.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources name headdresses, armbands, body paint, and the cord called *uluri*. Women wear the headdresses, armbands, and body paint during *lamurikuma*. During *kajatapá*, older women place an *uluri* on girls who have undergone the initiation.

## Music & performance

Flutes, singing, and ritual performance have important places in Wauja social life. The *kuwakuho* is the “house of the flutes,” and the *matapu* is made from wood and stored there. Women are forbidden from entering the *kuwakuho*. When the *matapu* is played during *akãi*, women remain in their homes with the doors closed so that they cannot see it.

During the male initiation ritual *pohoka*, flutes are played on the preceding day, and villagers sing throughout the night before the boy’s ear is pierced. During *kajatapá*, singing takes place throughout the day, and nearby villages are invited to attend. In *lamurikuma*, women wear ceremonial adornment and sing together.

The Wauja recognize three groups of shamans. The *yakapá* identifies the *apapaatai* or sorcerer responsible for a disease; the *pukaiwekeho* sings healing songs; and the *yatamá* is responsible for *hoká*, tobacco smoke that can cause healing.

## Dance & theatre

Dance occurs during *kajatapá*, when guests go fishing before returning to the village to dance. During *lamurikuma*, women sing together and can take part in fights in the village square. The sources do not distinguish a separate entertainment dance tradition from ceremonial dance.

## Festivals & rituals

*Akãi* occurs between September and November, when souari nuts begin falling from the trees. Men collect wood to make the *matapu*, which is played in the *kuwakuho*. While it is played, women remain inside their homes with the doors closed. After the sound ends, people bring food to the central square. Following the meal, men sing *kuri* and settle disputes. The festival lasts five days.

*Lamurikuma* takes place during the dry season, on a date decided by the *amunaw-iyajo*, the village chieftain. Neighbouring villages are invited. Women wear headdresses, armbands, and body paint, sing together, express their displeasure with men in the village square, and can take part in fights.

Boys come of age at 10 years old. The male initiation ritual *pohoka*, in which the boy’s ear is pierced, usually occurs during August. During the ritual period, the boy cannot eat fish, and his parents and godparents cannot have sexual intercourse. Girls come of age when they begin menstruating, but *kajatapá* is performed only for girls who are to become *amuluneju*, a female leader. The ritual concludes when older women place an *uluri* on the initiated girls.

For serious diseases caused by *apapaatai*, a feast called *pukai* is held and funded by the patient. After recovery, the patient must hold *pukai* repeatedly for a period determined by the shaman.

## Foodways

They state that boys undergoing *pohoka* cannot eat fish. During *akãi*, after the *matapu* performance ends, people leave their homes and bring food to the central square, where they eat. The sources also mention souari nuts in connection with the festival season, because *akãi* occurs when these nuts begin falling from the trees.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that men sing *kuri* during *akãi*, that villagers sing before the *pohoka* ear piercing, and that the *pukaiwekeho* sings healing songs. These references do not provide information about a broader oral-literary tradition.

## Language & religion

Waurá, also called Wauja, is an Arawakan language spoken in the Xingu Indigenous Park of Brazil by the Waujá people. It is “partially intelligible” with Mehináku, and the entire population speaks it. The sources describe its consonant and vowel sounds.

Wauja religious belief centres on shamanism and the idea that the human soul is fragmented and vulnerable to disease caused by *apapaatai*, dangerous non-human spirits that can also provide benefits. Men and women can both be shamans, although women cannot reach the highest training. After death, the soul goes to the land of the dead, *ywuejokupoho*, and travels along a path called *yakunapu* to the *ietula* tree. There, it must climb a ladder called *mapi'ya*. A person who dies in an accident goes to *Tamalaipoho*, a village in *ywuejokupoho*.
