## Overview

The Xipaya, also known as Xipai or Shipaya, are an Indigenous people of the Brazilian Amazon whose traditional territory lies in the Iriri and Curuá river basins of Pará. Today they inhabit recognized Indigenous territories in the Xingu region, particularly along the Iriri River, with villages such as Tukamã and Kaarimã serving as centers of community life and cultural revival. Historically skilled navigators and canoe builders, they have endured displacement, violence, forced labor, disease, and assimilation policies. Their society emphasizes extended families, collective decision-making, traditional leadership, and women’s roles in cultural transmission and resistance. The Xipaya language is described as belonging to the Jê family, while the language article identifies it as one of approximately 70 Tupian languages and closely related to Yuruna.

## Material culture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Xipaya craftwork includes basketry and pottery, which remain important for subsistence and cultural identity. The Xipaya were also historically skilled in building flat-bottomed canoes known as *ubás*, which enabled extensive travel across the region.

## Music & performance

They describe communal festivals involving Xipaya and neighboring riverine peoples known as *beiradeiros*. The December hunting festival and the Saint Sebastian Festival are described as occasions for communal celebration, food, and football matches. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription concerns the Xipaya or provides additional information about their music.

## Festivals & rituals

The Xipaya hold a December hunting festival and a Saint Sebastian Festival. These events bring together Xipaya and neighboring riverine peoples, or *beiradeiros*, for communal celebration, food, and football matches.

They do describe collective decision-making, traditional leadership, and women’s roles in cultural transmission and resistance.

## Foodways

The Xipaya practice swidden, or slash-and-burn, agriculture. They cultivate cassava, yams, and fruit trees including cupuaçu, mango, and papaya. They raise chickens and ducks, fish throughout the year in the Iriri River, and hunt deer, peccary, and river turtles. Food is also mentioned in connection with the December hunting festival and the Saint Sebastian Festival, which bring together Xipaya and neighboring riverine peoples for communal celebration and meals.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that the Xipaya began a process of reunification in the 1970s, rebuilding villages on the Iriri River and reclaiming territory under the leadership of families such as that of Tereza Xipaya de Carvalho. They also describe continuing efforts to revitalize Xipaya language, traditions, and governance.

## Language & religion

Xipaya is described as an endangered language spoken in the Pará region of Brazil. The language article identifies it as one of approximately 70 Tupian languages of South America and says that it is closely related to Yuruna. It records that, at last count, Xipaya was spoken by one elderly woman, Maria Xipaya, in Altamira, Pará. The ethnographic article instead describes the Xipaya language as part of the Jê family and states that it is spoken by elders, while Portuguese is now widely used.

They mention the Saint Sebastian Festival.
