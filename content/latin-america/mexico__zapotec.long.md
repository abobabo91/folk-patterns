## Overview

The Zapotec — *Bën za* in Valley Zapotec, "People of the clouds" — are an Indigenous people of Mexico concentrated in the southern state of Oaxaca, with communities in neighbouring Puebla, Veracruz and Guerrero and, after decades of labour migration, in Mexico City, Los Angeles, California's Central Valley and New Jersey. Estimates put the present population at 400,000 to 650,000; the 2020 census counted about 500,000 speakers of a Zapotec language, 5.7% of them monolingual. The languages form a main branch of the Oto-Manguean family, grouped with Chatino as Zapotecan. Four regional divisions are recognised: the *istmeños* of the Isthmus of Tehuantepec, the *serranos* of the Sierra Madre de Oaxaca, the southern Zapotec of the Sierra Sur, and the Central Valley Zapotec of the Y-shaped valleys of Etla, Ocotlán and Mitla around the Atoyac River. Their pre-Columbian civilisation, centred on Monte Albán, produced one of Mesoamerica's first writing systems.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Huipil** — The women's upper garment, in the two collected examples built from a rectangle with a cut neck opening. One is blue cotton printed with white flowers (catalogued as possibly Manchester cotton), lined with red cotton printed with white polka dots, the round neck bordered with machine-stitched red and yellow stripes and the edge carrying a band of red and yellow floral embroidery. A festive variant is made of organza with green brocade, with a lace collar, lace sleeves and lace hem all trimmed with red ribbon — imported industrial cloth and trim absorbed into an Indigenous cut.

**Blusa** — A white plain-weave blouse with short gathered sleeves from Oaxaca, satin-stitch embroidered in red, yellow, blue and green with flower motifs around the neckline front and back and on two patch pockets at the lower front, finished with a red yarn tassel below the neck opening.

**Falda** — A wrap skirt of two flared panels with ties, woven of red cotton with weft-stripe patterning in black, white, yellow and green. Two diagonal V-shaped fields and a rectangle along the lower edge are embroidered in satin stitch with geometric motifs in blue, orange, purple, yellow, green and white.

**Sashes and girdles** — Narrow bands woven on the backstrap loom with warp-patterned cotton: a white sash with brown and black edge stripes and a central geometric field read by the cataloguer as scorpions; another with a brown and black human dancer from the *Danza de la Pluma* on a yellow ground. Women's girdles use a red wool floating-warp geometric pattern down a white centre, or are woven of silk with stiffening strips carried in the weft for strength and a braided fringe.

**Rebozo** — A wool shawl woven on the backstrap loom, white-grounded with warp-patterned multicoloured stripes and warp fringe finger-knotted at each end.

**Morral** — A knitted wool shoulder bag in dark brown, worked with two mirror-image bird-like figures in white perched on a "T", with rows of letters — several reversed — running along the upper and lower edges.

**Chichicaxtle fibre** — A 1940s ethnographic photograph from the district of San Carlos Yautepec records the spinning of bark fibre of chichicaxtle, a thread source distinct from the cotton and wool of the loom traditions.

**Motif vocabulary.** Floral sprays in satin stitch (blouse necklines, huipil borders); cut-out flowers and leaves on pottery walls; warp-patterned geometric fields including forms identified as scorpions; a single plumed dancer from the Danza de la Pluma; mirrored birds flanking a "T"; fish, scratched into gourd and modelled in clay; diagonal black bands on plaited palm baskets; edge stripes in brown, black, yellow and burgundy.

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress in the collections combines the huipil or embroidered blusa with a wrapped falda, bound at the waist by a woven *girdle* or sash. The head is dressed with a *rodete*, a turban of black wool hair cords — two cord cables joined at one end and left as a long fringe at the other — and hair is also bound with ornaments of braided green wool wrapped in violet and orange silk, branching into multiple tassels. On the Isthmus the Juchiteca skirt and huipil constitute the recognised "native outfit", worn by women and by *muxes*, the third-gender members of Isthmus Zapotec communities. Men's festival dress appears in photographs of Oaxacan musicians, who perform in white clothing and sombreros. Clothing manufacture is itself a Zapotec trade: treadle sewing machines arrived around 1940 and from about 1960 women who had woven since pre-Columbian times began making and designing men's ready-made shirts and trousers for local and export markets. In the sixteenth century, Zapotec warfare used armour of cotton.

### Architecture

The Zapotecs were a sedentary people living in villages and towns, in houses built of stone and mortar. The monumental tradition is best documented at Monte Albán, founded on a mountain in the no-man's-land overlooking the three Central Valleys and the largest city in the southern Mexican highlands until about AD 700: its main plaza carries monumental buildings, ball courts and tombs, and the arrowhead-shaped Building J, the "Conquest Slab", is faced with more than forty carved stones bearing hieroglyphic place-glyphs and ruler heads. Religious building centred on the *yo hopèe*, "house of the vital force", where priests performed rites. Mitla, seat of the paramount priest, is known for its ruins, and other centres include Lambityeco, Dainzú, Yagul, San José Mogote, El Palmillo and Zaachila. Water engineering was part of the built landscape: canal irrigation drew on small streams, and a dam and canal on the south-eastern flank of the mountain carried water to Monte Albán, 400 metres above the valley floor. A tomb excavated in Oaxaca in January 2026 has a frieze of an owl's head over the burial-chamber entrance, polychrome murals of figures carrying bags of copal, and stone-carved guardians at the threshold. The sources do not describe present-day Zapotec house form.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The signature ware in the collections is burnished black pottery, one piece inscribed on the base "Dona Rosa SB coyotepec Oaxaca Mexico". Vessel forms include a round pot with three prongs on the base, a tapering neck and a flaring horizontal lip, its wall pierced with cut-out flowers and leaves; a second pot with flowers etched into the side; and an *olla* with orange slip, black firing marks across the lower half and a burnished sheen. Small modelled work is abundant: figurines and toys in the form of parrot, bird, lizard, lion, fish and turtle, with wings and eyes incised and vent holes pierced; whistles shaped as peacock, rooster and duck with the mouthpiece in the tail; and a toy flute of burnished black clay with four finger holes and a snake carved between them. Household equipment includes a gourd strainer, hollowed and pierced with small holes, its stained upper half scratched with fish; and palm baskets (*cesto de palma*) plaited in two-over-two twill over a square foundation with a round bottom and a rolled rim. Carved and painted wooden figures were made for sale at the Day of the Dead festival: a bullfighting group with a male spectator and a bull fitted with cane pieces to hold fireworks, a twisted-bodied horse and rider, and a female figure with a skull head whose spinal column shows between shirt and skirt. Hunting equipment survives in a 1948 photograph of Luis Sanchez, described as the last blowgun maker of Oaxaca de Juárez, demonstrating his *cerbatana*.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Monte Albán's tombs and grave goods included finely worked gold jewelry, and Zapotec and Mixtec artisans resident in the Aztec capital Tenochtitlan fashioned jewelry for the *tlatoanis*, Moctezuma II among them. A dedication cache in Mound III at the Cuilapan Temple Pyramid, dated to AD 700, held many jade beads, two jade earspools, three obsidian blades, shells, stones, a pearl and small bird bones; jade was valued for its scarcity and worked by elite artists, obsidian blades belonged to bloodletting, and shell and pearl stood for the underworld. Present-day adornment in the collections is ceramic and fibre rather than metal: a necklace strung from seven burnished black pottery birds alternating with black pottery fish, the wool and silk hair tassels, and the *rodete* hair cords.

## Music & performance

Zapotec music appears in the sources chiefly through performance context rather than organology. A set of black-and-white photographs from Oaxaca shows a line of musicians in white clothing and sombreros playing their instruments in an open space outside a large building, accompanying dancers in elaborate painted-feather headdresses, with crowds of spectators and, at one edge, a seated official in uniform with a sabre. The sources do not name the instruments played. Pottery whistles and a toy flute with four finger holes, both in burnished black ware, document sound-making objects of the household. One distinct vocal-adjacent practice is recorded: whistling, used by Zapotec people to communicate in a way their colonizers would not detect — a practice that sits alongside the tonal structure of the languages themselves, where up to four tonemes distinguish meaning and tones are used for word play. Zapotec-language broadcasting is extensive: the CDI stations XEGLO at Guelatao de Juárez and XEQIN-AM at San Quintín in Baja California carry Zapotec programming, and on the Isthmus the commercial Radio TEKA (1030 AM) is joined by community stations, most notably Radio Totopo (102.5 FM) in Juchitán and Radio Atempa in San Blas Atempa.

## Dance & theatre

The one named dance in the sources is the **Danza de la Pluma**, whose plumed dancer is woven as a figure into the central band of a backstrap-loom sash — a dance important enough to become a textile motif. The Oaxaca photographs show its performance setting: dancers in elaborate headdresses of painted feathers, at times turned to face one another, at times facing the musicians, in the open space before a large building and ringed by spectators. Dance has a specific social function in the segregation of the sexes that structures much Zapotec social life: men and women work separately and remain apart during ritual occasions except when dancing. The *velas* of the Isthmus are the reference point for public assembly — political demonstrations organised by the Coalición Obrera Campesina Estudiantil del Istmo are described as resembling them. The sources used do not describe a Zapotec theatre or puppet tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The major pilgrimage named in the sources is to the *Virgen de Juquila*, a Marian devotion founded at Santa Catarina Juquila in Oaxaca; many Zapotec Catholics travel annually to visit the statue during festivities running from 7 to 9 December. The Day of the Dead festival is attested materially rather than descriptively: carved wooden horses and riders were made for sale at it, and a skull-headed female figurine was carved under its inspiration. Pre-Hispanic ritual included dedication of buildings by cache — the Cuilapan Temple Pyramid cache of jade, obsidian, shell, pearl and bird bone, dedicating the structure to power, sacrifice and the link between underworld and cosmos — and there is evidence for human sacrifice in some rites. Church and state were not separate at the conquest: a Zapotec lord was required to be trained in religious practice before taking power, and the priests, *Copa pitào*, were drawn mostly from the nobility, with commoners admitted only to the lower ranks. Life-cycle practice is documented at both ends. Burial of the dead with valuables survives from ancient usage. Marriage is monogamous in value, women are generally free to choose partners, and in some communities the bloody sheet from the wedding night is publicly displayed — a Mediterranean custom brought by the Spaniards. Muxes commonly take on caregiving in the household, including remaining at home to care for elderly parents, and one account describes a young *mayordomo* treated with complete deference.

## Foodways

The Central Valleys are considered one of the cradles of maize, and maize is correspondingly central to Zapotec religion: the primary god of the Valley Zapotecs, *Pitao Cozobi*, was the god of maize and agriculture, and *Coqui Lao* was god of turkey hens. The agricultural year runs against a dry season from November to May; along the rivers it is possible to plant and harvest twice, and canal irrigation extended cultivation to fields away from the Atoyac, with crops also grown on less fertile foothill soils as Monte Albán's population grew. Farming innovation produced enough surplus to pay tribute to the Spanish conquerors and still feed the population through natural disaster and epidemic. Spanish settlers introduced animals unknown in Oaxaca before — horses, cows, goats, sheep, chickens, mules and oxen. Wild game was taken with the blowgun, still made in Oaxaca de Juárez in the 1940s. Labour around food was sharply gendered: men carried out planting and plowing, women weeded and harvested and, above all, supplied male workers with food during agricultural work, alongside the household duties of cooking and child care. The exonym *Zapotec* itself derives, on the long-standing reading, from Nahuatl *tzapotēcah*, "inhabitants of the place of sapote", though recent UNAM work argues for a hybrid form from *za* (cloud) and *pochteca* (merchant). The sources used do not name specific Zapotec dishes or beverages.

## Oral tradition & literature

Several origin legends are recorded, none transcribed before the Spanish conquest. In one, the Zapotec are the original people of the valley of Oaxaca, born from rocks or descended from big cats — pumas, jaguars and ocelots. In another they settled the Oaxaca valley after founding the Toltec empire and descend from the people of Chicomoztoc. Contemporary and historical Zapotec accounts have ancestors emerging from the earth or from caves, or turning into people from trees or jaguars, while the governing elite held that they descended from supernatural beings living among the clouds and would return to the clouds at death — the belief behind the self-designation *Be'ena' Za'a*, the Cloud People. Written tradition is old and interrupted: a logosyllabic script, one of Mesoamerica's first, with a glyph per syllable, is attested from about 500 BC on the Danzante stone (Monument 3) from San José Mogote and went out of use in the late Classic period; it remains undeciphered apart from calendar signs. Franciscan or Dominican friars published a vocabulary and grammar of Antequera Zapotec in the sixteenth century, the Isthmus Zapotec alphabet in use today was founded in the 1950s from work reaching back to the 1920s, and contemporary literature in Zapotec has been written by Irma Pineda, Natalia Toledo and Felipe Lopez. Andrés Henestrosa was a Zapotec scholar of the language and culture.

## Language & religion

Zapotec is a close-knit family of around fifty to sixty varieties in the Oto-Manguean stock, with a time depth comparable to the Romance languages; many varieties are mutually unintelligible and the Mexican government officially recognises sixty. Names for the language vary locally — *Diidxazá* in Juchitán, *Didxsaj* in Mitla, *Diža'xon* in Zoogocho, *Di'zhke'* in Coatec, *Dí'zdéh* in Miahuatec, *Tiits Së* in Santa Catarina Quioquitani — the first element meaning 'word'. All varieties are tonal and all contrast phonation types in vowels. Viability varies sharply: Loxicha Zapotec has over 70,000 speakers while San Agustín Mixtepec Zapotec reportedly has one. The Zapotec are now largely Catholic, with older practice visible inside it: San Pedro resembles the rain god *Cocijo*, and the dead are still buried with valuables. The pre-conquest religion was polytheistic, built around *pè*, the life force residing in wind and breath, with deities including Cocijo, *Pitao Cozaana*, *Pitao Hichaana*, *Pitao Pezelao*, *Copijcha*, *Coquihani* and *Pitao Xicala*, and a hierarchy topped by the *ouija-tào*, the great seer, who resided at Mitla.
