## Overview

The Kankuamo, also known as Kankuaka, Kankui or Kankuané, are an Indigenous people of Colombia living on the southern slopes of the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta and extending northward to the César department. Their communities include Atánquez, Guatapurí, Chemesquemena, Los Haticos, La Mina and Rio Seco. The population is estimated at 15,000 individuals. Historically, they spoke Sánha, a dialect of the Atanque language within the Chibchan family; today, the Kankuamo language is extinct and the people speak only Spanish. Their culture is distinguished in the sources by its relationship with the sacred Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta, distinctive clothing, agricultural economy and efforts to preserve knowledge of their former language.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Kankuamo clothing differs from that of the other Indigenous peoples of the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta. Women wear two overlapping cloths, while men wear short trousers and a hat made of reed.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta as sacred to the Kankuamo and state that their laws are borne from nature. In their native tongue, they call the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta *Umunukunu*.

## Foodways

The Kankuamo economy is based on agriculture. The sources name yuca, ñame, pigeon peas, corn, bananas, coca leaves, fique, arracacha and starch as its main products.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do report efforts, undertaken with members of the Kankuamo community and other Indigenous people of the Sierra who know the language, and supported by experts, to develop written materials promoting the dissemination of Kankuamo among children and young people.

## Language & religion

Kankuamo, also known as Kakatukua, Kampanake, Atanques or Atanque, is described as an extinct Chibchan language once spoken in the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta, particularly in communities of the Atánquez district. The language was spoken on the southeastern slope of the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta. Kankuamo people communicate mainly in Spanish, although tradition holds that speakers of the native language can still be found in the páramos, especially among some elders and mamos, described as spiritual leaders. The language is closely related to Wiwa in terms of phonetic innovations and belongs to the Arwako subgroup of Chibcha. Traces are preserved in regional toponyms, including the suffixes *-ka*, meaning “place, site,” and *-kua*, meaning “bower.”
