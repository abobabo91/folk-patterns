## Overview

The Q'eqchi' are a Mayan people whose language is the third largest in Guatemala. They live in the mountainous Verapaz region of Guatemala, as well as parts of Belize and Mexico. The available sources focus on their material culture, especially dance costume and religious texts, rather than providing a complete picture of Q'eqchi' life. Their language and traditions have survived partly because of the difficult mountain terrain of their homeland.

## Material culture

Q'eqchi' material culture is documented mainly through dance costumes, religious objects, and woven goods held in museums.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Most Q'eqchi' textiles in the record are cotton or synthetic fabrics made for dance costume rather than daily wear.

- **Embroidered blouse** — A short-sleeved lemon-coloured synthetic blouse with blue-and-white embroidered yoke featuring a blue dove and flowers.
- **Dance costume in red cotton** — A matching jacket, breeches and skirt in red cotton with black-and-white floral design, lined in white and trimmed with lace.
- **Red cotton kerchief** — A red square with black-and-white curvilinear edge band and repeating ring design at centre, probably part of dance costume.
- **Embroidered calendar cloth** — A white cotton cloth embroidered with symbols and names of the Mayan calendar in multi-coloured acrylic wool.
- **Dance head-dresses** — Cloth rectangles or fibre constructions decorated with bundles of coloured wool representing hair, worn in devil dancer costumes.

### Clothing & dress

Q'eqchi' documented dress divides into everyday wear and elaborate masked dance costume.

- **Embroidered blouse** — The only everyday garment recorded, made of synthetic fabric with elasticated cuffs and machine-friendly construction for ordinary women's use.
- **Devil dancer's cloth** — A piece of fabric catalogued as part of devil dancer's costume but not further described.

### Architecture

The sources do not describe Q'eqchi' house form or building materials.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Q'eqchi' craftspeople made pottery censers and woven carrying bags for practical use and ritual purposes.

- **Incensario** — A hand-modelled pottery censer with flat base and conical body, perforated and fitted with suspension loop, used for burning copal incense.
- **Netted-fibre carrying-bag** — A rounded bag with loop handles, used for carrying goods and practical daily tasks.
- **Devil-dance rattle** — An instrument assembled from tin, wood and cloth, tied to masked performance rather than independent music-making.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment in the record appears only as part of masked dance costume.

- **Mask bells and ears** — Bells hung from masks and tin or leather ears nailed to mask temples, worn by devil dancers.
- **Wool hair-bundles** — Blue and purple wool bundles attached to dance head-dresses to represent hair.

## Music & performance

Q'eqchi' music is documented mainly through religious songs and instruments connected to masked dance.

- **Gourd instrument** — An oblong gourd with carved ridges, holes on sides and end, curved wood insert, and red-green painted surface.
- **Qanimaaq Xloqʼal li Qaawaʼ** — A Catholic hymnal meaning 'We praise the Lord', very popular among Q'eqchi' Catholics and regularly updated with new songs.

## Dance & theatre

The devil dance is the only masked dance tradition documented, known through its carved wooden masks and costume pieces.

- **Devil dance** — A masked performance with crudely carved wooden masks painted in combinations of black, red, white, yellow and green.
- **Devil-dance masks with horns** — Monstrous carved heads with three detachable horns set into forehead, some with metal bells, leather ears, and elaborate animal and serpent decorations.
- **Animal-faced masks** — Masks carved as monkey, boar, and pig faces, painted in bright colours with added cloth snakes and tin ears.
- **Carved serpent figures** — Dance properties with green-painted wooden heads and stuffed cotton bodies, some coiled into bundles.

## Festivals & rituals

Q'eqchiʼ ritual life is documented through incense use, masked dance, and the Mayan calendar.

- **Copal incense burning** (*copal*) — Burned copal incense used in pottery censers for ceremonial purposes, with residue preserved in museum objects.
- **Devil dance ritual** (*devil dance*) — A masked performance with ritual or calendrical occasion, accompanied by carved serpent figures and elaborate costume.
- **Mayan calendar display** — The Mayan calendar itself treated as subject for textile art, embroidered on cloth with symbols and names.

## Foodways

The sources do not describe Q'eqchi' foodways or cooking practices.

## Oral tradition & literature

Q'eqchiʼ is becoming a written language through educational, religious, and recently secular texts.

- **Aprendamos Kekchí** — A widely used Spanish-language Q'eqchi' learning workbook published by the Summer Institute of Linguistics.
- **Qanimaaq Xloqʼal li Qaawaʼ** — A Catholic hymnal meaning 'We praise the Lord', very popular and regularly updated with new songs.
- **Q'eqchi' Popol Wuj** (*Popol Wuj*) — A free translation of the K'iche' creation narrative by Q'eqchi' translator Rigoberto Baq Qaal.
- **Q'eqchi' folk tales collection** — A collection of traditional Q'eqchi' stories, part of recent secular writing in the language.
- **Guatemalan Constitution in Q'eqchi'** — The national constitution translated into Q'eqchiʼ as part of postcolonial language standardization.

## Language & religion

Q'eqchiʼ is an ergative-absolutive Mayan language with 29 consonants and 10 vowels, written in the ALMG standard since 1990.

- **Q'eqchiʼ language** (*Q'eqchiʼ*) — A Quichean-branch Mayan language with stress on the final syllable, basic verb-object-subject word order, and two sets of pronominal affixes.
- **ALMG orthography** (*ALMG*) — The Academia de Lenguas Mayas de Guatemala's standard writing system adopted in 1990, replacing '7' with the apostrophe.
- **Eastern and western dialects** — Two dialect groups identified by linguist Terrence Kaufman, with eastern forms in Lanquín, Chahal, Chahabón and Senahú.

## Glossary

- *Q'eqchi'* — Mayan language of Guatemala, Belize, and Mexico
- *incensario* — Hand-modelled pottery censer used for burning copal in ritual
- *devil dance* — Masked performance tradition with carved wooden masks and elaborate costume
- *copal* — Incense resin burned in ceremonial rituals
- *Verapaz* — 'True peace', Spanish colonial name for Q'eqchi' homeland in Guatemala
- *Quichean* — Branch of Mayan language family to which Q'eqchi' belongs
- *Qanimaaq Xloqʼal li Qaawaʼ* — 'We praise the Lord', popular Q'eqchi' Catholic hymnal
- *Popol Wuj* — K'iche' Mayan creation narrative and sacred text
- *ALMG* — Academia de Lenguas Mayas de Guatemala, Q'eqchi' standard orthography since 1990
- *ergative-absolutive* — Grammatical system distinguishing agent and patient in Q'eqchi' verbs
- *Mayan calendar* — Traditional system of timekeeping used in Q'eqchi' ritual and art
- *netted-fibre carrying-bag* — Woven bag with loop handles used for transport in daily life
