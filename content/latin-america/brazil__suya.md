## Overview

The Suyá, who call themselves Kisêdjê, are an Indigenous people of Brazil living at the headwaters of the Xingu River and today in a village on the banks of the Suiá-Miçu River. Their population increased from 123 in 1970 to 330 in 2010; another account describes the community as numbering about two hundred inhabitants, while the article on Suyá music describes a tribe of about 150 people. They speak Kĩsêdjê, a Northern Jê language of the Jê and Macro-Jê families, closely related to Tapayúna. The Suyá are especially documented for their vocal music, ceremonial songs, oral traditions, body ornamentation, and collective social life. Their history includes migration to the Xingu region around 1840, cultural exchange with neighboring groups, and the later effects of disease, medical care, and the establishment of the Xingu Indigenous Park in the 1960s.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that the Suyá share clothes within the community. Body ornaments are described as significant markers of age and status. Historical accounts emphasize disks worn in the ears and lower lips, while ceremonial initiation includes the piercing of lips and ears.

### Architecture

Suyá houses are set up in a circle around the village square, where most ceremonies take place. The community also has a men’s meeting house, where singing occurs frequently.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention canoes and hammocks as aspects of Xingu culture adopted by the Suyá, and they describe wooden disks placed in pierced lower lips. Other everyday objects connected with Suyá music include rattles, flutes, gourds, wooden shafts, cotton, burity-palm fiber, seeds, and feathers.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Suyá body ornamentation includes ear and lip disks, also called lip plates. Historically, after marriage, Suyá men pierced the lower lip and placed a small wooden disk inside it; the disk was gradually enlarged, permanently changing the size of the lip. The Suyá decorated their earlobes and lower lips with heavy disks, which were associated with the importance of vocalization and listening. Body ornaments also marked age and status.

## Music & performance

Music is a central part of Suyá everyday and ceremonial life. Most Suyá music is vocal because it is transmitted orally. Singing is used in myth telling, speech making, ceremonies, and the initiation of boys. Boys learn particular songs under adult supervision in a special forest camp a short distance from the village. Some musical forms are restricted to adults, political leaders, or ceremonial leaders.

The Suyá word *ngére* means “music,” “ceremony,” and a specific genre of songs. The two principal genres are *akia*, a shouted song performed in a high register, and *ngére*, sung in a lower register and usually in unison during ceremonies. Both are divided into *kradi*, the first half, and *sindaw*, the second half. Each half contains *kwd kaikaw* (“song syllables”), *sinti sülü* (“approaching the name”), *sinti iarén* (“telling the name”), and *kuré* (“coda”). These are musical sections rather than merely divisions of the text.

Instruments play a small role. Singing may be accompanied by rattles or flutes. The rattle *hwin krā* is made from individual piqui pits strung on a central string with hand-spun cotton or burity-palm fiber; it may be held in the hands or tied to the knees. Other rattles use deer, tapir, or wild-pig hoofs. A gourd rattle contains seeds and has a wooden shaft, macaw feathers, and parrot-wing feathers. Red and black rattles are carried by different social groups in one ceremony. The sources also mention alto Xingu flutes and Upper Xingu woodwind instruments.

## Dance & theatre

 They state that idiophones accompany singing and dancing, and that ceremonial groups may chase singer-dancers and break their rattles. Suyá ceremonies were organized around rites of passage, especially the initiation of boys into the men’s house.

## Festivals & rituals

 They describe ceremonies connected with social life, initiation, healing, myth, and rites of passage. Suyá ceremonies were long, with a ceremonial period often lasting several months, and were organized especially around the initiation of boys into the men’s house.

Initiation involved teaching boys particular songs in a forest camp under adult supervision. The songs marked their passage into adulthood, and the initiation ceremonies included the piercing of lips and ears. Ceremonial singing was performed according to age, sex, and social group. Some verbal forms were restricted to a few people: invocations were private expressions used for healing and referred to attributes of animals, plants, or other natural objects.

## Foodways

 They state that the Suyá hunt, fish, gather supplies, and trade with frontier settlements to meet their basic needs. After migrating to the Xingu region, they adopted food-preparation techniques associated with neighboring groups. One principle described in the sources is that all food must be cooked before it is eaten.

Suyá myths explain the acquisition of garden crops through the mouse and connect the use of garden crops as food with the mouse ceremony myth. The sources also state that the Suyá acquired fire from the jaguar, while garden crops came from the mouse. They provide no information about tea, coffee, alcohol, dietary prohibitions, or named cooking vessels.

## Oral tradition & literature

Suyá oral tradition includes myths, songs, speech making, instructions, invocations, and forms of ceremonial expression. Songs derive from ancient ceremonial songs associated with myths, from outsiders who taught songs or myths, and from “men without spirits,” who are said to hear songs from animals, plants, and insects and bring new songs to the community.

The Suyá creation myth was memorized, while the story of the origin of corn could be told anywhere by anyone. This story is connected with the mouse ceremony myth, through which the Suyá learn to use garden crops as food. The sources state that there was no single way to tell a myth. They also describe invocations as based on animal metaphors: an invocation for an easy birth was called “small fish” because a fish can slip easily out of someone’s hands.

The Suyá community places strong importance on names. Names are understood as central to a person’s identity and to the groups to which that person belongs.

## Language & religion

Kĩsêdjê is a Northern Jê language in the Jê and Macro-Jê families. It is closely related to Tapayúna, and together the two languages form the Tapajós branch of Northern Jê. The shared past on the Tapajós River remains part of the oral history of Kĩsêdjê and Tapayúna. The sources describe phonological and grammatical features of Kĩsêdjê, including distinctions between finite and nonfinite verb forms and nominative–absolutive alignment in future, progressive, continuous, completive, and negated clauses. They record myths in which fire, garden crops, lip-disc ornaments, names, and songs were acquired from the jaguar, mouse, enemy Indians, a cannibal people living under the ground, and other sources. They also describe “men without spirits” as people who can hear songs from animals, plants, and insects. Healing invocations refer to animals, plants, and other natural objects.
