## Overview

The Bacairi, also called Bakairi, are an Indigenous people of Brazil who call themselves *Kurâ*. They live in the Santana and Bakairi Indigenous Territories of northern Mato Grosso, in the municipality of Paranatinga, in the northern cerrado south of the Amazon rain forest. Their earlier history is associated with the Teles Pires, Arinos, Batavi, Coliseu, and Xingu river regions. They speak Bakairi, also called Bacairí, a Cariban language. In 1999 there were about 950 Bakairi, of whom 898 lived in the two Indigenous Territories; in 1965, 261 were recorded. The sources document a history of movement, conflict, contact, mineral and rubber extraction, agriculture, cattle raising, resistance to assimilation, and the establishment of Indigenous territories and schools.

## Festivals & rituals

The Wikipedia material records one customary practice: among the Bakairi, each man ate by himself; when one person ate in another’s presence, the custom was to eat with the head averted, while the other turned his back and did not speak until the meal was over.

## Foodways

They do record the customary eating practice described above, in which each man ate by himself and eating in another person’s presence involved turning the head away, turning one’s back, and remaining silent until the meal was finished.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Wikipedia article records that the Bakairi reported their earlier residence in the rainforest on the Teles Pires below its confluence with the Verde and that, because of internal quarrels and conflict with neighboring Kayabí, they moved south in three groups.

## Language & religion

Bakairí, also called Bacairí, is a Cariban language spoken by the Bakairi people in Mato Grosso, Brazil. The sources state that Bakairi word order is either subject–object–verb or object–verb–subject. The consonant and vowel inventories are described in the language article; the sound /ʔ/ is reported only in the western dialect, while /ɨ, ɨ̃/ are reported only in the eastern dialect.
