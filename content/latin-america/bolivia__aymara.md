## At a glance

| Who | The Aymara are an Indigenous Andean people. |
| Where | They are concentrated in Bolivia’s Altiplano, around Lake Titicaca and Lake Poopó, and in high valleys toward La Paz and the Yungas; communities also live in Peru, northern Chile, and northwest Argentina. |
| How many | About 2.3 million people identify as Aymara across these countries. |

## Overview

The Aymara live mainly in the high Andes of Bolivia and neighboring countries. Their society is shaped by the ayllu, a local kin and landholding community. They have adapted to high-altitude farming, camelid herding, and seasonal exchange. Their culture is known for colorful textiles, geometric designs, ritual objects, coca use, music, dance, and layered religious traditions.

## Material culture

Aymara material culture combines colorful woven textiles, highland building methods, camelid products, practical tools, and objects used in ritual life.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Aymara weaving uses wool, cotton, bright colors, and geometric designs made on horizontal peg looms.

- **Carrying cloth** (*Awayu / aguayo*) — This rectangular woven cloth carries infants, firewood, produce, and other goods, and is marked by bright stripes and geometric bands.
- **Knitted ear-flap cap** (*Ch’ullu*) — This brightly patterned wool cap protects the ears from cold and may show regional or community identity.
- **Striped and geometric bands** — Colorful stripes and repeated geometric figures organize Aymara carrying cloths and ponchos.
- **Andean banner colors** (*Wiphala*) — The Wiphala has become a symbol of Aymara and wider Indigenous rights; its seven colors form diagonal bands.

### Clothing & dress

Aymara dress uses colorful woven textiles and differs by gender, setting, region, and festival.

- **Gathered women’s skirt** (*Pollera*) — This full skirt is often brightly colored and worn with a shawl or awayu.
- **Women’s carrying cloth** (*Awayu*) — Women wear it across the shoulders or use it to carry children and goods.
- **Garment pin** (*Tupu*) — This large metal pin fastens a woman’s shawl or carrying cloth.
- **Men’s wool cloak** (*Poncho*) — This garment is made from joined woven panels and often has strong stripes, contrasting borders, and geometric designs.
- **Bowler hat** — Bowler hats became widespread among Aymara and Quechua women in Peru and Bolivia and are now recognizable in urban and rural dress.

### Architecture

Traditional Aymara buildings use earth, stone, thatch, and simple compounds suited to severe highland weather.

- **Settlement center** (*Marka*) — A marka is a settlement or territorial center linked to surrounding communities and local authorities.
- **Burial tower** (*Chullpa*) — Chullpa are cylindrical or tower-like burial structures built for elite individuals.
- **Neo-Andean building** — Contemporary buildings in El Alto use bright colors, geometric ornament, and highland pattern references.
- **Adobe house** — Traditional houses use adobe or earth blocks, stone foundations, and pitched roofs covered with thatch or corrugated metal.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Aymara households use practical objects made from woven fiber, clay, wood, leather, metal, and camelid materials.

- **Metal garment pin** (*Tupu*) — The tupu joins clothing, ornament, and social presentation.
- **Woven coca bag** — Coca leaves are carried in woven bags for chewing, tea, ritual offerings, and medicinal use.
- **Clay vessel** — Clay vessels are used for food preparation and storage.
- **Clay foundation offering** — Miniature ceramic objects were placed in house foundations to invite good fortune; examples survive in the Museum of World Culture collection.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Aymara adornment combines metal pins, jewelry, hair arrangements, hats, ribbons, and festival decorations.

- **Garment pin** (*Tupu*) — The tupu fastens a shawl while also framing the wearer’s social appearance.
- **Earrings and hair ribbons** — Metal jewelry and ribbons complement braids, hats, shawls, and layered skirts.
- **Festival hat** — Festival participants may wear highly decorated hats according to the dance tradition.

## Music & performance

Aymara music supports seasonal work, community life, religious festivals, processions, and dance.

- **Panpipe** (*Siku*) — Siku ensembles may use interlocking parts in which different players contribute notes to complete a melody.
- **Flute** (*Pinkillu*) — The pinkillu is a principal flute associated especially with outdoor festivities and periods of rain and growth.
- **Notched flute** (*Tarqa*) — The tarqa is a wooden notched flute used in Aymara music.

## Dance & theatre

Aymara performance includes vigorous dances, processions, music, costumes, and ceremonial representations of spiritual powers.

- **Mountain-power performance** (*Auki auki*) — Auki auki performers represent Achachilas so that spirits can appear to dance upon the earth.
- **Festival dance** — Other Aymara festival dances use panpipes, flutes, drums, embroidered costumes, hats, masks, and processions.

## Festivals & rituals

Aymara rituals follow agricultural seasons, weather, Catholic feast days, and relationships with mountains, ancestors, and community.

- **Cycle of transformation** (*Pachakuti*) — Pachakuti describes transformation linked to seasonal change, historical rupture, and shifts in the natural world.
- **Mountain powers** (*Achachilas*) — Rituals honor the spirits or powers of mountains and highland places, including Illimani, Illampu, and Huayna Potosí.
- **Offering and sprinkling** (*Ch’alla*) — Ch’alla involves offering or sprinkling alcohol, coca, incense, sweets, and other substances on houses, vehicles, fields, workplaces, or community settings.
- **Llama fetus offering** (*Sullu*) — A sullu is obtained at the Witches’ Market and burned or placed in a building foundation.
- **Catholic feast day** — Christian feast days include masses, processions, music, dance, and community sponsorship while retaining older agricultural and spiritual associations.

## Foodways

Aymara foodways rely on high-altitude crops, pastoralism, exchange, preservation, coca, and shared ritual meals.

- **Freeze-dried potato** (*Chuño*) — Chuño is made by freezing potatoes at night, drying them in the sun, and processing them until they can be stored for years.
- **Highland stew** (*Chairo*) — Chairo is a La Paz dish made with chuño, meat, carrots, onions, and corn.
- **Coca leaf** — Coca is chewed, brewed as tea, mixed with a little wood ash, and used recreationally, medicinally, and ritually.
- **Ceremonial offering** (*Ch’alla*) — Food and drink take part in ch’alla, household blessings, agricultural offerings, and festival hospitality.
- **Sacred earth** (*Pachamama*) — Pachamama is invoked in ritual language as the earth and generative world.

## Oral tradition & literature

Aymara oral tradition carries stories, farming knowledge, ritual guidance, history, and ideas about humans, animals, and landscape.

- **Heavenly Llama** — This mythic llama drinks water from the ocean and releases it as rain, while llamas return to springs and lagoons at the end of time.
- **Mountain ancestors** (*Achachilas*) — Stories about Achachilas explain mountains as ancestral, protective, and materially powerful beings.
- **Community oral knowledge** — Knowledge is passed through ritual speech, songs, proverbs, agricultural instruction, and stories told in homes and villages.
- **Katarist thought** (*Katarist*) — Contemporary cultural activism supports Indigenous political thought, including Katarist intellectual traditions.

## Language & religion

Aymara religious life joins Catholic practice with Indigenous ideas about seasons, agriculture, mountains, ancestors, and reciprocity.

- **Aymara language** (*Aymara*) — Aymara is spoken from the northern shore of Lake Titicaca south toward Lake Poopó and has regional dialect differences.
- **Related language** (*Jaqaru*) — Jaqaru is one of the closest related languages to Aymara.
- **Nearly extinct related language** (*Kawki*) — Kawki is closely related to Aymara and is nearly extinct.
- **Earth and generative world** (*Pachamama*) — Pachamama is widely invoked as the earth and generative world.
- **Ritual specialist** (*Yatiri*) — Yatiri prepare offerings and blessings.
