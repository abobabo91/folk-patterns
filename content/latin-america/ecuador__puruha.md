## Overview

The Puruhá are an Indigenous people of Ecuador whose traditional area in the Andean highlands includes much of Chimborazo Province and parts of Bolívar Province. Their early economy included subsistence crops, guinea-pig raising, and trade with the Inca before the Inca takeover of the Andean region in the 15th century. At the time of the Spanish invasion and conquest in the 16th century, their population may have been as high as 155,000. The Puruhá are significant to the region’s cultural history because their language became extinct, their communities experienced major demographic and social disruption after colonization, and their religious practices combined older mountain-centered beliefs with Catholic Christianity.

## Music & performance

 The sources do mention *jambiri*, identified as medicine people or shamans, but they provide no information about musical or theatrical practices associated with them.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do document religious practices associated with the traditional religion: *jambiri* led it, and the people believed that gods were linked to mountains, which were sacred and dominated the skyline of the region. Offerings to the gods included tobacco and rum, practices described as typical of other Andean traditional religions as well.

The sources describe Catholic Christianity among the Puruhá as a syncretic faith. Many Puruhá gradually combined traditional ideas with their understanding and practice of Catholicism. In the 1960s, Protestant Evangelicalism became increasingly popular as an alternative to Catholicism. The sources connect this shift partly with opposition to alcohol: many Puruhá were attracted to the teetotalism of missionaries, and Evangelical missionaries were considered to emphasize healthy living.

The sources also record political unrest rather than a festival tradition. In 1871, Indigenous people rebelled against the ruling class of Chimborazo Province over taxation and labor drafts. The rebellion included many Puruhá and was led by Fernando Daquilema, with the Riobamba Canton as the main area of fighting. Whites and mestizos were expelled from Punín, but the movement ultimately failed. Many Puruhá received amnesty from the Gabriel García Moreno government, while several leaders, including Daquilema, were executed. The rebellion later acquired legendary status in the province’s history among Indigenous peoples.

## Foodways

The sources document an early Puruhá subsistence economy based on crops and the raising of guinea pigs. They also mention tobacco and rum as offerings to the gods, rather than describing them as ordinary foods or beverages.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They do state that the 1871 rebellion led by Fernando Daquilema has had legendary status in the history of Chimborazo Province among Indigenous peoples.

## Language & religion

Puruhá, also called Puruguay and Puruwá, is described as a poorly attested extinct language of the Marañón River basin in Ecuador. It is difficult to classify; it appears to have been related to Cañari, though it may have been Barbacoan. A grammar was reportedly written in the late 17th century, but it appears to have been lost.

By the 18th century, few speakers of the Puruhá language remained. Indigenous peoples had largely shifted to Quechua languages, introduced by the Inca during their takeover of the Andean region in the 15th century. Leaders of the local Catholic Church had preferred that Indigenous people speak Quechua, and the linguistic change adversely affected the Puruhá ability to maintain cultural distinctiveness from Quechua peoples.

The traditional religion was led by *jambiri*. Mountains were sacred, and the gods were believed to be linked to them. Catholic Christianity developed as a syncretic faith, while Protestant Evangelicalism became increasingly popular in the 1960s. The sources also describe continuing resentment toward the economic and political power of upper-class European and mestizo Ecuadorians, whom many Indigenous peasants considered discriminatory, especially economically.

The sources record Puruhá naming patterns. Family names frequently end in *-cela* or *-lema*, as in Duchicela and Daquilema. Place-name endings include *-shi*, *-tus*, and *-bug*, with more complex endings such as *-cahuan*, *-calpi*, and *-tactu*. Some of these can occur with Quechua roots, as in Supaycahuan, from Quechua *supay*, “devil.”
