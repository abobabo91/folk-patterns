## Overview

The Matis, also called *Matsë* in their own native language, are an Indigenous people of Brazil whose population was reported as 457 in 2014. They live in the far west of Brazil, in the Vale do Javari Indigenous Territory, an area covering 83,000 square kilometres, in three communities or villages called *maloca*. They practice hunting, fishing, foraging and agriculture, and also work as teachers, health assistants, guides, translators and territory-surveillance workers for FUNAI. The Matis language belongs to the Panoan branch of the Pano-Tacanan language family. Their folk-culture significance in the available sources lies especially in ritual body modification, hunting technologies, foraging and farming practices, oral understandings of the surrounding world, and the effects of first contact on cultural life.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name tattooing featuring both traditional and modern designs.

### Clothing & dress

 They do document facial ornaments including “whiskers” made of sticks, bone piercings, and ear and lip gauges made of shells and other materials. These forms of adornment are discussed as part of Matis ritual body modification rather than as a complete clothing system.

### Architecture

The Matis live in three communities or villages called *maloca*. They state that the Matis originally lived in the region between the Itui and Itacoai rivers and that, following the turmoil of the first-contact period, villages were lost and some were abandoned. The sources also state that some fruit trees harvested by the Matis had been planted by previous generations in villages deserted during that period.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do describe hunting tools and related objects: blowguns, bows and arrows, shotguns, darts, traps and the local vines used to make poison for blowgun darts. The darts have notches carved below the point so that the tip breaks off inside the prey animal. The sources also mention metal tools given to the Matis during visits to the Ituí Indigenous Attraction Post.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Matis ritual body modification includes extensive tattooing featuring traditional and modern designs. The sources also describe facial ornaments called “whiskers,” made of sticks, as well as bone piercings and ear and lip gauges made of shells and other materials. These practices have changed through time: coming-of-age tattooing practices declined after first contact, while some practices experienced a resurgence since the 1990s.

## Dance & theatre

 They do describe ritual body modification and beliefs about airplanes.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do document ritual body modification, including tattooing, facial “whiskers,” bone piercings and ear and lip gauges. Coming-of-age tattooing was among the cultural practices that declined after first contact, although some practices saw a resurgence since the 1990s.

The Matis were contacted by FUNAI during 1976–1978, and FUNAI employees began visiting the then-five Matis villages in 1978. The resulting disease outbreaks caused a large decline in population: by 1983, only 87 Matis people survived, compared with an initial population of 300 or more. The sources describe no Matis wedding, funeral, birth or named initiation ceremony beyond the reference to coming-of-age tattooing. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription is provided for the Matis.

## Foodways

Matis subsistence combines hunting, fishing, foraging and agriculture. Hunted animals include peccary, tapir, sloth, several species of monkey and caiman. Birds hunted include macaw, currasaw, jacu, cujubim and nhambu-hen. Fish caught and eaten include cara, piau, tamboata, traira, poraque, piranha, matipiri, branquinha, curumata, pacu and pirarucu. The eggs of the yellow-spotted river turtle are also collected and eaten.

Jungle foods include *patauá*, *buriti*, *puna*, cocoa and cupu. The Matis farm cassava, banana, peach palm and corn as staples. Farming takes place in rotating, deliberately burned areas called *rocas*. Each *roca* is planted with a succession of crops over several growing seasons and is then left to rewild when productivity decreases.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They do preserve several Matis words and explanations of language, including *matses*, meaning “human being,” and temporal words such as *nebi* (“now”), *uxtokin* (“yesterday”) and *inden* (“long time ago” or “back in the day”).

Before contact, the Matis understood passenger airplanes as *xokeke*, the spirits of their ancestors, and bush planes as *binkeke*, a kind of strange demon-bird. They regarded the two types of airplanes as distinct phenomena because passenger planes appeared smaller than bush planes, which flew closer to the ground.

The sources identify linguistic documentation and preservation efforts, including work by Vitória Regina Spanghero Ferreira and Rogério Vicente Ferreira. The Centro do Trabalho Indigenista, established in 1979, uses professors elected by local Indigenous peoples to support the preservation of cultural values, daily activities and languages.

## Language & religion

Matis is spoken by the Indigenous Matis people in the state of Amazonas in Brazil, on the border of Brazil and Peru. The language belongs to the Panoan branch of the Pano-Tacanan language family and lies on the Mayoruna branch. An estimated 350 Matis speakers are reported. The language has a high level of transmission, especially among women and children, who are generally monolingual in Matis. Almost all Matis men and many Matis women speak some Portuguese, while older Matis men speak Portuguese for trade purposes. They do record the pre-contact understanding of passenger airplanes as *xokeke*, the spirits of ancestors, and bush planes as *binkeke*, a strange demon-bird. The Matis language is described as polysynthetic, with prefixation, suffixation, reduplication and atonalization. It is an ergative-absolutive language, and its documented grammatical forms include personal, demonstrative, reflexive, possessive and interrogative pronouns.
