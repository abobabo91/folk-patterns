## Overview

The Wari’, also known as the Pakaa Nova, are an Indigenous people of Brazil who live in villages and settlements in the Amazon rainforest, in the state of Rondônia. Their first contact with European settlers occurred near the Pakaa Nova River, a tributary of the Mamoré River. Many Wari’ live within the Sagarana Indigenous Territory near Rodrigues Alves, between Rio Guaporé Indigenous Territory and Pacaás Novos National Park. The Wari’ language has about 2,700 speakers and belongs to the Chapacuran, or Txapakura, language family. Wari’ means “we, people” or “we!” in the language. Their social organization, semi-nomadic settlement pattern, distinctive understandings of bodies and souls, and former funerary practices are documented in the sources.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Wari’ villages contain nuclear-family houses and a separate house called *the men’s house*. This building serves as a dormitory for single adolescents and as a meeting place for adult men. The sources describe the Wari’ as semi-nomadic: villages are moved at least once every five years, and settlements remain away from floodplains while staying closer to the shores of small perennial rivers.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention hammocks in connection with warriors’ quarantine, and describe belongings of the dead being burned, but provide no catalogue of forms, materials, or techniques.

## Music & performance

 They mention ritual wailing and other ceremonies during mortuary preparation, but give no description of musical forms. During warriors’ quarantine, the men stayed in the men’s house, moved around as little as possible, remained in their hammocks for much of the day, and drank chicha.

## Dance & theatre

 They refer to rituals and ceremonies associated with funerals.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do describe funerary practices and rites connected with warfare.

Formerly, Wari’ endocannibalism was practiced for funerary purposes through strict and elaborate rituals. After death, close relatives hugged and embraced the deceased. The body was left for about three days, although the period depended partly on how soon relatives from other settlements could arrive. Mortuary preparation included ritual wailing and other ceremonies, building a fire, removing the visceral organs, and roasting the body. Close kin did not consume the body but urged attendant relatives to eat. The heart and liver were eaten, while much of the body and hair was burned. Belongings were also burned, and paths in the village were rerouted to avoid the deceased person’s former house. The practice continued until the 1960s; today, Wari’ bury their dead after two or three days of mourning.

Wari’ warriors formerly brought home the bodies of fallen enemies whenever possible. Those bodies were served to women and younger men who had remained at home, while children were prohibited from eating dead enemies. Warriors then withdrew to the men’s house in quarantine, where they drank chicha. Sexual relations were prohibited during this period. The quarantine ended when the women refused to continue preparing the chicha.

## Foodways

A maize swidden around the village provides the staple crop. Finding suitable earth for corn growing, including black earth or terra preta, is important in deciding where to establish a village.

Chicha is mentioned in connection with warfare. During quarantine after battle, warriors drank only chicha, while the women prepared it.

Former funerary practice involved the ritual roasting and consumption of a deceased person’s body. The sources state that this was understood as an expression of respect and compassion for the dead person and the family, and as part of social obligations among affines. The practice was distinct from the consumption of animals and was intended to destroy physical traces of the deceased so that relatives could begin moving on and the spirit would be less likely to return to the village.

## Oral tradition & literature

 One account of Wari’ beliefs includes a story in which a child gathering food with her mother in the forest notices a jaguar’s tail between her mother’s legs and screams until the animal flees and her mother is summoned again. The story illustrates the possibility that bodies and perspectives can transform.

A large grammar of the Wari’ language was written by Barbara Kern and Daniel Everett. Parts of the Bible were translated into Wari’ from 1975 to 1984.

## Language & religion

Wari’ is also known as Orowari, Wari, Pacaá Novo, Pacaás Novos, Pakaa Nova, and Pakaásnovos. It is the sole remaining vibrant language of the Chapacuran language family in the Brazilian–Bolivian border region of the Amazon and is written in the Latin script. The language has about 2,700 speakers. Dialects listed in the sources include northern and southern varieties associated with names such as Wari’-’Oro Waram, Wari’-’Oro Mon, Wari’-’Oro Não’, Wari’-’Oro ’Eo, and Wari’-’Oro ’At.

Wari’ concepts of body, soul, and humanity do not restrict humanity to humans. Anything that acts human, performs rituals, has family life, or drinks beer may be considered human. The body, *kwere-*, is described as a “way of being,” while the soul, *jam-*, gives instability and the capacity for transformation. The heart, *ximixi’*, refers to the physiological organ, the core of something, and emotional state, morality, and intelligence. Christianity altered Wari’ distinctions between humans, animals, and other beings. By the 2000s, a large majority had returned to earlier traditions; 30% remained Christian as of 2019.
