## Overview

The Bora are an Indigenous people of the Amazon in Peru, Colombia and Brazil, living in the country between the Napo, Putumayo and Caquetá rivers. The Wikipedia account of the group gives their number as approximately 2,000; counts of speakers of the Bora language are higher, with about 2,328 in Peru along the northeastern Yaguasyacu, Putumayo and Ampiyacu rivers and about 500 in the Putumayo Department of Colombia. Bora belongs to the proposed Bora–Witoto language family and is tonal — a trait it shares in the region only with Ticuna. Over roughly the last forty years the Bora have become a largely settled people in permanent forest settlements. In folk-culture terms they are known for a detailed working knowledge of rainforest plant life, for feather and fibre ornament made from hand-twisted cotton, and for large wooden signal drums recorded in the field in the 1940s.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources document one fibre tradition in any detail, and it is an ornament rather than a woven cloth.

**Twisted-cotton feather headdress** — A headdress in the Museum of Ethnography (accession 1865.01.0231) is built on a base of coarse, twisted cotton threads, with yellow and red feathers set so that a crescent of feathering stands around the head and a bunch of cotton threads hangs at the nape; the cotton cord is secured with a knot of plant-fibre thread. The museum's note records Thomas Whiffen's observation that twisted cotton was used in this region only by the Bora, who made a few head ornaments from it — which is what distinguishes the piece from the fibre work of neighbouring peoples.

The sources used here name no further textile traditions and no motif vocabulary.

### Clothing & dress

What the sources record of dress is ornament worn on the body: necklaces, wrist bands of snakeskin and ear ornaments, all collected in northwestern Amazonas (Brazil) on the Swedish Amazon expedition of 1923–1925, together with the feather headdress described above. Nothing in these records separates men's from women's dress, or ceremonial from daily wear.

### Architecture

The only statement the sources make about Bora building is that the Bora have, over roughly the last forty years, come to live mostly in permanent forest settlements; no house form, material or roof type is described.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

No ceramics or metalwork appear in the sources. Two classes of wooden object are documented. Bows and arrows are named as the main weapons used in person-to-person conflict. Large **wooden drums** were photographed in 1941 on the Yaguasyacu river, in the triangle between the Amazon and the Putumayo in northeastern Peru, during A. L. Wenner-Gren's expedition of 1940–1941; the catalogue distinguishes one of them as the "male" drum of a pair, and the accompanying original label records that the collection was sent on to the Museum of the American Indian in New York. The photographs are attributed on the catalogue card to Dr. Paul Fejos, though the museum notes that Norman Mathews was the expedition's photographer and has been assumed to be the maker.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Four of the objects shown are personal ornaments collected by Douglas Melin in northwestern Amazonas on the Swedish Amazon expedition of 1923–1925: two necklaces (1926.07.0024 and 1926.07.0025), a pair of wrist bands made of snakeskin (1926.07.0026a–b), and a pair of ear sticks of cane (1926.07.0027a–b) whose surfaces are decorated with designs burned into the material. With the feather headdress on its twisted-cotton base, these make up the whole of the body adornment the sources attest. No tattooing, body paint or hair practice is described in them.

## Music & performance

The sources document a single instrument, and they document it as an object rather than as a repertoire. The wooden drums photographed on the Yaguasyacu river in 1941 were made in at least two sizes or genders, the catalogue identifying one as the "male" drum by comparison with the companion image of the pair. The photographs come from the Wenner-Gren expedition of 1940–1941 and reached the Gothenburg museum in 1965 through Mrs. M. Wenner-Gren, Carl-E. Grenninger and Henry Wassén; the catalogue record cites Julian Steward's chapter "The Witotoan Tribes" in volume 3 of the *Handbook of South American Indians* (1946) as its ethnographic reference. The same record places the photographs with a Bora sub-group it names Bua, glossing the name as "crocodile." No song genre, singer, vocal style or performance occasion is named in any of the sources used here, and nothing in them describes how the drums were played or what they communicated.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources record two features of social and religious life. Bora families practise exogamy, marrying outside the group. The Bora worldview is animist and makes no distinction between the physical and the spiritual world, spirits being understood as present throughout it. The elaborate Bora knowledge of the plants of the surrounding rainforest, and in particular the complex importance of trees — a concern they share with other Indigenous peoples of the Peruvian Amazon such as the Urarina — is the one domain the sources treat as both practical and more than practical.

## Oral tradition & literature

No Bora narrative cycle or epic is named in the sources, but they record substantial documentation of Bora speech. A written form of the language was developed by the Wycliffe Bible Translators Wesley and Eva Thiesen with speakers in the village of Brillo Nuevo on the Yaguasyacu river: first school books from Bora into Spanish, then a translation of the New Testament, and finally a full dictionary and grammar, which in turn made further textbooks possible so that speakers could be taught to read and write their own language against the pressure of Spanish and Portuguese. Their daughter Ruth is the first non-native recorded to have learned the language. A published collection of Bora folkloric texts exists, and the Bora DoReCo corpus compiled by Frank Seifart holds audio recordings of narrative texts with phone-level transcription, translations and morphological annotation. Separately, the Universidad Nacional de Colombia, funded by the Modern Endangered Archives Program at the UCLA library, digitized a large body of recordings from three Indigenous groups of the Colombian Amazon, among them the Miraña.

## Language & religion

Bora is spoken in the western Amazon and belongs to the proposed Bora–Witoto family; early investigators took it to be related to Witoto, but there is very little similarity between the two, and a nineteenth-century museum note still describes Bora as a divergent dialect of Witoto. It is tonal, contrasts vowel length, and has 350 classifiers — more than any other language so far described. Miraña, spoken along the Caquetá-Japurá river from Colombia into Brazil, is 94 per cent mutually comprehensible with Bora proper; Muinane, on tributaries of the Caquetá in central Colombia, about 50 per cent. Loukotka (1968) also lists Bora (Boro) on the Cahuinari river and in a colony at Méria on the Igara Paraná, Imihitä (Emejeite) on the Jacaré river, and the poorly attested Fa:ai of the Sierra Futahy. Literacy in Bora among Peruvian speakers runs at 10 to 30 per cent, and in Spanish as a second language at 25 to 50 per cent. Religion, as the sources give it, is animist.

Two historical facts belong here because they shaped the rest. During the rubber boom and the Putumayo genocide around the turn of the twentieth century the Bora suffered enslavement and physical abuse, with hundreds held at rubber stations of Julio César Arana's company — Entre Rios, La Sabana, Santa Catalina and Abisinia — and an unknown number fleeing across the Caquetá river. The ancestral lands are now threatened by illegal logging, and the Bora hold no indigenous reserves.
