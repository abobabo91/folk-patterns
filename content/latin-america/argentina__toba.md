## Overview

The Qom are a hunter-gatherer people of the Gran Chaco whose material culture centers on the wild chaguar fiber, worked into carrying nets and bags using a looping technique unique to the region. Their music, dance, and ritual life blend ancestral shamanism with recent Pentecostal Christianity. Since the mid-twentieth century, many have migrated to city peripheries, though Qom-language radio, bilingual schools, and cooperative craft production keep cultural practice alive.

## Material culture

Qom material culture is built around the chaguar fiber from wild bromeliads, worked into textiles, nets, and bags using a leg-rolled cord technique shared across the Chaco but distinct from Andean traditions.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Chaguar fiber from wild bromeliads is the foundation of Qom textile work, processed into cord by rolling on the leg and worked into looped nets, bags, and woven bands.

- **Large carrying net** (*llica*) — Looped chaguar net slung from forehead or shoulder to hold firewood, fruit, or a child, with color-blocked and lozenge designs in the mesh structure itself.
- **Shoulder bag** (*yica*) — Smaller looped bag used for personal goods and hunting gear, now sold as an emblematic craft item at fairs and Indigenous outlets.
- **Narrow sash or band** (*chaguar faja / tejido en telar de cintura*) — Woven on a backstrap or staked frame in chaguar or sheep's wool, these carry the crispest geometric registers of Qom design.
- **Palm basketry** (*cestería de palma*) — Coiled and plaited baskets from palm and totora leaf for winnowing and storage, with chevron and step patterning from the plaiting rhythm itself.
- **Forest dyes** (*algarrobo, guayacán*) — Rust-brown and black dyes from bark, ash and mud mordants, and deep tan from palo santo create color in chaguar textiles and nets.

### Clothing & dress

Pre-conquest dress was minimal—hide breechclouts and wrapped skirts—but later added feathered headdresses and body paint as markers of leadership and ceremony.

- **Feathered headband** (*vincha de plumas*) — Headband mounting rhea, parrot, macaw, and toucan feathers, worn by women and men.
- **Radiating feather crest** (*penacho*) — Full crest worn by shamans and caciques at assemblies and political gatherings, often with seed-and-feather chest ornaments.
- **Body paint** (*pintura corporal qom*) — Geometric registers of red annatto, white clay, and charcoal applied to face, chest, and arms for war, curing, and initiation.
- **Leather head and shoulder armor** — Hardened leather pieces strapped on when riding through thorn forest to protect against quebracho spines and jaguars.

### Architecture

Classic Chaco dwellings are low dome or beehive huts of bent branches thatched with grass and palm, grouped in loose circles around central plazas for dancing and assembly.

- **Dome hut** — Low, beehive-shaped frame of bent palo santo and quebracho branches, thatched with grass, palm, and totora, entered by a single low opening with no interior walls.
- **Open-sided shelter** (*ramada*) — Post-and-thatch communal shelter where chaguar is processed and stories told, now the real center of rural community life.
- **Mission reduction house** (*adobe / chorizo*) — Mud-and-stick or mud-and-wattle walls with corrugated-iron or thatch roofs, built in gridded rows under Franciscan settlement from 1901 onward.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Qom pottery is coil-built and open-fired earthenware with incised geometric bands and red slip; palo santo carving produces bowls, spoons, and animal figures valued for wood's oiliness and scent.

- **Earthenware pottery** (*alfarería qom*) — Coil-built, unglazed water jars and cooking bowls tempered with ash or sherd, smoothed with pebble or corn cob, with incised geometric bands and red slip.
- **Aromatic wood carving** (*palo santo*) — Bowls, spoons, mortars, and animal figures carved from resinous palo santo heartwood, now a major cash craft.
- **Hunting kit** — Self bow, cane arrows with hardwood and bone points, fish spear, throwing club, and nets used into the twentieth century alongside firearms.
- **Gourd containers** (*porongos*) — Gourds used as containers and rattles for food and ritual purposes.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The characteristic Qom ornament is multi-strand necklaces of forest seeds, shell, bone, and feathers, often worn with feathered headdresses and body paint in ceremony.

- **Seed necklace** (*collar de semillas*) — Multi-strand necklaces strung from forest seeds like chañar and tala, alternated with snail shell, bone discs, feathers, and trade beads.
- **Feather ear ornaments** — Small feathers worn in ears, often paired with feather-and-seed chest ornaments in ceremony.
- **Shaman ornaments** — Bone and nut pendants, jaguar and puma claws, and gourd rattles worn by shamans as visible power equipment.
- **Facial tattooing and scarification** (*pintura corporal*) — Marks applied to girls at puberty rites and to men to show status among Guaycuruan peoples, historically a major body decoration.

## Music & performance

The signature one-string fiddle and double-headed drum accompany courtship, lament, and dance; shamanic song with rattles carries healing power.

- **One-string fiddle** (*nviké*) — Palo borracho or tin-can body with horsehair string and small bow, played with keening, speech-like melody for courtship and solo lament.
- **Double-headed drum** (*pim pim*) — Drum that names the dance it accompanies, central to night-long gatherings and celebrations.
- **Gourd rattle** (*sonajero*) — Gourd rattles used in shamanic curing and as percussion in community music.
- **Shamanic song** (*pi'oxonaq*) — Power songs acquired in dreams, sung over patients as the vehicle of the helper spirit rather than performance.
- **Mission hymn repertoire** — Qom-language hymnody sung in chorus with guitar and bass drum, now the most widely practiced Qom vocal music from Pentecostal churches.

## Dance & theatre

The pim pim is the principal Qom dance, danced in lines or circles with short stamping steps at night-long gatherings to mark harvests, hunts, and political assemblies.

- **Line dance** (*pim pim*) — Men and women dancing in line or open circle with short stamping steps to drum and voice, marking harvests, hunts, community visits, and Indigenous political gatherings.
- **Shamanic curing movement** (*pi'oxonaq*) — Rhythmic, rattle-led movement by the shaman around and over a patient, a curing practice rather than choreographed dance.
- **Trickster narrative performance** (*Fitzcalxaic*) — Storyteller enacting voices of the trickster fox, animals, and monsters before a night audience, now staged as bilingual dramatization in intercultural schools.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual year follows wild harvests rather than a fixed calendar; the great algarroba season brought bands together for brewing fermented beer and nights of pim pim.

- **Algarroba harvest gathering** (*algarroba*) — Late spring and early summer convergence of bands to harvest carob pods, brew aloja fermented beer, and hold nights of pim pim dancing.
- **Fermented carob beer** (*aloja*) — Ceremonial beer brewed from algarroba pods, central to harvest gatherings and collective celebration.
- **Massacre commemoration** (*19 July*) — Annual remembrance of the 1924 Massacre of Napalpí, in which some 200 Qom and Mocoví were killed; commemorated as key political-ritual date.
- **Life-cycle seclusion** — Birth involved parental seclusion and food restriction; girls' first menstruation brought seclusion with dietary prohibition, then emergence with painting and ornament.
- **Shamanic calling** (*pi'oxonaq*) — Boys marked as shamans through dream-encounter with a helper spirit and apprenticeship to an established shaman.

## Foodways

Qom subsistence was hunting, fishing, and gathering; the staple wild carbohydrate is algarroba carob pod, ground into flour cakes or fermented into ceremonial beer.

- **Carob flour cake** (*patay*) — Pressed, dried flour cake made from ground algarroba pods, the staple wild carbohydrate.
- **Algarroba beer** (*aloja*) — Fermented algarroba beer central to gatherings and collective ceremonies.
- **Wild honey** (*lechiguana*) — Honey taken from wild nests, the most prized food of all.
- **River fish** (*sábalo, surubí, dorado, bagre*) — Fish caught with nets, weirs, and spears, roasted on coals or in ash.
- **Communal maize stew** (*locro*) — Maize-and-meat stew entered from Creole contact, along with cassava, squash, and mate drunk communally from a gourd.

## Oral tradition & literature

Qom narrative centers on the trickster fox Fitzcalxaic and animal protagonists in etiological tales told at night by elders; a modern literary revival has grown through bilingual schools and Indigenous writing circuits.

- **Trickster fox** (*Fitzcalxaic*) — Central figure of Qom tales whose greed and cleverness explain features of the world.
- **Animal protagonists** (*Nesoxsot*) — Other animal characters in the narrative cycle alongside the trickster fox.
- **Origin tales** — Etiological stories of the origin of fire and algarroba, told at night-long gatherings.
- **Cosmology of owner-spirits** (*dueño*) — Forest-and-sky world where each animal, water, and tree species has an owner-spirit who becomes a shaman's helper.
- **Political testimony** (*Félix Díaz*) — Written and public testimony of Qom leaders, especially accounts of land conflicts and encampments, now widely circulated Indigenous political texts.

## Language & religion

Qom l'aqtaqa is a Guaycuruan language with high retention in rural areas but declining in cities; religion is layered—ancestral shamanism persists within overwhelming Pentecostal and Anglican affiliation.

- **Guaycuruan language family** (*Qom l'aqtaqa*) — Closely related to Pilagá and Mocoví, with roughly 59,800 speakers in Argentina in the 2000s, now written in Latin orthography for bilingual schooling.
- **Shaman** (*pi'oxonaq*) — Practitioner of dream-acquired power songs and curing, still active alongside mission Christianity.
- **Owner-spirits** (*dueño*) — Ancestral spiritual beings of animals and places that must be respected by hunters and become shamans' helpers.
- **Indigenous Evangelical church** (*Iglesia Evangélica Unida*) — Chaco church founded and run by Indigenous pastors, synthesizing curing by prayer with shamanic healing tradition.
- **Pentecostal congregations** — Overwhelming contemporary religious affiliation, often Qom-led, holding multi-day services and river baptisms.

## Glossary

- *chaguar* — Wild bromeliad fiber from caraguatá leaves, processed into cord and looped into nets and textiles
- *llica* — Large forehead or shoulder-slung carrying net of looped chaguar cord
- *yica* — Smaller shoulder bag of looped chaguar cord for personal goods and hunting gear
- *Qom l'aqtaqa* — Toba language, belonging to the Guaycuruan family
- *pi'oxonaq* — Shaman with dream-acquired power songs and healing abilities
- *nviké* — One-string fiddle with keening melody, associated with courtship and lament
- *pim pim* — Double-headed drum and the line dance it accompanies, marking celebrations and political gatherings
- *Fitzcalxaic* — Trickster fox, central protagonist of Qom narrative tales
- *algarroba* — Wild carob pod, ground into flour or fermented into beer, the staple food and ceremonial gathering season
- *aloja* — Fermented algarroba beer, central to harvest gatherings and ceremony
- *vincha de plumas* — Feathered headband worn by men and women in ceremony
- *penacho* — Radiating feather crest worn by shamans and caciques at assemblies
- *pintura corporal qom* — Geometric body paint in red annatto, white clay, and charcoal for war, curing, and initiation
- *ramada* — Open-sided post-and-thatch communal shelter for processing crafts and social gathering
- *alfarería qom* — Coil-built, open-fired earthenware pottery with geometric and slip decoration
- *palo santo* — Aromatic resinous wood carved into bowls, spoons, and decorative figures
- *collar de semillas* — Multi-strand necklace of forest seeds, shell, bone, and feathers
- *dueño* — Owner-spirit of animals, water, trees, and places that must be respected by hunters
- *sonajero* — Gourd rattle used in shamanic curing and community music
