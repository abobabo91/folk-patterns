## Overview

The Ye'kuana are a Cariban-speaking people of the borderlands between Venezuela and the northwestern Brazilian state of Roraima. The great majority live in Venezuela: the 2001 Venezuelan census counted 6,523 Ye'kuana there, and the language had roughly 5,900 speakers around that date, about 5,500 of them on the Venezuelan side. The speech community is concentrated in the Venezuelan states of Bolívar and Amazonas and, across the border, around the Auaris area. The language is known by a long list of alternative names — Maquiritari, Dekwana, Yekuana, Cunuana, Kunuhana, Pawana, Maquiritai, Maquiritare, Maiongong and Soto among them — a sign of how many observers approached the group from different directions. In folk-culture terms the Ye'kuana are known through their plaited cane work, their feather ornament and a body of mythology that the objects themselves carry: the basketry in this atlas is woven with named beings from that mythology.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources here document plaited cane rather than loom weaving, and it is the plaiting that carries the group's figural design vocabulary.

**Plaited storage baskets** — Cylindrical storage baskets built from split cane, woven in double thickness so that the design appears only on the outer surface and is picked out in black against the natural colour. Base and lid are separate plaited pieces; a lid recorded with one of these baskets carries a herringbone plait in black and natural.

**Figural basket panels** — The distinguishing feature of the storage-basket work is that the plaited field is not purely geometric: humanoid and animal figures are worked into it alongside the herringbone, so a container is also a statement about the beings in Ye'kuana cosmology.

**Split cane food trays** — Flat plaited trays for serving cassava, worked in brown and black, with geometric designs woven in and picked out in black across the interior surface. The decoration sits on the side that meets the food.

**Plaited dart quivers** — A bamboo cylinder for blow-gun darts, wrapped in a palm leaf, sewn along the top of the opening and bound with brown cotton, part of which is carried on to form the carrying loop.

**Cotton armbands** — Men's armbands woven of brown cotton and fitted with red drawstrings, made and worn as a pair.

**Motif vocabulary.** The figures named on the storage-basket panels are the frog, a human baby, the monkey, and a manifestation of a major deity; the bat appears as a carved emblem rather than a plaited one. Herringbone is the recurring geometric ground, with chevrons and dots used on carved wood.

### Clothing & dress

The catalogue records reach the body mainly through ornament rather than garments, and the Wikipedia source is linguistic, so everyday and ceremonial dress as such is not described in the sources used here. What they do show is the ornamental layer: men wear paired brown cotton armbands with red drawstrings, and separate feather ornaments are made to be mounted on an armband — small bundles of toucan feathers in black and red, black and yellow, or black, yellow and red, attached with fibre cord. Headdresses are the most elaborate items recorded. One is a band of split cane mounted with trimmed macaw tail-feathers in blues, greens, reds and yellows, with two tall red feathers rising from it, the band itself decorated in black and blue. The language has a word for clothing, *womü*, from which a verb meaning "undress someone" is derived.

### Architecture

The only architectural note they carry is indirect: the ethnographic literature of the 1960s and 1970s summarised in the language article took up village formation alongside mythology and political structure, which indicates that settlement organisation has been studied, but no description of it appears in these sources.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The recorded household and working objects are wood, cane, bamboo and calabash; no ceramics or metalwork appear among them. Cassava service runs through plaited split cane trays. Liquids are handled with a **calabash dipper**, its interior stained black with a coating called *cadimani*, the soot fixed in place with a tree resin, and a fibre cord threaded through a hole at the end of the handle for hanging. River transport is represented by a **paddle** carved from *mototo* hardwood with a semicircular handle. Hunting equipment is the blow-gun set: nineteen darts of split cane with cotton wadding wrapped around the tips, stored inside their bound bamboo quiver, which the catalogue names *madana*. Ceremonial woodwork includes a fork-shaped implement whose handle is carved as a shaman in trance, seated with elbows on knees and chin in hand, finished with plaiting in natural and black-stained split cane.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment is made from birds and animals taken in the forest. Necklaces are composite: peccary teeth strung on fibre cord, finished with terminal tassels of truncated feathers and a bird's head. Armband ornaments are bundles of toucan feathers — black with red, black with yellow, or all three colours together — bound with fibre cord so they can be mounted on the woven band. One ornament is a cluster of iridescent red and blue feathers bound with vegetable fibre, with small yellow, red and white flowers gathered at the base. Headdresses reach further: one is made from eleven gutted birds, probably toucans, with wings and heads removed and their black backs and vivid white, yellow and red breasts left showing, tied to a pierced wooden bar. Tattooing, body paint and hair practice are not covered by the sources used here.

## Music & performance

A single instrument is documented: a **bamboo flute** with a long slit and two holes pierced near the top, its lower end carved with zigzag designs. The sources record that oral material in the language was published by Guss in 1986 within a study of oral tradition, which is the nearest thing to a performance record among them. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage element is inscribed for Venezuela, so there is no inscription text to draw on for music. What the object record suggests indirectly is that musical and ceremonial life used the same materials as everything else — bamboo, cane, feather — and that decoration was applied to the flute as it was to the basket, with the carved zigzag placed at the end away from the player's mouth.

## Dance & theatre

The feather headdresses in the collection — the macaw-feather band said to possibly represent the sun, and the headdress of eleven birds carrying a central carved emblem of a bat with folded wings — were made for wearing on the body in some public context, but the catalogue does not say what that context was.

## Festivals & rituals

The annual calendar and the life-cycle rites are not covered by the sources used here, but ritual specialists are. Two objects belong to a shaman rather than a household. The first is a carved wooden **stool or bench** with a concave seat and pierced triangulated sides, cut from a single piece of wood and decorated as a jaguar: the legs are worked as the animal's limbs, the sides are stained red, and the surface is finished with black chevrons and dots — so the practitioner sits on the feline itself. The second is a fork-shaped ceremonial implement whose handle portrays a shaman in trance, elbows on knees and chin in hand, the posture of the practice depicted on the tool used in it. Mythological beings enter the object world directly: a headdress of eleven birds carries at its centre a carved bat with folded wings, identified in the register as *Dede*, a mythological bat, and a storage basket's plaited panel includes a manifestation of the major deity *Kushu* among its frog, baby and monkey figures. Mythology was among the subjects taken up by the ethnographic studies of the 1960s and 1970s. The sources name no festival and give no seasonal cycle.

## Foodways

Cassava is the staple the objects are built around. The split cane food tray recorded here is specifically a serving tray for cassava, plaited in brown and black with its geometric design picked out on the interior — the surface the food rests on. The calabash dipper, blackened inside with *cadimani* and hung by a fibre cord, serves liquids. Protein comes from hunting with the blow-gun: the quiver, called *madana*, holds nineteen split cane darts tipped with cotton wadding, and peccary teeth survive from the kill as necklace material. The language itself preserves a distinction relevant to diet — the verb *önöö* means specifically "eat (meat)", and its agentive nominalisation *t-önöö-nei* means "eater of meat" — and in one dialect certain nouns denoting fruit refuse the plural marker that other nouns accept.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ye'kuana oral tradition has been documented in publication: Guss's 1986 study of oral tradition includes texts in the language, and the ethnographic work of the 1960s and 1970s addressed the group's mythology alongside its political structure and village formation. The mythology is legible in the objects: the bat *Dede* is carved at the centre of a feather headdress, and a basket panel weaves in a manifestation of the deity *Kushu* together with a frog, a human baby and a monkey. Documentation of the language, which carries the tradition, runs from nineteenth-century wordlists by Schomburgk through twentieth-century grammatical sketches — Escoriaza in 1959 and 1960, Heinen in 1983–84 — to Hall's two volumes on morphosyntax and discourse analysis in 1988, Chavier's morphological work in 1999, Natália Cáceres's descriptive grammar, and Coutinho's 2013 study of the number system. A dictionary has been published on CD-ROM and an online dictionary exists, alongside an ELAR archive of documentation.

## Language & religion

The language is Cariban, within the Guianan Carib subfamily, where it forms a smaller unit with Wayumara. Ethnologue rates it Vigorous in Venezuela but Moribund in Brazil. Dialect variation is real and mapped: the Caura and Auaris dialects differ in which nouns accept the plural marker, and two dialect forms are distinguished by sound correspondences, one having a rhotic where the other has a glide. Younger city-dwelling speakers merge two sibilants. Several orthographies compete. The one in widest official use in Venezuela was devised in the 1970s on the conventions of the Venezuelan Indigenous Languages Alphabet and is widely adopted in Bolívar state; earlier missionary orthographies persist, and speakers in Brazil and in Amazonas often use a different system, with the same two vowel letters carrying different values on either side of the border. The sources used here do not describe the religious landscape, though they attest shamanic practice and named mythological beings.
