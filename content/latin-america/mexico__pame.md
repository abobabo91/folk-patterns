## Overview

The Pame are an Indigenous people of central Mexico, primarily living in the state of San Luis Potosí. Northern Pame communities extend from the north of Río Verde to the border with Tamaulipas, while Central Pame is associated with Santa María Acapulco. Southern Pame was spoken in Jiliapan, Hidalgo, and Pacula, Querétaro, but is assumed to be extinct. Population estimates range from 40,000–70,000 at contact with Spanish colonists in 1519, to approximately 10,000 in recent figures; the Pame language article gives around 12,000 speakers. Their languages belong to the Oto-Pamean branch of the Oto-Manguean family. Pame culture is documented through trading, hunting, mission history, ritual practices, agricultural change, and continuing language loss.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources describe Pame dwellings only in general terms. Many places of residence were reportedly assembled from cardboard, plastic, sticks, and whatever other materials were available. During missionization, Pame people were settled in and around missions, and the Pame of the Sierra Gorda were also described as living in towns by the sound of the bell.

## Music & performance

They do, however, record traditional dances as an important element of religious continuity. In the 1760s, Juan Guadalupe Soriano reported that the Pame remained “inclined to idolatry,” continued to follow their own religious leaders, and still practiced their traditional dances despite missionary indoctrination and violence by Spanish military officers.

The sources also describe ritual and religious activity more broadly. Many “rituals and religious festivals” were widely attended in the modern period. The Pame were traditionally nomadic hunters, traders, and warriors, and established relationships with other Indigenous groups in the region, but no musical repertory is associated with those activities in the supplied material. Pame-language programming is carried by the CDI radio station XEANT-AM, based in Tancanhuitz de Santos, San Luis Potosí.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention that Pame people continued to practice traditional dances during the mission period.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that many rituals and religious festivals continue to be widely attended, but give no dates, seasons, or festival names.

Pame religious practice includes ritual cleansings called *limpias*, which use an egg and prayers. The sources also describe beliefs in blood-sucking witches, spiritually powerful rocks, and a hierarchy of thunder divinities coexisting with the Catholic god. Healers or curanderos are called *kade*, with *kadet* as the plural, while shamans are called *kaju*, with *kajut* as the plural.

Missionization brought Christianization and attempted to replace the Pame’s largely nomadic way of life with sedentary farming. Yet missionary accounts differed: Francisco Palóu claimed that there was no pagan population remaining in the district, while Juan Guadalupe Soriano recorded continuing Pame religious leadership and traditional dances. In 1770, the Spanish declared the conversion of the Pame complete, and the missions were quickly secularized.

## Foodways

They state that the Pame were taught sedentary farming under missionization and that the Pame’s agricultural conditions later remained difficult. The land reserve established along the Sierra Madre in the 1920s and 1930s had hilly and rocky terrain unsuitable for farming. Most Pame continued to live in poverty with no agriculture or industry in the region.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record a historical oral tradition concerning the conquest: Gerónimo de Labra wrote in the early eighteenth century that there was no tradition persuading the conquest of the Ximpeces, while describing the Pames as similar to them and more applied to work and commerce with the Spanish.

Pame language has no written tradition. During the colonial period, two grammatical descriptions were written, and the earliest Pame grammar was composed by Juan Guadalupe Soriano in the 1760s.

## Language & religion

The Pame languages belong to the Oto-Pamean branch of the Oto-Manguean language family and are most closely related to the Chichimeca Jonaz language. Northern Pame and Central Pame are living varieties spoken in San Luis Potosí; Southern Pame, formerly spoken in Jiliapan, Hidalgo, and Pacula, Querétaro, is assumed to be extinct and is very sparsely documented. Pame languages are tonal, although the number of tonal contrasts is debated. Pame grammar includes complex morphophonemics, head-marking morphology, singular, dual, and plural number, an exclusive first-person category, and an octal counting system based on counting knuckles rather than fingers.

The religious landscape combines Catholic belief with Pame spiritual practices. It includes a Catholic god, thunder divinities, blood-sucking witches, spiritually powerful rocks, ritual cleansings, curanderos called *kade*, and shamans called *kaju*. Many Pame people today cannot read or write in Spanish.
