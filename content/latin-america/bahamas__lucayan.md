## Overview

The Lucayan people were the Indigenous people of the Bahamas and the Turks and Caicos Islands before European colonisation. They were connected to the Taínos, although their classification remains a scholarly debate. The Lucayans expanded throughout the Bahamas Islands from an initial settlement on Great Inagua Island, developing villages on the archipelago’s larger islands and nearby cays. At the time of first European contact, their population was about 40,000, but Spanish kidnapping, enslavement and exploitation led to their complete eradication from the Bahamas by 1520. Their language belonged to the Taíno branch of the Arawakan language family and was closely related to Ciboney and Classic Taíno. Lucayan culture is documented through archaeology, Columbus’s observations, linguistic evidence and surviving objects such as conch tools and duhos.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Lucayan clothing was limited and adapted to the warm island environment. Women past puberty wore a small skirt of cotton, while men might wear a loincloth made of plaited leaves or cotton. Lucayans did not generally wear the elaborate full-body clothing associated with colder climates; Columbus described them as customarily going almost completely naked. Some people wore head bands, waist bands, feathers, bones, and ear and nose jewelry. The sources also state that Lucayans were often tattooed and usually applied paint to their bodies and/or faces. Bixa produced reddish body paint, while jagua, identified as Genipa or Mamoncillo, produced black body paint. No footwear, distinctive ceremonial costume, or named head covering is documented.

### Architecture

Lucayans lived in multi-household houses resembling those used by Taínos in Hispaniola and Cuba. Their houses were shaped like round tents, tall, and made from poles and thatch. An opening at the top allowed smoke to escape. Columbus described Lucayan houses as clean and well-swept. Cotton nets, described as some kind of hammocks, served as beds and furnishings, and the houses were used mainly for sleeping. Each house sheltered an extended family.

Lucayan villages were generally linear rather than arranged around a central plaza. They were located along the coast, often on the leeward side of an island, but also on the windward side wherever tidal creeks provided protected shoreline. Classic Taíno villages in Hispaniola and eastern Cuba were often located along rivers and around central plazas, making the Lucayan coastal arrangement a documented regional distinction.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Lucayan pottery was mainly **Palmetto Ware**, including **Abaco Redware** and **Crooked Island Ware**. It was produced locally from red clay soils tempered with burnt conch shells, and it was usually undecorated. The red clay was derived from Saharan dust. Some sand-tempered pottery was imported from Cuba and/or Haiti.

Lucayans carved canoes, spears, bowls and ceremonial stools from wood. They made fishhooks from bone or shell and harpoon points from bone. Conch shells, including queen conch and Atlantic triton, supplied material for canoe gouges, hoes, hammers, picks, net mesh gauges and fishhooks. Stone chopping, cutting and scraping tools were imported from Cuba or Haiti.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Lucayan body adornment included ear and nose jewelry made with materials that included bone, as well as feathers and other ornaments. Some people wore head bands and waist bands. The sources state that Lucayans were often tattooed and usually painted their bodies and/or faces. Bixa was used for reddish paint, while jagua, identified as Genipa or Mamoncillo, was used for black paint. They also practiced head flattening.

## Music & performance

Lucayan musical instruments documented in the sources were made from conch shells. Trumpet-like instruments were played by blowing, and a specific term, *guamo*, referred to trumpets made from the largest snail available, the Atlantic triton. These instruments were used similarly to church bells: they called people into action and were also used for religious rites.

The sources also describe *arieto* ceremonies. During these ceremonies, food was offered to the zemi, and shamans, called *behique*, gave participants a piece of cassava bread that was kept preserved until the following year. The available evidence therefore connects Lucayan performance most clearly with conch trumpets and religious ceremony.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document religious ceremonies called *arieto*, during which food was offered to the zemi. Shamans, or *behique*, gave participants a piece of cassava bread that was preserved until the following year.

Lucayan religion involved a pantheon of spirits and objects known as cemís or zemís. The sources identify Yocahu as the male giver of manioc and Attabeira as the mother goddess. They also name the twin spirits Maquetaurie Guayaba, the lord of the dead, and Guabancex, the mistress of the hurricane. The twin spirits were attended by sets of twins.

## Foodways

Lucayan subsistence combined cultivation, hunting, fishing and gathering. At least half of the diet came from plant foods. The staple crop was manioc, or cassava, followed by sweet potato. Sweet manioc was peeled and boiled. Bitter manioc was peeled, ground and mashed; the mash was filtered through a basket tube to remove poisonous hydrogen cyanide. The filtered mash was dried and sieved into flour, which was cooked on a flat clay griddle to make pancake-like bread.

The poisonous juice was boiled and combined with chili peppers, vegetables, meat and fish to make a slow-boiling stew. Other crops reported for the Lucayans included cocoyams, arrowroot, leren, yampee, peanuts, beans and cucurbits. The sources also mention papayas, pineapples, guava, mammee apple, guinep and tamarind fruit as possible crops.

Marine food was central. More than 80 percent of the meat in the Lucayan diet came from marine fishes, including parrotfish, grouper, snapper and bonefish. Other important foods included queen conch, urchins, nerites, chitons and clams. Sea turtles and marine mammals, including West Indian monk seal and porpoise, supplied a very small portion of the meat. Land animals included hutias, rock iguanas, small lizards, land crabs and birds.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used do not preserve Lucayan folktales, epics, proverbs, riddles or named literary works. Christopher Columbus’s diario contains the only contemporaneous observations of the Lucayans, while further information about their customs comes from archaeology and comparison with Ciboney and Taíno cultures in Cuba and Hispaniola.

The Lucayan language was not written. What is known of Taíno and related Lucayan speech was recorded in Spanish transcription. The sources document modern Neo-Taíno revival projects, including efforts to construct a modern lexicon through comparative linguistics.

## Language & religion

The Lucayan dialects belonged to the Taíno language continuum within the Arawakan languages. The Ciboney dialect was essentially unattested, but colonial sources suggested that it was very similar to the Lucayan dialects of the Bahamas and to Classic Taíno. Julian Granberry and Gary Vescelius distinguished Ciboney, or Western, Taíno as spoken in the Lucayan Archipelago, central-western Cuba, probably rural areas of western Hispaniola and presumably Jamaica. Classic Taíno was associated with other parts of the Caribbean.

Taíno was not written and was recorded in Spanish transcription. The language declined drastically during Spanish colonisation and was replaced by Spanish, English and French. Taíno words entered European languages, including canoe, cassava, cay, guava, hammock, hurricane, hutia, iguana, maize, potato and tobacco.

Lucayan religion centred on cemís or zemís, which could mean both spirits and the objects representing them. The sources identify Yocahu, Attabeira, Maquetaurie Guayaba and Guabancex, and describe *arieto* ceremonies involving food offerings, cassava bread and *behique* shamans.
