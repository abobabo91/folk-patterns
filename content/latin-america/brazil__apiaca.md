## Overview

The Apiacá, also known as Apiaká or Apiake, are an Indigenous people of Brazil who live in northern Mato Grosso near the border of Pará. Their villages are located in the states of Mato Grosso and Pará, including Mayrob, Figueirinha, Mairowy, Bom Futuro, Vista Alegre, Minhocuçu, and Pontal. Earlier Apiacá territory lay between the left bank of the Arinos River and the right bank of the Juruena River; pressure from Neo-Brazilians later contributed to migration toward the São Manoel River. The Apiacá language belongs to subgroup VI of the Tupi-Guarani languages, although Portuguese and Munduruku are now more widely spoken. The people are significant in folk-cultural terms for their agricultural practices, communal settlements, woven and ceramic objects, body adornment, musical instruments, warfare traditions, shamanic practices, and efforts to preserve a severely endangered language.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Cotton hammocks** — Apiacá homes contained cotton hammocks made either from coarse fabric or net.

**Woven baskets, trays, sieves, and vessel supports** — These household objects were woven with strips of creeper.

### Clothing & dress

 They do describe distinctive ornaments associated with the chief during war and encounters with foreigners: a large, white shell collar, a feather diadem, and a large belt of black beads and human teeth. Cotton is also recorded as one of the crops cultivated by the Apiacá, and cotton hammocks were used inside houses.

### Architecture

Apiacá settlements were formerly located beside rivers and typically consisted of a single large house surrounded by a clearing. The huts were rectangular, with thatched roofs and walls made from bark or palm. Cotton hammocks were hung along the interior walls. Each communal hut constituted a settlement and had at least one chief. Large bark canoes provided transportation across the waterways.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Apiacá people manufactured ceramic pots, pans, and dishes. They also made baskets, trays, sieves, and supports for various vessels by weaving strips of creeper. Large bark canoes were important for transport. Iron tools obtained from Europeans were considered highly valuable, although the Apiacá occasionally stole them from Europeans. Weapons and ornaments were the only forms of private property described in the sources. The records do not provide named ceramic forms, metalworking techniques, or specialized tool names.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Body adornment included tattoos and ornaments. Boys were tattooed by women with thorns in a process completed at the age of 14. A rectangle around the mouth symbolized that the boy could eat human flesh; facial tattoos also extended from each ear toward the nose, chin, and mouth, while body designs displayed accomplishments in war and hunting. After marriage, women received a tattoo consisting of a rectangle on the chin with a band running toward the ears. The chief’s war and diplomatic ornaments included a large, white shell collar, a feather diadem, and a large belt of black beads and human teeth.

## Music & performance

Musical instruments formed part of Apiacá culture. The sources name drums, rattles, and bamboo trumpets. No performance setting such as a wedding, funeral, court, or festival is described.

Shamans were greatly respected and communicated with spirits through trances. They told the future and treated illness by blowing on the patient, sucking the afflicted part of the body, and washing the patient in a herbal bath. These practices are described as religious and healing activities rather than as musical performances.

## Dance & theatre

 They mention elaborate ceremonies associated with cannibalism and warfare.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do describe several life-cycle and warfare-related practices. Boys underwent tattooing by women, with the process completed at the age of 14. Women were tattooed after marriage. After childbirth, a mother was confined for only one day, and children were raised in an atmosphere described as loving and affectionate. With the exception of the chief, the Apiacá were monogamous and married at the age of 14. Divorce was allowed, and custody of children went to the father in such cases.

The dead were buried in shallow graves beneath their hammocks inside the house. After a year, their bones were exhumed and buried in the original grave. Warfare expeditions were undertaken after the harvest, involving 200 to 300 warriors, but only when shamans predicted victory. Several villages could cooperate under the leadership of a chief during war. Cannibalism involving prisoners and those killed in battle was practiced with elaborate ceremonies as late as 1848.

## Foodways

The Apiacá maintained large cultivated fields and grew sweet cassava, also called manioc, cara, yams, peanuts, sweet potatoes, maize, beans, lima beans, pumpkins, cotton, and, as of 1848, watermelons. Planting and harvesting were typically communal activities, although they were generally performed by women.

Apiacá people also hunted peccaries, tapirs, and capybaras. Fish were caught with baskets placed at the bottom of weirs across the mouths of streams. By the mid-19th century, they had pigs, dogs, chickens, ducks, and several other birds.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They describe a belief in a god who created the sky and earth and expressed anger and displeasure through thunder and lightning. Shamans told the future through trances and communication with spirits, but no narrative corpus or named myth is provided.

A contemporary preservation effort is the initiative to create a book known as the *Apiaca Word* in order to catalogue the language. For many years, the Apiacá have attempted to revive their language through schools in their villages, but the sources state that these efforts have so far been unsuccessful.

## Language & religion

Apiaká is a Tupi language of the Apiacá people and belongs to subgroup VI of the Tupi-Guarani languages. It is described as very close to Kagwahiva and possibly a dialect of that language. After contact with Neo-Brazilians, Apiaca changed through combined elements of the Lingua Geral, a Tupi-based trade jargon. Portuguese is used in formal conversations, while Munduruku and Kaiabi are spoken in Apiaca villages in domestic spaces and informal conversations. All Apiacá speak Portuguese, and those married to Munduruku or Kaiabi people speak or understand their spouse’s language.

The language is at grave risk of extinction. One source states that only four people over 50 spoke and understood it, while the language article describes Apiaká as recently extinct and says that only three people had some knowledge. The last fluent heritage speaker, Pedrinho Kamassuri, died in Juara, Mato Grosso, on April 10, 2011. The Apiacá do not allow Kaiabi to be taught in their village schools, but permit Munduruku teachers to give lessons in their own language. The sources describe belief in a creator god, respected shamans, trances, communication with spirits, and herbal healing.
