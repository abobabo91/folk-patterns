## Overview

The Mixe are an Indigenous people of Mexico who live in the eastern highlands of Oaxaca, particularly in the Sierra Mixe, northeast of the city of Oaxaca. Their region extends from mountainous temperate highlands through midland areas to the tropical lowlands of the Isthmus of Tehuantepec. The Mixe speak languages of the Mixe–Zoque family and call themselves *Ayuujkjä'äy*, meaning “people who speak the mountain language.” The 2020 census reported 140,000 people who identified their language as “Mixe,” while SIL International estimated that the language was spoken by 90,000 people in 1993. Mixe cultural life includes subsistence agriculture, brass-band music, weaving, ceramics, ritual calendars, female shamanic practices, and a cargo system of rotating civic and religious offices.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Backstrap-loom weaving** — In some Mixe towns, traditional weaving is practiced on backstrap looms. The sources identify weaving as a local craft tradition.

**Ceramics** — Some Mixe towns mainly produce ceramics. The sources connect ceramic production with Mixe material culture and also use pottery as an image for processes of creation.

**Mixe creation imagery** — The Mixe creation myth compares the making of beings to basket weaving and pottery. The being called *täätyunpï*, “The One Whose Activity is to Have Ideas,” molds and shapes the world’s beings, while *yë’ yïkjujykypyëjkp*, “The One Who Makes Being Alive,” gives life to the objects that have been created.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs or pattern terms.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Mixe towns are described as producing ceramics, and the sources compare the creation of the earth and the shaping of beings to pottery making. Mixe newborns receive ritual baths associated with the hardening of pottery in a kiln. The sources also mention brass bands as a prominent feature of Mixe towns.

## Music & performance

The Mixe are well known in Oaxaca for their large brass bands. Every Mixe town is described as having a band that performs during local festivals.

Music is connected to village organization and public celebration. Brass-band performance appears alongside the cargo system, in which civic and religious offices called *cargos* rotate among the men of each village. Cargoholders arrange and pay for certain religious celebrations, care for religious images, and carry out other public responsibilities.

The Mixe language has been carried by the CDI radio station XEGLO, based in Guelatao de Juárez, Oaxaca. The sources identify this as Mixe-language programming.

## Festivals & rituals

Mixe ritual life includes prayers and sacrifice to a non-human entity called *yë’ yïkjujykypyëjkp*, “The One Who Makes Being Alive,” who gives vitality. Rituals are also performed for the Rain, the Wind, and the Earth. Female shamans practice ceremonies involving protection, divination, childbirth, travel, and hunting. These practices are described as connected to ceremonial traditions of Mesoamerican ancestors identified in the source as the Olmec people.

The Mixe use a 260-day ritual calendar for divination, planning rituals, and determining children’s names. A Mixe person has a calendrical name in the Mixe language, given at birth, and a Spanish-Catholic name given at baptism. Newborns are given ritual baths intended to make them strong and hard, in an analogy with pottery hardened in a kiln.

Mixe religious life also includes a pantheon associated with particular domains. *Poj 'Enee*, “Thunder wind,” is a fertility and rain god and protector of Mixe towns. *Naaxwiiñ*, “Earth Surface,” is an earth and fertility goddess. *Yuuk* is the owner of the animals and deity of wild animals and the hunt. *Hɨgɨñ* is a goddess of rivers and springs, venerated by fishermen. *Mɨjku* is associated with wealth, luck, hurricanes, death, and the underworld, and is often equated with the Catholic devil.

The cargo system gives village men unpaid civic and religious offices for a year. Cargoholders may bear substantial financial obligations while organizing religious celebrations or caring for religious images. People who have held many cargos are respected, and those who have held all possible cargos may belong to a council of elders. The conversion of many Mixes to Protestant branches has challenged the system in many towns and produced social tension.

## Foodways

Mixe subsistence agriculture centers on maize, beans, squash, and potatoes, supplemented by hunting small game and fishing in smaller rivers and streams. At elevations above 2,400 meters, farmers cultivate maize, beans, potatoes, and squash. In the midland region around Zacatepec and Juquila, they grow coffee and chili peppers. In the tropical lowlands near Matías Romero and the Isthmus of Tehuantepec, at elevations from 35 to 1.000 meters, they grow bananas, plantains, and sugar cane.

In the past century, midland Mixe producers began commercializing coffee, usually as small producers who sell roasted beans to buyers from outside the Mixe area.

## Oral tradition & literature

Mixe migration oral history recounts the settlement of the holy mountain Cempoaltepetl. The legendary king *Condoy*, also known as *Ëy Konk* or *Ko’ong Oy*, is said to have led the Mixes in building a successful kingdom. The sources state that little is known about the Mixes in prehispanic times because archaeological remains in the Mixe area are scarce and written sources are equally limited.

Mixe creation traditions describe *täätyunpï*, “The One Whose Activity is to Have Ideas,” molding and shaping the world’s beings. These creations are likened to basket weaving or pottery. *Yë’ yïkjujykypyëjkp*, “The One Who Makes Being Alive,” then breathes life into them. The sources use this two-part process to explain connections among pottery, birth rituals, and rituals for rain to breathe life into the earth.

## Language & religion

Mixe languages belong to the Mixean branch of the Mixe–Zoque language family. The Oaxaca languages are called *Ayöök*, *Ayuujk*, *Ayüük*, or *Ayuhk* by their speakers. The sources describe North Highland, South Highland, Midland, and Lowland Mixe, with varieties spoken around Totontepec, Santa María Tlahuitoltepec, Ayutla, Tamazulapan, Juquila, Zacatepec, and San Juan Guichicovi. Mixe languages are polysynthetic and ergative, use a direct–inverse system, and generally have subject–object–verb constituent order.

Mixe religion combines Catholic elements with other beliefs and ritual practices. The sources describe a pantheon connected with rain, fertility, earth, animals, rivers, wealth, death, and the underworld. They also describe nagualism: each person is believed to be born simultaneously with an animal counterpart called a *Tso'ok*, which carries part of the person’s soul and shares a common destiny with them. The sources identify Catholicism and different Protestant branches as important contexts of religious change.
