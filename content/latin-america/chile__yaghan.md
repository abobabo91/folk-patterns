## Overview

The Yahgan, also called Yagán, Yaghan, Yámana, Yamana, or Tequenica, are Indigenous peoples of the Southern Cone of South America. Their traditional territory includes the islands south of Isla Grande de Tierra del Fuego, extending to Cape Horn. They were traditionally nomadic hunter-gatherers who travelled by canoe between islands, hunting sea lions, collecting shellfish, scavenging whale meat, and gathering berries and mushrooms. The Yahgan language is a language isolate and is now extinct, having been replaced by Spanish. Cristina Calderón, who died in 2022, was the last native speaker. The Chilean census recorded 1,685 Yahgan in 2002 and 1,600 in 2017. Their canoe-based adaptation to the cold southern environment and the historical record of missionary contact are central to their ethnographic significance.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that the early Yahgan wore little to no clothing despite the cold climate. They covered themselves in animal grease, which helped trap heat and provided an additional layer of fat. Men hunted sea lions, while women dove in cold waters to collect shellfish. Extended contact with Europeans changed clothing practices.

### Architecture

Yahgan settlements were temporary but often reused. The sources describe small domed shelters and the use of rock formations as protection from the elements. People also sheltered around small fires, including fires set in boats. A significant Yahgan archaeological site from the Megalithic period has been found at Wulaia Bay, referred to as Bahia Wulaia or Dome Middens.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do mention canoes, small fires set in boats, rock shelters, and the use of common tools in the context of the Fuegians taken to England by the crew of HMS Beagle.

## Music & performance

 The Yahgan language article notes the existence of words connected with voice, language, uttered words, speech, cries, and sounds. It also records several bird names that may reproduce calls or other nonvocal behaviour, together with imitative cries and sound words. These linguistic observations do not provide evidence for a documented musical repertoire or performance tradition.

## Dance & theatre

 The Wikipedia material describes highly animated imitative behaviour among speakers in the late nineteenth century.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do record missionary activity in Tierra del Fuego and the establishment of a mission in 1871 by Thomas Bridges and George Lewis. In the 1920s, some Yahgan were resettled on Keppel Island in the Falkland Islands by Anglican missionaries in an attempt to preserve the group.

## Foodways

Yahgan subsistence centred on marine and coastal resources. The men hunted sea lions, while the women dove for shellfish. The group also scavenged whale meat and gathered local vegetation, including berries and mushrooms. After European whalers and sealers depleted important calorie-rich food sources, the Yahgan were forced to rely more heavily on mussels chopped from rocks, which provided significantly fewer calories in relation to the effort required to gather and process them.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They do document an extensive linguistic record created through missionary and scholarly work. Thomas Bridges compiled a grammar and a 30,000-word Yahgan-English dictionary after learning the language on Keppel Island. His son Lucas Bridges also learned Yahgan and wrote about the period in his 1948 book *Uttermost Part of the Earth*. The sources describe later efforts to preserve the language, including a planned educational curriculum, a language nest in the community of Bahía Mejillones near Puerto Williams, and publication of a concise and illustrated dictionary. The Yahgan language has no remaining native speakers following the death of Cristina Calderón.

## Language & religion

Yahgan is also known as Yámana, Inčikut, Háusi Kúta, and Yágankuta, and is regarded as a language isolate. Linguists have attempted to relate it to Kawésqar and Chono, although the languages are described as completely different. Yahgan was spoken briefly at a missionary settlement on Keppel Island. Its grammar includes extensive case marking on nouns and voice marking on verbs, while word order is relatively less important for marking subject and object relations.
