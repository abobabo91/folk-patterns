## Overview

“Arawak” is an exonym applied to several Indigenous peoples of northern South America and the Caribbean, including the Lokono of South America and the Taíno, also called “Island Arawaks,” of the Greater Antilles and northern Lesser Antilles. The peoples described by this term did not historically call themselves “Arawak.” They spoke related Arawakan languages, part of one of the most widespread Indigenous language families in the Americas. The Arawak-speaking Lokono settled coastal areas of what is now Guyana, Suriname, Grenada, Bahamas, Jamaica, and parts of Trinidad and Tobago. In the 21st century, about 10,000 Lokono live primarily in Guyana, with smaller numbers in Venezuela, Suriname, and French Guiana.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Archaeological evidence associated with Arawakan-speaking peoples includes ringed villages, raised fields, and large mounds.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Elaborate pottery is documented among Arawakan-speaking peoples, alongside fishing equipment made from fibers, bones, hooks, and harpoons.

## Music & performance

The sources used state that Taíno influence has survived in the music of Caribbean cultures.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that Taíno culture and teachings continued to be passed down from generation to generation, sometimes secretly or disguised through Catholicism because of fear for survival and discrimination. The sources also mention Agua Dulce, also known as Tamani, a syncretic religion practiced in the Dominican Republic.

## Foodways

The sources describe maize and sweet potatoes as main crops among Arawakan-speaking peoples, with cassava and yautia also cultivated. They state that Arawaks fished using nets made of fibers, bones, hooks, and harpoons. Archaeological evidence indicates techniques for modifying soil by adding charcoal to transform it into black earth, which is noted for its agricultural productivity.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources identify Aiomun-Kondi as a Lokono deity who created the world in Lokono mythology. They also state that Taíno communities used oral history and maintained practice to preserve knowledge, and cross-referenced that knowledge with the journals of Spaniards to reconstruct parts of Taíno culture and religion.

## Language & religion

Arawakan languages may have emerged in the Orinoco River valley in present-day Venezuela and later spread widely along the Orinoco and Amazonian rivers and their tributaries. The Lokono language is classified as critically endangered. An assessment published by *Language Documentation and Conservation* in 2015 determined that fluent speakers had declined to approximately five percent of the known population. Attempts have been made to preserve the language, and efforts to revive Taíno speech include the Hiwatahia Hekexi dialect, using words surviving in local Spanish dialects and extrapolations from other Arawakan languages in South America. Lokono mythology includes Aiomun-Kondi, while Taíno religious practice includes Agua Dulce, also known as Tamani, described as a syncretic religion practiced in the Dominican Republic.
