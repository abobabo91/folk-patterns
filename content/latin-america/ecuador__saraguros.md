## Overview

The Saraguros live in the Andean highlands of southern Ecuador and speak Spanish and Kichwa. Oral tradition says their ancestors were resettled here by the Inca state. They are known for keeping their land, customs, and black-and-white wool clothing that they read as signs of ceremony and nobility, not mourning.

## Material culture

Saraguro material culture centers on wool textiles in black and white, built from simple rectangles with meaning carried through color and embroidery rather than woven designs.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Saraguros weave black and white wool textiles in simple rectangular forms, with design carried by color, embroidery, and symbolic motifs.

- **Anaco** — A women's garment made of wool, worn as a skirt with embroidered edges matching the sash and blouse.
- **Poncho** — A black wool men's garment worn for gala occasions, sometimes with fuchsia, red, or green stripes or contrasting thread.
- **Rebozo** — A black or blue sheep's wool cloth worn for warmth against the cold.
- **Zamarro** — A white wool garment covering the front of the body from ankle to waist, worn by men.
- **White wool hat** — A woman's heavy white wool hat with black designs, weighing up to a pound and a half.

### Clothing & dress

Men and women dress distinctly in wool garments that display wealth, fertility, and status through color, embroidery, and decoration.

- **Long braided hair** — Men wear long braids as a marker of nobility, a tradition traced to Inca elite soldiers.
- **Leather belt** — Men's belts are decorated with gold knobs and metalwork to display the wearer's wealth.
- **Girdle** — A woman's girdle holds the anaco and skirt and represents fertility and the protection of the womb.

### Architecture

The Saraguros maintained a Spanish-required inn (tambo) on the Inca road, which became key to their keeping their land and resources.

- **Tambo** — An inn or way-station that Saraguros were required by Spain to maintain on a major route, helping them retain their territory.
- **Kapak Ñan** — The Inca road running from Cuzco through Saraguro to Tumebamba and Quito, along which settlements were built.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Saraguro metalwork appears mainly in dress ornaments and fasteners made of silver and gold.

- **Tupu** — A silver pin with a colored pearl at its center, used to fasten cloth and hung from a chain around the neck.
- **Metal snaps** — Metal fasteners used at the bottom corners of wool trousers.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Saraguro jewelry centers on inherited silver ornaments and beadwork passed through families, especially from mother to daughter.

- **Mole** (*tupu*) — A silver pin with a colored pearl, fastening cloth and inherited by women down the generations.
- **Wallka** — A multi-colored fabric made with beads.
- **Crescent earrings and tendrils** — Earrings and decorative tendrils shaped like crescents, a recurring motif in Saraguro adornment.

## Music & performance

Young Saraguro musicians are reconstituting Kichwa identity through creative reinvention of musical traditions, while others participate in national heavy metal subcultures.

- **Contemporary Saraguro music scene** — Young musicians selectively discard, amplify, and reinvent what they see as authentic Saraguro music, reflecting new opportunities beyond tradition.
- **Heavy metal subculture** — Many Saraguro youth, primarily male, listen to metal bands, play in local bands, and organize concerts as an alternative form of self-expression.

## Dance & theatre

Little is documented about Saraguro dance or theater, except that Curiquingue symbolism appears on costumes at Kapak Raymi.

## Festivals & rituals

Kapak Raymi, the Great Celebration, centers on black-and-white symbolism of the sacred Curiquingue bird, while floral offerings represent contemporary spiritual resistance.

- **Kapak Raymi** — The Great Celebration held in Saraguro, where costumes carry the black-and-white symbols of the Curiquingue, a royal Inca bird.
- **Floral offerings** — Treated as a living form of Saraguro spiritual culture and resistance.
- **Indigenous justice** — Exercised within the community to harmonize the balance of life.

## Foodways

The Saraguros are traditionally farmers and livestock herders; many continue agro-pastoral activities alongside modern careers and advocate for food sovereignty.

- **Agro-pastoralism** — Raising sheep for wool and farming remain central practices, often combined with professional work.
- **Food sovereignty** — Saraguro activists campaign for food sovereignty and control over their agricultural resources.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral tradition traces Saraguro origins to Inca forced resettlement (mitma), while contemporary Saraguro intellectuals produce substantial written work on history, education, and culture.

- **Mitma** — The Inca forced-resettlement policy under which Saraguros' ancestors were moved to Saraguro from other regions, possibly Lake Titicaca areas.
- **Sisa Pacari Bacacela Gualan** — A leading Saraguro intellectual who has authored works on Indigenous literature, floral offerings, and Saraguro history and organization.

## Language & religion

Most Saraguros speak Spanish; Kichwa/Runashimi is also spoken and is undergoing revitalization as part of asserting Inca heritage and Indigenous identity.

- **Kichwa** (*Runashimi*) — A Quechua dialect spoken by Saraguros and the subject of language revitalization efforts.
- **Amawtay Wasi** — A Pluriversity supporting Indigenous education and Kichwa language preservation.
- **CORPUKIS** — The Coordination of Organizations of the Kichwa Saraguro People, supporting language and Indigenous education within larger Indigenous networks.

## Glossary

- *anaco* — A women's wool skirt garment with embroidered edges
- *poncho* — A black wool men's garment for gala occasions
- *rebozo* — A black or blue wool cloth worn for warmth
- *zamarro* — A white wool garment covering the front of the legs
- *tupu* — A silver pin with a colored pearl, inherited through families
- *wallka* — A multi-colored beaded fabric
- *tambo* — An inn or way-station on a major route
- *Kapak Ñan* — The Inca road from Cuzco through Saraguro to Quito
- *Kapak Raymi* — The Great Celebration, a Saraguro festival
- *Curiquingue* — A carunculated caracara bird symbolic of Inca royalty
- *Runashimi* — The Kichwa language, a Quechua dialect
- *mitma* — Inca forced-resettlement policy
- *Amawtay Wasi* — A Pluriversity supporting Indigenous education
- *CORPUKIS* — Coordination of Organizations of the Kichwa Saraguro People
- *mole* — Alternative name for the tupu pin
- *Kichwa nation* — The broader Indigenous nation to which Saraguros belong
