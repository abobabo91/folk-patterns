## Overview

The Yudjá, also known as the Juruna, are an Indigenous people of Brazil associated with the Xingu River. Formerly the major tribe along the Xingu, they are now divided between a westernized northern group near Altamira, Para, at the big bend of the Xingu, and a more conservative group in the Xingu Indigenous Park at the Xingu headwaters in Mato Grosso. The southern group lives in two villages near the mouth of the Maritsauá-Mitau River. Population estimates recorded for the group range from 7,000 in 1500 to 278 in 2001. They fish and raise crops such as manioc. Their language belongs to the Yurúna language family, one of the Tupian languages, and the group is especially documented here for its distinctive carinated pottery with zoomorphic figures on the rims.

## Material culture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**Carinated pots** — Yudjá make carinated pots with zoomorphic figures on their rims. The pots are similar to the *cambuchi caguaba* of the Tupi people.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The exonym “Juruna” apparently comes from Lingua Geral and means “black mouth,” referring to a kind of face paint or tattoo formerly used by the people.

## Foodways

The sources state that the Yudjá fish and raise crops such as manioc.

## Language & religion

The Yudjá or Juruna language is also known as Yudjá and is spoken in Brazil, including the Xingu Indigenous Park of Mato Grosso state. It belongs to the Yurúna language family, one of the Tupian languages, and is described as the last survivor of the Yuruna languages. In 2001 there were 278 native speakers. In 1989, only one member of the northern group was able to communicate in Juruna.
