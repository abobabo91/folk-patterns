## Overview

The Garifuna are an Afro-Indigenous people descended from Island Carib, Arawak, and African ancestry, originating on Saint Vincent in the Caribbean. After deportation by the British in 1797, they settled along the Central American coast and built communities as fishermen and farmers. Their culture blends African, Arawak, and Kalinago elements in music, dance, and ritual practice. In 2001, UNESCO recognized their language, dance and music as a Masterpiece of Oral and Intangible Heritage.

## Material culture

Garifuna material culture is built from checked cotton cloth, carved wood, plaited palm fibre, gourds and drums, with distinct everyday and masquerade dress traditions.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Woven and plaited textiles in checked cotton and palm fibre form the basis of Garifuna dress and household objects.

- **Gudu skirt** (*gudu*) — A woman's red gingham skirt worn with a wrap-around tie belt.
- **Dashiki tunic** (*dashiki*) — A short-sleeved smock made for men and boys in gingham with white trim and side slits.
- **Gown shift dress** (*gown*) — A v-necked shift dress pairing a white plain bodice with a red gingham skirt.
- **Jippy jappa hat** (*jippy jappa*) — A plaited palm-fibre hat with a rounded crown, open-work sides, and a blue silk ribbon.
- **Tie-tie basketry** (*tie-tie*) — Household vessels including a flat circular sifter and wicker-work basket plaited from palm fibre.

### Clothing & dress

Daily wear is built from checked cotton in simple named forms, while masquerade dress uses painted wire-mesh masks and decorated tunics with ribbons and shells.

- **Everyday dress ensemble** (*gudu, gown, dashiki*) — Women wear gudu skirts and gown dresses; men and boys wear dashiki tunics, all in checked cotton trimmed in white.
- **Dance mask** — Wire-mesh masks moulded as pale male faces with black eyebrows, moustache and beard, held on by a blue cotton strip.
- **Festival head-dress** — A cardboard arch covered in white printed cotton with pink stripes and blue hearts, hung with tissue-paper flowers and feathers.
- **Masquerade dancer's costume** — White tunic and pants with pink silk ribbons crossed on the chest, green ribbons at knees topped with white cloth sewn with shells, a head scarf, and a jewelled striped bandana.

### Architecture

No built structures are described in the sources; architecture appears only as painted scenes on vessels.

- **Thatched building scene** — A calabash gourd bowl painted with a thatched building with drums hanging from the eaves, inscribed 'Warasa, Belize'.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Garifuna household objects are made from gourds, carved wood and plaited palm fibre, with metal entering mainly through drum construction and masquerade masks.

- **Calabash bowls** — Drinking vessels carved from gourds, used both as functional containers and as painted surfaces for scenes and the Garifuna flag.
- **Mahogany grater** — A single rectangular block of mahogany curved along its sides, inlaid with stones and pierced with a hole for hanging.
- **Segunda drum** (*Segundo*) — A bass drum with a hollowed Santa María hardwood shell, a white-tailed deer skin head held by vine and tightened with wooden dowels.
- **Log drums** — Cylinders hollowed from mahogany or mayflower wood with deer, goat or peccary skin nailed to a wooden hoop and sinew or wire stretched across the head.
- **Dance-mask metal borders** — Wire-mesh masks with solid metal borders, tensioned by nails and trimmed in paint.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Garifuna jewelry and body adornment are limited in the sources to decorations sewn onto dance costume.

- **Shell knee ornament** — White shells sewn to white cloth worn above a dancer's knee-ribbons.
- **Jewelled bandana** — A striped head bandana set with a red glass jewel, two silver foil circles and feathers.

## Music & performance

Garifuna music is built on drums and shakers and fuses African, Arawak and Kalinago traditions, with sacred herbs used to bless new instruments.

- **Segunda drum** (*Segundo*) — A bass drum made from hollowed Santa María hardwood with a deer skin head tied with vine and polyethylene rope and tightened with wooden dowels.
- **Maracas** — Shakers made from hollowed round gourds on nailed wooden handles.
- **Sisira shaker** (*sisira*) — A gourd shaker filled with canna seeds and painted with the Garifuna flag, a toucan, or a portrait of a drummer.
- **Drum ensemble** (*dangbu*) — A group of drummers playing together in secular genres like punta, paranda and punta rock, or in ritual triple-meter drumming.
- **Named performers** — Sofía Blanco, Pen Cayetano, Paul Nabor, Aurelio Martínez, Andy Palacio and Umalali are recorded performers; Palacio's album Watina and Umalali: The Garifuna Women's Project were released on Stonetree Records.

## Dance & theatre

Garifuna dance combines masquerade performance with ritual practice, using painted masks and elaborate costume to commemorate the 1797 Settlement Day arrival.

- **Masquerade dance** — A costumed performance using wire-mesh pale male masks, flower-and-feather cardboard head-dress, and a full male dancer's dress with silk ribbons, shell-trimmed bands and jewelled bandana.
- **Settlement Day re-enactment** — A commemorative drama of the 1797 arrival painted onto a segunda drum, performed on Garifuna Settlement Day (19 November).
- **Dügü ritual dance** (*dügü*) — Ritual drumming accompanied by song and dance held to calm a person possessed by ancestral spirits.

## Festivals & rituals

Garifuna ritual life is centred on honouring ancestors and healing through shamanic ceremony, with the shaman leading all traditional spiritual practice.

- **Garifuna Settlement Day** — Observed on 19 November with a re-enactment performance commemorating the 1797 arrival on Roatán.
- **Healing ceremony** (*Adügürühani or dügü*) — A ritual held when a person falls ill, in which a shaman consults the ancestral spirits and triple-meter drumming with song and dance is performed over several days.
- **Illness inquiry** (*Arairaguni*) — An invocation to determine the nature of an illness.
- **Ancestor offering** (*Amuyadahani*) — A ritual in which family members make offerings to their ancestors.
- **Feeding of the dead** (*Achuguhani or chugú*) — A ritual in which the community gathers to feed and honour the dead.

## Foodways

Garifuna communities historically relied on farming and fishing, though land loss to foreign companies pushed many into wage labour in timber and chicle extraction.

- **Cassava wine** — Used to rub new drums during construction as part of blessing and consecration.
- **Canna seeds** — Used as the rattling fill of the sisira shaker.
- **Calabash gourd** — Hollowed into drinking bowls and used as painted surfaces for food and ritual occasions.
- **Fishing villages** — By 1981 the Central American Garifuna population was concentrated in fifty-four fishing villages across Guatemala, Belize and Nicaragua.

## Oral tradition & literature

Garifuna oral tradition preserves the people's own history and ancestors, with ritual songs composed largely by older women addressing grandmothers and great-grandmothers.

- **Oral history of ancestry** — Oral tradition traces African ancestry to West and Central African groups and describes how African and Carib families joined.
- **Ritual song** — Dügü song texts composed largely by older women and addressed to grandmothers and great-grandmothers, even when honouring a man.
- **Moral formula** (*Au Bun, Amürü Nu*) — Meaning 'I for you, you for me', a communal principle of mutual care, reciprocity and collective responsibility.
- **Feature film in Garifuna** — Garifuna in Peril (2012) is the first feature film made in the Garifuna language.
- **Heritage organizations** — The Garifuna Heritage Foundation (St Vincent) and the Garifuna Research Institute document and preserve the culture.

## Language & religion

Garifuna is an Arawakan language with heavy borrowing from Carib, French, English, Dutch and Spanish, spoken alongside Spanish or English by most Garínagu.

- **Garifuna language** (*Karif*) — An Arawakan language descended from Island Carib, with vigesimal numerals and French-derived numbers above two, verb-subject-object word order, and agglutinative marking of possession.
- **Gender-marked words** — A diglossic split survives in a handful of words used differently by men and women, men's forms generally Cariban (au, 'I') and women's Arawakan (nugía, 'I').
- **Bilingualism** — Almost all Garínagu are bilingual or multilingual, usually with Spanish or English as a first language.
- **Catholicism** — Most Garínagu have been Catholic since early encounters with Jesuits, Dominicans and Catholic colonial powers.
- **Shamanic practice** (*buyei*) — The shaman leads all Garifuna traditional spiritual practice, consulting ancestral spirits and conducting healing rituals.
