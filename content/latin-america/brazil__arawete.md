## Overview

The Araweté, also called Arawate, Araueté or Bïde, are an Indigenous people of Brazil and swidden horticulturalists native to Pará. They live on the Igarapé Ipixuna, a tributary of the Xingu River, near Altamira, in the Araweté/Igarapé Ipixuna Indigenous Land. Their territory contains one large village surrounded by liana forests. In 1978 they settled on their current homeland after pressure from the Parakanã and a government relocation in which 30 people died. Their population numbered 136 in February 1983 and 168 in February 1988. Araweté is a Tupi–Guaraní language, and the Araweté speak it; nearly all speakers were monolingual in 1986. Their documented folk-cultural profile includes swidden horticulture, maize cultivation, ancestor religion and a history of movement through the Xingu region.

## Material culture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources state that, during encounters with fur traders in the 1960s, the Araweté regarded white traders as a source from which to procure iron tools.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources state that ancestors are very important in Araweté religion.

## Foodways

The Araweté are swidden horticulturalists, and their primary crop is a rapidly maturing maize rather than manioc.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that there are no written references to the Araweté produced prior to the 1970s.

## Language & religion

Araweté is a Tupi–Guaraní language of Brazil, and the Araweté speak it. The language is similar to the Asuriní do Tocantins, Parakanã and Tapirapé languages; a separate description places it in the state of Amazonas, in the Amazon region of Brazil, and states that nearly all speakers were monolingual in 1986. They state that ancestors are very important in Araweté religion.
