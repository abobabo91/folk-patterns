## Overview

The Muisca, also called Chibcha, are an Indigenous people of the Altiplano Cundiboyacense in the Colombian Andes, between the north of Boyacá, the Sumapaz Páramo, and the western portion of the Eastern Ranges. Their territory covered around 25,000 km² and bordered the Panche, Muzo, Guane, Lache, Achagua, and Sutagao. Their economy combined agriculture, salt mining, trading, metalworking, weaving, and manufacturing. A 2005 census reported 14,051 Muisca people in Colombia; later reports recorded Muisca councils and communities in Cota, Chía, Sesquilé, Suba, and Bosa. The Muisca traditionally spoke Muysccubun, also called Muysca, Mosca, or Chibcha, a language of the Chibchan family. Their folk-culture importance is especially visible in goldworking, textiles, ceramics, music, ritual, mythology, and the continuing recovery of language and cultural organization.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Cotton mantles** — Muisca women made fine cotton mantles from traded cotton. These textiles were decorated with colours obtained from seeds, flowers, fruits, plant crusts and roots, cochineal insects, and minerals. Small textiles functioned as money.

**Fique cords** — Fique was used for cords, together with human hair. The Muisca developed a variety of textiles from fique or cotton, while people of the cold Altiplano traded much of their cotton with neighbouring peoples.

**Mantle-making** — The Muisca used pencils, coloured threads, and stamps to apply colour. Their textiles were produced with techniques described as similar to those of the Aymara of southern South America and Mesoamerican cultures.

**Ceremonial cotton blankets** — In wrestling matches, the winner received a finely woven cotton blanket from the chief and became qualified as a guecha warrior.

Bochica was said in Muisca mythology to have taught mantle-making and the use of spindles, while Nencatacoa protected weavers and painters of mantles.

**Motif vocabulary.** Frogs and toads, snakes, armadillos, lizards, zoomorphic figures, triangular heads, anthropomorphic figures, anthropo-zoomorphic elements.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe everyday and ceremonial clothing in relation to ritual, warfare, music, and dance. At rituals, Muisca participants wore feathers, animal skins—mainly jaguar—and body paint. Dancers could wear costumes representing bears, jaguars, and other animals. In the initiation ceremony represented by the Muisca raft, the new *psihipqua* covered himself with gold dust, while priests and caciques wore feathered golden crowns. Guecha warriors, priests, and caciques were permitted to wear multiple forms of jewelry, whereas common people used fewer jewels. Cotton mantles were important textile objects and could be used as gifts or signs of distinction.

### Architecture

Muisca houses and temples were made from degradable materials including wood, clay, canes, and reed. Houses had a conical form, small doors and windows, and little furniture; people typically sat on the floor. In the wider Muisca description, houses were called *bohíos* or *malokas*. Circular structures were built on slightly elevated platforms to reduce flooding. Small settlements of ten to one hundred houses were enclosed by wooden poles, forming an enclosure called *ca* in Muysccubun, with two or more entrances.

Houses and temples were built around a central wooden pole attached to the roof. Temples used the wood of *Guaiacum officinale*. Floors could be covered with straw or, for caciques, ceramic floors. Cloths attached to roofs were painted red and black. Houses and sacred places were decorated with tunjos and emeralds. The Muisca did not construct large stone monuments, and little of their architecture remains.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Muisca ceramics served cooking, salt extraction from brines, ritual decoration, and the consumption of chicha. Ocarinas were also made from ceramic. Ceramic pots and sculptures were painted with frogs, armadillos, snakes, and lizards. Production centres included Tocancipá, Gachancipá, Cogua, Guatavita, Guasca, and Ráquira.

Muisca goldworking used gold, copper, silver, and tumbaga. The small votive figures called *tunjos* were commonly made from tumbaga, an alloy of gold, copper, and silver. They were used to decorate temples and shrines, for offerings in sacred lakes and rivers, and in funerary practices. The Muisca raft, found in 1969 in Pasca, was made by lost-wax casting from tumbaga and contains 229 grams of gold.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Muisca jewelry was made from gold or tumbaga. Documented forms include diadems, nose pieces, breast plates, earrings, pendants, tiaras, bracelets, and masks. Guecha warriors, priests, and caciques could wear several types of jewelry, while common people used fewer ornaments.

The Muisca used *Bixa orellana* to paint their bodies, and tattoos were common as an expression of identity. Ritual participants also decorated their bodies with paint and wore feathers and animal skins. Priests and caciques in the Muisca raft ceremony are represented with feathered golden crowns.

## Music & performance

Muisca music was used in religious rituals, burial and initiation ceremonies, harvests, sowing, house construction, and warfare. Music accompanied worship of Sué, Chía, and other deities, and was associated with the installation of new caciques. The sources describe the early Spanish chronicles as characterizing Muisca music as sad and monotonous.

The Muisca used drums, flutes made from shells or ceramics, golden trumpets, *zampoñas*, ocarinas, fotutos made from shells or snails, and rattling cups containing a pebble. The Muisca raft depicts a cacique surrounded by assistants, including figures carrying rattles during a ceremony at Lake Guatavita. In mythology, the gods danced a long dance called *sas quyhynuca* with the music of the *fo* drum during the first ceremony, which was said to give origin to space and time.

Music and singing could continue during events lasting a full month. People sang about wars and asked the gods for victory. During sowing and harvest, they sang while removing loose rocks from agricultural fields. Music also accompanied sacrifices, including sacrifices described in the sources as sometimes human. During house construction, women and men sang, danced, and moved wooden elements according to the rhythm of the music. These ceremonies were associated with Nencatacoa, a deity connected with construction, drunkenness, artistic expression, and dance.

## Dance & theatre

Dance was closely connected to religious ritual, agriculture, warfare, house construction, burial, and initiation. The Muisca danced after victories in battle, during sowing and harvest, and at the installation of new caciques. Women and men danced together while holding hands during house-building ceremonies. Harvest and sowing dances used feathers and costumes representing bears, jaguars, and other animals. Mythological narratives concerned figures such as Bachué, Bochica, Chibchacum, Huitaca, Nencatacoa, and Chiminigagua, and were preserved through oral tradition and the chronicles of the Spanish conquest.

## Festivals & rituals

The Muisca calendar was vigesimal and included twenty months. The summer solstice, June 21, was understood as the Day of Sué, the Sun god. On the solstice, the *zaque* travelled to Suamox for a major festival with ritual offerings. It was described as the only day of the year when the *zaque* showed his face, because he was considered a descendant of the Sun god.

Pilgrimages took place to temples and sacred natural places, including Lake Guatavita, Lake Iguaque, Lake Fúquene, Lake Tota, the Siecha Lakes, Lake Teusacá, and Lake Ubaque. These journeys involved music, dance, and sometimes human sacrifice, and were led by priests known in Muysccubun as *ogques* and called *jeques* or *xeques* in Spanish.

Rituals accompanied sowing, harvest, warfare, house construction, funerals, and the installation of rulers. The initiation of a new *zipa* is represented by the Muisca raft: the ruler covered himself in gold dust and entered Lake Guatavita from a small boat while priests and attendants took part in the ceremony. The sources state that the last public religious ceremony of the Muisca was performed in Ubaque on December 27, 1563.

Oral tradition describes families giving a child to priests, who cared for the child as a sacred person until the age of 15. The sources state that human sacrifice was no longer practiced when the Spanish conquistadors arrived, although accounts of earlier practices survive in oral tradition and colonial writing.

## Foodways

Maize was the principal Muisca crop and the basis of their diet and of *chicha*, an alcoholic drink made from fermented maize and sugar. The Muisca roasted maize, ate it from the plant, made popcorn, and prepared arepas. The sources state that the first presence of the arepa in the Altiplano Cundiboyacense dates to roughly 3,000 years ago.

Other cultivated foods included potatoes, beans, mandioca, tomatoes, calabazas, peppers, quinoa, yuca, fruits, coca, achira, arracacha, ulluco, yacón, sweet potatoes, peanuts, and chachafruto or balú. The Muisca also used *quybsa*, or ají, as a spice. Their main meat was guinea pig, which was raised in homes. They also ate deer, llamas, alpacas, capybara, fish, birds, insects, and other animals. Trade brought cotton, tobacco, tropical fruits, sea snails, fish, spices, coca, feathers, and other foods from neighbouring peoples.

*Chicha* was served in ceramic pots called *urdu* or *aryballus*. Food was cooked in ovens heated with coal. The Muisca sat on the ground while eating, used their hands rather than cutlery, and served food on leaves or in ceramic pots.

## Oral tradition & literature

Muisca mythology includes accounts of the creation of the universe, the beginning of the world, the origin of humanity, sacred places, rulers, and the origin of El Dorado. Bachué emerged from Lake Iguaque and became the mother of the Muisca people. Bochica was described as a teacher of agriculture, religion, arts, crafts, and social organization, and as the figure who drove away floodwaters through the Tequendama Falls. Chiminigagua created light and the Earth.

Other narratives concern Chía, Sué, Huitaca, Chibchacum, Cuchavira, Nencatacoa, Thomagata, Idacansás, Goranchacha, Pacanchique, Hunzahúa, Meicuchuca, the Monster of Lake Tota, Hunzahúa Well, and Fura and Tena. El Dorado developed from a Muisca ritual associated with gold offerings at Lake Guatavita and the initiation of a new ruler.

Knowledge of these traditions was recorded by Spanish chroniclers and later studied by Javier Ocampo López, Pedro Simón, Lucas Fernández de Piedrahita, Juan de Castellanos, and Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada. Contemporary cultural recovery includes Muysccubun revitalization, Indigenous councils, and publications such as *Suati Magazine*, a periodical of poetry, literature, and essays about Muisca culture.

## Language & religion

Muysca, also called Muisca, Chibcha, Mosca, or Muysc cubun, belongs to the Chibcha linguistic family. It was historically spoken in the Bogotá savanna and across the Altiplano Cundiboyacense. The language was agglutinative and used prefixes and suffixes. The Muisca did not use a script for texts, but numerals were written with hieroglyphs. Their number system had individual lexemes from one to ten and for twenty, *gueta*; higher numbers were formed through additions and multiplications.

The language declined after Spanish colonization, and King Charles III officially banned its use in 1770. Revitalization efforts continue among Muisca communities and cabildos. A Spanish–Muyscubun dictionary containing more than 3000 words has been published online since 2008.

Muisca religion centred on Chiminigagua, creator of light and the Earth, and on Sué and Chía, the Sun and Moon deities. Priests led ceremonies and had influence in farming and war. Ritual practices included fasting, coca, tobacco, yopo, offerings of gold, emeralds, tunjos, food, cloths, snails, and colourful birds, and ceremonies at temples, lakes, rivers, forests, and rocks.
