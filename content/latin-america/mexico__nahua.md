## Overview

The Nahuas are a Uto-Nahuan ethnic group and one of the Indigenous peoples of Mexico, with minorities also in El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, Nicaragua, and Costa Rica. They comprise the largest Indigenous group in Mexico. Nahua populations are concentrated in central Mexico, especially in Puebla, Veracruz, Hidalgo, Guerrero, and San Luis Potosí, with additional communities in México State, Morelos, Mexico City, Michoacán, and Durango. Their historical core included the Valley of Mexico, the Toluca Valley, the eastern half of the Balsas River basin, Tlaxcala, and most of Puebla. Nahuatl, their principal language, belongs to the Uto-Aztecan family and consists of numerous related varieties. About 1.7 million Nahuas speak Nahuatl or a related Nahuan variety. Nahua history is central to the study of Mesoamerican urbanism, writing, political organization, and cultural change after the Spanish conquest.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources describe Nahua urbanism and monumental architecture in the pre-Columbian period. The Mexica established an island settlement in Lake Texcoco and developed Tenochtitlan as their capital. Nahua groups also lived in *altepetl* city-states, including Xochimilco, the Tepanec territory, and the Acolhua territory.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention monumental architecture and agricultural practices in general historical accounts.

## Music & performance

 They do identify *Cantares Mexicanos* as a collection of songs in Nahuatl and mention theatrical works among the kinds of literature composed in Classical Nahuatl during the colonial period. No instruments, performance contexts, or musical techniques are specified.

## Dance & theatre

 Colonial-period Nahuatl literature is described as including theatrical works.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe several religious and civic practices. Before the Spanish conquest, Nahua peoples adopted religious practices including a ritual calendar of 260 days, and the Mexica practiced human sacrifice. During the colonial period, Christian missionaries attempted to eradicate Indigenous religious practices through violence, threats, and education. Nahuas were baptized with Spanish names, while some continued pre-Christian practices and beliefs privately or incorporated them into Christian religion without colonial authorities noticing.

In the mid- to late sixteenth century, Christian confraternities, or *cofradías*, were established in local communities. They supported celebrations devoted to particular Christian saints and functioned as burial societies for their members. Churches and church complexes continued to be built and elaborated, sometimes with murals combining Indigenous and Spanish forms.

## Foodways

The sources state that Nahuas adopted maize agriculture after entering the Mesoamerican cultural area. The colonial sources mention tribute, agricultural land, and labor arrangements.

## Oral tradition & literature

Nahua literary production includes chronicles, histories, poetry, administrative documents, legal records, codices, theatrical works, Christian writings, ethnographic descriptions, and local records. After the Spanish conquest, Nahuas adopted the Latin alphabet and began composing texts in their own language within twenty years of the Spanish arrival. The Colegio de Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco was inaugurated in 1536 to educate young Nahua noblemen and teach languages including Spanish, Latin, Maya, Nahuatl, and Zapotec.

The sources name several works. The *Florentine Codex* is described as a twelve-volume compendium of Aztec culture compiled by Bernardino de Sahagún. *Crónica Mexicayotl* records the royal lineage of Tenochtitlan and is associated with Fernando Alvarado Tezozómoc. *Cantares Mexicanos* is a collection of songs in Nahuatl. The *Huei tlamahuiçoltica* describes the apparition of Our Lady of Guadalupe in Nahuatl. Nahuatl documentation also includes censuses, town-council records, petitions, bills of sale, wills, testaments, and “primordial titles” asserting Indigenous communities’ rights to territory.

## Language & religion

Nahuatl is a language or group of closely related languages in the Uto-Aztecan family. Varieties are spoken mainly in central Mexico, with smaller populations in the United States and southern Nahuan languages in El Salvador and Nicaragua. The Mexican Instituto Nacional de Lenguas Indígenas recognizes 30 individual varieties within the language group labeled Nahuatl, while the Ethnologue recognizes 28 varieties with separate ISO codes. The varieties include Classical Nahuatl, modern Nahuatl languages, and the related Nawat language of El Salvador and Nicaragua.

Nahuatl was the language of the Mexica and became a prestige language and lingua franca in central Mexico and wider Mesoamerica. Spanish colonists and missionaries introduced the Latin script, and Nahuatl became a major literary language during the 16th and 17th centuries. In 1570, Philip II decreed that Nahuatl should become the official language of the colonies of New Spain. Later decrees restricted Indigenous languages, and by 1770 Classical Nahuatl had ceased to function as a literary language. Mexico’s General Law of Linguistic Rights of the Indigenous Peoples, promulgated on 13 March 2003, recognizes Nahuatl and other Indigenous languages as national languages in the regions where they are spoken.

Nahua religious life changed substantially after the Spanish conquest. Missionaries promoted Catholic baptism and attempted to suppress Indigenous religious practices, while Nahuas also retained or incorporated pre-Christian beliefs and practices into Christian religious life.
