## Overview

The Mapuche — called *Araucanians* by the Spanish, a term the people themselves largely reject — are the Indigenous inhabitants of south-central Chile and southwestern Argentina, including parts of Patagonia. Their homelands once reached from the Choapa Valley to the Chiloé Archipelago and later spread east into *Puelmapu*, the Argentine pampa and Patagonia. Today they make up 77.16% of Chile's Indigenous population and about 8.8% of the national total, concentrated in the Araucanía region around Temuco, with large numbers in Santiago and Buenos Aires after a century of rural-to-urban migration; roughly 92% live in Chile. Around half a million Mapuche were counted at the close of the twentieth century. They speak Mapudungun, classified either as a language isolate or as a small Araucanian family alongside Huilliche. In folk-culture terms they matter above all for two things: textiles and silverwork.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Manta** — the woven blanket-poncho worn by men and women alike, and the single most widely collected Mapuche textile; the Gothenburg collections hold men's ponchos acquired in southern Chile and at San Martín in Argentine Patagonia between 1917 and 1919. Nineteenth-century studio photographs show *lonko* (male leaders) draped in the manta as a mark of office.

**Quipan** — a square cloth wrapped around the body as a woman's dress, documented in every surviving Mapuche cabinet-card portrait from Traiguén, usually worn with a woven belt and a shawl.

**Ikulla** — the woman's shawl, pinned at the breast with a silver *tupu* and worn over the quipan; catalogue records treat *ikulla* and *manta* as alternatives for the same shoulder covering.

**Trariwe / trarihue** — the long woven waist sash, and technically the most informative Mapuche textile in the record. Two belts measured at the Museum of World Culture are *dubbelväv* (double weave): one in wool, about 43 mm wide and 300 cm long, with a warp of black, undyed white, red and green two-ply S-twist yarn at 36 threads per cm against only 3–4 wefts per cm; the other, collected near San Martín, in cotton with red, grey-green and yellow cabled warp at roughly 70 threads per cm. The extreme warp-face density is what produces patterning that reads as drawn rather than woven.

**Chamall** — recorded as *chamal* in a Traiguén portrait of a Mapuche man photographed with a stick, worn with the manta.

The sources name no motifs by name, so none are listed here.

### Clothing & dress

Men appear in the photographic record in a *manta* or *chamal* with a *trarilonko* headband. Women's dress is more fully documented: the *quipan* wrapped square cloth, bound with a woven *trariwe*, over it the *ikulla* shawl fastened by a *tupu* pin, and on the head either a *trarilonko* hair lace, a *lloven nitrowe* women's headdress, or a *medella* — a headdress of chained coins. Jewellery completes it rather than decorating it: *chaway* earrings, a metal collar or silver coin necklace, a *trapelakucha* pectoral pendant, a *pentreor* chest hanging of three chains, and rings. Hair is worn in braids. Infants travel on the mother's back in a carrier of wood and cord. Footwear in the Argentine collections is practical and equestrian: pairs of horse-hide riding boots from the Junín de los Andes district, collected with the separate woven bands that were wound around them. The most extreme garment recorded is military — a coat of mail built from seven folds of horse hide sewn with leather thongs and painted, taken from the body of a man shot in a frontier skirmish, and pierced by what may be a bullet hole.

### Architecture

The sources used here say little about Mapuche dwellings. What they do document is defensive and ceremonial building: at the time of European arrival the Mapuche had organised a network of forts and defensive works, and earthwork mounds built for ceremonial purposes have been found near Purén. Two standing timber structures are described in detail. The *ngillatuwe*, also called *la cruza*, is the collective altar of a community — a pole carved with the face and arms of Ngünechen or of an ancestor, set facing east inside ritual fields that are left uncultivated, often flanked by white and blue crosses and frequently blessed by a Catholic priest after erection; its destruction is believed to bring catastrophe on both the destroyer and the community. The *rewe*, the machi's own pole, stands at her house: a laurel or oak trunk with steps notched into it, branches of *klon*, *triwe*, *foye* or *coligüe* tied to the side, sometimes crowned with a *llang llang*, a rainbow-shaped arch of vines. One rewe collected near Pelal, Temuco, in 1938 is now in Gothenburg.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Mapuche pottery in the Swedish collections is plain domestic ware — several *lerkärl* (clay vessels) from Chile, including one described as modern and bought in a pawnshop in the Temuco district in the 1920s. Woodwork is better represented and locally named: the *rali*, a wooden soup plate, and the *patia*, a wooden bowl used as a washbasin, both collected around Pelal near Temuco, together with carved ladles and bowls. The Mapuche adopted iron working quickly after contact — the Picunche already worked copper — and learned horse riding, cavalry warfare, wheat and sheep from the Spaniards; the equestrian kit that follows is well represented by wood-and-iron stirrups, silver bridle ornaments from Estancia Tres Picos in Neuquén, whips, and *boleadoras*, the thrown cord-and-weight weapon. Catalogue notes distinguish a three-ball boleadora used for guanaco from lighter *boleadoras de avestruzeras* for rhea, and record that they were also thrown at the forelegs of wild horses to entangle them. Lance points, an obsidian arrow point from Molleo, a hairbrush and a rhea-skin tobacco pouch round out the household and hunting material.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Mapuche silver is a colonial-era craft with a trade origin: three centuries of commerce with Spaniards, Argentines and Chileans put large numbers of silver coins into Mapuche hands, and the jewellery was wrought from them. The forms are consistent across the collections and almost all are women's: the *trapelakucha* (recorded also as *trapalacucha*) pectoral pendant; the *trarilonko*, which in one Gothenburg record is a silver chain necklace worn *para la frente*, across the forehead, and which Wikipedia describes as a coin headdress; the *medella* coin-chain headdress; *chaway* earrings in pairs; the *tupu* shawl pin; the *pentreor* of three chains; and silver breast pendants. Machi wear silver jewellery as part of their ritual costume, with blue or purple head scarves and black shawls. Face painting is recorded for ngillatun participants. The sources do not cover tattooing.

## Music & performance

The documented Mapuche instruments are the ones that belong to ritual. The *kultrun* (catalogued as *cultrunü*) is a shallow drum — a laurel or oak bowl with goatskin stretched over it, conceptualised as a womb, its head painted with a cross representing the *meli witran mapu*, the fourfold division of the world. Four objects are typically sealed inside, two reckoned male (darts, bullets, *foye* leaves, charcoal, volcanic rock) and two female (maize, seeds, wool, *kopiwe* flowers). One kultrun in Gothenburg is recorded simply as "drum used by a medicine woman (machi)," bought near Pelal, Temuco, in 1938. Collected with it were four *pifilca* whistles — written *pilfulco* on the labels — explicitly described as whistles for the medicine dance, and later a *trutruca* (*tultruka*). Vocal practice is prayer: *ngillatun* are the prayers themselves, delivered by machi mostly in Mapudungun but often mixing Mapudungun and Spanish, and the words a machi speaks in the *küymi* trance state are interpreted aloud by a *dungumachife*, usually a family member. There are frequently taboos on photographing these rituals.

## Dance & theatre

Dance at the *ngillatun* is collective and directional: the assembled community dances both in rows and in a circle around the *ngillatuwe*, while mounted men perform the *awün*, riding in circles around the dancers in imitation of the sun. The medicine dance is the other documented context, and it is masked — a wooden *longcoche* mask for a medicine dancer was collected near Pelal, Temuco, in 1938, alongside the pifilca whistles played for the same dance, and a second undated dance mask came from Chile in 1921. The sources describe no dramatic or puppet theatre.

## Festivals & rituals

Mapuche ritual life is not organised around a fixed annual calendar in these sources; it turns on the *ngillatun*, described as the main ritual of contemporary Mapuche religion and often performed both before and after the harvest. The whole community takes part within a consecrated space; the ceremony can run more than two days; participants wear traditional dress and may paint their faces; maize, beans and *muday* are laid at the foot of the *ngillatuwe*, and an animal such as a sheep is sacrificed, the offerings and the dancing together understood to hold the cosmic forces in balance and avert catastrophe. Until the early twentieth century male elders called *ngenpin* (orators) usually led it; by the twenty-first they still officiated in Pewenche and Williche areas, while machi led in the south-central valleys and Andean foothills. The machi's own rites punctuate her career: the *machiluwün* initiation that relieves the calling-illness *machikutran*; the *ngeykurewen* that renews her marriage ties to her ancestral spirit, the *filew*, after which the old *rewe* is often left in a river to decay; the diagnostic *uluntun* of prayers, massage and rattles; the *datun* healing ceremony; and *pewuntun* divination. The funeral is the *awn*, designed to send the deceased person's *am* on so that no witch can capture it, and to clear malevolent spirits from the gathering; neighbours feel obliged to attend, since staying away invites suspicion of having had a hand in the death. Wine or *chicha* is poured regularly for the ancestors.

## Foodways

The sources treat Mapuche food mainly through ritual and through the vessels collected. The traditional economy is agricultural; wheat and sheep were taken up from the Spanish, and after the nineteenth-century conquest and resettlement the Araucanían economy shifted from sheep and cattle herding toward agriculture and timber, with subsistence on the reservations moving from cattle rearing to sedentary cultivation. The foods named are those offered at the *ngillatun*: maize, beans and *muday*, with a sheep sacrificed; and the *chicha* or wine poured out for the ancestors. Eating equipment is wooden and locally named — the *rali* soup plate, the *patia* basin-bowl, and carved ladles and bowls, all collected in the Temuco countryside in the 1920s and 1930s. One cabinet card from Traiguén shows two women kneeling at work processing foodstuffs while a third stands with a child. Beyond this the sources used do not cover Mapuche cooking methods, dishes or drink traditions.

## Oral tradition & literature

There are no written records of Mapuche legend from before the Spanish arrival: the beliefs were transmitted orally, and they are not homogeneous — they vary between territorial groups, villages and families. The best-attested narrative is the deluge *epeu*, in which the world is destroyed and remade through the conflict of Kai Kai, the water that floods, and Tren Tren, the dry earth that brings sun; almost all humanity drowns, the few survivors live by cannibalism, and at last a single couple is told by a machi to give their only child to the waters, which restores order. Dreams (*pewma*) and visions (*perimontum*) are themselves a narrative genre, since they are how spirits summon and instruct a machi, and the *ngenpin* were valued precisely as orators. The Mapuche also keep the remembered history of their long independence. The great literary monument of the Arauco War, by contrast, is Spanish: Alonso de Ercilla's epic *La Araucana*, which carried the name Araucanian into world literature and which some Mapuche still value for recording their resistance. Fernando Zúñiga's 2006 *Mapudungun* includes a traditional folktale and six contemporary Mapuche poems with recordings.

## Language & religion

Mapudungun — from *mapu* "land" and *dungun* "speech" — is classified conservatively as a language isolate, or as one of two members of an Araucanian family with Huilliche. Robert A. Croese divided it into eight dialect sub-groups falling into northern, central, south-central and southern groups, the eighth (Huilliche, from Lago Ranco and Río Bueno south) not mutually intelligible with the rest; Temuco is the epicentre of the present-day Mapuche territory. There was no pre-contact writing system, and the competing Alfabeto Mapuche Unificado, Ragileo, Nhewenh and Azumchefi alphabets remain unresolved; Mapudungun has been co-official with Spanish in the Chilean commune of Galvarino since 2013. Religiously the landscape is syncretic and mostly Christian: a 2001 survey of 352 Mapuche in Chol-Chol and San Juan de la Costa found 40.9% Catholic, 23.6% Evangelical Protestant, 9.7% Seventh-day Adventist, 8% followers of Mapuche traditional religion and 3.1% both.
