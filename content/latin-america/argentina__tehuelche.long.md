## Overview

The Tehuelche, who call themselves *Aónikenk*, are an Indigenous people of eastern Patagonia in South America, historically ranging across the plateaus and river valleys between the Strait of Magellan and the Colorado and Río Negro rivers, with the Chubut River dividing the two largest subdivisions recognised by most specialists — "Southern" and "Northern" Tehuelche. Their language belongs to the Chonan family, alongside Teushen, Selkʼnam and Haush. Once nomadic hunters who followed guanaco herds between winter lowlands and the summer plateaus and Andean ranges, they were dispossessed in the nineteenth century by Argentine and Chilean colonisation, by the sheep estates established across Patagonia, and by introduced epidemic disease; most now live in the cities and towns of Argentine Patagonia. Argentina's 2010 census recorded 27,813 people identifying as Tehuelche. In folk-culture terms they matter as the makers of the painted guanaco-skin cloak and of the stone-and-cord hunting weapon that the earliest photographs of Patagonia show in use.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Quillango** — the painted cloak of sewn guanaco skins, named in the catalogue record of a nineteenth-century photograph of a Tehuelche chief who wears it wrapped about the body with a knife tucked into the waist. Museum photographs show the same skin cloak worn by men, by young men in studio portraits, and by women, who carried an infant in the back of it; it is the garment by which Tehuelche sitters are identified in the Patagonian photographic record.

**Ponchos of the trade fairs** — woven garments were among the goods exchanged at two important eighteenth-century fairs, at Cayrú in the western Tandilia system and at Chapaleofú, which the Jesuits of the period, Thomas Falkner among them, called "poncho fairs". Livestock, agricultural produce and garments moved there between the peoples of the humid Pampas, northern Patagonia and both flanks of the Andes, and the fairs were one engine of the cultural exchange between Tehuelche, Ranquel and Mapuche.

**Woven blankets** — photographic records describe Tehuelche women wrapped in a blanket or in a woven blanket rather than in skin, sometimes with a headband, in one case while riding a horse heavily loaded with goods.

The sources used name no motifs or pattern names for these textiles, so no motif vocabulary is given here.

### Clothing & dress

The documented dress is built around the guanaco-skin cloak. Men in the photographs wear a headband and the skin cloak, in one instance with ankle bands, and carry the stone-and-cord weapon in the hand with a knife at the waist of the cloak; another man appears with a headband, earrings and a guanaco cloak. Two figures leaning on a boat wear skin cloaks together with leggings, while four men in the distance behind them wear European clothing — a direct record of the period when the two wardrobes stood side by side. Women wear either a skin cloak or a woven blanket, with a headband, and necklaces appear in a portrait of a woman photographed alongside a Tehuelche boy. A boy is shown with a headband, a necklace and a wrap around the waist. Footwear is attested only indirectly and from the Spanish side: the explorers of 1520 were struck by footprints enlarged by the animal furs used as foot coverings, which is one account given for the name *Patagones*, "the big-footed", used by the Spaniards from Oviedo onward. The sources used do not distinguish a separate ceremonial costume.

### Architecture

Tehuelche groups were mobile but moved in defined circuits, predominantly west to east and back, and each season had its own camping places. The camps were called *aik* or *aiken* among the Tehuelche themselves and *tolderías* by the Spaniards and Creoles — a term and a way of living that the Mapuche subsequently adopted from them, one of the clearest cases of borrowing running from Tehuelche to Mapuche rather than the other way. Each group held specific hunting and gathering territories whose boundaries were fixed by inherited markers in the landscape — a hill, a trough, a hollow, an important tree — whose significance is not recorded; a group unable to meet its needs at home had to ask permission of neighbours of the same ethnic group to use their resources, and taking them without it meant war. Groups numbered between fifty and a hundred people. The sources used describe no house form, roof type or architectural ornament beyond the name of the camp itself.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Two object classes dominate the catalogued material. The hunting weapon of stone and cord — the *bolas* of the photographs, catalogued in Swedish as a *boleador* "for ostrich hunting" — is held in the hand of a chief and of a young man in studio portraits and survives as a collected specimen. The second is the stone knife: an old blade from Santa Cruz, worked from a flint-like stone into an elongated, rounded-triangular form, with fine edge retouch on one face only. Both belong to a much older lithic sequence in the region: the **Toldense industry** of some six thousand years ago, with two-sided sub-triangular projectile points, lateral and terminal scrapers, bifacial knives and bone tools, and the later **Casapedrense industry** of roughly 7000–4000 BC, with a higher proportion of blade tools, read as a specialisation in guanaco hunting that continues into later Tehuelche life. A boy photographed in shrubbery holds a bow and arrows with a pot or basket at his feet. The sources used record no ceramics or metalwork.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment is documented only through the photographs: headbands worn by men, women, a young man and a boy; earrings on a young man in a guanaco cloak; ankle bands, uncertainly identified, on a chief; and necklaces — two of them worn at once by a woman in a head-and-shoulders portrait, one by a boy. The sources used name no jewellery types, materials or ritual functions, and record no tattooing, body painting or hair practice.

## Festivals & rituals

No annual festival calendar appears in the sources used. What they document is ritual life without an institutional frame: like other peoples without a state structure, the Tehuelche had no organised religious system with a liturgy and a vertical hierarchy, but held a body of belief in their own myths and rites, narrated and renewed by shamans who also practised medicine with the help of spirits invoked within themselves. Earth spirits were many, and above them stood a supreme being who made the world and does not intervene in it. Evil spirits called *guarichos* were part of the same world, and *Gualichu* is named as an evil spirit of the mythology. The one life-cycle rite the sources describe is marriage, and they describe it as a transaction within a strongly ordered family unit in which men held authority and women were subordinate: a father would offer his daughter in marriage in exchange for goods, and a man could have two or three wives according to his standing. The sources used do not cover birth, coming-of-age or funerary rites. Among sacred places, Mount Fitz Roy is named as one of the sites held sacred by groups who moved up to the central Patagonian plateaus or into the Andes in summer.

## Foodways

Tehuelche subsistence was hunting and gathering across the thicket steppes of Patagonia, with seasonal movement toward the guanaco herds: winters in the low ground — meadows, wetlands, shores and lake margins — and summers on the central plateaus or in the Andes. Guanaco and rhea meat, the rhea known as *ñandú* or *choique*, were the mainstays, followed by South Andean deer, deer, Patagonian mara, and even puma and jaguar, together with certain plants; cultivation was learned only late. Fish and shellfish were subject to rules rather than appetite — some groups prohibited eating fish outright. The adoption of the horse, known to them from about 1570 and in domestic use widely by the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries under Mapuche influence, remade this economy by remaking mobility: ancestral territories and movement patterns were both altered by it. The sources used name no prepared dishes, cooking methods, ceremonial foods or drink traditions.

## Oral tradition & literature

The narrative tradition recorded in the sources is cosmological. In one version of the creation myth the deity *Kóoch* brings order out of the world's chaos and creates its distinct elements; the Selkʼnam of Tierra del Fuego tell a parallel story of a world-maker named *Kénos'*, a variant of Kóoch through a common root, and in it a certain *El Lal* is sent. Within Tehuelche myth, through the god *Temauckel*, *Erral* created human beings and taught them the use of the bow and arrow. These narratives were carried and updated by shamans. Documentation and revival rest heavily on the last fluent speaker, Dora Manchado, who died in 2019; the language was recorded from her and has since been recuperated and revitalised by groups of Aonekkenk working with linguists and anthropologists. A revival programme named *Kkomshkn e wine awkkoi ʼaʼien* — "I am not ashamed of speaking Tehuelche" — works on spreading both language and culture. The sources used name no epic cycle, proverb collection or riddle tradition.

## Language & religion

Tehuelche — also *Aonekkenk* or *Aonekko ʼaʼien* — is a Chonan language, grouped with Teushen, Selkʼnam and Haush; it is nominative–accusative and marks the nominative but not the accusative, a pattern reported from only six languages worldwide. Within the wider "Tehuelche complex" the available evidence distinguishes six languages: the Chon group, the language of the Gününa küne (Puelche, also *Gününa yajüch*, often treated as an isolate), and Querandí; a transitional central Patagonian language, *Tehuesh* or *Téushenkenk*, was gradually displaced by Aonekkenk but survives in plateau place names, the province name Chubut deriving from *chupat*. Decline began with Mapuche expansion from the north, continued under Argentine and Chilean occupation of Patagonia and state-facilitated genocide, and accelerated as Spanish became dominant: from some 5,000 speakers around 1900 to 29 in 1983/84, four by 2000, two by 2012, and none after 2019. Religion remains as described above — Earth spirits, a non-intervening creator, and shamanic practice — with no organised structure.
