## Overview

The Guaraní are culturally related Indigenous peoples of South America who are distinguished from the related Tupi by their use of the Guarani language. Their traditional range includes Paraguay between the Paraná River and lower Paraguay River, Misiones Province in Argentina, southern Brazil, parts of Uruguay and Bolivia, and the foothills of the Andes and Gran Chaco near the Pilcomayo River in southeastern Bolivia. The Guarani number around 280,000, while more than four million people speak Guaraní across the region. Guarani is a Tupian language belonging to the Tupi–Guarani branch and is one of the two official languages of Paraguay. Guarani culture is represented in the sources through communal settlement, agriculture, mythology, oral transmission, language preservation, and the history of the Jesuit missions.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

 One historical account states that Indigenous women were stripped of clothing during colonial commerce, and describes the exchange of a cloak for an Indigenous woman under the pretext of exchanging her service.

### Architecture

Early Guarani villages often consisted of communal houses called *malocas* or *ocas*. Each house accommodated several families under the leadership of a chief. The sources describe communities organized through common interest and language.

The Jesuits established missions in Guarani territory. Loreto, the first Guayrá mission, was established on the Paranapanema in 1610. The sources describe churches and other mission buildings, including their destruction during attacks.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention large jars in which the dead were placed, inverted on the ground, and also refer to bows and arrows, firearms, cattle, sheep, horses, fields, orchards, and churches in historical contexts.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 They do record a tradition among Simba Guarani in which men maintained braided hair, although most young men no longer upheld this practice.

## Music & performance

 They do preserve several sound-related elements within mythology. Kuarahy Jára is described as a spirit that whistles like birds and protects them. The Iguazu Falls are said to reveal the sound of ancient battles at certain times. The sources also describe Guarani myths and legends as having been transmitted through word of mouth, with regional differences in the stories of gods and spirits.

Guarani religious narratives include Tupã, the supreme god of creation; Arasy, the moon goddess; Ñamandu, described as “the true father, the first one”; Yporú, more frequently known as Tupã; Jasy, who rules the night; and Aña, a malign deity who dwells at the bottom of the Iguazu River.

## Dance & theatre

 They describe communal life, missionary ceremonies, religious conversion, warfare, and oral mythology.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do describe religious practices and historical rituals. Early Guarani society practiced a form of animistic pantheism, much of which survived in folklore and myths. Animals, plants, and minerals were understood as animated and capable of becoming anthropomorphic beings, or as transformed souls of people.

The sources state that cannibalism was practiced on captives and that Martin Dobrizhoffer recorded its possible use as a funerary ritual. Later accounts describe the dead being placed in large jars set inverted on the ground.

The Jesuit missions became major religious and social institutions. The first Guayrá mission, Loreto, was established in 1610. In 1750, the Treaty of Madrid transferred to Portugal the territory of seven missions on the Uruguay River, and the Guaranis were ordered to leave. They refused, and seven years of guerrilla warfare followed. In 1767, the Jesuits were expelled from Spanish dominions by royal edict. Guarani caciques from Mission San Luis wrote to the Governor of Buenos Aires on February 28, 1768, asking that the Jesuits remain, but their request was denied.

## Foodways

Early Guarani communities were sedentary and agricultural. They subsisted largely on manioc, maize, wild game, and honey.

The Guarani also produced goods to sell and trade in order to fund the Jesuit missions. They helped grow crops that sustained the mission populations. After the Paulista invasions, Father Antonio Ruiz de Montoya purchased 10,000 cattle and was able to convert the natives from farmers to stock raisers. The sources later mention cattle, sheep, and horses in connection with the decline of the missions.

## Oral tradition & literature

Guarani mythology consists of narratives about the gods and spirits of different Tupi-Guarani peoples, ancient and current. There are no written records of the ancient myths and legends, and the Guarani language was not a written language until modern times. Religious beliefs were largely passed down by word of mouth, so accounts of gods and myths vary from one locale to another.

Creation narratives describe Tupã creating the ocean, forests, animals, stars, and humanity, with the help of Arasy. Rupave and Sypave were the first humans, whose names mean “Father of the people” and “Mother of the people.” Their sons included Tumé Arandú, considered the wisest of men and the great prophet of the Guarani people; Marangatú, a benevolent and generous leader; and Japeusá, a liar, thief, and trickster who was resurrected as a crab.

The seven sons of Tau and Kerana are Teju Jagua, Mbói Tu'ĩ, Moñái, Jasy Jatere, Kurupi, Ao Ao, and Luison. Other figures include Angatupyry, Pytajovái, Pombero, Abaangui, Jurupari, Jande Jari, and Mala Visión. Guarani myths and legends were compiled by the Universidad Nacional de Misiones and published as *Myths and Legends: A Journey around the Guarani Lands, Anthology* in 1870, translated into English in 1906.

## Language & religion

Guarani is a Tupian language in the Tupi–Guarani branch. It is one of the two official languages of Paraguay, alongside Spanish, and is also an official language of Bolivia and of Corrientes Province in Argentina. The language is spoken in Paraguay, Argentina, Bolivia, and Brazil, and variants are spoken in northeastern Argentina, southeastern Bolivia, and southwestern Brazil. Guarani is classified by UNESCO as “vulnerable” because its use is restricted to particular contexts, although many Guarani children speak it.

The Guarani language became written following the arrival of the Jesuits. Joseph de Anchieta wrote the first Guarani grammar in 1595. Antonio Ruiz de Montoya produced *Arte de la lengua Guaraní* in 1640 and *Tesoro de la Lengua Guaraní* in 1639. Alonso de Aragona completed a pedagogical grammar in 1629, which was printed in 1979.

Guarani religious traditions include animistic beliefs, a pantheon of gods, spirits associated with forests and rivers, and myths concerning transformed people, animals, plants, and minerals. Many Indigenous Guarani people were assimilated into modern society, and their belief system was altered or replaced by Christianity, but core beliefs remain active in many rural areas.
