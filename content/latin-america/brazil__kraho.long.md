## Overview

The Krahô (Portuguese *Craós*) are an Indigenous Timbira Gê people of northeastern Brazil. They historically inhabited a portion of what is now Maranhão, along the Balsas River, and were pushed westward by pioneer settlement and cattle farmers. Today they live on the Terra Indígena Kraolândia, an Indigenous territory in the municipalities of Goiatins and Itacajá in Tocantins, near the Maranhão–Tocantins border; the territory covers 303,000 hectares (1,170 square miles) and has a population of 2,992. The Krahô have historically been seminomadic, combining hunting and gathering with shifting cultivation. Their language, *Mehĩ jarkwa*, is a dialect of Canela-Krahô, a Timbira variety within the Northern Jê group of the Macro-Jê family. In folk-culture terms they are known through a body of plaited palm-leaf, gourd, feather and cotton work collected in the mid-twentieth century.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources for this profile describe no loom-woven cloth tradition; the pattern-bearing work documented is plaiting in palm leaf, together with a small amount of cotton weaving and painted surface decoration.

**Twill-plaited palm leaf** — The dominant technique across the collected objects: sleeping mats, storage baskets, carrying bags, tobacco pouches, fire fans and headbands are all plaited from palm leaf, with the twill itself carrying the ornament. One sleeping mat is plain-coloured with only a slight pattern raised by the twilling; a square-based basket is patterned in black on natural.

**Painted plaitwork** — Plaited surfaces are coloured rather than dyed in the weave. A sleeping mat is painted with *urucu*; a boy's head-dress of plaited palm leaf is stained with the same pigment; the "water monster" masks carry *urucu*-painted bands across the top.

**Log-racing headbands** — Palm-leaf headbands made specifically for log-racing, sewn with fibre thread and painted in two colours, yellow-ochre (the catalogue identifies the yellow as possibly *urucu* pigment) and purple. Two examples are recorded, both registered in 1956.

**Woven cotton belts and armlets** — A woman's singing-belt is woven in red cotton and finished with tassels of boiled beads. A composite armlet combines plaited cotton with beads and is painted with *urucu*. Red cotton tassels and fastenings also secure several of the feather head-dresses.

**Plaited footwear** — A pair of sandals is plaited from palm leaf, with the twist of the plait projecting outward from the toe-strap.

### Clothing & dress

What the sources document is head-gear, belts and footwear rather than body garments. Ceremonial head-dresses are composite: one sets *arara* (macaw) and falcon feathers into a bast headband finished with red cotton tassels; another is built on plaited palm leaf with six *arara* feathers mounted on canes at the back, cotton tassels and cotton fastenings. A boy's head-dress follows the adult form at smaller scale — plaited palm leaf stained with *urucu*, crowned with a crest of *arara* feathers — indicating that the same dress vocabulary is made in children's sizes. Distinct from these are the plain palm-leaf headbands made for log-racing, painted yellow-ochre and purple, which are functional race gear rather than feather regalia. Women's dress is represented by the red cotton singing-belt hung with boiled-bead tassels, tying a garment directly to a musical role. Plaited palm-leaf sandals complete the documented outfit.

### Architecture

The sources used for this profile do not describe Krahô house forms, village layout or building materials.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

No ceramics or metalwork appear in the documented material; the household repertoire is plaited palm leaf, gourd, bamboo, horn and animal nail. **Fire fans** plaited from palm leaf occur in several examples. Storage is met by a plaited palm-leaf storage basket with a separate lid attached by a carrying string, a square-based basket patterned in black on natural, a palm-leaf-and-twill carrying bag with a shoulder sling, and a twill-plaited tobacco pouch with two loops for a sling or carrying handle — a set in which nearly every container is made to be carried, consistent with a seminomadic pattern of life. Sleeping mats are plaited from the same material, one left plain, one painted with *urucu*. Gourds serve as the body of rattles and whistles, and bee's-wax is used as a tuning plug.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Two lip ornaments of yellowish stone are recorded, the only stone objects in the documented set. Beadwork appears as "boiled beads", strung as tassels on the woman's singing-belt, on a double whistle, and on the bead strings that attach deer hooves to a gourd whistle; beads are also plaited together with cotton in an armlet that is then painted with *urucu*. Several whistles are worn: one hangs on a cord necklet, ornamented with yellow feathers and deer hooves, so that sound-producing objects double as neck ornaments. *Urucu* pigment recurs as the colouring agent across adornment, plaitwork and masks alike.

## Music & performance

The documented instruments are whistles and a rattle, and they are unusually varied in material. Whistles are built from a gourd with a red cotton loop and tassel; from a gourd ornamented with boiled beads and a cotton tassel; from a gourd with yellow feathers and deer hooves attached by bead strings, hung on a cord necklet; from the nail of a giant armadillo combined with a gourd disk and a cotton tassel; from a "cabibara" nail ornamented with yellow feathers; and from bamboo with a fibre loop, tuned with a plug of bee's-wax — the one recorded instance of deliberate pitch adjustment. A double whistle on a string combines boiled beads, tassels, gourd and horn in a single instrument. Percussion is represented by a composite dance rattle, a gourd mounted on a stick, brown in colour. The woman's red cotton **singing-belt**, hung with boiled-bead tassels, names a vocal performance role in the dress itself, and the deer-hoof pendants on the necklet whistle would sound with the wearer's movement. Beyond these objects, the sources used for this profile do not name song genres or describe performance occasions.

## Dance & theatre

The documented dramatic tradition is masked. Two **"water monster" dance masks** are recorded, both made of plaited palm leaf with two wooden horns mounted on them. The adult mask is decorated with *urucu*-painted bands across the top; the second is made for a child and has its horns decorated with deer hooves and red feathers, so the same masked figure is performed by children as well as adults. The composite gourd-and-stick dance rattle belongs to the same performance context.

## Festivals & rituals

The one ceremonial event the sources identify by name is **log-racing**, evidenced by two palm-leaf headbands made expressly for it, sewn with fibre thread and painted yellow-ochre and purple. The masked "water monster" performance, the gourd dance rattle and the feather head-dresses of *arara* and falcon feathers point to further ceremonial occasions, and the existence of a boy's head-dress and a child-sized mask shows that children take part, but the sources used for this profile do not set out a festival calendar, give seasons or dates, or describe birth, initiation, marriage or funeral rites.

## Foodways

The sources record the subsistence base but not the cuisine: the Krahô have historically been seminomadic, practising hunting and gathering alongside shifting cultivation. Hunting is corroborated obliquely by the materials of the collected objects — the nail of a giant armadillo and a "cabibara" nail worked into whistles, deer hooves strung as pendants on a whistle necklet and on a child's mask, horn in a double whistle, and *arara* and falcon feathers in head-dresses. Gourds, palm leaf, bast, fibre, cotton and bee's-wax complete the inventory of gathered and cultivated raw materials. The tobacco pouch is the only object pointing to a consumed plant.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used for this profile do not record Krahô folktales, narrative cycles, proverbs or storytelling occasions; the "water monster" of the dance masks is the only figure named, and the catalogue records say nothing further about it.

## Language & religion

The Krahô speak *Krahô*, called *Mehĩ jarkwa* [mẽˈhĩ jaɾˈkʰwa] by its speakers. It is a dialect of the Canela-Krahô language, itself a Timbira variety of the Northern Jê group within the Jê and Macro-Jê families, and it is spoken in Tocantins. Two phonological details are documented: a short /a/ can have the allophone [ə], and /ʋ/ is realised as [w] between vowels. The sources used for this profile do not describe the religious landscape of the Krahô or any spiritual practice beyond what the masked "water monster" figure implies.
