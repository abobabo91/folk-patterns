## Overview

The Aguano, also known as Awano, Ahuano, Hilaca, Uguano, Aguanu, Santacrucino, or Tibilo, are a people of Peru. They inhabit the lower Huallaga and upper Samiria Rivers and the right-bank tributary of the Marañon River. In 1959, they consisted of 40 families. Today they farm and have largely converted to Roman Catholicism. Their history includes contact with the Spanish in the 16th century, disease and warfare with the Jívaro people, and the joining of Aguano proper, Cutinana, and Maparina survivors into what became known as the Aguana people.

## Language & religion

Aguano is described as the extinct language of the Aguano people of Peru. Its classification is disputed in the sources: Loukotka identified it with Chamicuro, while Chamicuro speakers said that the Aguano people spoke Quechua; Steward stated that they adopted Quechua soon after the Conquest and that their original language was unclassified. Campbell and Grondona left Aguano unclassified because of lack of attestation, while Glottolog classifies it as Arawakan. The language has also been called Santa Crucino, and alternate spellings include Uguano, Aguanu, and Awano. Today the Aguano have largely converted to Roman Catholicism.
