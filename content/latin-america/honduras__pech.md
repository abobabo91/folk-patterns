## Overview

The Pech, previously known as the Paya, are an Indigenous ethnic group in north-eastern Honduras. They live particularly in Colón, Gracias a Dios and Olancho, including the Olancho Valley, the Río Guampú watershed, the upper Río Seco watershed and the foothills of the Sierra de Agalta. A 2007 census conducted by Indigenous organisations recorded 6,024 people who self-identified as Pech; another census listed 4,138. The Pech language belongs to the Chibchan languages and is severely endangered. Pech culture includes fishing, shifting agriculture, hunting, ceramics, liquidambar resin extraction, woven baskets and other handcrafted objects. Pech leaders continue efforts to preserve their language, land and cultural practices.

## Material culture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**Pech pottery and ceramics** include ceramic bowls, plates and jars, and some pieces are adorned with animal head sculptures. A collection found at the University of South Florida was identified as the craftsmanship of Honduran Indigenous peoples, the Pech. Archaeologists suggested that artefacts found at Islas de la Bahía were similar to those found in Pech territory on the mainland. A British archeologist collected Pech pottery and ceramics on Utila, where the objects can be seen in the Utila museum.

Pech hunting tools include the *cerbatana*, a type of blowgun, as well as bows, arrows, traps and harpoons. Fishing also involves ground soap plants used to poison water. Pech people sell handcrafted items including woven baskets, bags, placements and corn-grinding stones.

## Festivals & rituals

Pech traditional religion included ceremonies addressed to the spirit of the mountains, the spiritual owners of animals and the mermaid who cares for the fish.

## Foodways

Pech people have traditionally made a living through fishing and shifting agriculture. Their diet also includes food obtained through hunting, including birds, armadillo and agouti, as well as crustaceans and mollusks such as shrimp, crabs and jute, a freshwater snail. Formerly, they hunted quadrupeds such as deer, which are rare today. Fishing may involve a harpoon or the use of ground soap plants to poison water.

The sources describe the clearing of trees for agriculture through a “slash and burn” method. A stone axe and fire are used to clear trees and undergrowth, after which sticks are used for planting.

## Oral tradition & literature

Most Pech people today identify as Catholic and have retained only a small amount of myths and oral stories from their traditional religions. Pech cosmogony describes the structure and creation of the universe through dualities. The father God, named *Patako-ko*, is divided into celestial and terrestrial parts. The universe is described as a sphere supported by four beams; the subterranean world contains ferocious animals and ants, the earth is where humans live, and the sky has seven levels containing abysses, lakes and the eternal fire of the sun.

Pech leaders have worked to revitalise the native language.

## Language & religion

Pech or Pesh is a Chibchan language of Honduras, formerly known as Paya and also referred to as Seco. It is spoken in the Dulce Nombre de Culmí municipality of Olancho Department, and the language article records 300 speakers according to Yasugi (2007). Pech is the only remaining Chibchan language in Honduras and is classified as severely endangered. It is a tonal language with 16 consonants and 10 vowels, and it has high and low tones. Pech is an SOV language.

The Pech language uses the ethnonym *Pech* for the Pech themselves; *Pech-Hakua* means “other people.” The place-name element *taia* means “mine,” while *maia* refers to lands occupied by other, non-Pech people. Most Pech people today identify as Catholic, while traditional religious practices included ceremonies for the spirit of the mountains, the spiritual owners of animals and the mermaid who cares for the fish.
