## Overview

The Asháninka are Indigenous Amazonians who live in river valleys across central-eastern Peru and Brazil. They are known worldwide for their handwoven cotton robes and distinctive red face painting. Their way of life centers on slash-and-burn farming, hunting, fishing, and forest gathering, treating biodiversity as essential to survival.

## Material culture

Asháninka material culture is made entirely from natural materials—cotton textiles, forest seeds, animal bones and teeth, feathers, and cane—with no pottery or metalwork in the record.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Cotton weaving is the heart of Asháninka material culture, with all cloth made and dyed by women on household looms.

- **Cushma** — The central Asháninka garment—a full-length cotton robe dyed brown or bright royal blue, ornamented with seeds at the shoulders, taking up to three months for a woman to complete.
- **Striped woven cloth** — Vertical stripes in dark and light brown or beige appear throughout Asháninka woven cotton, used for tunics, bags, and hoods.
- **Painted cloth** — Decoration applied by hand paint to woven cotton, used on some smocks and bags alongside woven designs.
- **Baby-carriers** — Woven cotton carriers hung with ornaments of incised bone, seeds, and nuts as a permanent feature.

### Clothing & dress

The cushma serves as the everyday garment for all Asháninka, with gender distinctions in hair style and ceremonial dress.

- **Cushma** — Cotton robe worn daily by men and women as the standard garment.
- **Ceremonial head-dress (men)** — Woven circles of palm leaves decorated with feathers, worn by men for ceremonies.
- **Ceremonial head-dress (women)** — Woven cotton hood or head-cover, often with parrot feathers along the bottom, worn by women for ceremonies.
- **Face painting** (*achiote or annatto*) — Crushed red seeds of the annatto fruit applied to the face in various designs by men, women, and children.
- **Feather ornaments** — Belt fringes and hood decorations made from parrot feathers combined with seeds.

### Architecture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

No pottery or metalwork is documented; everyday objects are subsistence gear made from textiles and natural materials.

- **Hunting and fishing equipment** — Bows, arrows, and spears are the primary tools for hunting and fishing.
- **Woven cotton bags** — Plain or painted bags used to carry goods, some with sewn and tasselled straps.
- **Cane rings** — Ring-shaped pieces of worked cane, possibly used as head-dresses.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Asháninka adornment draws entirely from the forest—seeds, animal teeth, and feathers—with no use of metal.

- **Seed necklaces** — Multiple strings of black or colored seeds tied together and fastened around the neck.
- **Tooth ornaments** — Necklaces and bracelets made from the teeth of tapir, peccary, and monkeys.
- **Feather adornments** — Brightly colored feathers, especially parrot feathers, worked into necklaces, bracelets, and belt fringes.
- **Face painting** (*achiote*) — Crushed red seeds of the annatto fruit applied in various designs to the face.

## Festivals & rituals

No ceremonial calendar or lifecycle rites are recorded; ceremonies are marked primarily through special dress.

- **Ceremonial dress** — Men wear woven circles of palm leaves decorated with feathers; women wear woven cotton head-dresses for ceremonies.
- **Face painting** (*achiote*) — Crushed annatto seed applied by all ages in various designs as a general practice, not tied to specific occasions.

## Foodways

Asháninka subsistence is built on slash-and-burn agriculture combined with hunting, fishing, and forest gathering.

- **Cultivated crops** — Yucca roots, sweet potato, corn, bananas, rice, coffee, cacao, and sugar cane are grown in biodiversity-friendly cleared fields.
- **Hunting and fishing** — Bows, arrows, and spears are used to catch wild game and fish to supplement crops.
- **Forest gathering** — Fruit and vegetables are gathered from the jungle as a regular part of the diet.
- **Biodiversity as natural capital** — The forest itself is treated as the foundation of the Asháninka way of life, making forest loss a direct threat to food security.

## Oral tradition & literature

No folktales or storytelling traditions are recorded; language remains vibrant despite low literacy rates.

- **Language literacy** — Between 10% and 30% of Asháninka speakers read and write their own language; 15% to 25% are literate in Spanish as a second language.
- **Language standardization efforts** — Attempts to unify closely related Asháninka varieties around a single written standard have not succeeded.
- **Spanish language programs** — Since 1988, Peru has run programs teaching Spanish to Indigenous peoples including the Asháninka.
- **Indigenous activism** — Figures like Luzmila Chiricente Mahuanca are known as activists defending Indigenous rights and territories.

## Language & religion

Asháninka belongs to the Arawakan family and is classified as vulnerable; no organized religion is documented, though Catholic missions were historically resisted.

- **Asháninka language** (*Asháninka or Campa*) — An Arawakan language spoken by about 25,000 people in Junín, Pasco, Huánuco, Ucayali and adjacent regions; classed as vulnerable due to forest threats.
- **Arawakan relatives** — Related languages include Gran Pajonal Campa, Ashéninka, Machiguenga, Matsiguenga, Yine, and others in the Arawakan family.
- **Offensive term** (*Campa*) — The alternative name Campa, derived from Quechua for 'ragged and dirty,' is offensive to Asháninka speakers.
- **Catholic mission resistance** — Catholic missions from the 17th to 19th centuries, especially near Cerro de la Sal and Gran Pajonal, were resisted with some success.
- **Asháninka as official language** — Asháninka holds official language status in the regions where it is spoken.

## Glossary

- *achiote* — Annatto tree; its crushed red seeds used for face painting
- *annatto* — Bixa orellana; tree whose red seeds are crushed for face paint and dye
- *Asháninka* — The Indigenous people and their language of central-eastern Peruvian Amazon
- *Campa* — An alternative name for Asháninka (now considered offensive)
- *Arawakan* — Maipurean language family to which Asháninka belongs
- *Gran Pajonal* — Interfluvial grassland region where Asháninka settlements are located
- *Cerro de la Sal* — Salt Mountain; location where Asháninka resisted Catholic missions
- *slash-and-burn* — Agricultural method of clearing forest land by burning for cultivation
- *Junín* — Peruvian region where Asháninka people live
- *Pasco* — Peruvian region where Asháninka people live
- *Huánuco* — Peruvian region where Asháninka people live
- *Ucayali* — Peruvian region where Asháninka people live
- *Ene* — River in Asháninka territory
