## Overview

The Siona are an Indigenous ethnic group living near the border between Ecuador and Colombia, along the Aguarico, Cuyabeno and Putumayo rivers. In Ecuador, they live in Sucumbios Province, mainly in the Cuyabeno Wildlife Reserve; in Colombia, they live in the Department of Putumayo along the Putumayo River. Their population is approximately 400 people living in dispersed communities. The Siona call themselves *Gãtiya Bãĩ*, “People of the Wild Cane River,” and also refer to themselves as *bai*, meaning “people.” Their language, *baicoca*, belongs to the Western Tucanoan language family. Siona folk culture is expressed through dispersed river communities, communal labor and food-sharing, agriculture, hunting, shamanism, yajé ceremonies, ceremonial painting and the preservation of cultural practices under pressure from colonization, extraction and armed conflict.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name geometric designs associated with yajé experiences.

### Clothing & dress

 They do describe ceremonial clothing used during yajé ceremonies. Participants dress with feather crowns, beads, aromatic herbs and face paintings. These forms of adornment belong to the ritual setting in the designated yajé house, where the shaman guides participants through experiences associated with communication with other realms.

### Architecture

Siona communities consist of dispersed villages near rivers and streams. Houses are often constructed on stilts, a form influenced by colonial presence. In more recent times, houses have used tin roofs and nails rather than traditional vines and palm-leaf roofs. Siona communities traditionally move every 10–15 years, reflecting their seminomadic way of life. The sources also describe a specifically designated yajé house, where yajé ceremonies take place.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention traditional tools used in hunting, including the blow gun, spear and traps. In recent decades, the shotgun has become dominant as an efficient hunting tool. The sources also state that geometric designs associated with yajé experiences are translated into decorations on possessions such as pots. No specific ceramic technique, vessel name or metal object is identified.

### Jewelry & body adornment

During yajé ceremonies, participants wear beads and feather crowns, use aromatic herbs and apply face paintings. The sources connect the face paintings and decorations on possessions such as pots with geometric designs seen during yajé experiences. The sources also state that Siona people associate yajé with creative practices reflecting these designs. Hair is mentioned historically in connection with the name “Encabellados,” which Spaniards used to describe their habit of taking good care of their long hair.

## Music & performance

Yajé ceremonies are the principal Siona performance context described by the sources. Yajé is a tea made from a vine, *Banisteriopsis caapi*, and is used under the guidance of a shaman for mediation and communication with other realms, as well as for community or individual wellbeing related to game, weather, sickness or misfortune.

By some accounts, a yajé ritual usually lasts three days. The first night involves no singing; the second involves singing and transformation into animals; and the third culminates in reaching other realms. Each night lasts from sundown to sunrise. The shaman guides participants through the process and leads chants during the ceremony. These chants can promote communal images and reflect the communal orientation of Siona life. Participants may encounter ill-intentioned beings, and the shaman helps them through the experience.

## Dance & theatre

 They state that participants in yajé ceremonies may transform into animals and that the second night involves singing and transformation. This is described as part of the ritual experience rather than as a named dance or theatrical performance.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do describe yajé ceremonies, which are guided by a shaman and conducted inside a designated yajé house. Yajé is used for communication with other realms and for wellbeing connected with game, weather, sickness and misfortune.

A yajé ritual may last three days, with each night extending from sundown to sunrise. The first night involves no singing, the second includes singing and transformation into animals, and the third culminates in reaching other realms. Participants wear feather crowns, beads, aromatic herbs and face paintings. The shaman leads chants, protects and guides participants, and helps them when they encounter ill-intentioned beings. They do mention that shamans facilitate marriages, administer punishment for poor behavior and distribute food. Communal work is organized through the *minga*, in which friends and family help with manual labor; the person requesting help provides *chicha*, and the project ends in a party.

## Foodways

Siona subsistence includes slash-and-burn agriculture, fishing, gardening and hunting, together with imported foods. Manioc, also called cassava, and plantain are core vegetables cultivated on small farms. Families also maintain gardens around their houses, growing flowers, tobacco, cotton and fruit trees.

Named staple foods include *chucula*, a plantain drink; cassava, prepared as manioc flatbread; *chicha*, a fermented drink made from manioc and plantain; and manioc flour. Fruits include pineapple, sugarcane, papaya, guayaba and tree grape. Imported foods such as rice, noodles and chicken have become staples, while sugary beverages such as *guarapo* have become preferred in some contexts over traditional *chicha*.

Hunting focuses on large animals, including tapir, peccaries, woolly monkeys, howler monkeys, curassow and guans. Hunting is generally done by men. When someone catches a large animal, it is shared with the extended family. *Chicha* is also provided to people working in a *minga*, where food and drink form part of the social event.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They describe knowledge of the Siona worldview as compiled through conversations with six Siona elders. In this worldview, the world is divided into five realms resembling cassava griddles of sequentially smaller sizes. The bottom-most realm is “inside the earth,” followed by four “heavens,” the first being the earth that is readily perceived.

Natural things such as trees, clay and animals are associated with beings and forces in other realms. These realms affect one another, and communication with their beings can be accessed through yajé ceremonies. The sources also describe contemporary cultural preservation efforts, including yajé-house construction, workshops with elders, women-led enterprise and protection of the yajé vine through the Ceibo Alliance.

## Language & religion

Siona is a Tucanoan language of Colombia and Ecuador and is part of the Western Tucanoan language family. It is also known as Bain Coca, Pioje, Pioche-Sioni, Ganteyabain, Ganteya, Koka and Kanú. As of 2013, it was spoken by about 550 people. Ecuadorian Siona and Colombian Siona, as well as Secoya, have a high level of mutual intelligibility, although they differ lexically, morphologically and phonologically. The Siona refer to their language as *baicoca*, “the language of the people.”

Siona religious practice centers on shamans and yajé. Shamans serve as spiritual leaders and guide yajé ceremonies; they also have duties involving punishment, marriage, food distribution and community protection. The Siona describe five interdependent realms, and yajé enables communication with beings in them. Shamanic apprenticeships were disrupted by Capuchin missionaries and a boarding school in nearby Puerto Asís, but the shaman system has begun to be rebuilt since the 1990s.
