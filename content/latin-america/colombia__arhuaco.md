## Overview

The Arhuaco are an Indigenous people of the Sierra Nevada in Colombia who maintain strong traditions of handcraft and spiritual life. Their material world—woven bags, bast caps, reed flutes, and clay pots—is still actively made rather than remembered. They organize their society around spiritual leaders called mamos and the sacred law Kunsamü. They practice subsistence agriculture split between high and low altitude lands, grow coffee for sale, and their famous mochilas have become fashionable in Colombian cities.

## Material culture

Arhuaco material culture centers on textiles woven from locally grown cotton and wool, clay pottery, and carved wooden objects, all still actively made for household and ceremonial use.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The handmade mochila bag is the Arhuaco's best-known craft, now sold in major Colombian cities, while other woven goods serve specific household functions.

- **Arhuaca mochila** (*mochila*) — A handmade bag for household use and sale, now fashionable in Bogotá and other large Colombian cities.
- **Woman's mantle** (*Mákke*) — A cotton garment with contrasting bands created by drawing thread through the top and bottom.
- **Child-carrying cloth** (*Posa*) — A wool cloth with black stripes used to carry children until they can walk well.
- **Bast child-carrier** (*Si-sittjå*) — A second kind of child-carrier made of bast instead of wool, showing two fiber traditions for the same object.
- **Dyed bast bag** (*Totto*) — A looped-bast bag colored with aniline dye, combining imported dyestuff with local technique.

### Clothing & dress

Men make all traditional Arhuaco clothing, including bast caps for everyday wear and a feathered cotton cap reserved for secret religious dances.

- **Everyday bast cap** (*tóttosåma or tottasåma*) — A bast cap worn by men daily, made from becha fiber.
- **Ceremonial feathered cap** (*kákko*) — A cotton cap with feather ornaments made for the secret religious dance, ideally from every bird on earth, sharing its name with the sun.
- **Cotton poncho** — A long rectangular piece woven of cotton and wool with a center slit for the head.
- **Arhuaco trousers** — Traditional trousers worn with other clothing as part of male dress.

### Architecture

Arhuaco settlements include square-shaped houses and circular temples, with each family traditionally maintaining two houses at different altitudes for seasonal movement.

- **Circular temple** (*kankurwa*) — A circular ceremonial building, with separate ones for men and for women in major towns like Nabusimake.
- **Spiritual leader's hut** — A round grass dwelling occupied by a mama (spiritual authority), a distinct building type separate from square houses.
- **Wooden stool** (*kankávoe*) — A simple wooden seat used for sitting while weaving, cooking, and other work, found in every dwelling.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Arhuaco make clay vessels for cooking and ritual use, carved wooden household goods, and iron-tipped weapons for hunting.

- **Cooking pot** (*mandtjiki*) — A clay vessel for boiling, with old examples highly valued and kept in graves; a separate pot is reserved for religious feasts.
- **Drying tray** (*kampátiru*) — A tray used for drying clay vessels.
- **Lime vessel for coca** (*dtjåborrå*) — A vessel for holding lime, lifted on a stick, mixed with saliva and coca juice until it turns yellow.
- **Honey gourd with lid** (*yoámsi*) — A carved wooden gourd with lid for holding honey and strong paste seasoning, decorated with a circle-and-dot motif.
- **Hunting bow** (*kámme*) — A bow made of guava wood used for hunting.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Arhuaco wear glass bead necklaces in quantity as markers of wealth and status, alongside simpler seed necklaces and magical stone beads.

- **Glass bead necklace** (*tjikrúsa*) — Green imported glass beads worn in dozens of strands at a time as a sign of means and dress.
- **Seed necklace** (*minágro*) — A simple necklace strung from seeds, worn as plain adornment by poor girls.
- **Named necklace** (*tjamitj*) — A third type of necklace found in collections.
- **Magical stone bead** (*tumas*) — A polished stone bead used for amuletic rather than decorative purposes.

## Music & performance

Arhuaco play reed flutes, panpipes, drums, and rattles in ceremonial contexts, with paired flute playing a distinctive performance practice.

- **Reed flute** (*tjáro*) — Two types of reed flutes share this name: one with five finger holes always played as a duet with a companion, and one with a single hole.
- **Panpipe** (*ponnkri*) — A reed pipe instrument appearing in several forms, including versions with nine pipes.
- **Wooden drum** (*tjambår*) — A percussion instrument with a skin stretched over it.
- **Rattle from fruit** (*maráka*) — A dried fruit shell with seeds inside used as a shaken instrument.
- **Gourd rattle** (*marákka*) — The same rattle idea made from a gourd.

## Dance & theatre

The sources record a secret religious dance for which the feathered kákko cap is made, though no details of choreography or other dramatic traditions appear.

- **Secret religious dance** — A ceremonial dance for which the feathered kákko cap is made, whose name is shared with the sun.

## Festivals & rituals

Arhuaco ritual life centers on the mamo, a spiritual authority trained in caves for nine to fifteen years, who serves the sacred law Kunsamü.

- **Spiritual authority** (*mamo (also mamü)*) — A religious leader chosen at ages eight to ten and trained for nine to fifteen years in a cave before deciding whether to continue, specializing in philosophy, priestly office, medicine, or community counsel.
- **Sacred universal law** (*Kunsamü*) — The single immutable law of nature and society, pre-existent, represented by the boy Mamo Niankua.
- **Sacred mountain peaks** — Mountain peaks themselves are sacred, which is why the 1962 television tower on Mount Alguacil caused outrage.
- **Ritual feast pot** — A separate clay pot kept exclusively for cooking at religious festivals.

## Foodways

Arhuaco practice subsistence agriculture split sharply by altitude, growing different crops in high and low lands, while coca chewing is a traditional non-commercial practice.

- **High-altitude crops** — Potatoes, onions, cabbages, lettuce, blueberries, tamarillos, pumpkins, garlic and wheat grown in cool high lands.
- **Mid-altitude crops** — Corn, beans, yuca, arracacha, malanga, coca, cotton, pineapple, papaya, guava, passion fruit, granadilla, oranges and limes grown in mid-lands.
- **Coca chewing practice** — A traditional non-commercial practice mixing coca leaves with lime and saliva, distinct from cocaine produced by traffickers since the 1970s.
- **Strong paste seasoning** — A dark brown hard paste decocted from a thick-leaved plant with a painkilling agent, taken on the fingernail and chewed with coca leaves.

## Oral tradition & literature

Arhuaco transmit cosmology orally through nine to fifteen years of cave training with elders, centering on creator Kakü Serankua and the sacred law Kunsamü.

- **Creator god** (*Kakü Serankua*) — The creator or father who engendered the first gods and material living things.
- **Sacred universal law** (*Kunsamü*) — The account of origins of matter, its evolution, equilibrium and harmony, represented by the boy Mamo Niankua.
- **Oral transmission of knowledge** — Nine years in a cave with elders, followed by nine to fifteen years of training covering philosophy and specialisms, the mechanism by which cosmology is transmitted.

## Language & religion

Arhuaco speak Ikʉ, a vigorous Chibchan language with 8,000 speakers and 90% monolingual use, and practice their own cosmology centered on the Sierra Nevada as the heart of the world.

- **Arhuaco language** (*Ikʉ*) — A Chibchan language with about 8,000 speakers, 90% monolingual, spoken with vigor and used as a marker of epistemic modality in verb person-marking.
- **Creator god** (*Kakü Serankua*) — The creator figure at the center of Arhuaco religion.
- **Sacred law and world center** (*Kunsamü and Sierra Nevada*) — The sacred law and the Sierra Nevada as the heart of the world on whose well-being everything else depends.
- **Spiritual authority** (*mamo*) — The spiritual leader and highest religious authority in Arhuaco society.
