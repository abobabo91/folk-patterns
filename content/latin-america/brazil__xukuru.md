## Overview

The Xukuru, also called Xucuru, are an indigenous people of Brazil living in the state of Pernambuco. Their population is approximately 8,500. Their homeland is associated with the Ororubá Mountains and the Serra do Ororobá. Historical and geographical references also place them in the municipality of Pesqueira and in settlements formerly belonging to the aldeia of Cimbres. The Xukuru are related to the Tarairiú people. Their language, Xukuru, is a poorly attested extinct language known from a few word lists. The group matters to the documentation of folk culture through its continuing connection to indigenous land reclamation, the preservation and attempted reintroduction of its language, and ethnographic work conducted by William D. Hohenthal, Jr.

## Oral tradition & literature

The available language documentation consists of a few word lists and other fragmentary records. Curt Nimuendajú visited the Xukuru in 1934 and collected 137 words from four old individuals. His manuscript was lost after his death, although a short excerpt survived. Cicero Cavalcanti visited the Xukuru in 1944 and collected a vocabulary and sentences strung together by Xukuru speakers; these sentences contained no grammatical information. William D. Hohenthal, Jr. visited the Xukuru in 1951, conducted an ethnographic study, and collected vocabulary. An illustrated dictionary in Xukuru has been produced, and the Xukuru have attempted to reintroduce what remains of the language into community and daily life.

## Language & religion

Xukuru is also known as Xucuru, Shukurú, Ichikile, and Xukurú: Brobo. It is described as a poorly attested extinct language of Brazil, once spoken by the Xukuru people and known only from a few word lists. It was originally spoken in the Serra de São José and on the Meio River, Capibaribe River, and Taperoa River in Pernambuco and Paraíba. The Xukuru are associated with the Serra Ararobá and Pesqueira, formerly containing the aldeia of Cimbres. The last generation of Xukuru speakers was estimated to have been born in the 1860s to the 1870s. By Nimuendajú’s visit in 1934, the language had gone extinct.

Loukotka considered Xukurú to form a small family with Paratió and the unattested Garanhun (Garañun). Glottolog, following Fabre, treats Xukuru and Paratió as dialects. The sources describe the suffixes -go and -men, the negative adverb biá, and the adaptation of the Portuguese preposition em as ĩ. They also state that Xukuru vocabulary was mixed with material from Kariri, Pankararú, Iatê, and the língua geral.
