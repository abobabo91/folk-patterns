## Overview

The Pirahã are an Indigenous people of the Amazon rainforest in Brazil, the sole surviving subgroup of the Mura people and a hunter-gatherer society. They live mainly on the banks of the Maici River in Humaitá and Manicoré, in the state of Amazonas. As of 2024, they numbered 900 individuals. The Pirahã call themselves the *Híaitíihi* or *Hiáitihí*, roughly translated as “the straight ones.” Their language, Pirahã, is the only surviving dialect of the Mura language; other Mura dialects died out as groups shifted to Portuguese. Pirahã culture is documented through its hunting, fishing, canoe use, palm-leaf work, necklaces, oral cosmology, and distinctive language, which can also be whistled, hummed, or sung.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no Pirahã motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Men wear T-shirts and shorts obtained from traders. Women sew their own plain cotton dresses.

### Architecture

Pirahã build simple huts.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pirahã huts contain a few pots, pans, knives, and machetes. The people make scraping implements for producing arrowheads, loosely woven palm-leaf bags, bows, and arrows. They use canoes every day for fishing and for crossing the river beside which they live. When canoes wear out, they use pieces of bark as temporary canoes, while relying on neighboring communities’ canoe work for replacement canoes.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Pirahã decoration consists mostly of necklaces, used primarily to ward off spirits. Brazil nuts, wood, and sorva are traded for soda-can pull-tabs, which are used for necklaces.

## Music & performance

Pirahã can whistle their language, and Pirahã men use whistled communication when hunting in the jungle. The language can also be sung or hummed: consonants and vowels may be omitted altogether, with meaning conveyed through variations in pitch, stress, and rhythm. Keren Everett states that mothers teach children the language through constantly singing the same musical patterns.

They describe the Pirahã language as having a complex array of tones, stresses, and syllable lengths, and report that it can be encoded in music.

## Festivals & rituals

Pirahã religious beliefs include spirits that can take the shape of jaguars, trees, people, or other visible and tangible things. They believe in Igagai, a creator deity who restores the world through an eternal alternation of destruction and repair; Igagai also has a son who produces the cosmos every day. Their cosmology involves layers of land arranged as parallel planes. The Pirahã do not know the exact number of layers but can reduce it to roughly five; the middle level is inhabited exclusively by human beings. The kaoaiboge and toipe are described as posthumous transformations of humans, and Kaoaiboge is fed upon by the cannibalistic toipe.

## Foodways

Pirahã are hunter-gatherers and understand the behavior of local animals, including how to catch and avoid them. They fish, gather fruit and nuts, and obtain small game. They do not store food in any quantity and generally eat it when they get it. They have ignored lessons in preserving meat by salting or smoking.

Pirahã cultivate manioc plants that grow from spit-out seeds and make only a few days’ worth of manioc flour at a time. They trade Brazil nuts and sex for consumables or tools, including machetes, gunpowder, powdered milk, sugar, and whiskey. They also trade Brazil nuts, wood, and sorva, a rubbery sap used in chewing gum, for soda-can pull-tabs.

## Oral tradition & literature

They report that stories may contain subordinate ideas inside other ideas, a phenomenon Daniel Everett describes as recursion of ideas. Pirahã cosmology includes accounts of spirits, deities, layered planes of land, and posthumous transformations.

The sources also describe a historical account in which the Pirahã said that Xigagaí, one of the beings living above the clouds, stood on a beach and threatened to kill them if they entered the jungle. Everett and his daughter could see nothing, but the Pirahã insisted that Xigagaí remained there.

A 2012 documentary called *The Grammar of Happiness*, aired on the Smithsonian Channel, reported that a school had opened for the Pirahã community, where they learned Portuguese and mathematics.

## Language & religion

Pirahã is the indigenous language of the Pirahã people of Amazonas, Brazil. It is the only surviving dialect of the Mura language, while suspected relatives such as Matanawi are extinct. Speakers refer to their language as *Apáitisí* and to their ethnic group as *Hiáitihí*. The Pirahã language is vigorous, and the community is mostly monolingual. Most remaining speakers know only a few words of Portuguese; most men understand Portuguese, while women have little understanding of it and never use it as a form of expression.

Pirahã has no cardinal or ordinal numbers according to the general description in the sources, although analyses discuss *hói* and *hoí* as terms for a small quantity and a larger quantity. It has no grammatical distinction between singular and plural, even in pronouns. The language can be whistled, hummed, sung, or encoded in music. Linguists debate its phonology, clause structure, recursion, pronoun history, and number terminology.

Pirahã religion includes spirits, Igagai, layered planes of land, abaisi, kaoaiboge, and toipe. The sources present differing descriptions of Pirahã beliefs and do not document formal institutions or named religious specialists.
