## Overview

The Cora are an Indigenous ethnic group of northwestern Mexico who call themselves *náayerite* in the plural and *náayeri* in the singular. They live chiefly in the municipalities of El Nayar, Rosamorada, Ruiz, and Tepic in Nayarit, as well as in Mezquital, Durango, and settlements in Jalisco. They inhabit rugged mountain and canyon country and reside within *comunidades indígenas* and *ejidos*. The 2000 Mexican census reported 24,390 people in Cora-speaking households; 16,357 were reported to speak Cora. Cora belongs to the Corachol branch of the Uto-Aztecan language family. Their folk culture is documented here through agriculture, religious syncretism, named deities, myths, dialects, and historical relations with neighboring peoples.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do describe a religious system shaped by both the pre-Conquest religion and Catholicism. The Cora yielded to Spanish control in 1722, and Spanish accounts state that many became Christian and continued to practice “Catholic-derived customs.”

## Foodways

The Cora cultivate maize, beans, and amaranth and raise some cattle.

## Oral tradition & literature

Cora mythology includes a creation myth concerning the fifth sun and a myth in which the human race descends from a man and a dog-woman who were the only survivors of a mythical cataclysmic deluge. The sources describe these myths as including Mesoamerican elements and, in the latter case, motifs shared with the geographically and linguistically adjacent Huichol.

## Language & religion

Cora is an indigenous language of Mexico in the Uto-Aztecan family and forms the Coracholan subgroup with Huichol. It is a verb-initial language with agglutinative and polysynthetic grammar; its usual word order is verb, subject, and object. The sources describe two main variants recognized by Ethnologue: Cora del Nayar, also called Cora Meseño, and Cora de Santa Teresa, also called Cora Tereseño. Cora speakers recognize five dialects, while the Instituto Nacional de Lenguas Indígenas recognizes nine. The named varieties include Jesús María, Dolores, Rosarito, San Blasito, San Juan Corapan, Santa Teresa, La Mesa del Nayar, Presidio de los Reyes, and San Francisco.

Cora religion is a syncretism of the pre-Conquest religion and Catholicism. Its three principal ancestral divinities are Tayau, the sun god and “our father”; Tetewan, the underworld goddess associated with the moon, rain, and the west; and their son Sautari, “the flower picker,” associated with maize and the afternoon. Sautari is also associated with Jesus Christ. Tayau’s priests, the *tonatí*, formerly held the highest authority in Cora communities.
