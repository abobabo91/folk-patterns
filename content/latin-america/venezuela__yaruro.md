## Overview

The Pumé people, also known as Yaruro, are a Circum-Caribbean Indigenous people native to the Llanos ecoregion of Venezuela, west of the Orinoco River. They are divided into River Pumé, who live along major drainages of the Orinoco River, and more nomadic Savanna Pumé, who reside on the Llanos. The Pumé language is an unclassified Indigenous language isolate spoken along the Orinoco, Cinaruco, Meta, and Apure rivers. It was spoken by approximately 9,500 people as of 2015. The Pumé are significant in folk-culture terms for their seasonal mobility, palm-fiber technologies, bows and arrows, all-night tohé dances, verse and response singing, and plant-based medicines. “Yaruro” is a historical name still used in Venezuela, while the people refer to themselves as Pumé, meaning “real human.”

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Palm-leaf fiber work* — Savanna Pumé use moriche palm leaves and fibers to make string, rope, hammocks, basket containers, basketry mats, and diverse woven containers and objects. River Pumé primarily use fibers from macanilla palms.

*Whole-leaf carrying baskets* — Women make carrying baskets from whole palm leaves, with tumplines for carrying gathered plant foods and other materials.

*Woven containers and mats* — Palm fibers are woven into containers and mats used for subsistence, storage, and other critical technologies. Collection and processing of the fibers are especially common during the dry season.

*Hammocks* — Nylon hammocks are among the market goods desired by Savanna Pumé, while hammocks made from palm fibers form part of the locally manufactured material culture.

### Clothing & dress

Manufactured clothing and cloth have been readily available to the Pumé since at least the 19th century. Western-style dress is common among Savanna and especially River Pumé. Among Savanna Pumé, some traditional elements of women’s clothing have persisted, while some older men recently still eschewed pants in favor of loincloths.

### Architecture

Savanna Pumé are mobile hunter-gatherers who shift their primary residence during every dry and wet season. During the dry season they live in small brush shade structures; during the wet season they use more robust structures thatched with palm leaves. They also establish temporary camps for fishing, raw-material collection, and staging movements between their primary camps. Houses are constructed mainly from wood, palm-leaf thatch, and vines used to tie the framework together. Moriche palms are a critical resource for roofing and other technologies. Architectural wood and thatch may be moved with the camp and used to rebuild structures. Some zinc lamina is used for roofing and extensively reused during camp moves. River Pumé, who are currently sedentary, increasingly construct hybrid forms combining traditional and Criollo-influenced materials and designs.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Savanna Pumé manufacture most of their material technology, while obtaining some market goods through trade, primarily with River Pumé. Outside goods include cooking pots, steel knives, machetes, shovels, used clothing, matches, tobacco, nylon hammocks, whetstones, and other tools. Women use wooden digging sticks, including sticks with steel tips, and both men and women use shovels and machetes for garden work and harvesting. Men use knives, machetes, bows, arrows, specialized harpoons, and arrows for caimans or turtles. Metal arrowpoints include scavenged, traded, and reused pieces of metal; bone was reported as an earlier material.

## Music & performance

The central Pumé performance tradition described in the sources is the *tohé*, an all-night dance held for religious and social purposes. These dances last 11 hours and are held 3–4 nights a week throughout the year. Among Savanna Pumé, all camp members gather in the plaza. Smaller River Pumé communities also hold village-wide dances, while larger River Pumé communities may organize separate dances attended by extended families.

The tohé includes verse and response singing throughout the night, with increasing speed and frequency of circulation around a central pole erected in the dance plaza. A male dance leader performs most of the singing and leads the energetic dancing. Men sing primary verses and chorus responses, while women sing the choruses, join the movement around the pole, and perform specialized dance steps. The songs are partly formulaic but also contain substantial improvisation, and they are rhythmically and melodically complex. Rattles are the only musical instrument used by the Pumé in this performance.

A specialized women’s performance is the *añikuí tohé*, or “little sister tohé.” Among Savanna Pumé, older women sing it continuously from their hammocks for 11 hours, from approximately sunset until sunrise, without musical instruments. River Pumé women normally conduct it during the daytime, often for a shorter duration. The practice is associated with curing and with personal insight into problems affecting the community, singer, or family.

## Dance & theatre

The tohé is both a dance and a religious-social institution. Participants circulate around a central pole in the dance plaza while singing, and women perform specialized steps several times during many dances. The performance can involve healing events for people experiencing physical diseases or psychological distress, including depression, grief, and frustration. It can also lead to trance-states in the dance leader or other individuals.

During trance, possession by spirits or deceased Pumé individuals is understood to occur. The possessed person speaks through those personages to the community or to particular individuals about current concerns. The *añikuí tohé* is a related women’s singing and curing performance, especially associated with older women.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do describe seasonal movements and ritual performances. Savanna Pumé shift their primary residence during every dry and wet season, with additional temporary camps for fishing, collecting raw materials, and organizing movement between camps.

The tohé is held throughout the year, 3–4 nights a week, and lasts 11 hours. During these performances, men, women, and children smoke tobacco, while men also take hallucinogenic snuff in group bouts and sometimes chew a second hallucinogen. No food is consumed during the dances. Tohé performances can be directed toward healing, psychological support, community concerns, and communication involving spirits or deceased Pumé individuals.

Marriage consists of establishing co-habitation by the couple and involves no formal ceremony, exchange of goods, or overt negotiations among adults. By the age of 15, most Savanna and River Pumé girls are married. Boys also marry young. A small percentage of men have more than one wife, usually sisters.

## Foodways

Savanna Pumé subsistence changes substantially between the dry and wet seasons. During the wet season, men primarily hunt small terrestrial game such as armadillos, tegu lizards, other small lizards, and rabbits. Larger game, including capybara, deer, anteaters, and caimans, is obtained very infrequently. Women collect wild tubers that form the main wet-season food. Both sexes perform some garden work, producing complementary manioc without abandoning the collection of wild plants.

During the dry season, men fish with bows and arrows, hook and line, and fish poison. They also hunt birds and capture small numbers of turtles and tortoises. Women, accompanied by some men, collect feral mangos in prodigious quantities, together with a few less important small fruits. In the past, turtle eggs were collected, but this is now very uncommon. Manatees are recognized as potentially edible, though no recent reports of their consumption are given.

River Pumé depend more on cultivated crops, fish year round in the major rivers, hunt and gather foods, and may work in wage labor. They raise small numbers of chickens and pigs.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Pumé tohé songs are rhythmically and melodically complex, partly formulaic, and substantially improvisational. Their verse and response singing continues throughout the night. The women’s *añikuí tohé* also consists of extended singing, with Savanna Pumé women performing continuously for 11 hours. These performances address curing, personal insight, and current problems affecting the singer, family, or community. Portions of the Bible were translated into Pumé in 1999. The Pumé alphabet was devised in 1982, and linguistic research and a Yaruro language dictionary are identified in the source material.

## Language & religion

Pumé is an Indigenous language isolate, although its classification is not settled. It is also called Yuapín or Yaruro and is spelled Llaruro or Yaruru. The language is spoken along the Orinoco, Cinaruco, Meta, and Apure rivers of Venezuela. The Pumé refer to their own language as *pũmɛ̃́ mãɛ̃́*, “language of the Pumé.” It has 19 consonants and 15 vowels, making 34 phonemes. The Pumé alphabet was devised in 1982.

The Pumé engage in religious practices centered on the tohé dance. Trance may involve possession by spirits or deceased Pumé individuals, who communicate through the possessed person. Pumé healing also includes plant-based medicines, medicinal chants, touching, and the “sucking-cure.” The sources identify Puana as a goddess in Yaruro mythology. They also state that all Pumé are aware of the efficacy of western medicines and desire greater access to them.
