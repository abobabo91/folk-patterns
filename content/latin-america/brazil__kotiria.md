## Overview

The Kotiria are an Indigenous people of Brazil and Colombia who live in northwestern Amazonia, on the Vaupés River. Their total population is estimated at 1560, with 447 in Brazil. They speak Kotiria, also known as Wanano, a Tucanoan language belonging to the Eastern Tucanoan family and its Northern branch. Kotiria remains the first language of most of the population and is described as a healthy Indigenous language. The sources document Kotiria history, multilingualism, language structure, and research traditions, including work by Kristine Stenzel, Janet Chernela, Nathan Waltz, and Carolyn Waltz. They also record ceremonies involving dance and burial practices, although the supplied sources provide little description of material culture.

## Language & religion

Kotiria, also known as Wanano, is a Tucanoan language spoken in the northwest part of Amazonas in Brazil and in Vaupés in Colombia. It belongs to the Eastern Tucanoan family, specifically its Northern branch, along with Tucano. Kotiria has an SOV sentence structure and a nominative-accusative system. Its grammatical categories include nouns, verbs, particles, pronouns, and interrogatives. The language has evidential, imperative, interrogative, and irregular verb suffixes, and it has no adjectives. Gender is marked on nouns with human referents. The sources state that Jesuit and later missionary activity encouraged Indigenous peoples to abandon their beliefs, practice Christianity, and speak Portuguese.
