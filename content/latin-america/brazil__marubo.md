## Overview

The Marúbo are an Indigenous group who primarily inhabit the western Amazon rainforest in Brazil, especially the Vale do Javari Indigenous Territory in the far west of the country. This territory covers 83,000 square kilometres and is also home to the Kanamari, Tsohom Djapa, Matis, Kulina Pano, Korubo, Matses or Mayoruna, and other groups. The Marúbo speak a Panoan language, known as the Marúbo language, which is threatened by competition with Portuguese. Their society is organized into 18 matrilineal clans and into communal settlements centered on malocas. Their history includes contact with rubber workers, forced involvement in the rubber industry, later trade with plantations and river merchants, missionary activity, and the political unification of formerly independent subgroups.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state that Marúbo women craft clothing and beads, and that village storehouses contain clothing and other materials.

### Architecture

Marúbo villages are organized around communal huts called *Malocas*. These buildings stand at the center of the village and are inhabited by several nuclear families and an owner. Each wife and her children occupy an area of roughly 9 square meters, marked with posts, where they cook and sleep; husbands may stay periodically with one of their wives. Inside the maloca are two benches where men eat their two daily meals, one before work and one after. Women eat on mats in the center of the room, where there is also a trough used for crushing grains. Storehouses on the outskirts of the village hold tools, machines, clothing, and other materials.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention tools, machines, clothing, beads, hammocks, bows and arrows, shotguns, fishing equipment, troughs, benches, mats, and storehouses. During the rubber industry period, outside technologies and guns became important, and by the 1940s the Marúbo traded animal pelts and raw rubber for industrial products.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Marúbo women engage in body painting and craft beads.

## Music & performance

They state that the Marúbo have a special variety of their language used only during special rituals. The external reference associated with the Marúbo language includes collections in the Archive of the Indigenous Languages of Latin America described as “Wordlist & Songs,”.

Ritual practice includes the use of tobacco, snuff, and ayahuasca by married men over the age of thirty. The Marúbo take ayahuasca to connect with the forest and use both snuff and ayahuasca during shamanic rituals.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document shamanic rituals in which the Marúbo use snuff and ayahuasca. Ayahuasca is taken in order to connect with the forest. The sources state that only married men over the age of thirty can take tobacco, snuff, or ayahuasca, while younger men are generally viewed and treated as subservient to older men.

They do describe polygamy: a man may marry one or more of his wife’s sisters. They also describe major historical changes in Marúbo social life, including the work of João Tuxaua, who brought different Marúbo subgroups together to make peace and stop fighting. The Marúbo subsequently became a confederation of independent tribes unified into a larger whole.

## Foodways

The Marúbo hunt animals with bows and arrows and shotguns. Hunting with bows and arrows is becoming less common. Animals hunted with firearms include spider monkeys, woolly monkeys, collared peccaries, tapir, white-lipped peccaries, paca, piping guan, and curassow. The Marúbo also practice hook-and-line fishing, trap monkeys, and sometimes keep them as pets.

Their cultivated plants include maize, banana, manioc, papaya, guava, nettles, and cotton. Women tend swiddens, cook, and harvest bananas and manioc. Inside the maloca, men eat two daily meals on benches, one before work and one after, while women eat on mats in the center of the room. A trough in this central area is used for crushing grains.

The Marúbo drink *Caisuma*, a beverage fermented with saliva.

## Oral tradition & literature

According to Marúbo tradition, their language originated with the Chaináwavo, a now-extinct subgroup of the Marúbo, before spreading to other Marúbo subgroups. The sources state that this account suggests earlier cultural interaction and that linguists find the explanation controversial. The origins of the Marúbo people are not definitively documented.

They mention collections in the Archive of the Indigenous Languages of Latin America described as “Wordlist & Songs,” but provide no further description of this documentation.

## Language & religion

Marúbo is a Panoan language of Brazil. It is considered threatened because of competition with Portuguese. The main language of the Marúbo is the Marúbo language, while many older Marúbo people have some knowledge of Spanish and Quechua because of historical contact with Peru. In more recent times, Brazil has become the main point of contact with the outside world: most young men have learned Portuguese, while fewer young women have done so. Marúbo has agglutinative grammar and subject-object-verb word order. Its tense system is graded according to the distance between the time of reference and the time when an event occurred.

The name “Marubo” is an accepted exonym, and the sources state that there is no known name the Marubo use for themselves. Individuals may have different names within the tribe and for use with the outside world. Religious practice includes shamanic rituals involving snuff and ayahuasca, with ayahuasca used to connect with the forest.
