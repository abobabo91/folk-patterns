## Overview

The Karajá live in villages along the Araguaia and Javaés rivers and on Bananal Island in central Brazil. Their culture centers on the river's seasonal rhythms, with festivals during the dry season and fishing camps during the rains. They are known above all for women's painted ceramics and ceramic dolls, for men's feather ornaments, for distinctive body painting, and for the masked aruanã ceremonies. Their population has declined from about 45,000 in the early twentieth century to roughly 3,200–3,700 today.

## Material culture

Karajá material culture draws on feathers, bone, shell, wood, paint and plant fibers, with distinctive patterns and ornaments made by both women and men for different purposes.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Body painting with genipap juice, charcoal and annatto dye is the living pattern medium of Karajá art, practiced above all by women.

- **Body painting** — Women paint the body with genipap juice, charcoal and annatto dye, with black circles on the cheeks as the Karajá signature design.
- **Featherwork diadems** (*raheto, lori-lori*) — Men make light pink and white feathers glued to a plant-fiber ring, with a thread-cross of black and white cotton yarn topped by feather tufts or blue feathers.
- **Feather-and-raffia belts** (*wetakana*) — Men wear waist ornaments combining feathers with plaited raffia palm fiber.
- **Basketry** (*taririny*) — Both women and men weave baskets of plaited grass with feather bunches; basketwork also forms the body of the aruanã mask.
- **Knitted cotton forearm cuffs** — Pairs of tightly knitted red-dyed cotton cuffs with flared edges worn on the forearms.

### Clothing & dress

Karajá dress is built from distinct ornamental pieces — feathered head ornaments, belts, cuffs and jewelry — rather than a full garment.

- **Feather diadem** (*raheto, lori-lori*) — A men's head ornament of feathers glued to a low plant-fiber ring, worn at special ceremonies.
- **Bast loin skirt** — A skirt of plant fiber worn by men, shown on ceramic dolls and the aruanã dancer.
- **Lip ornament** (*olyò, tembetá*) — A quartz or wooden ornament worn in the lip by men.
- **Wooden ear ornament** (*nõhõtisana*) — Incised wooden ornaments for the ears, with simpler ear-staves worn by youths.
- **Leg rattle** (*weru*) — A tortoise-carapace rattle with feathers and wood worn on a man's upper leg so he sounds his own step.

### Architecture

The Karajá are highly mobile, moving between villages on high ground during the rains and temporary fishing camps during the dry season.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Women make painted pottery vessels and modeled clay animals; men work gourd, wood and bone into dishes, pipes and weapons.

- **Clay pot** (*butxi (north), boti (south)*) — Painted vessels with black or brown rhomboid ornament, made by women and used for storage and serving.
- **Ritual clay dolls** (*ritxokó*) — Seated painted clay figures with bast skirts, commonly made for export, representing women.
- **Clay animal figurines** — Modeled and painted clay crocodile, turtle, jaguar and macaw figures, often incised.
- **Gourd rattle** — Calabash rattles on wooden handles with feather tufts and burnt-line fields, used in music and dance.
- **Scarificator** (*latxi*) — A tool of wood, bone and wax used to mark the skin, indicating ritual scarification practices.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Karajá adornment is made from feather, bone, shell, wood, quartz, cotton and paint, with men's pieces dominating collections.

- **Lip ornament** (*olyò, tembetá*) — A quartz or wooden ornament worn in the lip by men.
- **Wooden necklace** (*nõhõ*) — A necklace of wood, feathers and vegetable fiber worn by men.
- **Forearm cuff** (*dexi*) — Knitted red cotton cuffs worn tightly on the forearms with flared edges.
- **Body scarification** (*latxi*) — The scarificator tool of wood, bone and wax shows that the skin itself was worked with ritual markings.
- **Hair ornament** — Black hair worn with red braids, rendered in painted decoration on ceramic dolls.

## Music & performance

Karajá instruments include rattles and a trumpet-decoy; performance takes place during the dry season festivals.

- **Gourd-and-bamboo trumpet** (*jurona*) — A decoy instrument used in music and potentially in hunting.
- **Gourd rattle** — Calabash rattles on wooden handles, some with feather tufts and burnt-line decorations.
- **Tortoise-shell leg rattle** (*weru*) — A rattle tied to a man's upper leg so the dancer sounds his own footsteps.
- **Song** (*wi (north), wiu (south), wii*) — Sung performance with male and female speech differences, with the word borrowed into neighboring languages.

## Dance & theatre

The aruanã masquerade is the named Karajá ceremonial dance tradition, performed in the dry season.

- **Aruanã mask** (*aruanã*) — A tall basketwork and feather hood with two accompanying grass skirts, worn for ceremonial use.
- **Txakohi mask** (*txakohi*) — A ceremonial mask borrowed from the Apyãwa people and incorporated into Karajá practice.
- **Irabure mask** (*irãwore*) — A ceremonial mask given by the Karajá to the Apyãwa through cultural exchange.
- **Dance board** (*bykyrè ritxore*) — A feathered mat board of feathers, raffia and wood bearing dance figures.

## Festivals & rituals

Karajá festivals happen during the dry season when the rivers are passable, with ritual specialists like shamans and mourning practices for the dead.

- **Shaman's staff** (*obi*) — A staff covered with red feathers and white down, finished with a blue macaw feather and painted, carried by ritual specialists.
- **Grave post** — Painted and feathered models of grave posts made by men and used for both sexes in funeral rites.
- **Age-marked ornament** — Ear-staves made for youths indicate ritual marking and progression through life stages.
- **Ceremonial feather diadem** (*raheto, lori-lori*) — A feather head ornament worn by men only at special ceremonies.

## Foodways

Karajá livelihood rests on farming, fishing, hunting and craft, with fishing of the first importance and diverse crops from swidden cultivation.

- **Pirarucu fish** (*bdòlèkè (north), bdòkùjkè (south)*) — A large fish caught and eaten, named in the Karajá language.
- **Arowana fish** (*idjasò*) — A fish species caught in local rivers.
- **Turtle stew** (*marara*) — A prepared dish of turtle, borrowed into Apyãwa from Karajá, showing long culinary contact.
- **Puba flour** (*bèrò*) — A cassava flour dish borrowed into Mẽbêngôkre as bero, showing food exchange between groups.
- **Manioc and maize** — Staple crops grown by swidden cultivation, along with bananas, beans, potatoes, yams and watermelons.

## Oral tradition & literature

Karajá oral tradition includes the hunter Uaica; the language itself is well documented in grammars and dictionaries by linguists and community projects.

- **Uaica the hunter** (*Uaica*) — A figure in Karajá legend and narrative tradition.

## Language & religion

Karajá is a verb-final Macro-Jê language with four dialects, obligatory direction marking on verbs, and distinct male and female speech forms.

- **Karajá language** (*Iny rybè*) — A Macro-Jê language with simple nominal and complex verbal morphology, noun incorporation and obligatory verb direction marking.
- **Northern Karajá dialect** — One of four dialects, spoken in the northern villages along the Araguaia River.
- **Xambioá dialect** (*Xambioá*) — A southern dialect reported with only eight elderly fluent speakers in 1998, now highly endangered.
- **Male k-drop** — Men drop the /k/ sound that women pronounce, a mark of masculinity that women resist by keeping the conservative form.
- **Shamanism** — The religious practice shown through shaman's staffs and grave-post ritual objects for the dead.
