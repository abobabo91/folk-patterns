## Overview

The Wayana are a Carib-speaking people living in the southeastern Guiana highlands, across Brazil, Suriname, and French Guiana. In French Guiana they live along the upper Maroni River and its tributaries, including the Litani and Lawa rivers. The Wayana language belongs to the Cariban family. A 1980 census recorded approximately 1,500 Wayana: 1,000 in French Guiana, 400 in Suriname, and 150 in Brazil, although later counts differ. Wayana society developed through the amalgamation of smaller groups, including the Upului, Upurui, Opagwana, Kukuyana, and Kumarawana. Their folk-cultural significance in the sources lies especially in village organization, the *tukusipan* community house, the *ëputop* coming-of-age ritual, shamanic practice, and the continued documentation of their language and material arts.

### Architecture

Wayana villages often include a community house called a *tukusipan*. Villages commonly consist of no more than one extended family and are linked to neighboring villages through kinship, marital exchanges, shared rituals, and trade. They are not permanent and may be abandoned after the death of a leader. Missionaries and state representatives encouraged settlement in larger villages.

## Festivals & rituals

The principal named ritual in the sources is *ëputop*, also called *maraké*. For a long period, coming of age was associated with this ceremony. A wicker frame filled with stinging ants or wasps was applied to the bodies of adolescent boys and girls, who emerged from the ceremony as adult men and women. Older Wayana partly defined their Wayanahood by the number of *ëputop* ceremonies they had undergone during their lives. Many younger Wayana rejected the necessity of the ritual for becoming a valued member of society, and few ceremonies occurred in the period described by the source.

One recent ceremony took place in 2004 in the village of Talhuwen. It was organized by Aïmawale Opoya, grandson of Wayana leader Janomalë, in consultation with French film director Jean-Philippe Isel. Despite its decline, *ëputop* was included in the inventory of intangible cultural heritage prepared by the French Ministry of Culture in 2011.

## Foodways

 They mention hunting and fishing as matters about which village shamans may be consulted, and they refer to a Wayana-Aparai documentation project focused on food systems and traditional knowledge of flora and fauna. The supplied text does not provide the names or preparation of any foods.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that Wayana history is known through oral tradition as well as descriptions by twentieth-century European explorers. Oral tradition contributes to accounts of the relatively recent emergence of the Wayana as a distinctive group and their relationship to smaller groups such as the Upului, Opagwana, and Kukuyana.

Several projects document Wayana knowledge and language. The Brazilian government’s ProDocult project created digital archives and trained Indigenous peoples in documentary filmmaking and anthropological research. The Wayana-Aparai project focused on food systems and traditional knowledge of flora and fauna. The sources also list linguistic grammars, dictionaries, ethnographic studies, and a recording of Wayana.

## Language & religion

Wayana is a Cariban language spoken mainly in the borderlands of French Guiana, Brazil, and Suriname. The language has been described as morphologically rich, with extensive use of affixes. Its speech classes include nouns, verbs, postpositions, adverbs, and particles. The language uses two principal verb sets and has a split-ergative system. The sources state that the language is vigorous in French Guiana and developing in Suriname, while its condition in Brazil is described as moribund by Ethnologue, although it remains in active use in some villages.

Wayana villages are often led by a shaman, or *pïyai*. Shamans mediate contact with the world of spirits and deities, act as healers, and are consulted about hunting and fishing. They state that American missionaries encouraged conversion from 1962 onward and provided health care and schooling.
