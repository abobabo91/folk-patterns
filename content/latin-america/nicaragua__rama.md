## Overview

The Rama are an Indigenous people living on the eastern Caribbean coast of Nicaragua. A majority live on Rama Cay, an island in the Bluefields Lagoon, while additional communities are dispersed on the mainland from Bluefields to Greytown. Sumu Kaat, Tiktik Kaanu, Wiring Cay, Monkey Point, Bangkukuk Taik, Corn River, Punta Gorda, and Cane Creek are recognized as predominantly Rama communities. Their population has increased in recent years to around 2,000 individuals. The Rama language belongs to the Chibchan family and is severely endangered. Rama cultural life includes subsistence fishing and farming, hunting, navigation, dory construction, mutual assistance, and the continued revitalization of their traditional language.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Before the arrival of missionaries, the Rama population on Rama Cay reportedly lived in homes lacking exterior and interior walls.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do identify dories as culturally significant wooden objects: the Rama are commonly known for their skill in crafting dories and for their reputation as the best navigators on the coast. The language sources also mention tongs, hooks, lances, and pots in example sentences.

## Music & performance

The Rama language article gives examples involving speaking and learning Rama, including *Rama kuup alkwisbang*, translated as “Let’s speak Rama,”. The sources also state that language revitalization efforts have achieved some success as the Rama increasingly associate their traditional language with their identity.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe historical and religious changes associated with Christian missionary activity. German Moravian missionaries began work in Bluefields in 1847, and the first Moravian mission was established on Rama Cay in 1858. The mission contributed to a shift away from traditional practices and the use of the Rama language. Today the Rama predominantly practice in the Moravian Church.

## Foodways

Rama foodways combine subsistence farming, fishing, and hunting. Poor soil conditions in the rainforest limit extensive agriculture, so the Rama cultivate small fields along river banks. Crops named in the sources include bananas, plantains, corn, rice, beans, coconuts, pineapple, breadfruit, and pejibaye. Fishing contributes significantly to the diet, including oysters, cockles, freshwater shrimp, coppermouth, and snook. Hunting is practiced primarily on the mainland, where white-lipped peccary, tapir, and deer can be found. The sources also describe sexual division of labor: males are responsible for hunting, fishing, and planting, while females participate in gathering and domestic duties. Some Rama engage in small-scale commercial fishing, but a majority continue traditional occupations such as subsistence fishing and subsistence farming.

## Oral tradition & literature

Rama oral tradition states that the Miskitu gifted Rama Cay to the Rama in the 18th century in recognition of their help in fighting the Teribe people of Costa Rica. Language preservation is an important contemporary effort. In 1985 the Rama Language Program was launched in response to requests to “save the Rama language.” Early efforts to locate and communicate with native speakers were difficult because inhabitants of Rama Cay still rejected use of the language. More recent revitalization efforts have achieved some success, and some speakers describe the language as “rescued.”

## Language & religion

Rama is a Chibchan language spoken by the Rama people on Rama Cay and south of Lake Bluefields on Nicaragua’s Caribbean coast. The language is severely endangered. In 1992, approximately 36 fluent speakers were found among an ethnic population of 649 individuals, while only 4 speakers were reported on Rama Cay. The Rama language had become increasingly displaced by Rama Cay Creole, a form of English creole. The language contains subject prefixes, verbal tense and mood suffixes, postpositions, and a basic SOV pattern in transitive sentences. It has borrowed words from Miskito, English, Rama Cay Creole, and Spanish. Moravian missionary activity contributed to language loss, and the Rama predominantly practice in the Moravian Church. Individuals in southeastern Nicaragua make up 96% of the membership of the Moravian Church in Nicaragua.
