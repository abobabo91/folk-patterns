## Overview

The Achi are a Maya ethnic group in Guatemala. They live in municipalities of the department of Baja Verapaz, including Cubulco, Rabinal, San Miguel Chicaj, Salamá, San Jerónimo, and Purulhá, as well as parts of Granados and el Chol. Their language, Achi, is a Mayan language closely related to Kʼicheʼ. The language has two dialects: Rabinal Achi, spoken in the Rabinal area, and Cubulco Achi, spoken in the Cubulco area west of Rabinal. Achi cultural life includes textile production, ceramics, basketry, woodworking, metalworking, foodways, cofradías, ceremonial dances, oral traditions, and the performance of Rabinal Achí.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

** *Telar de cintura* (backstrap loom)** — Achi textiles are made on the backstrap loom or with *palitos* (sticks). Housewives produce *güipiles*, *bandas* (sashes), *servilletas* (napkins), and *manteles* (tablecloths), teaching the processes to their daughters.

** *Güipil*** — The women’s *güipil* has colors and embroidered figures associated with names and thoughts related to human life. The meaning of the figures is described as a matter reserved for women, who weave without knowing the symbolism.

** *Corte*** — The *corte*, or trim, is part of women’s dress. Unlike the *güipil*, cortes or *enaguas* (petticoats) are made by men on *telares de pie* (standing looms).

** *Ixkak*** — Achi weavers use *ixkak*, described as white and brown dyed cotton. The raw material is collected at the end of winter and is called *mish*.

** *Cinta en colores*** — The colored ribbon is part of women’s clothing. The red trim was created later and is manufactured in the urban area of Rabinal and other places in the Achi region.

** *Cordón* and *uxaryo*** — The cord and *uxaryo* (collar or necklace) were brown or black, along with the colored ribbon.

Textiles are produced through processes that include placing thread in the loom, separating the thread, adding threads for embroidery, and forming figures.

### Clothing & dress

Achi clothing has traditionally been made with traditional instruments and is known for its colorfulness. Men’s attire was commonly made by women from cotton: the same thread was used to weave the shirt, white pants, and red sash. Men planted and harvested the cotton, produced the thread, and used natural paint to dye the prepared thread black. The masculine dress includes *camisa* (shirt), *pantalón blanco* or *calsonsillo Blanco* (white pants or white underpants), and *banda roja* (red ribbon).

Women’s dress includes the *güipil*, *corte*, *cinta en colores*, *cordón*, *uxaryo*, and *cordel* (cord). Rural women continue to make their own *güipil*, though the trim is more technical and is often made with fine threads and colors from the western part of the country. The *cordel* was used to tie women’s hair. Both men’s and women’s costumes had ceremonial and everyday variants.

The colors have stated meanings: white represents dawn, bones and teeth, purity, and well-being; red represents life, strength, sunlight, and blood; black represents hair, the pupil of the eye, night, and the place where the dead lie; yellow relates to corn, crops, and family; green represents nature and plantations; blue represents rivers, lakes, seas, and sky; and brown represents earth.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Achi ceramics include handmade and technologically produced forms. Handmade pottery begins with clay taken from a communal plot called the “*capilla del barro*” (chapel of clay). The clay is dried, moistened, stamped until smooth, and shaped with the body into pots, jars, pitchers, jugs, skillets, and bowls. Handmade pottery is fired in the open air, surrounded by dung and wrapped in dry straw, called *zacate* or *zacatón*.

Technological ceramics use a foot-operated potter’s wheel and may be produced in parts when pieces are large or tall. These objects include vases, flowerpots, lamps, amphorae, and tableware. Glazed and aniline-painted ceramics from Rabinal and Chicaj are used to personify dances.

Achi metalworkers use wrought iron in Cubulco, Rabinal, San Miguel Chicaj, and Purulhá for door knockers, balconies, doors, and ironwork. Tin is used in Salamá and Rabinal for lanterns, candelabras, and candlesticks. Woodworkers make furniture, maracas, rattles, guitars, violins, masks, and toys.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They mention the *uxaryo* as part of women’s dress and describe the *cordel* as a cord used to tie women’s hair.

## Music & performance

Achi musical practice includes marimba ensembles with drums, whistles, and *chirimías* (shawms). The *tun*, described as an instrument of pre-Hispanic origin, accompanies the Rabinal Achí dance with long trumpets. The *adufe* is a square drum of Arab origin, while three small drums called *aj ec* are used in the dance of the Negritos in Rabinal. Harp, violin, and *guitarrilla* groups, with a corresponding *puñetero* or *bordonero*, are common in Baja Verapaz and are described as having Q'eqchi' ancestry.

The Achi also produce musical instruments as part of woodworking, including guitars and violins, and as other products they make the *tun* and *chirimía*. The sources associate instruments with ceremonial dances and regional performance.

Rabinal Achí is a Kʼicheʼ-language performance of pre-Hispanic origin. It is performed in Rabinal during the Festival of San Pablo Apóstol from 17 January to 25 January. The performance has seven main characters and presents the proclamation of the Rabinal people to Kʼicheʼ rulers, the capture and sentence of Kʼicheʼ Achí, and his sacrifice after he says goodbye to his people. *Xajooj tun* means “dance of the tun.”

## Dance & theatre

The Achi perform many dances, which transmit knowledge of customs. Before public performances, dancers rehearse while elders guide younger people through scenes and movements. Offerings are made to *uk'u'x kaaj* and *uk'u'x uleew*, and an *ajq'iij* (lieutenant) conducts a vigil with the participants’ masks, candles, incense, and liquor called *awasib'al*, invoking ancestors associated with the dances.

Regional dances include Rabinal Achí, also called *Xajooj Tuun*, in Rabinal; *Palo Volador* in Cubulco; *El Venado* and *El Costeño* in Salamá; *El Venado* and *Los Mazates* in Purulhá; and *El Baile de la Pichona* and *De Toritos* in San Miguel Chicaj. Ceramics from Rabinal and Chicaj are used to personify Rabinal Achí, Venado, Negritos, La Conquista, Torito, and Animales.

Rabinal Achí is both a dramatic tradition and a performance associated with the Festival of San Pablo Apóstol.

## Festivals & rituals

Achi ceremonies revolve around *cofradías*, fraternal groups whose members maintain the proper conduct of festivities. The Achi also celebrate Easter, Christmas, and Christmas Eve. The sources identify sacred shrines and mounds, including Chipichek, Chusxan, B'ele tz'ak, and Cuwajuexij. The *Tzolk'in* calendar directs agricultural rites and ritual cosmogony.

The Fiesta de la Virgen del Patrocinio is celebrated in Rabinal from November 18 to November 22. A carved image is shared from house to house, and a novena is held in the home of the family that requests the Virgin. The couple with this responsibility is called the *mayordomos de la cofradía de la Virgen*. During the celebration, the Convite de Rabinal and Santa Cruz del Quiché are presented and cofradía members use fireworks.

Rabinal Achí is performed from 17 January to 25 January during the Festival of San Pablo Apóstol. Each year, a new *Alí Ajaw* (Achí Princess) of Rabinal is elected.

Cofradías are present in Rabinal, San Miguel Chicaj, Salamá, Cubulco, and San Jerónimo. The sources state that Rabinal has 16, San Miguel Chicaj 8, and Salamá 3. In Rabinal, the cofradía of Corpus Christi is celebrated 60 days after Holy Week. It invokes rain, wind, and clouds for a good corn harvest; corn is described as sacred and as the basis of Achi food.

## Foodways

Corn is central to Achi foodways. The main drinks are *atol blanco*, *tres cocimientos*, *shuco*, *atol de arroz*, and *arroz con leche*. The first three are made from corn. *Atol blanco* and *tres cocimientos* can use white or yellow corn, while *shuco* uses black corn. *Atol de arroz* is made with rice and milk, either cow’s milk or powdered milk, and with *panela de dulce* and the essence of *atol blanco*. These drinks are made by women and served as snacks for workers and family members. The custom is passed from mother to daughter.

The two best-known dishes are *pinol* and *bochbol*, also called *boschbol*. *Pinol* is made from roasted, ground corn with seasonings and *chompipe* (turkey). *Bochbol* uses tender *ayote* leaves, corn dough, toasted and ground ayote seed, and tomato. The mixture is shaped into small cylindrical rolls and cooked in a pot over a fire. Ground seeds and tomato are added when it is served, and it is eaten hot. *Pinol* is food for special parties, while *bochbol* is commonly eaten. Recipes are passed from mothers to daughters.

Rabinal’s markets trade ceramics, textiles, oranges, pinol, boxboles, and atoles.

## Oral tradition & literature

Achi oral traditions include legends narrated by several types of storytellers. Specialized storytellers preserve historical memory, advise the community, and act as sentinels of regional cultures. Among the Achi, this storyteller is called *ajtzij* (Anciano Mayordomo, or Elder Steward). Occasional storytellers narrate with less mastery but retain much ancestral wisdom and tradition.

During rituals on sacred hills and at shrines, *ajtzij* narrate legends recalling mythical history. An important genre is Señores y Dueños de Cerros, or Owners of the Hills, shared largely with Alta Verapaz. In Cubulco, the *cerro Cajiup* in the *valle del Urram* is said to be inhabited by *rajawales*, spirits of the community and region.

The Achi and Pocomchí associate myths with the spread of corn from their department to Guatemala and the world. Variations of “Cuando el Dios Mundo encerró al maíz” (“When the God of the World locked away the corn”) are deeply rooted in Cubulco, San Jerónimo, San Miguel Chicaj, and Purulhá.

Rabinal Achí is an important Kʼicheʼ-language theatrical work. Achi oral knowledge is transmitted by elders to younger generations through oral tradition.

## Language & religion

Achi is a Mayan language closely related to Kʼicheʼ. It is spoken primarily in Baja Verapaz, Guatemala, and has two dialects: Rabinal Achi and Cubulco Achi. Salamá, Cubulco, Rabinal, San Miguel Chicaj, and San Jerónimo are identified as municipalities where Achi is the mother tongue.

Achi religion is described as a syncretic complex of Christianity and animism. Sacred shrines and mounds are important, and the most sanctified sites named by the sources are Chipichek, Chusxan, B'ele tz'ak, and Cuwajuexij. Achi ceremonial dances request permission from *uk'u'x kaaj* and *uk'u'x uleew*. Rain, wind, clouds, and corn are treated as sacred elements connected with subsistence.
