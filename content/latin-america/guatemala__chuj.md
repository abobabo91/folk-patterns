## Overview

The Chuj or Chuh are a Maya people whose homeland is in Guatemala and Mexico. Most Chuj live in the Guatemalan department of Huehuetenango, especially in the municipalities of San Mateo Ixtatán and San Sebastián Coatán, with additional communities in parts of Nentón and smaller numbers in the Mexican state of Chiapas. Estimates of the total population range from 30,000 to over 60,000. The Chuj language belongs to the Q'anjobalan branch of the Mayan languages and is most closely related to Tojolab'al, spoken in Mexico. Following emigration to the United States in the 1980s, many Chuj also lived in Los Angeles. Their folk-culture profile includes distinctive town-based clothing, communal landholding, traditional beliefs connected with hills and caves, and a history of resistance shaped by land loss and poverty.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Trade garments** — Chuj clothing varies between the towns. Men usually wear a wool short-sleeved tunic lightly embroidered at the neck and arms, while women wear a cotton broadcloth overblouse elaborately embroidered in red, yellow, green, and black.

*Chuj* — The term traditionally refers to the loose wool overgarment worn by Chuj men. According to folk tradition, the name Chuj was coined by Tzeltal conscripts of the Spanish and originally described this garment.

### Clothing & dress

Men usually wear a wool short-sleeved tunic with light embroidery at the neck and arms. Women wear a cotton broadcloth overblouse embroidered in red, yellow, green, and black. The sources distinguish clothing between the towns.

### Architecture

They do record an archaeological Chuj urban settlement called *Wajxaklajun*, also known as Ystapalapán, on the outskirts of modern San Mateo Ixtatán. It includes mounds and plazas and dates from between 600 and 900 A.D. The sources also mention Maya structures underneath the modern city of San Mateo and a pagan shrine built in the hills among the ruins of pre-Columbian temples.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Chuj grammar sources give *teʼ* as the word for wood in the nominal classification of houses and *kʼen* as the word for metal in the classification of knives.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The available clothing description concerns wool tunics, cotton overblouses, and embroidery only.

## Music & performance

They do record religious practices in which incense and offerings were burned and turkeys were sacrificed at a shrine in the hills among the ruins of pre-Columbian temples. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription in the supplied material concerns the Chuj.

## Festivals & rituals

Traditional Chuj beliefs hold that natural features such as hills, rock outcrops, streams, and caves are inhabited by spirits. The spirits in caves are often understood as ancestors of the townspeople and may be approached for aid and advice. Death is described as a transition to “ancestorhood,” while deathbed instructions become binding obligations enforced by spirits through illness and misfortune.

Spirits may be approached at family altars, cave entrances, hilltops, and, in San Mateo, at cross-sites and accesses to the Maya structures beneath the modern city. In the late 17th century, the missionary Alonso de León reported that Chuj inhabitants burned incense and offerings and sacrificed turkeys at a pagan shrine in the hills.

Marriage is traditionally arranged, although Church weddings are relatively rare because of the cost of having a priest officiate. Chuj men also practise “bride kidnapping,” in which a woman is abducted rather than her family being formally petitioned for her hand. Descent is reckoned bilaterally, and the nuclear family commonly shares a compound with the husband’s brothers and parents.

## Foodways

They state that, like other Maya, the Chuj were a settled farming people who cultivated maize and beans. The late 17th-century account by Alonso de León mentions the sacrifice of turkeys in a religious setting.

## Oral tradition & literature

The ethnonym Chuj is explained through a folk tradition according to which Tzeltal conscripts of the Spanish used the term for the loose wool overgarment worn by Chuj men. The Chuj themselves use autonyms based on town of origin: *ajSan Matéyo* from San Mateo Ixtatán, *ajSan Sabastyán* from San Sebastián Coatán, and *ajNenton* from Nentón.

The Chuj language remains viable, although children, particularly in urban centres, increasingly do not learn it as a first language or do not learn it at all. Language conservation and revitalization efforts are taking place in San Mateo Ixtatán through groups such as the Academia de Lenguas Mayas de Guatemala. The Chuj Talking Dictionary was produced in 2014 by K’ulb’il Yol Twitz Paxil, also known as The Academy of Mayan Languages, and the Living Tongues Institute for Endangered Languages.

## Language & religion

Chuj is a Mayan language in the Qʼanjobʼalan branch, alongside Tojolabʼal, Qʼanjobʼal, Akateko, Poptiʼ, and Mochoʼ. Together with the Chʼolan branch, these form the Western branch of the Mayan family. The Chujean branch emerged approximately 2,000 years ago. The two main dialects are the San Mateo Ixtatán dialect and the San Sebastián Coatán dialect. Chuj speakers also use Spanish and commonly borrow Spanish words or code-mix; the language is estimated to be 70% purely Chuj.

Traditional beliefs remain strong and are connected with spirits inhabiting natural features, caves, family altars, hilltops, and ritual sites. Catholicism is prevalent. In San Mateo it is syncretic with traditional beliefs, while in San Sebastián there is a sharp division between those holding traditional beliefs and those following the activist Catholicism of the Catholic Action group.
