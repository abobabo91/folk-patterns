## Overview

The Xavante are an Indigenous people of eastern Brazil numbering about 30,000, speaking a Jê language called Xavante with about 9,600 speakers. Their society is organized into two clans, the Âwawẽ and the Po'reza'õno, which structure marriage rules and compete in ceremonial contests. They are known for complex male initiation rites, intricate plaited and feathered objects, and a language that encodes social relationships through honorific and endearment forms. The Xavante have a documented history of resisting outside intrusion, from eighteenth-century enslavement through twentieth-century government programs.

## Material culture

Xavante material culture centers on plaited palm fibre, native cotton, feathers, bark, and bamboo, with no woven cloth or metalwork recorded in the known collections.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Xavante work primarily with plaited vegetable fibre and hand-spun native cotton rather than weaving.

- **Plaited vegetable-fibre basketry** — Open carrying baskets with twisted or plaited handles, and lidded storage baskets whose cotton-thread handle passes through a separate plaited lid.
- **Shallow plaited bowls** — Flat bowls combining plaited fibre with string.
- **Palm-fibre matting** — Sleeping mats woven of palm fibre and finished with a fringe along the bottom.
- **Native cotton thread** — Hand-spun cotton used throughout for structure and decoration, often dyed with urucu.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday and ceremonial dress focuses on bark, palm fibre, and featherwork rather than woven cloth.

- **Penis sheath** — Made from the inner bark of the palmito palm.
- **Ankle and wrist bands** — Paired white bands cut from the inner bark of a tree.
- **Headband** — A strip of bark decorated with urucu, used as religious or ritual equipment.
- **Dance cape** — Made of palm fibre hung by a suspension loop, with a stout plaited bundle wrapped with stripes and a deep fringe.
- **Dance mask** — A bundle of long grass strands bound at the top with white native cotton and decorated with urucu.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Xavante everyday and weapon forms are made of wood and bamboo; no metalwork is recorded.

- **War club** — A cylindrical, tapering club curved in side-section with a burned-on speckled design.
- **Child's arrow** — Bamboo, sharpened to a point and fletched with two black flight feathers.
- **Composite combs** — Teeth set between two bamboo slivers bound with native cotton, with thirty-three or fifteen teeth in recorded examples.
- **Fibre carrying bag** — A woven bag with a painted handle.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment is built from cotton string, feathers, wax, seed beads, and animal hoof and nail.

- **Necklets** — Several forms survive: white cotton strings with tassels and feathers fixed with wax; urucu-coloured cotton with beads and deer's hooves with feather inserts; and cotton with seed beads, animal toe-nails, and feathers.
- **Feather-holders** — Sets of four bamboo holders bound with white cotton, holding small bunches of red, yellow, or blue feathers.
- **Ear adornments** — Small wooden sticks inserted into the earlobes at age fourteen and enlarged over the course of a man's life.

## Music & performance

The documented musical tradition centers on sound-producing objects used in ceremony and performance.

- **Men's ceremonial whistles** — Composite instruments made from seed pods with one end cut off, the other waxed and holding beads, with pale pink feathers inserted.
- **Dance rattle** — Deer's hooves attached to a cotton band painted with urucu.

## Dance & theatre

Dance traditions are evidenced through material objects; competitive performance centres on the log race.

- **Dance cape** — Palm fibre hung by a suspension loop with a plaited bundle wrapped with stripes and a deep fringe.
- **Dance mask** — Bound grass strands decorated with urucu.
- **Log race** — A competitive performance where the two clans race each other carrying palm tree trunks weighing up to 80 kg to a defined point.

## Festivals & rituals

Ritual life centers on the dual clan organization and complex male initiation rites.

- **The two clans** (*Âwawẽ and Po'reza'õno*) — The two clans that compose Xavante society; marriage between members of the same clan is forbidden.
- **Log race** — The clans compete to carry palm trunks up to 80 kg to a fixed point.
- **Male initiation rites** — At fourteen, small wooden sticks are inserted into a boy's earlobes and enlarged for the rest of his life.
- **Formal betrothal relationship** (*dasiwasini*) — An arranged couple must use respectful grammatical forms to one another.
- **The a'ama** (*a'ama*) — A group of men trained in new forms of pre-existing words, addressed by clan members with specific morphological forms.

## Foodways

The sources record no Xavante staple crops, cooking methods, or dishes.

## Oral tradition & literature

Xavante speech and language documentation are preserved in several archives.

- **Xavante Language Collection** — Maintained by Laura Graham at the Archive of the Indigenous Languages of Latin America, containing recorded narrative.
- **PRODOCULT collection** — Run by J. R. Welch and R. Costa, maintains images, text, and cartography at the Museum of the Indian.
- **Xavante-Portuguese dictionary** (*Pequeno Dicionário*) — Compiled by Joan Hall, Ruth McLeod, and Valerie Mitchell in 1987 and revised in 2004.

## Language & religion

Xavante is a Jê language with nine vowel qualities and only ten consonants, featuring honorific and endearment forms.

- **Xavante language** (*Akuen, Akwen, A'uwe, A'we*) — A Central Jê language with remarkable phonology: nine vowel qualities, only ten consonants, and a historical loss of velars.
- **Ergative word order** — Xavante follows an object–agent–verb pattern.
- **Non-Xavante people** (*waradzu*) — The Xavante term for non-indigenous outsiders, used with distrust rooted in eighteenth-century enslavement and twentieth-century massacres.

## Glossary

- *Âwawẽ* — One of the two Xavante clans
- *Po'reza'õno* — The other of the two Xavante clans
- *dasiwasini* — Formal betrothal relationship between an engaged couple
- *a'ama* — Men trained in new forms of pre-existing words, addressed with specific morphological forms
- *urucu* — A red dye made from a plant, used to colour cotton, bark, and grass
- *palmito* — A palm tree whose inner bark is used for penis sheaths
- *waradzu* — Non-Xavante or non-indigenous people
- *Jê language* — A language family of central Brazil to which Xavante belongs
- *Araguaia River* — The river east of which the Xavante originally lived in Goiás province
- *Mato Grosso* — The Brazilian state where the Xavante relocated between 1830 and 1860
- *PRODOCULT* — Programa de Documentação de Culturas Indígenas, an archive maintaining Xavante documentation
- *log race* — Competitive performance in which clans race carrying heavy palm tree trunks
