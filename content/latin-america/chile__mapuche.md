## Overview

The Mapuche are the largest Indigenous group in Chile, living mainly in the Araucanía region and in cities after a century of migration. They speak Mapudungun and practice a syncretic religion blending Catholicism with traditional beliefs centered on ritual and healing. They are known for their distinctive silver jewelry, woven textiles especially the manta poncho, and the role of the machi healer in community life.

## Material culture

Mapuche material culture centers on woven textiles and silver jewelry made from melted coins, along with practical items for herding, riding, and household work.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The manta blanket-poncho is the most collected Mapuche textile, worn by men and women as a mark of status and everyday dress.

- **Manta** — A woven blanket-poncho worn by men and women, often worn by male leaders as a mark of office.
- **Quipan** — A square cloth wrapped around the body as a woman's dress, usually worn with a woven belt and shawl.
- **Ikulla** — A woman's shawl pinned at the breast with a silver tupu and worn over the quipan.
- **Trariwe waist sash** (*trariwe / trarihue*) — A long woven waist sash, often made in double weave with high warp density that creates a drawn appearance.
- **Chamall** — A garment worn with the manta, documented in photographs from Traiguén.

### Clothing & dress

Men wear the manta or chamall with a trarilonko headband; women's dress is more complex, layering the quipan, ikulla, and various jewelry pieces.

- **Trarilonko** — A headband worn by men and women, also used as a silver chain worn across the forehead.
- **Lloven nitrowe** — A women's headdress.
- **Medella** — A headdress made of chained coins worn by women.
- **Riding boots** — Horse-hide riding boots from the Junín de los Andes district, worn with separate woven bands wound around them.
- **Mail coat** — A military coat made from seven folds of horse hide sewn with leather thongs and painted.

### Architecture

Mapuche built defensive forts and ceremonial structures, particularly ritual poles and community altars.

- **Ngillatuwe** (*ngillatuwe / la cruza*) — A collective altar pole carved with the face and arms of Ngünechen or an ancestor, set facing east in uncultivated ritual fields and often flanked by white and blue crosses.
- **Rewe** — A machi's personal pole made from laurel or oak trunk with steps notched into it, branches tied to the side, sometimes crowned with a rainbow-shaped arch of vines.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Mapuche pottery is plain domestic ware; woodwork and metalwork—adopted quickly from Spanish contact—are better represented and locally named.

- **Rali** — A wooden soup plate used for eating.
- **Patia** — A wooden bowl used as a washbasin.
- **Boleadoras** — A thrown cord-and-weight weapon, with three-ball versions for guanaco and lighter versions for rhea.
- **Stirrups** — Wood-and-iron stirrups used in equestrian gear.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Mapuche silver jewelry comes from a colonial trade in Spanish and Argentine coins, and almost all documented pieces are women's ornaments.

- **Trapelakucha** (*trapelakucha / trapalacucha*) — A pectoral pendant worn on the chest.
- **Chaway** — Earrings worn in pairs.
- **Tupu** — A shawl pin used to fasten the ikulla at the breast.
- **Pentreor** — A chest hanging made of three chains.

## Music & performance

Mapuche music belongs to ritual; the kultrun drum and pifilca whistles accompany the medicine dance and prayers.

- **Kultrun** (*kultrun / cultrunü*) — A shallow drum made from a laurel or oak bowl with goatskin stretched over it, painted with a cross representing the fourfold division of the world.
- **Pifilca** (*pifilca / pilfulco*) — Whistles played for the medicine dance.
- **Trutruca** (*trutruca / tultruka*) — A wind instrument used in ritual.
- **Ngillatun** — Prayers delivered by machi, usually in Mapudungun, sometimes mixing Spanish, interpreted aloud by a dungumachife when spoken in trance.

## Dance & theatre

Dance at the ngillatun is collective, performed in rows and circles around the community altar; the medicine dance is masked.

- **Awün** — A mounted dance where men ride in circles around the ngillatun dancers in imitation of the sun.
- **Longcoche** — A wooden mask worn for the medicine dance.

## Festivals & rituals

The ngillatun is the main contemporary ritual, a community ceremony performed before and after harvest, lasting more than two days.

- **Ngillatun** — The main ritual of Mapuche religion, performed by the whole community in a consecrated space with maize, beans and muday offered at the ngillatuwe, an animal sacrificed, and dancing to hold cosmic forces in balance.
- **Machiluwün** — A machi's initiation ritual that relieves the calling-illness machikutran.
- **Datun** — A healing ceremony performed by a machi.
- **Pewuntun** — A divination ritual performed by a machi.
- **Awn** — The funeral ritual, designed to send the deceased person's am onward and clear malevolent spirits from the gathering.

## Foodways

Mapuche food traditions center on ritual offerings and agricultural products adopted from Spanish contact, particularly maize, beans, wheat and sheep.

- **Muday** — A drink offered at the ngillatun.
- **Chicha** — Wine or fermented drink poured regularly for the ancestors.

## Oral tradition & literature

Mapuche beliefs were transmitted orally and vary by territory and family; the deluge epeu is the best-attested narrative, along with dreams and visions as spirit communication.

- **Deluge epeu** (*epeu*) — A narrative in which the world is destroyed and remade through conflict between Kai Kai (floodwater) and Tren Tren (dry earth), with few survivors and restoration through a couple's sacrifice of their child.
- **Pewma** — Dreams, a narrative genre through which spirits summon and instruct a machi.
- **Perimontum** — Visions experienced by Mapuche, another form of spirit communication.

## Language & religion

Mapudungun is classified as a language isolate or one of two Araucanian languages; religion is syncretic, mostly Christian with a minority practicing traditional Mapuche beliefs.

- **Mapudungun** — The Mapuche language, from mapu (land) and dungun (speech), divided into eight dialect sub-groups; co-official with Spanish in Galvarino since 2013.
- **Huilliche** — A related language spoken south of Lago Ranco and Río Bueno, not mutually intelligible with most Mapudungun dialects.

## Glossary

- *manta* — Woven blanket-poncho worn by men and women
- *quipan* — Square cloth wrapped as a woman's dress
- *ikulla* — Woman's shawl, fastened with a tupu pin
- *trariwe* — Long woven waist sash, often double-weave
- *trarilonko* — Headband or forehead chain worn by men and women
- *tupu* — Silver shawl pin
- *trapelakucha* — Silver pectoral pendant
- *chaway* — Silver earrings worn in pairs
- *medella* — Coin-chain headdress for women
- *kultrun* — Shallow ritual drum with goatskin head
- *pifilca* — Whistles played for the medicine dance
- *ngillatuwe* — Community altar pole, face of ancestor or Ngünechen
- *rewe* — Machi's personal ritual pole
- *ngillatun* — Main ritual ceremony and prayers to hold cosmic forces in balance
- *machi* — Healer and spiritual leader, usually female
- *machiluwün* — Machi's initiation ritual
- *datun* — Healing ceremony
- *pewuntun* — Divination ritual
- *awn* — Funeral ritual
- *am* — Deceased person's spirit
- *epeu* — Deluge narrative, world destruction and recreation
- *pewma* — Dreams, narrative genre of spirit communication
- *Mapudungun* — Language of the Mapuche, from mapu (land) and dungun (speech)
