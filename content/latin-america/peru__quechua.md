## Overview

The Quechua are Indigenous peoples of the central Andes who speak the Quechuan language family. Most live in Peru, with identity oriented locally by dialect rather than nationally. In folk-culture terms they are known above all for weaving, a tradition from Inca times that still carries patterned cloth into daily and ceremonial life. They have been nominally Catholic since colonial times while retaining older beliefs in a fully integrated syncretism.

## Material culture

Quechua material culture centers on handwoven textiles in wool and cotton using natural dyes and numerous woven patterns, alongside wooden kero drinking beakers, beaded adornment, and woven herding gear.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Weaving using cotton and wool from camelids and natural dyes is the foundation of material culture, with woven patterns called pallay worked into belts, bags, mantas and ponchos.

- **Poncho** — A handwoven garment, nearly every Quechua man and boy has one, generally red and decorated with intricate designs distinctive to each district.
- **Manta** — A carrying-cloth and mantle worn at weddings and daily, woven in geometric and zoomorphic designs in wool or alpaca wool.
- **Woven belt** (*chumpi*) — A woven belt worn to protect the lower back when working in fields, carrying geometric and zoomorphic designs.
- **Coca pouch** (*chuspa*) — Small woven pouches men have worn since ancient times to carry coca leaves, made in wool or wool and cotton with geometric designs.
- **Woven patterns** (*pallay*) — The general term for woven patterns worked into belts, bags, mantas and ponchos in complementary weave, including geometric and zoomorphic designs.

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress mixes pre-Spanish styles with Spanish colonial peasant dress, while men wear handwoven wool trousers, chumpis, and ponchos for formal occasions.

- **Knitted earflapped hat** (*ch'ullu*) — A knitted earflapped hat frequently worn, often with floral designs; a child's first ch'ullu is traditionally knitted by their father.
- **Felt hat** (*sombrero*) — A felted wool hat with silk ribbon, worn by women since the 1920s when British railway workers brought the fedora-style to the country.
- **Wool trousers** (*bayeta*) — Dark knee-length handwoven wool trousers worn by older men with a chumpi belt.
- **Headband** (*wincha*) — A beaded glass headband made for weddings and hair-dressing.
- **Sandals** (*ajotas*) — Sandals cut from recycled tires, standard footwear for both sexes.

### Architecture

Houses are built of air-dried clay bricks or wattle-and-daub with thatched roofs, dispersed across altitude ranges for pastoral and arable farming, with land owned by the local community, the ayllu.

- **Clay bricks** (*tika*) — Air-dried clay bricks, also called adobe in Spanish, used as the standard material for house construction.
- **Community labor work party** (*mink'a*) — Collective labor in which people work together on projects of common interest such as communal facilities.
- **Reciprocal assistance** (*ayni*) — Reciprocal labor in which members of an ayllu help one family with large private projects such as house construction, to be repaid in kind later.
- **Community landholding** (*ayllu*) — The local community that owns land, either worked jointly or redistributed annually.
- **Roof thatch grass** (*ichu*) — The puna grass used to cover roofs, along with straw and reeds.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The kero, an Andean drinking beaker in wood inlaid with resin and sometimes tin or metal, is the strongest named form, supplemented by bronze knives and woven herding gear.

- **Andean drinking beaker** (*kero*) — A wood drinking beaker inlaid with pigmented resin, sometimes with tin studs or sheet tin, dating from the 16th to 18th century.
- **Knotted cord record** (*quipu*) — The knotted cord record held by Inca officials and used for record-keeping.
- **Carrying strap** (*sling*) — A wool or alpaca wool strap in cream, ochre and brown geometric design used as a whip for hunting, herding and carrying.
- **Horse hobble** (*hobble*) — A braided woollen horse hobble in brown and white stripes with red and white tassels.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment is mainly beadwork and silver, with glass beads decorating headbands, hair-bands and hats, and silver pins collected in the Cuzco region.

- **Beaded headband** (*wincha*) — A wool headband decorated with glass beads, made for weddings.
- **Beaded hairband** (*tulma*) — A braided hairband decorated with glass beads.
- **Hat tassel** (*t'ika*) — Large tassels adorning ch'ullu hats in the Ausangate region, often with white beads.
- **Hat band** (*centillo*) — A finely decorated band worn on felt sombreros set over the ch'ullu.
- **Silver shawl pin** (*sjalnål*) — A silver pin collected in the Cuzco region and attributed to the area near Lake Titicaca.

## Music & performance

Music and dance are attached to the Catholic festival calendar, performed at the church door and at the cross, documented mainly through early twentieth-century photographs showing panpipes.

- **Panpipes** — Wind instruments played at the Feast of the Cross and at Christian Indigenous dances.
- **Film in Quechua language** (*Yawar Mallku*) — A Quechua-language feature film made by Bolivian director Jorge Sanjinés in 1969.
- **Contemporary musician** (*Luzmila Carpio*) — A Bolivian Quechua musician performing in the language.
- **Contemporary musician** (*Renata Flores Rivera*) — A Peruvian Quechua musician of the contemporary generation performing in the language.
- **Contemporary musician** (*Magaly Solier*) — A Peruvian Quechua musician of the contemporary generation performing in the language.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is placed in the Catholic festival cycle rather than secular entertainment, documented in two photographs showing performance at the Feast of the Cross and at churches.

- **Feast of the Cross dancing** — A festival where Quichua danced in 1905 at Mojos in Caupolicán with panpipes before an altar.

## Festivals & rituals

The festival calendar is dominated by Catholic celebrations blended with veneration of Pachamama, apu and wak'a, marking life-cycle events through ritual objects and ceremonial dress.

- **Mother Earth** (*Pachamama*) — The earth deity to whom burnt offerings and libations are made regularly for fertility.
- **Mountain spirits** (*apu*) — Mountain spirits still venerated, especially in southern Peru, to whom offerings are made.
- **Local deities** (*wak'a*) — Lesser local deities still venerated in traditional religious practice.
- *Feast of the Cross* — A Catholic festival where Quichua gathered at Mojos to dance and make offerings.
- **First hat ceremony** (*ch'ullu*) — A childhood marker where a child's first ch'ullu is traditionally knitted by their father.

## Foodways

Potatoes, quinoa, dried meat, pit-cooked pachamanca, coca leaves and chicha beer structure the diet, supplemented by guinea pigs, beans, peppers and barley.

- **Domesticated tuber** (*potato*) — Domesticated in the Andes by the Quechua, who have cultivated thousands of varieties used for food and medicine.
- **Dried meat** (*ch'arki*) — Dried and sometimes salted meat, traditionally llama sun- and freeze-dried, now often horse or beef, the origin of the English word jerky.
- **Pit-cooking technique** (*pachamanca*) — A Quechua pit-cooking technique combining meats, tubers including uqa and mashwa, maize, fava beans, seasonings and sometimes cheese.
- **Maize beer** (*chicha*) — Maize beer made as both drink and ritual substance.
- **Stimulant leaf** (*coca*) — Leaves chewed as a mild stimulant that suppresses hunger, thirst, pain and fatigue and relieves altitude sickness, carried in the chuspa.

## Oral tradition & literature

Myth and literature express Quechua responses to historical tragedy, from the Nak'aq murderer to the Inkarrí myth linking communities across southern Peru.

- **White murderer figure** (*Nak'aq*) — The white murderer, also called Pishtaco or butcher, who in myth sucks fat from the bodies of Indigenous people he kills.
- **Victory myth** (*Wiraquchapampa*) — A myth in which the Q'ero people recount the victory of the Apus over the Spaniards.
- **Regional myth** (*Inkarrí*) — A myth current in southern Peru that links Quechua groups across the region from Ayacucho to Cusco.
- **First grammar** (*Domingo de Santo Tomás*) — A missionary who reached Peru in 1538 and published the Grammatica o arte de la lengua general de los indios de los reynos del Perú in 1560.
- **Colonial chronicle** (*Guaman Poma de Ayala*) — A chronicler whose illustrated manuscript survives in Copenhagen, showing spinners, weavers, chasqui messengers and quipu-keepers.

## Language & religion

Quechuan is an Indigenous language family of 8–10 million speakers in 2004, divided into Central and Peripheral branches, recognized officially by Peru in 1975, Ecuador in 2006 and Bolivia in 2009.

- **Language name** (*Runa simi*) — The Quechuan language, called people's language in Southern Quechua.
- **Central branch** (*Waywash*) — Quechua I, the Central dialect branch.
- **Peripheral branch** (*Wamp'una*) — Quechua II, the Peripheral dialect branch that splits into Yunkay, Northern and Southern.
- **Mother Earth deity** (*Pachamama*) — The deity to whom offerings are made in a fully integrated syncretism of Catholicism with older Andean beliefs.
