## Overview

The Amanayé, also known as Amanaié, Ararandeuara, Araradeua, Manaye, Manazewa, Manajo, Manaxo, Ararandeuras, and Turiwa, are a Tupi-Guaranian Indigenous people of Brazil’s Amazon basin. They live between the cities of Belém and Brasília, in the state of Pará, near the municipality of São Domingos do Capim. Their historical movements connected the Pindaré River area of Maranhão, the Alpercatas River on the border of Maranhão and Piauí, the Caju River, the igarapé Ararandeua, and the Capim River. A group numbering up to 66 remained in 1999; the population rose to 195 in 2001, fell to 134 in 2010, and rose again to 178 by 2014. The Amanayé language belongs to the Tupi–Guarani family, although continued use of the language is unknown.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state only that the Amanayé population has undergone historical intermarriage with Mestizos and other Indians, and that contemporary culture provides little disapproval for such intermarriage.

### Architecture

Amanayé houses are isolated residences surrounded by their respective *roças*, or planting fields, and scattered through the area. The houses are made of *pau-a-pique*, a wattle-and-daub construction, with or without plaster. The arrangement of rooms varies from family to family, but domestic life is centered in the kitchen, around the wood stove. The kitchen is where the family gathers, while visitors are entertained in the living room.

Next to the house is usually the *casa de farinha*, or flour mill. This building can also serve as a meeting place for visitors and for people who are working. The spatial arrangement described in the source connects household construction with subsistence activity and social life: residences are placed among planting fields, kitchens organize domestic life, and the flour mill provides a setting for shared work and gathering. Amanayé villages are frequently relocated as the soil becomes exhausted, and possibly also to avoid enemies.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention the wood stove in the kitchen, the *casa de farinha*, planting fields, and agricultural practices, but provide no catalogue of everyday objects or their manufacture.

## Festivals & rituals

The historical account does describe encounters involving missionaries and neighboring peoples, including the settlement of Amanayé together with the Guajajara at the urging of Father David Fay, a Jesuit missionary among the Guajajara of the São Francisco do Carará village.

The Amanayé were first contacted in 1755, when they tended to avoid white missionaries but made a deal with Father David Fay. Fay persuaded them to settle in a village together with the Guajajara, whom the source identifies as their traditional enemies. Shortly afterwards, part of the group moved to the Alpercatas River, settling near the village of Santo Antônio. The sources describe these events as historical contact and relocation, not as continuing ritual practices.

## Foodways

Amanayé subsistence includes slash-and-burn agriculture and the cultivation of cassava, cotton, and tobacco. The source also states that the Amanayé frequently relocate villages as their soil becomes exhausted. Their planting fields, or *roças*, surround isolated residences, linking food production directly to the organization of settlement.

The *casa de farinha* is a flour mill located next to the house. It can also be a place where visitors and people working can gather. Cassava processing is therefore represented in the sources through this named building and workplace.

The sources state that nursing lasts for approximately one year. After the second month, a newborn child begins to be fed with *carimã*, described as fine manioc meal, and *croeira*. These are the only named Amanayé food items in the supplied material.

## Oral tradition & literature

They mention the historical account of Nimuendajú, according to which Amanayé near the igarapé Ararandeua began to identify themselves as Ararandeuara or Turiwara in order to disguise their identity.

## Language & religion

Amanayé belongs to the Tupi–Guarani family. Aryon Rodrigues classified it in 1984 together with the Anambé and Turiwara languages. The language classification supplied is Tupian, Tupi-Guaraní, Subgroup VIII, Amanayé.

Amanayé is described as a possibly extinct Tupi language last spoken in the town of São Domingos on the Capim River in Pará State, Brazil. A closely related but possibly distinct language is Ararandewara, spoken at the headwaters of the Moju River. It is unknown whether the Amanayé continue to speak Amanayé. The oldest members, and some of the youngest members, still use some words from their native language in everyday speech and integrate them with Portuguese. In general, the Amanayé speak Portuguese fluently.
