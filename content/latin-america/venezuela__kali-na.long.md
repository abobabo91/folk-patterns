## Overview

The Kali'na — also written Kalina, Caliña, Karìna, Kariña, Kalihna and Kalinya, and long known in European languages as the Caribs or mainland Caribs — are an Indigenous people of the northern coastal zone of South America, today living mostly in villages along the rivers and coasts of Venezuela, Guyana, Suriname, French Guiana and Brazil. Venezuela holds the largest share of the population, concentrated in two separate zones: the Llanos of the Orinoco river valley and the valley of the Cuyuni River, part of which lies in Guyana. Their language, *Karìna auran*, belongs to the Cariban family; the self-designation *Kalina* literally means "person." Before 1492 Kali'na settlement ran along the coast from the mouth of the Amazon to that of the Orinoco. For a folk-culture atlas they matter as a people whose history was carried orally rather than in writing, and whose material record survives largely in early twentieth-century collections of pottery, bows, basketry and instruments.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here — the Wikipedia articles on the Kalina people and the Kali'na language, and the catalogue records of the objects shown — say nothing about Kali'na weaving, loom work or textile patterning, so no textile tradition can be described from them.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe no garment types, head coverings, belts or footwear, for either everyday or ceremonial use; the only dress-related practice they record is body-painting, which forms part of the *Epekotono* mourning-conclusion celebration among the Kali'na of the Guianas.

### Architecture

House form, building materials and roof construction are not covered by these sources. What they do record is the settlement geography: in French Guiana the communities between the Maroni and the Mana rivers — Awala-Yalimapo (the only one where Kali'na are a majority), Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni, Mana and Iracoubo — plus the Amerindian village of Kourou and, in smaller numbers, the island of Cayenne. In Suriname the main settlements are Bernharddorp, Wit-Santi, Galibi, Powakka and Bigi Ston, with a large part of the population in the Para District, often in villages shared with the Lokono; Konomerume, near the Wajambo River, has about 349 residents, a majority of whom identify as Kari'nja. In Brazil, São José dos Galibi was founded in 1950 on the right bank of the Oyapock River by families from the Mana River region, while the Galibi Marworno live further inland along the Uaçá River, with Kumarumã as the main settlement. Archaeology on 310 km² of land recovered from the Sinnamary River by the Petit-Saut Dam has yielded 273 Amerindian sites, some about two thousand years old.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery is the best-documented Kali'na craft in these collections. British Museum records list flat-bottomed globular vessels tapering to a neck and flaring at the rim, some in red slip with pale geometric designs, some varnished; a double-bodied bridge vessel; a jug with a looped handle from shoulder to rim; a lidded earthenware pot with slip-painted lines and spots; basins and bowls with waved rims and glazed, decorated interiors; a rounded vessel with a looped handle across the top, a spout to one side and bird wings and tail modelled around the others; and a group of polychrome-slipped figurines in the form of a frog balanced on four feet, an armadillo, a dog in a loose collar, an unidentified quadruped and a standing human figure with raised arms. Kali'na women prized pebbles of red porphyry, *takuwa*, collected around the Essequibo River for polishing their pottery; the same term also denotes jade. Stone axes and axe-heads are in the British Museum collection, and before contact the Kali'na worked with stone axes and hardwood machetes, iron tools arriving with Europeans. Hunting gear and containers reached the Museum of World Culture in Gothenburg: a strung bow 191 cm long, barbed iron-tipped arrows with feather fletching, and a lidded basket collected by Walter Roth on the Barama River in 1927.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Two armlets collected by Walter Roth on the Barima River in north-western Guyana in 1927 are the only ornaments the sources record; materials and construction are not described. Body-painting is attested as part of *Epekotono*. Nothing in these sources covers tattooing, hairdressing or other body modification.

## Music & performance

Kali'na music is built mainly on percussion. The *sanpula* (also *sambula*) is a large drum with a skin stretched over each end of the shell, the two heads held by hoops drawn together with cord and struck with a mallet. Two kinds of rattle are used, the *kalawasi* (or *kalawashi*) and the *malaka*. The flute, *kuwama*, is still made, though increasingly displaced by the European flute, and there is also a terra-cotta horn, the *kuti*. A bone flute with three finger-holes and incised ornament, collected by Walter Roth on the Barima River in 1927, is among the objects shown here. Music, along with dancing and body-painting, is one of the elements of *Epekotono*, which these sources identify as nowadays the only occasion for gatherings of this kind among the Kali'na. Song genres, instrumental repertoires and professional or specialist performers are not described in the sources used.

## Dance & theatre

Dancing is recorded only as a component of the *Epekotono* celebration, where it accompanies body-painting, music and the symbolic burning of the deceased's belongings; the sources name no individual dances and no dramatic or masked traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

The one ceremony these sources treat in detail is *Epekotono*, the event that closes mourning among the Kali'na of the Guianas. The death of a family member opens a mourning period that can last a year or more, and *Epekotono* ends it. Preparations are organised by a respected member of the village and can take several months to assemble; collecting money for the event is a more recent addition to that role. The celebration is public and draws people from neighbouring villages. It includes body-painting, music, dancing and the symbolic burning of the dead person's belongings, marking the departure of the spirit. When it concludes, mourning is over, ordinary social behaviour resumes, and widows may remarry. Non-Kali'na may attend as guests, but the occasion works to reinforce Kali'na identity, signalled above all by the explicit use of the Kali'na language. *Kasiri*, a cassava-derived beer, is an important part of traditional Kali'na celebrations. Beyond this, the sources record no annual or seasonal festival calendar and no birth, coming-of-age or marriage rites. One modern date is documented: Sylvia Kajoeramari, a Kali'na member of Suriname's National Assembly, successfully led the effort to make the International Day of the World's Indigenous Peoples a public holiday in Suriname.

## Foodways

The sources name a single Kali'na foodstuff: *kasiri*, a beer made from cassava, central to traditional celebrations. No staple dishes, cooking methods, ceremonial foods or dietary rules are described in the material used here.

## Oral tradition & literature

Having no written form of their language before the arrival of Europeans, the Kali'na transmitted their history orally from one generation to the next through tales of myth and legend, and Kali'na remains primarily an oral language today. European observers long failed to distinguish between the various Caribbean peoples, and interest faded after the age of exploration until the late twentieth century, when the French researcher Gérard Collomb, a CNRS specialist in Kali'na culture, took up the subject and the Kali'na themselves began to set down their own history — in particular Félix Tiouka, president of the Association of Amerindians of French Guiana (AAGF), and his son Alexis. Collomb and Tiouka's *Na'na Kali'na — Une histoire des Kali'na en Guyane* (2000) came out of that work. Episodes of contact are preserved in Kali'na vocabulary: Europeans were first understood as *Palanakiłi*, spirits of the sea, a word still used for white people, and France was *Pau:wa*, "The Land of the Whites," the destination of the fifteen Kali'na from Sinnamary and Iracoubo sent in July 1882 to be exhibited at the Jardin d'Acclimatation in Paris, and of a second group in 1892. Edmundo Magaña and Fabiola Jara's study "The Carib sky" (1982) treats Carib celestial knowledge, and *Adaheli* is named as the Sun in the mythology of the Orinoco region.

## Language & religion

Kali'na — also called Kari'nja, Carib and Galibi — is a Cariban language of the Guianan Carib branch, treated as "Carib proper" for its complex pronominal prefix system and ergative-absolutive alignment. The Kalina people article gives above 10,000 speakers (Venezuela 5,000, French Guiana 3,000, Suriname 2,500, Guyana 475, Brazil 100); the language article gives around 7,400 and classifies the language as highly endangered, spoken by elders. Dialects are Venezuelan Carib (1,000), Guyanese Carib (2,000), Western Surinamese Carib (500) and Eastern Surinamese and French Guianese Carib (3,000); Suriname's two varieties are Aretyry in the west and centre and Tyrewuju, used by the majority there. The alphabet has 18 letters, but standardisation is hampered by orthographies shaped by Spanish, Portuguese, Dutch, French and English — the ethnonym alone is written in nine different systems. These sources describe no religious affiliation or ritual specialists.
