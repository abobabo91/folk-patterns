## Overview

The Maya are Indigenous peoples of Mesoamerica with strong cultural ties to the ancient Maya civilization. In Guatemala, many Maya communities live in the western highlands, while others live in lowland areas and western Belize. Maya cultural life includes multilingual communities, weaving, ritual calendars, music, dance, foodways, architecture, writing, and religious practice. Contemporary traditions continue alongside Catholic institutions and memories of the ancient past.

## Material culture

Maya material culture includes woven textiles, ceramics, carved stone, stucco, wood, jade, obsidian, painted murals, bark-paper books, bags, hammocks, and everyday tools.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Maya textiles use bright colors and village-specific patterns to show where a person comes from.

- **Woven blouse or shirt** (*huipil*) — A huipil is a brightly colored woven shirt or blouse, and each village has its own pattern.
- **Woven belt** (*faja*) — A faja holds up a skirt or trousers and may have brocade, geometric motifs, zigzag lines, or a snake motif.
- **Multipurpose cloth** (*tzute*) — A tzute is a woven cloth used as a covering, carrier, holder for sacred objects, or table cloth.
- **Wraparound skirt** (*corte*) — A corte is a skirt with varied patterns and colors, generally woven on a step loom.
- **Shoulder bag** (*morral*) — A morral is an everyday shoulder bag made from wool or maguey fibres and decorated with designs such as horses, quetzals, or suns.

### Clothing & dress

Women’s dress centers on a woven shirt, long skirt, belt, hairband, and multipurpose cloth, while men wear woven shirts and shoulder bags.

- **Women’s blouse** (*huipil*) — Women may wear a multicolored huipil made from woven cotton and decorated with brocade.
- **Women’s skirt** (*falda*) — A falda is a patterned or plain skirt in the female clothing repertoire.
- **Hairband** (*cinta*) — A cinta is a woven hairband used by women to decorate the hair and head.
- **Men’s shoulder bag** (*morral*) — Men wear and knit the square wool shoulder bag called a morral.
- **Festival hat** (*El Baile de la Conquista*) — A feathered festival hat is used during El Baile de la Conquista, a dance about the invasion led by Don Pedro de Alvarado y Contreras.

### Architecture

Ancient Maya ceremonial centers used plazas, stairs, temple pyramids, palaces, water systems, and connected settlements.

- **Causeway** (*sacbeob*) — Sacbeob were dam-like causeways connecting ceremonial centers with other habitation nuclei.
- **Temple pyramid** — Temple pyramids stood above broad plazas connected by steep stairs.
- **Ball court** — Ball courts were named building types in Maya ceremonial centers.
- **Sweat bath** — Sweat baths were part of Maya architectural complexes.
- **Twin pyramid complex** — Twin pyramid complexes were one type of Maya architectural ensemble.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Maya objects included decorated pottery, carved wood and stone, bark-paper books, tools, bags, hammocks, and bee hives.

- **Cylinder vessel** — Cylinder vessels were among the decorated pottery forms made without a potter’s wheel.
- **Incense burner** — Incense burners could have modeled faces of deities or kings attached to hollow tubes.
- **Bark-paper book** — Bark-paper books were among the documented Maya objects.
- **Grinding stone** (*metate*) — A metate was used to grind nixtamalized maize.
- **Slit-drum** (*tun*) — The tun was a wooden slit-drum used in Maya music.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The profile gives little information about Maya jewelry or body adornment beyond woven hairbands and head coverings.

- **Woven hairband** (*cinta*) — Women used cintas to decorate the hair and head.
- **Head covering cloth** (*tzute*) — A tzute could cover the head, shoulders, and arms.

## Music & performance

Maya music used wind and percussion instruments and accompanied rituals, funerals, celebrations, theatre, dance, warfare, and religious events.

- **Upright drum** (*kayum*) — The kayum was an upright single-headed cylindrical or kettle-shaped drum.
- **Slit-drum** (*tunkul*) — The tunkul was a wooden slit-drum used in Maya music and Christian religious festivities.
- **Musical director** (*holpop*) — The holpop was a respected musical director, principal singer, teacher, and keeper of instruments.
- **Flute** — Maya flutes included tube flutes, flutes with a goitre chamber, and ocarinas.
- **Ritual beverage** (*balché*) — Balché was a slightly fermented honey drink served by the hmen during rain, maize, and bee ceremonies.

## Dance & theatre

Maya dance joins music, ritual, theatre, warfare, and religious festivals.

- **Dance drama** (*Rabinal Achí*) — Rabinal Achí combines dance and text and tells dynastic stories about Rabinal and Qʼumarkaj.
- **Dance of the Conquest** (*El Baile de la Conquista*) — El Baile de la Conquista represents the invasion led by Don Pedro de Alvarado y Contreras and his confrontation with Tecún Umán.
- **Warrior Dance** (*Kʼicheʼ Warrior Dance*) — The Kʼicheʼ Warrior Dance dates to the Postclassic era and was accompanied by flutes and drums.

## Festivals & rituals

Maya ritual life centers on sacred places, offerings, pilgrimage, calendars, agriculture, healing, hunting, warfare, kingship, and ancestors.

- **Ritual calendar** (*tzolkʼin*) — The 260-day tzolkʼin ritual calendar is still used in the highlands of Guatemala and Chiapas.
- **Marginal period** (*Uayeb*) — Uayeb was a five-day marginal period in the Maya calendar.
- **Twenty-year period** (*katun*) — A katun was one of thirteen twenty-year periods in the Maya calendar.
- **Child-carrying rite** (*Hetz mek*) — Hetz mek marks a child’s movement from cradling or carrying to the mother’s hip.
- **Rain and maize ceremony** (*hmen*) — The hmen served balché during rain, maize, and bee ceremonies.

## Foodways

Maya foodways center on maize, beans, squash, chili peppers, hunting, farming, forest resources, and aquatic foods.

- **Maize porridge** (*atole*) — Atole is a dense drinking gruel made from maize.
- **Maize stew** (*pozole*) — Pozole contains large grains of maize in turkey broth.
- **Maize griddle** (*comal*) — A comal was used to cook tortillas.
- **Maize grinding stone** (*metate*) — Maize was ground on a metate after being soaked and cooked in an alkaline solution.
- **Honey drink** (*balché*) — Balché is a slightly fermented beverage made with honey and served during ceremonies.

## Oral tradition & literature

Maya literature preserves stories about creation, heroes, rulers, maize, the Sun and Moon, and religious knowledge.

- **Sacred book of the Maya** (*Popol Vuh*) — The Popol Vuh is the most significant work of Guatemalan literature in the Kʼicheʼ language and preserves Maya stories and traditions.
- **Hero Twins** (*Hunahpu and Xbalanque*) — Hunahpu and Xbalanque are the Hero Twins in Maya mythology.
- **Dance drama text** (*Rabinal Achí*) — Rabinal Achí preserves mythical and dynastic narratives about Rabinal and Qʼumarkaj.
- **Book of Chumayel** (*Book of Chilam Balam of Chumayel*) — The Book of Chilam Balam of Chumayel is a colonial text connected with Maya narrative and religious knowledge.
- **Book of ritual knowledge** (*Ritual of the Bacabs*) — The Ritual of the Bacabs is a colonial text connected with Maya religious knowledge.

## Language & religion

Maya languages form a large family, and Maya religion combines Indigenous practices with Catholic institutions and ceremonies.

- **Priest** (*jmen*) — The jmen is the Yucatec village priest.
- **Priest** (*ah kʼin*) — Ah kʼin is a general word for priest.
- **Maya script** — Maya script used about 1,000 distinct characters and combined syllabic signs with logograms.
- **Syncretic religious figure** (*Maximón*) — Maximón, also known locally as San Simon of Guatemala, reflects Maya religious practice in Guatemala.
- **Language branch** (*Kʼichean*) — Kʼichean is one of the major branches of the Mayan language family.
