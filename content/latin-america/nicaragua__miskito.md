## Overview

The Miskito are a Native people of Central America whose territory extends from Cape Camarón in Honduras to Río Grande de Matagalpa in Nicaragua, along the Mosquito Coast in the Western Caribbean zone. Their population was estimated in 2025 at 543,775, including 461,000 people living in Nicaragua. Around 150,000 Miskito people live in Nicaragua, distributed among more than 300 communities in 23 territories throughout the Caribbean Lowlands and the Mosquito Coast. The Coco River, also known as the Wangks River, forms a central point of Miskito territory and serves as a border between Nicaragua and Honduras. Miskito belongs to the Misumalpan language family. Their folk culture includes *kisi* folktales, round dances, traditional songs, theatrical pieces, wooden funeral masks, shamanic practices, and regional language traditions.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The Miskito commonly adopted European dress and English names during the independence era.

## Music & performance

The Miskito have musical traditions that include round dances and traditional songs. Their theatrical pieces include representations of the Miskito king.

Miskito shamanic practice included ritual dancing and drinking a beverage known as *mishla*. Before contact, the *Sukya* was regarded by the community as a healer and discovered cures by dreaming about them and blowing smoke on the affected area. In the 1980s, shamans and group ceremonies took place in private.

The Moravian Church established missions and later developed schools and religious materials and services in the Miskito native language. The sources describe its institutional presence.

## Dance & theatre

The sources identify round dances and theatrical pieces connected with the Miskito king.

Ritual dancing formed part of the traditions associated with Miskito shamanism. The sources also state that group traditions included drinking *mishla*. No further details are supplied about the occasions, participants, movements, or musical accompaniment.

## Festivals & rituals

Funeral practice included a commemorative ceremony held one year after death called *Sikro*. Funeral ceremonies also involved wooden masks.

Before contact, Miskito people practiced a form of shamanism in which the healer, known as *Sukya*, was understood to obtain cures through dreams and to blow smoke on an affected area. Only one leading shaman, known as the Supreme Sukya or *Okuli*, could exist at a time. The *Okuli* was revered by neighboring tribes and represented the evil spirits called *Lasas*. Miskito people were polytheist in the pre-monarchic era, although the sources state that there are no records of human sacrifices like those of the Maya or Incas.

## Foodways

During the conflict in 1927–1933 between Augusto César Sandino and the United States over the United States occupation of Nicaragua, both sides attempted to enlist the Miskito to provide food and transport. This reference concerns wartime activity rather than a documented food tradition.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Miskito share folktales called *kisi*, which serve as entertainment. These stories often include a trickster rabbit named *Tibang* or *Bangbang*, as well as kings. Their themes concern authority, human nature, and general social experience. Some stories include myths of *Duhindu*, creatures similar to gnomes that sometimes kidnap children.

The sources state that Miskito people share poems and stories, but do not have widely disseminated pieces of literature and that nothing had been published in the account provided. Media in the area is largely in Spanish, with some programming in English and regional languages.

The Miskito language collection of Natalia Bermúdez and Wanda Luz Waldan Peter preserves audio and video recordings and text transcriptions of historical narratives from native speakers through the AILLA archive. The sources also identify a recording of a song in Miskito with an interview in English from the Collection of Miskito, Quechua and Tseltal of June Nash at AILLA.

## Language & religion

Miskito, called *Miskitu* in the Miskito language, is a Misumalpan language spoken by the Miskito people in northeastern Nicaragua, especially in the North Caribbean Coast Autonomous Region, and in eastern Honduras. It has around 150,000 speakers and is the most widely spoken language of the Misumalpan family in Nicaragua and Honduras. The family name is formed from parts of the names Miskito, Sumo, and Matagalpan. Miskito has been influenced by English, German, and Dutch, and also contains influences from Spanish, Sumi dialects, Arawak, Rama, Carib, and certain Western African languages.

The Miskito alphabet consists of 19 letters. The language’s orthography remains unsettled despite printed grammars, Bible translations, and other texts. Word stress is generally placed on the first syllable. Twenty bilingual schools existed in the 1990s after groups promoted bilingual education to preserve the language.

Many Miskitos are Christians. The Moravian Church began proselytizing among them in 1849 and later established schools and religious materials and services in Miskito. Catholic converts existed in the Miskito region as early as the 1930s. Earlier Miskito religion included polytheism and shamanism, with named gods associated with the sun, moon, stars, thunder, lightning, trees and plants, oceans, lakes and rivers, living creatures, wisdom, fertility, healing, dead souls, and death.
