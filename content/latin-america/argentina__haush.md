## Overview

The Haush, also called Manekʼenk, were an Indigenous people of the Mitre Peninsula at the eastern end of the Isla Grande de Tierra del Fuego. They were culturally and linguistically related to the Selkʼnam, also known as Ona, and to the Tehuelche of southern mainland Patagonia. The Haush were hunter-gatherers whose subsistence differed from that of the Selkʼnam in relying heavily on marine sources, including seals and sea lions. Their language belonged to the Chonan language family and was related to Selkʼnam, Gününa Yajich, Teushen, and Tehuelche. The language is extinct: the last speaker died around 1920. Their population declined sharply after European contact.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The Haush used animal skins for the few items of clothing they wore. These included capes and foot coverings made from guanaco skins, as among the Selkʼnam, and also from sealskins. Women wore small “figleafs.”

### Architecture

A European report from 1619 recorded fifty huts in a Haush camp, described as the largest gathering of Haush ever reported.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Their material culture included small bows and stone-tipped arrows, which they shared with their neighbors the Selkʼnam.

## Festivals & rituals

The Haush shared an initiation ritual for male youth with the Selkʼnam. Groups from several family territories gathered for rites, exchanged gifts, and exploited stranded whales.

## Foodways

The Haush were hunter-gatherers who obtained a large part of their food from marine sources. Evidence from burial-site bones indicates that pre-European-contact Haush obtained most of the meat they ate from marine sources, including seals and sea lions. Guanacos were relatively scarce in Haush territory, and the Haush probably traded with the Selkʼnam for guanaco skins. Groups from several territories also gathered to exploit stranded whales.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Selkʼnam had a tradition that a land bridge once connected the Isla Grande de Tierra del Fuego to the mainland and later collapsed; this tradition was rejected as geologically implausible by Lothrop. Salesian missionaries produced texts documenting Haush culture and language, including a vocabulary prepared by Father José María Beauvoir and a dictionary compiled by Lucas Bridges.

## Language & religion

Haush, also called Manekʼenk, was an Indigenous language formerly spoken on Tierra del Fuego. It belonged to the Chonan language family and was considered related to the Selkʼnam, Gününa Yajich, Teushen, and Tehuelche languages. The Haush and Selkʼnam languages were similar but mutually intelligible “only with difficulty.” Martin Gusinde reported that *Manekʼenk* in the Haush language meant “people in general,” while several authors stated that it was the people’s name for themselves.
