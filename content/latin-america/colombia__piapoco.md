## Overview

The Piapoco, also called Wenaiwika, Tsáse nái, Enaguas, or Piapoco, are an Indigenous people of Colombia and Venezuela. In Colombia they inhabit locations between the Meta River and the Guaviare River, in the departments of Guainía, Meta, and Vichada, and they also live along the Orinoco River in the Venezuelan state of Amazonas. The sources describe more than 18,000 Wenaiwika individuals, while about 3,000 Piapoco speakers remain. Piapoco is an Arawakan language and a Northern Arawak language related to Achagua. Piapoco culture combines agriculture, fishing, hunting, pottery, hammock-making, basketry, ritual practice, clan organization, and oral accounts of migration and world creation.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Cumare-fiber hammocks** — Both men and women produce hammocks from the fibers of the cumare palm. Some are made for personal use, while finer and intricately crafted hammocks are sold.

**Mapíri** — The *mapíri* is a basket used for storing “fariña,” the toasted flour made from bitter cassava.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**Púali or budare** — The *púali*, also called a “budare,” is a large griddle used for roasting cassava starch. Piapoco women make these objects from clay. On the day a budare is molded, potters must avoid bathing or drinking water so that it does not deform or crack.

Women also make clay pots and bowls for sale.

## Music & performance

They state that Piapoco shamans can be older men or women who undergo rigorous study and several trials, including periods of fasting.

## Festivals & rituals

The most important ritual named in the sources is female initiation, also known as the “fish prayer.” They state that both older men and women can be recognized as shamans after rigorous study and several trials, including periods of fasting.

Piapoco social life is organized through exogamous patrilineal clans and residential units. Marriage alliances have been established with the Sikuani, Curripaco, Sáliba, and Achagua.

Traditional accounts describe the creation and ordering of the world. Furna Minali, or Kuwai, made the world habitable by defeating Kemeine, a cannibalistic anaconda, and banishing it into space, where it became the Milky Way. Kâali duápeni brought humans out of the yuluatá stone and distributed the territory among them. Kuwai organized human society and gave each people their language.

## Foodways

Piapoco subsistence combines agriculture with fishing and hunting. Cultivated foods include bitter cassava, corn, beans, yams, cocoyams, sweet potatoes, peach palm, pineapple, chili, cashew, plantain, rice, and sugarcane. Chickens, pigs, and cattle are also raised.

Bitter cassava processing is a prominent task for women. They extract starch to make *casabe*, a flatbread, and *fariña*, a toasted flour. The squeezed cassava juice is used to cook *mingao*, described in the sources as a traditional beverage. The *mapíri* basket is used to store fariña, and the púali or budare is used for roasting cassava starch.

Bows and arrows are used for both hunting and fishing, which become particularly important during the summer.

## Oral tradition & literature

Piapoco traditional accounts locate the people’s origin in the Negro River basin. According to these accounts, they migrated through the Vaupés River, crossed the Guaviare River, and reached their current territory. Their place of origin was the yuluatá stone in the Hípana rapids of the Aiarí River, a tributary of the Isana River. From there they traveled by river and land routes through the Vaupés to the upper Guaviare, the Llanos, and the upper Orinoco.

The sources also preserve accounts of Furna Minali or Kuwai, Kemeine, and Kâali duápeni. The Wenaiwika belonged to a sib of the Hohódene phratry of the Curripaco, who occupied the Isana River basin.

## Language & religion

Piapoco is an Arawakan language of Colombia and Venezuela and is classified as a Northern Arawak language. Achagua is included in the same broader Arawak language family. Piapoco speakers live in the Meta, Vichada, and Guaviare river areas of Colombia, and also in Venezuela. Piapoco is endangered, and most speakers also speak Spanish. The word Piapoco is described as a Spanish nickname referring to the toucan.

The sources describe a language with eighteen segmental phonemes: fourteen consonants and four vowels. They also identify grammatical forms including the plural suffix *-nai* for animates, masculine *-iri*, feminine *-tua*, *-mi* meaning “late, defunct,” nominalizing *-si*, and declarative mood marker *-ka*. A Piapoco-Spanish dictionary containing 2,500 words was written by Deloris Klumpp.

Piapoco cosmology includes Kuwai, Furna Minali, Kemeine, and Kâali duápeni.
