## Overview

The Tehuelche are an Indigenous people of Patagonia who historically moved seasonally between lowlands and plateaus, hunting guanaco and rhea. They wore distinctive painted skin cloaks and used stone-and-cord hunting weapons. Colonisation, disease, and the introduction of sheep estates in the nineteenth century dispossessed them; most now live in Patagonian towns and cities. Their language declined from 5,000 speakers in 1900 to none after 2019, though revival efforts are underway.

## Material culture

Tehuelche material culture centres on the guanaco-skin cloak, stone hunting weapons, and woven textiles obtained through regional trade fairs.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Woven garments and painted skin cloaks were central to Tehuelche dress and were exchanged at major regional trade fairs.

- **Quillango** — A painted cloak made of sewn guanaco skins, worn wrapped around the body with a knife at the waist, and the defining garment of Tehuelche identity in photographs.
- **Ponchos from trade fairs** — Woven garments exchanged at eighteenth-century fairs like Cayrú and Chapaleofú, where goods moved between peoples of the Pampas, Patagonia, and the Andes.
- **Woven blankets** — Blankets worn by Tehuelche women, sometimes with a headband, as an alternative to or in place of skin cloaks.

### Clothing & dress

Tehuelche dress was built around the guanaco-skin cloak, worn with headbands, necklaces, and sometimes leggings or ankle bands.

- **Guanaco-skin cloak** (*quillango*) — The primary garment worn by men and women, painted and decorated, with a knife tucked into the waist.
- **Headbands** — Worn by men, women, and children, documented in numerous photographs.
- **Necklaces** — Worn as adornment, with some individuals wearing two at once.
- **Furs used as foot coverings** — Animal furs wrapped around the feet, whose enlarged impressions gave the Spanish the name Patagones, meaning 'the big-footed'.
- **Earrings** — Worn by young men, documented in studio portraits.

### Architecture

The Tehuelche were mobile but followed defined seasonal circuits, camping in sites called aik or aiken, with group sizes of fifty to a hundred people.

- **Aik or aiken** (*aik; aiken*) — The name for Tehuelche camps, later called tolderías by Spanish and Creole observers, a term the Mapuche later adopted.
- **Tolderías** — The Spanish and Creole term for the seasonal camps and way of living of Tehuelche groups.
- **Inherited landscape markers** — Fixed boundaries of hunting and gathering territories marked by hills, troughs, hollows, and important trees.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Tehuelche material objects reflect a long lithic tradition of stone tools and weapons for hunting, with no recorded ceramics or metalwork.

- **Bolas** (*boleador*) — A stone-and-cord hunting weapon held by chiefs and young men in studio portraits and shown in early photographs of Patagonia.
- **Stone knives** — Worked from flint-like stone into elongated, rounded-triangular forms with fine edge retouch on one face, continuing a tradition from the Toldense and Casapedrense industries.
- **Toldense industry tools** (*Toldense*) — A lithic tradition from six thousand years ago with projectile points, scrapers, bifacial knives, and bone tools.
- **Casapedrense industry tools** (*Casapedrense*) — A later lithic tradition from roughly 7000–4000 BC with a higher proportion of blade tools, specialised for guanaco hunting.
- **Bows and arrows** — Documented in a photograph of a boy holding a bow and arrows with a pot or basket nearby.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment is documented only through photographs and includes headbands, necklaces, earrings, and ankle bands.

- **Headbands** — Worn by men, women, young men, and children across documented photographs.
- **Necklaces** — Worn by women and children, sometimes two at once.
- **Earrings** — Worn by young men, visible in studio portraits.
- **Ankle bands** — Uncertainly identified adornment visible on a chief in photographs.

## Festivals & rituals

The Tehuelche had no organised religious system but held body of belief in myths and rites renewed by shamans, with shamans also serving as healers.

- **Shamans** — Religious specialists who narrated and renewed myths and rites, and practised medicine with the help of spirits invoked within themselves.
- **Earth spirits** — Many spirits associated with the earth, existing alongside a supreme creator being.
- **Gualichu** — An evil spirit in Tehuelche mythology.
- **Guarichos** — Evil spirits that were part of the Tehuelche world view.
- **Mount Fitz Roy** — A sacred place held by groups who moved to the central Patagonian plateaus or into the Andes in summer.

## Foodways

Tehuelche subsistence was based on seasonal hunting of guanaco and rhea, supplemented by deer, mara, and gathered plants, with fish and shellfish subject to group-specific prohibitions.

- **Guanaco** — The primary prey animal followed seasonally between winter lowlands and summer plateaus.
- **Rhea** (*ñandú; choique*) — A large flightless bird hunted as a staple food.
- **South Andean deer** — One of the secondary game animals hunted by the Tehuelche.
- **Patagonian mara** — A small rodent hunted as food.
- **Seasonal movement patterns** — Winters spent in lowlands, meadows, wetlands, shores, and lake margins; summers on central plateaus or in the Andes.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tehuelche narrative tradition is centred on cosmological myths carried and updated by shamans, with creation stories involving gods who brought order and taught human skills.

- **Kóoch** — A deity in one version of the creation myth who brings order out of chaos and creates the world's distinct elements.
- **Temauckel** — A god through whom Erral created human beings and taught them the use of the bow and arrow.
- **Erral** — A creator figure who made human beings and taught them hunting skills.
- **Kénos** — A world-maker figure in Selkʼnam mythology, a parallel to Kóoch through a common linguistic root.
- **Kkomshkn e wine awkkoi ʼaʼien** — A language and culture revival programme meaning 'I am not ashamed of speaking Tehuelche'.

## Language & religion

Tehuelche is a Chonan language with unusual grammar; it declined from 5,000 speakers around 1900 to none after 2019, though community revival efforts are underway.

- **Aonekkenk** (*Aonekkenk; Aonekko ʼaʼien*) — The name Tehuelche people call themselves and their language.
- **Chonan language family** (*Chonan*) — The language group to which Tehuelche belongs, alongside Teushen, Selkʼnam, and Haush.
- **Nominative-accusative grammar** — Tehuelche marks the nominative but not the accusative, a pattern found in only six languages worldwide.
- **Tehuesh or Téushenkenk** (*Tehuesh; Téushenkenk*) — A transitional central Patagonian language gradually displaced by Aonekkenk, surviving in plateau place names.
- **Dora Manchado** — The last fluent speaker of Tehuelche, who died in 2019 and was the primary source for language documentation.
