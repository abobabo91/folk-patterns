## Overview

The Botocudo are several South American peoples of eastern Brazil, also called Aimoré, Aymore, or Aimboré. Their original territory was in Espírito Santo and extended inland to the headwaters of the Rio Grande (Belmonte) and Doce River on the eastern slopes of the Espinhaço Mountains. European colonists expelled them westward beyond the Serra dos Aimorés into Minas Gerais. The Botocudo consisted of dozens of tribes speaking dialects or closely related languages. The last Aimoré group to retain its language is the Krenak, whose language is also called Borum or Botocudo and is classified as an independent branch of the Macro-Gê languages. In 2010, 350 Krenak were living in Minas Gerais.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

 They do record body ornaments: wooden disks or plugs were worn in the lower lip and the lobe of the ear. The tembeitera was made from the especially light and carefully dried wood of the barriguda tree, which the Botocudo called embur. It was worn only in the under-lip, chiefly by women in the period described, although men formerly wore it as well.

### Architecture

The Botocudo were nomadic hunter-gatherers living in the forest. Their dwellings were rough shelters made of leaf and bast and were seldom 4 feet (1.2 m) high.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Botocudo made their implements and domestic utensils from wood. Their weapons were reed spears and bows and arrows.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The most conspicuous Botocudo body ornament was the *tembeitera*, a wooden plug or disk worn in the lower lip and the lobe of the ear. It was made from barriguda wood, called embur by the Botocudo. Lip preparation could begin as early as the eighth year: an initial boring was gradually enlarged with larger disks or plugs, sometimes reaching 10 cm in diameter. Ear plugs could distend the lobe down to the shoulders. A Brazilian chief presented to King Henry VIII in 1532 wore small bones hung from his cheeks and a semi-precious stone from his lower lip; these were described as marks of great bravery.

## Music & performance

The Botocudo’s only musical instrument described in the sources is a small bamboo nose flute.

## Festivals & rituals

 They record several practices associated with death and dangerous natural events. At graves, the Botocudo kept fires burning for some days to scare away evil spirits. During storms and eclipses, arrows were shot into the sky to drive away demons. They attributed the blessings of life to the Day Fire, identified with the Sun, and evil to the Night Fire, identified with the Moon.

## Foodways

 They state that poisoned food was scattered in the forests during the earlier frontier wars of 1790–1820, but provide no account of Botocudo foodways.

## Oral tradition & literature

 The Botocudo language article records lexical loans from one of the Língua Geral varieties, including *tuŋ*, “flea,” and *krai*, “non-Indigenous person, foreigner.”

## Language & religion

The Krenak language, also called Borum or Botocudo, is an Indigenous language of Brazil classified as an independent branch of the Macro-Gê languages. It was once spoken by the Botocudo people in Minas Gerais and is known primarily by older women today. The language has dialects and closely related languages associated with the many Botocudo tribes. The sources describe beliefs involving the Day Fire, or Sun, and Night Fire, or Moon, and practices intended to repel evil spirits and demons.
