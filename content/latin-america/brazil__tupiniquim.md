## Overview

The Tupiniquim, also known as *Tupinã-ki*, *Topinaquis*, *Tupinaquis*, *Tupinanquins* and *Tupiniquins*, are an Indigenous people of Brazil belonging to the Tupi family. They live in three Indigenous territories—Caieiras Velhas, Pau-Brasil and Comboios—near Santa Cruz and Vila do Riacho in the municipality of Aracruz, in northern Espírito Santo state, southeastern Brazil. Caieiras Velhas lies along the Piraquê-Açu River, Pau-Brasil is near Sahy Creek, and Comboios lies on the Comboios River. A 2010 census recorded 2,630 Tupiniquim in the three territories. Historically, their territory extended along the Brazilian coastline from approximately 200 km south of Salvador to the São Mateus River, a distance of about 600 km. Their cultural significance in the supplied sources is chiefly connected with Tupi history, language, settlement and colonial encounters.

## Material culture

### Architecture

They identify the Piraquê-Açu River, Sahy Creek and Comboios River as locations of Indigenous territories, but provide no architectural information about them.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The language source gives examples of Old Tupi words meaning “stone bowl,” “sword,” “arrow,” “forest,” “village,” “canoe” and other things.

## Music & performance

The language source states that Old Tupi literature included catechisms, poems and plays, and that José de Anchieta reportedly wrote more than 4,000 lines of poetry in *tupinambá*. It also mentions *Teatro* and *Catecismo na Língua Brasílica* in connection with examples of the language. These references concern Old Tupi documentation and literature, not specifically Tupiniquim music or performance.

## Dance & theatre

The Old Tupi language source refers to plays and to a work titled *Teatro*.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that the Tupiniquim were among the first Indigenous peoples to meet the Portuguese at Porto Seguro in April 1500 and that they inhabited the territories near Santa Cruz and Nova Almeida from the founding of those settlements, but no ritual practices are given.

## Foodways

The Old Tupi material includes language examples referring to eating, milk, manioc beer, fish, shrimp, medicine, a pigeon, an armadillo and other terms, but it does not present these as a documented Tupiniquim food system. It also states that Old Tupi had numerals from one to four because the need for mathematical precision was considered small in a primitive economy; this does not provide information about Tupiniquim foodways.

## Oral tradition & literature

The supplied sources describe a substantial written record of Old Tupi. Old Tupi had a written history spanning the 16th, 17th and early 18th centuries. Its literature included catechisms, poems and plays. The first written documents containing information about the language were produced from 1575 onwards, when Jesuits André Thévet and José de Anchieta translated Catholic prayers and biblical stories into it. Jean de Lery wrote a Tupi phrasebook containing entire dialogues. José de Anchieta reportedly wrote more than 4,000 lines of poetry in *tupinambá* and the first Tupi grammar. Luís Figueira wrote a second grammar, published circa 1628, while later works included a catechism, dictionaries and a chrestomathy. The most recent dictionary named in the source is *Old Tupi Dictionary* (2013), by Eduardo de Almeida Navarro.

## Language & religion

The supplied sources connect the Tupiniquim with the Tupi family, while the language article chiefly describes Old Tupi, also called Ancient Tupi or Classical Tupi. Old Tupi belongs to the Tupi–Guarani language family and was spoken by Indigenous Tupi people who inhabited coastal regions in South and Southeast Brazil. In the early colonial period, it served as a lingua franca among Europeans and Amerindians throughout Brazil. Its written documentation includes Catholic prayers, biblical stories, catechisms and grammars produced by Jesuits. The first Tupi grammar, written by José de Anchieta, was published in 1595. A catechism appeared in 1618, with a second edition in 1686; Luís Figueira’s other grammar was published in 1687. When the Portuguese Prime Minister Marquis of Pombal expelled the Jesuits from Brazil in 1759, the language began to decline rapidly.
