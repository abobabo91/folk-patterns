## Overview

The Guanahatabey, also spelled Guanajatabey, were an Indigenous people of western Cuba at the time of European contact. They lived primarily in what is now Pinar del Río Province and parts of Habana and Matanzas Provinces, adjacent to Taíno communities elsewhere in Cuba and the Greater Antilles. Archaeological and historical studies describe them as archaic hunter-gatherers with a distinct language and culture. They practiced small-scale horticulture, shellfish gathering, foraging, fishing, and hunting rather than large-scale agriculture. They lacked ceramic pottery and made stone, shell, and bone tools. Their language disappeared before substantial information about it was recorded, and they seem to have disappeared before further information about them could be documented.

## Material culture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Guanahatabey lacked ceramic pottery and made stone, shell, and bone tools using grinding and lithic reduction techniques.

## Music & performance

The sources state that the Guanahatabey language was distinct from Taíno and that a Taíno interpreter accompanying Christopher Columbus could not communicate with them.

## Festivals & rituals

The historical sources do report that Bartolomé de las Casas and Diego Velázquez de Cuéllar described the Guanahatabey as primitive cave-dwellers who chiefly ate fish, but these accounts were second-hand and came from Taíno informants.

## Foodways

The Guanahatabey subsisted mostly on small-scale horticulture, shellfish, and foraging, supplemented by fish and game. Unlike the neighbouring Taíno, they practiced no larger-scale agriculture. Historical accounts described them as chiefly eating fish, although those accounts were second-hand and scholars have questioned whether they reflected Taíno legends rather than Guanahatabey life.

## Oral tradition & literature

The historical descriptions preserved by Diego Velázquez de Cuéllar and Bartolomé de las Casas were second-hand accounts evidently derived from Taíno informants. The first recorded use of the name “Guanahatabey” was in a 1514 letter by Diego Velázquez de Cuéllar, and Bartolomé de las Casas referred to them in 1516.

## Language & religion

Guanahatabey was the unattested language of the Guanahatabey people, a hunter-gatherer society that lived in western Cuba until the 16th century. Spanish accounts indicate that it was distinct from, and mutually unintelligible with, the Taíno language spoken in the rest of Cuba and throughout the Caribbean. Not a single word of Guanahatabey has been documented, although Julian Granberry and Gary Vescelius identified five placenames they considered non-Taíno and possibly derived from Guanahatabey. They noted parallels with the Warao language and suggested a possible connection with the Macoris language of Hispaniola.
