## Overview

The Chontal Maya are a Maya people of the Mexican state of Tabasco. They refer to themselves as the *Yokot'anob* or *Yokot'an*, meaning “the speakers of Yoko ochoco”; “Chontal” derives from the Nahuatl word *chontalli*, meaning “foreigner.” The Yokot'an inhabit 21 towns in an area known as “la Chontalpa,” extending across Centla, El Centro, Jonuta, Macuspana, and Nacajuca. Nacajuca contains a majority of the population. Chontal Maya is a Maya language of the Cholan family, spoken in 2020 by around 60,000 Chontal Maya people. The people consider themselves descendants of the Olmecs, although they are not related to the Oaxacan Chontal. Their culture is closely connected to rivers, lagoons, wetlands, fishing, agriculture, canoes, palm work, and changing religious practices.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Traditional Chontal Maya houses are rectangular and made of palm and wood. They are supported by six to eight posts, while their roofs are steep to reduce the effects of heavy rains. Houses are commonly surrounded by overhangs used for outdoor work, and kitchen work is generally carried out beneath one of these awnings. Hired professionals build the roofs. The use of nails is described as a modern element incorporated into house construction.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do describe several everyday objects and forms of craft production. Hats are woven from palm, while sleeping mats called *petates* are woven from the fibers of *cañita*, identified as *Cyperus giganteus*. The primary craft of the Yoko'tan people is the dugout canoe and its smaller counterpart, the *cayuco*. Canoes are used for fishing and for reaching the many islands used for planting. Oyster-shell lime was formerly manufactured for mortar, although mass-produced building materials reduced demand and made its production no longer profitable.

## Music & performance

The sources provide only limited information about Chontal Maya music and performance. They state that the Catholic Church in the 1980s discouraged music and dance as part of its opposition to *la costumbre*, described as traditional Maya religious practices.

Public religious displays center on feast days following the adoption of the Catholic faith. The sources describe feast-day celebration as an important context for collective religious expression, although a strong Catholic stance against *la costumbre* led to a general withdrawal from service to the Church and to the decentralization of feast-day observance. The feasts are now celebrated privately among families.

## Festivals & rituals

Chontal Maya public religious displays center on feast days associated with Catholic practice. The most important feasts are those of Our Lady of Mount Carmel, the Immaculate Conception, and the Archangel Michael. The Immaculate Conception is observed on December 8, while the feast of the Archangel Michael is observed on September 29. April 29 is celebrated for the flood season.

The sources also describe a religious practice involving *ofrendas*, defined as dedications to saints upon the building or purchase of a new house. Catholic studies of the Gospel and neighborhood catechists were used to discourage these dedications, as well as music and dance. During the 1980s, Protestant missionaries had established themselves in the region as opponents of alcoholism and *la costumbre*. The Catholic Church responded by organizing neighborhood catechists and taking a hardline stance.

The resulting conflict over religious practice contributed to the withdrawal of many people from Church service and to the decentralization of feast-day celebration. Whereas public religious displays once centered on feast days, the feasts are now celebrated privately among families.

## Foodways

The traditional Chontal Maya economy is based on agriculture, fishing, livestock raising, and hunting small game. The principal crop is maize. Maize, beans, and squash are planted seasonally, and fish, fruit, and maize were plentiful in the historical Yokot'an homeland.

Agriculture is connected to high-altitude lands enriched with mineral-rich loam by flooding. Fishing remains an activity of the people, especially during periods of abundance, although environmental degradation has reduced its importance compared with the past. The sources distinguish three groups of fishermen: “libre” or “free” fishermen, who use simple “hoop and basket” technology and work in small groups led by an elected “boss”; members of official cooperatives; and well-equipped business owners who work with contractors.

The raising of livestock is a growing part of the economy and often occurs at the expense of agriculture and fishing. Shallow lagoons formerly used for fishing have been drained for pastureland. Small game is important in hunting, although the Yoko'tan also hunt White-tailed deer.

## Oral tradition & literature

The original mythology and cosmogony of the Yokot'an is only beginning to be studied. Their myths include supernatural water- and mangrove-creatures, and the story of La Llorona is also told.

The historical term Putún is typically considered a synonym for Chontal Maya. Chontal Maya history is also represented in narratives concerning the Spanish conquest of Yucatán, including accounts by Bernal Díaz del Castillo and Hernán Cortés. The sources mention official preservation efforts that helped Yoko ochoco flourish in the 1980s after the language had been in danger of dying out.

## Language & religion

Chontal Maya, known to native speakers as *Yokotʼan*, is a Maya language of the Cholan family. Most Yoko't'an are bilingual. According to the National Catalog of Indigenous Languages of Mexico-INALI, Yokotʼan has at least four dialects: Nacajuca, identified as Central; Centla, identified as Northern; Macuspana, identified as Southern; and Tamulte, identified as Eastern. The language was in danger of dying out but flourished in the 1980s after official preservation efforts.

The original Yokot'an mythology and cosmogony includes supernatural water- and mangrove-creatures. Public religious displays center on Catholic feast days, while *la costumbre* refers to traditional Maya religious practices. Protestant missionaries also established themselves in the region during the 1980s. Catholic catechists discouraged *ofrendas*, music, and dance, and the resulting conflict contributed to the privatization and decentralization of feast-day celebrations.
