## Overview

The Mazahua are an Indigenous people of central Mexico, living northwest of Mexico City across some 6,068 km² of the northern and western State of Mexico and into small parts of Michoacán and Querétaro. Their heartland is the mountainous, temperate-to-cold country of the San Andrés range and the upper Lerma River basin, with the densest settlement in the municipalities of San Felipe del Progreso, San José del Rincón, Villa Victoria and Villa de Allende; migration has built large communities in Toluca, Mexico City and Zapopan near Guadalajara. The 2010 Mexican census counted 116,240 speakers of the language in the State of Mexico, 53% of all Indigenous-language speakers there. The language is Oto-Pamean, within the Oto-Manguean family. In folk-culture terms the Mazahua matter above all for women's dress and for the woven sash, whose designs carry declared meanings.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Quechquemitl** — the closed shoulder cape (also written *quezquémetl*), made of two rectangular webs joined so that the points fall front and back. Museum examples are plain-woven on a backstrap (hip) loom from hand-spun white cotton or blue striped cotton, joined and bordered at the neck with contrasting bands, and finished with looped or fringed edges; the collection's 1935 pieces have a spiral-worked edge enclosing embroidered flowers and a red-embroidered shoulder yoke with blue neck trim. Wool quechquémetls are also made for sale in the craft towns.

**Cross-stitch embroidered quechquemitl** — a later variant in acrylic yarn, woven in two sections and covered in cross-stitched birds, flowers and geometric fields in red, pink, fuchsia and blue, with red and burgundy fringe.

**Chincuete** — the heavily pleated wrap skirt, usually of satin and lace, intricately folded around the waist. It has largely displaced the older *lía*, two lengths of fabric sewn together horizontally with an embroidered edge.

**Woven sash** — the woman's waist-sash, backstrap-woven on a cream or white cotton ground with fine warp-brocaded repeats in red and blue or dark blue, laid out as a wide inner border of geometric figures and an outer border of repeated diamonds, sometimes edged in blanket stitch in orange, pink and green, with one end plaited or twisted and the other loose. It is held to be the most important garment: worn at the waist, understood as the energy centre linked to the cosmos and Mother Earth, and woven to convey ideas, stories, feelings and experiences.

**Four-panel wool skirt-blanket** — a piece serving as skirt or blanket, built from four stitched wool panels, the overlapping inner two black with pink and green warp stripes, the outer two blue with pink and yellow stripes, trimmed on the short edges with factory-printed floral cloth, yellow gingham and cotton tartan and hand-stitched with coloured acrylic yarn.

**Wool household weaving** — blankets, rugs, cushions, tablecloths and carrying bags from San Felipe del Progreso, Temascalcingo, Ixtlahuaca and Atlacomulco, woven and embroidered from fabric and thread bought in Toluca and Zitácuaro according to set rules for arranging designs and colours. Comparable cloths are made as offerings to cover altars and walls on saints' days, and Day of the Dead altars carry cloths hand-embroidered with Mazahua motifs.

**Maguey fibre work** — clothing was originally woven from maguey fibre dyed with vegetable and mineral pigments; the fibre is still used for bags and belts.

**Motif vocabulary.** Birds in abundance (beauty, freedom, grace); a bird with a thorn in its leg (physical or spiritual pain); a stylized star (guardian of the night, bringer of messages, protector of health); repeated diamonds and warp-brocaded geometric borders; warp stripes; spiral edging; floral and abstract embroidered neck bands.

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress is one of the two things the Mazahua have most deliberately kept since the colonial period, and its elements carry concrete meanings and values. The outfit layers a *saco* — an embroidered blouse — under a *quechquemitl* and/or a *rebozo*, with the pleated *chincuete* skirt over an underskirt whose embroidered edge shows beneath it, plus an apron and the woven sash that holds the skirts in place. The layering, especially of skirts, gives the wearer a deliberately robust silhouette. The handwoven wool version is in danger of disappearing despite efforts to keep the skills alive. Factory work changed the dress directly: women at the Pastejé plant gave up a warming petticoat because it swept the factory floor. Street vendors in Mexico City were so visibly identified with the outfit that they were nicknamed *marías*, after the 1970s television character La India María, who wore a similar costume. The sources do not describe men's dress, beyond straw hats made at Atlacomulco and the heavy costume of the Xita Corpus dancers.

### Architecture

The sources cover building only in passing. Houses were traditionally of adobe; cinderblock and cement have replaced it as wage work changed household means. The one named built complex is the **Centro Ceremonial Mazahua** at the forest village of Santa Ana Nichi, 32 km from San Felipe del Progreso, begun in the 1980s: three kiosk-like buildings used for ceremonies such as the spring equinox and for assemblies, plus a museum of handcrafts and objects presenting Mazahua life and worldview.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Craft production is regionally specialised. Temascalcingo is dominated by red clay pottery — cooking pots, flowerpots and crucibles. San Felipe del Progreso and Villa Victoria make brooms and brushes; Ixtlahuaca makes gloves, scarves and sweaters; Atlacomulco makes straw hats; San Felipe del Progreso is also the silversmithing town. Carrying bags are woven from recycled plastic strips alongside the wool ones. Rag dolls, long sold in the streets of Mexico City by Mazahua women, became an exhibition piece in 2011, when dolls made by Mazahua women were shown at the Museo de Arte Popular dressed in the manner of international designers for a Fashion's Night Out event sponsored by Vogue México.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Almost all women in the Mazahua region wear crescent earrings. They stand in place of an engagement ring: the groom buys a pair for his fiancée, and traditional silversmiths make them from silver coins the groom provides. The craft nearly died out; preservation work led in the 1970s by María Teresa Pomar eventually produced a Mazahua silversmith guild whose members have won prizes. The sources record no other body adornment.

## Music & performance

The sources name no instruments, song genres or musical repertoire. What they do record is a broadcasting institution and a set of danced occasions: Mazahua-language programming is carried by the CDI radio station XETUMI-AM from Tuxpan, Michoacán, and dances are performed on patron-saint days and other special occasions (see below).

## Dance & theatre

Three dances are named as performed on special occasions: the **Danza de Pastoras**, the **Danza de Santiagueros** and the **Danza de Concheros**. The most elaborated local performance is the **Xita Corpus** of Temascalcingo, which stages and reinterprets a myth of the *xita*, the "old ones", who arrive in town after a journey, ask for food, find none, and are asked instead to pray for rain; the rains come and the harvest is plentiful. It is now held with Corpus Christi, as the corn is planted just before the rainy season, and its central element is the costume worn by the dancers playing the old travellers, imitating traditional Indigenous wayfarers and weighing up to 55 lb. The ceremony has kept its standing even though corn is no longer the area's main income.

## Festivals & rituals

The annual calendar is Catholic in frame and syncretic in content, each community keeping its own patron saint — most often Isidore the Laborer. The two largest festivals are the **Feast of the Cross** (3 May) and the **Day of the Dead** (2 November). On the Day of the Dead the souls of the ancestors are welcomed back with the foods they preferred in life, with pulque, beer, bread, sweets and fruit; the Mazahua hold that the dead return as monarch butterflies to enjoy the fruit and bread left on altars, which are dressed with flowers, cut paper and embroidered Mazahua cloths. A procession goes from the church to the cemetery to receive them and in the reverse direction to send them off. The **New Fire ceremony** falls on 19 March, a springtime blessing of fire coordinated by the head of the Mazahua people: the participants form a circle with points aligned to the cardinal directions, each honouring a different deity, and the wood at the centre is blessed, lit and the flame distributed by candle. The **Ofrenda al Agua** (Offering to Water) is held on 15 or 16 August beside rivers and lakes at the close of the rainy season, thanking water for its part in the agricultural cycle and asking forgiveness for abuses of it. Communal labour obligations, called *faena*, build schools, markets and roads.

## Foodways

Mazahua cooking is tied to ritual and cosmology and closely resembles Otomi cooking. Common ingredients are squash, *pipián* sauce, the greens called *quelite*, and a wide range of mushrooms gathered in the forest at their seasons; farming yields corn, beans, wheat, barley, oats and potatoes, with sheep and cattle, some fish farming, and firewood and charcoal from the woods. The feast dish is turkey in mole sauce, generally reserved for the locality's patron-saint day and for weddings. The feast drinks are *zende* and *pulque*. Zende is made locally from sprouted corn, brewed and coloured with a little chili pepper, then started fermenting with a small amount of pulque added; it is ready in about four days and tastes sweet and sour. Diet sits inside a wider idea of health as both physical and spiritual, with ailments sorted into "good" ones sent by God — diarrhea, pneumonia, bronchitis, intestinal parasites — and "bad" ones caused by someone's ill will or by supernatural agency, such as the evil eye, "fright" and "bad air"; the class of ailment determines whether it is met with herbal remedies, massage, ceremony or professional medicine.

## Oral tradition & literature

No epic is recorded for the Mazahua in these sources. The transmitted narratives are the *xita* myth danced at Temascalcingo and two competing origin accounts: that the Mazahua were one of five Chichimeca groups migrating into the area in the 13th century under a chief named Mazahuatl, or that they descend from the Acolhuas. A third etymological tradition derives the name from the first chief, Mazatlí-Tecutli, against the reading of *Mazahua* as Nahuatl for "deer-foot", after those who tracked deer — a hunt that ended with the loss of the deer habitat. Narrative is also carried non-verbally: sashes are woven to convey stories, feelings and experiences, and textiles are said to attest to how the people live and view their world. Documentation and preservation efforts include the museum and ceremonial programme at the Centro Ceremonial Mazahua and a published Mazahua translation of the first article of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights.

## Language & religion

The Mazahua language (Central Mazahua *Jñatrjo*) belongs to the Otomian subgroup of the Oto-Pamean branch of Oto-Manguean, its closest relatives being Otomi, Matlatzinca and Ocuilteco/Tlahuica. Speakers call themselves *Tetjo ñaa jñatjo*, roughly "those who speak their own language", and also *Hñatho*. It is tonal, with high, low and falling tones on every syllable but the predictably stressed final one, and carries an unusually large inventory of about sixty phonemes — eight oral vowels, seven contrastive nasal vowels and up to forty-five consonants including ejectives, implosives, contrastive voiceless sonorants and the rare ejective fricatives. Its orthography adapts the Spanish alphabet with strikethrough for reduced vowels, underlining for nasal vowels, an apostrophe for ejectives, *j* for aspiration and *u* for labialization. In 2003 it was recognised as an official language alongside Spanish wherever it is spoken. Religion blends Catholic and Indigenous cosmology; some families have converted to Protestantism, especially the Jehovah's Witnesses.
