## Overview

The Guna (also Kuna or Cuna; self-designation *Dule* or *Tule*, "people") are an Indigenous people of Panama and northwestern Colombia, numbering roughly 50,000–70,000 speakers of *Dulegaya* ("people-mouth"), a language of the Chibchan family. Most live in Guna Yala, the autonomous *comarca* strung along Panama's Caribbean coast, where some 49 communities occupy densely built coral islets of the San Blas archipelago and a strip of mainland forest; two further comarcas, Guna de Madugandí and Guna de Wargandí, lie inland along the Bayano and Chucunaque drainages, and large communities live in Panama City and Colón. They matter to folk-culture study for two reasons: the *mola*, an appliqué and reverse-appliqué textile that became one of the world's most recognisable Indigenous art forms, and an unbroken political-ritual institution — the sung congress of the *saila* — that still governs daily life.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Mola** — the defining Guna textile: layered rectangular panels of plain-woven cotton cloth in which the maker cuts through the upper layers and turns the raw edges under with fine blind stitching, so the design emerges from what has been removed rather than added. Two panels, front and back, make the body of a woman's blouse. Unlike appliqué traditions that build motifs upward onto a ground, the Guna reverse-appliqué technique is subtractive and multi-layered — two to seven strata of contrasting cloth, the finest work identified by stitch count and by how narrow the exposed lines of underlying colour are.

**Dule mor** — literally "Guna clothing," the complete women's dress ensemble of which the mola blouse is one part; the term frames the mola not as a craft object but as an element of national costume.

**Tulemola / dulemola** — the Guna term for the mola blouse itself, the sewn garment as distinct from the flat panel sold to outsiders. *Mola* alone simply means "clothing" in Dulegaya.

**Mola kantule** — panels whose imagery derives from the chants and ritual knowledge of the *kantule*, the ceremonial flute-master and song specialist, rather than from everyday observation; these belong to the older, more abstract and esoteric end of the mola repertoire.

**Mola olo** — the "gold" mola, named for the prestige colour term *olo*; a high-status register of panel associated with wealth and ceremonial display.

**Saburet** — the wrapped skirt cloth worn below the mola blouse, a length of printed or dark patterned cotton wound around the hips; commercially printed trade cloth, incorporated into Guna dress through the archipelago's long mercantile contact with Colombian, Mexican and Chinese shipping.

**Muswe** — the red-and-yellow patterned head cloth draped over the hair and shoulders, the third fixed component of the women's ensemble alongside blouse and wrap skirt.

**Wini mor** — beadwork treated as part of dress rather than as separate jewellery: the strung glass-bead *winis* wound on forearms and lower legs are conceived, like the blouse, as clothing for the body.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Naa Ukuryaa* — the ancestral four-armed hooked cross of the Guna flag, read as the four quarters of the world or as the world-making octopus; *nuchu* — the carved tutelary figure, also rendered in cloth; *picha* — bird imagery; geometric labyrinth and maze fields (the older abstract stratum, closest to body-painting design); split and mirrored bilateral compositions; marine motifs from the reef economy (lobster, fish, turtle, crab); *ogob*, the coconut palm; introduced pictorial subjects — ships, aeroplanes, political emblems, commercial labels — absorbed wholesale into the panel format.

### Clothing & dress

Guna women's dress is worn daily, not reserved for display, and is one of the most consistently maintained Indigenous costumes in the Americas. The ensemble is the *dule mor*: the *tulemola* blouse with its two mola panels joined by a printed yoke and puffed sleeves, the *saburet* wrap skirt, the red-and-yellow *muswe* head cloth, tightly wound *winis* bead bands on forearms and calves, gold nose ring and earrings, and a line of dark pigment drawn down the ridge of the nose. Hair is cut short and often covered. Men's dress is Western — trousers and shirt — and has been since the early twentieth century; ceremonial male costume survives chiefly in the regalia of the *kantule* flute-master and in the feathered and painted dress of chanters at the *inna* ceremonies. Girls receive the full *dule mor* at the puberty ceremony, and the shift from child's to woman's clothing is itself the visible marker of that rite.

### Architecture

Island villages are built to the waterline, houses packed wall to wall along narrow sand lanes with almost no open ground — a settlement form dictated by the tiny coral cays. The dwelling is a rectangular post-and-beam hut with a steeply pitched thatch roof of palm leaf, walls of split cane or bamboo lashed to the frame, an earth or sand floor, and no windows; hammocks slung from the posts serve for sleeping, and the cooking hearth often occupies a separate adjoining structure. Two public buildings anchar every community. The **onmaked nega** (also *Ibeorgun Nega*, "congress house") is the largest structure in the village: a great open-sided thatched hall where the *saila* lies in a central hammock and sings the sacred history, laws and legends of the people, with the *voceros* translating from the high ritual register into everyday Dulegaya, and where all political decisions are taken. The **inna nega** is the long chicha house where the fermented drink of the puberty ceremony is brewed and consumed. Both are ritual and civic at once — the Guna make no separation between the two.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Guna material life is built from palm, cane, wood, gourd and imported metal rather than from a ceramic or smithing tradition of its own. The carved wooden figure — **nuchu**, cut from balsa or other light woods in a stiff, frontal, often European-featured form — is the central object: made by specialists, it houses a tutelary spirit, is kept in the household basket, and is used by the curer in healing chants. Related to it is the **nudirbi**, a hardwood protective figure of the same family. Household equipment runs to gourd bowls and dippers, low wooden stools, dugout canoes (*ulu*) used for reaching the mainland garden plots and water rivers, basketry carrying trays, fishing and lobster gear, and the hammock, which is furniture, bed and the *saila*'s seat of office. Gold ornament is worked by Panamanian and Colombian smiths to Guna order rather than locally forged.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Guna adornment is dense, permanent and worn from childhood. **Winis** are long strings of small glass trade beads wound in tight, patterned spirals around the forearms and lower legs, building continuous geometric bands of colour that read as a second skin — the closest surviving relative of the older Guna body-painting designs that the earliest molas echo. Gold is the prestige metal: the nose ring (*olasu* class of gold ornament), large flat disc earrings, and heavy gold pectoral plates and necklaces worn at ceremonies, the accumulated gold of a household being its visible wealth. The red nose ring was important enough that in 1942 a red ring representing it was added to the Guna flag, encircling the *Naa Ukuryaa* so the emblem would not be mistaken for a Nazi swastika. Face painting — the black line down the nose, and fuller red-and-black designs from *achiote* and other plant pigments at ceremonies — completes the ensemble.

## Music & performance

The central Guna musical institution is not entertainment but the sung word. In the *onmaked nega* the *saila* chants village history, myth and law in a specialised high register, half-lying in his hammock, answered by a *vocero* who interprets; the chant is simultaneously scripture, legislation and archive. A parallel specialist, the **kantule**, is the flute-master who chants the long ceremonial sequences of the *inna* (chicha) festivals and directs its music. The signature instrument is the **gammu burui** (*gaa burui*), a bundled set of end-blown cane flutes of graded length played in interlocking pairs, giving the hocketed, interwoven melodic texture characteristic of Chibchan panpipe traditions; single cane flutes, gourd and seed rattles (*nasis*), conch trumpets, and turtle-shell and skin drums accompany the ceremonies. Curing is also musical: the *nele* (seer) and *inatuledi* (herbalist-curer) recite long therapeutic chants addressed to the *nuchu* figures, a genre documented under names such as the *Igar* chants — *Muu Igala*, the "Way of Muu," sung to assist difficult childbirth being the best known. Joel Sherzer's work on Guna ways of speaking established this repertoire as one of the most fully documented verbal-art systems in the Americas.

## Dance & theatre

Guna performance is tied to the chicha ceremonies rather than to a theatrical stage. At the *inna* festivals, lines of dancers move in circling and processional formation to the paired cane flutes and rattles, women and men in separate rows, the *kantule* leading. There is no mask drama, shadow-puppet theatre or court dance tradition; the dramatic element belongs instead to the curing chant, where the chanter narrates a journey through the spirit world on the patient's behalf and the carved *nuchu* figures act as the cast. Since the later twentieth century, folkloric troupes from Guna Yala have performed the flute-and-dance repertoire at Panamanian national festivals and for visitors in the Carti tourist zone, a presentational context distinct from the village *inna*.

## Festivals & rituals

The Guna ceremonial year is organised around life-cycle events rather than an agricultural or liturgical calendar. The great public ceremonies are the **inna** — multi-day chicha festivals for which maize or sugarcane beer is brewed in the *inna nega* and consumed to exhaustion, accompanied by flute music, dancing and the *kantule*'s chants. The most important is the girl's puberty ceremony (*inna suid*, the "long chicha"), in which the girl's hair is ritually cut, she is painted, and she is dressed for the first time in the full *dule mor*; a smaller earlier rite marks the naming and the first haircut. Marriage is enacted rather than celebrated: Guna families are matrilineal and matrilocal, and the groom moves into the bride's household and takes her family name, the ceremony centring on his being placed in the hammock with her. Death brings burial in mainland forest cemeteries, the grave covered with a small thatched roof and the deceased laid in a hammock, with mourners' chants (*masar igar*) sung over the body. Overarching all of these is the political-ritual calendar of the congress: daily or near-daily evening gatherings in the *onmaked nega*, and periodic sessions of the Guna General Congress under the three *Saila Dummagan*. The secular anniversary that matters most is the **Dule Revolution** of February 1925 — the successful rising led by Iguaibilikinya Nele Kantule of Ustupu against Panamanian suppression of Guna custom — commemorated annually with re-enactments in the villages. A cosmological observance persists alongside: at a lunar eclipse only *sipu*, people with albinism, go outdoors, to shoot at the dragon devouring the moon with specially made bows and arrows.

## Foodways

The Guna diet rests on three things: plantains, coconuts and fish. Mainland garden plots reached by canoe supply plantains and bananas (boiled, roasted, or pounded into the thick plantain soups and drinks that are the everyday staple), along with yuca, yams, maize, rice, sugarcane and tree fruits. From the reef come fish, octopus, crab, sea turtle and above all lobster (*skungit*), which with coconut (*ogob*) forms the archipelago's main export. Coconut is currency as much as food — historically traded to Colombian schooners — and its milk is the base of the region's best-known dish, fish or lobster simmered in coconut milk with plantain or rice. Wild game and a few domestic animals supplement this, and imported rice, flour, sugar, coffee and tinned goods arrive through the Guna-owned retail stores that are a hallmark of the comarca's mercantile economy. Ceremonial consumption is dominated by *inna*, the fermented maize or sugarcane chicha brewed specifically for the puberty festivals and drunk only in the *inna nega*; its preparation is a specialist role. Guna nutritional patterns have drawn medical attention: blood pressure averages around 110/70 mm Hg with no age-related rise, and cardiovascular and cancer mortality are a fraction of mainland Panamanian rates — 8 and 4 per 100,000 against 119 and 74 for the period 2000–2004 — findings widely associated with high consumption of minimally processed cacao alongside fish and plant staples.

## Oral tradition & literature

Guna oral tradition is unusually well recorded and still fully functional. Its backbone is the corpus of *igar* ("ways" or "paths") — long chanted texts, each a route through mythic or therapeutic terrain, memorised by specialists: the *saila*'s historical and legal chants, the *kantule*'s ceremonial sequences, and the curers' healing chants such as **Muu Igala** ("The Way of Muu") for obstructed childbirth. The founding culture-hero narrative concerns **Ibeorgun**, who taught the Guna their law, their crafts and the form of the congress house that bears his name; further cycles concern the great *neles* (seers), the origin of maize and of the *inna*, and the octopus or four-quartered world of the *Naa Ukuryaa*. Everyday speech genres — formal greetings, courtroom-style argument in congress, and the "way of speaking" that shifts register between chant, interpretation and ordinary talk — were documented by Joel Sherzer in a body of work that made Guna one of the reference cases in the ethnography of speaking. Guna-language literacy programmes, the Congreso General de la Nación Gunadule's 2010 orthographic reform (which standardised *Guna* for the older *Kuna*), and published bilingual collections of chants such as *Igargan Cantos* carry the tradition into print.

## Language & religion

*Dulegaya* is a Chibchan language, the primary medium of daily life in the comarcas and spoken by most Guna children, though classified as endangered against the pressure of Spanish, which dominates schooling and written administration. A markedly distinct high register — with its own lexicon and formulaic diction — is reserved for chant, and the interpretive role of the *vocero* exists precisely to bridge it to ordinary speech. Religion is an indigenous cosmology only lightly overlaid by Christian mission: a creator (*Baba* and *Nana*, Father and Mother), a layered universe traversed by the chanter, tutelary spirits embodied in *nuchu* figures, and specialist offices — *nele* the seer, *inatuledi* the herbalist-curer, *absogedi* the chant-therapist. The congress house is a religious building as much as a political one, and the *saila* is both offices in one person.
