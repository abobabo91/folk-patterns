## Overview

The Aguaruna, also known by their endonym *Awajún*, are an Indigenous people of the Peruvian Amazon-Andes divide. They live primarily along the Marañón River and its tributaries—the Santiago, Nieva, Cenepa, Numpatakay and Chiriaco—in northern Peru near the border with Ecuador. Their titled community lands extend across Amazonas, Cajamarca, Loreto and San Martín, with a significant population in the Alto Mayo river basin. Peru’s 1993 Census recorded approximately 5,000 Aguaruna, while World Census data for 2000 listed just over 8,000. Their language belongs to the Chicham family. Their folk culture includes dispersed kin-based settlements, mutual-aid institutions, hunting and gathering knowledge, subsistence agriculture, ceramics, basketry, textiles, canoe construction, feather adornment and spiritual practices involving ayahuasca and other hallucinogenic plants.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Textiles** — Textile production is described as a masculine craft activity.

**Cotton ribbons** — Men make cotton ribbons with feathers and human hair attached to their ends.

**Basketry** — Basketry is listed among the principal masculine crafts.

### Clothing & dress

They state that men make headdresses from exquisite feathers, as well as cotton ribbons whose ends carry feathers and human hair. These adornments are kept in bamboo cases.

### Architecture

Awajún families traditionally lived in dispersed neighborhoods of kin related through descent and marriage. Houses were built in traditional forms and distributed asymmetrically, usually in a linear arrangement along a river. Towns with nucleate populations are called *yáakat* in the Awajún language; they do not have streets, footpaths or squares, but consist of traditionally constructed houses. Road construction and the establishment of bilingual schools and health posts encouraged more clustered settlements and, in some cases, densely populated hamlets. Examples of Awajún towns include Yutupiza on the Santiago River and Japaime on the Nieva.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Ceramics are described as a principal feminine craft. Everyday tools include the traditional *wái*, a sharp-ended stick made from pijuayo palm wood, together with the axe, machete and shovel. Spears were traditionally made with pijuayo, a palm tree with very hard wood, and were used in hunting; the spear has now been almost completely displaced by the pellet shotgun, although the blowpipe continues to be used.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Women make necklaces from seeds, insects’ small wings and beads. Men make feather headdresses and cotton ribbons decorated with feathers and human hair; these adornments are stored in bamboo cases.

## Music & performance

They mention the blowpipe, spear and other material objects.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe religious and life-related practices involving spirits, hallucinogenic plants and warfare. Young men traditionally took hallucinogenic plants, including ayahuasca, to obtain visions. These visions were believed to be the souls of dead warriors; if the young man showed no fear, he would receive a spirit power known as *ajútap*, which was believed to make a man invulnerable in battle. The Aguaruna traditionally believed in many spirits and mythological figures, including Zeus, or the Sun; Núgkui, or Mother Earth, associated with agricultural success and clay for ceramics; Tsúgki, water spirits living in rivers; and Bikut, or father shaman. In the distant past, they practiced shrinking human heads to make *tsantsa*. A traditional mutual-aid meeting called *ipáamamu* was held on Aguaruna territory during negotiations over medicinal knowledge; one such meeting was attended by over eighty representatives of sixty Aguaruna communities.

## Foodways

Traditionally, the Aguaruna economy was based mostly on hunting, fishing and subsistence agriculture. They hunt animals including sajino, huangana, Brazilian tapir or sachavaca, little red brocket, ocelot and otorongo, or jaguar. Less commonly hunted species include majaz, ronsoco, achuni, añuje, carachupa, otter, monkeys and birds. Hunting supplies meat, while skins, feathers, teeth and bones are used for handicrafts, medicines and items associated with witchcraft.

Gathered foods and materials include wild fruit from palm trees, fruit from shrubs, palm buds, stems, bark, resins, leche caspi, honey from wild bees, edible worms called *suris*, beetles, medicinal plants and lianas. The sources state that gathered materials are used for food, crafts, traditional medicine, witchcraft or fuel, according to an ancestral pattern of self-sufficiency. Traditional agriculture includes yuca, peanuts, rice, coffee, cocoa and bananas; rice, coffee, cocoa and bananas are now cultivated by some communities for sale.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that the Aguaruna communicate in their own language and that a dictionary, *Vocabulario aguaruna del Amazonas* (*Aguaruna Vocabulary of Amazonas*), was compiled by Mildred L. Larson and published by SIL International in 1966. They also mention several Aguaruna–Spanish and Spanish–Aguaruna dictionaries.

## Language & religion

Aguaruna, whose endonym is *Awajún*, is an Indigenous American language of the Chicham family spoken in northern Peru. It is used in written and oral domains and is written with the Latin script. The language has two major varieties: one around the Nieva River, described as more conservative, and another around the Marañón River. The Chicham family is described as comprising Aguaruna, Huambisa, Shuar and Achuar-Shiwiar; Aguaruna speakers claim mutual intelligibility with Huambisa speakers. Aguaruna has twelve consonants and four vowels, each with oral and nasal forms, and typically uses verb-final clauses with subject–object–verb order.

The Aguaruna traditionally believed in numerous spirits and mythological figures, including Zeus or the Sun, Núgkui or Mother Earth, Tsúgki and Bikut. Ayahuasca and other hallucinogenic plants were associated with visions and spirit power. Evangelical missionaries began contacting the Aguaruna in the mid-20th century, and many Aguarunas have converted to Christianity.
