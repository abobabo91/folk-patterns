## Overview

The Kaʼapor are an Indigenous people of Brazil who live on a protected reserve in the state of Maranhão, in a heavily deforested area of Pre-Amazonian forest. They have managed to protect the forest within their designated reserve, although the reserve is under attack from illegal loggers. The Kaʼapor speak Kaapor, also known as Kaʼapor, Kaaporté, Urubú, Caapor, or Urubú-Kaapor, a Tupi–Guarani language. It is spoken as a primary language by the Kaʼapor people and as a second language by non-Ka'apor ethnic groups, including Tembé. The sources identify ethnobotany, historical ecology, deafness, sign language, language, and forest protection as important aspects of Kaʼapor life.

## Music & performance

 They state that, because there is a high degree of congenital deafness among the Kaʼapor, most of the hearing community knows sign language.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do record that in September 2014 the Kaʼapor attacked a group of illegal loggers, tied them up, humiliated them, destroyed logs extracted from the forest, and burned the logger’s lorry before eventually setting them free.

## Language & religion

Kaapor, also known as Kaʼapor, Kaaporté, Urubú, Caapor, or Urubú-Kaapor, is a Tupi–Guarani language spoken as a primary language by the Kaʼapor people of Brazil. It is also spoken as a second language by non-Ka'apor ethnic groups, including Tembé. There is a high incidence of congenital deafness among the Kaʼapor people, and most grow up bilingual in Urubu-Kaapor Sign Language, which the source describes as potentially indigenous to them.
