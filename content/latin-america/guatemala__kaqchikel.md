## Overview

The Kaqchikel, also called Kachiquel, are an Indigenous Maya people of the central and midwestern highlands of Guatemala and of southern Mexico. They constitute Guatemala’s third-largest Maya group and make up approximately one sixth of the country’s Maya population. Their principal concentration is in southern Guatemala, especially the department of Chimaltenango, with communities also in Sololá, Sacatepéquez, Guatemala, Baja Verapaz Department, and Escuintla. Kaqchikel communities in Mexico are located in Chiapas, with smaller communities in Campeche. The Kaqchikel language belongs to the Quichean branch of the Mayan language family. The sources describe Kaqchikel cultural identity through language, historical memory, migration, traditional food, clothing, and cultural customs.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources mention clothing as one of the means through which Kaqchikel migrants preserve and express Indigenous heritage.

The sources identify houses only through the Kaqchikel word *jay*, meaning “house.” Historically, the sources identify Iximché as a Kaqchikel capital and state that the Acajal, also known as Chajoma, retained political autonomy, with the ruins of Mixco Viejo identified as their capital.

## Music & performance

The sources name *tlincheʼ* as the Kaqchikel word for “marimba,” but provide no description of the instrument, its construction, musical repertoire, or performance context. They also identify the 2015 film *Ixcanul*, directed by Jayro Bustamante, as having been filmed mostly in Kaqchikel. No Kaqchikel song genres, musical ensembles, dances, performance settings, or ceremonial uses of music are described.

The sources do provide information about language use in education and media. Kaqchikel is taught in Guatemala’s intercultural bilingual education programs, and the language has been used in film. A Kaqchikel translation of the New Testament was produced with José Chicol and the missionary William Cameron Townsend; it was published in 1931, and Chicol’s revision was published in 1954.

## Festivals & rituals

 They describe earlier Kaqchikel political organization, in which four lords—Tzotzil, Xahil, Tucuché, and Acajal—were responsible for administrative, military, and religious affairs.

Kaqchikel historical tradition describes four political “dawns,” written *saqarik* or *saqer*, each representing a transition away from dependence on other groups, especially the Kʼicheʼ. The first followed an alliance with the Kʼicheʼ maintained through marriages between Kaqchikel men and Kʼicheʼ women. The second followed relocation to present-day Chichicastenango in Quiché Department under Kʼicheʼ rule. The third involved permission to establish a separate government and the founding of Chi Awär around 1430 CE. The fourth followed independence from the Kʼicheʼ and the establishment of Iximche around 1470 CE.

## Foodways

The sources identify traditional food as part of Kaqchikel cultural identity, especially in the context of migration. The Kaqchikel vocabulary includes *ixim*, “corn,” and *wäy*, “tortilla.” It also includes *yaʼ*, “water,”.

The sources state that some Kaqchikel migrants use traditional food, along with language, clothing, and cultural customs, to identify themselves through Indigenous heritage rather than through the “Latino” and “Hispanic” terms used by the United States to identify Guatemalan Indigenous immigrants and Guatemalan Americans.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Kaqchikel recorded their history in the *Annals of the Cakchiquels*, also known as *Memorial de Sololá*. They do, however, describe the four political “dawns” as stages in Kaqchikel political development and connect them with alliances, migration, government, and independence.

Kaqchikel language preservation is described as an important contemporary effort. Today, the language is being revitalized, and education is used to restore language and culture. The Academy of the Mayan Languages of Guatemala standardized an alphabet for the Mayan languages in 1986, beginning a new movement for linguistic conversation. Kaqchikel is also taught in public schools and at universities in the United States, including Tulane University and the University of Kansas.

## Language & religion

Kaqchikel is an Indigenous Mesoamerican language in the Quichean–Mamean branch of the Mayan language family. It is closely related to Kʼicheʼ and Tzʼutujil, with Tzʼutujil described as its closest dialect or language. Kaqchikel is spoken in central Guatemala and in communities in Chiapas and Campeche. The sources give different current estimates: one account states that it is spoken by around 450,000 people in Guatemala, while another states that it is spoken by 400,000 people.

The language has dialectal variation, including differences in vowel inventories and pronunciation. Its grammar includes prefixes and suffixes, agreement with subjects, objects, and possessors, and an ergative-absolutive pattern. Its possible word orders include VSO, VOS, SVO, and SOV. The sources discuss religious affairs in relation to the four historical Kaqchikel lords.
