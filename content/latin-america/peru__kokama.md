## Overview

The Kokama are an indigenous Amazonian people whose settlements stretch across Peru, Brazil, and Colombia along major river systems. Today most speakers are trilingual in Cocama, Portuguese, and Spanish. The sources document them mainly through their language and pottery, with limited information on broader folk culture. Language revitalization efforts through radio and school programs are actively underway.

## Material culture

The Kokama are known primarily for their pottery, which features geometric designs in red, black, and orange on cream backgrounds.

### Textile & pattern traditions

No textile traditions are documented in the sources.

- **Kene design** (*kene*) — A geometric pattern used on polychrome pottery vessels.

### Clothing & dress

Kokama clothing and dress are not documented in the sources.

### Architecture

Kokama communities are located along riverbanks, but specific house forms and building practices are not documented.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery dominates the documented Kokama material culture, with vessels featuring geometric patterns in multiple colors.

- **Pottery bowl** — Plain ceramic bowls are among the common Kokama pottery forms.
- **Water jar** — A ceramic vessel type used for storing water.
- **Looped-handle jug** — A jug decorated with white and red paint and featuring a looped handle.
- **Geometric jars** — Large jars with circular mouths glazed red and bodies painted cream with red and black geometric designs.
- **Polychrome vessel** — A decorated vessel with dark red interior, cream exterior, and orange geometric patterns in thin and thick dark red-brown lines.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Kokama jewelry and body adornment practices are not documented in the sources.

## Music & performance

No traditional instruments or song genres are named, but the Cocama language is preserved and taught through songs.

- **Children's rap video** — In 2013, residents of Nauta created a children's rap video in Kukama-Kukamiria dialect with Radio Ucamara to preserve the language.
- **Ikuar school** — A school run by Radio Ucamara that teaches the Cocama language through songs and storytelling.

## Oral tradition & literature

Traditional storytelling is used as a tool for language preservation rather than as a documented cultural tradition.

- **Radio Ucamara** — A radio station in Nauta, Peru that has worked for years to preserve Cocama through broadcasts and the Ikuar school.
- **Bible translation** — Parts of the Bible have been translated into Cocama, supporting literacy at 3% compared to 50% in Spanish.

## Language & religion

Cocama is spoken by thousands across western South America with three dialects, and most speakers are trilingual in Cocama, Portuguese, and Spanish.

- **Kokama-Kukamiria dialect** (*Kukama-Kukamiria*) — The robust main dialect, also known by many names including Ucayali, Xibitaoan, Huallaga, and Pampadeque.
- **Cocamilla dialect** (*Cocamilla or Kokamíya*) — A moribund dialect spoken only by people over 40 by 1999.
- **Mari Luz Canaquiri Murayari** — A Kokama environmentalist honored by Time 100 and recipient of the Goldman Environmental Prize.
- **Lilia Isolina Java Tapayuri** — A Kokama environmentalist born in 1985.

## Glossary

- *Kokáma* — Alternative spelling of Cocama language

- *Cocamilla* — A moribund dialect of Cocama, nearly extinct by the 1990s
- *Ucayali* — A river where Kokama communities live; also a dialect name
- *Marañón* — A river where Kokama communities live
- *Huallaga* — A river where Kokama communities live; also a dialect name
- *Omagua* — A nearly extinct language closely related to Cocama
- *Radio Ucamara* — A radio station in Nauta, Peru preserving the Cocama language
- *Ikuar* — A school teaching Cocama language through songs and storytelling
- *Nauta* — A town in Peru's Loreto Province with Kokama communities
- *Xibitaoan* — An alternative name for the main Kokama dialect
- *Pampadeque* — An alternative name for the main Kokama dialect
