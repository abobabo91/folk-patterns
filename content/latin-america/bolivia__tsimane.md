## Overview

The Tsimané, also known as the Tsimane' or Chimane, are an indigenous people of lowland Bolivia, living chiefly in the Beni Department municipalities of San Borja, San Ignacio de Moxos, Rurrenabaque, and Santa Ana del Yacuma. They are the main residents of the T'simane Council Territory and the Pilón Lajas Reserve. Their communities commonly contain 20 to 30 families. The Tsimané depend mainly on subsistence farming, especially the cultivation of bananas and manioc through swidden agriculture, while hunting, fishing, and gathering contribute significantly to food supplies. Their language, Tsimané, is a South American language isolate with several dialect varieties. The sources describe a culture shaped by subsistence agriculture, forest and river foodways, small communities, and the preservation of customs and language.

## Foodways

Tsimané foodways are based chiefly on subsistence farming, hunting, fishing, and gathering. Bananas and manioc are cultivated through swidden agriculture. Hunting, fishing, and gathering contribute significantly as a source of food for almost all communities, and these activities remain important alongside farming. A typical daily pattern described in the sources involves working during the day, gathering around a fire while cooking food, sharing a meal, and remaining by the fire as it gets dark.

## Oral tradition & literature

They describe evening gatherings around a fire in which people share stories and information. The sources also state that the Tsimaneʼ have preserved customs and traditions, including their language.

## Language & religion

Tsimané, also called Mosetan, is a South American language isolate. Chimané and Mosetén are described as dialects, with Mosetén further divided into Mosetén of Santa Ana and Mosetén of Covendo; these variants are mutually intelligible in some cases. Tsimaneʼ proper has at least 4,000 speakers, while Covendo Mosetén has around 600 speakers and Santa Ana Mosetén around 150–200 speakers. The dialects are in different sociolinguistic situations: the Mosetén varieties are fading quickly and their speakers are generally bilingual in Spanish, while Tsimaneʼ proper is growing and most of its speakers are monolingual. Chimane has been written since 1980 in a Spanish-based alphabet devised by Wayne Gill. In 1996, Colette Grinevald created an alphabet for Moseten and Chimane, and Bolivian Law 3603 of 2007 Jan 17 recognized language rights for the Chimane and Moseten.
