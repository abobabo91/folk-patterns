## Overview

The Purépecha are an Indigenous people centered in the northwestern region of Michoacán, Mexico, especially around Cherán, Pátzcuaro, Lake Pátzcuaro, and Cuitzeo. They also historically occupied parts of Guanajuato and Jalisco. Their pre-Columbian empire had its capital at Tzintzuntzan and was one of the major empires of the Pre-Columbian era. The Purépecha language is spoken in rural communities and highlands of Michoacán; it is generally classified as a language isolate or small language family. The sources give different population figures: nearly 200,000 people in Michoacán are described as speaking Purépecha in one account, while another describes approximately 140,000 speakers. Purépecha culture is represented through fishing, weaving, pottery, embroidery, architecture, religious observances, music, dance, and oral and written language traditions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Embroidery* is described as complex and as using traditional patterns with various techniques.

The sources also identify weaving as a recognized Purépecha skill.

### Clothing & dress

The sources mention traditional Purépecha clothing in connection with the logos of “La Michoacana” ice-cream shops, which often incorporate a girl in traditional Purépecha clothing as a reference to Michoacán.

### Architecture

Purépecha architecture is noted for step pyramids shaped like the letter “T.” Temples created by the Purépecha did not resemble their Mesoamerican counterparts. Many Purépecha live in wooden cabins within compounds surrounded by dry-stone walls, although many of these structures are being replaced by houses made of brick and concrete.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Purépecha are known for pottery and for skill in working with metal. Pre-Columbian artisans made feather mosaics that extensively used hummingbird feathers, which were regarded as luxury goods throughout the region. Purépecha communities also exchanged metal and resources with the Purépecha kingdom, while the Aztecs traded with them mainly for metal tools and weapons.

## Music & performance

The sources state that Purépecha traditions of folkloric performance became a source of indigenista pride during the Cárdenas era. They also mention music as part of the customs, food, and community portrayal associated with the Purépecha in *The Casagrandes Movie*. No Purépecha instrument, song genre, musical form, or performance setting is named. The radio station XEPUR-AM, located in Cherán, Michoacán, broadcasts Purépecha-language programming and is described as a project of the National Commission for the Development of Indigenous Peoples.

## Dance & theatre

In San Juan Nuevo Parangaricutiro, unmarried men dance the **Dance of the Cúrpites**. The dance helps men express their masculinity and court their sweethearts, and it is celebrated during the Christian holiday of Epiphany. The sources also mention Purépecha folkloric performance as part of regional and indigenista identity.

## Festivals & rituals

The **Jimbani Uexurhina**, or New Fire, is celebrated on February 2. It combines traditional Indigenous and Catholic elements. During the ceremony, the community lights a fire called the *chijpiri jimbani*, or “new fire,” in a ritual honoring the four elements. Mass is also celebrated in the Purhépecha language.

The **Day of the Dead**, or “Día De Los Muertos,” is celebrated on November 1 and 2. Family members take part in all-night vigils at the graves of deceased relatives, and Purépecha people believe that the souls of the dead watch over their living relatives during the observance.

The sources describe a newborn-baptism practice in which the mother and child have forty days of separate rest. The infant is then swaddled for six weeks and kept in physical contact with the mother or a close female relative.

## Foodways

The sources state that Purépecha foodways include fishing, because the people mainly lived around the Pátzcuaro lakes, and they refer generally to food in descriptions of Purépecha customs.

## Oral tradition & literature

Purépecha tradition includes stories of migration from the Pacific Ocean to the people’s current locations. The sources also describe a legend of Princess Eréndira, a 16- or 17-year-old Purépecha who led her people in war against the Spanish and whose people learned to ride stolen Spanish horses. During the Cárdenas era, the story of Princess Eréndira was promoted as a symbol of resistance, and muralists were commissioned to depict Purépecha history.

Purépecha became a literary language in the early colonial period through the work of Spanish friars. Written sources from that period include dictionaries, confessionaries, and land titles. The sources name the grammar of 1558 and dictionary of 1559 by Fray Maturino Gilberti, as well as the grammar and dictionary of 1574 by Juan Baptista de Lagunas.

## Language & religion

Purépecha, also called Tarascan or Tarasco, is described as a language isolate or small language family. The government of Mexico recognizes three dialectal variants: Lacustrine, spoken near Lake Pátzcuaro; Sierran or Meseta, spoken in the Highlands of northwestern Michoacán; and Cañada, spoken in the Cañada de los Once Pueblos region. Purépecha uses the Latin script, and the official alphabet is called the *PʼURHEPECHA JIMBO KARARAKUECHA*. The language received official status as a national language in 2003.

Purépecha religious traditions combine Indigenous and Catholic elements. The sources name Kurikaweri, the God of the sky and war; Kweawaperi, the Goddess of earth, life, and climate; and Xaratenga, the Goddess of the sea and underworld.
