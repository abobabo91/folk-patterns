## Overview

The Yawalapiti, also known as Jaulapiti, Yaulapiti, or Yawalapití, are an Indigenous tribe in the Amazonian Basin of Brazil. Their current village is situated between the Tuatuari and Kuluene River, in the Xingu Indian Park and Upper Xingu region. Their villages are situated around Lake Ipavu, six kilometres from the Kuluene River. Their population was 156 in 2011, following 237 in 2010 and a low of 25 in 1954. The Yawalapiti live alongside Kiabi, Yudja, and Suya tribes, whose ways of life are described as quite similar despite differences in language. Yawalapiti is a Central Maipuran language of the Maipuran family and is related to Waurá and Mehináku.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state that men paint themselves for ceremonies in the men’s house or at nearby riverbanks, but provide no further description of the materials, designs, or clothing associated with this practice.

### Architecture

The Yawalapiti village is circular, with communal houses surrounding a square called *uikúka*, which is cleared of vegetation. In the center stands the men’s house, where sacred flutes are stored and played. The men’s house resembles the residential houses but has only one or two doors, always smaller than those of the residences, and these doors face the central square. During the day, the flutes may be played only inside the men’s house; at night, after the women have retired, they may be played in the patio.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention arrows in a linguistic example, but provide no catalogue or ethnographic description of Yawalapiti arrow-making.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They state only that men paint themselves for ceremonies.

## Music & performance

The sources identify sacred flutes as an important feature of Yawalapiti village life. These flutes are stored in the men’s house, which is frequented only by men. During the day they may be played inside the house, while at night, after the women have retired, the men can play them in the patio.

The sources also state that men gather in the men’s house or at nearby riverbanks to talk in the twilight and paint themselves for ceremonies. No song genres, instrumental ensembles, dances, or named performance traditions are described.

## Festivals & rituals

The Quarup, also written Kuarup, is a ritual performed to honour deceased tribe members. It is held together with neighbouring tribes.

The sources also describe ceremonial self-painting by men, undertaken in the men’s house or at nearby riverbanks. No further life-cycle rites, annual festivals, initiation ceremonies, weddings, birth observances, or funeral practices are covered. During the COVID-19 pandemic, Watatakalu Yawalapiti, identified as a Chief of the tribe, lost eight direct family members.

## Oral tradition & literature

The available linguistic material includes vocabulary examples such as “countryside,” “my aunt,” “canoe,” “water,” “pequi oil,” “termite,” “head,” “face,” “saliva,” and “arrow,” but does not present these as an oral-literary tradition.

## Language & religion

Yawalapiti is an Arawakan language of Brazil and is classified as a Central Maipuran language within the Maipuran language family. Speakers live in a village along the Tuatuari, a tributary of the Kuluene River, in the southern part of the Xingu Indigenous Park in the state of Mato Grosso. The language is related to Waurá and Mehináku, and the Agavotaguerra, also called Agavotoqueng, reportedly spoke the same language.

The language has oral and nasal vowels, no /e/ sound, and an agglutinative grammatical structure using affixes, especially suffixes. Its constituent order is SVO. The sources describe sacred flutes and the Quarup or Kuarup ritual.
