## Overview

The Saraguros are a people of the Kichwa nation, most of whom live in Saraguro Canton in the Loja Province of southern Ecuador, at intermediate Andean elevations between about 1,800 and 2,800 metres. Ecuador's INEC census of 2010 counted 30,183 people in the canton, a figure that covers both the Indigenous and the non-Indigenous residents. Most Saraguros now speak Spanish, while *Runashimi* or Kichwa, a Quechua dialect, is also spoken and is the object of revitalization work. Oral tradition holds that their ancestors were resettled into the area by the Inca state, and the people are known for having kept much of their land, their customs and their distinctive dress — a black-and-white wool costume whose colours they read as signs of ceremony and nobility rather than of mourning.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Saraguro textiles documented in the sources are wool garments, several of them built from plain rectangles joined by seams, with colour and embroidery carrying the design rather than figurative weaving.

**Anaco** — the *anaco* is a women's garment made, most of the time, of wool; a skirt is worn beneath it, its edge embroidered in designs chosen to match the sash and the blouse.

**Rebozo** — the *rebozo* or cloth is made from sheep's wool and dyed black (blue versions are also now found) and is worn against the cold.

**Poncho** — the black wool *poncho* is men's dress reserved mostly for gala occasions. British Museum examples are built from two rectangles of black wool sewn along their long sides with a head-hole left at the centre; some carry fuchsia stripes, others are joined with contrasting red or green thread, and one is blue cloth with a red stripe running down each long side and the neck opening woven in rather than cut.

**Tunic** — catalogued tunics are a single rectangle of black cloth folded in half and sewn partway down the sides, with a hole cut for the neck; one is stitched with fuchsia wool.

**Zamarro** — the *zamarro* is made of white wool and covers only the front of the body, from the ankles to the waist.

**Wool trousers** — the museum's Saraguro trousers show three treatments: a two-piece white wool garment whose rectangles are sewn to a multicoloured waistband and fastened around the leg with metal snaps at the bottom corners; blue wool trousers with a multicoloured waistband; and black wool trousers lined inside the waistband with white cotton striped in blue. Everyday men's trousers described in the sources are black and reach the knee.

**White wool hat** — the woman's hat is woollen and white with black designs, and can weigh as much as a pound and a half.

**Motif vocabulary.** Black and white — the paired colours of ceremony and nobility, attributed by the Saraguros, following the chronicler Cieza de León, to Inca usage; the black-and-white plumage of the *Curiquingue* (carunculated caracara), a bird symbolic of Inca royalty whose signs still appear on Kapak Raymi costumes; crescent forms, used for earrings and tendrils; the black figures worked on the white hat; and the fuchsia, red and green stripes and seam threads of the ponchos and tunics.

### Clothing & dress

Women wear the *anaco* over an embroidered skirt, with a blouse or shirt whose embroidery now appears in many designs, a girdle that holds both anaco and skirt and is understood to represent women's fertility and the protection of the womb (its designs matching the shirt), the black *rebozo* for warmth, and the heavy white-and-black wool hat. Men wear black knee-length trousers, the white *zamarro* over the front of the legs, a leather belt whose gold knobs and other decoration display the wearer's wealth, and the black wool *poncho* for gala events. Saraguros did not wear shoes until the 1950s; their footwear is now black. Long braided hair is kept by men as a marker that the sources trace to Inca nobility. Black dress read as mourning for Atahualpa is a debunked theory, though black-as-mourning has been adopted, especially by younger generations.

### Architecture

The sources describe no vernacular house form. What they do record is that Saraguros were required by the Spanish to keep a *tambo* — an inn or way-station — on a major communication route, and that their successful argument that running it obliged them to retain their land and its resources helped them hold their territory; they managed the tambo until a motor road reached the area in the 1940s. The settlements lie along the Inca road, the *Kapak Ñan*, which ran from Cuzco to Tumebamba and on to Quito, and Inca ruins stand nearby, although the town of Saraguro itself appears to have been founded by the Spanish. In the twentieth-century celebration of Inca heritage, Inca architecture has been copied.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources name no Saraguro ceramic tradition. Metalwork appears in dress: the *tupu*, a silver jewel with a coloured pearl at its centre, and the gold knobs of the men's leather belt. The British Museum trousers are fastened with metal snaps.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Three forms are documented. The *tupu* is a silver pin set with a coloured pearl, used to fasten the woman's cloth and hung from a chain worn around the neck; within families the tupu (also called the mole) passes from mother to daughter as inheritance down the generations. The *wallka* is a multi-coloured fabric made with beads. Earrings and tendrils take crescent shapes. For hair, the sources record men's long braids, understood as a nobility marker retained from the Inca army's elite soldiers.

## Music & performance

The sources name no Saraguro instrument or song genre; what they document is a contemporary scene. Young, cosmopolitan Saraguro musicians in Ecuador's southern highlands are working to reconstitute a Saraguro Kichwa identity through a creative and selective process in which they discard, amplify and reinvent what they take to be authentic Saraguro musical culture — a break from tradition that signals both their aspiration to lives other than those tradition and stereotype assign them, and their actual opportunity to lead such lives. Greater participation in formal education, occupational diversity and mobility underpin this. The sources also record ways of self-expression that are compatible with Indigenous self-identification without depending on ties to an Inkaic past: a large number of Saraguro youth, primarily male, affiliate with a heavy metal subculture, listening to national and international metal bands, playing in local bands and organising local concerts. Musicians are among the occupations contemporary Saraguros hold.

## Festivals & rituals

The one festival named in the sources is **Kapak Raymi**, the Great Celebration, held in Saraguro, at which costumes continue to carry the symbols of the *Curiquingue*, the black-and-white carunculated caracara that inhabits the Saraguro parish and páramos and was a bird of Inca royalty. Twentieth-century Saraguros celebrate their Inca heritage more broadly: schools have been named after Inca emperors and Inca customs recreated. Floral offerings are treated as a living form of Saraguro spiritual culture and resistance, and are the subject of Sisa Pacari Bacacela Gualan's *Cultura espiritual: Una resistencia de los Saraguros en la actualidad: Las Ofrendas Florales*. Indigenous justice is exercised, in Carmen Lozano's account of her own work, to harmonize the balance of life within the community. The sources record no life-cycle rites, except that the *tupu* passes from mother to daughter as an inheritance.

## Foodways

The sources record that Saraguros are traditionally farmers and livestock herders, that sheep's wool supplies their cloth, that many continue agro-pastoralist activities alongside professional careers, and that Saraguro activists and intellectuals campaign for food sovereignty.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral tradition is the principal source for Saraguro origins: it holds that the ancestors of the present-day people were moved to Saraguro by the Incas from other areas, possibly from the Colla or other peoples of the Lake Titicaca and Cuzco regions, under the forced-resettlement policy called *mitma*, and it transmits — alongside historical records — the attribution of the black and white of Saraguro clothing to ceremony and nobility. Written and documentary work by Saraguro intellectuals is substantial. Sisa Pacari Bacacela Gualan is the author of *Ecuador Chinchasuyupi Quichuacunapac Ñaupa Rimai = Literatura Indígena En Los Andes Del Sur Del Ecuador*, of the study of floral offerings, and of *El Quinto Gobernador de los Saraguros: Historia Social y Organizativa*. Luis Fernando Sarango Macas wrote *The Education Paradigm of Abya Yala*. Inti Cartuche Vacacela has published academic articles on interculturality and plurinationality. The 2010 film *Saraguro: Historia Escrita con Sangre Inka* traces the people's origins, combining anthropological data with Inkan cosmovision in consultation with the Saraguros themselves.

## Language & religion

The Saraguros belong to the Kichwa nation. Most now speak Spanish; *Runashimi* or Kichwa, a Quechua dialect, is also spoken, and language revitalization efforts are being implemented, with the preservation of Kichwa named among the ways the people assert their Inca heritage. Institutional support for the language and for Indigenous education includes the Pluriversity "Amawtay Wasi" and the Coordination of Organizations of the Kichwa Saraguro People (CORPUKIS, within ECUARUNARI and CONAIE), and Bacacela Gualan has served as Secretary for Bilingual Education in the Unión Nacional de Educadores del Ecuador. On religion the sources are nearly silent: they document spiritual culture only through floral offerings, described as a contemporary Saraguro resistance.
