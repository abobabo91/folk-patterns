## Overview

The Chorotí — also recorded as *Chorote*, *Yofúaha* and *Tsoloti* — are an indigenous people of northwestern Argentina, with communities extending into Paraguay and the far southeast of Bolivia. Their speech forms a small group of closely related language varieties within the Matacoan family, whose nearest relative is Wichí; Ethnologue (Gordon 2005) treats Chorote as two distinct languages rather than one. The sources used here give no population figure and no further detail on the specific river valleys or settlements involved. In folk-culture terms the Chorotí are documented above all through a body of museum-held objects built from the materials of the dry Chaco environment: *chaguar* plant fibre, gourd, bone, wood, reed, leather, shell and feathers, worked into netted bags, whistles, rattles, water vessels and body ornaments.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The catalogue records behind this atlas document three fibre traditions and no named vernacular terms for them beyond the fibre itself.

**Chaguar netting** — Net bags (four examples in this collection) are worked from *chaguar*, the plant fibre that is the Chorotí textile staple; the same fibre is spun into string for a fishing-net.

**Chaguar tunic** — A tunic recorded as a war shirt is made of *chaguar*, placing the fibre in ceremonial and male dress as well as in utility carrying gear.

**Striped wool cloak** — A cloak built from two rectangles of wool, each woven with brown and beige stripes, sewn together lengthwise with the edges folded over and hemmed. A separate bag is also made of wool, so both a plant-fibre and an animal-fibre line run through the same repertoire.

**Motif vocabulary.** The decoration the catalogue names is geometric rather than figurative: incised geometric designs on a gourd rattle, incised triangles ringing a wooden whistle around a raised central circle, a row of triangles cut down the shaft of a turkey-feather head ornament, and brown-and-beige woven stripes on the wool cloak. The sources give no vernacular names for any of these.

### Clothing & dress

Chorotí dress in this collection is built from *chaguar*, wool, leather, cloth and shell. The *chaguar* tunic or war shirt is the one garment recorded with a ceremonial or martial function. The wool cloak — two striped panels joined lengthwise — is the outer garment. A belt is made of leather and carries a fringe. A cap combines cloth with shell, the same shell that appears in necklaces and on an ornamented whistle band, so the material crosses from adornment onto headwear. The sources distinguish neither a men's from a women's wardrobe nor everyday from festival dress beyond the war shirt, and they record no footwear.

### Architecture

The sources used for this profile say nothing about Chorotí house form, building materials or settlement layout.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Chorotí pottery is represented by water vessels: bottle-jars of pottery combined with cord, a pairing that suggests they were made to be carried or suspended rather than set down. No metalwork appears in the collection at all. Gourd carries the household repertoire instead — a gourd spoon and a gourd water scoop — and the same material is used for the rattles described below, so a single cultivated container plant serves eating, drawing water and sound-making. Tools are worked from bone, wood and fish parts: a bone awl that doubled as a tattooing instrument, a male fire-stick of wood, and a lice comb made from the lower jawbone of a fish. Cordage and netting extend to a fishing-net of plant-fibre string.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ornament is drawn from feathers, down, shell, wood, leaf and skin. Head ornaments include one of two turkey feathers joined at the shaft ends, with most of each vane cut away to leave a row of triangles along the shaft; one of purple feathers on thread; and a circlet made of leaf. Anklets come as a pair of circlets of down, cloth and vegetable fibre. A necklace is strung with equal-sized circular shell discs on a single fibre strand. Ear-plugs are a pair of wooden cylinders, each with a hole carved in one side, and a finger ring is made of lizard skin. Imported glass beads — red, pink and blue — appear alongside round shell beads on the fibre band of a breast whistle. The bone awl recorded as a tattooing instrument is the collection's only evidence of body marking.

## Music & performance

Chorotí instruments in this collection are aerophones and rattles, with no strings and no drums recorded. Three whistles survive: one of bone with a single hole carved near one end; one of reed, with a mouthpiece cut near the top, three holes on the lower half and the top end sealed with resin; and one of wood, worn on the breast, an ovoid with a hole in the base, one face incised with triangles around a raised central circle, hung from a fibre band beaded with glass and shell. Two gourd rattles are catalogued as death dancer's rattles, one of them incised with geometric designs and repaired or fitted with a plastic button fixed underneath with resin — a modern material on an otherwise wholly local instrument. That designation is the only performance context the sources supply: a rattle carried by a dancer in a mortuary rite. The sources name no song genres and no other performance occasions.

## Dance & theatre

Beyond the two gourd rattles identified as the property of a death dancer, the sources used here document no Chorotí dance or dramatic tradition by name.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used for this profile give no festival calendar and no life-cycle rites. The only ritual thread they carry is material: the death dancer's rattles, which attach a gourd instrument to mortuary performance, and the bone tattooing awl, which implies a body-marking practice whose occasion is not recorded.

## Foodways

The sources used here say nothing about Chorotí staples, cooking or dishes. What they show is the equipment: a gourd spoon, a gourd water scoop, pottery bottle-jars rigged with cord for water, a wooden fire-stick, and a plant-fibre fishing-net — so fishing and the transport of water are the two food-related activities the objects attest.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used for this profile record no Chorotí folktales, epic, proverbs or storytelling contexts, and no documentation or revival projects.

## Language & religion

Chorote belongs to the Matacoan family and stands closest within it to Wichí. Ethnologue's 2005 edition splits it into two languages, so what is called Chorotí is a small cluster of varieties rather than a single standard, spoken chiefly in northwestern Argentina and across the borders into Paraguay and far-southeastern Bolivia. *Yofúaha* and *Tsoloti* are recorded as alternative names for the same speech. No historical script is attested. The sources used here say nothing about the present religious landscape or about spiritual practice, except that the gourd rattles are catalogued as belonging to a death dancer.
