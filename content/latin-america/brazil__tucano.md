## Overview

The Tucano are an Indigenous Amazonian people living along the Vaupés River whose social structure is built on marriage between different language groups. Men marry women who speak other languages and their wives move into the husband's longhouse, creating households where multiple languages are spoken daily. They practice swidden farming, hunting, fishing and gathering, and are documented primarily through expedition photographs and museum collections from 1903–1925. Their material culture, music and dance center on festival celebrations, particularly for the ripening of the bacába palm fruit.

## Material culture

Tucano material culture is made from forest and hunt materials: bark cloth, feathered ornaments, bone instruments, black clay vessels, and woven plant fibres.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Textiles are decorated with geometric designs in pigment or beads, and woven from bark, bast, palm leaf and plant fibre.

- **Bark cloth** — A rectangle with wavy texture decorated with geometric designs in red pigment.
- **Painted bast dance skirts** (*dansskört*) — Cloth skirts with painted ornament worn for dancing.
- **Beaded dance skirts** — Dance skirts made entirely of glass beads.
- **Feather-and-fibre headbands** — Woven fibre bands with two rows of yellow, red and white feathers and a fibre tumpline.
- **Palm-leaf boxes** — Lidded boxes woven from borassus palm leaves, tied together with tucum string.

### Clothing & dress

Festival dress is fully documented through feathered head ornaments, dance skirts and decorative ropes; everyday clothing is not described.

- **Feather browband** (*uká*) — A band holding a spray of fine heron feathers at the back and a white down ornament at the lower edge.
- **Monkey hair rope** — A rope fastened to the back of a festival head-dress, finished with tassels of palm-fruit shell hanging down the back.
- **Straw ring head ornament** — A straw ring set with feathers.
- **Feather head-dress** — A head ornament collected at Taracuá.

### Architecture

Tucano live in longhouses where women move in on marriage, creating multilingual households.

- **Longhouse** — Multi-family dwelling hosting several languages and extended family groups.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Black unglazed pottery, carved wood and bone are used for cooking, food service, hunting and grooming.

- **Black waisted pot** — A clay pot from Rio Papurí with remains of carayarú pigment for healing and magic.
- **Quiver** — A pitch-coated container holding 13 poison darts for hunting with blowguns.
- **Dart wadding case** — A cane case holding samaúma tree silk for winding around blowgun dart butts.
- **Plaited comb** — A double-sided comb with plaited plant-fibre centre, once decorated with feathers at the short ends.
- **Painted wooden stool** — A piece of carved woodwork with painted surface.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Body ornaments are made from seeds, shells, quartz, feathers and fibre, worn as necklaces, arm ornaments and dance pendants.

- **Seed necklace** — Necklaces strung from seeds.
- **Quartz neck ornament** — A drilled cylinder of carved quartz threaded on fibre cord and hung with seed cases.
- **Feather wristlet** — An arm ornament with a pendant of feather and shell.
- **Dance ornament** — Four beaded cords hanging from a fibre loop, each ending in a black conical fruit shell with multicoloured parrot feathers.
- **Carayarú body paint** (*carayarú*) — Paint from Bignonia chica plant credited with healing and magical power.

## Music & performance

Instruments are small and made from forest and hunt materials: bone flutes, pan flutes, drums, rattles and shell percussion.

- **Pan flute** — Small flutes collected from Rio Tiquié and Rio Papurí, including one sized for a boy.
- **Bone flute pipe** — End-blown flute cut from deer bone or monkey bone.
- **Bone whistle** — A whistle from Taracuá.
- **Dance drum** — Percussion drum collected on Rio Papurí.
- **Tortoise shell percussion** — A shell instrument with one end closed with beeswax, used as a musical instrument.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is the main performance form, using carved sticks, feathered ornaments and rattles, with documented ring games at festivals.

- **Dancing stick** — A V-shaped carved piece with a flat blade lashed to the shorter arm, hung over the left shoulder while dancing.
- **Dance staff ornament** — Separate ornaments made for dance staffs, collected at Taracuá.
- **Ring game** — A game played during the bacába festival at Juquirára.

## Festivals & rituals

The main festival celebrates the ripening of the bacába palm fruit with processions of dancers, ring games and appearance of the chief in ceremonial dress.

- **Bacába palm festival** (*bacába*) — Celebration when the fruit ripens, held at Juquirára on 22 May 1924, featuring fruit bearers dancing and ring games.
- **Dance feast** — A prolonged celebration at Pary-Cachoeira on Rio Tiquié lasting long enough to require meal breaks.
- **Linguistic exogamy** — The rule that men marry women who speak other languages, considered incestuous to marry within one's own language group.
- **Carayarú paint ritual use** (*carayarú*) — A substance held to have healing and magical power in ritual contexts.

## Foodways

The Tucano are swidden farmers who hunt, fish and gather, using blowguns with poison darts and black clay vessels for cooking.

- **Blowgun hunting** — Hunting with poison darts stored in pitch-coated quivers.
- **Swidden farming** — Clearing forest patches to grow manioc and other staples.
- **Bacába palm fruit harvest** (*bacába*) — Wild fruit gathered in quantity and carried in baskets to festivals.
- **Fishing** — A documented food source with large catfish caught among neighbouring Arapáso.

## Oral tradition & literature

The outstanding document is a series of twelve watercolours by Tucano artist Feliciano Lana depicting the Tukano origin narrative.

- **Feliciano Lana watercolours** — Twelve paintings by a Tucano artist in the British Museum rendering the origin story from the Upper Rio Negro.
- **Multilingual speech** — Speakers move between languages within single conversations so naturally they barely notice they are doing it.
- **Tucanoan Languages Collection** — Audio recordings, transcriptions, translations and field notes assembled by Janet Chernela, archived at AILLA.

## Language & religion

Tucano is an endangered Tucanoan language; most speakers are multilingual due to linguistic exogamy, and descent groups marry outside their own language.

- **Tucano language** (*yeʼpâ-masa yee uúku͂sehé*) — A Tucanoan language of Amazonas in Brazil and Colombia, with distinctive prenasalisation of voiced stops.
- **Bara Tukano** — A named descent group practising linguistic exogamy.
- **Barasana** — A named descent group within the Tucano cluster.
- **Desana** — A named descent group within the Tucano cluster.
- **Wanano** — A named descent group within the Tucano cluster with documented masked performance traditions.

## Glossary

- *ye'pâ-masa* — Tucano word for plural
- *yeʼpâ-masa yee uúku͂sehé* — The Tucano language's name for itself
- *bacába* — A palm tree whose ripening fruit is celebrated with festivals
- *carayarú* — Paint from Bignonia chica plant with healing and magical power
- *uká* — A feather browband ornament
- *dansskört* — Dance skirt of bast cloth with painted ornament
- *tucum* — String used to tie palm-leaf box lids
- *samaúma* — Tree silk used for winding around blowgun dart butts
- *linguistic exogamy* — Marriage practice where men marry women from other language groups
- *swidden* — Shifting agriculture where forest patches are cleared temporarily for crops
- *Tucanoan* — A language family of northwestern Amazon
- *longhouse* — Multi-family dwelling where women move in on marriage
- *descent group* — A named kinship group such as Bara Tukano or Desana
- *bark cloth* — Textile beaten from bark with geometric painted designs
- *tumpline* — A line for carrying or securing items
