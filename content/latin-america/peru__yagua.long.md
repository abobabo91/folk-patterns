## Overview

The Yagua are an indigenous people of northeastern Peru and adjacent Colombia, numbering roughly 6,000. They live along the Amazon, Napo, Putumayo and Yavarí rivers and their tributaries, dispersed across some thirty communities in a block of Amazon basin about 200 miles wide and 350 miles long, running south from the second to the fifth parallel and west from the 70th to the 75th meridian; since the early 2000s some families have moved north into Colombia near Leticia. Their language belongs to the small Peba–Yaguan family. In folk-culture terms the Yagua are known above all for palm-fibre technology — hammocks, carrying nets, bags, whole garments and dart quivers worked from *chambira* and other palm fibres — and for the blowgun and curare-tipped dart. Villages are small, 2 to 30 families, and native arts and crafts remain a significant part of household income.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Chambira fibre work** — The dominant fibre craft in the museum record is spun palm fibre identified in the catalogues as *chambira*, used for hammocks (photographed in use on the Río Ampiyacu in 1941) and for the netted carrying bags, or *bärnät*, that women use to move produce and household loads.

**Palm-leaf dart quiver (*koger*)** — A 30-cm quiver of palm leaf holds a stick around which blowgun darts are wound and lashed with *chambira* thread at top and bottom, so that the stored darts resemble, in Paul Fejos's phrase, "a Venetian blind or a machine gun belt". A separate container of fibre yarn belongs to the same three-part set, described and illustrated in Fejos's *Ethnology of the Yagua* (1943, p. 50).

**Netted bags** — A British Museum example is made of string and cotton, indicating that spun cotton as well as palm fibre entered Yagua bag-making.

**Mauritia and Astrocaryum fibre garments** — Fibre drawn from *Mauritia flexuosa* was worked into complete men's costumes, while women's neck ornaments were made from *Astrocaryum* palm fibre; the 1941 photographs document both the making and the wearing.

The sources name no motif vocabulary for these textiles.

### Clothing & dress

Palm fibre, not cloth, defines Yagua dress in the documented record. One of the two proposed etymologies of the name *yagua* is the Spanish word for 'royal palm', applied by Spanish explorers precisely because so much Yagua clothing is made of palm fibre. Photographs taken by Paul Fejos on the Río Ampiyacu in 1941 show complete men's costumes made of fibres of *Mauritia flexuosa*, and the manufacture and wearing of a women's neck ornament of *Astrocaryum* palm fibre. A pair of arm ornaments collected by Günter Tessmann in the Ucayali region, acquired by the Göteborg museum in 1930, is the earliest dated dress item in the collection. Face painting with *achiote*, the blood-red seed of the annatto plant (*Bixa orellana*), is a Yagua custom old and conspicuous enough that it underlies the competing etymology of the group's name from Quechua *yawar* 'blood', or *yawar runa*, 'the blood-red people'. The same 1941 series records the use of special walking staffs. The sources describe no footwear, belts or head coverings.

### Architecture

The sources used do not describe Yagua house form, materials or decoration; dwellings appear only incidentally, as huts in the background of the 1941 expedition photographs.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The household inventory documented here is of clay, wood, palm and fibre, with no metalwork. A cooking vessel of fired clay called *apati* appears in a 1941 photograph of a woman cooking, and was collected alongside it. Grinding was done in a wooden trough of *quinilla* wood, photographed in use for pounding yucca (manioc). Hunting equipment is the best-represented class: the blowgun itself, with its separate blowgun cleaner, and darts made of leaf midrib, some of them poisoned — Fejos identifies the poison as curare. The quiver set described above completes the kit. Fibre hammocks, netted carrying bags and walking staffs round out the everyday objects, most of them collected by Fejos during the Wenner-Gren expedition of 1940–41 among the Wuisana ("Ants") subgroup of the Yagua.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment in the record is fibre and pigment rather than metal or bead. Women's neck ornaments were made of *Astrocaryum* palm fibre, and their manufacture as well as their wearing was photographed in 1941. A pair of arm ornaments from the Ucayali region, collected by Günter Tessmann and entering the Göteborg collection in 1930, is the only other ornament type documented. Body paint is attested indirectly but firmly through the etymology of the group's name: the Yagua paint their faces with *achiote*, the blood-red seeds of *Bixa orellana*, a practice conspicuous enough that Quechua speakers may have called them *yawar runa*, 'the blood-red people'. The sources mention no tattooing, hair practices or metal jewellery.

## Music & performance

Instrumental practice appears in the sources through a single documented instrument: a panpipe, photographed in use among the Wuisana subgroup on the Río Ampiyacu during the Wenner-Gren expedition of 1941, with the specimen itself sent to the Museum of the American Indian in New York.

## Festivals & rituals

The clearest ritual evidence in the museum record is a set of balsa-wood figures made in memory of ancestors — three male and three female — photographed by Fejos on the Río Ampiyacu in 1941 among the Wuisana and documented in his original labels for the collection sent to the Museum of the American Indian. Ritual specialists are attested through the Yagua word *rimyurá* 'shaman', which may lie behind the name Dirimara, one of two chiefs seized by Francisco de Orellana near present-day Pebas in January 1542; the other, Aparia, matches both a village name and the Yagua clan name *(j)ápiiryá* 'red macaw clan'. Clan names of this kind are still used by many Yagua as family names, which makes the clan the one social-ritual institution the sources name directly. Religious history is largely a history of outside missions: regular European contact began in 1686 with the Jesuit mission of San Joaquín de los Omaguas, on an island in the Amazon probably near the mouth of the Ampiyacu, and from the seventeenth century to the later nineteenth contact ran mainly through Jesuit and Franciscan missionaries.

## Foodways

Manioc is the staple the sources document: a 1941 photograph records a trough of *quinilla* wood used for grinding "yucca (= manio[c])", and the trough was collected with its accompanying labels. Cooking was done in fired clay, including the vessel called *apati* photographed in use by a Yagua woman. Protein came from blowgun hunting, and the Yagua technology for it is unusually well documented: blowgun, blowgun cleaner, and darts of leaf midrib carried in a palm-leaf quiver, a portion of them poisoned with curare. Carrying nets of *chambira* fibre moved food and other loads between garden, forest and village.

## Oral tradition & literature

Yagua narrative was told at night, when conditions were not conducive to sleep and listeners were not preoccupied with other matters. Most stories open with a formula attributing them to a dead relative — "My deceased father (mother, grandfather, grandmother etc.) used to tell me" — a framing that lends the telling its claim to truth. The corpus has been collected and analysed in two book-length studies: Paul Powlison's *Yagua Mythology: Epic Tendencies in a New World Mythology* (1985), whose title reflects his argument that the narratives tend toward epic, and Thomas E. Payne's *The Twins Stories: Participant Coding in Yagua Narrative* (University of California Press), a linguistic study of how speakers track characters across a single story cycle. Ethnographic description rests on Fejos (1943) and P. Powlison (1985); the group's history and migrations are treated by Chaumeil (1983).

## Language & religion

Yagua is a branch of the Peba–Yaguan family; its only documented close relatives, Peba and Yameo, are both extinct. The language has six vowels, both oral and nasal, and eleven consonants; it is highly agglutinative, with words built from many morphemes and sometimes more than one root. Clauses are verb-initial (VSO), there are numerous postpositions and no prepositions, over forty noun classifiers and essentially no adjectives — adjective-like nouns do the work instead, with the head of a phrase determined by which noun persists in the following discourse. Counting is quinary, with separate number words for animate and inanimate reference. Around 2000, some 2,000 Yagua in Peru were monolingual, three quarters of them women; most of the rest are bilingual in Spanish to varying degrees, and Yagua is the language of instruction in local primary schools. The one plausible self-referent is *nijyąąmíy* 'people', used in contrast with *mááy* 'white people' and *munuñúmiy* 'savages, enemies, non-Yaguas'; the group is also known as Llagua, Nijyamïï Nikyejaada, Yahua, Yava and Yegua. Quechua loanwords outnumber Spanish ones in Yagua, which has been read either as evidence of pre-conquest contact with the Incas or as a legacy of Spanish missionaries imposing Quechua as a common language.
