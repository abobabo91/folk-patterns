## Overview

The Sápara, also known as Zápara or Záparo, are an indigenous people native to the Amazon rainforest along the border of Ecuador and Peru. Their former territory extended between the Napo River and the Pastaza, while the Sápara Nation of Ecuador has territory between the Pindoyacu, Conambo and Alto Corrientes rivers in Pastaza province. The head office of the Sápara Nation is in the city of Shell, Pastaza. The organization is officially known as Nación Sápara del Ecuador (NASE), and its legal status as an autonomous or self-governing nation was confirmed by CONDENPE. Early in the 20th century, there were some 200,000 Zapara, but their numbers later declined severely. The Sápara are significant in folk-culture studies because their language, oral heritage and cultural manifestations have been recognized in international heritage discussions, while their territory and way of life face pressure from oil development.

## Material culture

### Architecture

 The Wikipedia material mentions a hamlet on a river inhabited by Pedro Ernesto Santi and his family, but gives no architectural description of it.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do record blowguns and bamboo darts as hunting equipment, and describe fishing in the rivers of the jungle home.

## Music & performance

 They do mention oral heritage and cultural manifestations, and describe the Záparo language as an endangered language with only a very small number of elderly speakers retaining some command of it. The sources therefore provide no basis for identifying particular musical forms or instruments.

The source material lists two documentaries concerning Sápara life and cultural survival. *Talking with Fish and Birds – The Záparo Indians in the Ecuadorian Jungle* is a documentary directed by Rainer Simon, with cinematography by Frank Sputh, produced in Germany in 1999. *The Last Guardians* is a social and environmental documentary by Tierra Productions, Joe Tucker and Adam Punzano. It concerns the Sápara and Kichwa Nations in the Ecuadorian Amazon and presents their struggle to preserve their culture and way of life against the encroachment of oil companies.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do record political and juridical gatherings connected with territorial defense. The Sápara Nation organized a congress in the Community of Torimbo, where it nominated native leaders. The nomination was ratified through an Indigenous Sentence in the Community of the Jandiayacu, with Sápara elders serving as judges. The sentence was recognized by CONAIE, CONFENIAE, the Sápara government and CODENPE.

The sources also describe the Sápara struggle against extractive industry. The Sápara, together with the Kichwa and Shiwiar, expressed opposition to oil exploitation in their territory through assemblies, congresses and indigenous declarations. Gloria Ushigua, president of the Sápara Women’s association, Ashinwaka, was described as leading efforts to protect Sápara territory.

## Foodways

The sources describe palm hearts as the Sápara’s main vegetable. They also state that Sápara people fished the many rivers of their jungle home and hunted tapirs, peccaries, wood-quail and curassows with blowguns and bamboo darts. They did not hunt spider monkeys because they believed those animals to be their ancestors.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They do, however, connect Sápara cultural significance with oral heritage and cultural manifestations. UNESCO declared the Záparo language an “Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity” in 2001, according to the Wikipedia source.

The Záparo language has undergone a severe decline. The language article states that it was spoken by only one person out of a total population of 170 in Pastaza Province in 2000, while the Sápara article states that only four Sápara people, all aged over 70, still spoke the language with some degree of fluency. The oldest surviving Zaparo-speaker identified in the source is Pedro Ernesto Santi, described as about 80 years old and living with his family in a hamlet on the river.

A language revival effort is underway. The Sápara have collaborated with Juan Casco, an Ecuadorian type designer, in discussions concerning a syllabary system for writing the language. Brenda J. Bowser has also worked to document the linguistic and oral history of the Záparo of Ecuadorian Amazonia.

## Language & religion

Záparo is described as a nearly extinct language spoken by the Sápara, or Záparo, people of Ecuador. It is also known as Zápara and Kayapwe. The language article states that Sápara people now speak Quichua, while Záparo is distinct from Andoa. It describes Záparo as having subject–verb–object word order, four vowels and 14 consonants. The Sápara article explains that the Sápara alphabet does not contain the character Z and ends with W; this information was attributed to Bartolo Ushigua Santi on December 10, 2009.

The sources state that the use of Záparo was progressively discontinued in favor of Quechua after the disruption caused by the rubber boom and intermarriage with neighboring tribes. They do record the belief that spider monkeys were ancestors and mention Sápara elders serving as judges in an Indigenous Sentence.
