## Overview

The Kulina are an Indigenous people of Brazil and Peru. In Brazil, 2,540 Kulina live in Amazonas and Acre; in southeastern Peru, 400 live along the Purus and Santa Rosa Rivers. Their traditional settlements are also described along the Purus and Envira rivers in Acre and Ucayali, and around the Juruá, Tarauacá and Jutaí rivers in Amazonas. The people are also known as Corina, Culina, Kulína, Kulyna, Madihá and Madija. Their language belongs to the Arawan family and is spoken by about 4,000 Kulina people. Kulina is considered a threatened language, while parts of the Bible have been translated into it. The available sources document the group primarily through population, geography and language rather than through material or intangible cultural practices.

## Language & religion

Kulina is an Arawan language of Brazil and Peru. It is also called Kulína, Kulyna, Culina, Curina, Corina, Korina, Culina-Madijá, Madijá, Madija, Madiha, Madihá and Madija. The language is traditionally spoken in Acre and Amazonas in Brazil and in the Ucayali region of Peru. In Acre and Ucayali, villages are found along the Purus and Envira rivers; in Amazonas, they are around the Juruá, Tarauacá and Jutaí rivers. According to Dienst (2014), Kulina forms a Madihá dialect continuum with Western Jamamadi and Deni. The term *madihá* means “people” in all of these languages. Kulina is similar to Deni, and the two have been considered different dialects of the same language. Its basic constituent order is subject–object–verb; it is predominantly head-marking, agglutinative and head-final, with many more suffixes than prefixes.
