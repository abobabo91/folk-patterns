## Overview

The Kambeba, also known as the Omagua, Umawa, and Cambeba, are an Indigenous people of Brazil’s Amazon Basin. Their historical territory lay along the Amazon River upstream from the present-day city of Manaus and extended into Peru. In Brazil, communities live on the middle and upper Solimões in Amazonas, including lands predominantly occupied by the Ticunas, with smaller groups in Manaus; populations are also divided between Brazil and Peru. The sources describe a population estimated at around 1,500 in Brazil and approximately 3,500 near Nauta, Peru, while an official 2002 census identified 325 individuals in Brazil. The Omagua language may derive from Tupi-Guarani, although its precise history is disputed. Kambeba history is significant for its large pre-Columbian settlements, complex political organization, woven clothing, canoe use, agriculture, and distinctive body practices.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Woven cotton clothing* — Omagua men and women wove cotton garments for their own use and for exchange with neighboring peoples. The cloth could be woven in different colours or painted so precisely that the two techniques were difficult to distinguish.

**Multi-colored cotton cloth** — Samuel Fritz described men’s cotton breeches and shirts and women’s cotton garments. The sources distinguish these textiles by their colourful woven or painted surfaces and by their circulation in trade with neighboring communities.

**Cloth traded with neighbors** — Omagua weaving included items made beyond immediate clothing needs. These objects were described as “very beautiful cloth” and formed part of exchange relationships along the Amazon.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs or pattern terms.

### Clothing & dress

Men wore multi-colored cotton clothing, including breeches and shirts. Women wore two cotton pieces: one served as a small apron and the other provided an indifferent covering for the breasts. Cristóbal de Acuña described both men and women as decently dressed and emphasized that they produced woven and painted cloth for themselves and for trade.

Ceremonial dress is described in connection with the puberty ritual for girls. After a period in a hammock, a girl was washed, painted from the face to the middle of the body, and sent home naked to be adorned with feathers. The community celebrated her new womanhood with music and dancing.

### Architecture

The sources describe tall houses with elevated bark floors, adapted to seasonal flooding along the Amazon. Omagua people stored maize and other fruits in the high parts of their houses, while canoes provided movement between places during floods. Manioc was stored underground in specially designed pits to protect it from floodwater until the next planting season. The sources also report that Omagua settlements once included many villages and that some communities were located on islands as a means of self-defense.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Cambeba ceramics were found in archaeological levels associated with semi-domesticated orchards and areas enriched with terra preta. These discoveries have been used to support the interpretation that a large and organized civilization existed before European contact. Everyday objects named in the sources include canoes, underground storage pits, elevated bark floors, presses used in head-flattening, small boards, larger supporting boards, lances, blowguns, boqueteras, and small sticks used in a puberty ceremony.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources describe body painting rather than jewelry. Men and women painted large portions of their bodies, faces, and even their hair with the juice of a forest fruit called *jagua*, described as darker than mulberry. During the puberty ceremony, girls were painted from the face to the middle of the body and adorned with feathers. Newborn children underwent head-flattening: the forehead was compressed with a small board and the skull with a larger board that supported the body. This practice was associated with the name Cambeba and was intended to distinguish the group from neighboring peoples.

## Music & performance

The sources mention music and dancing in the communal celebration that followed a girl’s puberty ceremony. After being washed, painted, and adorned with feathers, she was welcomed into her new womanhood by the community with music and dancing. Other women gave her small quantities of manioc beer, and the oldest man in the village struck her shoulders with a small stick while giving her the names she would bear for the rest of her life.

The sources also describe Omagua social and political life, including their obedience to principal caciques and their treatment of men and women with consideration. They state that Gaspar de Carvajal recorded information about Omagua culture, diet, housing, settlement patterns, and political structure, while Samuel Fritz supplied later observations. No specific musical tradition is identified beyond music performed during the puberty ceremony.

## Dance & theatre

The sources identify dancing as part of the communal celebration accompanying girls’ transition into womanhood.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe a puberty ritual for girls. When a girl reached puberty, she was placed in a hammock under an awning fixed to the top of the house and kept there for a month with little water, dry farinha, and cotton for spinning. At the end of this period, she was taken down, carried to the river, washed, painted, and sent home naked to be adorned with feathers. The community celebrated with music and dancing, and the other women gave her small quantities of manioc beer.

The oldest man in the village then struck her shoulders with a small stick and gave her the names she would bear for the rest of her life. After this ritual, men were allowed to ask for the girls as wives.

## Foodways

The Kambeba cultivated manioc, maize, and other crops on island mud-flats and in swiddens. Manioc was stored underground in specially designed pits so that it would remain protected from floodwater and could be eaten during the next planting season. Maize and other fruits of the soil were hung in the high parts of houses for preservation. Manioc beer was consumed by women during the puberty ceremony.

The sources describe hunting and fishing with lances, blowguns, and boqueteras. The boquetera was a sling that discharged hard clay bullets and was used to kill manatees, river turtles, and pirarucú fish. The sources also record historical accusations and observations concerning human flesh, including a statement that the Kambeba were associated with the use of human skulls as trophies; later visitors were told that the group had abandoned human flesh consumption.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do identify several written accounts by European observers, including Gaspar de Carvajal’s *Relación del nuevo descubrimiento del famoso río Grande que descubrió por muy gran ventura el capitán Francisco de Orellana*, Cristóbal de Acuña’s descriptions of the Amazon and the Omagua, and Samuel Fritz’s observations from the period when he lived in the Amazon.

Missionaries also produced religious texts in Omagua. In the early mission period, a bilingual Quechua-Cambeba translator assisted in producing ecclesiastical texts, including a catechism. Samuel Fritz later developed his own Cambeba catechism. The sources state that Cambeba is now spoken fluently only by tribal elders on formal occasions, in meetings, and in some school classes.

## Language & religion

The Omagua language is spoken by the group. Some linguists argue that it derives from Tupi-Guarani and became distinct relatively recently, while other evidence indicates that Omagua and the closely related Kokama language already existed in forms similar to their modern forms when European missionaries arrived in Maynas in the seventeenth century. Language use declined through the schooling of young people, and Cambeba is now spoken fluently mainly by tribal elders in formal settings, meetings, and some school classes.

Jesuit and Franciscan missionaries sought to convert the Omagua to Christianity. Samuel Fritz arrived in 1687, produced a Cambeba catechism, baptized mainly children, and concentrated Indigenous communities into Jesuit reductions. The sources describe resistance among some adults to abandoning certain practices. Missionary activity continued until the Portuguese expulsion of the Jesuits in 1767.
