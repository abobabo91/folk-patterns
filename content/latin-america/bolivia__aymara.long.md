## Overview

The Aymara are an Indigenous Andean people concentrated around Bolivia’s Altiplano, Lake Titicaca, the basin of Lake Poopó, and the high valleys descending toward La Paz and the Yungas. Aymara communities also live in Peru, northern Chile, and northwest Argentina; approximately 2.3 million people identify as Aymara across these countries, with the largest population in Bolivia. Their language, Aymara, belongs to the small Aymaran language family and is closely associated with Jaqaru and the nearly extinct Kawki. Aymara society has been shaped by the *ayllu*, a local kin and landholding community, and by long adaptation to high-altitude agriculture, camelid herding, and seasonal exchange. Their visual culture is especially notable for brightly colored woven textiles, geometric patterning, ritual objects, coca use, and a syncretic religious life linking Catholic feast days with Indigenous understandings of mountains, ancestors, agriculture, and the natural environment.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

***Awayu* / *aguayo*** — A rectangular carrying cloth woven on a horizontal peg loom from sheep, alpaca, llama wool, or cotton. Women carry infants, firewood, produce, and other goods in it; bright stripes and geometric bands distinguish it from a merely utilitarian blanket.

***Ch’ullu*** — A knitted wool cap with ear flaps and ties, worn especially in cold highland environments. Ch’ullus are often brightly patterned and may indicate regional or community identity.

***Pollera*** — A full, gathered women’s skirt, commonly made in layered or brightly colored fabric. In Aymara urban and rural dress it is worn with a shawl or *awayu*, and its form is distinct from the men’s poncho-centered ensemble.

***Tupu*** — A large metal garment pin used to fasten a woman’s shawl or carrying cloth. The tupu is both functional and ornamental, and historically belongs to a wider Andean tradition of dress fasteners.

***Poncho*** — A rectangular wool garment made from joined woven panels. Aymara ponchos commonly use strong stripes, contrasting borders, and geometric organization rather than pictorial imagery.

Aymara weavers spin alpaca and llama wool, dye fibers with vegetable and mineral substances, and use horizontal peg looms. Traditional garments such as carrying cloths and ponchos are constructed by joining two woven pieces, since broad looms are not characteristic of the tradition.

**Motif vocabulary.** Aymara cloths use bright horizontal stripes and repeated geometric figures; some depict camelids and other features of highland life. The *Wiphala*, a seven-color banner, is a symbol of Aymara and wider Indigenous rights.

### Clothing & dress

Aymara women commonly wear a blouse, a full gathered *pollera*, a shawl or *awayu*, and a *tupu* pin. The *awayu* is worn across the shoulders or used to carry children and goods. Bowler hats became widespread among Aymara and Quechua women in Peru and Bolivia during the twentieth century and are now an especially recognizable form of urban and rural dress. Men’s clothing centers on wool trousers, shirts, woven belts, and a poncho, with the *ch’ullu* providing protection from the cold. Both men and women use woven textiles with vibrant colors and striped or geometric designs. Festival clothing is more elaborate than everyday dress and may include embroidered garments, distinctive hats, masks, or accessories associated with a particular dance fraternity or community. In cold highland settlements, wool socks, leather footwear, and layered garments are practical as well as culturally legible.

### Architecture

Traditional Aymara houses are generally built from adobe or earth blocks, with stone foundations and simple pitched roofs covered with thatch or, increasingly, corrugated metal. In the high Altiplano, thick earthen walls moderate severe temperature changes between day and night. Domestic compounds may organize rooms around a working yard for cooking, storage, animals, and agricultural tasks. Communal identity is also expressed through the *marka*, a settlement or territorial center linked to surrounding communities and local authorities. Older funerary architecture includes *chullpa*, cylindrical or tower-like burial structures built for elite individuals, with variation in size, masonry, and burial chambers. In contemporary El Alto, Aymara cultural aesthetics appear in brightly colored “Neo-Andean” buildings, whose façades use exuberant color, geometric ornament, and references to highland pattern traditions.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Aymara households use woven bags, carrying cloths, clay vessels, wooden tools, leather containers, and agricultural implements adapted to high-altitude life. Pottery is associated with food preparation and storage, while metal objects include cooking vessels, pins, bells, and festival accessories. The *tupu* is a particularly recognizable metal object because it joins clothing, ornament, and social presentation. Coca leaves are carried in woven bags and prepared for chewing, tea, ritual offering, or medicinal use. Agricultural storage practices are equally distinctive: potatoes are freeze-dried into *chuño*, an enduring highland food kept for periods of scarcity. Llama and alpaca products—wool, leather, meat, and bone—enter domestic, economic, and ritual life.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Aymara adornment centers on silver or other metal garment pins, earrings, necklaces, bracelets, and decorative hair ornaments. The *tupu* fastens a shawl while also framing the wearer’s social appearance. Women’s hats, braids, ribbons, and layered skirts form an integrated visual ensemble rather than isolated accessories. Festival participants may add embroidered collars, metallic ornaments, artificial flowers, masks, or highly decorated hats according to the dance tradition being performed. Coca leaves, incense, candles, and other materials sold at La Paz’s Witches’ Market belong to ritual presentation as well as domestic use. Permanent tattooing and henna are not central documented features of Aymara adornment.

## Music & performance

Aymara music is closely tied to seasonal work, community organization, religious festivals, and dance. The principal aerophones include the **siku** or panpipe, **pinkillu** flute, and **tarqa**, a wooden notched flute. Panpipe ensembles may perform interlocking parts, in which different players contribute complementary notes to produce a complete melody. Drums and percussion support processions, dances, and ceremonial gatherings.

Music is often organized through community groups associated with an *ayllu* or *marka*. Instruments and repertories are linked to the agricultural and seasonal calendar: flute music is especially associated with outdoor festivities and periods of rain and growth, while panpipes are heard in processional and dance contexts. **Ch’alla**, the ceremonial sprinkling or offering of alcohol, coca, and other substances, may accompany communal celebrations. Catholic feast days frequently provide the public setting, but their meanings are layered with Aymara relationships to mountains, ancestors, agricultural fertility, and reciprocal obligation.

## Dance & theatre

Aymara festival dances use panpipes, flutes, drums, embroidered costumes, hats, masks, and processions. **Auki auki** performers represent or embody *Achachilas*, the mountain and ancestral powers understood to protect and provide for communities. These figures enable spirits to “dance upon the earth” during festivals. Performance therefore ranges from social entertainment and public display to explicitly ceremonial representation.

## Festivals & rituals

The Aymara ritual year follows agricultural seasons, weather, and Catholic feast days. Indigenous spirituality is often described through *pachakuti*, a cycle of transformation linked to seasonal change, historical rupture, and shifts in the natural world. Rituals directed toward *Achachilas*—the spirits or powers of mountains and highland places—express respect for Illimani, Illampu, Huayna Potosí, and other prominent peaks.

**Ch’alla** is a widespread offering practice involving the sprinkling or dedication of alcohol, coca leaves, incense, sweets, and other substances to a house, vehicle, field, workplace, or community setting. At La Paz’s Witches’ Market, ritual specialists and householders obtain coca, incense, candles, soaps, potions, and *sullu*, the dried fetus of a llama used as an offering. A *sullu* can be burned or placed in a building foundation.

Christian feast days are celebrated through masses, processions, music, dance, and community sponsorship, while retaining associations with older agricultural and spiritual events. Birth, marriage, agricultural work, and house construction all involve reciprocal obligations among kin and community members. Funerary customs historically included burials in cairns, dolmens, cylindrical graves, and *chullpa* towers. The dead remain connected to household, landscape, and ritual memory rather than being understood solely as absent individuals.

## Foodways

Aymara foodways are based on high-altitude agriculture, pastoralism, exchange, and careful preservation. Potatoes are a staple crop, and *chuño* is produced by exposing potatoes to freezing night temperatures, drying them in the sun, and repeatedly processing them until they become storable for years. *Chairo*, a well-known dish associated with La Paz, is a stew prepared with *chuño*, meat, carrots, onions, and corn.

Other important crops include quinoa, purple corn, maca, olluco, yacón, lucuma, cherimoya, and native or locally adapted fruits and vegetables. Quinoa is valued for both seeds and leaves. Llama and alpaca products contribute meat, wool, leather, and ritual materials, while sheep and other introduced animals also enter modern diets. Coca leaves are chewed whole, brewed as tea, or combined with a small amount of wood ash; they have recreational, medicinal, and ritual uses. Coca is also offered during ceremonies and community work. Food and drink participate in *ch’alla*, household blessings, agricultural offerings, and festival hospitality. Alcohol may be poured onto the ground or presented to sacred powers before communal consumption.

## Oral tradition & literature

Aymara oral tradition preserves origin accounts, mountain narratives, agricultural knowledge, ritual instructions, historical memories, and explanations of relations between humans, animals, and landscape. A prominent mythic image concerns the Heavenly Llama, which drinks water from the ocean and releases it as rain; at the end of time, llamas return to springs and lagoons. Stories about *Achachilas* explain mountains as ancestral, protective, and materially powerful beings.

Aymara understandings of time do not always divide the past into a strict prehistoric and historical sequence. Natural cycles, the arrival of the Spanish, mythic time, and periods of transformation are interwoven. Community knowledge is transmitted through ritual speech, songs, proverbs, agricultural instruction, and narratives told in domestic and village settings. Aymara-language writing developed under colonial and modern conditions despite early difficulties in representing Aymara phonetics with Spanish orthography. Contemporary cultural activism supports the preservation of Aymara language, historical memory, music, and Indigenous political thought, including Katarist intellectual traditions.

## Language & religion

Aymara is an Aymaran language spoken from the northern shore of Lake Titicaca south toward Lake Poopó, with regional dialect differences. It is an official language of Peru alongside Spanish and Quechua and is widely used in Bolivia. Jaqaru and Kawki are its closest related languages. Colonial missionaries produced grammars and religious materials, including work by the Jesuit Ludovico Bertonio in the early seventeenth century.

Aymara religion is syncretic: Catholic liturgy, saints’ days, and Christian festivals coexist with Indigenous spirituality focused on seasons, agriculture, mountains, ancestors, and reciprocity with the natural environment. *Achachilas* are mountain and ancestral powers, while *Pachamama* is widely invoked in contemporary Andean ritual language as the earth and generative world. Ritual specialists known as *yatiri* prepare offerings and blessings. Coca, incense, llama offerings, candles, alcohol, and food connect domestic life with sacred places. The result is not a simple replacement of Indigenous religion by Christianity, but an interwoven ritual system whose meanings vary by community and national context.
