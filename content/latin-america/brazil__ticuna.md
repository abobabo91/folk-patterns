## Overview

The Ticuna, also known as Magüta, Tucuna, Tikuna, or Tukuna, are an Indigenous people of Brazil, Colombia, and Peru. They are the most numerous tribe in the Brazilian Amazon. According to the 2022 Brazilian census, 74,601 Ticuna people live in Brazil, making them the most populous Indigenous group in the country. Their language is spoken in the Amazon Basin and is generally classified as a language isolate. Ticuna communities have maintained distinctive cultural and genetic characteristics through historical residence relatively far from rivers, endogamy, and long interaction with neighboring peoples. Their cultural life includes the *pelazon* coming-of-age ceremony, clan exogamy, shamanic traditions, oral histories, and bilingual education and preservation projects.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Eagle-feather and snail-shell dress** — The sources describe a ceremonial dress made from eagle feathers and snail shells for girls undergoing the *pelazon* coming-of-age ceremony.

**Tree-bark garments** — Traditional garments made from tree bark are mentioned as clothing that most Ticuna people now wear mainly on special occasions or for tourists.

### Clothing & dress

Most Ticuna people now dress in western clothing. Traditional garments made from tree bark are generally worn on special occasions or for tourists. In the *pelazon* ceremony, the girl’s whole body is painted black, a clan symbol is drawn on her head, all her hair is pulled out, and she wears a dress custom-made from eagle feathers and snail shells.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The *pelazon* ceremony includes black body painting, the drawing of a clan symbol on the head, the removal of all the girl’s hair, and ceremonial clothing made from eagle feathers and snail shells.

## Music & performance

They do mention recordings of the Magütá, Miraña, and Murui, made to preserve oral histories and traditions of Indigenous groups in the Colombian Amazon. The sources also state that a weekday radio show is broadcast in Ticuna, Portuguese, and Spanish by the Latin American Ministries. No instrument names, musical forms, or performance occasions are provided.

## Dance & theatre

The *pelazon* ceremony includes the requirement that the girl continuously jump over a fire, but the sources do not characterize this as a dance or theatrical performance.

## Festivals & rituals

The Ticuna historically practiced shamanism. Ta'e was the Ticuna creator god who guarded the earth, while Yo'i and Ip were mythical heroes in Ticuna folklore who helped fight demons. Christian missionary influence has made shamans rare in all but the most isolated communities.

The principal life-cycle rite described is the *pelazon*, a coming-of-age ceremony for girls when they reach puberty. After the girl’s first menstruation, her body is painted black and a clan symbol is drawn on her head. Her hair is pulled out, she puts on a dress made from eagle feathers and snail shells, and she must continuously jump over a fire. After four days, she is considered a woman and is eligible for marriage. The ritual is now shorter and less intense than it was historically.

Ticuna men and women must marry outside their own clans. The Ticuna follow rules of exogamy, and members of the same moiety are not permitted to marry. Divorce is permissible but infrequent.

## Oral tradition & literature

Yo'i and Ip are described as mythical heroes in Ticuna folklore who helped fight off demons. Ticuna oral traditions have also been recorded and written down in a large-scale project intended to provide literate Ticunas with literature for practice. The UCLA library holds digitally available recordings from three Indigenous groups of the Colombian Amazon, including the Magütá, Miraña, and Murui, collected to maintain oral histories and traditions.

Ticuna has written literature in Brazil, and books are published every year in both Brazil and Peru. In school, native teachers use textbooks in Ticuna, Portuguese, and Spanish depending on the country and educational setting.

## Language & religion

Ticuna, also known as Tikuna, Tucuna, Tukuna, Magta, Maguta, and Tukna, is spoken by approximately 50,000 people in the Amazon Basin, including Brazil, Peru, and Colombia. It is generally classified as a language isolate, although it has been associated with the extinct Yuri language and with several speculative language groupings. Ticuna is tonal, with 8–12 phonemic tones depending on the dialect, and is written in the Latin script.

According to the 2022 Brazilian Census, Brazil had 51,978 Ticuna speakers, representing 69.7% of the country’s Ticuna population of 74,601. Ticuna is the most widely spoken Indigenous language in Brazil. The language is taught in Ticuna schools, and education is also provided in Portuguese or Spanish. Historically, Ticuna people practiced shamanism. Christian missionaries have influenced religious life, and estimates in the sources state that between 30% and 90% of Ticuna people are Christian. Catholic, Baptist, Presbyterian, and Evangelical missionaries are active among the Ticuna.
