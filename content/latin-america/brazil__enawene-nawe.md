## Overview

The Enawenê-Nawê are an Indigenous people of Brazil who live in Mato Grosso state, in a large village near the Iquê River within the Enawenê Nawê Indigenous Land. They practice agriculture, fishing, and gathering, and do not hunt or eat red meat. They were first contacted in 1974 by Vicente Cañas and numbered 566 in 2012, 320 in 2000, and 737 in 2014. Their language is an Arawakan language, classified in one account as Central Maipuran and in another as South Arawak. Their folk-cultural life is closely connected with fishing, the rivers, social and cosmic order, and the ritual known as Yaokwa.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources identify Yaokwa as a ritual for the maintenance of social and cosmic order. They state that the Enawene Nawe believe nearby land is home to important spirits and that ranchers have threatened violence when members of the group perform their rites.

## Foodways

The Enawene Nawe practice agriculture, fishing, and gathering. They do not hunt or eat red meat, and fish is a central source of food. The sources state that dams built or under construction on the Juruena river pollute the water and kill many fish; without fish, there is no food for the Enawene Nawe people.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that the Enawene Nawe believe nearby land is home to many important spirits.

## Language & religion

Enawene Nawe, also called Enawené-Nawé, Enawenê-Nawê, Eneuene-Mare, or Salumã, is an Arawakan language of Brazil. One source describes it as a Central Maipuran language within the Arawakan language family; another describes it as a South Arawak language and notes classifications that place it with Paresi in the Paresi–Xingu branch of Arawakan languages. The language has four oral vowels and four nasal vowels, according to the cited description. The sources describe beliefs in important spirits and identify Yaokwa as a ritual maintaining social and cosmic order.
