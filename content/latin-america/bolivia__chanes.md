## Overview

Chané is the collective name for the southernmost Arawakan-speaking peoples. Historically, they lived in the plains of the northern Gran Chaco and in the foothills of the Andes in Paraguay, Brazil, Bolivia, and Argentina. The historical Chané consisted of the Chané proper, who lived in eastern Bolivia, and the Guaná, who lived in Paraguay and adjacent Brazil. Twenty-first-century survivors include the Izoceño people of Bolivia and 3,034 descendants reported in Argentina by the 2010 census. The Chané developed an agrarian culture, built densely populated villages, cultivated corn, peanuts, cotton, and squash, and became known for ceramics, graphics, wooden masks, and fabric clothing.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Fabric clothing** — Chané people made fabric clothing.

**Graphics** — Chané graphics have been found mainly in the pampas of Bolivia surrounding Santa Cruz de la Sierra and in Samaipata, Portachuelo, Valle Abajo, Okinawa, Cotoca, El Pari, Mataral, and Warnes.

### Clothing & dress

The Chané are described as making fabric clothing.

### Architecture

The Chané built densely populated villages.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**Ceramics** — The Chané are described as famous for their ceramics, and Chané ceramics and graphics have been found mainly in the Bolivian pampas surrounding Santa Cruz de la Sierra and in Samaipata, Portachuelo, Valle Abajo, Okinawa, Cotoca, El Pari, Mataral, and Warnes.

## Festivals & rituals

 An ancient Chané religious site dating from about 300 CE is identified as El Fuerte de Samaipata, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Foodways

The Chané developed an agrarian culture and cultivated corn, peanuts, cotton, and squash. The Guaná, the eastern branch of the Chané, supplied Mbayá chiefs with agricultural products and textiles in exchange for protection and European goods such as iron tools.

## Language & religion

The Chané are identified as Arawakan-speaking peoples. The Chané proper lived in eastern Bolivia, while the Guaná lived in Paraguay and adjacent Brazil; twenty-first-century survivors include the Izoceño people of Bolivia, and the Guaná’s survivors include the Tereno and Kinikinao of Mato Grosso do Sul province in Brazil. Chané communities in the Izozog region of Santa Cruz, Bolivia, and in Yacui and Che-Renda near Tartagal, Argentina, have been influenced by the Guaraní language and culture while retaining their Chané identity.
