## Overview

The Kokama — also spelled Cocama, and Cocamas in Portuguese — are an indigenous people of the Amazon who historically spoke the Cocama (Kokáma) language and today live across Peru, Brazil and Colombia. Their settlements lie along the banks of the north-eastern lower Ucayali, the lower Marañón and the Huallaga rivers, with further communities in Amazonas state in Brazil and an isolated area in Colombia. The figures in the sources differ by what they count: a known Kokama population of 786 in 2005, against a projected ethnic population of 15,000 for the language's speaker base, of whom roughly 2,000 Cocama speakers live in Peru. The sources used here document the people mainly through their language and through a small body of museum-held pottery and fishing gear; they give no general account of Kokama folk culture.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used — the Wikipedia articles on the Kokama people and the Cocama language, and the British Museum catalogue records for the objects shown here — document no Kokama textile tradition, so none is described here. The one named pattern term they contain belongs to pottery rather than cloth, and is given below.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Kene* — the design named in the catalogue entry for a polychrome vessel; otherwise the records specify only geometric decoration in black, red and orange on cream, and thin and thick dark red-brown lines.

### Architecture

The sources used say nothing about Kokama house forms, building materials or settlement layout beyond the riverbank locations of Kokama communities on the Ucayali, Marañón and Huallaga.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery is the one craft the museum records document in quantity. The British Museum objects attributed to the Kokama are predominantly vessels: plain **pottery bowls**, a **water jar**, a **jug with a looped handle** painted white and red, and two larger **jars** whose circular mouths are glazed red while the body is painted cream and carries red and black geometric designs — on the two-handled example as on the single-mouthed one. The most fully described piece is a **polychrome vessel** decorated with *kene* design: its interior slipped in dark red and burnished, the rim inturned and chipped, the body walls round and concave and tapering towards the base into a second concave section, the exterior slipped in cream and worked in thin and thick dark red-brown lines with an orange geometric pattern. Alongside the ceramics the collection holds two **fish harpoon-heads** of iron with twined grass rope still attached — the only metal and fibre work among the objects shown.

## Music & performance

The sources name no Kokama instruments or song genres. What they do record is song used as a vehicle for the language: in 2013 residents of Nauta, in Peru's Loreto Province, made a children's rap video in the Kukama-Kukamiria dialect with the local station Radio Ucamara, and the school the station runs, Ikuar, teaches the language through songs as well as storytelling.

## Oral tradition & literature

Traditional storytelling appears in the sources only as an instrument of language revitalization: Radio Ucamara in Nauta, which has worked on preserving Cocama for several years, manages the Ikuar school with the aim of teaching the language through songs and traditional story telling. No epic, tale cycle or proverb collection is named. On the written side, grammar rules have been developed for the language, it is written in the Latin script, and parts of the Bible have been translated into it, against a 3% literacy rate in Cocama compared with 50% in Spanish.

## Language & religion

Cocama (Kokáma) is spoken by thousands of people in western South America and is closely related to Omagua, a nearly extinct language of Peru and Brazil; the sources assign it to no wider family by name. It has three dialects. The robust one goes under many names — Cocama, Kokama, Kukama-Kukamiria, Ucayali, Xibitaoan, Huallaga, Pampadeque and Pandequebo — while Cocamilla (Kokamíya) was already moribund by 1999, spoken only by people over 40. In Brazil the language is called Kokama or Kokamilla, and the Wikipedia article reports that 50 of 411 ethnic Chayahuitas there speak it. Most speakers are trilingual in Cocama, Portuguese and Spanish; very few are monolingual. In Colombia's Lower Putumayo area, where 20 ethnic groups live, the number of Cocama-Cocamilla speakers is unknown and the language may now be extinct. The sources say nothing about Kokama religion.

Two Kokama figures are named in the sources, both environmentalists: Mari Luz Canaquiri Murayari, a Time 100 honoree and Goldman Environmental Prize winner, and Lilia Isolina Java Tapayuri (born 1985).
