## Overview

The Quechua — also written Quichua or Kichwa — are the Indigenous peoples of South America who speak the Quechuan languages, a family that originated among the Indigenous people of Peru and spread through the central Andes. Most speakers live in Peru, with significant populations in Ecuador, Bolivia, Chile, Colombia and Argentina: roughly 5.1 million in Peru, 1.8 million in Bolivia and 2.5 million in Ecuador, plus some 33,800 in Chile and 55,500 in Argentina. The Quechua word for a Quechua speaker is *runa* or *nuna* ("person"), plural *runakuna*; speakers call themselves Runa, "the people". Identity is locally oriented rather than national — the dialects are in some cases mutually unintelligible, and only a slight sense of common identity exists across Peru, Bolivia and Ecuador. In folk-culture terms the Quechua matter above all for weaving, a tradition handed down from Inca times or earlier and still carrying patterned cloth into daily and ceremonial life.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Handicrafts are an important aspect of material culture in almost all Quechua ethnic groups. The weaving tradition uses cotton and wool from llamas, alpacas, guanacos and vicuñas, a multitude of natural dyes, and numerous woven patterns (*pallay*). The objects in this atlas — overwhelmingly British Museum wool textiles — let the repertoire be read by form.

**Pallay** — the general term for the woven patterns themselves, worked into belts, bags, mantas and ponchos. The catalogued pieces are almost all wool, in warp-faced plain weave and "complimentary weave", the latter carrying the figurative designs.

**Poncho** — the handwoven *poncho* is the most distinctive part of men's clothing; nearly every Quechua man and boy has one, generally red and decorated with intricate designs, and each district has a distinctive pattern. A catalogued example is made from two pieces sewn together down the centre, warp-faced plain weave with a decorative stripe at each side and complementary weave with zoomorphic patterns, brown with red, pink and grey.

**Manta** — a carrying-cloth and mantle. The collection holds a man's woollen *manta*, red with stripes of geometric and zoomorphic designs, worn at weddings; a woman's manta of alpaca and sheep's wool woven in geometric design; and a brown one striped with geometric designs.

**Chumpi** — a woven belt that protects the lower back when working in the fields. The British Museum's man's wool *chumpi* carries geometric and zoomorphic designs.

**Chuspa** — small woven pouches men have worn since ancient times to carry coca leaves. Two catalogued *chuspa* bags are wool, or wool and cotton, with geometric designs and carrying-straps; a related ceremonial wool bag adds tassels.

**Alforja and capote** — bags for gathering herbs. The *alforja* appears as a saddle-bag and as a bag with zoomorphic or geometric-and-zoomorphic designs; the striped *capote* carries both design families plus a carrying-strap.

**Weaving sampler** — a practice cloth in complementary weave with cat and bird designs, in natural brown and grey wool, documenting how the figurative vocabulary is learned.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Pallay* (woven patterns generally); geometric designs, the commonest class across belts, bags, mantas, shawls and head-bands; zoomorphic designs on ponchos, mantas, belts, bags and head-bands; cat and bird figures, named on the sampler; floral designs on a knitted cap; stripes, used structurally in shawls (black, blue, red, violet and fuchsia separated by multicoloured brocading) and in slings woven in cream, ochre and brown.

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress today mixes pre-Spanish styles with Spanish colonial peasant dress. From puberty girls wear multiple layers of petticoats and skirts, showing the family's wealth and making a more desirable bride; married women do the same. The collection holds a gathered wool skirt with appliqué decoration, a woman's embroidered cotton shirt, a woman's shawl of two wool lengths sewn lengthwise, a wool jacket decorated with buttons and beads, and a woman's felted wool *sombrero* with silk ribbon. Many women wear colourful attire with a fedora-style hat, worn by Quechua and Aymara women since the 1920s, when British railway workers brought it to the country. Younger men generally wear Western clothing — synthetic football shirts and tracksuit trousers; older men still wear dark knee-length handwoven *bayeta* wool trousers with a *chumpi* belt. Men's fine dress includes a woollen waistcoat, the *chaleco*, often richly decorated, resembling the sleeveless *juyuna* women wear. Ponchos are daily wear in Huilloc, Patacancha and villages of the Lares Valley, but elsewhere are reserved for festivals, village meetings and weddings. The *ch'ullu*, a knitted earflapped hat — the collection's example has floral designs — is frequently worn; a child's first *ch'ullu* is traditionally knitted by their father, and in the Ausangate region *chullos* are adorned with white beads and large tassels called *t'ikas*. Men sometimes set a felt *sombrero* over the *ch'ullu*, banded with finely decorated *centillo*. *Ajotas*, sandals cut from recycled tires, are standard footwear for both sexes. Headbands (*wincha*) and braided hairbands (*tulma*), both beaded with glass, were made for weddings and hair-dressing.

### Architecture

Houses are usually built of air-dried clay bricks (*tika*, Spanish *adobe*), or of branches and clay mortar in wattle-and-daub, with roofs covered in straw, reeds, or the puna grass *ichu*. The typical Andean community extends over several altitude ranges, combining arable cultivation lower down with pastoral farming in the higher puna, so settlement is dispersed across ecological tiers rather than concentrated. Land is usually owned by the local community, the *ayllu*, and either worked jointly or redistributed annually. Two kinds of collective labour build and maintain the settlement: *mink'a*, in which people work together on projects of common interest such as communal facilities, and *ayni*, reciprocal assistance in which members of an *ayllu* help one family with a large private project such as house construction, to be repaid in kind later. A 1905 Museum of World Culture photograph records a Quichua homestead in the Saqui valley.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The strongest named form in the atlas is the *kero*, the Andean drinking beaker, represented by nine Metropolitan Museum examples dating from the 16th to the 18th century. Most are wood — escallonia in one case, possibly *prosopis* in others — inlaid with pigmented resin; several add tin studs or sheet tin, and one is wood with a copper-silver alloy. The Met also holds a bronze knife with figures, 16th–18th century. Herding and transport gear is woven rather than made of leather: a wool sling with geometric designs used as a whip for hunting and herding, a sling or carrying strap of alpaca wool in cream, ochre and brown geometric design, and a braided woollen horse hobble in brown and white stripes with red and white tassels. Striped wool textiles with geometric designs served as carrying-cloths. The *quipu*, the knotted cord record, appears in drawings after Guaman Poma de Ayala's manuscript, held by an Inca *contador* and by an astrologer.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment in the sources is mainly beadwork and silver. Glass beads decorate the wool *wincha* head-band made for weddings, the braided *tulma* hair-band, and a wool jacket that combines beads with buttons; in the Ausangate region *ch'ullu* hats are ornately adorned with white beads and large tassels, *t'ikas*. One catalogued bag is decorated with coins along one side. For silver, the Museum of World Culture holds a shawl pin (*sjalnål*) in silver collected in the Cuzco region and attributed to the area northeast or east of Lake Titicaca in La Paz department, Bolivia. Women's hats carry silk ribbon and, in one modern example, a plastic buckle; men's felt hats take *centillo* bands.

## Music & performance

The sources document performance mainly through early twentieth-century photographs. A 1905 Museum of World Culture image from Río Mojos, Caupolicán, Bolivia, shows Quichua dancing at the Feast of the Cross and playing, among other instruments, panpipes, with an altar visible to the right; it was published in Erland Nordenskiöld's 1905 account of Quichua-speaking Indians on the eastern slopes of the Andes in the Peru–Bolivia border country. A 1929 photograph from the Peruvian highlands shows Christian Indigenous people dancing outside a church. Beyond the panpipe no instrument is named in the sources, and no song genre is identified. What the record does establish is the performance context: music and dance attached to the Catholic festival calendar and performed at the church door and at the cross, by communities the catalogue describes as Christian while simultaneously retaining much of their older belief. One musical fact reaches the sources from another direction: the Bolivian director Jorge Sanjinés made the Quechua-language feature film *Yawar Mallku* in 1969, and Luzmila Carpio (Bolivian) and Renata Flores Rivera and Magaly Solier (Peruvian) are named as Quechua musicians — Flores Rivera and Solier belonging to a contemporary generation performing in the language.

## Dance & theatre

Two photographs carry the whole of the dance record. In 1905, at Mojos in Caupolicán, Quichua danced at the Feast of the Cross with panpipes before an altar; in 1929, in the Peruvian highlands, Christian Indigenous dancers performed outside a church. Both place dance in the Catholic festival cycle rather than in secular entertainment. The sources used name no individual dance, no mask tradition and no dramatic form.

## Festivals & rituals

Peru has no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions in the material supplied, so the festival calendar here rests on the Wikipedia articles and the museum catalogue. Practically all Quechua in the Andes have been nominally Catholic since colonial times, and traditional religious forms persist blended with Christian elements in a fully integrated syncretism. The Feast of the Cross is documented as a dancing festival among Quichua at Mojos. Offerings structure the ritual year: burnt offerings and libations are made regularly to Mother Earth, *Pachamama*, who grants fertility, and the mountain spirits (*apu*) and lesser local deities (*wak'a*) are still venerated, especially in southern Peru. Cemeteries are a documented ritual site — a 1904 photograph shows Quichua at the churchyard in Santa Rosa, with the catalogue noting that these people are Christians who at the same time retain much of their older conception of things, and another photograph of the same year records inhabitants at the cemetery. Weddings are the best-attested life-cycle rite in the object record: a man's red *manta* with geometric and zoomorphic stripes was worn at weddings, a beaded wool *wincha* head-band was made for a wedding, and ponchos in most communities are brought out for festivals, village meetings and weddings. Childhood has its own marker — a child's first *ch'ullu* is knitted by their father — and so does puberty, when girls begin wearing the layered petticoats and skirts that display family wealth. Certain regional products, chicha corn beer, coca leaves and local potatoes, are held to have religious significance, though not uniformly across communities.

## Foodways

Potatoes were domesticated in the Andes by the Quechua, who have cultivated thousands of varieties used for food and for medicine; climate change now threatens them and other traditional crops, and communities are mounting conservation and adaptation efforts. Quinoa is the other staple. *Ch'arki* — the origin of the English word *jerky* — is dried and sometimes salted meat, traditionally llama sun- and freeze-dried by Andean day heat and night cold, now often horse or beef, with variation by country. *Pachamanca*, a Quechua word for a pit-cooking technique used in Peru, combines meats (chicken, beef, pork, lamb or mutton), tubers (potatoes, sweet potatoes, yucca, *uqa*/*ok'a*, and *mashwa*), maize and fava beans, seasonings, and sometimes cheese in a small pot or tamales. Guinea pigs are raised for meat; llama and alpaca meat, beans, barley, hot peppers, coriander and peanuts round out the diet. *Chicha*, maize beer, is both drink and ritual substance; a 1904 photograph from Caupolicán shows women brewing it. Coca leaves, chewed as a mild stimulant that suppresses hunger, thirst, pain and fatigue and relieves altitude sickness, are taken during fieldwork and construction breaks and carried in the *ch'uspa*. Quinine, from cinchona bark, is used against malaria-like symptoms.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Quechua came to terms with repeated historical tragedy through myth. The *Nak'aq* or *Pishtaco* ("butcher") is the white murderer who sucks the fat from the bodies of the Indigenous people he kills; a song tells of a bloody river. In the myth of *Wiraquchapampa* the Q'ero people recount the victory of the *Apus* over the Spaniards. The *Inkarrí* myth, current in southern Peru, links Quechua groups across the whole region from Ayacucho to Cusco. The written record begins with the missionary Domingo de Santo Tomás, who reached Peru in 1538 and published his *Grammatica o arte de la lengua general de los indios de los reynos del Perú* in 1560; colonial officials later banned Quechua from public use in Peru after the Túpac Amaru II rebellion, suppressing even pro-Catholic texts such as Garcilaso de la Vega's *Comentarios Reales*. The chronicle of Guaman Poma de Ayala, whose drawings of spinners, weavers, *chasqui* messengers and *quipu*-keepers appear among the objects here, survives in Copenhagen. Recent revivals are documented: Carmen Escalante Gutiérrez defended Europe's first Quechua thesis in 2017; Pablo Landeo wrote the first novel in Quechua without a Spanish translation the same year; Roxana Quispe Collantes completed a thesis in the language in 2019, on the poet Andrés Alencastre Gutiérrez.

## Language & religion

Quechua, *Runa simi* ("people's language") in Southern Quechua, is an Indigenous language family — not a single language, since mutual intelligibility fails between branches — estimated at 8–10 million speakers in 2004 and just under 7 million in censuses to 2011. It divides into Quechua I (Central, *Waywash*) and Quechua II (Peripheral, *Wamp'una*), the latter splitting into Yunkay, Northern (Kichwa, in Ecuador and Colombia) and Southern Quechua, which runs from Huancavelica through Ayacucho, Cusco and Puno into Bolivia and northwestern Argentina. It has been written in the Roman alphabet since the Spanish conquest; Peru adopted a new orthography in 1975 and a three-vowel variant in 1985, and the spellings remain controversial. Peru recognised Quechua officially in 1975, Ecuador in 2006, Bolivia in 2009. Religion is a fully integrated syncretism of Catholicism with *Pachamama*, *apu* and *wak'a* veneration.
