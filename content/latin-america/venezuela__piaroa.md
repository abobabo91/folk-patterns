## Overview

The Piaroa, who call themselves *Huottüja* or *De'aruhua*, are an Indigenous people of South America living primarily in the middle Orinoco Basin of Venezuela and Colombia. Their territory is circumscribed by the Suapure, Parguaza, Ventuari, and Manapiare rivers and the right bank of the Orinoco. The population was about 15,000 according to INE in 2002, with an estimated 2,500 living on the left bank of the Orinoco River in Colombia. Piaroa communities consist of many small, self-governed villages spread across a wide area. Their language belongs to the Piaroa–Saliban language family. Their folk-culture significance in the sources lies in their language, shamanism, mythology, subsistence practices, pottery, basketry, beadwork, and continuing efforts to preserve their cultural identity and territory.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources mention that some Piaroa make baskets and string beads.

### Clothing & dress

 They state only that some Piaroa string beads and that the people identify themselves through their cultural identity, language, heritage, and spirituality.

### Architecture

 They do mention small, self-governed villages, community centers, cemeteries, and conucos, which are described as familiar garden patches.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery is documented in connection with clay from the Guanay valley. The source describes this clay as a valuable commodity and the best clay for making pottery in the region. They also mention baskets, beads, vines collected for rattan furniture, and conucos used as garden patches.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 They state that some Piaroa string beads, but give no further information about their materials, forms, or functions.

## Music & performance

 They describe traditional Piaroa religion as involving shamanism and identify Wahari as a creator god who was said to have incarnated as a tapir. The sources also discuss ayahuasca, called yagé, and yopo tourism among De'aruhua communities.

The sources mention that the Piaroa preserve culture, customs, heritage, and spirituality with the earth in some of the most self-isolated and independent villages on the Upper Cuao, Upper Catañiapo, and Upper Parguaza rivers.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do describe traditional religious practice. Piaroa religion involves shamanism and is centered around Wahari, a creator god said to have incarnated as a tapir. The Huottüja were influenced by Christian missions beginning with the mission in San Fernando de Atabapo in the late 1700s, followed in the 1940s by Salesians and Jesuits, and later by Baptist and Presbyterian missionaries. In 2006, the national Venezuelan government expelled most foreign Christian missionaries, particularly those connected with the New Tribes Missions.

The sources state that some communities on the Upper Cuao, Upper Catañiapo, and Upper Parguaza rivers have preserved their culture, customs, heritage, and spirituality with the earth. They also describe renewed public interest in Piaroa shamanism and entheogenic plant use, including ayahuasca, or yagé, and yopo.

## Foodways

Piaroa subsistence includes farming, fishing, gathering fruit, and hunting small game. Since the 1980s, communities have grown anon, cassava, cocoa, cupuacu, manaca, moriche, and other native plants as commercial agroforestry crops. Hunting and gathering are generally not exploited commercially, although fishing and the collection of some fruits supplement or generate income.

Cassava is also called manioc in the sources. Traditional Piaroa villages cultivate cassava and plant cocoa under the forest canopy or as a canuco replacement crop, filling the void in the forest that results from cultivating manioc. The sources state that Piaroa communities grow the cocoa cultivars Criollo, Forastero, and Trinitario. Cocoa harvested by the Piaroa since 2008 became world-famous through the work of artisan craft chocolate makers in Canada, Germany, France, the United States, and Venezuela.

Some Piaroa also raise cattle.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They do identify Piaroa mythology through the traditional religious account of Wahari, a creator god who was said to have incarnated as a tapir.

The sources describe efforts to preserve Piaroa culture, identity, language, and heritage. The Huottüja developed their own alphabet based on the accepted Latin Language codes ISO 639-3 PID guidelines of the Summer Institute of Linguistics. They also developed an elementary dictionary and grade school curriculum readers. Christian Piaroa are described as supporting initiatives, projects, and programs that promote original culture, identity, and language, including shamanism and mythology.

## Language & religion

Piaroa is an Indigenous language of Venezuela and Colombia. It belongs to the Piaroa–Saliban languages and was spoken by 14,500 people in 2017. The related Wirö language and Saliba form part of the Piaroa–Saliban language family, and the source states that the relationship between Piaroa and Saliba was first recognized by Filippo Salvatore Gilii in 1782. Piaroa is also known by variations of Wotuja, Wo’tiheh, and Huottüja. The people use several names for themselves, including Huottüja, De'aruwa, De'aruhua, Uwottüja, Huottöja, Wothuha, Huǫttųją, Wötʰïhä, De'atʰïhä, and u̧wo̧tju̧ja̧.

The traditional religion involves shamanism and centers on Wahari. About 50% of the Piaroa had converted to Christianity according to the source, compared with an earlier estimate of 80%. The influence of shamans over local communities has waned, while village captains or chiefs are described as being in charge of daily movement. Piaroa continue to identify themselves first as Huottuja, then as Venezuelans or Colombians, and third as Christians or non-Christian.
