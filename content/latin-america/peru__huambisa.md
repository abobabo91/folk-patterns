## Overview

The Huambisa, also known as the Wampis, are an Indigenous people of Peru and Ecuador and one of the Jivaroan peoples. They live in the upper Marañón and Santiago river areas, with ancestral territory extending along the Santiago and Morona rivers. In the 1980s, they numbered about 5,000 people; in 2012, nearly 8,000 speakers of Huambisa were recorded worldwide. Their language belongs to the Jivaroan language family and is closely related to Achuar-Shiwiar, Shuar, and Aguaruna. Huambisa communities have maintained political independence from one another and have repeatedly resisted outside incursions, including those of the Inca Empire, Spanish colonizers, missionaries, and rubber interests. Their folk-culture significance in the available sources lies chiefly in their language, agricultural practices, hunting and fishing, settlement form, and history of territorial resistance.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Huambisa communities primarily live in small patrilineal communities that maintain political independence from one another. Each community lives together in a thatched longhouse, typically 80 feet long by 40 feet wide.

## Music & performance

The available linguistic material does, however, document aspects of Huambisa speech. The language has been studied as an example of sound symbolism, in which phonological form is connected with semantic meanings. The sources state that sounds in Huambisa are significant not only literally but also symbolically. They also describe a system of 14 consonants and 8 vowels, including oral and nasal vowels, although this phonological information does not constitute a description of musical practice.

## Festivals & rituals

The historical sources do describe repeated encounters with outside powers. In the 15th and 16th centuries, the Huambisa faced incursions by the Inca Empire and resisted them. After the Spanish conquest of the Inca Empire in the 1530s, Spanish forces attempted to subjugate the Huambisa. The first recorded encounter with the Spanish occurred in 1549, and during the following decade Juan de Salinas launched incursions into their homeland. Spanish Jesuit and Franciscan missionaries attempted to evangelize the Huambisa in the 18th and 19th centuries, but these efforts failed. During the Amazon rubber boom, an expedition in 1910 attempted to entrap or enslave local people for rubber extraction; at least sixty-seven members of the expedition were killed in retaliation.

## Foodways

Huambisa subsistence is based primarily on agriculture. They grow plantains and cassava especially, as well as tobacco, cotton, and other plants, using slash-and-burn methods. Hunting and fishing supplement their diets.

The available material therefore supports a description of staple crops and subsistence activities.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources do record a history of oral and linguistic significance through the documentation of Huambisa vocabulary and grammar. The language has been extensively documented during the last century, and its vocabulary distinguishes among words according to highly specific contexts. For example, the sources describe several different word sets corresponding to forms of “to open,” depending on whether the object is a bag, book, door, fruit, the eyes, or the mouth. This linguistic specificity is presented as a feature of the Huambisa lexicon rather than as a named oral-literary genre.

## Language & religion

Huambisa is an Indigenous language of the Huambisa people of Peru and belongs to the Jivaroan language family. It is closely related to Achuar-Shiwiar, Shuar, and Aguaruna. The language is spoken chiefly between the Condorcanqui Province of the Amazonas Region and the Datem del Marañón Province in the Loreto Region, along the Santiago River. The sources identify varieties associated with the Upper Santiago, Middle Santiago, Katirpisa, and Morona, as well as the Wachiycu dialect and the Santiago regional dialect.

In 2012, nearly 8,000 people were recorded as Huambisa speakers worldwide, including 5,000 living along the Morona and Santiago rivers. In 2010, Huambisa was declared official in the Amazonas Department together with Aguaruna and Chachapoyas Quechua. A new alphabet was approved in 2012. Many Huambisa also know Spanish and Quechua.
