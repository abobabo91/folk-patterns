## Overview

The Tzotzil are an Indigenous Maya people of Chiapas in southern Mexico, with major populations in Chamula, San Cristóbal de las Casas (Jovel), and Zinacantán. Their language belongs to the Mayan family, descended from proto-Ch'ol, and the name Tzotzil means 'bat people.' They are known primarily for their distinctive backstrap loom weaving, producing textiles with patterns and colors specific to individual townships. Their culture blends Catholic practices with indigenous Maya beliefs about ancestral gods, the Earth Lord, and spiritual concepts like the inner soul and animal companions.

## Material culture

Tzotzil material culture is dominated by handwoven textiles created on backstrap looms, with distinctive regional patterns, complemented by functional household objects and figurative works.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Nearly all Tzotzil textiles are woven on a backstrap loom, combining handspun yarn with commercial thread in narrow webs that are sewn together.

- **Backstrap loom** (*mecapal*) — A tree-anchored loom with dowels, tapered battens, and a plaited fiber strap worn around the weaver's waist.
- **Brocaded and embroidered cloth** — Woven webs feature burgundy, yellow and green wool on white cotton with geometric and floral brocading.
- **Commercial and handspun thread mix** (*hilaza*) — Cotton thread and wool yarn are combined in weaving, with acrylic yarn and artificial silk used in later embroidered pieces.

### Clothing & dress

Women wear a huipil blouse, indigo-dyed enredo skirt, woven sash and shawl; men wear a shirt, short pants, neckerchief, hat and wool poncho.

- **Huipil** — A woman's blouse or long overdress of three sewn white cotton webs with a rounded neck opening, brocaded in red geometric patterns.
- **Enredo** — A wrap-around indigo-dyed skirt, often two narrow webs joined by cross-stitch seams, with white stripes and multicolored embroidery.
- **Faja** — A woven sash or belt in warp-faced weave, typically red with green or yellow stripes, that holds the skirt up.
- **Duk** — A man's square cloth of striped or checked cotton with wool tassels at the corners, worn around the neck and shoulders.
- **Braided palm hat** — A wide-brimmed hat with a pointed crown, brown band at rim, and orange and red zigzag decoration around the crown's centre.

### Architecture

Houses were traditionally built of wattle and daub or lumber with thatched roofs; the built environment is religiously charged with ceremonial circuits and a sacred earth mound at the settlement's center.

- **Navel mound** — A mound of earth at the middle of a settlement, representing the center of a world conceived as a square.
- **Ceremonial circuits** — Priests walk counterclockwise around the four corners of houses and fields, ending at the centre with offerings.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The most distinctive Tzotzil pottery form is the incensario, a ritual incense burner; spinning equipment and backstrap looms are common household objects, along with maize-leaf dolls.

- **Incensario** — A pottery incense burner on a circular base that tapers to a cylinder and flares into a bowl, with three sets of three prongs on the rim.
- **Wooden spindle with pottery whorl** — A double-pointed wooden spindle used with a pottery whorl for spinning thread.
- **Maize-leaf dolls** — Figurative works made from maize leaves with cloth heads and braided corn-silk hair tied with colored yarn.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources do not describe Tzotzil jewelry or body adornment in detail.

## Music & performance

Tzotzil musical traditions include guitar-playing in procession and the language preserves a word for flute; recordings document humorous narratives, songs and prayers.

- **Tzotzil guitar** — A single-piece hourglass-shaped wooden body with a central soundhole, glued-on bridge and neck, wooden frets, twelve tuning pegs and metal strings, with leather loops for standing play.
- **Flute** (*'ama*) — A wind instrument whose name is preserved in the Tzotzil language.
- **Songs and prayers in festival context** — Recorded between 1964 and 1972, Tzotzil songs and prayers are embedded in township fiestas rather than performed on a concert stage.

## Dance & theatre

Named Tzotzil dances are not documented; the closest performance genre is ritual humor and the nativity scene built as a tableau of maize-leaf figures.

- **Ritual humor** — Licensed joking documented at township fiestas of Saint Lawrence, Saint Sebastian, Christmas, New Year's Day and Epiphany.
- **Nativity scene** (*nacimiento*) — A tableau of maize-leaf figures mounted on wooden pegs, featuring a shepherd with a painted face and an apron, and a cowled Wise Man in a dark robe holding a nut shell as an offering.

## Festivals & rituals

The festival calendar follows Catholic saints' days—Saint Sebastian, Epiphany, New Year's Day, Christmas and Saint Lawrence—while parallel indigenous rituals honor the Earth Lord and ancestral gods.

- **Catholic saint veneration** — Carved wooden or plaster images of Catholic saints are revered, dressed in colonial and Zinacanteco-style clothing.
- **Earth Lord offerings** (*Yahwal Balamil*) — Anyone drawing water, mud, trees or limestone owes a compensating ceremony to the Earth Lord, pictured as a wealthy Ladino living underground.
- **Soul-loss curing** (*ch'ulel*) — A person who loses one or more of their thirteen-part inner soul needs a curing ceremony performed by a shaman, caused by fright, demons, ancestral punishment or witchcraft.
- **Animal companion spirits** (*chanul*) — Each person shares a second soul with a wild animal—jaguar, ocelot, coyote, squirrel or opossum—penned in corrals by the ancestral gods; release of the animal puts the person in mortal danger.
- **Ancestral god mountains** (*Totilme'il*) — The Fathers–Mothers inhabit local hills and are the most important gods; each town has its sacred mountain installed by a patron god.

## Foodways

The sources document production and trade rather than cooking: Tzotzil made salt from wells near Ixtapa and traded it throughout Chiapas, and exported quetzal feathers and amber to the Aztec capital.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tzotzil narratives are cosmological, centered on creation by the god Manojel-Tojel, who led humans from sacred caves, and the deities governing earth, water and celestial bodies.

- **Creation myth** (*Manojel-Tojel*) — The god who created human beings by leading them out of the caves of the original hills.
- **Earth and water deity** (*Yahwal Balamil*) — Lives inside the earth, rides a deer with serpent bridles, releases water-filled clouds through caves and announces himself with frog croaks.
- **World-bearing spirits** (*Vashak*) — Celestial beings upon whose shoulders the world rests.
- **Stone bat god** — A stone bat discovered and worshiped by the ancestors of Zinacantán and considered God.
- **Recorded spoken genres** — Humorous narratives, songs, prayers, and didactic lessons and texts recorded on tape between 1964 and 1972.

## Language & religion

Tzotzil is a Mayan language with verb–object–subject word order and ergative–absolutive agreement; religion syncretically blends Catholicism with indigenous Maya beliefs about celestial deities.

- **Tzotzil language** (*Bats'i k'op*) — Meaning 'true language,' descended from proto-Ch'ol and closely related to Tzeltal; six dialects are named for their regions.
- **Six regional dialects** — Chamula, Zinacantán, San Andrés Larráinzar, Huixtán, Chenalhó and Venustiano Carranza dialects are named for their regions.
- **Numeral classifiers** — Counted nouns take numeral classifiers matched to the shape of the thing counted in Tzotzil grammar.
- **Sun and Moon identification** — The Sun, 'Our Father Heat,' has been identified with God the Father or Jesus Christ; the Moon, 'Our Holy Mother,' with the Virgin Mary.
- **Venus veneration** (*'Sweeper of the Path'*) — Venus is honored because it precedes the Sun in the sky.

## Glossary

- *Bats'i k'op* — Tzotzil language, meaning 'true language'
- *mecapal* — Backstrap loom weaver's strap plaited from vegetable fiber and worn around the waist
- *hilaza* — Commercial cotton thread used in weaving alongside handspun yarn
- *huipil* — Woman's blouse or long overdress, built from sewn webs with brocaded patterning
- *enredo* — Wrap-around indigo-dyed skirt with white weft stripes and embroidered bands
- *faja* — Woven sash or belt that holds the enredo skirt up
- *duk* — Man's square cloth of striped or checked cotton with wool tassels, worn around neck and shoulders
- *incensario* — Pottery incense burner on a circular base with a flared bowl and prongs on the rim
- *nacimiento* — Nativity scene built as a tableau of maize-leaf figures mounted on wooden pegs
- *ch'ulel* — The inner soul, composed of thirteen parts that can be lost through fright or witchcraft
- *chanul* — A wild animal companion spirit—jaguar, ocelot, coyote, squirrel or opossum—that shares a person's soul
- *Yahwal Balamil* — Earth Lord deity who owns all land and resources; modern depictions show him as a wealthy Ladino underground
- *Totilme'il* — The Fathers–Mothers, ancestral divine couples who inhabit local hills and are the most important gods
- *Vashak* — Celestial beings upon whose shoulders the world rests
- *Manojel-Tojel* — Creator god who led human beings out of the caves of the original hills
