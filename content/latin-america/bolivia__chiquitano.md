## Overview

The Chiquitano, or Chiquitos, are an Indigenous people of Bolivia, with a small number also living in Brazil. They primarily live in the Chiquitania tropical savanna of Santa Cruz Department, with others in Beni Department and Mato Grosso, Brazil. In the 2012 census, self-identified Chiquitanos made up 1.45% of Bolivia’s total population, or 145,653 people. Approximately 40,000 to 60,000 Chiquitanos speak Chiquitano in Bolivia. Their ethnicity emerged among socially and linguistically diverse populations brought together by the Jesuit Missions of Chiquitos, where the Chiquito language became a common means of communication.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Loom-based weaving** — The Jesuits promoted loom-based weaving as part of economic life in the mission settlements.

### Architecture

The Chiquitos are associated in the sources with Indian huts whose small doors were said to have inspired the Spanish name Chiquitos, meaning “little ones.” During the mission period, Chiquitanos were confined to settlements known as reductions, including San Javier de los Piñocas, Concepción, San Ignacio, Santa Ana, San Rafael, San José, San Juan, Santiago, Santo Corazón, and San Miguel.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe Jesuit evangelization and state that the missionaries emphasized prayer and work as the main activities of a worthy life.

## Foodways

They mention agriculture, cattle husbandry, and the illnesses malaria, beriberi, and scurvy in the context of rubber labor.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record several published grammatical and lexicographic works, including a grammar and basic vocabulary of Chiquitano and a dictionary of Migueleño Chiquitano.

## Language & religion

Chiquitano, also called Tarapecosi, is a group of linguistic varieties spoken in eastern Bolivia and Mato Grosso, Brazil. It is sometimes considered a language isolate; other classifications connect it with Macro-Jê or describe it as a sister or branch of Macro-Jê. The language is written in the Latin script, and men’s and women’s speech differ grammatically. Four dialects identified in one account are Manasi, Peñoqui, Piñoco, and Tao. Modern varieties include Bésɨro, Migueleño, Ignaciano, and Santiagueño. The Chiquitano ethnicity was shaped by Jesuit evangelization and settlement in reductions from the arrival of missionaries at San Javier de los Piñocas in 1692 until their expulsion in 1767.
