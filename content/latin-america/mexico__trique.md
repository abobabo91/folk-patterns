## Overview

The Triqui, also called Trique, are an Indigenous people of the western part of the Mexican state of Oaxaca, centered in the municipalities of Juxtlahuaca, Putla, and Tlaxiaco. They live in a mountainous region known as *La Mixteca Baja*, where elevations range between 1,500 and 3,000 m. The sources give a population of around 23,000 according to Ethnologue surveys, while the related language article describes 30,000 Trique people in Oaxaca and Baja California in 2007 and 5,000 immigrants in the United States. The Triqui language belongs to the Mixtecan branch of the Oto-Manguean family. Triqui cultural life is documented through woven *huipiles*, baskets, and *morrales*, agricultural cooperation, livestock raising, migration, local markets, basketball tournaments, and a complex tonal language tradition.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Woven huipiles** — The sources identify distinctive woven *huipiles* as a notable feature of Triqui material culture.

**Baskets** — Baskets are named among the distinctive objects associated with Trique peoples.

**Morrales** — *Morrales* are identified as handbags associated with Trique peoples.

No further pattern-bearing textile traditions or named motifs are covered by the sources used.

### Clothing & dress

The sources mention woven *huipiles* and *morrales*. Head coverings, belts, footwear, textile materials, and ceremonial clothing are not covered by the sources used.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources identify baskets and *morrales* as culturally distinctive objects.

## Music & performance

They do document Triqui-language broadcasting through the CDI radio stations XEQIN-AM, based in San Quintín, Baja California, and XETLA, based in Tlaxiaco, Oaxaca. A 2020 bibliography entry concerns sung performances of children’s songs in Chicahuaxtla Triqui. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription concerns this ethnic group.

## Dance & theatre

The documentary film *Gigantes Descalzos* is identified as chronicling a team of Triqui youth basketball players, but the sources give no further information about its form or content.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe local festivals as occasions for basketball tournaments in which athletes participate. Educational institutes also hold basketball tournaments among surrounding schools.

A notable custom is bride price. During pre-colonial and colonial times, the practice was common among Native Americans in Mesoamerica, and groups such as the Mixtecs of Oaxaca continue practicing bride-price marriage. In Trique culture, a man typically offers the bride’s family money, food, and other products in exchange for the bride’s hand in marriage. The prospective husband and wife generally know each other beforehand, and the arrangement requires consent. The sources record disagreement about the custom: opponents compare it to slavery or prostitution, while those opposing intervention argue that consent is required and that the custom is not conceived of as immoral.

## Foodways

Triqui agriculture includes corn, beans, pumpkin, chili peppers, *quelite*, and lima beans. The most important crops are described as banana and coffee bean. These products are sold in *tianguis*, or open-air markets, in Chicahuaztla, Copala, San Martín, Itunyoso, Tlaxiaco, Putla, and Juxtlahuaca.

The sources state that men may offer money, food, and other products in bride-price arrangements.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources record several publications concerning Triqui oral tradition. *The Origin of the Sun and Moon: A Copala Triqui Legend* was published in 2009. Other listed works include *El origen del sol y de la luna – cuatro versiones en el trique de Copala*, published in 1977; *Three Trique myths of San Juan Copala*, published in 1988; and the 2020 work *Dàj guruguiˈ yumiguiì ‘de como apareció la gente del mundo’: leyenda en triqui de Chicahuaxtla*. A 2025 publication concerns the Chicahuaxtla Triqui legend *Ruhuâ Ruˈman Hioˈóo Gatsii ‘In the Hole of White Dirt’*. They do document linguistic and documentary work, including dictionaries, wordlists, recordings, and studies of children’s songs.

## Language & religion

Triqui or Trique is a family of Oto-Manguean languages in the Mixtecan branch, together with the Mixtec languages and Cuicatec. The sources identify three major varieties: Triqui de Copala, spoken by 15,000 people in a 1990 census; Triqui de San Andrés Chicahuaxtla, spoken by 6,000 people; and Triqui de San Martín Itunyoso, spoken by 2,000 people in a 1983 survey. In 2008, Mexico’s Instituto Nacional de Lenguas Indígenas identified four varieties of Trique.

All varieties are tonal. Copala Triqui has eight tones, Itunyoso Triqui has nine, and Chicahuaxtla Triqui has at least 10 and possibly as many as 16. Triqui uses different orthographies according to audience, including fully marked linguistic writing and a simpler practical orthography for native speakers. Its morphology includes a *k-* prefix for completive aspect and a related form with tonal change for potential aspect.
