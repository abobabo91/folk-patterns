## Overview

The Motilones-Barí, also called Barís, Motilones, or Dobocubis, are Indigenous people living in the Catatumbo River basin in Norte de Santander Department in Colombia, in South America. They speak the Barí language, a member of the Chibchan language family. The Barí are descendants of the Tairona culture concentrated in northeastern Colombia and western Venezuela. The Barí have been affected by Spanish settlement, oil drilling, Christian missions, and the integration of Western goods into local life. Their documented folk-culture significance includes collective fatherhood, extensive ethnobotanical knowledge, gardening and cacao production, Indigenous-run schools, literacy programs, medical clinics, and efforts to introduce Christianity to surrounding tribes.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The name “Motilones,” used by Spanish-speaking Colombians and Venezuelans, means “shaved heads” in Spanish and is not the name the Barí use for themselves.

### Architecture

They do record a network of schools, community centers, and health clinics established after large numbers of Barí converted to Christianity.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that the Barí use 80% of the plants around them and possess extensive knowledge of biodiversity in Amazonia.

## Music & performance

The Barí were among the peoples depicted in the 1921 documentary *Blandt Syd-Amerikas urskovsindianere* (*Among the Primeval Forest Indians of South America*), but the supplied material does not identify its music or performance content. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription or museum catalogue record concerning Barí music is supplied.

## Dance & theatre

The 1921 documentary *Blandt Syd-Amerikas urskovsindianere* included the Barí among the peoples it depicted.

## Festivals & rituals

They document collective fatherhood: a child has multiple fathers, understood as the men who had sex with the mother before she gave birth, with one man often serving as the “primary” father and the others as “secondary” fathers. The sources also state that large numbers of the Barí converted to Christianity and that the Motilone Bari undertook efforts to introduce Christianity to surrounding tribes.

## Foodways

The Barí people’s chief economic activity is growing *Theobroma cacao*, the plant from which chocolate is made. They export cacao and use the proceeds to help maintain schools, community centers, and health clinics. Since the initial contact in 1650, Barí land has been reduced to 7% of its original mass, and Barí production has shifted toward gardening cash crops used to acquire Western goods.

## Oral tradition & literature

They mention the missionary Bruce Olson’s book *Bruchko*, in which he writes that he began living with the Barí in 1962 and became the “blood brother” of a chieftain’s son. Olson describes what he calls the “Motilone Miracle,” involving Indigenous-run schools, literacy programs, medical clinics, and efforts by the Motilone Bari to introduce Christianity to surrounding tribes. The sources also identify the French ethnologist Robert Jaulin as having studied the Barí.

## Language & religion

Barí is a Chibchan language spoken in Northwestern South America by the Barí, also called Motilon. The supplied source reports 5,000 speakers between 2007 and 2008, and states that 150 speakers, primarily elderly, were monolingual in Barí as of 2004. Children learn Barí as their first language, while young men between 20 and 40 tend to use Spanish in everyday conversation and use Barí when there are strangers. Spanish is taught in school. Barí has six vowels, each with nasalized forms. The language is described as not endangered, although the Barí have acculturated to the national culture and the community has assimilated culturally to Venezuelan culture while keeping a few traditional customs. Current estimates state that 95% of the Barí people are Catholics.
