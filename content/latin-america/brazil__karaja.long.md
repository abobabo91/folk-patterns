## Overview

The Karajá, who call themselves *Iny*, are an Indigenous people of the Araguaia River valley in central Brazil, spread across a band of country some 180 miles long in the states of Goiás, Mato Grosso, Pará and Tocantins. They live in villages beside lakes and tributaries of the Araguaia and Javaés rivers and on the Ilha do Bananal — twenty-nine villages by one count, around twenty by another — and number roughly 3,200 to 3,700 people today, down from a reported 45,000 earlier in the twentieth century. Their language, *Iny rybè*, belongs to the Macro-Jê family and is still the first language children learn. In folk-culture terms the Karajá are known above all for women's painted ceramics and ceramic dolls, for men's feather diadems, for body painting, and for the masked *aruanã* ceremonies of the dry season.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Body painting** — The living pattern medium of Karajá art, practised above all by women, using paints made from genipap juice, charcoal and annatto dye. A museum photograph of Kudioéna, a young chief on Bananal Island dressed in festival ornaments, records the black circles on the cheeks the cataloguer calls typical of the Karajá.

**Featherwork diadems** (*raheto*, *lori-lori*) — Men's head ornaments, made by men. Light pink and white feathers are glued to a low ring of plant fibre, or bundled onto a fibre net; at the front rises a thread-cross of black and white cotton yarn crowned by feather tufts, or a set of upright sticks and tall blue feathers. One collected example mounts feathers of roseate spoonbill and blue-and-yellow macaw, each in a tubular casing knotted with cotton laces through two armadillo belly plates, and was worn by men only at special ceremonies.

**Feather-and-raffia belts** (*wetakana*) — Waist ornaments combining feathers with raffia; the same pairing of plumage and plaited palm fibre recurs in a mat board, *bykyrè ritxore*, worked in feathers, raffia and wood and catalogued as carrying dance figures.

**Basketry** (*taririny*) — Both women and men weave. A collected basket is of plaited grass with a dark cotton carrying loop and three bunches of yellow feathers; basketwork also forms the body of the *aruanã* mask and the patterned plaiting at the handle of a gourd rattle.

**Knitted cotton forearm cuffs** — Pairs of cuff-shaped forearm ornaments in red-dyed cotton yarn, tightly knitted with flared edges. Paul Ehrenreich illustrated such cuffs, and a warrior wearing them, in *Beiträge zur Völkerkunde Brasiliens* (Berlin, 1891).

**Painted pottery surface** — Women's vessels carry black and red linear schemes: rhomboid ornaments in black over a brown ground, black line patterning on the upper body of a tall jar, red lines dividing the inside of a plate into four fields.

**Pyro-engraved and incised gourd** — Calabash dishes and rattles worked by men and used by both sexes, their surfaces cut into crossing rectangular fields or panels of burnt line, sometimes painted.

**Motif vocabulary.** Black cheek circles (the diagnostic Karajá face design); rhomboid ornament in black; a red rim band enclosing a stylized human figure with lesser linear figures around it (described as the style most common at Tapirapé); four fields marked off by red lines; crossing rectangular fields on gourd; squares standing for the plates of a turtle's shell; three groups of three strokes along the flanks of a jaguar figure; the dyed "cross" and the woven thread-cross on head-dresses.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe adornment far more fully than garments. A ceramic doll wears a bast loin skirt, and the *aruanã* mask comes with two grass skirts that clothe the dancer. Men's dress, as collected, is a set of parts rather than a costume: the feather diadem (*raheto*, *lori-lori*) or the plain head-ring; a raffia ornament of two joined discs with a dyed cross design belonging to a *raheto*; the feather-and-raffia belt *wetakana*; a textile armlet, *dexi*, described as a man's; knitted red cotton cuffs on the forearms; a necklace, *nõhõ*, of wood, feathers and vegetable fibre; ear ornaments of incised wood, *nõhõtisana*, with plainer ear-staves recorded as a youth's; a lip ornament, *olyò* or *tembetá*, in quartz, and a wooden lip-stave for a man; and a rattle, *weru*, of tortoise carapace, feathers, wood and string, worn as a man's ornament on the upper leg. Ceremonial dress is explicitly set apart: one feather ornament is noted as worn only by men during special ceremonies, and the chief Kudioéna was photographed in festival ornaments. A comb, *sihò*, is made of tucum spines.

### Architecture

The sources used here describe Karajá settlement rather than Karajá building. The people are highly mobile: families often put up temporary fishing camps, the dry season is the time of festivals, and in the rainy season families move to villages on higher ground. House form, materials, roofing and any architectural decoration are not covered by the sources used.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Only women make ceramics. A documented potter, Shurerea, of Santa Isabel on the Bananal Island side of the Araguaia, made a tall jar painted with black patterns, a wide bowl with brown and black rhomboid ornament, and a seated painted figure; other vessels from the same village include flat-bottomed plates and small jars in pale ware with black line work. The language itself names the form — *butxi* (northern) or *boti* (southern), 'clay pot'. Modelled clay animals are a second strand: crocodile, turtle, jaguar and macaw figurines, incised and painted. Best known are the *ritxokó* clay dolls, among them women's figures with painted ornament and bast skirts; ceramic dolls are commonly made for export. Men work gourd and wood: painted calabash spoons and incised calabash dishes, a wooden smoking pipe (*werikoko*), a scarificator of wood, bone and wax (*latxi*), painted and feathered models of grave posts, and the weapons — bow (*waxiwahatè*) of wood and cotton string, arrow (*wyhy*), fish-arrow (*wyhy kotura rbuna*) tipped with bone, club (*kòhòtè*) and spear (*tõnõri*). The sources record no metalworking; metal enters the language as loans, *mỹkawa* 'firearm' and *nieru* 'money'.

## Jewelry & body adornment

Karajá adornment is of feather, bone, shell, wood, quartz, cotton and paint. Men's pieces dominate the collections: lip ornaments of quartz and of wood, incised wooden ear ornaments (*nõhõtisana*) with simpler ear-staves for youths, wooden-and-feather necklaces (*nõhõ*), knitted cotton forearm cuffs, feather belts and diadems, and the tortoise-carapace leg rattle *weru*. The scarificator *latxi*, of wood set with bone and fixed with wax, shows that the skin itself was worked. Painting is the most conspicuous practice — genipap juice, charcoal and annatto, with the black cheek circles as the Karajá signature — and the ceramic dolls preserve a hairstyle as well: black hair with a red braid rendered in the painted decoration.

## Music & performance

The instruments recorded are a gourd-and-bamboo trumpet, *jurona*, catalogued as a decoy, and rattles: gourd rattles on wooden handles, one with a feather tuft and fields of burnt line, another with patterned basket plaiting at the join, and the tortoise-shell *weru* tied to a man's upper leg so that the dancer sounds his own step. Song has its own vocabulary and its own genderlect: 'song' spoken by a man is *wi* in Northern Karajá and *wiu* in the South, and the Karajá word *wii* 'song, chant' was borrowed into Mẽbêngôkre as *wiwi*. Performance belongs to the dry season, when the villages host festivals.

## Dance & theatre

The named tradition is the **aruanã** masquerade. A mask collected at Barra do Tapirapé in December 1962 is a tall hood of basketwork and feathers with two accompanying grass skirts, catalogued for ceremonial use. Masking is attested further by loanword evidence: Karajá took *txakohi*, the Txakohi ceremonial mask, from Apyãwa, and gave Apyãwa *irãwore*, the Irabure ceremonial mask. The feathered mat board *bykyrè ritxore* is recorded as bearing dance figures.

## Festivals & rituals

The festival calendar follows the river's year: the Karajá hold their festivals during the dry season, while the rains scatter families to villages on high ground and to fishing camps. Ritual objects in the collections point to specialists and to the dead — a shaman's staff, *obi*, its stem covered with red feathers and white down and finished with a blue macaw feather, and painted, feathered models of grave posts, made by men and used for both sexes. The scarificator *latxi* and the ear-staves made for a youth indicate bodily rites and an age-marked progression in ornament, and one feather head-dress is recorded as worn by men only at special ceremonies. Life-cycle rites of birth, marriage and death are not otherwise described in the sources used.

## Foodways

Karajá livelihood rests on farming, fishing, hunting and craft, worked by swidden (slash-and-burn) cultivation. The crops are diverse: bananas, beans, manioc, maize, peanuts, potatoes, watermelons and yams. Fishing is of the first importance, and the language names the catch — *bdòlèkè* (northern) or *bdòkùjkè* (southern) 'pirarucu', *idjasò* 'arowana', *ati* 'mandi fish' in Xambioá. Other food words include *bdi* 'honey', *tãtã* 'banana' (a Karajá word carried into Apyãwa as *sata*), *makiti* 'sugarcane' (Javaé and Xambioá *biditi*), *kòmỹta* 'beans' and *jykyra* 'salt', both from Língua Geral, and *rkù* 'gourd'. Two prepared dishes surface through loanwords: *marara*, turtle stew, borrowed into Apyãwa from Karajá, and *bèrò*, puba flour, borrowed into Mẽbêngôkre as *bero*. The vessels match the diet: wide plates and flat-bottomed bowls, tall ollas, small jars, a painted calabash spoon and an incised calabash dish made by men and used by both sexes. The sources used describe no ceremonial dishes and no tea or coffee custom.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources name one figure of Karajá narrative: Uaica, a hunter in Karajá legend. Documentation, rather than published literature, is where the sources are rich. Eduardo Rivail Ribeiro's *A Grammar of Karajá* (2012) is the thorough descriptive treatment, covering phonology, verb and noun morphology, direction, valence and the male and female genderlects. Earlier and parallel work includes Fortune and Fortune's phoneme study (1963), Adriana Viana on attribution (1995), Marcus Maia on Karajá clause structure, interrogatives and evidentials and on bilingualism in Karajá schooling, do Socorro Pimentel da Silva on the pressure of Portuguese, and Fulop and Warren on advanced tongue root in the vowel system. The Museu do Índio's ProDocLín project, coordinated by Cristiane Oliveira, worked with community members on media and recording technique to digitize and expand documentation of Karajá language and culture, with an encyclopedic dictionary among its aims.

## Language & religion

Karajá, or *Iny rybè*, is a Macro-Jê language, proposed for that family as early as 1886 but left unclassified for most of the twentieth century until about 1967. It is verb-final, with simple nominal and complex verbal morphology, noun incorporation, and obligatory inflection for direction on every verb. There are four dialects — Northern Karajá, Southern Karajá, Javaé and Xambioá — of which Xambioá was reported with only eight fluent speakers, all elderly, in 1998. Men's and women's speech differ, most conspicuously in that men drop a /k/ that women pronounce; Ribeiro suggests the k-drop became a mark of masculinity that women resisted, keeping the more conservative form. Loanwords from Apyãwa, from Língua Geral and from Brazilian Portuguese record long contact. On religion the sources say only what the objects show: a shaman's staff and grave-post figures.
