## Overview

The Guna are an Indigenous people of the Panamanian and Colombian Caribbean with about 50,000–70,000 speakers of Dulegaya. They live mainly on coral islands and mainland forest, organized around three comarcas and communities in larger cities. They are known worldwide for the mola, a layered textile created by reverse appliqué, and for an unbroken political-ritual system in which the saila sings village history and law in a congress house. Guna women wear a complete daily dress ensemble of mola blouse, wrap skirt, head cloth, and beadwork that is one of the most consistently maintained Indigenous costumes in the Americas.

## Material culture

Guna material culture is built from palm, wood, cane and imported trade goods rather than from local ceramics or metalworking traditions.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The mola is a layered reverse-appliqué textile that is cut and stitched to reveal contrasting colours underneath, and it has become one of the world's most recognizable Indigenous art forms.

- **Mola (blouse panel)** (*tulemola or dulemola*) — Two rectangular panels of plain-woven cotton in which the maker cuts through two to seven layers and turns edges under with fine blind stitching, so the design emerges from what has been removed.
- **High-status gold mola** (*mola olo*) — Panels named for the prestige colour olo, associated with wealth and ceremonial display.
- **Ritual specialist mola** (*mola kantule*) — Panels derived from the chants and ritual knowledge of the kantule flute-master, belonging to the older, more abstract and esoteric end of the mola repertoire.
- **World symbol** (*Naa Ukuryaa*) — The ancestral four-armed hooked cross of the Guna flag, read as the four quarters of the world or as the world-making octopus.
- **Carved tutelary figure** (*nuchu*) — Motif carved in wood and also rendered in mola cloth, representing a spiritual guardian.

### Clothing & dress

Guna women's dress is worn daily and is one of the most consistently maintained Indigenous costumes in the Americas, with each piece serving a distinct purpose.

- **Complete women's dress ensemble** (*dule mor*) — Literally 'Guna clothing,' consisting of the mola blouse, wrap skirt, head cloth, bead bands, and gold ornaments, worn as daily costume from puberty onward.
- **Wrap skirt** (*saburet*) — A length of printed or dark patterned cotton wound around the hips, made from commercially traded cloth.
- **Head cloth** (*muswe*) — Red-and-yellow patterned cloth draped over the hair and shoulders as the third fixed component of the women's ensemble.
- **Bead bands** (*wini mor or winis*) — Strung glass-bead bands wound tightly on forearms and lower legs, conceived as clothing for the body rather than separate jewellery.
- **Nose line** — A line of dark pigment drawn down the ridge of the nose as the final element of daily dress.

### Architecture

Island villages are built wall to wall along narrow sand lanes to fit the tiny coral cays, with two public buildings anchoring every community.

- **Dwelling** — A rectangular post-and-beam hut with steeply pitched thatch roof of palm leaf, split cane or bamboo walls, earth or sand floor, no windows, and hammocks for sleeping.
- **Congress house** (*onmaked nega or Ibeorgun Nega*) — The largest structure in the village, a great open-sided thatched hall where the saila lies in a central hammock and sings sacred history, laws and legends while voceros translate.
- **Chicha house** (*inna nega*) — A long thatched hall where the fermented drink for puberty ceremonies is brewed and consumed, serving as both ritual and civic centre.
- **Cooking structure** — An adjoining separate structure where the cooking hearth occupies space apart from the main dwelling.
- **Canoe** (*ulu*) — Dugout canoe used to reach mainland garden plots and water rivers.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Guna lack a ceramic or smithing tradition of their own, instead carving wooden figures and working with palm, cane, gourd and imported metal.

- **Tutelary figure** (*nuchu*) — A carved wooden figure cut from balsa or light wood in stiff, frontal form, houses a tutelary spirit, kept in the household basket, and used by curers in healing chants.
- **Protective figure** (*nudirbi*) — A hardwood protective figure related to the nuchu, part of the same spiritual guardian family.
- **Hammock** — Serves as furniture, bed, and the saila's seat of office.
- **Carrying tray** — Basketry trays for household transport.
- **Bowl and dipper** — Gourd vessels for food preparation and serving.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Guna adornment is dense, permanent and worn from childhood, with beaded spirals on the limbs and gold ornaments marking wealth and status.

- **Beaded limb bands** (*winis*) — Long strings of small glass trade beads wound in tight patterned spirals around forearms and lower legs, building continuous geometric bands of colour that read as a second skin.
- **Gold nose ring** (*olasu*) — A prestige gold ornament worn in the nose, important enough that a red ring representing it was added to the Guna flag in 1942 to prevent mistaking the emblem for a Nazi swastika.
- **Gold earrings** — Large flat disc earrings worn as prestige metal adornment.
- **Gold pectoral ornaments** — Heavy gold pectoral plates and necklaces worn at ceremonies, representing accumulated household wealth.
- **Face painting** — Black line down the nose worn daily, with fuller red-and-black designs from achiote and plant pigments at ceremonies.

## Music & performance

The central Guna musical institution is the sung word: the saila chants village history and law in a specialized register, answered by an interpreter.

- **Saila's chant** — Half-lying in his hammock in the congress house, the saila chants village history, myth and law in a specialized high register, simultaneously scripture, legislation and archive.
- **Flute-master** (*kantule*) — A parallel specialist who chants the long ceremonial sequences of the inna festivals and directs its music.
- **Bundled cane flutes** (*gammu burui or gaa burui*) — A set of end-blown cane flutes of graded length played in interlocking pairs, giving the hocketed, interwoven melodic texture characteristic of Chibchan panpipe traditions.
- **Healing chant** (*Igar chants, such as Muu Igala*) — Long therapeutic chants recited by the nele seer and inatuledi herbalist-curer, addressing nuchu figures for purposes like assisting difficult childbirth.
- **Rattles** (*nasis*) — Gourd and seed rattles that accompany the ceremonies.

## Dance & theatre

Guna performance is tied to the chicha ceremonies rather than to a theatrical stage, with lines of dancers moving in formation to paired flutes and rattles.

- **Inna dance** — Lines of dancers move in circling and processional formation to paired cane flutes and rattles at the chicha festivals, with women and men in separate rows and the kantule leading.
- **Curing chant performance** — The dramatic element of performance belongs to the curing chant, where the chanter narrates a journey through the spirit world on the patient's behalf and carved nuchu figures act as the cast.
- **Folkloric troupe performance** — Since the later twentieth century, folkloric troupes from Guna Yala have performed the flute-and-dance repertoire at Panamanian national festivals and for visitors in the Carti tourist zone.

## Festivals & rituals

The Guna ceremonial year is organized around life-cycle events, with the great public ceremonies being the multi-day inna chicha festivals.

- **Girl's puberty ceremony** (*inna suid or 'long chicha'*) — A multi-day festival in which the girl's hair is ritually cut, she is painted, dressed for the first time in the full dule mor, and chicha is brewed and consumed.
- **Early childhood rite** — A smaller earlier ceremony marking the naming and first haircut.
- **Marriage ceremony** — The groom is placed in the hammock with the bride; Guna families are matrilineal and matrilocal, so he takes her family name.
- **Death ritual** (*masar igar mourners' chants*) — Burial in mainland forest cemeteries with the deceased laid in a hammock under a small thatched roof, with mourners' chants sung over the body.
- **Political congress** — Daily or near-daily evening gatherings in the onmaked nega, and periodic sessions of the Guna General Congress under the three Saila Dummagan.

## Foodways

The Guna diet rests on plantains, coconuts and fish, supplemented by mainland garden crops and reef resources.

- **Plantain dishes** — Boiled, roasted, or pounded into thick plantain soups and drinks that are the everyday staple.
- **Fish and lobster in coconut milk** — The archipelago's best-known dish, simmered with plantain or rice in coconut milk base.
- **Lobster** (*skungit*) — The primary seafood export from the reef, forming the archipelago's main trade commodity along with coconut.
- **Ceremonial fermented drink** (*inna*) — Fermented maize or sugarcane chicha brewed specifically for puberty festivals and drunk only in the inna nega by specialist brewers.
- **Garden crops** — Yuca, yams, maize, rice, sugarcane and tree fruits reached by canoe to mainland garden plots.

## Oral tradition & literature

Guna oral tradition is unusually well recorded and still fully functional, centred on long chanted texts called igar that specialists memorise and perform.

- **Historical and legal chants** (*igar*) — Long chanted texts memorised by the saila, each a route through mythic or legal terrain forming the backbone of Guna oral tradition.
- **Childbirth assistance chant** (*Muu Igala or 'The Way of Muu'*) — A healing chant sung by curers to assist difficult childbirth, one of the best-known examples of the igar genre.
- **Culture-hero founding narrative** (*Ibeorgun*) — A narrative cycle about the figure who taught the Guna their law, their crafts and the form of the congress house bearing his name.
- **Cosmological symbol** (*Naa Ukuryaa*) — The octopus or four-quartered world central to Guna cosmology and rendered in mola cloth and flag design.
- **Published chant collection** (*Igargan Cantos*) — A bilingual printed collection carrying the oral tradition into written form.

## Language & religion

Dulegaya is a Chibchan language with a markedly distinct high register for chant, while religion is an indigenous cosmology lightly overlaid by Christianity.

- **Creator deities** (*Baba and Nana*) — Father and Mother, the creator figures at the centre of Guna cosmology.
- **Specialist seer** (*nele*) — A religious specialist with visionary knowledge of the spirit world.
- **Herbalist-curer** (*inatuledi*) — A healer specializing in medicinal plants and therapeutic chants.
- **Chant-therapist** (*absogedi*) — A specialist in therapeutic chanting and healing through song.
- **High ritual register** — A markedly distinct version of Dulegaya reserved for chant, with its own lexicon and formulaic diction, interpreted to ordinary speech by the vocero.

## Glossary

- *Dule or Tule* — Guna self-designation meaning 'people'
- *Dulegaya* — The Guna language, literally 'people-mouth'
- *mola* — Reverse-appliqué layered textile panel; also means 'clothing' in Dulegaya
- *tulemola or dulemola* — The mola blouse as a sewn garment
- *dule mor* — Complete women's dress ensemble, literally 'Guna clothing'
- *saburet* — Wrap skirt worn below the mola blouse
- *muswe* — Red-and-yellow patterned head cloth
- *nuchu* — Carved wooden tutelary spirit figure
- *Naa Ukuryaa* — The four-armed hooked cross symbol of the world or world-making octopus
- *onmaked nega* — Congress house where the saila sings and political decisions are made
- *inna nega* — Chicha house where ceremonial fermented drink is brewed and consumed
- *saila* — Village chief and sung-word specialist who chants history and law
- *kantule* — Flute-master and ceremonial chant specialist
- *vocero* — Interpreter who translates the saila's high ritual register into everyday speech
- *gammu burui or gaa burui* — Bundled set of end-blown cane flutes played in interlocking pairs
- *inna* — Chicha festival or fermented maize/sugarcane drink served at it
- *igar* — Long chanted text or 'way' or 'path' memorised by specialists
- *Muu Igala* — 'The Way of Muu', a healing chant for difficult childbirth
- *nele* — Seer or visionary specialist in healing and spirit knowledge
- *inatuledi* — Herbalist-curer specialist
- *ulu* — Dugout canoe used to reach mainland
- *skungit* — Lobster
