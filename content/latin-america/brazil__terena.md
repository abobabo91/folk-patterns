## Overview

The Terena are a Brazilian Indigenous people whose earlier homeland was the northeastern Paraguayan Chaco, west of the Paraguay River. They now live mainly in the municipalities of Aquidauana and Miranda in Mato Grosso do Sul, as well as in Mato Grosso and São Paulo. The Aquidauana-Miranda region lies between 20° and 22° S and 54° and 58° W and includes approximately 29 villages. The Terena are one of four Guaná subgroups that relocated, alongside the Exoaladi, Layana, and the Kinkinau. Their population increased from approximately 3,000 people in the mid 1840s to 3,800 in 1950 and 12,000 in the 1980s. Their language belongs to the Arawak family, and agriculture, pottery, cotton spinning, and hospitality are documented aspects of their cultural life.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document several historical events affecting the Terena. The Paraguayan War began in 1864 and ended in 1870; during the late 1860s, Terena people fought jointly with Brazilian military forces and suffered attacks and losses. The Buriti Farm incident began with a re-occupation on May 15, 2013, and the occupants were forcibly evicted on May 30. These are historical and political events rather than festivals or ritual observances.

## Foodways

The Terena are described as an agriculturally focused society. Principal traditional crops include cassava, rice, beans, maize, potatoes, and sugarcane. Both men and women have historically gathered food, and surplus crops are sold in the Mato Grosso do Sul center, which the Terena can reach nearly daily by train. Cattle-raising is also prominent on Terena farms, although limited reserve land has made it a source of internal conflict. Chickens and pigs are also kept.

## Language & religion

Terêna, also called Etelena, is an Arawakan language spoken by the Terena people of Brazil, particularly in Mato Grosso do Sul. It has a dictionary and written grammar. About 20% of speakers are literate in their language and 80% are literate in Portuguese; many Terena people have low Portuguese proficiency. Terêna originally had four varieties: Kinikinao, Terena proper, Guaná, and Chané. These have sometimes been treated as separate languages, but Carvalho (2016) demonstrated that they are the same language, and only Terena proper is still spoken. The language has active–stative syntax and verb-object-subject as its default word order. Christianity has influenced Terena marriage practices, and monogamy has become typical.
