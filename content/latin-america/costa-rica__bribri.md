## Overview

The Bribri, also known as Abicetava, are an Indigenous people of eastern Costa Rica and northern Panama. They are indigenous to the Talamanca region and live in the mountains and Caribbean coastal areas, including the Talamanca Valley and the Sixaola River area. Estimates of their population vary: official estimates describe about 11,500 Bribri people in Costa Rica and about 1000 in Panama, while other estimates reach 35,000. Bribri is a Chibchan language spoken in the Isthmo-Colombian Area. The group is culturally significant for its matrilineal clan system, cacao ceremonies, shamanic healing, conical-house cosmology, oral transmission of knowledge, and continued political efforts to preserve land, language, and cultural practices.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state that Bribri communities collect housing materials in the forest and that some Bribri people sell cacao, bananas, plantains, and other produce.

### Architecture

The central architectural form described in the sources is the conical house. Bribri spiritual practice centers on this structure, which represents the universe. It is supported by eight pillars symbolizing the animals that helped Sibú construct the Universe. The house has four levels corresponding to four levels of the world. The ground level is the plane inhabited by people; the second contains the spirits of plants and animals, the owners of the rivers, and Sibú’s helpers; the third contains spirits associated with disease and suffering; and the highest level is where Sibú and his helper, the king of vultures, live. Three further levels are located beneath the inhabited world, including one where Bribri souls go after death.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention housing materials collected in the forest, but provide no catalogue of particular forms or techniques.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They state that iguanas are hunted for food and skin in the community of Kekoldi.

## Music & performance

Music and performance are closely connected with Bribri ritual and healing. The *awa*, or shaman, learns special songs that establish a connection with the spirits of the plant, the disease, and the person being treated. Once this connection has been made, the *awa* converses with the three spirits and, with the aid of the plant spirit, persuades the disease to leave the person. Knowledge of healing is transmitted orally from an older *awa* to an apprentice.

The *oko* is the only person allowed to touch the remains of the dead, sing funeral songs, and prepare the food eaten at funerals. The sources therefore connect funeral singing with a particular male social role.

The sources state that the Bribri language is tonal and that its stressed syllables distinguish high and falling tones.

## Dance & theatre

They mention ritual songs, funeral songs, ceremonies, and rites of passage.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe several ritual contexts. Cacao has special significance in Bribri culture, and the cacao tree is understood to have formerly been a woman whom Sibú turned into a tree. Cacao branches are not used as firewood, and only women may prepare and serve the sacred cacao drink. Cacao is used during special occasions, ceremonies, and rites of passage, including the first menstruation of young girls.

Bribri social organization is based on clans composed of extended families. The clan system is matrilineal: a child’s clan is determined by the mother’s clan. Women inherit land and prepare the sacred cacao drink essential to ritual life. Men’s roles are defined by their clans; examples include the *awa* and the *oko*.

Funeral practice includes the work of the *oko*, who touches the remains of the dead, sings funeral songs, and prepares funeral food. Bribri healing combines herbal medicine and spiritual healing. Illnesses may be attributed to evil spirits from the ocean in the west, immoral behavior, or witchcraft from envious neighbors.

## Foodways

Bribri subsistence and economy center on agriculture. The main crops grown for sale include cacao, bananas, and plantains. Beans, rice, corn, and other produce are consumed, while some Bribri families also raise livestock. Their economy includes barter, the sale of excess produce, tourism, and the collection of food and medicine in the forest.

Cacao is the most specifically documented ceremonial foodway. Women prepare and serve a sacred cacao drink, and cacao is used during ceremonies and rites of passage. Several Bribri women’s associations produce organic handmade chocolate as a livelihood.

The Kekoldi community practices iguana farming. The community has about 200 people, and its iguanas are released into the forest so that other Bribri can hunt them for food and skin. The farm has operated for 11 years and has about 2,000 iguanas and 2,000,000 eggs. Iguanas remain on the farm until five years of age, when they are released into the wild.

The sources state that the *oko* prepares the food eaten at funerals.

## Oral tradition & literature

Bribri knowledge is transmitted orally. In healing, an older *awa* passes knowledge to an apprentice, including the songs and knowledge needed to communicate with the spirits of plants, diseases, and people. The sources also describe oral transmission as central to the preservation of spiritual practices and cultural knowledge.

The Bribri language has been documented through courses, dictionaries, stories, linguistic studies, recordings, and transcriptions. The bibliography names *I ttè. Historias bribris*, *Kó késka. El lugar del tiempo*, *Seꞌ ẽꞌ yawö bribri wa. Aprendemos la lengua bribri*, and *Seꞌ ttö́ bribri ie. Hablemos en bribri*. An external project provides Bribri texts, audios, and transcriptions by Carla V. Jara and Alí García Segura.

## Language & religion

Bribri, also known as Bri-bri, Bribriwak, and Bribri-wak, is a Chibchan language. As of 2002, about 11,000 speakers remained. A National Census of Costa Rica estimate from 2011 found that Bribri was spoken by 54.7% of 12,785 Bribri people, or about 7,000 individuals. The language has three traditional dialects: Coroma in the western Talamanca mountain range, Amubre in the eastern region, and Salitre in the South Pacific area. Bribri is also called *Su Uhtuk*, meaning “our language.” It has subject–object–verb word order and is tonal.

Bribri spiritual life centers on Sibú, described as God, and on a cosmology represented by the conical house. The king vulture, *Sarcoramphus papa*, links Sibú with the other worlds because it is believed to be the only being able to fly to the top of the Universe. The *awa* combines herbal and spiritual healing, while the *oko* has a specialized funeral role.
