## Overview

The Chamacoco, whose autonym is *Ishír* (also written *Ɨshɨr*, *Yshyr*, *Ishiro*, and sometimes *Jewyo*), are an Indigenous people of the Gran Chaco in Paraguay, with a small presence in Brazil. They fall into two major divisions: the **Ebytoso** (*ybytoso*, *ebitoso*), who have lived for centuries along the Paraguay River, and the **Tomáraho**, who lived inland in the forest. Their speakers are concentrated in the northeastern Chaco Boreal near the origin of the Río Verde, in the district of Fuerte Olimpo and the Bahía Negra region, with the largest communities at Puerto Diana and Puerto Esperanza and the smallest at Puerto Caballo. The 2002 census counted 1,571 people in Paraguay who identified as *yshyr*; the Brazilian Social and Environmental Institute recorded 40 individuals in the Kadiwéu reservation in 1994. Their language, *Yshyr ahwoso*, belongs to the Zamucoan family. In folk-culture terms they matter above all for the feather work and ritual masking tied to the *ahnapzöro* myth cycle, and for the Tomáraho retention of initiation ritual, shamanic knowledge, and myth through a century of displacement.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

What the sources do document is a plaited and corded craft economy and a feather-based ceremonial art. The museum records of the objects shown in this atlas, chiefly the collections Guido Boggiani made in the Chaco and the material now at the Museum of World Culture and the British Museum, list bags, pouches, belts and fly-whisks alongside feather ornaments, all built from *string*, *twine* and *fibre* cordage.

**Bags and pouches** (*väska*, *bolsa*; *påse*) — The most repeated object type in the Boggiani material, with four bags and one sack catalogued from the Chaco in 1917. The records give no fiber identification or ornament description.

**Men's belt** (*mansgördel*) — A single man's girdle collected by Boggiani in the Paraguayan Chaco.

**Fly-whisk** (*flugfläkt*) — A hand-held whisk, catalogued from the same Boggiani series.

**Feather mantle** — A man's dancing cloak of feathers and string held by the British Museum, the one large garment-scale piece in the set and the clearest surviving link between ceremonial dress and feather work.

The sources name no motif vocabulary for Chamacoco pattern.

### Clothing & dress

The sources carry no description of everyday Chamacoco clothing. Ceremonial dress is documented only through the objects themselves, and those are almost entirely men's dance regalia in feather and cordage: a man's feather **mantle or dancing cloak**, men's **dancing tassels and pendants** of feathers and string, a **shoulder ornament** worked from egret feathers, quills and twine, and a feather **band** of uncertain use. A man's belt and a fly-whisk complete the worn and carried set. For the head, the Museum of World Culture holds a **feather head-dress** (*fjäderprydnad*, *huvudprydnad*) described as a feather ornament stuck into the hair rather than a fitted cap — a construction detail worth noting, since it makes the ornament a hair fixture rather than headgear. The Boggiani photographs reproduced as Buenos Aires postcards and published by R. Lehmann-Nitsche record individual sitters — a girl named "Iázzet" and a youth named "Ziguizi," both at Puerto 14 de Mayo, and three men from the Rio Nabiléque — but the catalogue entries describe only the subjects, not their garments. The sources say nothing about footwear.

### Architecture

The sources record only the circumstances of settlement and displacement: the Ebytoso long established along the Paraguay River, the Tomáraho inland in the Gran Chaco until the 1970s, the Tomáraho's confinement to the San Carlos logging camp, their move from San Carlos to Potrerito in 1986 and later to land at María Elena, and the resettlement by the Instituto Nacional del Indígena (INDI) of the Tomáraho alongside the Ebytoso in the community of Puerto Esperanza during the 1980s. As of 2009 three Ebytoso communities held legal status and owned land, while the Tomáraho community held legal status and allocated land without legal title, totalling 25,828 hectares.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources name no Chamacoco ceramic or metalworking tradition. The one tool documented among the museum objects shown here is a **stone axe** at the British Museum, catalogued tentatively as a woman's axe and made of stone, wood, feather and string — a composite in which the hafting cordage and a feather element sit alongside the working stone head. The Wikipedia account of the present-day economy records that the Chamacoco make crafts for sale, specifically baskets and wood carvings, which places basketry and carving as the living object trades; the sources give no named forms, regional types or techniques for either.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Chamacoco personal ornament as it survives in collections is feather work, and the Boggiani series and the British Museum holdings agree closely on its forms. The Museum of World Culture holds a feather **earring** (*örhänge*) and two matching **arm ornaments** (*armprydnad*) of brown feathers, catalogued as a pair from the Chaco. The British Museum holds two feather-and-fibre **wristlets** or bracelets, two men's dancing **tassels** or pendants of feathers and string, a side-worn **shoulder ornament** of egret feathers, quills and twine, and a feather **band**. Read together, these cover wrist, upper arm, shoulder, ear and hair, with the feather head ornament pushed into the hair. The sources document no tattooing, body painting, or hair practices.

## Music & performance

The only evidence of performance in the record is indirect and material: several of the British Museum objects are catalogued explicitly as dancing regalia — a man's dancing cloak, two men's dancing tassels or pendants — which places feather ornament at the centre of a documented dance practice whose music the sources leave unrecorded.

## Dance & theatre

Dance survives in these sources as costume rather than choreography. The British Museum catalogues a man's feather **mantle (dancing)** and two men's feather **tassels or pendants (dancing)**, the only objects in the atlas set whose records name a performance context. The ritual frame they belong to is the boys' initiation ceremony, which the Tomáraho still practised when the Ebytoso had abandoned their rituals under pressure from the New Tribes Mission evangelical missionaries. In the "Big Myth," the *ahnapzöro* (also *anapsoro*) — powerful, terrible gods with no features in their faces, covered in feathers, hair or strange colours — initiated the *yshyr* into their ritual ceremonies before the killings in the initiation rites brought the coexistence of gods and people to a crisis. The featheredness of the *ahnapzöro* in the myth and the featheredness of the surviving regalia belong to one tradition; the sources do not describe the masks, steps, or sequence of the ceremony itself.

## Festivals & rituals

Paraguay has no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions, so no inscribed element bears on the Chamacoco. The ritual life the sources do document is the **boys' initiation ceremony**, retained by the Tomáraho together with a detailed knowledge of myth and shamanism after the Ebytoso had given their rituals up under missionary pressure. Its mythic charter is the arrival of the *ahnapzöro*, who lived among the *yshyr*, taught them to hunt and to use tools, and initiated them, until young men began dying in the harsh initiation rites. *Eshönewörta* (also *Ashnuwerta*), a leading *ahnapzoro*, then showed the *yshyr* that the gods could be killed by a blow to the ankle, where their throats were, and all were exterminated but *Eshönewörta* herself and *Nemur*, who fled. At the place called *Karcha Balut*, *Nemur* took up a snail — from the ground, or from the thick plumage of his body, depending on the version — and from its shell loosed a raging river, today's Paraguay River. Across it *Syr* and *Nemur* exchanged words a last time: *Nemur* told *Syr* that his people were numerous but would be forever obliged to follow the words, and that if they failed, sickness, hunger and enemies would decimate them until the last *Kytymaraha*, *Syr*'s clan, was gone. A second ritual institution governs hunting: every animal has its master, its *balut* or spokesperson, who both makes the hunt possible and punishes excess severely.

## Foodways

The sources document subsistence rather than cuisine: the Chamacoco were traditionally hunter-gatherers and now farm and raise sheep, goats, pigs, cows, horses and poultry, working as ranch hands, day labourers and domestic servants. Dissident *yshyr* groups known as *yacareceros* survive by alligator poaching, a prohibited activity — contingents of fifteen to twenty men go upriver by canoe into the marshlands of the Río Negro and hunt for a month, sleeping in their boats and exposed to Brazilian rangers who shoot before asking.

## Oral tradition & literature

The central Chamacoco narrative is the **"Big Myth"** of the *ahnapzöro*, the gods who taught the *yshyr* to hunt and to use tools and then were destroyed, surviving in versions that differ between Ebytoso and Tomáraho tellers. Its closing episode — *Nemur*'s snail, the river, and the final exchange of words with *Syr* — is simultaneously an origin account of the Paraguay River and a binding obligation on the people to "follow the words." Ticio Escobar, who visited the Tomáraho in 1985 when they numbered only 87, published this material as *The Curse of Nemur: In Search of Art, Myth, and Ritual of the Ishir*. Language documentation is active: an *Ɨshɨr* (Chamacoco) Living Dictionary is under construction through the Living Tongues Institute for Endangered Languages, curated with *Ɨshɨr* language activists including Andres Ozuna Ortiz, and a Chamacoco Talking Dictionary is supported by Living Tongues, National Geographic and Swarthmore College. The Tomaráho variety has a documentation archive at ELAR.

## Language & religion

*Yshyr ahwoso* ("the words, the language of the Chamacoco people") is a Zamucoan language, grouped with Ayoreo and Ancient Zamuco; the name "chamacoco" probably relates to *chamóc* or *zamúc*, the Zamucoan ethnonym. Jesuits described the language in the eighteenth century. Four dialects are identified: *Héiwo* around Fuerte Olimpo, *Ebidóso* and *Hório* in Bahía Negra, and *Tomaráho* in the Alto Paraná Atlantic forests. Over 2,000 speakers were estimated in 1930; Hório and Ebidóso speakers were put at 800 in 1970, with fewer than 200 for Tomaráho. UNESCO considers the language endangered under pressure from Spanish and Guaraní, which most speakers use as second and third languages. The language is tenseless, inflects verbs by personal prefixes, divides nouns into possessable and non-possessable, and shows rare para-hypotactical structures also documented in early Romance. Religiously, the Ebytoso converted to Christianity under the New Tribes Mission, while the Tomáraho preserved the shamanism and myth of the *ahnapzöro* tradition.
