## Overview

The Pauserna, also known as Guarasu, Warázu, Guarasugwé, and Guarasu’we, are an Indigenous people of Bolivia and Brazil who have historically lived along the upper Guaporé River, known as the Iténez River in Bolivia. Current communities are located in Bella Vista in southeastern Beni, Picaflor in Santa Cruz department, and Pimenteiras do Oeste in Rondônia, Brazil. Recent census results estimated 149 Guarasugwé people in Bolivia and 101 in Brazil. The Pauserna language is closely related to Guaraní and belongs to the Tupí language family; Guarasú'we is also described as an endangered member of the Tupi–Guaraní family, most closely related to Sirionó and Yuki (Yuqui). Their documented culture includes bark cloth, woven cotton, pottery, open-shed dwellings, horticulture, body marking, and distinctive birth and burial customs.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Bark cloth* — The Pauserna make and wear bark cloth garments.

*Woven cotton garments* — Pauserna clothing also includes woven cotton garments. Women spin cotton thread using a drop spindle and a vertical loom.

*Cotton thread* — Cotton thread is produced by women with a drop spindle and used in weaving. No named pattern vocabulary is provided.

### Clothing & dress

The Pauserna make and wear bark cloth and woven cotton garments. Women spin cotton thread using a drop spindle and a vertical loom. They do record that the dead were formerly buried in graves over which a hut was built, and that today they are buried in their huts wearing their paint and ornaments, wrapped in mats, and facing west.

### Architecture

The Pauserna formerly lived in multifamily dwellings and now live in single-family open sheds. Inside these houses are food-storage platforms and cotton hammocks. Men sit on benches, while women use mats.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pots are made with clay tempered with crushed potsherds. Household objects mentioned in the sources include food-storage platforms, cotton hammocks, men’s benches, and women’s mats.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Girls were secluded for a month at puberty, fed a restricted diet, and tattooed on their arms and breasts. Boys were often scarified and bled to make them strong. The sources also state that the dead are buried wearing their paint and ornaments. No particular jewelry forms, materials, hair practices, or ornament names are given.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document several life-cycle practices. At puberty, girls were secluded for a month, given a restricted diet, and tattooed on their arms and breasts. Boys were often scarified and bled to make them strong.

Marriage preferences included cross-cousin marriage and marriage between a man and his sister’s daughter. A girl or woman could not marry without the consent of her father and brother, and polygyny was common. Postmarital residence was first matrilocal and later neolocal.

During pregnancy, women observed certain food taboos. Fathers observed couvade by remaining in hammocks for three days after the birth of their children. This practice was connected to the belief that a child’s soul follows the child’s father and might be injured if the father exerted himself immediately after the birth.

The dead were formerly buried in graves over which a hut was built. Today they are buried in their huts, wearing paint and ornaments, wrapped in mats, and facing west.

## Foodways

The Pauserna were originally foragers and later became horticulturists. They once raised a great variety of food and other plants available before contact, but later ceased raising manioc and adopted the cultivation of rice and caripo, identified in the source as yams. Groups clear and prepare fields for planting. Men plant maize, while women plant manioc and assist in the harvest.

Some Pauserna collect rubber and ipecac, which is used to make pharmaceuticals. It records that Erland Nordenskiöld wrote that the Pauserna enjoyed eating the meat of alligators.

Pregnant women observed certain food taboos.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do document a contemporary language-preservation effort in Picaflor. Amalia Pereira and her sister Carmen Frey (Takva’sim), both granddaughters of community leader Miguel Frey, lead a language revitalization effort there. Pereira’s language reconstruction draws on the knowledge of several elder relatives and resulted in the publication of an official alphabet in 2023. The Guarasú'we language is taught in a community school sponsored by the Bolivian government.

## Language & religion

Only a few older people speak the Pauserna language. It is closely related to Guaraní and belongs to the Tupí language family. The Guarasú'we language is described as an endangered member of the Tupi–Guaraní language family, most closely related to Sirionó and Yuki (Yuqui).

They do record a belief associated with couvade: a child’s soul was believed to follow the child’s father and might be injured if the father exerted himself immediately after the birth. The sources also document burial with paint and ornaments, wrapped in mats and facing west.
