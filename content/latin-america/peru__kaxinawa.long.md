## Overview

The Kaxinawá — a name also written Kashinawa, Kaxynawa, Caxinawa, Caxinawá and Cashinahua — are an Indigenous people of the western Amazon basin, living in Peru along the Curanja and the Purus Rivers and across the border in the Brazilian state of Acre. Their language, *Hãtxa Kuĩ* (also written *Hantxa Kuin*), belongs to the Panoan family and is spoken by about 1,600 people in Peru and 400 in Acre. In folk-culture terms the people are documented here chiefly through a body of museum-held objects: cotton cloth woven on a back-strap loom, knobbed and painted pottery, feather head-dresses and back-racks built on cane and wood frames, and painted two-legged stools used in initiation. The sources assembled for this profile are a linguistic article and the catalogue records of those objects, so what follows stays within them.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Cotton is the one fibre that runs through nearly every textile and composite object in the collection, and it appears both as woven cloth and as cord and binding. The catalogue records name no vernacular textile terms, so the traditions below are grouped by object type rather than by indigenous name.

**Back-strap weaving** — The collection includes a weaving back-strap of cotton textile, the body-worn element of a back-tensioned loom, alongside the finished cloth described below. It is the only loom part among the objects.

**Woven bed covers** — A cotton bed cover is woven as an uneven rectangle, wider at the ends than at the middle, and patterned in blue and white geometric designs. The irregular outline is recorded as a feature of the piece rather than a defect.

**Tube skirts and belts** — Women's dress in the collection includes a tube skirt of cotton fibre and a cotton belt, both catalogued without decoration.

**Cotton carrying gear** — A shoulder bag of cotton textile and a baby sling of cotton fibre show the same fibre carried into transport and child-carrying.

**Striped cord binding** — Cotton string in blue, red and white stripes is wrapped around the handle of a carved wooden club, so dyed cordwork also serves as a finishing technique on hardwood objects.

**Plaited basketry** — A shallow basket with a round rim is plaited from vegetable fibre, with a string of white wool threaded through the rim.

**Motif vocabulary.** The records name no motifs by name; they describe only blue-and-white geometric designs on woven cotton and pink-and-black geometric designs painted on a bamboo head-dress ring.

### Clothing & dress

The garments in the collection are cotton. A shirt is catalogued simply as a garment of textile, probably cotton; a tube skirt and a belt are of cotton fibre. Body ornaments worn with dress include chest-straps — one made of seeds and tin, another of seeds and animal teeth — and a pair of leg-bands of cotton and snail shell. Ceremonial dress is represented by head-dresses of two distinct constructions. One is a length of bamboo folded into a circle, its ends tied together with yellow wool thread, with the outer face painted in pink and black geometric designs. The others are feather head-dresses built on a cane ring: small red, yellow and blue feathers are attached to the ring, which is bound with a strip of wool (white and black on one, white, purple and red on another, white and blue on a third), and a second, larger ring of long feathers stands behind the small one — blue on one example, white on another, and alternating bands of white and blue on a third. One of these carries two tassels of green feathers and of brown and white feathers tied at the bottom.

### Architecture

The sources used — a linguistic article and the catalogue records of the objects shown — say nothing about Kaxinawá house forms, building materials or settlement layout.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Three pottery vessels are recorded, all rounded bowls: one with three knobs spaced evenly around the rim, one with four, and a third catalogued as a painted ceramic bowl. The knobbed rim is the recurring formal trait. Woodwork covers both tools and furniture. A club is carved with a flat blade, a handle and a knob at the handle's end, the grip wrapped in striped cotton string. A wooden stirrer has paired protrusions on either side of the handle where it meets the flat blade, and three more on either side of the handle's end. Two initiation stools are carved from wood with two legs and a round top; on one, the legs are wrapped with a strip of bark and the stool is painted with geometric designs. Fishing equipment appears as a net of wood and cotton. Two back-racks combine harpy eagle feathers with wood and cotton. No metalwork is recorded apart from the tin used in one seed chest-strap.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment in the collection is made from gathered animal and plant material rather than metal or stone: chest-straps strung from seeds with tin, and from seeds with animal teeth; leg-bands of cotton and snail shell; and the feather and bamboo head-dresses described above, whose makers used harpy eagle plumage for the back-racks and small red, yellow and blue feathers for the ring head-dresses.

## Music & performance

The one adjacent body of recorded material they name is the Cashinahua DoReCo corpus of narrative audio compiled by Sabine Reiter, which carries transcriptions time-aligned at the phone level, translations and, for some texts, time-aligned morphological annotations — a linguistic rather than a musical record.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used say nothing about Kaxinawá dance or dramatic traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used give no festival calendar and no account of birth, marriage or funerary rites. They do carry one ritual thread, in the catalogue rather than the text: two of the carved wooden objects are classed as initiation stools, each a two-legged seat with a round top, one of them with bark-wrapped legs and painted geometric designs. The record names the rite only by that label, without describing who sat on the stool, at what age, or in what sequence of events, so nothing further can be said here. The back-racks of harpy eagle feather, wood and cotton and the cane-ring feather head-dresses are the kind of regalia that implies ceremonial use, but the catalogue entries assign them to no named occasion.

## Foodways

Two objects in the collection bear on food production and preparation: a fishing net of wood and cotton, and a carved wooden stirrer with protrusions flanking the handle at both the blade end and the grip end.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used contain no folktales, epics or proverbs, but they point to where Kaxinawá narrative has been documented. The Cashinahua DoReCo corpus, compiled by Sabine Reiter, consists of audio recordings of narrative texts with transcriptions, translations and partial morphological annotation. Work on animacy and mythology in *Hãtxa Kuĩ* has been carried out at the Department of Linguistics of the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology, and Richard Montag's 2008 study of participant referencing in Cashinahua, published by SIL International, is a study of how narrative keeps track of its characters. On the lexicographic side, a dictionary of the language has been compiled and published since 1980.

## Language & religion

*Hãtxa Kuĩ* is a Panoan language of western South America, spoken by about 1,600 Kaxinawá in Peru on the Curanja and Purus Rivers and by 400 in the Brazilian state of Acre. Three named dialects survive or are recorded: Brazilian Kashinawa, Peruvian Kashinawa, and the extinct Juruá Kapanawa — the Capanahua of the Juruá River — together with Paranawa, also extinct. Between five and ten percent of speakers have some proficiency in Spanish; forty percent are literate, and twenty to thirty percent are literate in Spanish as a second language. Kenneth M. Kensinger's 1963 study of the phonological hierarchy of Cashinahua is among the earliest descriptive work.
