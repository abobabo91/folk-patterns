## Overview

The Tzeltal are a Maya people of the highlands of Chiapas, in southern Mexico, whose traditional territory lies northeast and southeast of San Cristóbal — the municipalities of San Juan Cancuc, Chanal, Oxchuc, Tenejapa, Altamirano, Sitalá, Socoltenango, Yajalón, Chilón, Ocosingo, Amatenango del Valle and Aguacatenango, with Tzotzil country to the west, the Chʼols to the north and northeast and the Tojolabal to the southeast. They call themselves *Winik atel*, "Working Men," and *batzilʼop*, "those of the original word," a reference to Maya oral tradition. The 2000 census counted 278,577 speakers aged five and over in Chiapas, about 34.41% of the state's Indigenous population, with the total group estimated at some 500,000 — the largest Indigenous ethnicity in Chiapas. Their language belongs to the Tzeltalan subgroup of the Mayan family. In folk-culture terms they matter above all for backstrap-loom weaving and painted pottery, and for a village-scale social order in which each community keeps its own dialect, dress and saints.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Handcrafts are the main specialization of Tzeltal who live in the larger villages, with surpluses moving through a regional market system tied into the wider Mexican economy. Textiles dominate: cloth woven on backstrap looms and decorated with traditional Mayan designs, made as *huipils*, shirts and tablecloths or napkins, either kept for the house or sold. The strongest weaving reputations belong to **Tenejapa**, **Pantelhó**, **Larráinzar** and **Chenalhó**.

**Huipil** — The woman's main woven garment, built from two panels of white or off-white cotton woven on a backstrap loom, joined along their long sides (sometimes with multicoloured decorative stitching) and left open at the centre for the head. One British Museum example carries weft-brocaded wool in brown, yellow and green across the top third, in bands of *pata de perro* ("dog's paw") alternating with bands of geometric design.

**Brocaded huipil in *hilaza*** — A second type is woven with commercial cotton thread (*hilaza*), its shoulders brocaded with orange, turquoise, pink, green and yellow diamonds on a burgundy ground, a row of coloured diamonds below, and the neck opening outlined with multicoloured triangles in wool and acrylic yarn.

**Man's sash** — Woven and brocaded on the backstrap loom from white cotton warp and indigo-dyed blue cotton weft, so the band reads dark blue with a white stripe down the centre. Each end is brocaded in acrylic yarn with an orange X set in black cross-hatching on a red ground, and finished with over-and-over multicoloured stitching.

**Blusa bordada** — The embroidered blouse: white factory-made cotton with a black cotton square neck opening embroidered in multicoloured flowers in acrylic yarn, the black cloth and the cuffs gathered with fuchsia thread. It shows how industrial cloth and acrylic yarn now sit alongside handspun wool in the same wardrobe.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Pata de perro* ("dog's paw"); bands of geometric design; diamonds, single and in rows; multicoloured triangles edging a neck opening; a brocaded X in cross-hatching; embroidered flowers.

### Clothing & dress

Dress is strongly gendered. Women are recognizable by a black skirt with a wool belt and an undyed cotton blouse embroidered with flowers; the hair is tied with ribbons and covered with a cloth. The woven *huipil* and the machine-cotton *blusa bordada* both belong to this wardrobe, the first made at home on the backstrap loom, the second assembled from factory cloth and embroidered. Men, as a rule, no longer wear traditional attire, though the brocaded man's sash in the British Museum collection shows what that attire included. Dress is also local rather than ethnic: each community has its own clothing alongside its own dialect and territory, and the sources treat garment style as one of the markers that distinguish one village from the next.

### Architecture

The sources used say almost nothing about Tzeltal house form, materials or roofing — only that the language has a compound noun *pasna*, "house construction," from *pas* ("make") and *na* ("house"). What they do describe is settlement structure: each community is built around a main village or town with a number of smaller dependent settlements, and this pattern is often mirrored in the state's official municipality system. The seat is the political, religious and commercial centre of the whole community, and is itself divided into two or more neighbourhoods called *barrios* or *calpuls*, each with its own local authorities and sometimes its own patron saint. Since the 1930s a second settlement geography has been added: Tzeltal migrants, together with other Indigenous people and mestizos, moved from the crowded highlands into the Lacandon Jungle to clear forest, grow crops and raise cattle, founding lowland communities shared with other groups.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Painted pottery is the Tzeltal craft best represented in museum collections. The bodies are round with a flattened bottom, tapering to a neck that flares out to a plain or wavy lip, with one, two or three looped handles spaced around the sides; one pot is specifically coiled and burnished. Nearly all carry an ivory slip painted in brown and red: zigzag lines around the neck, bands of spirals, triangles, diagonal and vertical lines, curly and wavy lines, dots inside the rim. Jugs add a bent spout opposite the handle, and miniature jugs repeat the form at toy scale. Potters also work the same slip-and-paint technique into lighting and play: a candlestick with three tubes on a tapering stem, painted with curvilinear designs and hashmarks; a candle-holder modelled as a four-antlered deer with a candle tube on its back and red chevrons along its sides; a slotted money-box animal with long ears and orange and brown spots; and a dog figurine with its tail curled over its back. Pots can also be figural in detail — one has a brown lizard painted above each handle and a flower between them.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used cover almost no jewelry. What they record is cloth and hair: the wool belt worn with the black skirt, and the ribbons with which women tie their hair before covering it with a cloth. Nothing in these sources describes metal ornament, tattooing or other body modification.

## Music & performance

The one documented broadcast tradition is spoken rather than sung: Tzeltal-language programming carried by the CDI radio station XEVFS, which broadcasts from Las Margaritas, Chiapas.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual year runs on the saints' calendar. Most ceremonies and festivals are attached to saints' days and follow an annual cycle, each organized by sponsors called *mayordomos* with assistants called *alfereces*; because the *mayordomos* who run the ceremonies are frequently also leaders in secular village affairs, the festival system doubles as a system of local office. Individual *barrios* within a community seat may have their own patron saint, so the cycle is layered: neighbourhood observances inside community ones.

Alongside this, shamanism and magical practice remain active, and ritual is bound up with health. Tzeltal cosmology turns on the interaction of body, mind and spirit, and on how these in turn interact with the community, the world and the supernatural. Illness is read as a loss of harmony: it can be a sanction imposed by the saints or the gods for breaking the rules of society, or the work of witchcraft by someone seeking harm, and there are rituals against both. Healing therefore aims at restoring harmony rather than at the symptom alone, and traditional medicine is usually the first and, where modern medical facilities are lacking, the only recourse. Barn owls are traditionally regarded as "disease givers." The sources used do not describe birth, coming-of-age, wedding or funeral rites, though they note that more conservative communities inherit land through patriarchal lineages with an elaborate kinship terminology, while the nuclear family is the norm.

## Foodways

Agriculture is the base of Tzeltal economic life, and the field crops are the classic Mesoamerican set: maize, beans, squash and chili peppers are the most important. Around them grow wheat, manioc, sweet potatoes, cotton, chayote, some fruits, other vegetables and coffee. Domestic animals are pigs, donkeys, cattle and domestic fowl; in the Lacandon Jungle settlements founded since the 1930s, cattle-raising is a particular object of the migration. Maize reaches the table as *waj*, tortilla, and the act of making it is lexicalized as *pakʼwaj*, "tortilla baking," from *pakʼ* ("strike with the hand") and *waj* — the compound itself records the handmade tortilla as a named daily task. For many households neither farming nor craft income is enough, so wage labour supplements both.

## Oral tradition & literature

One of the group's two self-designations is itself a claim about oral tradition: *batzilʼop*, "those of the original word," refers to the Maya oral tradition the Tzeltal carry. The sources document how the language stands today, which bears directly on its oral literature: Ethnologue rates Tzeltal 5 of 10, "Developing," and describes its use as "vigorous," but that use is almost exclusively oral — schools rarely use Tzeltal materials, and as a result nearly everyone under thirty is bilingual in Spanish, while many grandparents remain monolingual Tzeltal speakers. Two institutional supports exist: Tzeltal radio programming on XEVFS from Las Margaritas, and the liturgical translations approved by Pope Francis in 2013 covering the prayers for Mass, marriage, baptism, confirmation, confession, ordination and the anointing of the sick. Bishop Arizmendi said that the texts took roughly eight years to translate and would be used in his diocese and in the neighbouring Archdiocese of Tuxtla Gutiérrez.

## Language & religion

Tzeltal (also written Tseltal) is a Mayan language of Chiapas, forming with Tzotzil the Tzeltalan branch, which joins the Chʼolan languages in Cholan–Tzeltalan; the branches are believed to have split about 1,400 years ago, and Tzeltal and Tzotzil to have begun separating around 1200 CE. It is concentrated in twenty of Chiapas' 111 municipalities and had some 371,730 speakers as of 2005, including roughly 50,000 monolinguals. Two main dialects are named in the older division — highland, or Oxchuc, and lowland, or Bachajonteco — while a four-way description gives north, central (Oxchuc), south and southeast, the last now spoken only by a few scattered elderly speakers. The language has five vowels and twenty-one consonants including the glottal stop, no standardized orthography, stress on the final syllable, and is ergative–absolutive, agglutinative and head-marking. Religion is a syncretism of Catholic and Indigenous elements, with saints' festivals, *mayordomos* and persisting shamanic and magical practice.
