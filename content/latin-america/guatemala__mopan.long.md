## Overview

The Mopan are an Indigenous sub-ethnic group of the Maya peoples, living in the Petén Department of Guatemala and in the Maya Mountains region of Belize. Their recent history is one of forced movement: in the 18th and 19th centuries the British pushed the Mopan out of Belize into Petén, where forced labour and heavy taxation drove them back east again. They first settled near what is now Pueblo Viejo, and when Guatemalan officials insisted that site still lay inside Guatemala, they moved further east around 1889 and founded San Antonio. Guatemalan towns where the language is prominent include San Luis, Poptún, Melchor de Mencos and Dolores. In the 2010 Belize census 10,557 people reported Mopan Maya ethnicity, about 3% of the population. Their language belongs to the Yucatecan branch of the Mayan family. Because the Mopan have no written tradition, culture passes by oral transmission — which makes the embroidered cloth, coiled basketry and painted dance masks held in museums unusually load-bearing as evidence.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources document Mopan cloth almost entirely through embroidered women's blouses and the skirts worn with them, and through one woven bag; they name no vernacular term for any of these.

**Cross-stitch embroidered blouse** — A blouse of predominantly white cotton, with the neck and sleeves worked in a base fabric of open-weave embroidery cloth and embroidered in black cross-stitch. The shoulders carry repeated horizontal rows of tucks, over-sewn by machine with vertical rows of stitching; one example is left unhemmed at the bottom edge, and another adds two vertical rows of black rick-rack appliquéd across the shoulders over the tucks. Necklines can be wide and round with a drawstring, over short sleeves.

**Pleated and tucked skirt** — An orange cotton skirt decorated with pleats, white lace and rows of tucks, finished with a narrow cream waistband and long ties, cut from calf to full length.

**Backstrap-loom bag** — A woven acrylic carrying bag on a yellow base colour with multi-coloured vertical stripes, woven on a backstrap loom, with a purple woven striped handle and a fringe along one top edge.

**Motif vocabulary.** Birds and flowers, worked in black cross-stitch at neck and sleeve; geometric star shapes, one set described as inspired by the *tommygoff* snake; and a *fer de lance* snake design.

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress in the museum record is a two-piece outfit: the white cotton blouse with black cross-stitch at neck and sleeves, worn with a pleated cotton skirt in a strong colour under a narrow tied waistband. The cloth doll made by a Mopan maker confirms how the pieces go together and what else belongs to the ensemble — the doll wears a white blouse with black embroidery at collar and cuffs and a fuchsia skirt with horizontal pleats accented with yellow wavy lines, her hair braided down the back and tied with multicoloured yarn, and wire earrings set with green glass beads at the sides of the head. Blouse shoulders are stiffened and textured by rows of horizontal tucks held down with machined vertical stitching, a construction repeated on every blouse in the collection. The sources describe no men's garments, no head covering and no footwear. Ceremonial dress is represented only by what dancers carry rather than wear: painted wooden masks and animal-head dance equipment (see *Dance & theatre*).

### Architecture

The sources used here say nothing about Mopan house form, building materials or construction.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Mopan pottery in the collection is lightly fired and fire-stained. A hanging **incense-burner** has a semicircular bowl with a slightly flattened bottom, three strips of pottery rising from the sides — each carrying three bumps along its outside — meeting at a knob pierced for hanging. Modelled figurines take animal and composite forms: a toucan standing on two feet with incised lines for wings and incised circles for eyes, and a human figure standing on the back of a four-legged turtle whose shell is bordered with a band of geometric design. Basketry is the other strong craft. Lidded baskets are coiled from *jippy jappa*, a palm-like fibre, with a bundle of wrapped fibre laid in a wavy line around the rim and lids finished with an openwork star and a central knob, sometimes decorated with plastic beads and buttons. Open baskets are made in wicker-work technique from *basket tie-tie* palm fibre, with rounded bottoms left without weft strips at the centre; one is specified as a container for shelled corn. Other household gear includes a netted fibre carrying bag with a plant-fibre handle tied to each upper corner, a plaited hearth-fan whose handle splits into strips plaited into a round blade, and a wooden bird trap built log-cabin style from slats lashed at the corners with coloured string, propped open with two loose sticks.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Necklaces in the collection are strung on fishing line and combine pottery with seeds in fixed repeating orders — two small round pottery beads, a larger oblong pottery bead (some coloured grey), two more small round beads, then an oblong red seed; or three small round pottery beads followed by an oblong grey seed. Both end in a modelled pottery pendant: one a snake incised with geometric patterns on the outside, the other a triangle with incised lines. Beadwork in glass is worked flat: a rectangular bracelet shows an orange and fuchsia diamond pattern on a green ground, fastened at the wrist with a metal hook and eye. Earrings appear on the cloth doll as wire hoops hung with green glass beads. The sources record no tattooing or other body marking.

## Music & performance

The sources used here name no Mopan instruments, song genres or musical occasions.

## Dance & theatre

Masked dance is the best-documented Mopan performance tradition in the collection, though the sources do not name the dance itself. The masks are carved from wood in the form of human faces, with holes cut for eyes and mouth — or, in one case, cut above the eyes only — and holes bored at the temples through which string is threaded so the mask can be hung. Two colour schemes recur: pink faces with black eyebrows, sometimes with black hair, white eyes with black pupils and a white mouth; and black faces with pink worked around the eye and mouth openings. Alongside the human masks, dancers use carved animal figures classed as dance equipment: a deer's head and upper torso with a slit mouth, indented eyes and separately carved antlers inserted into holes on either side of the head, painted pink with black antlers tipped in white; and dogs' heads and upper torsos with pointed ears and slit mouths, painted black with a white patch running from between the ears to the end of the snout. Each has a hole in the bottom and a string for hanging. One dog head is priced and signed by its maker in pink across the white patch, and a lidded basket in the same collection carries a seller's sticker from Santa Elena — the makers of these pieces sold them by name.

## Festivals & rituals

Mopan ritual life centres on a Maya-Catholic spirituality. The cacao tree, called *cucu*, is central to it: both the tree and the beans it produces are said to contain spirits, and cacao beverages are drunk at religious celebrations. The pierced pottery incense-burner in the collection, made to hang, belongs to this ceremonial equipment. Since the 1970s many Mopan villagers have left the Maya-Catholic faith for Protestant groups and in consequence reject beliefs about the spiritual aspects of the natural world; the influence of Protestant evangelical missionaries is the main cause of the decline of traditional practice. The sources describe no annual festival calendar and no birth, coming-of-age, marriage or funeral rites.

## Foodways

Mopan livelihood rests on agriculture, and the community has a long history of smallholding independent farmers with extensive knowledge of the local flora and fauna. Maize and beans are cultivated and rice is planted; these serve as cash crops. Cacao cultivation has been financially rewarding, and Mopan growers have built relationships with buyers including the Hummingbird Highway Hershey Company and Green and Black's. Cacao is also consumed ritually rather than only sold — cacao beverages are drunk at religious celebrations. Two objects in the collection sit directly in the food economy: a wicker-work *basket tie-tie* basket made for shelled corn, and a plaited fibre hearth-fan for working a cooking fire. The sources name no prepared dishes and give no cooking methods or dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

There is no written tradition among the Mopan, so the preservation of their culture depends on oral transmission — the sources state this as the defining fact of Mopan literature without naming any tale, epic or proverb. Written Mopan is a colonial-era import: since that period the language has been written in the Latin script, in a wide range of competing orthographies, and the spelling has recently been standardized by the Guatemalan Academy of Mayan Languages (ALMG). A Mopan New Testament was published in a 2004 edition. The scholarly documentation of the language includes Charles Andrew Hofling's *Mopan Maya–Spanish–English Dictionary* (2011).

## Language & religion

Mopan belongs to the Yucatecan branch of the Mayan languages, alongside Yucatec, Lacandon and Itzaʼ, and began to diverge from them at least a thousand years ago. Between three and four thousand speakers live in Guatemala and six to eight thousand in Belize. The grammar has features worth noting for anyone reading Mopan object labels: basic word order is verb-object-subject, though subject-verb-object is also common; two noun classifiers mark gender, *ix* (feminine) and *aj* (masculine), as in *aj much*, "toad (masculine)", but they are used for most nouns only irregularly, need no agreement elsewhere in the sentence, can be omitted, and do not always follow natural gender — *ix tʼutʼ*, "parrot", is normally feminine whatever the bird's sex. Every numeral takes a numeral classifier as a suffix describing the thing counted: *-kuul* for round objects, *-tzʼiit* for long thin ones, and most commonly *-pʼeel* for inanimate things and *-tuul* for people and animals, out of more than 70 in all. Religion is Maya-Catholic, with a growing Protestant minority since the 1970s.
