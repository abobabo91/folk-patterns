## Overview

The Mixtecs — *Mixtecos* in Spanish, from the Nahuatl *mixtecah*, "cloud people," and self-named *ñuù savi*, *nayívi savi*, *ñuù davi* or *nayivi davi*, all translatable as "the land of the rain" — are an Indigenous Mesoamerican people of southern Mexico. Their homeland, *La Mixteca* or *Ñuu Dzahui*, covers more than 40,000 km² across western Oaxaca, southern Puebla and eastern Guerrero, including the Costa Chica and La Montaña regions, and is conventionally divided into the mountainous Mixteca Alta (*Ñuu Savi Sukun*), the hot, dry Mixteca Baja (*Ñuu I'ni*) and the humid Mixteca Costa (*Ñuu Andivi*). Roughly 800,000 Mixtec people live in Mexico today, against an estimated 1.5 million before the Spanish conquest, with large communities in California (about 150,000 as of 2011), New York City, Tijuana, San Diego and Tucson. The Mixtec languages form a major branch of the Oto-Manguean family. In folk-culture terms the Mixtecs matter twice over: as the makers of the surviving pictorial codices, and as weavers of shellfish- and cochineal-dyed cloth whose motifs are still catalogued in museum collections.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Brocaded wedding *huipil*** — A three-panel ceremonial *huipil* woven on the backstrap loom from stripes of hand-spun white cotton, lilac cotton dyed with the secretion of shellfish, and *hiladillo*, cochineal-dyed silk in burgundy; the neck opening is edged with purple satin ribbon and a double-headed bird motif is brocaded in four bands running across the stripes.

**Embroidered wedding *huipil*** — A second wedding form in cotton and silk: three panels of handspun white cotton joined by green silk ribbon, with thicker weft threads inserted periodically to raise horizontal bands in the cotton ground. Silk ribbon runs along the side seams and neck opening, the neck is embroidered with animals and flowers in silk thread, and the double-headed bird appears below the neck opening front and back and again on the shoulders.

***Posahuanco*** — The wrap-around skirt, built from three webs of cloth sewn together and woven in horizontal bands of blue, mauve and maroon with some red. Its distinction lies in the dye triad: cotton coloured with indigo and with shellfish dye, and silk with cochineal.

**Warp-patterned wrap cloth** — A cloth of two webs of cream hand-spun cotton carrying seven warp bands of woven decoration in mauve hand-spun cotton and maroon silk, the bands forming repeating geometric, human and animal designs. In the documented example the mauve dye has bled into the base cloth.

***Servilleta* in *coyuche* cotton** — A backstrap-loom cloth of hand-spun naturally brown cotton (*coyuche* in Spanish) with warp stripes alternating brown and white and a twisted warp-thread fringe at both ends.

**Plaited palm basketry** — Lidded baskets plaited from palm fibre, patterned by setting fuchsia-dyed fibre against an undyed ground in zigzag or geometric registers, with the inside of a base sometimes worked wholly in the dyed fibre and the bottom and lid finished with a single thin cross stitch.

**Motif vocabulary.** Double-headed bird (the dominant wedding-*huipil* motif), owl, scorpion, butterfly, deer, fox — including a fox wearing a crown — cats, rabbit, birds, human figures, flowers, horizontal and warp-band geometric registers, and zigzags in dyed palm.

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress in the documented material is built from two loom-woven pieces: the *huipil* above and the wrapped *posahuanco* below, both made of narrow webs sewn together rather than cut and tailored. Ceremonial dress is clearly set apart from daily wear — the wedding *huipil* carries brocaded or embroidered double-headed birds, satin or silk ribbon trim at the neck and seams, and the costly dye pair of shellfish purple and cochineal red, materials that signal expense as much as pattern. The ordinary *servilleta*, by contrast, is left in the undyed browns and whites of *coyuche* cotton. A painted pottery figurine shows a woman in a conical skirt wearing a sombrero and carrying a basket over one arm, which is the only indication of headgear in the records used here. For masquerade there is a carved wooden dance-belt in the form of a horse, painted white with a brown saddle blanket trimmed in red, gold and green, fitted with leather ears, a horsehair mane and twisted-cord reins and stirrups, tied to the dancer's waist with fibre cord.

### Architecture

The sources describe the Mixtec built environment archaeologically rather than as a living house form. Settlement began with small agricultural villages from about the 16th century BC — the Cruz phase in the Alta, the Pre-Ñudée and Ñudée phases in the Baja, the Charco phase on the coast — and the first substantial towns, Monte Negro and Huamelulpan in the Alta and Cerro de las Minas in the Baja, appear towards the end of the Middle Preclassic. Mixtec urbanism is distinguished from the rest of Mesoamerica by its plan: Cerro de las Minas was laid out around a group of several small plazas rather than a single great one, with the population distributed around them. The hillside was reshaped with terraces called *coo yuu* (lama-bordo), so the city is threaded with stairways, and its walls carried reliefs inscribed in the *ñuiñe* script. During the Classic period the Baja cities — Cerro de las Minas, Diquiyú and others — were fortified, with administrative and religious buildings set on hill slopes and dwellings on ground of easier access. The Mixtecs also built at Monte Albán after taking the city from the Zapotecs, and made major constructions at Tilantongo, Mitla, Cuilapan, Yucuñudahui and Tututepec.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The documented domestic pottery is burnished and slipped, in orange or white, often lightly fired and fire-stained. Standard forms are the water-jug — ovoid or round-bottomed, tapering to a long neck that flares at the lip, with two or three looped handles evenly spaced around the shoulder — the round water-bottle, and the miniature versions of both. Painted wares carry bands of geometric design above animals: scorpions in white on a gourd-shaped double-bellied pot, butterfly, bird and flower across the handles of another, green and red geometric work on white slip. A parallel figural repertoire covers candle-holders modelled as fish, as long-necked four-legged animals with a candle tube on each flank and a smaller animal on the back, and a pottery *candelabrum* built as a tree of life: two tree shapes on four legs, an angel on wire at the base of the trunk, above it a figure with a cross in one hand and a skull in the other, a bird and dog on wire, five candle tubes along the outer edge, and flowers, birds and leaves mounted on wire. Gourds are carved on the outside with geometric fields and animals and fitted with lids tied on by twisted cord. In metal, the Mixtecs were, in West's phrase, "the foremost goldsmiths of Mesoamerica," working by lost-wax casting of gold and its alloys.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Mixtec mastery of jewelry and mosaic, with gold and turquoise prominent, was regarded throughout ancient Mesoamerica, and the output of Mixtec goldsmiths formed an important part of the tribute the Mixtecs paid to the Aztecs. Turquoise mosaic masks carried political and religious weight at once: they were given as gifts to seal political alliances, worn in ceremonies in which the wearer impersonated a god, and fixed to funerary bundles that were treated as oracles.

## Music & performance

Instruments are thinly documented here: the one object is a rattle made from a round gourd mounted on a wooden dowel, filled with pebbles or seeds, its surface cut away to leave brown designs of two birds, a rabbit and a fox on a beige ground, the handle painted in fuchsia and green stripes. Beyond objects, the sources make one substantial point about Mixtec music, and it concerns transmission rather than repertoire: through the five centuries after the Conquest, when Mixtec literature was confined to the popular sphere, it survived chiefly by oral means — through music and through the conduct of ritual. In the contemporary period, the poem *Yunu Yukuninu* ("Tree, Hill of Yucuninu") by Juan de Dios Ortiz Cruz of the Mixteca Alta was set to music by Lila Downs, named in the sources as one of the leading figures in contemporary Mixtec music. The same writer has collected the lyrical compositions of his region.

## Dance & theatre

The sources document dance only through one object, the carved wooden horse dance-belt described above, worn at the waist on fibre cords threaded through four holes around its central opening — a horse-mounted masquerade of the kind the catalogue records without naming the dance. No named dance or dramatic tradition appears in the sources used.

## Festivals & rituals

What the sources do document is ritual practice attached to places and objects. The Colossal Natural Bridge, a cave site in the Coixtlahuaca Basin, is an important sacred place for the Mixtec; among the *ñuiñe* "colossal heads," small stone sculptures of anthropomorphic heads found in the Baja, some are objects of worship in the Indigenous communities of the Mixteca guerrerense today. In the pre-Hispanic period, turquoise mosaic masks were worn in ceremonies of divine impersonation and attached to oracular funerary bundles. The Classic period saw the consolidation of the cult of rain and lightning in the divinised figure of Dzahui, whose local version appears on Baja urns alongside the Old God of Fire, effigies close in character to the Zapotec Pitao Cocijo. Of life-cycle rites, the one the collection records directly is marriage: the three-panel wedding *huipil*, woven in shellfish purple and cochineal silk and marked with the double-headed bird, is a garment made for that occasion and distinct from everyday cloth. The founding myth of the Arrowman of the Sun ties ritual to the agricultural year, since the people, settled on land won from the Sun, were hastened to plant their corn *milpas* that same night.

## Foodways

The sources treat subsistence rather than cuisine. The economy of the first agricultural settlements in La Mixteca, from around the third millennium BC, rested on the four basic Mesoamerican crops: chili, corn, beans and squash. Corn is also cosmological — in the Mixtec creation account the four creator gods make humanity from corn — and the corn *milpa* is the field the first Mixtecs are said to have planted overnight to hold the land they had won from the Sun. In the colonial period local exchange turned on agricultural commodities and Indigenous crafts, handled regionally in the second half of the period by bilingual Mixtec merchants dealing in both Spanish and Indigenous goods. The vocabulary of newly introduced foods entered the language directly: *limun*, from Spanish *limón*, is recorded in San Martín Duraznos, Oaxaca, where the lemon is also called *tzikua Iya*, "sour orange." The sources used do not describe cooking methods, named dishes, ceremonial food or drink traditions.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Mixtec are known in anthropology for their codices, phonetic pictures painted on deerskin in screenfold "fold-book" form. Four survive, narrating the exploits of Lord Eight Deer, named for the day of his birth and surnamed Jaguar Claw, who conquered and united most of the Mixteca: the Codex Bodley, the Codex Zouche-Nuttall and the Codex Vindobonensis in European collections, and the Codex Colombino in the National Museum of Anthropology in Mexico City. The genealogical screenfold shown in this atlas records twenty-six generations of a noble family, with subsidiary lines and a commentary in a native Mexican language, on sixteen double-sided leather pages. After the Conquest, codices gave way to *lienzos*, large pictorial cloths glossed in the Latin alphabet, of which the Lienzo Seler II from Coixtlahuaca is the most notable. The key to reading the codices was recovered only in the mid-20th century, largely by Alfonso Caso. The modern revival runs through the Asociación de Escritores en Lenguas Indígenas, founded in 1993, the Casa del Escritor en Lengua Indígena three years later, the Nezahualcóyotl Prize, and writers of the Mixteca Alta such as Raúl Gatica, whose *Asalto a la palabra* gathered work by several Mixtec poets.

## Language & religion

Mixtec belongs to the Mixtecan group of Oto-Manguean and is closely related to Trique and Cuicatec. Its varieties, spoken by over half a million people, form a dialect continuum with abrupt boundaries; depending on the criteria, there are between a dozen and fifty-three Mixtec languages, and SIL International has assigned distinct ISO codes to more than fifty. Speakers call the language *tu'un savi*, *sahan sau*, *dahan davi* and many other local forms, meaning "word of the rain"; Classical Mixtec had *dzaha dzavui*. Tone is central — commonly three levels, with nine in Yoloxóchitl Mixtec — alongside contrastive nasalization and glottalization. The pre-Hispanic pictorial system was supplemented after the Conquest by missionary grammars and a Latin orthography, revised in recent decades by the Academy of the Mixtec Language and the Secretariat of Public Education. The chief pre-Hispanic deity was Dzahui, god of rain and patron of the Mixtec nation, with Nine Wind-Coo Dzahui as the civilising hero who brought agriculture; Roman Catholicism arrived with the missionaries of the 16th century.
