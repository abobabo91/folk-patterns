## Overview

The Chatinos are an Indigenous people of Mexico whose communities are located in the southeastern region of Oaxaca, in southern central Mexico. Chatino populations are found in Santos Reyes Nopala; San Juan Quiahije, also called *Kichinᴬ kiqyaᶜ*; San Miguel Panixtlahuaca, also called *Kchinᴬ Skwiᴱ*; Santiago Yaitepec, also called *Keᴳ Xinᴱ*; Santa Cruz Zenzontepec, also called *Qyaᶜ ytiᴮ*; San Juan Lachao, also called *Tsoᴴ*; Santa María Temaxcaltepec, also called *Xyanᴬ*; Santa Catarina Juquila, also called *Sqweᶠ*; and Tataltepec de Valdés, also called *Jloꞌo*. Their languages belong to the Zapotecan branch of the Oto-Manguean language family and have close cultural and linguistic ties with the Zapotec peoples. Chatino-language sources give different population figures: about 23,000 speakers in one account and 45,000 Chatino people in another.

## Music & performance

They state that audio and video recordings have documented the language during everyday life interactions and that recordings exist of narratives, ceremonies, conversations, and music in Amialtepec, San Juan Quiahije, Yolotepec, and Zacatepec Chatino. Chatino-language programming is carried by the CDI radio station XEJAM, based in Santiago Jamiltepec, Oaxaca.

## Festivals & rituals

The traditional authorities of the Chatino people are organized through civil and religious roles, with advice from elders treated as the greatest authority. The sources state that Chatinos believe in the Holy Grandmother, the Holy Father Sun, the Holy Mother Earth, and the Holy Mother Moon. They also worship the deities of water, wind, rain, the mountain, and fire.

## Foodways

They state that many Chatino people have traditionally been involved in agriculture and that agriculture depends greatly on the climate. Some Chatinos have emigrated to other parts of the district of Juquila to work on coffee plantations.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Chatino language was oral until 2003, with no written form. The Chatino Language Documentation Project began in the summer of 2003 to document and preserve Chatino and its dialects. The project used audio and video recordings of everyday life interactions and helped create a written form of the language. The project’s resources were intended to support revitalization and the creation of educational materials such as books. The sources also mention audio recordings of narratives, ceremonies, conversations, and music in Amialtepec, San Juan Quiahije, Yolotepec, and Zacatepec Chatino.

## Language & religion

Chatino is a group of Indigenous Mesoamerican languages in the Zapotecan branch of the Oto-Manguean language family. The Chatinos have close cultural and linguistic ties with the Zapotec people. One source gives the Chatino self-name *chaqꟳ tnyaᴶ*, while the Chatinos of San Juan Quiahije call themselves *neqᴬ tnyaᴶ* and call their language *Chaqᶠ tnyaᴮ*. Campbell describes three main languages: Zenzontepec Chatino, Tataltepec Chatino, and Eastern or Highland Chatino. Zenzontepec Chatino is spoken in about 10 communities in the district of Sola de Vega; Tataltepec Chatino is spoken in Tataltepec de Valdés; and Eastern Chatino is spoken in about 15–17 communities. Chatino is recognized as a national language in Mexico. The sources describe religious beliefs concerning the Holy Grandmother, the Holy Father Sun, the Holy Mother Earth, the Holy Mother Moon, and the deities of water, wind, rain, the mountain, and fire.
