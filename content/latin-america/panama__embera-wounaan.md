## Overview

The Embera-Wounaan, also known as Emberá-Waunana and formerly widely known as Chocó, are a semi-nomadic Indigenous people of Panama. They live in Darién Province along the Chucunaque, Sambú, and Tuira Rivers and their waterways. They speak the Embera and Wounaan languages, which belong to the Choco language family. Collectively, the Chocó are divided into the Emberá of the upper Atrato and San Juan rivers and the Wounaan, or Waunana, of the lower San Juan River. Their folk-culture profile in the sources is especially documented through riverine settlement, raised houses, clothing, body painting, subsistence, and household objects.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Embera men traditionally wear a minimal loin cloth when they are not in towns and sport “bowl cut” hairstyles. Embera women traditionally wear brightly colored cloth wrapped around the waist as a skirt. Except when in towns, women traditionally do not cover their torsos and wear long, straight black hair. Embera children go naked until puberty, and traditionally no one wore shoes. In some areas, traditional clothing and shoes are being replaced by western, manufactured clothing and shoes. Some children attend schools that require uniforms.

### Architecture

Embera houses are raised about eight feet above the ground and stand on large posts set into the earth. They are spaced approximately 20–50 feet, or 6–15 metres, apart and have thatched roofs made from palm fronds. The houses have no walls because the roof is tall, and all the joinery is made with *bejuco* vines. Floors are constructed from split black palm trunks or *cana blanca*, meaning white cane. Each house has a kitchen on a clay platform about three feet square, with cooking pots supported over a fire by a tripod of sturdy sticks. Access is provided by a sloped log with deep notches serving as a ladder. Houses are found in small villages of 5 to 20 dwellings along riverbanks.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The calabash tree is used to make cups, bowls, and spoons from its gourds. Household objects hanging from the supporting posts and beams include hammocks, baskets, pots, bows and arrows, mosquito nets, and clothing. Dugout canoes are commonly seen pulled up on riverbanks.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The Embera paint their bodies with a black dye made from *Genipa americana*, the berry of a species of genip tree. The dye is thought to repel insects, and the designs are known as *jagua tattoos*. On special occasions, intricate geometric patterns are printed over the body with wood blocks carved from balsa wood. Women also wear silver necklaces and silver earrings on these occasions. Many necklaces are made from old silver coins, which are pierced and threaded onto silver chains. The sources state that many of these coins date to the 19th century and are passed from mother to daughter.

## Music & performance

They state only that Wounaan actors appeared in Hollywood films, including *The Mission*, *1492: Conquest of Paradise*, and *The End of the Spear*. In those films they portrayed other Indigenous peoples while speaking their own language. The sources name Bercelio Moya and Alejandrino Moya as notable Wounaan actors and identify Robert De Niro, Jeremy Irons, Liam Neeson, Gérard Depardieu, Sigourney Weaver, Armand Assante, Frank Langella, and Ray McAnally among actors with whom members of the tribe performed.

## Dance & theatre

They describe film appearances in which Wounaan actors portrayed the Guaraní, Taíno, Carib, and another Indigenous people, rather than documenting an Embera-Wounaan performance tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that intricate geometric body patterns, silver necklaces, and silver earrings are used on special occasions.

## Foodways

The Embera diet includes wild fish and game, hunted with snares, blow guns, bows and arrows, and firearms. Cassava is an essential food. The source describes it as a poisonous root that must be pressed before cooking into a flatbread that stores well and can absorb fluids during a meal. Some communities farm rice, coffee, plantains, and other plants, and raise pigs, chickens, and cattle for food. When a hunter obtains a larger animal, such as a peccary or a tapir, the meat is shared by everyone in the village. The calabash tree provides cups, bowls, and spoons.

## Oral tradition & literature

They mention that the people have their own unwritten rules and their own form of government.

## Language & religion

The Embera-Wounaan speak the Embera and Wounaan languages, which belong to the Choco language family. The sources list several names associated with these populations, including Emberá, Wounaan, Waunana, and Chocó, as well as the meaning “people” for the name Embera. They state that healthcare is primarily provided by trained shamans. The people have their own form of government and live according to unwritten rules, while avoiding dealings with the Panamanian National Police and other branches of the Panamanian or Colombian governments.
