## Overview

"Arawak" is an exonym, not a self-designation: early Spanish explorers and administrators split the peoples they met in the Caribbean into *Caribs*, those they judged hostile, and *Arawaks*, those they judged friendly. None of the peoples so labelled called themselves that. The term has since been applied to two distinct groups — the *Lokono* of the South American mainland and the Taíno or "Island Arawaks" of the Greater Antilles and northern Lesser Antilles — both speaking languages of the Arawakan family, which may have emerged in the Orinoco valley of present-day Venezuela and became the most extensive language family in South America at the time of European contact. The group that did self-identify as Arawak, the Lokono, settled coastal Guyana, Suriname, Grenada, the Bahamas, Jamaica and parts of Trinidad and Tobago. About 10,000 Lokono live today, mostly in Guyana. In folk-culture terms their importance lies partly in what survived colonisation: Taíno influence persists in Caribbean religion, language and music.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here — the Wikipedia articles on the Arawak and on the Lokono language, and the British Museum catalogue records for the objects shown — do not describe named, vernacular textile traditions. What they do document is a body of plaited cane and fibre work, and one composite textile object.

**Twilled plaited cane boxes and baskets** — Square lidded boxes and baskets worked in twilled plaited cane with reinforced rims. The lids carry the pattern while the bottom may be left plain cane, and the design is built up by alternating dark dyed cane against natural cane. One catalogued lid bears a *'dog'* design; another box, patterned on the lid, held five cassava or vegetal shavings and is questioned in the record as a possible noise-maker.

**Plaited palm-leaf work** — Bags plaited from palm leaf, and figurative plaiting: a model fish made of *awarra* leaf.

**Composite hammock** — A hammock of single straps of twined pineapple fibre, crossed by widely spaced bars of plaited cotton yarn and ornamented with red and white cotton tassels. The pineapple-fibre warp with spaced cotton crossbars is the technical signature of the piece.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Dog* design (on a basketry box lid); geometric design (carved on the back of a *duho* seat). The sources name no other motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources used do not describe Arawak or Lokono clothing, everyday or ceremonial; the only wearable element they record is a headband on a carved figure (see Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects), embellished with a row of decorative elements that may represent shell plaques.

### Architecture

The sources touch on settlement form only archaeologically. Michael Heckenberger, an anthropologist at the University of Florida and a founder of the Central Amazon Project, and his team documented ringed villages, raised fields and large mounds among Arawakan-speaking peoples, alongside elaborate pottery and evidence of regional trade networks — together taken as indicating a complex society. The same work found deliberate soil modification: charcoal added to the earth to transform it into black earth, still famed for its agricultural productivity. House form, roofing materials and architectural ornament are not covered by the sources used here, and no named building type appears in them. Two competing models for how Taíno ancestors reached the islands bear on where such settlement traditions came from: the "Circum-Caribbean" model proposes an origin in the Colombian Andes, connected to the Arhuaco people, while the Amazonian model places the origin in the Amazon basin where the Arawakan languages developed.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery is the diagnostic craft in the archaeological record — Heckenberger's team treated elaborate pottery and other cultural traits as evidence that these people belonged to the Arawakan language family. Among the objects shown, a pottery bowl is ovoid, with a handle at each end of the rim, the handles painted white. Woodwork carries the most elaborate pieces. The *duho*, a ritual seat, is sculpted from the dense tropical hardwood guayacan in the form of a powerful male figure crouching on all fours; its eyes, mouth and shoulders are inlaid with gold and the back of the seat is carved with a geometric design. A carved wooden figure of a bird-man spirit, now known as **The Birdman**, has a prominent beak and extended wings, exposed genitals marking it masculine; inlaid shell once marked the eyes and still survives in the beak as teeth. Hunting and utility gear includes a bamboo quiver with a fitted lid holding poisoned arrows, and a *macquarie* whip with a wooden handle and a bast-fibre thong. Fishing, per the sources, used nets made of fibres, bones, hooks and harpoons.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used do not document Arawak or Lokono jewelry, tattooing, or hair practice. The nearest evidence is indirect and sculptural: gold inlaid into the eyes, mouth and shoulders of the *duho* seat, shell inlay in the eyes and beak of the Birdman figure, and that figure's headband with its row of elements that may represent shell plaques.

## Music & performance

The sources record instruments only as objects, without naming song genres, ensembles or performance repertoire. Two classes of rattle appear among the pieces shown. A **dance-rattle**, used in dances, is cylindrical with one end squared off, plaited from leaf and containing seeds, with patterns worked in black. Two **baby's rattles** are plaited from cane and patterned with dyed cane, each finished with featherwork on the handle — the same twilled cane technique as the boxes and baskets, applied to a sounding object. One further catalogue record hesitates over function: a patterned basketry box with lid, holding five cassava or vegetal shavings, is twice queried as a possible noise-maker, which would make plaited containers themselves part of the sound-making repertoire. Beyond the objects, the sources make one broad claim about music: Taíno influence survives to the present in the religions, languages and music of Caribbean cultures. Trinidad and Tobago has no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions, so no inscribed musical element attaches to this group.

## Dance & theatre

No named dances or dramatic traditions appear in the sources used. Dance is attested only through a single object: the cylindrical plaited rattle, black-patterned and seed-filled, catalogued as used in dances.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not describe an annual festival calendar or life-cycle rites, and Trinidad and Tobago carries no UNESCO ICH inscriptions. What the sources do document is ritual paraphernalia and religious survival. The *duho* is explicitly a ritual seat, gold-inlaid and carved in the form of a crouching male figure; the Birdman is a carved bird-man spirit. In the modern period, religion is where continuity is clearest. After the so-called paper genocide — the disappearance of Indians from colonial registers, so that Puerto Rico's 1787 census listed 2,300 "pure" Indios while the 1802 census listed none — the belief took hold that the Taíno were extinct, and practice continued in secret or disguised through Catholicism out of fear of discrimination. *Agua Dulce*, also known as *Tamani*, is a syncretic religion practised in the Dominican Republic and named in the sources as part of this passed-down inheritance. From the late 1980s and early 1990s a movement across the Caribbean and its diasporas called for the protection, revival and restoration of Taíno culture, cross-referencing oral history and maintained practice against Spanish journals to reconstruct what colonisation had broken. Some *Yukayekes* (Taíno tribes) have been reformed as a result, including **Higuayagua** and **Yukayeke Taíno Borikén**, with Yukayekes now in Cuba, Jamaica and Puerto Rico.

## Foodways

Cassava is the plant the name itself turns on: Arawak is described in the sources as a tribal name referring to the main crop food, the cassava root, commonly known as manioc — a woody shrub grown in tropical and subtropical regions and a staple for millions in South America, Asia and Africa. The archaeological and ethnographic record names maize and sweet potatoes as the main crops, with cassava and yautia also grown, supported by raised fields and by the charcoal-enriched black earth still valued for its productivity. Fishing supplied protein, using nets made of fibres, bones, hooks and harpoons. Cassava reappears in the object record: a plaited basketry box was found to contain five cassava or vegetal shavings. The sources used do not name prepared dishes, cooking methods, ceremonial foods, beverages or dietary rules, so nothing is set down here about how these crops reached the plate.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources name one figure from Lokono mythology: *Aiomun-Kondi*, the Lokono deity who created the world. They record no epic cycle, folktale corpus, proverb collection or storytelling context. Preservation work, by contrast, is documented in detail on the language side and in the recovery of religious knowledge. The Taíno revival movement of the late 1980s and 1990s worked by pooling knowledge held individually — transmitted as oral history or as maintained practice — and cross-referencing it against the journals of Spaniards to fill in parts of Taíno culture and religion long thought lost. Language revival has followed the same method: attempts to bring back the Taíno language, including the *Hiwatahia Hekexi* dialect, draw on words that survived into local Spanish dialects and extrapolate from other Arawakan languages in South America to supply what was lost.

## Language & religion

*Lokono*, also called Arawak (Arowak, Aruák; in Eastern Maroon Creole *Aluwakatongo*), is the eponymous language of the Arawakan family and is spoken in eastern Venezuela, Colombia, Guyana, Trinidad and Tobago, Suriname and French Guiana. Speakers call it *Lokono Dian*, "the people's speech"; *Lokono* itself means "the people". It is an active–stative language, written in a system close to the Roman alphabet with minor changes and additions. Grammatically it distinguishes event from stative verbs, marks masculine and feminine, splits nouns into inalienably and alienably possessed classes, and places tense at the end of the sentence — *bura* or *bora* for past, from *ubura* "before"; *dikki* for future, from *adiki* "after"; *loko* or *roko* for present continuous. The language is critically endangered: roughly 2,500 fluent and semi-fluent speakers remain, about five percent of the ethnic population by a 2015 assessment published in *Language Documentation and Conservation*, because it is not being transmitted to children. On religion, the sources name the creator deity *Aiomun-Kondi* and the syncretic *Agua Dulce* (*Tamani*) of the Dominican Republic, and note that Taíno practice was long disguised through Catholicism.
