## Overview

The Otavalo are indigenous highland people of northern Ecuador known as weavers and traders. They speak Kichwa and Spanish, having lost their earlier Barbacoan language after the sixteenth century. Commerce and handcrafts are their main economic activities, giving them a higher standard of living than most indigenous groups in Ecuador. Tourism brings visitors to the area for the distinctive local culture and dress.

## Material culture

Otavalo material culture centers on textiles, beadwork, and costume pieces, with items typically obtained through the Otavalo market.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Otavalo weaving uses reversible cotton bands and warp-patterned techniques, with alpaca and llama wool textiles exported internationally.

- **Woman's woven waist-band** — Cotton band with two faces, one pink and one red, decorated with wool in pink, red and green; another type is bright pink with yellow and white bands and tassels.
- **Warp-patterned band** — Long narrow band in warp-rips technique with white and blue warp against sparser weft, ending in loops and fringe.
- **Embroidered woollen shawl** — White wool shawl with embroidered decoration, part of the Otavalo costume.
- **Embroidered girl's dress** — White dress with pale blue floral decoration at neck and shoulders, with lace trim.
- **Export weaving** — Textiles in Otavalo style woven with alpaca or llama wool, made for the textile industry and sent to North America by Otavalo businesspeople.

### Clothing & dress

The Otavalo costume comprises embroidered shawls, felt hats, woven waist-bands, glass-bead necklaces, and cloth slippers.

- **Woman's embroidered wool shawl** — White shawl fastened at the breast with a long metal pin.
- **White felt hat** — Woman's hat worn as part of the Otavalo costume.
- **Cloth slippers** — Pair of cloth slippers with internal stiffening.
- **Glass-bead necklaces** — Multiple strands of glass beads worn as costume jewelry.

### Architecture

No documentation exists for Otavalo house forms or architectural traditions.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Household objects documented are modest, focusing on costume items and a large cooking pot.

- **Metal shawl pin** — Metal pin used to fasten the shawl of the Otavalo costume at the breast.
- **Large pot** — Vessel form shown in photograph, carried on the back by an Otavalo woman.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Beadwork in gold-coloured and red glass comprises the primary documented adornment, all purchased from the Otavalo market.

- **Multi-cord gold glass-bead necklace** — Small gold-coloured glass beads threaded on fourteen separate cords.
- **Four-cord gold glass-bead necklace** — Large gold-coloured glass beads strung on four cords.
- **Red glass-bead necklace** — Single cord of red glass beads long enough to be wound around the neck.

## Music & performance

One musical instrument is documented: a bamboo panpipe bought in the Otavalo market.

- **Bamboo panpipe** — Wind instrument made of bamboo, purchased in the Otavalo market.

## Dance & theatre

No information is available on Otavalo dance or dramatic traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

No documentation exists for Otavalo festivals, life-cycle rites, or ceremonial practices.

## Foodways

Otavalo subsistence historically centered on irrigated farming of maize and potatoes, with guinea pigs and wild game for protein.

- **Raised fields** (*camelones or Waru Waru*) — Irrigated agricultural fields built to regulate water supply to crops.
- **Long-distance traders** (*mindaláes*) — Merchants who brought cotton, coca, salt and dried fish from the Pacific coast into the highlands.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Otavalo are often identified with pre-Columbian Cara people and descendants of the semi-mythical Quitu culture.

## Language & religion

The Otavalo replaced their earlier Barbacoan language with Kichwa, probably after the sixteenth century, and later adopted Spanish.

- **Kichwa** — The Quechua dialect of Ecuador spoken by the Otavalo today alongside Spanish.
- **Caranqui or Cara** (*Kara*) — Extinct Barbacoan language formerly spoken in the Otavalo area, replaced by Quechua perhaps surviving until the eighteenth century.

## Glossary

- *Kichwa* — The Quechua dialect spoken in Ecuador
- *camelones* — Raised agricultural fields built for irrigation
- *Waru Waru* — Raised fields that regulate water supply to crops
- *mindaláes* — Long-distance traders who brought goods from coast to highlands
- *Caranqui* — Extinct Barbacoan language formerly spoken in the Otavalo area
- *Cara* — Another name for Caranqui, the extinct language
- *Barbacoan* — Language family of northern Ecuador and southern Colombia
- *Otavalo costume* — Named ensemble of traditional local dress including shawl, hat, waist-band and necklaces
- *alpaca* — South American camelid animal whose wool is used in textiles
- *llama* — South American camelid animal whose wool is used in textiles
