## Overview

The Itza are a Maya ethnic group descended from the Chanes of the Chontal region of Tabasco. They migrated through Bacalar, northern Yucatán, and Champotón before settling around Lake Petén Itzá in the 15th century. Their last independent kingdom remained outside Spanish rule until 1697. Today, Itza descendants are settled chiefly in San José, north of Lake Petén Itzá in Guatemala’s department of Petén. The 2002 census recorded 1,983 ethnic Itza, while the language has become nearly extinct; only 36 elderly people are described as native and fluent speakers. The Itza are important to folk-culture research because their language preserves distinctive agricultural and ecological knowledge, while their agro-forestry practices retain information about pre-colonial Maya life.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state that the use and teaching of traditional clothing were banned in the department of Petén during the 1930s as part of government policies of cultural assimilation.

### Architecture

They identify Nojpetén as the Itza capital in the Petén Basin and state that *Noj peten* means “great island” in Itza’.

## Music & performance

They state that Itza discourse makes heavy use of repetition and linguistic parallelism, and that common and mythological discourse often employs framing particles.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record several historical ritual references. Hunac Ceel was captured and was to be sacrificed by being thrown into the cenote of Chichén Itzá, but survived the attempted sacrifice and related a prophecy of the rain god Chac. In 1523, Hernán Cortés visited Nojpetén and celebrated mass with the Kan Ek’ of the Itza’. Spanish priests peacefully visited and preached to the last Itza king as late as 1696. On March 13, 1697, the Itza kingdom submitted to Spanish rule under a force led by Martín de Ursua, governor of Yucatán.

## Foodways

They state that Itza agro-forestry practices include the use of dietary and medicinal plants. The language has a rich vocabulary for crops and animals and encodes information about different varietals and individual members of species. Agricultural terms have remained virtually uninfluenced by contact with Spanish, providing insight into commonplace vocabulary from pre-contact Itza.

## Oral tradition & literature

The books of Chilam Balam recount the history of the Itza and the demise of their empire. Their account describes Hunac Ceel, a Mexicanized Putún Maya leader and founder of the Cocom dynasty of Mayapan, who fought the Itzas and was taken captive before the attempted sacrifice at the cenote of Chichén Itzá. After surviving, he related a prophecy of the rain god Chac concerning the coming harvest. Later, as lord of Mayapan, he orchestrated the destruction of Chichén Itzá with the aid of sorcery.

The Itza language also preserves discourse practices based on repetition and linguistic parallelism. Narrative discourse distinguishes what a person knows from personal experience centered in the home and town from what is less known but imaginable farther away in space and time.

## Language & religion

Itzaʼ, also known as Itza or Itzaj, belongs to the Yucatecan branch of the Mayan languages. The other languages in this branch are Yucatec, Lacandon, and Mopan. Itzaʼ is spoken near Lake Petén Itzá in north-central Guatemala and neighboring Belize, especially on the lake’s north shore in San José, Petén Department. It has fewer than 40 fluent speakers, all older adults, according to the language source.

The Guatemalan government banned the speaking of Itzá in the 1930s, and two generations of Itzá Maya grew up learning only Spanish. The Academia de Lenguas Mayas de Guatemala was established to help develop and preserve Mayan languages, including Itzaʼ. Itzaʼ has VOS word order, although VSO is also common, and its grammatical system includes ergative-absolutive alignment, grammatical gender, and an irrealis mood for future tense.
