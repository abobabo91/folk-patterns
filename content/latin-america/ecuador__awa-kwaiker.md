## Overview

The Awá, also known as the Kwaiker or Awa-Kwaiker, are an Indigenous people of Ecuador and Colombia. They primarily inhabit Carchi and Sucumbios in northern Ecuador and Nariño and Putumayo in southern Colombia. Their population is around 32,555, and they speak Awa Pit. The Awa Reserve was established in northwestern Ecuador in 1987, combining Indigenous and forestry legislature so that the Awá could manage the forest and their own lands. The reserve lies in the Choco Forest within the Tumbes-Chocó-Magdalena region, described as one of the most biodiverse places on the planet. Logging and mining interests are illegally active there.

## Foodways

The Awá traditionally hunt, gather, fish, and cultivate plants. They also farm livestock, including chickens, ducks, guinea pigs, and pigs. Their agriculture includes a practice called “slash and mulch”: small parcels of about 1.25 to 5 acres are cleared, while fallen plants and trees are left to decay. Within days, this vegetation becomes a layer of humus favorable for planting. Parcels are cultivated for two or three seasons and then left fallow for periods of over seven years. Intercropping is practiced, with many varieties of manioc and plantains cultivated alongside corn, Colocasia, Xanthosoma, beans, sugarcane, hot peppers, chirimoya, tomato, tamarind, mango, achiote, borojo, naranjilla, papaya, inga, avocado, peach palm, and other useful plants. Hunting includes Central American Agouti, paca, collared peccary, brocket deer, iguana, and several birds, and hunting is regulated on Awa land.
