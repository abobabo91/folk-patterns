## Overview

The Tojolabal are a Maya people of the Mexican state of Chiapas. They traditionally speak Tojolabal, also called Tojol-ab'al, a Mayan language related to Chuj. The largest group is believed to live in Las Margaritas, followed by Comitán, and the population is distributed across 439 Tojolabal villages around the municipalities of Chiapas. Tojolabal culture is represented in the sources primarily through its language, its relationship to Maya identity, its village settlement pattern, and the ancient site of K'atepan. The language’s name derives from *tohol aˈbal*, meaning “right language.” Nineteenth-century documents sometimes called the language and its speakers “Chaneabal,” meaning “four languages.” The sources also document an official writing standard and indigenous-language radio programming.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The Tojolabal are associated with the ancient site of *K'atepan*, described as a temple plaza set against a mountainside and reached by large stairways.

## Music & performance

Tojol-abʼal-language programming is carried by the National Institute of Indigenous Peoples radio station XEVFS, which broadcasts from Las Margaritas. The sources also mention the Tojolabʼal Collection of Jill Brody at the Archive of the Indigenous Languages of Latin America, including audio recordings; access is restricted but available to researchers by request.

## Festivals & rituals

## Oral tradition & literature

Carlos Lenkersdorf’s *Los hombres verdaderos. Voces y testimonios tojolabales* is listed as a work concerning Tojolabal voices and testimonies, language and society, nature, culture, arts, and the cosmic community.

## Language & religion

Tojol-ab'al is a Mayan language spoken in Chiapas, Mexico, principally in the departments of the Chiapanecan Colonia of Las Margaritas, by about 70,000 people. It is related to Chuj. The name *tohol aˈbal* means “right language,” while nineteenth-century documents sometimes used “Chaneabal,” meaning “four languages,” possibly referring to Tzotzil, Tzeltal, Tojolabal, and Chuj. The language has an official writing standard, *Skujlayub'il Sts'ijb'ajel K'umal Tojol-ab'al*, whose Spanish title is *Norma de Escritura de la Lengua Tojol-ab’al*. Published in 2011 by the Instituto Nacional de Lenguas Indígenas, it established an official alphabet, grammar rules, and other linguistic features for indigenous education. The alphabet is officially called *Tsome sat ts'ijb'anel*, or “sign set,” and contains 28 letters: 23 consonants and 5 vowels.
