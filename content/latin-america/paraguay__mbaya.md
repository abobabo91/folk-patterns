## Overview

The Mbayá or Mbyá are an Indigenous people of South America who formerly ranged on both sides of the Paraguay River, along the northern and northwestern Paraguay frontier, in eastern Bolivia, and in the adjacent province of Mato Grosso do Sul, Brazil. They have also been called Caduveo, while the Kadiwéu people of Brazil are the surviving branch of the Mbayá. The Mbayá called themselves the Eyiguayegis, “people of the palm,” referring to the abundant palm trees in their home country. They spoke a Guaycuruan language and developed an equestrian culture after acquiring horses from the Spanish. Their history is marked by mobility, raiding, trade, warfare, relations with the Guaná, and resistance to Spanish and Portuguese settlers, missionaries, and governments.

## Material culture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources document that the Mbayá acquired iron tools and weapons from the Spanish by theft or trade. The Guaná provided agricultural products and textiles, while the Mbayá supplied protection and European goods such as iron tools.

## Music & performance

The linguistic sources document Kadiweu–Portuguese dictionaries, Bible translations, grammatical analysis, and language documentation.

## Festivals & rituals

The historical sources do record Roman Catholic missionary activity: the first moderately successful Roman Catholic mission among the Mbayá was established in 1760 east of Concepción, Paraguay, and the Santo Corazon mission was established in 1760 in Bolivia. These accounts concern mission history rather than a documented Mbayá ritual system.

## Foodways

They state that the Guaná provided Mbayá chiefs with agricultural products and that the Mbayá later adopted agriculture and weaving as relations between the two peoples developed.

## Oral tradition & literature

The linguistic record includes an eighteenth-century ethnographic account of the Kadiwéu by F. José Sanchez-Labrador, a Kadiweu–Portuguese dictionary published by Glyn Griffiths in 2002, and a translation of the Old and New Testament into Kadiweu by Glyn Griffiths.

## Language & religion

Kadiwéu is a Guaicuruan language spoken by the Kadiweu people of Brazil and historically by other Mbayá groups. It is spoken near the Brazil-Paraguay border in the state of Mato Grosso do Sul, and the nearest town named by the source is Bodoquena, 60 kilometers away. The language is mainly subject–verb–object and has variants of its name including Kaduveo, Caduveo, Kadivéu, and Kadiveo. Data collected in 1999 by FUNAI gave a total Kadiwéu population of 1,014, while data collected in 2014 gave 1,413; data from 1976 recorded 500 speakers. The sources state that Kadiweu has no tense markers but uses aspect marked on the verb, including completive, incompletive, durative, telic, atelic, repetitive, and intensive aspects. The sources describe Spanish and Portuguese colonial missions and Roman Catholic activity.
