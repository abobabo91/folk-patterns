## Overview

The Pai Tavytera are an Indigenous people of Paraguay and Brazil. In Paraguay, they primarily live in Amambay Department; in Brazil, they live in Mato Grosso do Sul. They are also known as the Ava, Caaguá, Caingua, Caiwá, Kaa'wa, Kainguá, Kaiowá, Kaiwá, Kayova, Montese, Paï, Paï-Cayuä, Paï-Tavyterä, Paingua, Pan, and Tavytera people. “Paï-Tavytera” is described as an arbitrary name for northern Guaraní people of eastern Paraguay. They are closely related to the Guarani-Kaiowá people of Brazil. Their culture includes a sacred wooden altar, swidden agriculture, body painting, headdresses, necklaces, basketry, ceramics, ritual music, dance, and oral traditions associated with Jasuka Venda.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name the “footstep style” of petroglyphs at Jasuka Venda.

### Clothing & dress

They do document cotton and feathers, including toucan feathers, as materials used for headdresses. Urucú, a red dye made from *Bixa orellana*, is used for body painting.

The altar is associated with ritual objects rather than a complete clothing system. Its medicine man’s gourd rattle is used by the spiritual healer to begin a prayer involving song and dance, while a taquara bamboo staff is described as a woman’s musical instrument that gives rhythm to rituals.

### Architecture

The central built ritual structure documented in the sources is the wooden altar, called *mba’e marangatu* in Guaraní. It is considered a sacred sanctuary and a focal point of community life. Altars usually stand in the homes of spiritual leaders or important leaders, and the community gathers around them for worship or to discuss matters important to the community.

The altar includes wooden rods representing deities or saints. A gourd used for baptizing children hangs from those rods, together with the medicine man’s gourd rattle.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Women make ceramics, while men typically weave baskets. The altar’s wooden rods, baptismal gourd, medicine man’s gourd rattle, and taquara bamboo staff are identified as culturally significant objects connected with religious practice.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Pai Tavytera people make necklaces from carved wood and colorful seeds of different fruits. Labrets are made from resin. Urucú, a red dye made from *Bixa orellana*, is used for body painting. Cotton and feathers, including toucan feathers, are used for headdresses.

## Music & performance

The sources describe music as part of religious practice centered on the wooden altar. The medicine man’s gourd rattle is the most important item for the spiritual healer to begin a prayer. The prayer is a song and a dance intended to communicate with the spirits. The taquara bamboo staff is a woman’s musical instrument and gives rhythm to the rituals.

The altar is where the community gathers for worship and for discussion of important community matters. The sources therefore connect performance with communal and spiritual activity rather than documenting a separate professional music tradition. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription concerns this ethnic group.

The language article identifies Pai Tavyterã as a Guarani language and records its relationship with the Kaiwá language of Brazil.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is included in the prayer of the spiritual healer, together with song and the playing of the medicine man’s gourd rattle.

The altar provides the principal documented performance setting. The community gathers around it for worship, and the ritual includes the taquara bamboo staff, which gives rhythm to the proceedings. No additional performance contexts are described.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe religious practices connected with the wooden altar, *mba’e marangatu*. The altar is a sacred sanctuary and a focal point of the community, generally located in the home of a spiritual leader or important leader. The community gathers there for worship and to discuss matters important to the community.

A gourd hanging from the altar is used for baptizing children. The medicine man’s gourd rattle supports the spiritual healer’s prayer, which consists of song and dance intended to communicate with the spirits. The taquara bamboo staff gives rhythm to the rituals.

## Foodways

Pai Tavytera agriculture is practiced in the sub-tropical environment of eastern Paraguay. They practice swidden agriculture, with maize as the primary crop and manioc as a supplement. They also cultivate citrus trees, bananas, cotton, pineapples, rice, soybeans, and medicinal plants.

Chickens, pigs, cattle, horses, and donkeys are identified as popular farm animals.

The sources connect recent changes in the environment with Pai Tavytera subsistence. An influx of settlers has disrupted their hunting lifestyle. No further details about hunting methods, game animals, fishing, gathering, or food exchange are provided.

## Oral tradition & literature

Jasuka Venda is an important cultural site for the Pai Tavytera. The hill contains petroglyphs in the “footstep style,” and Pai Tavytera oral history says that God created the universe there.

The Pai Tavytera are also being consulted in interpreting ancient rock art in Amambay. This consultation connects oral history and cultural knowledge with the interpretation of the site’s petroglyphs.

## Language & religion

Pai Tavyterã is a Guarani language and belongs to the Tupi-Guarani language family, in the Guarani I division. It is spoken in eastern Paraguay, including Amambay, eastern Concepción, eastern San Pedro, and northern Canindeyú Departments. The language article states that about 600 Pai Tavytera people speak it. It has 70% lexical similarity with the Kaiwá language spoken in Brazil, and language use is shifting toward Guaraní. The language is written in the Latin script.

The wooden altar, *mba’e marangatu*, is central to Pai Tavytera religious beliefs. Wooden rods represent deities or saints, and the altar is a place of worship and spiritual communication.
