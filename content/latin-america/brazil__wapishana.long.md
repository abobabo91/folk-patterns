## Overview

The Wapishana — also written Wapichan, Wapisiana, Wapitxana, Vapidiana and Wapixana — are an Indigenous people of the Roraima area straddling the Brazil–Guyana border. In Brazil they live in the state of Roraima, north and east of Boa Vista, concentrated in the Serra da Lua (Moon Ridge) region between the Branco River and its affluent the Tacutu, with mixed Wapishana–Macushi villages on the lower Uraricoera. In Guyana they occupy the southern Rupununi savannas between the Takutu, Rupununi and Kwitaro rivers, bordering Macushi territory at the Kanuku Mountains to the north and Wai-Wai land to the south. Population estimates give about 9,441 in Brazil (2014), 6,000 in Guyana (1990) and 37 in Venezuela (2011). Their language is Arawakan — the only surviving Arawakan language of the circum-Roraima area. For folk culture they matter above all for cassava processing technology and the basketry and handspun cotton that serve it.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The museum objects shown here document two textile lines — handspun cotton weaving by women, and the men's basketry that dominates the collection.

**Hammock (*shrimék*)** — A double-woven hammock of spindle-spun cotton; the Museum of Ethnography example made at Sand Creek measures 290 by 154 cm, of which the woven body is 123 cm, with cord work of about 70 cm at each side. A British Museum hammock in handspun cotton is likewise double-woven, off-white with tassels along both long sides and off-white and brown cotton ropes joined at each short end as the suspension.

**Baby slings and hammocks** — Women spin cotton and weave the thread into baby slings as well as hammocks; this, with clay pots, is the women's side of household manufacture.

**Beaded woman's apron** — A woman's apron worked entirely in beads, collected in British Guiana by Walter Roth, is the one garment in the collection.

**Basketry sifters and squeezers (*nirsh*)** — The manioc press named *nirsh* in Wapishana is a long plaited cylinder, open at one end and fitted with heavy loops at both; filled with grated cassava and hung from the roof of the dwelling, a lever threaded through the lower end is pressed down so the cylinder stretches and the poisonous juice is squeezed out. Plaiting is men's work. Sifter trays are woven on a square wooden frame in black and white fibre with red feathers at the corners; squeezers combine the same black-and-white fibre with red feather trim.

**Backpack basketry** — Carrying baskets are woven onto a wooden and cane framework, the rim edged in bark wrapping and bound with fibre rope; a child's version adds red, blue and black feathers, and a separate plaited cover (the "knapsack-cover") sheathes the back-basket. A child's basket in graded browns has wooden rim supports and red and blue feathers.

**Mats and trays** — A square plaited mat and a "freak-tray" made from half an *ite* leaf, both collected by Roth, round out the plaited forms.

### Clothing & dress

The sources used describe only one item of Wapishana dress: a woman's apron made of beads, collected in British Guiana. Introduced crafts include needlework and dressmaking, but the sources give no account of garment types, head coverings, belts, footwear or ceremonial dress distinct from daily wear.

### Architecture

Early Wapishana settlements were temporary clusters of homes; since the twentieth century villages have been permanent and usually arranged around a church, with schools, meeting houses and shops added by government. Villagers gather at the church or school to discuss local affairs, and men often play soccer in open ground nearby afterwards; the dwelling itself appears in the sources only through its roof, from which the manioc press is hung. In some places people have preferred to live in open country at a distance from their fields, putting up separate structures at the farms for food processing; a few families live on their farms, though others in the community look on this poorly. Land is not held as private property, and house and garden space is assigned flexibly, with newcomers required to obtain permission from villagers before settling. Brazilian villages average about 150 inhabitants and Guyanese ones about 500.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Women make clay cooking pots. The storage jar in the Museum of Ethnography is built up from clay coils in a yellow-grey clay full of mica particles — most likely added — and is conical with the point downward, its narrow mouth slightly everted; it held cassava beer and belonged to a woman in Sand Creek who had received it in turn from the southern Wapishana. Men make wooden stools, and the basketry sieves, squeezers and presses used in preparing cassava and other foods. Cassava flour is toasted on iron griddles. Men also make arrows, fencing wire into points, but bows and arrows obtained from other Indigenous people have been almost entirely replaced by shotguns. Rustic furniture making is a recent introduction.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The collection holds one ornament: a child's bead necklace with a tapir hoof and toucan beaks hung from it, collected by Walter Roth in British Guiana. Feather decoration otherwise appears on objects rather than persons — red, blue and black feathers finish the corners and rims of sifters, squeezers, backpacks and children's baskets.

## Festivals & rituals

Healing and death furnish what the sources record of ritual life. Certain men once specialised in curing: they beat leaves and "blew" cures, and could use the same techniques to make people sick or to kill. No Wapishana now admits to these rituals and few follow them, but men and women perform a curing shaped by Catholicism, northeastern Brazilian folk medicine and other non-Indigenous practice. In most villages a Wapishana man serves as catechist, leading Sunday service when no priest is present. Deaths were attributed to evil spirits or to *kanaima* — healers who turned their powers to harm, acting out of envy to kill those wiser, better loved or more fortunate; the concept is tied to outsiders and enemies of the social group, in contrast to the kinship and cooperation binding its members. Contemporary Wapishana name hepatitis, malaria and pneumonia as causes of death while still often holding the true cause to be *kanaima* or another malevolent spirit. A person's prized possessions are buried with them for use in the afterlife. Polygyny, once practised by leaders, has nearly disappeared, possibly under missionary pressure, and most marriages are now consecrated by a Catholic priest; divorce is not uncommon and may be initiated by either spouse, with a village leader sometimes intervening to keep a couple together. Plants with magical properties form their own class in the language, *wapananinau*, with important functions for shamans of the traditional beliefs.

## Foodways

Cassava is the crop at the centre of Wapishana life, and has a bewildering variety of names in the language. Women grate it, express its juice, sieve it and toast it on iron griddles into flour and thick flat breads, locally called *beiju*; the poisonous juice is driven out beforehand in the plaited press, *nirsh*. Cassava beer was stored in coil-built clay jars. Other crops include sweet potatoes and further roots, squashes, tomatoes, greens, onions, dozens of kinds of hot pepper and many more. Men hunt deer, agouti, wild turkeys and birds; the whole family fishes. Cattle, swine, chickens, ducks and other animals introduced over the past two centuries are now part of the ordinary diet. Exchange of produce, animals and homemade food brings in the small sums of cash used to buy store food and household goods. Peddlers occasionally try to trade, but these transactions are exploitative and avoided by all but the most isolated.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used do not record Wapishana folktales, epic or storytelling occasions, but they document the language work that carries oral knowledge. Many plants and animals endemic to the region are known only in Wapishana, and the language has its own taxonomy: three plant classes — *karam'makau* for plants gathered wild, *wapaurib bau* for domesticated plants, often named from the place or farmer they came from, and *wapananinau* for plants with magical properties — encode the cultivation criteria of their users. Wapishana was first used as a teaching language in Indigenous schools in 1987; it was recognised as an official language in the Brazilian municipalities of Cantá and Bonfim in the Serra da Lua region in 2014; Roraima Federal University opened an extension programme for learning it in 2009; and in Guyana organisations such as Wapichan Wadauniinao Ati'o work on preservation. A Wapishana dictionary is published in the Intercontinental Dictionary Series.

## Language & religion

Wapishana belongs to the Arawakan family and is spoken by over 13,000 people on both sides of the border — the only remaining Arawakan language of the circum-Roraima area. Kaufman (1994) treated Wapishana, Atorada and Mapidian as dialects; Aikhenvald (1999) separates Mawayana/Mapidian/Mawakwa from Wapishana and places them in a Rio Branco branch; Ramirez (2020) counts Atorai a dialect of Wapishana. Ethnologue puts Atorada's lexical similarity to Wapishana at 50% and Wapishana's to Mapidian at 10%. Wapishana and the Cariban language Pemon have borrowed heavily from one another through intensive contact. More than 80% of Wapishana speak the national language they are in contact with — Portuguese in Brazil, English in Guyana — and many speak both along with the mother tongue. Religious life today runs through Catholicism, village catechists and a curing practice blended with northeastern Brazilian folk medicine, alongside persisting belief in *kanaima*.
