## Overview

The Taíno were Indigenous peoples of most of the West Indies, extending from the Lucayan Archipelago through Cuba, Jamaica, Hispaniola, and Puerto Rico to Guadeloupe. They belonged to the Arawak group of Indigenous peoples and are also called Island Arawaks or Antillean Arawaks. Historically, they lived in agricultural societies organized into settlements and chiefdoms ruled by caciques, with matrilineal kinship and inheritance. Their religion centered on zemis, spirits or ancestors. The Taíno historically spoke an Arawakan language with Classical and Ciboney varieties. Many people today identify as Taíno or as having Taíno descent, especially in Puerto Rico, Cuba, and the Dominican Republic, while revivalist communities seek to incorporate Taíno culture into contemporary life.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

 They describe cotton, woven goods, and feathered adornments.

**Cotton hammocks** — Taíno household furnishings included cotton hammocks, used for sleeping and sitting.

**Cotton aprons** — Married women wore a small cotton apron called a *nagua*.

**Cotton belts** — Caciques used cotton woven belts among the garments and adornments that identified their status.

**Cotton masks** — Caciques used masks or *guaizas* made of cotton with feathers.

**Woven mats** — Sleeping and sitting mats were made of palm.

**Woven seats** — Wooden chairs called *dujo* or *duho* had woven seats and platforms.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no specific textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Taíno women commonly wore their hair with bangs in front and longer in the back. They occasionally wore gold jewellery, paint, and shells. Taíno men and unmarried women usually went naked, while married women wore a small cotton apron called a *nagua*. Caciques wore status garments and adornments including tunics of cotton and rare feathers, crowns, cotton masks or *guaizas* with feathers, coloured stones, shells or gold, cotton woven belts, and necklaces of snail beads or stones with small masks made of gold or other material. These objects identified the cacique’s role.

### Architecture

Taíno settlements were called *yucayeques* and varied in size according to their location. A typical village had a central plaza used for games, festivals, religious rituals, public ceremonies, and *areitos*. The general population lived in large circular buildings called *bohios*, made from wooden poles, woven straw, and palm leaves. These houses surrounded the central plaza and could hold ten to fifteen families each. The cacique and the cacique’s family lived in rectangular buildings called *caney*, made with similar materials and equipped with wooden porches. The sources describe the village plaza as oval, rectangular, narrow, or elongated.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources associate Taíno archaeological evidence with pottery and describe an early form of proto-writing in petroglyphs. Important wooden objects included *dujo* or *duho* stools and chairs, wooden war clubs called *macanas*, bows, arrows, fishing equipment, and the wooden hoe called a *coa*. Canoes, or *kanoa*, were dugouts of various sizes. Caciques carried a *guanín*, an alloy of gold and copper of South American origin, as a status symbol. In ritual contexts, sacred stools, ceremonial “vomit sticks,” and zemi depictions were laminated with *guanin*, an alloy of gold, silver, and copper that had to be imported because there was no smelting culture.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Taíno women occasionally wore gold jewellery, paint, and shells. Cacique adornment included coloured stones, shells or gold, necklaces of snail beads or stones, and small masks made of gold or other material. Caciques also used crowns, rare feathers, cotton masks or *guaizas*, tunics, and cotton woven belts to identify their position. A *guanín* was a principal status symbol carried by the cacique. Zemi amulets could take the form of trigonolites and might adorn the head, chest, or bicep. The sources describe hair worn with bangs in front and longer in the back.

## Music & performance

Taíno mythology was recorded in communal sacred performances called *areitos*, which involved dance, music, oratory, fabric, and trance. Areitos were performed for important social events including harvest time, births, marriages, and the deaths of chiefs. The sources also describe chiefs such as Anacaona as poets and musicians who contributed to the mythic archive of the areitos. Areitos preserved narratives and religious knowledge, although most areitos are now lost. They do mention rattle gourds as part of Taíno ritual material culture and identify singing, music, oratory, dance, fabric, and trance as elements of performance. Religious practice involved priests called *behikes* or *bohikes*, while chiefs or *kasikes* also played important roles. Ritual performance could include the cohoba ceremony, in which hallucinogenic substances were insufflated after fasting, river bathing, and the use of sacred “vomit sticks.”

## Dance & theatre

The principal named performance tradition is the *areito*, a communal sacred performance combining dance, music, oratory, fabric, and trance. Areitos marked harvests, births, marriages, and the deaths of chiefs, and served as a medium for recording mythology and social knowledge. The sources also describe the ceremonial ball game *batey*, played by teams of men and occasionally women, using a solid rubber ball. Games took place in village plazas or rectangular ball courts and are believed to have helped resolve conflicts between communities.

## Festivals & rituals

 They identify *areitos* as communal sacred performances connected with harvest time, births, marriages, and the deaths of chiefs. The central plaza of a *yucayeque* was used for festivals, religious rituals, public ceremonies, and games.

Taíno religious practice centered on the veneration of zemis, ancestors, and mythic heroes. Priests called *behikes* or *bohikes* interpreted the will of the gods, cured the sick, healed the wounded, and advised caciques. Before performing these functions, they carried out cleansing and purification rituals, including fasting for several days and inhaling sacred tobacco snuff. The cohoba ritual involved fasting, river bathing, and sacred “vomit sticks,” followed by the insufflation of a powder made from the seeds of *Anadenanthera peregrina*. The sources describe caves as important ritual sites and identify the caves of Iguanaboina and Cacibajagua in Taíno mythology. Funerary practice included burning the deceased and placing the skull and long bones inside a gourd or woven basket hung from the roof of the house.

## Foodways

Taíno staples included vegetables, fruit, meat, and fish. The sources name yuca, batata, maize, squash, beans, peppers, peanuts, pineapples, palm nuts, guavas, and Zamia roots. Yuca was the staple crop in many areas. Fields were prepared in mounds called *conucos*, and yuca was planted with a wooden hoe called a *coa*. Women processed poisonous cassava by squeezing out its toxic juices, then grinding the roots into flour for bread. Batata was the next most important root crop.

Maize was cultivated in clearings made with slash-and-burn techniques. It was cooked and eaten off the cob rather than ground into flour, and it was also used to make an alcoholic beverage called *chicha*. Tobacco was grown around houses and used in medicine and religious rituals. Taíno people smoked dried leaves in pipes and cigars or inhaled crushed leaves through a hollow tube.

Fishing involved nets, spears, weirs, hook and line, and a method using a remora attached to a line secured to a canoe. The Taíno also collected mussels and oysters, hunted birds and small animals, and kept fish, turtles, hutias, and dogs in enclosures. Mythology identifies *casabe* bread as a staple food learned by Deminan and his brothers.

## Oral tradition & literature

Taíno mythology consists of narratives about origins, life, the Earth, the universe, social laws, and proper and improper behavior. Important stories include the emergence of the Sun and Moon from the cave of Iguanaboina; the creation of the ocean from a gourd containing Yayael’s bones; and the journeys of Deminan Caracaracol and his brothers, who acquired knowledge of agriculture, fire, casabe bread, tobacco, shamanism, healing, magic, and cohoba.

The mythology was transmitted through *areitos* and was recorded by Spanish chroniclers. Friar Ramón Pané produced *Legends and Beliefs of the Indians of the Antilles* after studying the religion of the Indigenous people of northern Hispaniola. The original Spanish manuscript has been lost, and the surviving Italian version contains errors of transliteration. Bartolomé de las Casas also recorded information about Taíno customs in *Historia de las Indias*. Contemporary revival projects seek to reconstruct Taíno language and culture, although the sources state that these projects differ in their objectives and methods.

## Language & religion

Taíno was an Arawakan language and, at the time of Spanish contact, was the most common language spoken throughout the Caribbean. Classical Taíno was spoken in Puerto Rico, most of Hispaniola, the Lesser Antilles, and easternmost Cuba. Ciboney Taíno was spoken in central and western Cuba, the Lucayan Archipelago, western Hispaniola, and probably Jamaica. The language was not written; it was recorded through Spanish transcription. Some Taíno words entered Spanish and English, including *barbacoa*, *hamaca*, *kanoa*, *tabaco*, *sabana*, and *juracán*.

Religion centered on zemis, ancestors, and mythic heroes, with no strict distinction between natural and supernatural domains. Major named beings include Atabey, Yúcahu, Guabancex, Yaya, and Opiyelguobiran. The dead could become *hupia*, spirits associated with a remote earthly paradise called Coaybay. Priests called *behikes* or *bohikes* administered religious practice, while caciques held both temporal and spiritual functions. The sources also describe internal diversity among Island Arawak religions and their coexistence with other Indigenous religions and cultures.
