## Overview

The Atikum, also known as *Huamuê* or *Uamué*, are an Indigenous people of Brazil who live in Bahia and Pernambuco. They have 20 villages within the Atikum Indigenous Land, near Carnaubeira da Penha. They are also known as the “caboclos of the Serra do Umã.” The Atikum sought federal recognition from the Brazilian government beginning in the 1940s. Today, Atikum people speak Portuguese. Formerly, they spoke Atikum, also called Uamué, Huamoé, Uman, or Aticum, an extinct language of Brazil that is too poorly attested to classify.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Atikum language source states that the people preserve some of their language in the context of nature, including *toê*, meaning “fire,” and *sarapó*, meaning “large edible snake.” It also states that three Aticum word lists were recorded in 1961 by Menno Kroeker from three informants in Pernambuco State and published in Meader (1978). The lists were inconsistent between informants and contained a number of modified Portuguese words; Kroeker therefore speculated that the informants intentionally gave him incorrect forms.

## Language & religion

Today the Atikum speak Portuguese and have entirely switched to it. Formerly they spoke Atikum, also known as Uamué, Huamoé, Uman, Huamuê, Huamoi, Uame, Wamoé, Umã, Aticum, and Araticum. The language is extinct and too poorly attested to classify; it has been described as a linguistic isolate. The source notes that there has been speculation that Huamoé was a Kariri language, while the Pankararú language was historically spoken just to the south.
