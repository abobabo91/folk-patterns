## Overview

The Kadiwéu are an Indigenous people of Brazil and the largest surviving branch of the Mbayá people. In 1998, they lived in four villages, while some families lived independently in the jungle. Today they live in the Kadiweu Indigenous Land, a large reserve established in 1903 in Mato Grosso do Sul, in the municipality of Porto Murtinho, between the Serra de Bodoquena and the Nabileque and Aquidavão rivers. The Kadiwéu are known for their horse riding skills. Their language belongs to the Guaicuruan language family, and they are the last surviving group of Mbayá peoples. The Kadiwéu population was recorded as 1,014 in 1999 and 1,413 in 2014, while the language had around 1,200–1,800 speakers in Brazil.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Kadiwéu language has been documented through linguistic and religious works. Glyn Griffiths translated the Old and New Testament of the Bible into Kadiweu, and an influential 18th-century ethnographic account was written by F. José Sanchez-Labrador.

## Language & religion

Kadiwéu is a Guaicuruan language spoken by the Kadiweu people of Brazil and historically by other Mbayá groups. It is mainly a subject–verb–object language and is spoken near the Brazil-Paraguay border in Mato Grosso do Sul. The nearest town is Bodoquena, which is 60 kilometers away. The language has variants of its name including Kaduveo, Caduveo, Kadivéu, and Kadiveo. According to Sandalo, Kadiweu has no tense markers but marks aspect on the verb, including completive, incompletive, durative, telic, atelic, repetitive, and intensive aspects. The sources describe translations of the Old and New Testament into Kadiweu.
