## Overview

The Kʼicheʼ are Indigenous peoples of the Americas and one of the Maya peoples. They primarily live in the western highlands of Guatemala, especially in Totonicapán, El Quiché, Quetzaltenango, Sololá, and Suchitepéquez. El Quiché forms their heartland, while pre-Columbian settlements and influence also reached the valley of Antigua and coastal areas in Escuintla. According to the 2018 census, Kʼicheʼ people constituted 11% of Guatemala’s population, accounting for 1,610,551 people. Their language belongs to the Mayan language family. Kʼicheʼ history is associated with the ancient Maya civilization, the highland state of Qʼumarkaj, the Popol Vuh, and continuing Indigenous cultural and political movements.

### Architecture

They state that Qʼumarkaj was the political, ceremonial, and social center of the Kʼicheʼ people, covering an estimated area of 3.25 km2 across the Resguardo plateau. Its ruins can still be seen a short distance from Santa Cruz del Quiché.

## Music & performance

They mention the Dance of the Conquest, which commemorates Tecun Uman and tells the story of the conversion of Indigenous people to Christianity following the Spanish Conquest.

## Dance & theatre

The **Dance of the Conquest** is identified as a Kʼicheʼ-associated performance that tells the story of Indigenous conversion to Christianity following the Spanish Conquest. The sources connect it with the annual memorialization of Tecun Uman’s death on February 20, 1524.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that Tecun Uman’s death on February 20, 1524 is memorialized each year by the Guatemalan people, partly through the Dance of the Conquest. ## Oral tradition & literature

The Popol Vuh is described as one of the most significant surviving Mesoamerican literary documents and a primary source for knowledge about Maya societal traditions, beliefs, and mythology. Originally written around the 1550s, it contains mythological and ethno-historical narratives drawn from earlier pre-Columbian sources and oral traditions. Its sacred narrative includes the creation of the world and humans by the gods, the story of the divine brothers, and Kʼicheʼ history from migration into their homeland to the Spanish conquest.

The Popol Vuh remains an important symbol of Indigenous culture for present-day Guatemalans and people of Mayan descent. It has been used in religious and spiritual ceremonies, political movements and protests, historical research, and efforts to defend traditional lands and political rights. In 1971 it was declared the official book of Guatemala. Luis Enrique Sam Colop is identified as a Kʼicheʼ writer known for translating the Popol Vuh from Kʼicheʼ into modern Spanish while restoring its original poetry element.

## Language & religion

Kʼicheʼ, also known as Quiché and natively called *Qatzijobʼal* among its speakers, is a Mayan language spoken by the Kʼicheʼ people in the central highlands of Guatemala and Mexico. It has over a million speakers and is the second most widely spoken language in Guatemala after Spanish. The language has East, West, Central, North, and South dialects. Related Maya languages named by the sources include Uspantek, Sakapultek, Kaqchikel, and Tzutujil.

Historically, different orthographies were used to write Kʼicheʼ. The classical orthography of Father Ximénez, who wrote down the Popol Vuh, was based on Spanish orthography and was replaced by a standardized orthography defined by the Academia de Lenguas Mayas de Guatemala. Kʼicheʼ religious life has been influenced by Catholic Christianity, while the Popol Vuh continues to be used in religious and spiritual ceremonies.
