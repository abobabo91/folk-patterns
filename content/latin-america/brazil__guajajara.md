## Overview

The Guajajara are an Indigenous people in the Brazilian state of Maranhão, in Brazil. They are one of the most numerous Indigenous groups in Brazil, with an estimated 13,100 individuals living on Indigenous land. Their language is Guajajara, one of the two sociolinguistic varieties of Tenetehára, a Tupi–Guarani language also spoken by the Tembé people. Guajajara communities live in territories including Arariboia Indigenous Land, at the north-eastern edge of the Amazon rainforest. Their contemporary public profile is closely connected to the Guardians of the Forest, a volunteer group that protects the rainforest from illegal loggers, miners, land grabbers, and drug traffickers. The sources describe their relationship with the environment as reciprocal, emphasizing kinship and an ancestral obligation to protect the land.

## Language & religion

Tenetehára is a Tupi–Guarani language spoken in Maranhão. Sociolinguistically, it consists of Guajajara and Tembé, which are mutually intelligible. Guajajara is spoken by two-thirds of its 20,000 people, while Tembé was spoken by less than a quarter of its ethnic population of 820 in 2000. Tenetehára has 21 phonemes, including 14 consonants and 7 vowels, and has verb-subject-object word order.
