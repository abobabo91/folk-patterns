## Overview

The Cofán, who call themselves *Aʼi*, are an Indigenous people of Sucumbíos Province in northeast Ecuador and of southern Colombia. Their territory lies between the Guamués River, a tributary of the Putumayo River, and the Aguarico River, a tributary of the Napo River; communities are also located in the Colombian departments of Putumayo and Nariño. Population estimates in the sources range from about 1,500 in a 2000 survey to 2,100 in a 2010 survey, compared with approximately 15,000 in the mid-16th century. They speak Aʼingae, also called Cofán, a language isolate with around 1,500 speakers. Their cultural and political history includes resistance to Inca and Spanish expansion, the protection of ancestral lands, and campaigns against oil extraction.

## Material culture

### Architecture

They state that traditional skills include housebuilding, but provide no further description.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do state that Cofán communities traditionally used wooden dugout canoes for river travel and have more recently built large fiberglass canoes for sale and for their own use.

## Music & performance

They describe Aʼingae language documentation and mention the translation of the New Testament into the language.

## Festivals & rituals

The Cofán religious tradition is described as shamanistic, and the sources identify drinking *yaje* as a cultural practice associated with participation in Cofán life.

## Foodways

They state that a Cofán foundation works to preserve traditional foods in the rivers, that communities in Zabalo are raising turtles and caimans for release, and that they are beginning to raise chickens as a food source. The sources also identify traditional hunter-gathering and mention hunting as a cultural practice.

## Oral tradition & literature

They mention Randall Borman’s work on Cofán cosmology and history as revealed in legends, and identify *The North Wind and the Sun* as a text translated into Aʼingae. The sources also describe efforts connected with language preservation, including a school conducted in the Cofán language and the development and adoption of a new Cofán writing system.

## Language & religion

Aʼingae, also called Cofán or Kofán, is the primary language of the Cofán people and is a language isolate with no known genetic relatives. It is spoken in Sucumbíos Province in northeastern Ecuador and in Putumayo and Nariño in southern Colombia. The sources give an estimate of approximately 1,500 speakers; the language remains relatively vital in Ecuador and is severely endangered in Colombia. Aʼingae has 27 consonants, 5 oral monophthongs, and 6 oral diphthongs, each with a nasal counterpart. Its syllable structure is described as (C)V(ʔ), and its basic constituent order as SOV or SO-predicate, although matrix-clause order is relatively flexible. The language has two principal Latin-alphabet orthographies: one developed by missionaries Marlytte and Roberta Borman and a newer writing system created and widely adopted by the Cofán community. The sources describe Cofán religious tradition as shamanistic and identify harmonious conviviality as a key cultural value. They also state that language and cultural practices such as drinking *yaje*, hunting, and housebuilding contribute to Cofán identity and social status.
