## Overview

The Tupinambá are one of the Tupi ethnic groups of present-day Brazil. Before and during their first contact with Portuguese settlers, they lived along the entire Eastern Atlantic coast of Brazil. In a more specific historical usage, the name refers to Tupinambá communities that inhabited the right shore of the São Francisco River in the Recôncavo Baiano, Bahia, and the coast from Cabo de São Tomé in Rio de Janeiro to São Sebastião in São Paulo. In the 21st century, Tupinambá communities live in Pará and in southern Bahia, around Olivença. The Tupinambá language survives today in the form of Nheengatu. Their cultural history is documented through agriculture, hunting, fishing, gathering, language, education, land recognition, and historical accounts produced during the colonial period.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources state that Tupinambá communities settled into communities that sustained populations of about 100 people.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Old Tupi language source gives linguistic examples involving a *stone bowl*, an *arrow*, a *sword*, and a *canoe*.

## Music & performance

The sources describe historical written works concerning the Tupinambá, including André Thevet’s 1572 *Cosmographie universelle*, Jean de Léry’s 1578 *Histoire d’un voyage faict en la terre du Brésil*, and Hans Staden’s *Warhaftige Historia und beschreibung eyner Landtschafft der Wilden Nacketen*. They also describe José de Anchieta’s poetry in Tupinambá and his work on Tupi grammar.

## Dance & theatre

The Old Tupi source mentions José de Anchieta’s *Teatro* as a source for a linguistic example.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources mention historical descriptions of Tupinambá cannibalism by Hans Staden and the influence of accounts by Thevet and Léry on Montaigne’s essay *Of Cannibals*.

## Foodways

The Tupinambá relied on agriculture for most of their resources and used slash-and-burn techniques. Both women and men worked in the fields, with women often tilling the soil before men carried out their duties. They also hunted, fished, and gathered resources, although the sources state that these activities were not as extensive as their agricultural labor. The Old Tupi language source gives examples involving *manioc beer*, *milk*, *shrimp*, *fish*, *armadillo*, *paca*, and *medicine*, but these examples are linguistic data and do not constitute a description of Tupinambá foodways.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources document a substantial written record about the Tupinambá rather than a specific Tupinambá oral-literary corpus. André Thevet described the Tupinambá in his 1572 *Cosmographie universelle*. Jean de Léry wrote *Histoire d’un voyage faict en la terre du Brésil* in 1578, and Hans Staden described Tupinambá cannibalism in *Warhaftige Historia und beschreibung eyner Landtschafft der Wilden Nacketen*. Thevet’s and Léry’s writings influenced Montaigne’s essay *Of Cannibals* and the creation of the “noble savage” myth during the Enlightenment. The Old Tupi source also records catechisms, poems, plays, grammars, dictionaries, and a phrasebook, including works by André de Thévet, José de Anchieta, Jean de Lery, and Luís Figueira.

## Language & religion

Old Tupi, also called Classical Tupi, was a classical Tupian language spoken by Indigenous Tupi peoples of Brazil. It belongs to the Tupi–Guarani language family and has a written history spanning the 16th, 17th, and early 18th centuries. Old Tupi was used as a lingua franca throughout Brazil by Europeans and Amerindians during the early colonial period, but it was later suppressed almost to extinction. Its sole living descendant is the Nheengatu language. The first written documents containing linguistic information were produced from 1575 onwards, when Jesuits André Thévet and José de Anchieta translated Catholic prayers and biblical stories into the language. The sources discuss Catholic missionary work and Christian texts.
