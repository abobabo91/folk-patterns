## Overview

The Akawaio are an Indigenous people of the Guiana highlands, living in Guyana, in Roraima in Brazil and in Venezuela. They are closely related to the Ingarikó and the Patamona, and like them call themselves and their language *Kapóng* — "Sky People" — a Cariban language whose Akawaio dialect has between 5,000 and 6,000 speakers. In Guyana their settlements cluster along the upper Mazaruni, Barama, upper Pomeroon, Demerara, Wenamu and upper Cuyuni rivers, with population centres at Kamarang, Jawalla, Waramadong and Kako; further south, villages recorded along the Ireng River and the Rio Cotingo south-east of Monte Roraima carry Akawaio and Patamona material in the same collections. The first colonial records of them date to the mid-seventeenth century, when they were known as prominent regional traders. They matter in folk-culture terms both for the beadwork and plaited fibre work documented in early twentieth-century collections and for *alleluia*, the Indigenous religion that fuses Christian and older Kapóng belief.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The pattern-bearing work documented for the Akawaio is of two kinds: glass-bead panels worn on the body, and plaited fibre with woven-in black designs. The catalogue records name the fibres but not a loom.

**Beaded women's skirt** — A rectangular bead panel worked in white, violet and blue glass beads arranged in patterns, its outer lower edge finished with tassels of white glass beads and *mambetuna*. A second collected example, taken unfinished, shows the working method: the skirt is beaded in a bow-shaped frame, in that case white beads carrying a red pattern.

**Beaded girl's skirt** — The same garment in a smaller form and an inverted palette: a ground of black glass beads carrying blue, red and white patterning.

**Man's beaded belt** — A *livrem* that is half plaited *kamoa* and half white glass beads strung on cotton thread, so that one technique continues into the other along a single band.

**Plaited *mana* work** — *Mana* is the fibre used for the patterned flatwork: a rectangular fan of 36 × 26 cm with a black design, a shoulder bag plaited of the same material with black patterning, and the food-serving fan below. The black figure is worked in during plaiting rather than applied.

**Cotton binding and tassels** — Hand-spun cotton is the finishing material throughout: hanging bands, edges and tassels on otter-skin and jaguar-skin bags, cotton string on war clubs, and the white cotton tufts and binding on arrows.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name only two recurring figures: black woven patterning on plaited *mana*, and incised crosses ornamenting a bone flute; bead designs are described by colour combination rather than by motif name.

### Clothing & dress

Dress as collected is built from bead panels, plaited bands and fibre footwear rather than cut cloth. Women wore the beaded skirt described above; girls a smaller version in a darker ground. Men wore the composite plaited-and-beaded belt, which was wound twice about the waist, with the skirt then laid up over it front and back — a fastening detail the collector thought worth recording. Footwear is a pair of sandals, *otatsa*, made from palm stem with cords of bast. For the head, a crown or head-dress of feathers, reed, cotton and cord is held in the British Museum. Ceremonial dress is represented by the dance girdle hung with fruit-shell rattles, worn so that movement sounds, and by the *kévei* rattle band of cotton with fruits attached, mounted on a staff rather than on the body. The sources do not describe everyday garments beyond these, and give no separate account of mourning or wedding dress.

### Architecture

The sources carry one direct record of Akawaio building: a 1912 photograph of a dwelling in the Akawaio village of Kwipa or Arawai on the Vemau River at the British Guiana border. No building type is named, no plan, materials or roof form described, and no ornament recorded, so the vernacular architecture of the group is not covered by the material used here. What the records do fix is the settlement geography — riverside villages named Waipa on the Chimepir, Marituaka, Kurikuparu, Sapritik and Sukoabi on the Ireng, Orinduik, and Palmak on the Rio Cotingo — and one twentieth-century episode of village founding: after half the Akawaio of the Middle Mazaruni moved upriver in the 1950s to avoid conflict with gold and diamond miners, they returned in the 1970s and founded Isseneru in response to a proposed dam and hydroelectric plant that would have flooded their land.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The household inventory is plaited, gourd and wood; the sources record no pottery and no metalworking, and the only iron in the collections is an imported arrow point. Calabash supplies the drinking and serving bowls, including one ornamented example and one holding *ulali*, the poison for blowpipe darts. A fruit-shell bowl called *komeli*, fitted with a cord, served as a storage box, and another bowl is plaited from two palm leaves. *Wo?woy*, a plaited *mana* serving fan 29 cm across and glossed in the catalogue as a "deep fan", carried food; *pare* is a paddle-shaped spoon. Hunting and fishing gear is the largest group: the bow *urába* of *pairá* wood, 109 cm long, strung with *itewá* of *kurauyák*; blowpipe quivers, one with two bundles of poisoned darts; wooden war clubs bound with cotton string; a man's fish-arrow of wood, cane and feathers; a game arrow with a long iron point; the scoop net *pómoe* on its hoop; and a model corrial, a dugout canoe, in wood.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment in these collections is beadwork and feather work rather than metal. Glass trade beads in white, violet, blue, red and black are the prestige material, worked into the skirts, tassels and the man's belt; the feather, reed, cotton and cord crown is the one head ornament recorded. Two skin bags — one of otter, one of jaguar — carry cotton edging and tassels and function as display pieces as much as containers, as does a bag made from an armadillo shell. One body practice is documented in detail: *kasiuok*, a calabash tube used to draw pepper water into the nose. The sources record no tattooing, no body paint and no hair practices for the Akawaio.

## Music & performance

Three instruments are documented, all collected in Ireng River villages in 1912. *Sarákoa* is a three-holed bone flute: one example is made from a jaguar bone, another from deer bone — *oaiking* — bound with cotton band and ornamented with incised crosses. *Paiaka létik* is a single-holed cow horn blown as a trumpet. The third is percussive and worn rather than held: *kévei*, a cotton band hung with *kévei* fruits and fastened to a staff, alongside a dance girdle strung with fruit-shell rattles. Beyond the instruments themselves the sources name no song genres, no singers and no performance occasions, so the repertoire, tuning and context of Akawaio music are not covered here. The one performance setting the sources do describe is religious rather than musical: the shaman's meeting with the god during hallucinogenic ritual, undertaken with tobacco and a prescribed diet.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is present in the collections only as equipment: the girdle of fruit-shell rattles and the staff-mounted *kévei* rattle, both catalogued explicitly as dance gear. No dance is named, no choreography or occasion described, and the sources record no theatrical or masking tradition for the Akawaio.

## Festivals & rituals

There are no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions for Guyana, and the sources name no annual festival calendar or life-cycle rites. What they document instead is religious practice. Akawaio belief is polytheistic, with Makunaima the most important god because, in their account, he created the people; Iwarrika is blamed for flooding the earth, and Kanaima and Sigu are further figures of the mythology. William Henry Brett, who proselytized among the Akawaio in the nineteenth century, reported Makonaima as their supreme deity. Much of the Kapóng language turns on sun worship, sun spirits and a higher being in the sky, a belief system traced back to the sixteenth century among Carib-speaking peoples and only examined from the nineteenth. The shaman is central to ritual, reaching the god through hallucinogens, tobacco and diet. The religion of the Akawaio today is *alleluia* or hallelujah, a combination of Christianity and older belief. It was created by Bichiwung, a Macushi who travelled the coast with a priest and claimed a revelation that missionaries were concealing God's true nature from Indigenous people. Bichiwung, who holds the role of Jesus in the religion, was killed for his teaching and resurrected by a white medicine given him by God; dismembered, he was restored when his wife gathered his parts and smeared them with the medicine; dismembered a second time, he could not be restored because some parts were missing.

## Foodways

Cassava is the staple the collections attest to. A fragment of a cassava-bread smoother, already described in 1927 as an obsolete type, was recovered from an old Akawaio camp on the Upper Demerara River — the single oldest datum on Akawaio cooking in these records. Prepared food was carried and served on plaited ware: the *wo?woy* fan, a bowl plaited from two palm leaves, a palm-leaf basket the catalogue glosses as a "pepper pot carrier", and the paddle-shaped spoon *pare*. Pepper water appears twice, once as the implied contents of that carrier and once in the *kasiuok* tube used to draw it into the nose. Protein came from the river and the forest: the scoop net *pómoe*, fish-arrows, game arrows and blowpipe darts poisoned with *ulali*. One insect food is named — *kokong*, gathered by torchlight and regarded as a great delicacy, with the torch itself collected as an object. The sources describe no ceremonial dishes, no fermented drink and no dietary prohibitions.

## Oral tradition & literature

The mythology is named but not narrated in the sources: Makunaima, Kanaima, Iwarrika and Sigu are described as important figures of Akawaio culture, and the founding of *alleluia* by Bichiwung is itself transmitted as a narrative of death, dismemberment and restoration by white medicine. Beyond this the sources record no epic, no folktale cycle, no proverbs or riddles and no storytelling occasion. The language evidence does show two stylistic resources of Kapóng that bear on verbal art: similes are formed freely by suffix, with *-kasa* 'like' and *-walai* 'similar to', so that ordinary words become comparisons. Documentation rather than literary revival is what the sources point to — Audrey Butt's 1960 study of *alleluia* in the *Journal of the Royal Anthropological Institute*, and the twentieth-century collecting expeditions whose catalogue cards preserve most of the vernacular vocabulary cited above.

## Language & religion

*Kapóng* is a Cariban language spoken in lowland tropical South America — in Guyana chiefly in the forests of the Upper Mazaruni basin, in Brazil in the Roraima Indigenous Terra Raposa, and in Venezuela in Bolívar and Monagas states. Its dialects are Akawaio, Ingarikó and Patamona; Macushi speakers call the language Ingarikó. Word order is preferentially subject–object–verb but flexible, there are no gender distinctions in pronouns or affixes, *yamok* pluralises a noun and *-da* marks possession. Vocabulary carries the sky-centred cosmology directly: *Kapóng* "Sky People", *kapo* "in the sky", *akwa* "God's place", *akwalo* "the spirit", *Iopotari akuru* "chief spirit", *taemogoli* "grandfather", *waica* "warrior". A 2013 Inter-American Development Bank survey found 20% fluency among Akawaio, the highest of any Indigenous group sampled in Guyana, while no Indigenous language was in use among the Guyanese Patamona; in Brazil there are about 10,000 speakers and transmission is considered sound.
