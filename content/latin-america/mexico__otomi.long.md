## Overview

The Otomi are an Indigenous people of central Mexico, spread across a deliberately discontinuous territory that runs from northern Guanajuato to eastern Michoacán and southeastern Tlaxcala, with the great concentrations in the Mezquital Valley of Hidalgo, the Eastern Highlands of Puebla and Veracruz, the semi-desert around the Peña de Bernal in Querétaro, and the north of the State of Mexico. Mexico's National Institute of Indigenous Peoples counted 667,038 Otomi in 2015, the fifth largest Indigenous people in the country, of whom a little over half speak the language — a cluster of Oto-Pamean varieties within the Oto-Manguean family. There is no single endonym: *ñätho* in the Toluca Valley, *hñähñu* in the Mezquital, *ñäñho* in Santiago Mexquititlán, *ñ'yühü* in the northern highlands of Puebla. In folk-culture terms the Otomi matter above all for two crafts documented in the objects shown here: backstrap-loom weaving and embroidery in *maguey* fibre and cotton, and the bark paper (*amate*) of San Pablito, Pahuatlán.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Quechquemitl** — A shoulder garment made of two woven rectangles sewn together to form a diamond. One example in the collection is gauze-woven in white cotton with pink silk ribbon along the seams; another joins a band of white cotton, a band of fuchsia wool with an orange stripe outlined in black, and a band of white cotton embroidered with eight-pointed stars and eight-petalled flowers in black with violet accents.

**Huipil** — A rectangle of commercial off-white cotton with a slit cut for the neck, hemmed at the short sides and covered in multicoloured wool embroidery; the neck opening is trimmed in green and burgundy wool and framed by a rectangle of geometric work.

**Blusa bordada** — The embroidered woman's blouse, cut from factory calico and sewn with navy thread, with the yoke and shoulders worked in running stitch in floral and geometric designs. A related white cotton dress is seamed in fuchsia, with roosters and small birds on the yoke and a line of birds across two patch pockets.

**Faja and belt** — Backstrap-loom waist sashes on a cream cotton ground, with a black central stripe of stylised birds, animals and geometric patterns, red stripes of repeating diamonds edged in dark red, and braided fringes at both ends; a plainer belt carries navy geometric supplementary weft-brocading on white cotton.

**Ayate** — The open-mesh carrying cloth woven on a backstrap loom from agave fibre (*ixtle*), two webs seamed along the long side into a square. One example is embroidered at the centre with the eagle and serpent of the Mexican national emblem, flanked by flowers in cross-stitch worked in hand-spun brown wool.

**Morral** — The shoulder bag, also of *ixtle*, a folded rectangle with the flap turned over the top and a brown-and-white wool cord sewn around the sides; the fronts carry two birds facing each other across a tree, or a rooster, with stars on the flap and crocheted wool buttons and loops for a closure.

**Manta embroidery and brocaded bedspreads** — Rectangles of off-white cotton cloth (*manta*) embroidered in satin stitch with multicoloured cotton thread; one shows birds, a rabbit, a deer and flowers. The same figurative repertoire appears weft-brocaded in bright acrylic yarn on a bedspread of four backstrap-loom panels, banded from top to bottom with animals, birds with outstretched wings, dogs, horses and finally men and women, between borders of red and blue diamonds.

**Motif vocabulary.** Birds and roosters; double-headed eagles; the eagle holding a serpent; donkeys, horses, dogs, deer and rabbits; two birds facing a tree; eight-pointed stars; eight-petalled flowers and vases of flowers; repeating diamonds; plants and fruit borders.

Tools accompany the cloth: a backstrap loom set up for double-weaving, with a section of red and black wool already woven in bird motifs, and spindles with clay whorls — one hooked for spinning agave fibre and still carrying a skein of undyed *ixtle*, one with a heavy whorl incised with diamonds and roughly spun brown wool. A 1967 photograph from San Pablito shows a woman weaving at a loom tensioned by a band around her waist.

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress in the documented examples combines a wrapped skirt, a sash and a shoulder garment. The skirts are backstrap-woven from two webs of brown wool with a blue stripe, sewn together and left with one raw and one selvedge edge; a separate woven skirt strip is dark red with a lighter red geometric pattern. Over the blouse — either the embroidered *blusa bordada* or a *huipil* — goes the *quechquemitl*, points to the sides. The one documented hat is plaited from palm fibre with a tall crown indented at the sides and top, a wide brim with white spiral stitching, a string left exposed at the back for a tassel and a black plastic band stapled on. Ceremonial dress is attested indirectly, through paper votive figures dressed in miniature versions of the same wardrobe: the female figure wears a white *blusa bordada* with green embroidery, a fuchsia silk skirt ribboned in yellow, and a lace *quechquemitl* standing in for the gauze-woven one; her male consort wears white gauze trousers, a cotton shirt, a red felt apron bordered in yellow silk ribbon and a gauze scarf embroidered in black and red at the points. The sources say nothing about footwear.

### Architecture

The sources are thin here. In the Mezquital Valley the *maguey* plant was depended on to the point that huts were built from its leaves, and dwellings were dispersed and built so low and so concealed that densely settled country could be mistaken for empty land. The village best documented in the collection is San Pablito, on the side of the Guajalote Mountain in the Sierra Norte de Puebla, in the Pahuatlán municipality of Puebla: photographs taken in 1935 record its houses and walls with women and children before them.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

One pottery vessel stands for Otomi ceramics: a lightly fired, fire-stained jug with a circular base tapering in sharply at the neck, a flared vertical lip and three looped handles, painted above the handles with geometric and floral designs in a pitch-like substance (*chapopote*) and blackened with the same pitch around the rim. Plaited work is represented by a diagonally plaited basket (*canasta*) bought at the market in Ixmiquilpan, Hidalgo. For paper there is a purpose-made tool: a rectangular stone with a ridge around its centre, used to beat bark into *amate*. Wikipedia records that the Otomi were blacksmiths who traded metal ornaments and weaponry with other Indigenous confederations, including the Aztec Triple Alliance, though metal weapons were less effective than obsidian; no metalwork appears among the objects shown.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The only adornment the sources describe is on a paper votive figure of the earth deity: pink glass earrings, and a black-and-white braided string hanging down the back.

## Festivals & rituals

Prehispanic Otomi religion was polytheist and closely related to that of the Nahua peoples, with Otomi counterparts for most of the major Nahua deities — Ehecatl-Quetzalcoatl as *Edähï*/*Ek'ënmaxi*, Tlaloc as *Hmü'ye*, Tlazolteotl as *Nopot'ejä*, Tlaltecuhtli as *Hmühai*, Mictecacihuatl as *Nohmüdü*. The most important were the fire-death god Otontecuhtli, also called Cuecuex and patronised by the Tepanec; the moon goddess *Zäna*, patronised at Xaltocan, Metztitlán and San Bartolo Tutotepec; and Yocippa. Two further deities are named: *Bizamofo*, "Harvest", and *Ojädäpo*, "Lord of the Mountain". The Otomi calendar closely resembled the Aztec one, most of the names being semantically equivalent, and many Otomi populations continue to practise shamanism and hold pre-Hispanic beliefs such as Nagualism. The ritual objects shown belong to this living practice. From San Pablito come cut-paper spirit figures with hands raised and plants growing from head, arms and legs, identified in the records as the spirits of particular crops — long beans (*frijol largo*), the chile plant — along with a batch of twelve female figures cut from dark bark paper, with eyes and mouth pierced, spikes on the head and a wide skirt. Paper dolls (*muñeca de papel*) in white tissue paper and a white paper napkin (*servilleta*) are catalogued as used for sorcery, and one silhouette was identified by its collector as *Señor de Noche*, companion of the "Señor de Montezuma". Dressed paper votive figures represent *La Madre Tierra*, Mother Earth, and her male consort.

## Foodways

Like most sedentary Mesoamerican peoples the Otomi subsisted on maize, beans and squash — the cognate vocabulary for maize, beans and chili across the Otomi languages is the evidence for how early that agriculture was adopted — but the decisive plant was the *maguey*, or century plant, grown both for fibre (*henequen*, and the *ixtle* of the bags and carrying cloths) and for alcohol. *Pulque*, the fermented unfiltered juice of the maguey, played a central part in both the Otomi economy and Otomi nutrition; the staple diet documented is tortillas, pulque and whatever fruit was at hand. A nutritional survey of Otomi villages in the Mezquital Valley in 1943–44 found that despite an arid climate and land unfit for agriculture without irrigation, the people depended chiefly on maguey production, and that the diet, unbalanced by Western standards, nevertheless kept them in reasonably good health. In the Mezquital, where the land was too dry for farming, many Otomi hired each other as labourers and relied heavily on pulque. The Spanish first banned the drink and then tried to make a business of producing it, with the result that the Otomi restricted it to their own consumption; large-scale production has since caused the practice to decline. The sources used describe no ceremonial dishes.

## Oral tradition & literature

The precolumbian Otomi had no fully developed writing system of their own, though Aztec ideographic writing could be read in Otomi as well as Nahuatl, and place names were often translated rather than borrowed: Nahuatl Tenochtitlān, "place of Opuntia cactus", was rendered *ʔmpôndo* in proto-Otomi with the same meaning. After the conquest friars alphabetised the language, and a substantial literature in Classical Otomi survives — secular and religious documents of which the best known are the Codices of Huichapan and Jilotepec, alongside Pedro de Cárceres's *Arte de la lengua othomí* (written perhaps as early as 1580, published 1907), Alonso de Urbano's trilingual Spanish–Nahuatl–Otomi dictionary of 1605, the eighteenth-century *Luces del Otomi* and Neve y Molina's dictionary and grammar. Otomi lost its status as a language of education after independence, ending that literary period. Today in Guanajuato and Hidalgo elders still tell tales to the young who understand the language, and Adela Calva Reyes is listed among Otomi writers; one living author is represented in the collection by three books made of *amate* paper and signed "Antonio López Maya". The sources name no epic cycle.

## Language & religion

Otomi belongs to the Otomian subgroup of the Oto-Pamean branch of Oto-Manguean, its closest relative being Mazahua, from which it split around the eighth century CE; diversification into the modern varieties began about 1000 AD. It is a complex of closely related languages rather than one language — INALI and Ethnologue recognise nine varieties, David Charles Wright Carr argues for four — grouped dialectologically into Northwestern (Mezquital, Querétaro, Guanajuato), Southwestern (the Toluca Valley and the western State of Mexico) and the more conservative Eastern dialects of the Puebla and Veracruz highlands, Tlaxcala and two Toluca Valley villages. All varieties are tonal, most with three tones. A negative stereotype of the Otomi promoted by the Nahuas and perpetuated by Spanish chroniclers who relied on Nahua informants drove language shift toward Spanish; in 2003 the Ley General de Derechos Lingüísticos recognised Otomi, with 61 other Indigenous languages, as a national language. Religiously, the Ixtenco Otomi converted largely to Roman Catholicism while holding to older custom, and shamanism and Nagualism persist.
