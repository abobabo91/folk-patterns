## Overview

The Kalinago are an Indigenous people of the Lesser Antilles in the Caribbean, historically also called Island Caribs or Caribs. They live especially in Dominica, where a population of around 3,400 survived in the Kalinago Territory in northeast Dominica as of 2008. The territory occupies 3,700 acres on the island’s east coast and was granted to the people by the British government in 1903. The Kalinago language was an Arawakan language historically spoken in the islands, although it became extinct by about 1920; an offshoot survives as Garifuna in Central America. Kalinago culture is represented in canoe manufacture, fishing, foodways, bush medicine, oral traditions, and continuing Indigenous political identity.

## Material culture

### Architecture

They do identify the Kalinago Territory in northeast Dominica as a place where Kalinago communities continue to live and the Touna Auté living village as associated with Irvince Auguiste.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Kalinago material culture includes canoes used for transport, fishing, and the development of the fishing industry. Canoes are constructed from Burseraceae, *Cedrela odorata*, *Ceiba pentandra*, and *Hymenaea courbaril* trees; their purposes vary according to height and bark thickness. The *Ceiba pentandra* is described as both functional and spiritual, because it is believed to house spirits that become angered if disturbed. Cayo pottery and Suazan Troumassoid pottery are identified in archaeological discussions of the historic Island Carib world. Cayo pottery has been found in the Lesser Antilles from Grenada to Basse-Terre and possibly Saint Kitts.

## Music & performance

They do mention a BBC documentation project, *The Quest of the Carib Canoe*, which recorded a 1997 canoe voyage undertaken by Dominica Carib artist Jacob Frederick and Tortola artist Aragorn Dick Read. The voyage travelled by canoe to the Orinoco delta to meet local Kalinago communities and re-establish cultural connections with communities along the island chain. This documentation concerns canoe building, travel, and cultural connection rather than a named musical tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do describe several historical and spiritual practices. The *Ceiba pentandra* tree is believed to house spirits that would become angered if disturbed. The Caribs are described as having kept the bones of ancestors in their houses, with missionaries reporting that ancestral spirits were believed to look after the bones and protect their descendants. The sources also describe reports of ritual eating of war enemies and the taking of human trophies, while noting that the accuracy of cannibalistic accounts remains debated and that one source states the Caribs did not practice cannibalism. The Caribs are believed to have practiced polytheism. During Spanish colonization, missionaries attempted to convert Indigenous people to Catholicism.

## Foodways

Kalinago cuisine is shaped by location and the seasonal availability of ingredients. The diet consists mainly of fish, supplemented by meat, greens, root vegetables, local herbs, and spices. In contemporary cuisine, fish is the preferred choice, followed by domestic and imported meats. Wildlife such as crabs, crayfish, manicou, and agouti is less common.

Named specialties include *bwigo*, *chaloup*, *viyo*, and *chatou water*, which are regarded as energy boosters. A traditional breakfast may include *vizou kann*, or cane juice, with cocoa and cinnamon; *lêt koko*, or cocoa nut milk; roast plantains; or breadfruit served with roast meat or *pweson boukanne*, accompanied by hot pepper and *shieve*. Lunch may consist of *sòs koko*, or cocoa nut cream, served with *pweson boukanné*, local herbs and spices, roucou, *ton ton*, and hot pepper. The one-pot meal contains root vegetables such as dasheen, yams, tannia, and green bananas, together with fresh fish or smoked meat cooked in coconut cream. Herbal teas remain common, although canned chocolate and milk are increasingly replacing them.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe oral testimonies and contemporary traditions concerning Kalinago origins, identity, and relations with the Taínos and mainland Caribs. The traditional account says that Caribs were a warlike people who moved through the Lesser Antilles and displaced earlier inhabitants called the Igneri. Other historical interpretations propose that the Kalinago developed from Indigenous peoples of the Antilles or that different groups became integrated into a political alliance or fusion.

Spanish accounts reported that Taínos described the Caribs as fierce warriors and cannibals who raided Taíno communities. These accounts are presented as contested representations rather than established facts. Raymond Breton recorded ethnographic and linguistic notes during his residence in Guadeloupe and Dominica from 1635 until 1653. The 1997 canoe voyage documented by the BBC is described as a contemporary effort to re-establish cultural connections among Kalinago communities.

## Language & religion

Kalinago, also known as Island Carib and Igneri, was an Arawakan language historically spoken by the Kalinago of the Lesser Antilles. It was not closely related to the Carib language of the mainland Caribs. Linguistic studies determined that it was related to Lokono on the South American mainland and more distantly to Taíno. During French colonization in the 17th century, and possibly earlier, Kalinago men also used a Cariban-derived pidgin. Kalinago proper became extinct by about 1920, while Garifuna survives in Central America.

The sources describe several explanations for the language’s Arawakan character, including the adoption of an earlier island language by incoming Caribs and the possibility that Igneri communities adopted a Carib identity through economic and political relations. Douglas Taylor reported that, when he visited Dominica in 1930, informants identified Ma Gustave as the last speaker, a woman who had died roughly twelve years earlier. The sources describe Carib religion as polytheistic and connect spiritual belief to ancestral bones and the *Ceiba pentandra* tree.
