## Overview

The Mehinako, also known as Mehináko, Mehinacu, and by several other names, are an Indigenous people of Brazil. They live in the Indigenous Park of the Xingu, around the headwaters of the Xingu River in Mato Grosso, in an area around the Tuatuari and Kurisevo Rivers. Their population was 254 in 2011, compared with 200 in 2002. The Mehinaku speak Mehináku, an Arawakan language, as well as some Portuguese. Their culture, as described in the sources, is especially documented through settlement history, communal domestic life, subsistence practices, and language.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Mehinaku family houses accommodate ten or twelve people and have no internal walls. The houses are situated around an open area that remains in constant view, reflecting the transparency of Mehinaku social arrangements.

Historical settlement is documented through named places. The oldest known village established by the Mehinaku was called *Yulutakitsi* and was set up sometime around or before 1850, although its exact former location is unknown because the community no longer exists. Historical villages were located along the Tuatuari River, north of the main Aweti village. After migration, the Yawalapiti gave the Mehinaku one of their houses at *Jalapapuh*, while Aweti agreed to divide territory along a trail between their village and the new Mehinaku center.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record a tribal taboo associated with living in places where many people had died.

The sources also describe major events in Mehinaku history. In the 1950s, the Ikpeng invaded Mehinaku territory and drove the Mehinaku from it. The Yawalapiti were forced to migrate as well, producing a political shift in the upper Xingu region. An outbreak of flu and measles killed around 15 people in the 1960s, after which the Mehinaku relocated to a nearby area. They moved again in 1981, without going far from their original community.

## Foodways

The Mehinaku hunt, fish, and farm to provide for themselves. Their primary crops are manioc and maize.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that the Mehinaku do not keep detailed, chronological historical records extending back more than a few generations. Their accounts of historical villages, migration, territorial change, and past events therefore form the only described source of historical tradition in the material provided.

## Language & religion

Mehináku, also called Meinaku, is an Arawakan language spoken by the Mehinaku people in the Xingu Indigenous Park of Brazil. The language has five vowels, which can be oral or nasal, and thirteen consonants. One dialect, *Waurá-kumá*, is somewhat intelligible with Waurá because of influence from that language. The Mehinaku also speak some Portuguese.
