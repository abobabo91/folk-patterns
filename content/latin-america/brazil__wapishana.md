## Overview

The Wapishana are an Indigenous people concentrated in the Serra da Lua region between the Branco River and the Tacutu in Brazil, and in the southern Rupununi savannas in Guyana. They speak Arawakan, the only surviving Arawakan language of the circum-Roraima area. Their folk culture centers on cassava processing, handspun cotton textiles, and men's basketry that serves food preparation. Religious life today blends Catholicism with traditional beliefs and folk medicine.

## Material culture

Wapishana material culture divides between women's handspun cotton weaving and men's basketry and wooden work, all centered on cassava processing and household life.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Women weave handspun cotton into hammocks, baby slings and textiles, while men make the basketry that dominates the collection.

- **Double-woven hammock** (*shrimék*) — A hammock woven of spindle-spun cotton with cord work at each side for suspension.
- **Manioc press** (*nirsh*) — A long plaited cylinder fitted with heavy loops, hung from the roof to squeeze poisonous juice from grated cassava.
- **Sifter trays** — Woven on a square wooden frame in black and white fibre with red feathers at the corners.
- **Child's backpack basket** — A carrying basket woven onto a wooden and cane framework with red and blue feathers and a plaited cover.

### Clothing & dress

Sources describe only one garment: a woman's beaded apron.

- **Beaded woman's apron** — A woman's apron worked entirely in beads, collected in British Guiana.

### Architecture

Villages have been permanent since the twentieth century, usually arranged around a church with schools, meeting houses and shops.

- **Village church** — The center of village life where people gather to discuss local affairs and where a Wapishana catechist leads Sunday service.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Women make clay cooking pots while men make wooden stools, basketry sieves, squeezers and presses for food preparation.

- **Coil-built clay storage jar** — Built up from clay coils in yellow-grey mica-filled clay, conical with a narrow mouth, used to hold cassava beer.
- **Wooden stools** — Made by men for household use.
- **Iron griddles** — Used to toast cassava flour and thick flat breads.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Feather decoration appears mainly on objects rather than persons.

- **Child's bead necklace** — A necklace with a tapir hoof and toucan beaks hung from it.

## Festivals & rituals

Healing and death are the main ritual occasions, shaped by Catholicism, northeastern Brazilian folk medicine and traditional beliefs.

- **Kanaima** — Healers who could turn their powers to harm, acting out of envy; the concept is tied to outsiders and enemies.
- **Traditional curing** — Certain men once specialized in curing by beating leaves and blowing cures, techniques now blended with Catholic and folk medicine.
- **Magical plants** (*wapananinau*) — A class of plants with magical properties important for shamans of traditional beliefs.

## Foodways

Cassava is at the centre of Wapishana life, processed by women into flour and thick flat breads.

- **Cassava flour and breads** (*beiju*) — Women grate, express and sieve cassava, then toast it on iron griddles into flour and thick flat breads.
- **Cassava beer** — Made from cassava and stored in coil-built clay jars.
- **Game hunted** — Men hunt deer, agouti, wild turkeys and birds; the whole family fishes.

## Oral tradition & literature

The language carries taxonomies of plants and animals endemic to the region, with three classes encoding cultivation criteria.

- **Plants gathered wild** (*karam'makau*) — A plant class in Wapishana language.
- **Domesticated plants** (*wapaurib bau*) — A plant class often named from the place or farmer they came from.
- **Wapishana Dictionary** — Published in the Intercontinental Dictionary Series.

## Language & religion

Wapishana is an Arawakan language spoken by over 13,000 people on both sides of the border, the only remaining Arawakan language of the circum-Roraima area.

- **Arawakan family** — Wapishana belongs to this language family and has borrowed heavily from the Cariban language Pemon through intensive contact.

## Glossary

- *shrimék* — double-woven hammock
- *nirsh* — manioc press for squeezing cassava juice
- *beiju* — thick flat cassava breads
- *kanaima* — healers who turned powers to harm
- *wapananinau* — plants with magical properties
- *karam'makau* — plants gathered wild
- *wapaurib bau* — domesticated plants
- *ite* — palm leaf used for trays
- *Serra da Lua* — Moon Ridge region in Roraima
- *Rupununi* — southern savannas in Guyana
- *Arawakan* — language family spoken by Wapishana
- *catechist* — Wapishana man leading Sunday service
- *Cariban* — language family with contact-based borrowing
- *Pemon* — Cariban language with heavy borrowing with Wapishana
- *Branco River* — river in Brazil near Wapishana lands
- *Tacutu* — affluent of Branco River
