## Overview

The Pehuenche, also called Pewenche, are an Indigenous people of South America living in the Andes, primarily in present-day south central Chile and adjacent Argentina. In the 16th century, their territory extended from approximately 34 degrees to 40 degrees south. Their name derives from dependence on the seeds of the *Araucaria araucana*, or monkey-puzzle tree, called *pewen* in Mapudungun. They later became Araucanized and partially merged with Mapuche peoples, while retaining some ancestral lands in the 21st century. Their present language is Mapundungun; their earlier distinct language may have been Huarpean.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

A Spanish writer described Pehuenche clothing in 1558 as blankets made of animal skins. The writer also described their houses as four poles covered with skins and said that the people moved from place to place without permanent habitation. Juan Ignacio Molina later described Pehuenche dress as distinct from that of other Mapuche: men wore skirts rather than trousers, as well as earrings and mantillas.

### Architecture

The 1558 description records houses made from four poles covered with skins. It also states that the Pehuenche moved from place to place and had no permanent habitation.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The 1558 account mentions bows and arrows used for hunting, while the historical and cultural descriptions also refer to horses used for meat and transport.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Molina’s 1787 account states that Pehuenche men wore earrings and mantillas.

## Festivals & rituals

They state only that Juan Ignacio Molina considered Pehuenche religion similar to that of other Mapuche.

## Foodways

The Pehuenche name derives from dependence on the seeds of the *Araucaria araucana*, locally called *Pehuen*. A Spanish writer in 1558 stated that they did not sow and sustained themselves by hunting in the valleys they occupied. The account names guanacos, jaguars, cougars, foxes, small deer, mountain cats, and birds among the available animals, and says that bows and arrows were used for hunting.

After feral horses of Spanish origin reached the eastern foothills of the Andes, the Pehuenche adopted horse meat. They first hunted horses as game and later raised them for meat and transport. Horse meat was preserved by sun-drying it to make *charqui*, meaning “jerky.”

## Language & religion

The Pehuenche speak Mapundungun today. In the past, they spoke their own distinct language, which may have been Huarpean. The related Wikipedia source describes Mapudungun as spoken in south-central Chile and west-central Argentina and states that Mapuche is classified either as a language isolate or, more conservatively, as an unclassified language. Other authorities classify Mapuche as one of the two languages forming the Araucana family with Huilliche.

Mapudungun was historically known as Araucanian, a name given by the Spanish. Its speakers call it *mapudungun* or *mapundungun*, “speech of the land,” and use other names including *mapuchedungun*, *mapunchedungun*, and *chedungun*. The language is now written with the Latin script, although there is no consensus over orthography. The sources state that Pehuenche religion was similar to that of other Mapuche.
