## Overview

The Machiguenga, also known as Matsigenka and Matsigenga, are an Indigenous people living in the high jungle, or montaña, on the eastern slopes of the Andes and in Amazon Basin jungle regions of southeastern Peru. In 2020 their population amounted to about 18,000. Formerly hunter-gatherers, the majority are now sedentary swidden cultivators. Manioc, maize, and bananas are the main crops, while coffee and cacao have become increasingly important commercial crops. Fish has become more important as peccary and monkeys have become scarcer. The Matsigenka language belongs to the Campa group of the Maipurean, or Arawakan, language family. Their folk-culture profile in the sources centers on swidden cultivation, kin-based social organization, cotton clothing, palm-built houses, animist beliefs, healing, and oral language traditions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Cotton tunic* — The Matsigenka used to wear a handwoven, homemade cotton tunic made by women.

### Clothing & dress

The Matsigenka used to wear a handwoven and homemade cotton tunic made by women. In local Spanish, this garment is called a *cushmas*. The men’s version was designed with a V neck, while the women’s version had a straight neck.

### Architecture

Matsigenka huts are made with palm tree poles used as a frame and palm leaves thatched for the roof. They state that, during the early period of a marriage, a couple prepares its own garden and builds its own house not far from the woman’s parents.

## Music & performance

 They provide a Matsigenka version of the Lord’s Prayer as a sample text, beginning “Apa Tasorintsi timatsirira enoku,”.

## Festivals & rituals

 The sources state that most Matsigenka are now Christian, mainly Catholic, while commonly continuing to follow animist beliefs. Spirits and demons are described as influencing everyday life, whereas creator gods have withdrawn and are indifferent to humans. Shamans formerly played a prominent role in local society; they are less visible today, and certain functions have been taken over by healers.

Marriage and family organization are described in social rather than ceremonial terms. Formerly, women married around the age of 16; today they commonly enter family relations some years later. During the first year or years of marriage, the relationship is often unstable and separation is common. Matsigenka are uxorilocal: the man moves to his wife, who usually still lives with her parents. During the early period of the relationship, the couple prepares a garden and builds a house near the woman’s parents. Formerly, prominent men had multiple wives.

## Foodways

Formerly hunter-gatherers, the Matsigenka are now predominantly sedentary swidden cultivators. Their main crops are manioc, maize, and bananas. Coffee and cacao are increasingly important commercial crops. The sources emphasize a preference for self-sufficiency in cultivating essential crops, supported by generous land allocation per capita and the lack of conflict in their area.

Peccary and monkeys were formerly the main sources of protein, but fish has become more important as game animals have become increasingly scarce. The sources connect this scarcity with encroachment by highland immigrants and the exploitation of the Camisea gas finds.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They state that Matsigenka cosmology is recounted in *The Storyteller*, a 1987 novel by Mario Vargas Llosa, but do not summarize the cosmology or identify the oral materials on which the novel draws.

The Matsigenka language article includes a sample text consisting of the Lord’s Prayer in Matsigenka. They mention Matsigenka texts collected by Lev Michael and Christine Beier at the Archive of the Indigenous Languages of Latin America, but provide no further description of the collection.

## Language & religion

Matsigenka belongs to the Campa group of the Maipurean, or Arawakan, language family. One source states that it is spoken by approximately 12,000 people in Peru, while the separate language article gives around 6,200 speakers in the Urubamba River Basin and along the Manu River in the Cusco and Madre de Dios departments of Peru. The language has dialects associated with the Upper Urubamba, Lower Urubamba, and Manu area. Matsigenka proper refers to the Manu variety; Nanti is considered by some a distinct language, while Matsigenka speakers see it as a variety of their language. The language article also describes Nanti as partially mutually intelligible but ethnically distinct.

Matsigenka is described as close enough to Nomatsiguenga that the two are sometimes considered dialects of a single language. Spanish and Quechua exert pressure in the Urubamba region, while Matsigenka is active and healthy in the Manu region, where most speakers are monolingual. The language is considered polysynthetic and has agglutinative morphology, using suffixes and prefixes. Most Matsigenka are Christian, mainly Catholic, but animist beliefs remain common.
