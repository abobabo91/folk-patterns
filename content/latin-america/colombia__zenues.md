## Overview

The Zenú, also called Sinú or Cenú, are an Indigenous people and pre-Columbian culture of Colombia. Their ancestral territory comprises the valleys of the Sinú and San Jorge rivers, together with the Caribbean coast around the Gulf of Morrosquillo, in the departments of Córdoba and Sucre. The 2018 Colombian Census recorded 307,091 Zenú people in Colombia. Their civilization reached its peak from about 200 BCE to about 1600 CE. Communities lived in the valleys of the Sinú, San Jorge, Cauca and lower Nechí rivers, where they practiced farming, fishing, hunting and trade. They constructed extensive systems of canals and artificial terraces, produced gold ornaments and developed related artistic and religious traditions. The Zenú language disappeared around 200 years ago.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Pre-Columbian textiles and wickerwork are almost completely lost, although needles and spindles made of bone, shells and ceramics survived. Woven fabrics are known through representations on gold and ceramic objects.

Women were portrayed wearing long woven skirts with a variety of patterns. The designs of textiles and clay baskets were related across the communities of the river valleys and formed part of what is called the Zenú tradition.

**Motif vocabulary.** They describe a broader web or net symbolism reflected in fishing nets, textiles, pottery and goldwork.

### Clothing & dress

They do record long woven skirts worn by female figures represented in pre-Columbian objects. Ceremonial dress is documented chiefly through gold ornaments: important women and chiefs wore golden breastplates during ceremonies. These breastplates symbolized the pregnancy of women and the virility of men. The sources also describe gold pendants and ornaments placed on the head of a staff.

### Architecture

Zenú settlement and environmental engineering were closely connected to the river valleys. From 200 BCE onward, communities constructed channels to control flooding and make large areas suitable for habitation and agriculture. The channels could be as long as four kilometers and were connected to natural waterways. Smaller irrigation ditches ran perpendicular to them, while excavated soil was used to form long artificial terraces two to four meters high. Houses were built on these terraces. Burial mounds were constructed over graves, and a tree was planted on top, with golden bells hung from its branches.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Zenú material culture includes clay female figurines, pottery, clay baskets and tools used in textile production. Female figurines were placed in graves and symbolized human and agricultural fertility, conception and rebirth in the underworld. Needles and spindles made of bone, shells and ceramics survived even though most textiles and wickerwork did not.

Goldwork was made by casting, including semi-filigree produced through the lost-wax method, and by hammering gold into plates and reliefs. Gold ornaments were commonly made from an alloy with a high degree of gold. In the mountains of San Jacinto, goldsmiths used alloys containing a relatively large amount of copper and applied a chemical heating process that left the surface golden.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Zenú goldwork included fine cast circular and semi-circular filigree earrings, nose rings with horizontal extensions, circular or n-shaped nose rings, pendants, bells, staff heads and ornaments for the head of a staff. Gold objects also represented people, animals and amphibious beings. Waterfowl, alligators, fish, cats and deer appeared as both sources of food and elements of cultural symbolism.

The sources connect jewelry with social, ceremonial and religious meanings. Golden breastplates worn by important women and chiefs represented pregnancy and male virility. Golden bells were hung from the tree planted above a grave.

## Music & performance

They state that the funeral ceremony was attended by the whole community with music and dance. During this ceremony, a mound was built over the grave, a tree was planted on it and golden bells were hung in the branches.

Objects from the mountains of San Jacinto portray musicians with flutes and maracas. These figures appear among representations of people holding gourds, people sitting on chairs with high backs and people standing.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention dance in connection with the funeral ceremony, which was attended by the whole community with music and dance.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document funerary practices and beliefs concerning fertility, death and rebirth.

Female clay figurines were frequently placed in graves as symbols of human and agricultural fertility. Their presence represented conception and rebirth in the underworld, compared with the way seeds germinate and grow. Funeral ceremonies involved the whole community, with music and dance. A mound was built over the grave, a tree was planted on top and golden bells were hung from its branches.

Women were regarded in Zenú culture as symbols of fertility, wisdom and respect and held substantial social and political significance. When the culture was encountered by the Spaniards in the 16th century, the religious center of Finzenú at the Sinú River was led by Toto, a female chief who governed several nearby villages.

The sources also describe a political and religious organization involving three chiefdoms. Finzenú, in the Sinú valley, was governed by Toto; Panzenú, in the San Jorge basin, was governed by Yapel; and Zenúfana, between the Cauca and Nechí rivers, was governed by Nutibara. According to the Zenú, Chief Zenúfana had organized the territory of Great Zenú and assigned political, economic and religious duties to the chiefs of Finzenú and Panzenú.

## Foodways

They state that pre-Columbian communities supported themselves through hunting, farming, fishing and trading in raw materials and finished products. Water management enabled agriculture on the floodplain: during high water, channels carried water toward cultivated areas, and when the water withdrew, nutrient-rich sludge enriched the land.

Animals represented in goldwork included waterfowl, alligators, fish, cats and deer. The sources describe these animals as sources of food as well as elements of Zenú culture.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record a traditional account of Chief Zenúfana as a mythical figure who governed the lower Cauca and Nechí area. During the Spanish conquest, he was regarded as the foremost of the ancient chiefs because he had organized the whole territory of Great Zenú and assigned political, economic and religious duties to the chiefs of Finzenú and Panzenú. Laws and regulations attributed to him were still said to be in force when Pedro de Heredia invaded the country.

## Language & religion

The Zenú language disappeared around 200 years ago.

Pre-Columbian Zenú religious concepts connected women, fertility, death and rebirth. Female figures were placed in graves, funeral mounds were built over the dead and trees with golden bells were planted above them. Finzenú was described as a religious center, and Zenú near the marsh Betanci was an important holy place and cemetery where dignitaries were buried.
