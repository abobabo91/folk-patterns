## Overview

The Zapotec are an Indigenous people concentrated in Oaxaca, Mexico, with a population of 400,000 to 650,000 speakers across multiple regional varieties. Their pre-Columbian civilization centred on Monte Albán produced one of Mesoamerica's first writing systems. The Zapotec languages form a main branch of the Oto-Manguean family, with regional divisions including the istmeños, serranos, southern Zapotec, and Central Valley Zapotec. Today they are largely Catholic, though older religious practices remain visible.

## Material culture

Zapotec material culture centres on textiles made on the backstrap loom, burnished black pottery, and carved wooden objects, with Spanish colonial influences absorbed into Indigenous forms.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Backstrap-loom weaving produces patterned cotton and wool textiles in distinctive geometric and figural motifs.

- **Huipil** — A women's upper garment built from a rectangle with a cut neck opening, made of blue cotton with white flowers or organza with green brocade and imported lace and ribbon.
- **Falda** — A wrap skirt of two flared panels woven of red cotton with weft-stripe patterning, embroidered with geometric motifs in multiple colours.
- **Rebozo** — A wool shawl woven on the backstrap loom with warp-patterned multicoloured stripes and finger-knotted warp fringe.
- **Morral** — A knitted wool shoulder bag in dark brown worked with mirror-image bird-like figures in white and rows of letters along the edges.
- **Sashes and girdles** — Narrow bands woven on the backstrap loom with warp-patterned cotton or silk, some carrying geometric motifs identified as scorpions or dancers from the Danza de la Pluma.

### Clothing & dress

Women combine the huipil or embroidered blusa with a wrapped falda, bound by a woven girdle or sash, and dress the head with a rodete and braided wool ornaments.

- **Rodete** — A turban of black wool hair cords joined at one end and left as a long fringe at the other.
- **Blusa** — A white plain-weave blouse with short gathered sleeves, satin-stitch embroidered in multiple colours with flower motifs and finished with a red yarn tassel.
- **Juchiteca skirt and huipil** (*Juchiteca*) — On the Isthmus, the recognised native outfit worn by women and by muxes, the third-gender members of Isthmus Zapotec communities.

### Architecture

The Zapotecs were a sedentary people building stone and mortar houses, with monumental architecture centred on Monte Albán, the largest city in the southern Mexican highlands until about AD 700.

- **Monte Albán** — Founded on a mountain overlooking the three Central Valleys, with monumental buildings, ball courts, tombs and the arrowhead-shaped Building J, the Conquest Slab, faced with carved stones bearing hieroglyphic place-glyphs and ruler heads.
- **Yo hopèe** — The house of the vital force where priests performed rites.
- **Mitla** — Seat of the paramount priest, known for its ruins.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Burnished black pottery is the signature ware, with vessels often pierced or etched with floral and figural designs, alongside modelled figurines, whistles and household equipment.

- **Burnished black pottery** — The signature ware including pots with cut-out flowers and leaves, modelled figurines and toys in the form of parrot, bird, lizard, lion, fish and turtle, and whistles shaped as peacock, rooster and duck.
- **Olla** — A pot with orange slip, black firing marks across the lower half and a burnished sheen.
- **Cesto de palma** — Palm baskets plaited in two-over-two twill over a square foundation with a round bottom and a rolled rim.
- **Cerbatana** — A blowgun used for hunting wild game, still made in Oaxaca de Juárez in the 1940s.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ancient Zapotec artisans worked gold and jade for elite jewelry; present-day adornment uses ceramic birds and fish alternating on necklaces and fibre hair ornaments.

- **Jade earspools and beads** (*Jade*) — Worked by elite artists and valued for its scarcity, included in dedication caches at temples.
- **Black pottery necklace** — Strung from seven burnished black pottery birds alternating with black pottery fish.

## Music & performance

Zapotec music appears primarily through performance context, with musicians playing in white clothing and sombreros alongside elaborate feathered-headdress dancers.

- **Whistling** — Used by Zapotec people to communicate in a way their colonizers would not detect, sitting alongside the tonal structure of the languages.
- **Toy flute** — A burnished black clay flute with four finger holes and a snake carved between them.
- **Pottery whistles** — Shaped as peacock, rooster and duck with the mouthpiece in the tail.

## Dance & theatre

The Danza de la Pluma is the one named dance, performed by dancers in elaborate painted-feather headdresses, and serves a social function in the separation of the sexes.

- **Danza de la Pluma** — A dance important enough to become a textile motif, performed by dancers in elaborate headdresses of painted feathers facing musicians and spectators in an open space.
- **Velas** — The reference point for public assembly on the Isthmus, where men and women work separately and remain apart during ritual occasions except when dancing.

## Festivals & rituals

Major religious observances include the pilgrimage to the Virgen de Juquila and the Day of the Dead, with pre-Hispanic ritual including dedication of buildings by cache and evidence of human sacrifice.

- **Virgen de Juquila** — A Marian devotion founded at Santa Catarina Juquila in Oaxaca, drawing many Zapotec Catholics on an annual pilgrimage during festivities running from 7 to 9 December.
- **Day of the Dead** — A festival for which carved wooden horses and riders were made for sale, and skull-headed female figurines were carved.
- **Copa pitào** — The priests, drawn mostly from the nobility, with commoners admitted only to the lower ranks, required to train in religious practice before taking power.
- **Cuilapan Temple Pyramid cache** (*Cuilapan*) — A dedication cache dated to AD 700 holding jade beads, jade earspools, obsidian blades, shells, stones, a pearl and small bird bones, dedicating the structure to power, sacrifice and the link between underworld and cosmos.

## Foodways

Maize is central to Zapotec agriculture and religion, with the Central Valleys considered one of the cradles of maize, and irrigation extending cultivation beyond the rivers.

- **Pitao Cozobi** — The primary god of the Valley Zapotecs, god of maize and agriculture.
- **Coqui Lao** — God of turkey hens.

## Oral tradition & literature

Origin legends describe the Zapotec as the original people of the valley of Oaxaca, born from rocks or descended from big cats, with a logosyllabic script attested from about 500 BC.

- **Danzante stone** — Monument 3 from San José Mogote bearing a logosyllabic script, one of Mesoamerica's first, attested from about 500 BC.
- **Be'ena' Za'a** — The Cloud People, the self-designation based on the belief that the governing elite descended from supernatural beings living among the clouds.
- **Bën za** — The Valley Zapotec term meaning People of the clouds.

## Language & religion

Zapotec comprises around fifty to sixty mutually unintelligible varieties with time depth comparable to the Romance languages, all tonal and contrasting phonation types in vowels.

- **Diidxazá** — The name for the language in Juchitán, with the first element meaning word.
- **Didxsaj** — The name for the language in Mitla, with the first element meaning word.
- **Cocijo** — The rain god, whom San Pedro resembles in Catholic practice.
- **Pè** — The life force residing in wind and breath in the pre-conquest polytheistic religion.
- **Ouija-tào** — The great seer, who topped the hierarchy of deities and resided at Mitla.
