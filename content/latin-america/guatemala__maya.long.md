## Overview

The Maya are an ethnolinguistic group of Indigenous peoples in Mesoamerica, descended generally from populations associated with the ancient Maya civilization. In Guatemala, Indigenous people of Maya descent comprise around 42% of the population, with the largest populations in the western highlands, including Baja Verapaz, Quiché, Totonicapán, Huehuetenango, Quetzaltenango, and San Marcos. Maya communities also live in the lowland areas of Alta Verapaz, Petén, and western Belize. The Mayan language family consists of thirty languages, grouped into major branches including Yucatecan, Huastecan, Chʼolan–Tzeltalan, Qʼanjobʼalan, Mamean, and Kʼichean. Maya cultural life is expressed through multilingual communities, textiles, weaving, ritual calendars, music, dance, foodways, architecture, writing, and continuing relationships between contemporary practice and the ancient Maya civilization.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Huipil** — The Maya people are known for brightly colored, yarn-based textiles woven into shirts, blouses, huipiles, capes, and dresses. Each village has its own distinctive pattern, making it possible to distinguish a person’s home town. A museum record describes a red *huipil* of unknown local origin from Guatemala.

**Faja** — A *faja* is a woven belt used to fasten and hold up a wraparound skirt or trousers. Museum examples are woven on a hip loom from wool or cotton, sometimes with acrylic fabric, and decorated with brocade, geometric motifs, zigzag lines, or a snake motif. Belts are worn by both women and men, although some examples are identified as women’s daily wear.

**Tzute** — A *tzute* or *sut* is a square or rectangular woven cloth used for many purposes. Museum records describe it as a covering for the head, shoulders, and arms, protection from the sun, a carrier for children or groceries, a holder for sacred objects and images of saints, and a cloth for storing bread or setting tables during ceremonial occasions.

**Corte** — A *corte* or *falda* is a skirt in the female clothing repertoire. The sources distinguish a wraparound skirt of ancient origin from a folded skirt of Spanish origin. Skirts vary in pattern and color and are generally woven on a step loom.

**Cinta** — A *cinta* is a woven hairband used by women to decorate the hair and head. A museum example from Colotenango is made from wool, consists of one woven piece of textile, and is entirely red.

**Montera** — A *montera* is a children’s hat. One museum example is woven on a hip loom from cotton and acrylic fabric and decorated with brocades and multicolored animal motifs. It is used by children up to 2 years old for everyday protection from cold and bright sun.

**Morral** — A *morral* or *bolsa* is a shoulder bag. Museum examples are made from wool or maguey fibres and are intended for everyday use; one wool bag bears a horse, zigzag lines, square blocks, and two suns, while another carries a quetzal image.

**Motif vocabulary.** Geometric motifs, zigzag lines, square blocks, animal motifs, snake motif, horse, quetzal, birds, double-headed eagles, two suns, maize.

### Clothing & dress

Women’s clothing consists of a shirt and a long skirt. The documented female clothing repertoire includes a multicolored top called a *huipil*, a patterned or plain skirt called a *falda* or *corte*, an embroidered or woven belt called a *faja*, head and hair adornment called a *cinta*, and a cloth for various purposes called a *tzute* or *servilleta*. Museum records describe women’s shirts and blouses made from woven cotton and decorated with brocade. One red women’s shirt has long sleeves and is categorized as a blouse or shirt rather than a huipil.

Men’s dress includes woven shirts and shoulder bags. A museum record describes a striped shirt woven from cotton on a hip loom, with cotton brocade on the sleeves and collar. The *morral* is described as a square wool shoulder bag worn and knitted by men. A woven belt can be used by both men and women. A cloth may also be used as a head ornament. Footwear is not covered by the sources used here.

Ceremonial clothing includes the feathered festival hat used during *El Baile de la Conquista*. A museum record states that this dance is performed in different parts of Guatemala and represents the invasion led by Don Pedro de Alvarado y Contreras and his confrontation with the Kʼicheʼ Maya ruler Tecún Umán.

### Architecture

Ancient Maya towns and cities, especially ceremonial centers, were organized around immense horizontal stucco floors and plazas often located at different levels. Broad, frequently steep stairs connected these spaces, which were surmounted by temple pyramids. Main buildings were enlarged under successive reigns through additional layers of fill and stucco coating. Irrigation channels, reservoirs, and drains formed part of the hydraulic infrastructure.

Named building types include ceremonial platforms, courtyards and palaces, residential buildings, temples and temple pyramids, ball courts, and sweat baths. Outside the ceremonial center were structures of lesser nobles, smaller temples, individual shrines, and the wards of commoners. Dam-like causeways called *sacbeob* connected ceremonial centers with other habitation nuclei.

Architectural ensembles included triadic pyramids, E-groups, and twin pyramid complexes. Corbelled vaults were common in palaces and temple rooms. In the Guatemalan Highlands, Postclassic hilltop sites such as the Kʼicheʼ capital Qʼumarkaj show strong Toltec influences.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Maya material culture includes ceramics, carved stone, stucco, wood, jade, obsidian, and painted murals. Decorated pottery included cylinder vessels, lidded dishes, tripod plates, vases, and bowls. It was made without a potter’s wheel and could be painted, carved into relief, or incised. Ceramic scenes represented palace life, courtly ritual, mythology, divinatory glyphs, and dynastic texts.

Ceramic sculpture included incense burners and burial urns. Classic burners from Palenque had modeled faces of deities or kings attached to elongated hollow tubes. The sources also describe bark-paper books, leather leaves, metates, agricultural tools, woven bags, bark containers, hammocks, and log hives for stingless bees. Maya wooden objects included lintels, boxes, drums, and other carvings. Metal instruments generally had no place in Classic Maya music, apart from pellet-bell rattles.

## Music & performance

Ancient Maya music is described in Native and Spanish sixteenth-century texts and represented in Classic-period art. Instruments included trumpets, flutes, whistles, ocarinas, drums, rattles, and turtle- or tortoiseshell drums. Stringed instruments such as guitars were not invented in the region during the ancient period. Music accompanied funerals, celebrations, rituals, theatrical events, dance, and warfare.

Trumpets could be made of clay or wood. A wall painting from the Bonampak ceremonial complex, dating from about 775 CE, depicts twin trumpeters in a twelve-person orchestra. Wooden trumpets were described as long, thin instruments with twisted gourds at their ends. Flutes included tube flutes capable of producing three-note chords, flutes with a goitre chamber, and ocarinas with four or five holes. The Dresden Codex depicts people playing drums and flutes.

Percussion instruments included the *kayum*, an upright single-headed cylindrical or kettle-shaped drum, standing kettle drums, portable kettle drums, slit-drums called *tun* or *tunkul*, and turtle- or tortoiseshell drums. In the Rabinal Achí, a Highland Maya dance drama dating from the 16th century, the stage and music director usually plays the ancient wooden slit-drum accompanied by two trumpets.

The *holpop* was a musical director in Yucatán and was held in high esteem. According to the sources, the *holpop* was a principal singer who set the key and taught what was necessary to sing, as well as a keeper of musical instruments. Contemporary Indigenous Mayan music can still be heard in Yucatán and Chiapas. The *tunkul* and *bulalek* are played in Yucatán during Christian religious festivities. Guatemalan highland music combines Colonial and post-Colonial Spanish music with Indigenous Maya music in dance plays and communal performances.

## Dance & theatre

Maya dance was connected with music, ritual, theatrical performance, warfare, and religious festivals. Group dances were considered highly sacred, and men and women could dance separately in particular musical ceremonies. Some early dances were associated with shamanistic rituals and altered states of consciousness. Dance, music, and burning offerings could be understood as manifestations of supernatural forces.

The *Rabinal Achí* is a dramatic work consisting of dance and text. It is preserved as it was originally represented, is thought to date from the 15th century, and narrates the mythical and dynastic origins of the Toj Kʼicheʼ rulers of Rabinal and their relationships with neighboring Kʼicheʼ of Qʼumarkaj. It is performed during the Rabinal festival of January 25, the day of Saint Paul.

The museum record for a feathered festival hat identifies *El Baile de la Conquista* as a traditional dance performed in Totonicapán and other parts of Guatemala. It represents the invasion led by Don Pedro de Alvarado y Contreras and his confrontation with Tecún Umán. Guatemalan highland dance plays include themes of conquest, hunting, and sacrifice. The Kʼicheʼ Warrior Dance dates to the Postclassic era and was accompanied by flutes and drums.

## Festivals & rituals

Maya ritual life is organized through sacred places, offerings, pilgrimage, prayer, calendrical cycles, agricultural rites, healing, hunting, warfare, kingship, and ancestor worship. Mountains, wells, caves, and cenotes can be assigned to particular ancestors and deities. Pilgrimages connect local communities with distant sanctuaries; around 1500, Chichen Itza attracted pilgrims from surrounding kingdoms to its large cenote.

Offerings included maize breads, maize and cacao drinks, honey liquor, flowers, incense, rubber figures, and cigars. The sources describe a pre-Hispanic New Year drink made from exactly 415 grains of parched maize and another ritual involving 49 grains of maize mixed with copal. Sacrifice could involve deer, dog, quail, turkey, fish, and, on exceptional occasions, human beings. Bloodletting was practiced by high officials and members of royal families.

The Maya calendar was fundamental to ritual life. It included a 260-day cycle, a 365-day year, a five-day marginal period called *Uayeb*, and thirteen twenty-year periods called *katuns*. The 18 months had festivals dedicated to specific deities and associated with occupational groups such as hunters, fishermen, beekeepers, cacao planters, curers, and warriors.

Life-cycle practices included rituals for making young boys and girls marriageable and the Yucatec Maya *Hetz mek*, which marks a child’s movement from cradling or carrying to the mother’s hip. Agricultural rituals focused on sowing and harvesting maize, thanksgiving, and rain-making.

## Foodways

Ancient Maya foodways centered on maize, beans, squash, and chili peppers. Maize was domesticated from teosinte and became a staple crop used in agriculture, mythology, and ritual. Food was obtained through hunting, foraging, large-scale agriculture, tree-cropping, forest gardens, and the exploitation of aquatic and maritime resources. Agricultural methods included slash-and-burn cultivation, crop rotation, terracing, raised fields, check dams, drained fields, kitchen gardens, forest gardens, and irrigation.

Maize was nixtamalized by soaking and cooking it in an alkaline solution, then ground on a *metate*. It was prepared as tortillas, tamales, atole, and pozole. Tortillas were cooked on a *comal* and used to wrap meat, beans, and other foods. Tamales consisted of corn dough, often with a filling, wrapped in a corn husk and steamed. Atole was a dense drinking gruel, while pozole contained large grains of maize in turkey broth. Chili peppers, cacao, wild onions, and salt were used to diversify flavor.

Other documented foods include tomato, avocado, breadnut, guava, soursop, mammee apple, papaya, pineapple, pumpkin, sweet potato, chaya, chayote, vanilla, epazote, achiote, garlic vine, Mexican oregano, and allspice. Hunting supplied deer, manatee, armadillo, tapir, peccary, monkey, guinea pig, turtle, and iguana. Coastal communities also used fish, lobster, shrimp, conch, and other shellfish.

Cacao was used in drinks and ceremonial contexts. A museum record on Maya beekeeping states that honey and wax from endemic stingless bees retained religious importance, and that honey was used in *balché*, a slightly fermented beverage served by the *hmen* during rain, maize, and bee ceremonies.

## Oral tradition & literature

The *Popol Vuh* is the most significant work of Guatemalan literature in the Kʼicheʼ language and one of the most important works of Pre-Columbian American literature. It is a compendium of Maya stories and legends intended to preserve Maya traditions. Its first known version dates from the 16th century and was written in Quiché transcribed in Latin characters. It was translated into Spanish by the Dominican priest Francisco Ximénez in the beginning of the 18th century.

Maya mythology includes creation narratives, stories of the Hero Twins Hunahpu and Xbalanque, the creation of humankind from maize dough, the defeat of bird demons and deities of disease and death, the Maize Hero, the transformation of brothers into the Sun and Moon, and jaguar-slaying heroes associated with Tzotzil, Tzeltal, and Chʼol Maya traditions.

The *Rabinal Achí* combines dance and text and preserves mythical and dynastic narratives concerning Rabinal and Qʼumarkaj. The sources also describe the *Book of Chilam Balam of Chumayel*, the *Ritual of the Bacabs*, and other colonial texts as sources for Maya narrative and religious knowledge. Contemporary Maya communities continue to use the 260-day *tzolkʼin* ritual calendar in the highlands of Guatemala and Chiapas.

## Language & religion

The Mayan languages form a family spoken in Mesoamerica. Guatemala formally recognized 21 Mayan languages by name in 1996. The family includes Yucatecan, Huastecan, Chʼolan–Tzeltalan, Qʼanjobʼalan, Mamean, and Kʼichean branches. The Guatemalan Maya include Achi, Akatek, Chuj, Ixil, Jakaltek, Kaqchikel, Kʼicheʼ, Mam, Poqomam, Poqomchiʼ, Qʼanjobʼal, Qʼeqchiʼ, Tzʼutujil, and Uspantek. Some Mayan languages were written in Maya script during the pre-Columbian period, especially during the Classic period. The script used about 1,000 distinct characters and combined syllabic signs with logograms.

Traditional Maya religion is part of the wider Mesoamerican religious framework and has interacted for centuries with Roman Catholicism. Contemporary Maya religion includes Indigenous ritual practice, Catholic brotherhoods, civil-religious hierarchies, diviners, curers, pilgrimage, offerings, prayer, and rain and maize ceremonies. The sources describe the Yucatec village priest as a *jmen*, and the *ah kʼin* as a general word for priest. In Guatemala, Maya religion is also reflected in the syncretic figure Maximón, known locally as San Simon of Guatemala.
