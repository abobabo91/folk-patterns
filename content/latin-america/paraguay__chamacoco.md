## Overview

The Chamacoco are an Indigenous people of Paraguay divided into two groups: the Ebytoso, long established along the Paraguay River, and the Tomáraho, who lived inland until displacement in the 1970s. Their language, Yshyr ahwoso, belongs to the Zamucoan family and is now endangered. While the Ebytoso abandoned their rituals under missionary pressure, the Tomáraho retained initiation ritual, shamanic knowledge, and myth. They are known above all for their feather-work ceremonial art and the mythic tradition of the ahnapzöro, the gods who taught them to hunt and use tools.

## Material culture

Chamacoco material culture is documented chiefly through feather ornaments, cordage bags and pouches, and dancing regalia collected in the early twentieth century.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources document a plaited and corded craft economy built from string, twine and fibre cordage.

- **Bags and pouches** (*väska, bolsa, påse*) — The most repeated object type in museum collections, with four bags and one sack catalogued from the Chaco in 1917.
- **Men's belt** (*mansgördel*) — A single man's girdle collected in the Paraguayan Chaco.
- **Fly-whisk** (*flugfläkt*) — A hand-held whisk from the same Boggiani collection series.
- **Feather mantle** — A man's dancing cloak of feathers and string held by the British Museum, the clearest surviving link between ceremonial dress and feather work.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday clothing is not described in the sources; ceremonial dress is documented only through men's dance regalia in feather and cordage.

- **Feather head-dress** (*fjäderprydnad, huvudprydnad*) — A feather ornament stuck into the hair rather than a fitted cap, making it a hair fixture rather than headgear.
- **Dancing tassels and pendants** — Men's ornaments of feathers and string catalogued as dancing regalia.
- **Shoulder ornament** — Worked from egret feathers, quills and twine.
- **Feather band** — An ornament of uncertain use worked from feathers.

### Architecture

The sources do not describe house forms or building materials, only the history of settlement and displacement.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

No ceramic or metalworking tradition is named in the sources, but basketry and wood carving are documented as living object trades.

- **Stone axe** — Catalogued tentatively as a woman's axe made of stone, wood, feather and string, a composite in which hafting cordage and feather elements sit alongside the working stone head.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Personal ornament as it survives in collections is feather work covering wrist, upper arm, shoulder, ear and hair.

- **Feather earring** (*örhänge*) — Of brown feathers, catalogued as a piece from the Chaco.
- **Arm ornaments** (*armprydnad*) — Two matching pieces of brown feathers catalogued as a pair.
- **Wristlets or bracelets** — Two feather-and-fibre pieces held by the British Museum.

## Music & performance

No instruments, song genres, or musical performance contexts are described; evidence of performance is indirect and material.

- **Dancing regalia** — A man's dancing cloak and two men's dancing tassels or pendants place feather ornament at the centre of documented dance practice.

## Dance & theatre

Dance survives in the sources as costume rather than choreography, documented through the boys' initiation ceremony.

- **Boys' initiation ceremony** — The ritual context for feather dancing regalia, retained by the Tomáraho after the Ebytoso abandoned their rituals under missionary pressure.
- **Ahnapzöro** (*anapsoro*) — Powerful, terrible gods with no features in their faces, covered in feathers, hair or strange colours, who initiated the yshyr into their ritual ceremonies.

## Festivals & rituals

The central ritual is the boys' initiation ceremony, whose mythic charter is the arrival and destruction of the ahnapzöro.

- **Boys' initiation ceremony** — Retained by the Tomáraho together with detailed knowledge of myth and shamanism, governed by the story of the ahnapzöro who taught hunting and tool use before being destroyed.
- **Eshönewörta** (*Ashnuwerta*) — A leading ahnapzoro who showed the yshyr that the gods could be killed by a blow to the ankle.
- **Karcha Balut** — The place where Nemur took up a snail and loosed the raging river that became the Paraguay River.
- **Balut** — The master or spokesperson of each animal who makes the hunt possible and punishes excess severely.

## Foodways

The Chamacoco were traditionally hunter-gatherers and now farm and raise livestock, working as ranch hands and day labourers.

- **Yacareceros** — Dissident yshyr groups who survive by alligator poaching, a prohibited activity conducted upriver in the marshlands of the Río Negro.

## Oral tradition & literature

The central narrative is the Big Myth of the ahnapzöro, whose closing episode is simultaneously an origin account of the Paraguay River and a binding obligation on the people.

- **The Big Myth** (*ahnapzöro*) — The central Chamacoco narrative of gods who taught the yshyr to hunt and use tools, then were destroyed, surviving in versions that differ between Ebytoso and Tomáraho tellers.
- **Nemur** — One of two ahnapzöro who escaped destruction and created the Paraguay River from a snail shell.
- **Syr** — A figure in the myth who received Nemur's final words binding the yshyr people to follow the words or face sickness, hunger and enemies.
- **Kytymaraha** — Syr's clan, the last of which will be decimated if the yshyr fail to follow the words.

## Language & religion

Yshyr ahwoso is a Zamucoan language with four dialects, now endangered under pressure from Spanish and Guaraní; the Ebytoso converted to Christianity while the Tomáraho preserved shamanism.

- **Yshyr ahwoso** — The Chamacoco language, meaning the words, the language of the Chamacoco people, a Zamucoan language grouped with Ayoreo and Ancient Zamuco.
- **Héiwo** — A dialect spoken around Fuerte Olimpo.
- **Ebidóso** — A dialect spoken in Bahía Negra.
- **Hório** — A dialect spoken in Bahía Negra.
- **Tomaráho** — A dialect spoken in the Alto Paraná Atlantic forests, with fewer than 200 speakers as of 1970.
