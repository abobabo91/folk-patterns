## Overview

The Atacama people, also called Atacameño, are an Indigenous people of the Atacama Desert and altiplano region in northern Chile and Argentina and southern Bolivia, mainly in the Antofagasta Region. Their historical settlements include San Pedro de Atacama, Calama, Toconao, Quitor, Chiu-Chiu, Lasana, Turi, Topayín, Susques and Antofagasta de la Sierra. In Argentina, 13,936 people identified as first-generation Atacameño in the 2010 census; Chile was home to 21,015 Atacameño people in 2002, and the population was estimated at 21,015 in 2007. Other names include Kunza and Likanantaí. Their folk-culture record includes terraced agriculture, llama and alpaca herding, ceramics, copper and gold work, stone settlements, ritual tablets and a history connected with Tiwanaku, Inca and Spanish rule.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name carved images of human figurines, condors and religious objects on ritual tablets.

### Clothing & dress

 They state only that Atacameño livestock supplied wool, as well as meat and hide, and that the historical museum collection contains textiles.

### Architecture

Atacameño villages were protected by strong stone walls known as *pukara*, a Quechua word. During the third ceramic period, from 1200 AD to 1500 AD, the construction of stone “pukara” fortresses reflected the influence of the Inca civilization. Historic settlements that still exist today with the same names include Quitor, Chiu-Chiu, Lasana, Turi, Topayín, Susques, Calama, Toconao, Antofagasta de la Sierra and San Pedro de Atacama.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Atacameños developed ceramic crafts, copper work and gold work. Copper was extracted from Chuquicamata. In the first period, between 400 AD and 900 AD, they produced pink glazed pottery, including anthropomorphic jugs, as well as golden jewelry and cups. Between 900 AD and 1200 AD, black glaze pottery showed the influence of Tiwanaku culture. The sources also describe tablets for inhaling hallucinogens from the Huilco tree and San Pedro cactus, with carved images of human figurines, condors and religious objects. The R.P. Gustavo Le Paige Archeological Museum holds more than 380,000 pieces of ceramics, textiles and metallic artifacts.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources state that golden jewelry was produced during the first ceramic period, between 400 AD and 900 AD. They also record gold work and copper work among Atacameño material practices. No further jewelry types, body-adornment practices, tattoos, hair practices or ritual adornment are documented in the sources used.

## Music & performance

 They describe smoking ceremonies as a central part of religious culture. Finely carved tablets held a substance that was inhaled through wooden or bone tubes, and the ceremonies were believed to bring humans closer to the gods, allowing the smoker to take on the power of birds, cats or snakes.

## Festivals & rituals

 They describe religious practices associated with smoking hallucinogenic substances. Smoking ceremonies were believed to bring humans closer to the gods and to allow the smoker to take on the power of birds, cats or snakes. The Atacameños adopted the Inca sun cult and constructed altars in high places, especially on Licancabur volcano, which was considered sacred and the home of their many gods.

The sources also state that the Atacameños believed in life after death and buried their dead with all the necessary belongings needed for the journey. In historical accounts, the Atacameño Tomás Paniri joined uprisings led by the Peruvian Túpac Amaru II and the Bolivian Túpac Katari in the 18th century.

## Foodways

The Atacameño were llama and alpaca herders who used their livestock as pack animals and for meat, hide and wool. They developed terraced agriculture to prevent water run-off. Crops included pumpkins, zucchini, chili, beans, tobacco, melon, corn and, above all, potatoes and quinoa. They fertilized crops with guano from seabirds, transported inland on llama.

They also ate meat from their livestock and obtained fish and shellfish from coastal peoples in exchange for *ch'arki*, a dried, salted meat they produced.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They record historical figures and events, including Tomás Paniri’s participation in 18th-century uprisings led by Túpac Amaru II and Túpac Katari.

## Language & religion

The original language of the Atacameños was Kunza, which the sources describe as recently extinct. Other names for the people include Kunza and Likanantaí. In small settlements around Cordillera Domeyko and Salar de Atacama, both within the traditional lands of the Likan Antay, residents speak Spanish without yeísmo, a feature otherwise widespread in Chilean Spanish.

Atacameño religious culture included smoking hallucinogenic substances through wooden or bone tubes, the Inca sun cult and altars on high places. Licancabur volcano was considered sacred and the home of many gods. The sources also describe beliefs in life after death and burial with belongings for the journey.
