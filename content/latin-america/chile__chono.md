## Overview

The Chono, or Guaiteco, were a nomadic Indigenous people or group of peoples associated with the archipelagos of Chiloé, Guaitecas and Chonos. They travelled by canoe and lived as hunter-gatherers, relying heavily on marine resources. Men hunted marine mammals, especially sea lions, while women gathered shellfish and seaweed. Their territory included western Patagonia, and historical references also connect them with Taitao Peninsula, Guar Island and other parts of Chiloé Archipelago. The Chono language is a poorly attested extinct language, known mainly from an untranslated 18th-century catechism and place names. Chono culture combined practices shared with southern sea-farers, especially the Kawésqar, with influences from the Mapuche world. The Chono largely disappeared from the historical record after the 18th century.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state only that Chono healing places consisted of caves or leather structures.

### Architecture

The Chono used wood for boats and houses. Both the Chono and Kawésqar used *Pilgerodendron uviferum* for firewood and for making oars, paddles, boats and houses. The sources also describe leather structures used as healing places.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Chono used nets and spears to gather food from the sea and travelled in small boats used with their *dalcas*. Dogs were associated with fishing and were not themselves a source of food in the Chono diet. Iron objects were highly valued and were obtained from Spanish settlements in Chiloé by trade or theft; some iron was scavenged from European shipwrecks.

## Festivals & rituals

They provide historical information about Spanish missions, population transfers and the eventual absorption of Chono communities into other populations. In 1710, a large group of Chono arrived voluntarily at the Spanish settlement of Calbuco while escaping internal conflicts, and the Spaniards settled this group in Guar Island. In the 1740s, Spanish authorities ordered the depopulation of the archipelagos of Chono and Guaiteca, leading to population transfers to Chiloé Archipelago and movement by some Chono south of Taitao Peninsula.

## Foodways

Chono subsistence was primarily marine-based. Men hunted marine mammals, especially sea lions, while women gathered shellfish and seaweed. Nets and spears were used to obtain food from the sea, and marine foods remained central to the Chono diet over centuries or millennia. Potatoes and other plants from small gardens supplemented the marine catch.

Isotope studies of human bones from former Chono territory indicate that land-based food became more important after the time of Spanish contact, dated in the source to approximately 1550. They also state that dogs were used for fishing and were not themselves a source of food in the Chono diet.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Chono language is attested primarily through an 18th-century catechism that has not been translated into Spanish. Various place names in Chiloé Archipelago have Chono etymologies. The sources also state that much knowledge from Spanish records is filtered through a Huilliche worldview, because Huilliches and Huilliche language were used to communicate with Chonos.

## Language & religion

Chono is described as a poorly attested extinct language of confusing classification. It is known primarily from an untranslated 18th-century catechism and from place names in Chiloé Archipelago. Glottolog characterizes Chono as a language isolate in relation to Mapuche and Kawésqar, while noting lexical parallels with both. Viegas Barros proposed correspondences between part of the Chono vocabulary and grammatical forms and one of those languages, although Chono is not close to either. Campbell concluded that the language called Wayteka or Wurk-wur-we was spurious, based on mixed and perhaps invented vocabulary. They mention that some Chono entered Jesuit missions and that Chono culture was described by some authors as “Fuegian,” in contrast to more northern Mapuche groups. They also record healing places consisting of caves or leather structures.
