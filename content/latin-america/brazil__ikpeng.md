## Overview

The Ikpeng, also known as Txikāo, are an Indigenous community living in the Xingu Indigenous Park in Mato Grosso, Brazil. Their earlier territories were associated with the Iriri River, the Jari River, the Teles Pires-Juruena river basin, the Formosa Mountain formation, the Upper Xingu Basin, and later the Ronuro and Jabotá rivers. Their population was 459 in 2010, after reaching a low of 50 in 1969. The Ikpeng language belongs to the Carib, also called Karib or Cariban, language family and has approximately 500 speakers. Ikpeng cultural documentation includes language research, audiovisual recordings of conversation, songs, stories, and ceremonial speech, as well as participation in the Vídeo nas Aldeias project.

## Material culture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources mention that Ikpeng weapons are represented in the National Museum of the American Indian, but provide no catalogue description of those objects.

## Music & performance

The Museu do Indio’s Documentation Project of Indigenous Languages recorded Ikpeng conversations, songs, stories, and ceremonial speech. Ikpeng filmmakers who received training through the Vídeo nas Aldeias project include Karané Ikpeng, Kumaré Ikpeng, and Natuyu Yuwipo Txicão. Natuyu Yuwipo Txicão is identified as the first Indigenous woman to join that project. The sources name the films *Marangmotxíngmo Mïrang: From the Ikpeng Children to the World* (2001), *Moyngo, the Dream of Maragareum* (2000), and *Pïrinop, My First Contact* (2007).

## Festivals & rituals

They state that the Museu do Indio documentation project recorded ceremonial speech and that the Ikpeng language documentation includes cultural and linguistic aspects of Ikpeng life.

## Foodways

The Ikpeng are described as hunter-gatherers who also fish and farm. Their primary crops include cotton, gourds, maize, manioc, and urucu.

## Oral tradition & literature

One oral history traces Ikpeng ancestral territory as far as the Jari River. The sources also describe audiovisual documentation of Ikpeng stories and ceremonial speech, and identify the Documentation Project of Indigenous Languages as preserving extensive lexical data and transcriptions in a digitized data bank.

## Language & religion

Ikpeng is a Carib language spoken by approximately 500 people in the Xingu Indigenous National Park in Mato Grosso, Brazil. It has high transmission from parent to child, and all members speak the language; the majority are also bilingual in Portuguese. Ikpeng teachers developed a form of writing with the help of linguists in 1994 through the Instituto Socioambiental’s Teacher Training program. Ikpeng children learn their own language alongside Portuguese in the Ikpeng School. The sources describe research on Ikpeng morphology, orthography, grammar, prosody, phonology, and syntax, including the desiderative suffix /–tɨne/, the immediate-past morphemes /–lɨ/ and /–lan/, and the causative morpheme /-nopo/.
