## Overview

The Tapirapé are an Indigenous people of Brazil who live in the Amazon rainforest, near the Araguaia River in the border area of Mato Grosso and Tocantins. Their language, Tapirapé, is also known as *Apyãwa* and *Tapi'irape* and belongs to the Tupí-Guaraní language family. As of 2020, the language had around 917 speakers belonging to the Tapirapé ethnic group. The sources describe a people who sustained much of their culture and customs despite European conquest, colonization, epidemics, conflict with neighboring peoples, and increasing contact with Brazilian society. Their material and social life includes loghouses, gardens, hammocks, baskets, bead strings, body painting, Bird Societies, Feast Groups, shamanism, hunting, fishing, and ceremonies involving kawi, a manioc drink. Anthropologists Herbert Baldus and Charles Wagley, together with the Little Sisters of Jesus, documented important aspects of Tapirapé life.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

They mention hammocks, baskets, strings of beads, skirts, anklets, wristbands, special masks, and other clothing.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no Tapirapé textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The Tapirapé wear no clothing in daily life. Men are covered with a small cone attached to the prepuce, while women squat and sit with their legs together. Women and men paint their bodies with diverse designs according to age and gender.

During special ceremonies and dances, Tapirapé wear skirts, anklets, and wristbands. By 1965, after the Tapirapé had become concentrated in New Village and contact with Brazilian society had increased, some women had begun wearing skirts and blouses, while men had begun wearing shorts at least when receiving visitors or trading goods.

### Architecture

Tapirapé villages were organized around loghouses arranged in an oval formation. Each loghouse housed four or five families related through maternal links and had a leader. Every loghouse owned a garden, although agricultural and hunting products could be shared with people from other houses.

At the center of the settlement was the *takana*, a sort of men’s club and the home of the six Bird Societies. Men spent part of the day there, and boys could begin going to the Takana after becoming adolescents.

By 1965, loghouses had become smaller, and some had become single-family houses built with mortar. The Takana and the Bird Societies still existed, although Takana activities then included manufacturing artifacts for trade.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do record individually owned tools, hammocks, baskets, strings of beads, and other objects. Tools and trade goods made of iron were introduced by European Brazilians during sporadic contact beginning in 1910.

Tapirapé material culture was also involved in exchange. Shamans and midwives were paid with goods, goods could serve to make peace, and an annual gift exchange distributed wealth among less fortunate people. By 1965, the Takana was involved in manufacturing artifacts for trade, and some men possessed Brazilian bank notes.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Tapirapé body adornment includes body painting, strings of beads, anklets, wristbands, and the small cone attached to the men’s prepuce. Women and men paint their bodies with different designs according to age and gender. Anklets and wristbands are worn during special ceremonies and dances. In spirit impersonation, members of the relevant Bird Society wear a special mask and other pieces of clothing until their whole body is covered.

## Music & performance

They do describe ceremonial dancing and the performance of spirit impersonation in the village.

Tapirapé religion includes spirits associated with particular Bird Societies. Spirits were believed to live in the Takana on a cyclic basis. When a spirit was present, two members of the corresponding Bird Society impersonated it by wearing a special mask and other clothing that covered their whole bodies. They then danced around the village and received good kawi from every loghouse. Good kawi is described as a manioc drink.

Music and alcohol were also part of cultural change. By 1965, Brazilian music was beginning to be heard at parties, and alcohol had become common despite strong protests from the Little Sisters of Jesus and officers of the Indian Protection Service.

## Dance & theatre

The sources document ceremonial dancing associated with spirits and Bird Societies. Two members of the Bird Society belonging to a spirit wore a special mask and additional clothing covering the body, impersonated the spirit, and danced around the village. Their performance was connected with the receipt of good kawi from each loghouse.

The sources also state that skirts, anklets, and wristbands were worn during special ceremonies and dances.

## Festivals & rituals

The Tapirapé practiced an annual gift exchange ceremony in which all men in the village had the opportunity to take a sip of “bad kawi,” a drink producing intense nausea. Wealthy people generally chose not to taste it but had to donate gifts to those who did. Less fortunate people usually drank in order to receive donations. The ceremony distributed goods among people with fewer resources.

A coming-of-age ceremony was performed when children became young adults. Boys could begin going to the Takana after becoming adolescents. The Takana was the men’s club and the home of the six Bird Societies, each named after a bird species. Membership in a Bird Society followed parental lineage, and each society included people from several loghouses. Bird Societies organized group hunts and expeditions to clear gardens.

Both men and women belonged to Feast Groups used for food sharing in the village plaza. Marriage involved a change of residence: after marriage, men lived with their wife’s families.

Shamans were called upon to give the spirit to a child about to be born. They also purified agricultural and hunting products and were believed to go to Shaman Village after death. The sources describe no named annual festival beyond the gift exchange ceremony.

## Foodways

Tapirapé subsistence combined slash-and-burn horticulture, hunting, and fishing. Forest patches were cleared and burned to produce fertile soil. A patch was planted once or twice before the people moved to another one. Each loghouse owned a garden, while agricultural and hunting products could be shared among people from other houses.

The sources name kawi, a manioc drink, in two forms. Good kawi was given to spirit impersonators by every loghouse after they danced around the village. Bad kawi was consumed during the annual gift exchange ceremony and produced intense nausea. The distinction between these drinks is described in the sources.

Food was also organized socially. Feast Groups consisting of both men and women were used for food sharing in the village plaza. Shamans purified agricultural and hunting products.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Tapirapé had a rich mythology, although the sources do not preserve the names or narratives of individual myths, folktales, epics, proverbs, riddles, or songs. Cultural heroes were formerly very powerful shamans who had performed valuable services.

The sources identify the anthropologists Herbert Baldus and Charles Wagley as important writers on the Tapirapé. Wagley reported on visits in 1953, 1957, and 1965 and described changes caused by expanding contact with Brazilian society. The Little Sisters of Jesus had been involved with the Tapirapé since 1953.

## Language & religion

Tapirapé is also known as Apyãwa and Tapi'irape. It is a Tupí-Guaraní language of Brazil spoken near the Araguaia River, in the border area of Mato Grosso and Tocantins. As of 2020, it had around 917 speakers. The term “Tapirapé” originated in Tupi and means “tapir’s way”; it referred to the Milky Way and was a name given to the people by foreigners. The people called themselves *apyãwa*, although Tapirapé later became a term they used for their language and themselves.

The sources describe Tapirapé religion as based on shamanism. Shamans communicated with spirits that could be kindly or unfriendly, entered trance states by gulping tobacco, blew smoke on sick people, and performed massage to remove a bad spirit or object from the body. They could be accused of sorcery when related people died from disease. Shamans also gave a spirit to a child about to be born and purified agricultural and hunting products. Spirits were connected with the Takana and the Bird Societies.
