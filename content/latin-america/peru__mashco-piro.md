## Overview

The Mashco Piro, also known by the endonym *Nomole*, are an indigenous tribe of nomadic hunter-gatherers living in remote regions of the Amazon rainforest. They inhabit Manú National Park in Peru’s Madre de Dios Region and have actively avoided contact with non-native peoples. Their number was estimated at around 100 to 250 in 1998, compared with 20 to 100 in 1976; in 2024, their number was believed to be above 750. The Mashco Piro speak a dialect of the Piro language, and their language is described as a poorly attested Arawakan language. The people are significant to the study of contemporary Indigenous lifeways because their nomadic movement, avoidance of outsiders, and limited language documentation have restricted outside knowledge of their culture.

## Material culture

### Architecture

During the dry season, the Mashco Piro are reported to construct palm-leaf huts on riverbanks, where scientists believe they fish. During the wet season, they retreat to the rain forest. Similar huts were observed in the 1980s.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do record seasonal movement: palm-leaf huts are associated with the dry season, while during the wet season the Mashco Piro retreat to the rain forest. The Peruvian government has banned contact with the Mashco Piro because of concern that strangers could transmit diseases to which they have not built up immunity.

## Foodways

They do state that the people fish from riverbanks during the dry season. In August 2013, a group of Mashco Piro people was reported to have appeared to ask neighboring villagers for food.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that around 300 words in the language were recorded, while language documentation remains limited because the Mashco Piro are highly nomadic hunter-gatherers who avoid contact with outsiders.

## Language & religion

Mashco-Piro is described as a poorly attested Arawakan language spoken in Peru by the Mashco Piro or *Nomole*. The term *Nomole* is glossed as “brothers” or “countrymen” in Mashco Piro and Yine, while another source gives the meaning “relative.” The language is very similar to Piro, also called Yine, with an estimated 60% inherent intelligibility. One account considers Mashco-Piro a dialect of Piro, while another suggests that it may instead be a dialect of Iñapari. According to the Yine, Mashco-Piro is more archaic than modern Yine and is between 50 and 80% comprehensible with it.
