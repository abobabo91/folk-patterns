## Overview

The Lokono are a Caribbean people whose name was given to them by Spanish explorers as "Arawak." About 10,000 remain today, mostly in Guyana. Their culture survived colonisation through hidden religious practice and oral tradition, and from the late 1980s a movement across the Caribbean has worked to recover and restore Taíno heritage. The sources document their material culture through museum objects and archaeological evidence of complex settlements.

## Material culture

Lokono material culture centres on plaited and twined plant fibres, carved wooden ritual objects inlaid with shell and gold, and pottery.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Lokono textile work uses plaited cane and fibre, with patterns built from alternating dyed and natural materials.

- **Twilled plaited cane boxes and baskets** — Square lidded boxes worked in twilled plaited cane with reinforced rims, patterned on the lid with designs like dog motifs built from dark dyed cane against natural cane.
- **Plaited palm-leaf work** — Bags and figurative objects plaited from palm leaf, including a model fish made of awarra leaf.
- **Composite hammock** — A hammock of single straps of twined pineapple fibre crossed by spaced bars of plaited cotton yarn, ornamented with red and white cotton tassels.

### Clothing & dress

- **Headband with shell plaques** — A headband on a carved figure is embellished with a row of decorative elements that may represent shell plaques.

### Architecture

Archaeological evidence shows Arawakan peoples built complex settlements with ringed villages, raised fields, and large mounds.

- **Ringed villages** — Circular settlement patterns documented among Arawakan-speaking peoples, indicating organized community structure.
- **Raised fields** — Agricultural fields built above ground, used to grow cassava, maize, sweet potatoes, and yautia.
- **Black earth** — Soil deliberately modified by adding charcoal, still famed today for its agricultural productivity.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Lokono carved wooden objects are the most elaborate pieces, decorated with gold and shell inlay; pottery and fishing gear complete the record.

- **Duho ritual seat** (*duho*) — A ritual seat sculpted from guayacan hardwood in the form of a crouching male figure, with eyes, mouth and shoulders inlaid with gold and a geometric design carved on the back.
- **Birdman spirit figure** — A carved wooden bird-man spirit with a prominent beak, extended wings, exposed genitals marking it masculine, and shell once marking the eyes and teeth.
- **Bamboo quiver** — A bamboo quiver with a fitted lid holding poisoned arrows for hunting.
- **Macquarie whip** (*macquarie*) — A whip with a wooden handle and a bast-fibre thong used as hunting and utility gear.
- **Pottery bowl** — An ovoid pottery bowl with a handle at each end of the rim, the handles painted white.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources do not document Lokono jewelry, tattooing, or hair practice; evidence is indirect through sculptural objects.

- **Gold inlay** — Gold inlaid into the eyes, mouth and shoulders of the duho ritual seat.
- **Shell inlay** — Shell once marked the eyes of the Birdman figure and still survives in the beak as teeth.

## Music & performance

Lokono musical instruments include rattles used in dances and possibly noise-makers from basketry.

- **Dance-rattle** — A cylindrical rattle with one end squared off, plaited from leaf and containing seeds, with patterns worked in black, used in dances.
- **Baby's rattles** — Two plaited cane rattles patterned with dyed cane and finished with featherwork on the handle.
- **Patterned basketry box** — A plaited container with lid holding cassava or vegetal shavings, queried as a possible noise-maker that would make plaited containers part of the sound-making repertoire.

## Dance & theatre

No named dances or dramatic traditions appear in the sources; dance is attested only through a cylindrical seed-filled rattle.

## Festivals & rituals

Ritual paraphernalia and religious survival mark Lokono festivals; from the late 1980s a Caribbean movement has worked to revive Taíno culture.

- **Duho ritual seat** (*duho*) — An explicitly ritual seat, gold-inlaid and carved in the form of a crouching male figure, used in ceremonies.
- **Agua Dulce** (*Agua Dulce (Tamani)*) — A syncretic religion practised in the Dominican Republic, representing Taíno practice maintained and transmitted through disguise as Catholicism.
- **Higuayagua** (*Yukayeke*) — A reformed Taíno tribe as part of the late 20th-century cultural revival movement.
- **Yukayeke Taíno Borikén** (*Yukayeke*) — A reformed Taíno tribe with communities now in Cuba, Jamaica and Puerto Rico, part of the Caribbean cultural restoration movement.

## Foodways

Cassava, maize and sweet potatoes were the main crops, supported by raised fields and charcoal-enriched black earth; fishing supplied protein.

- **Cassava** — A woody shrub grown in tropical regions, the staple crop that gives the Arawak people their name and remains a staple for millions worldwide.
- **Raised fields** — Agricultural fields built above ground to grow cassava, maize, sweet potatoes, and yautia.
- **Fishing nets** — Nets made of fibres, bones, hooks and harpoons used to catch fish for protein.
- **Maize and sweet potatoes** — Main crops grown alongside cassava in raised fields supported by black earth.

## Oral tradition & literature

Lokono mythology names one creator deity; cultural and language revival since the late 1980s has pooled oral history and cross-referenced Spanish journals to recover lost knowledge.

- **Aiomun-Kondi** — The Lokono deity who created the world, from Lokono mythology.
- **Taíno revival movement** — A movement from the late 1980s and 1990s across the Caribbean that pooled individually held oral knowledge and cross-referenced Spanish journals to recover Taíno culture, religion, and language.
- **Hiwatahia Hekexi dialect** (*Hiwatahia Hekexi*) — A dialect of the Taíno language preserved in words that survived into local Spanish dialects and reconstructed through comparison with other Arawakan languages.

## Language & religion

Lokono is a critically endangered active-stative language spoken by roughly 2,500 fluent speakers; religion centres on the creator deity Aiomun-Kondi and syncretic Agua Dulce.

- **Lokono** (*Lokono (Arawak, Arowak, Aruák)*) — An active-stative language spoken in eastern Venezuela, Colombia, Guyana, Trinidad and Tobago, Suriname and French Guiana, with roughly 2,500 fluent and semi-fluent speakers remaining.
- **Lokono Dian** — The Lokono name for their language, meaning 'the people's speech'.
- **Aiomun-Kondi** — The Lokono creator deity who created the world.
- **Agua Dulce** (*Agua Dulce (Tamani)*) — A syncretic religion practised in the Dominican Republic representing Taíno practice maintained through Catholicism to escape discrimination.

## Glossary

- *Lokono* — The people; the language and self-designation of Arawak people
- *Lokono Dian* — The people's speech; the Lokono name for their language
- *Arawak* — An exonym applied by Spanish explorers to Caribbean and South American peoples; the tribal name refers to cassava, the main crop
- *Taíno* — Island Arawak peoples of the Greater Antilles and northern Lesser Antilles
- *duho* — A ritual seat carved from hardwood and inlaid with gold
- *macquarie* — A whip with wooden handle and bast-fibre thong used for hunting
- *Aiomun-Kondi* — The Lokono creator deity
- *Agua Dulce* — Syncretic religion blending Taíno practice with Catholicism, practised in the Dominican Republic
- *Tamani* — Alternative name for Agua Dulce syncretic religion
- *Yukayeke* — Taíno tribe; reformed communities exist in Cuba, Jamaica and Puerto Rico
- *awarra* — A palm leaf used in plaited fibre work
- *Hiwatahia Hekexi* — A dialect of the Taíno language, reconstructed from surviving words and other Arawakan languages
- *Arowak* — Alternate spelling of Arawak
- *Aruák* — Alternate spelling of Arawak
- *black earth* — Soil modified by adding charcoal, valued for agricultural productivity
- *raised fields* — Agricultural fields built above ground for growing crops in wetlands
- *cassava* — A woody shrub root crop, staple food and namesake of the Arawak people
- *bora* — Alternate past tense marker in Lokono language
- *loko* — Present continuous tense marker in Lokono language
