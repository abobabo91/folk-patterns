## Overview

The Cayubaba, also called Cayuvava, Cayuwaba, or Kayuvava, inhabit the Beni region of Bolivia, west of the Mamoré River and north of Santa Ana Yacuma. Their territory forms part of the region historically known as Mojos or Moxos, which covers approximately 200,000 square kilometers of what is currently the Department of Beni. Cayubaba is a moribund language and is considered a language isolate, although lexical similarities with the Arawak, Bororo, Takana, and Tupi language families have been attributed to contact. The Cayubaba population is described in the source as 794 inhabitants; the 2012 Bolivian census recorded 2,203 Cayubaba, including 1,246 who learned the language in childhood and only 12 for whom it was the main language. Their documented folk-culture profile centers on farming, tobacco cultivation, language, and mission and river-port history.

## Material culture

### Architecture

They do record the Missions of San Carlos, Conception, and Peñas, as well as the Mission of Exaltation of the Holy Cross, which was primarily inhabited by the Cayubaba.

## Music & performance

The linguistic material gives examples of Cayubaba vocabulary, including *boro* “give,” *tavu* “swim,” *veje-veje* “wind,” *ñoko* “monkey,” *ãre-ai* “1SG,” *ãre-a* “2SG,” *kóra* “perhaps,” and *ñõhõ* “now,”.

## Festivals & rituals

The historical material records Catholic mission foundations, including the Mission of Exaltation of the Holy Cross at the beginning of the 18th century.

## Foodways

Cayubaba farming includes rice, yucca, corn, bananas, sugar cane, beans, pumpkins, and sweet potatoes. The community also raised livestock on a small scale. Tobacco was an important regional crop: the Cayubaba region was famous for growing tobacco, and during the exploitation of rubber its commercialization was intense throughout the country. Exaltación became a busy port on the Mamoré River, although tobacco cultivation was almost stopped in the mid-20th century by the mass emigration of Cayubaba to Exaltación during a measles epidemic.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do identify Key’s *Lexicon-dictionary of Cayuvava-English*, published in 1975 as part of the Language Data Amerindian Series, and mention the online edition of Lenguas de Bolivia and the Cayuvava entry in the Intercontinental Dictionary Series. These are language documentation resources rather than accounts of Cayubaba oral literature.

## Language & religion

Cayubaba, also called Cayuvava, Cayuwaba, or Kayuvava, is a moribund language of the Bolivian Amazon and is considered a language isolate. It became one of Bolivia’s official indigenous languages following Supreme Decree N.º 25894 on September 11, 2000; it was included in the Political Constitution introduced on February 7, 2009. The language has five distinct word classes: verbs, nouns, pronouns, modifiers, and particles. Its basic numerical system includes forms for “one,” “two,” “three,” “four,” and “five,” with combinations forming numbers up to ten; forms for eleven to nineteen use the augmentative suffix *-hiiñe*, while borrowed Castilian words are used from one hundred. The sources describe Jesuit missionaries and Catholic missions.
