## Overview

The Wayuu, also known as Wayu, Wayú, Guajiro, or Wahiro, are an Indigenous people of the Guajira Peninsula in northern Colombia and northwest Venezuela. Their territory lies on the Caribbean Sea coast and straddles the Venezuela–Colombia border. The Ranchería River in Colombia and the El Limón River in Venezuela are major sources of water in this arid environment. According to a 1997 census, Colombia had approximately 144,003 Wayuu, while the 2001 Venezuelan census recorded 293,777. The Wayuu language, wayuunaiki, belongs to the Arawakan language family. Wayuu culture is especially represented by its weaving, crocheting, music, dance, oral traditions, rancherías, and continued resistance to outside control.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Wayuu bags** — *Mochilas* are crocheted bags made today from acrylic thread. They occur in many sizes and styles, including the *susu*, a backpack usually 20–30 cm wide and 35 cm high, used to carry personal and work items. Their decorating patterns are inspired by nature and by what Wayuu culture sees around it.

**Crocheted hammocks** — Wayuu women crochet hammocks for sleeping. The tradition is associated with the mythical spider Walekeru, who is said to have taught Wayuu women to crochet.

**Belts** — Crochet instruction traditionally includes making belts for men, alongside hammocks, shoes, bracelets, and bags.

**Bracelets** — Wayuu women learn to crochet bracelets as part of the same textile tradition that produces bags, hammocks, belts, and footwear.

**Crocheted shoes** — Shoes are among the objects associated with Wayuu crochet instruction and production.

Wayuu bags were formerly made from natural fibers, including locally grown cotton, and were dyed with plants and other elements of the surrounding environment. These earlier bags had shades of brown, red, and other natural hues. Contemporary crochet pieces are made from acrylic threads, producing brighter colors.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name decorating patterns inspired by nature and the surrounding environment.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe clothing in connection with the Majayura ritual. During the courtship dance, the young Wayuu girl covers her head and wears a fabric shawl and dress. The sources also state that girls undergoing puberty seclusion are taught how to become wives, including cooking and crocheting Wayuu bags. They rest in a *chinchorro*, or large hammock, and are required to shave their heads. The sources mention men’s belts and crocheted shoes as products associated with Wayuu textile work.

### Architecture

A traditional Wayuu settlement consists of five or six houses forming *caseríos* or *rancherías*. Each ranchería is named after a plant, animal, or geographic place. Rancherías are generally isolated and far from one another so that goat herds do not mix.

The typical house is a small structure called a *piichi* or *miichi*. It is generally divided into two rooms, with hammocks used for sleeping and storing personal belongings. Near the main house is a common area called a *luma* or *enramada*, similar to a living room but largely open to the surroundings. It has six pillars and a flat roof and is used for everyday duties, receiving visitors, and business activities. Family members also hang hammocks there for the noon nap.

Walls are traditionally made of *yotojoro*, a wattle-and-daub material of mud, hay, and dried canes. The dagger cactus *yosú* is preferred for roofing and for yotojoro wood. Other plants used in construction include *trupillo* or *turpío*, *jattá*, *kapchip*, and *kayush*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources mention ceramics as household objects used to keep water and as burial containers. The typical house contains ceramics alongside acrylic-fiber purses or *mochilas*. After five years, the bones of the dead may be exhumed, placed in ceramics or a *chinchorro*, and reburied in the clan’s cemetery.

The sources also describe *yotojoro* construction, cactus wood, hammocks, purses, water containers, and objects used in weaving and crochet.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They mention crocheted bracelets and the practice of shaving girls’ heads during puberty seclusion.

## Music & performance

Traditional Wayuu music is closely connected with economic and social life. Wayuu people sing to their cattle and use music during meetings, celebrations, and mourning rituals at funerals.

Named instruments include the *kashi*, *sawawa*, *ma’asi*, *totoy*, *taliraai*, and *wootoroyoi*. The *sawawa* is described as a type of flute, the *taliraai* as a tubular flute, and the *wootoroyoi* as a type of clarinet. Adult men play traditional drums and musical instruments during the Majayura dance.

Music also accompanies social gatherings and ritual events. The sources describe it as part of celebrations and funerals.

## Dance & theatre

*La Yonna* is a traditional dance used to honor guests.

The Majayura includes a dance at the center of the ritual passage of the “young Wayuu virgin.” A girl dances in a courtship dance with prospective suitors. With her head covered and wearing a fabric shawl and dress, she moves forward with small steps and outstretched arms, swooping like a bird, inside a circle formed by people from the village. A male dancer moves backward before her, approaching and yielding as they circle until he falls to the ground. Adult men play drums and other instruments in a ring around the dancers.

If the male is respected in his clan and accepted, he must pay a dowry to the girl’s mother and male relatives.

## Festivals & rituals

The Wayuu territory has four weather seasons. The rainy season from September to December is called *Juyapu*. The dry season from December to April is known as *Jemial*. A second rainy season, *Iwa*, lasts from April to May, followed by a second dry season from May to September.

Birth traditionally takes place at home, assisted by the mother-in-law or the nearest female relative. The well-being of the child has priority, and women feed children first. They also follow strict diets when the survival of children is not assured.

Puberty rituals concern girls more strongly than boys. Girls may enter seclusion as early as 12 years old or when menstruation begins. The seclusion lasts from two months up to two years. During this period, girls shave their heads, rest in a *chinchorro*, receive instruction in cooking and crocheting, eat a special vegetarian diet called *Jaguapi*, and bathe frequently.

Traditional marriages are generally arranged and accompanied by a dowry given to the mother’s brothers and uncles. Young girls may be promised to men of the clan as young as 11 years old. Men may have multiple wives.

Burials are important. The body is first buried with personal belongings. After five years, the bones are exhumed, placed in ceramics or a *chinchorro*, and reburied in the clan’s cemetery. The village of Uribia celebrated Christmas and New Year’s Eve for the first time in 1942.

## Foodways

The sources mention several foods and dietary practices. During puberty seclusion, girls eat *Jaguapi*, a special vegetarian diet.

The dagger cactus *yosú* is used in several ways. Its young shoots are fed to goats, while its fruit, called *iguaraya*, resembles pitahaya and is a popular food among the Wayuu. The sources also state that the Wayuu historically traded pearls and brazilwood with British and Dutch merchants.

## Oral tradition & literature

Wayuu oral tradition records experiences of coerced labor. It states that Wayuu people were often tricked into entering coerced labor after being offered free transportation to other settlements for wage labor and then taken to places where they worked without payment.

The sources also connect weaving to the mythical spider Walekeru. Walekeru is said to have created magical pieces with thread from her mouth and taught Wayuu women to crochet hammocks, belts, shoes, bracelets, and bags. They do describe efforts to preserve wayuunaiki, including the creation of the first illustrated Wayuunaiki–Spanish and Spanish–Wayuunaiki dictionary by the Kamusuchiwo’u Ethno-educative Center.

## Language & religion

Wayuu, or wayuunaiki, belongs to the Arawakan language family. It is spoken in northwestern Venezuela and northeastern Colombia on the Guajira Peninsula and around Lake Maracaibo. The two main dialects are Wüinpümüin, spoken in the northeast of the peninsula, and Wopumüin, spoken in the southwest. They are mutually intelligible and differ minimally. Younger Wayuu commonly speak Spanish fluently, while the preservation of the traditional language remains important.

The central figure of Wayuu religion is Maleiwa, the creator of everything, the Wayuu, and the founder of society. Pulowi and Juya are spiritual beings associated with procreation and life. Pulowi is connected with the wind and dry seasons, while Juya is a nomad associated with hunting and described as a powerful killer. Wanülu represents an evil spirit connected with illness and death.

The sources also describe the Wayuu as having experienced evangelization by Capuchin friars and influence from the Catholic Church.
