## Overview

The Tzotzil are an Indigenous Maya people of the central highlands of Chiapas in southern Mexico, numbering about 298,000 as of 2000. Their largest municipal populations are Chamula (48,500), San Cristóbal de las Casas (30,700) — known by its Tzotzil name *Jovel*, and still regarded as the cultural capital of Chiapas — and Zinacantán (24,300). Their language belongs to the Tzeltalan sub-branch of the Mayan family, descended from the proto-Ch'ol spoken in the late classic period at sites such as Palenque and Yaxchilan; the name *tzotzil* originally meant "bat people," from *sotz'* "bat," while speakers call the language *Bats'i k'op*, "true language." In folk-culture terms the Tzotzil matter above all for backstrap-loom weaving: the museum holdings shown here are dominated by brocaded and embroidered garments whose patterning is specific to individual highland townships.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Backstrap loom weaving** — Nearly every Tzotzil textile in these collections was woven on a backstrap loom: a tree-anchored twined rope, a series of wooden dowels wrapped with "heading cords," flat tapered battens, and a weaver's strap, or *mecapal*, plaited from vegetable fibre and passed around the weaver's waist. One collected loom was acquired with its weft-brocaded web still in progress, burgundy, yellow and green wool on a white cotton ground. Cloth is woven in narrow webs and seamed; spinning was done with a wooden stick pointed at both ends and a pottery whorl.

**Huipil** — The woman's blouse or long overdress. A Chiapas example is built from three webs of white cotton sewn together, folded and seamed at the sides with a cut rounded neck opening, and brocaded in red geometric patterning with multicoloured accents around the neck and across the shoulders. Commercial cotton thread, *hilaza*, is used alongside handspun yarn.

**Enredo** — The wrap-around skirt, indigo-dyed. A San Andrés Larráinzar skirt is a dark blue plain weave of two narrow webs joined by cross-stitch seams worked on the front face in red, yellow, pink, green and violet wool, with white stripes produced by white weft threads. Another wrap skirt is embroidered in satin stitch with multicoloured acrylic yarn and artificial silk: stripes along the seams, a band of flowers and birds at the hem, two bands of flowers at the top.

**Faja** — The woven sash or belt that holds the *enredo* up. Zinacantán women's belts are warp-faced weaves of red yarn with ornamental green stripes, the warp beginning and ending in loops; Huixtán belts are red with yellow edge borders, described in the catalogue as characteristic of that township. A wool belt woven on the backstrap loom is red with black stripes, with braided fringes knotted together at each end. One woman's waist-sash of coarsely handspun wool is red with a yellow stripe along either edge.

**Woman's cape and cloak** — A cotton cape woven with four selvages is patterned in red and white warp stripes, edged with a narrow multicoloured warp band, and fastened at the neck with two braided cords and tassels. A heavier woman's cloak of wool and orlon carries a blue and light-blue geometric field with orange, blue and fuchsia side borders and ties wrapped in fuchsia, orange, turquoise and green.

**Duk** — The man's square cloth of striped or checked cotton with pink wool tassels at the corners, worn around the neck and over the shoulders at Zinacantán. The same kerchief, blue and white checked with a fuchsia wool tassel in each corner, appears on a miniature man's costume.

**Bolsa** — Small carrying bags. One Larráinzar *bolsa*, handwoven of cotton and embroidered in green, red and orange, was used for carrying money. A bag of factory-made cotton, *manta*, is covered in running-stitch embroidery: bands of red alternating with turquoise, orange, purple, green, violet and yellow stripes forming diamond patterns, gathered with a red drawstring. Shoulder-bags are netted from dyed and undyed maguey fibre with a purple and fuchsia band at the top and a leather strap.

**Motif vocabulary.** Red geometric brocade at neck and shoulder; warp stripes in red and white; bands of flowers and of flowers-with-birds along skirt hems; diamond patterns in embroidered bands; indigo grounds broken by white weft stripes; cross-stitch on seams; zigzag lines on hat crowns; a border of toad-like animals on a baby's bonnet.

### Clothing & dress

Men's dress consists of a shirt, short pants, a neckerchief, a hat and a wool poncho; women wear a blouse or long overdress, the *huipil*, an indigo-dyed *enredo*, a cotton sash and a shawl. The collected hats are of palm or other vegetable fibre: a braided strip coiled and sewn into a wide-brimmed hat with a rounded crown ending in a triangular point, a brown band at the rim and around the top of the crown, and an orange and red zigzag sewn around the crown's centre. A miniature costume shows the festive version of the same hat — slats forming a pointed crown, a dark fibre band, and ribbons in yellow, pink, violet, green, white, fuchsia, blue and yellow sewn to the top and sides — worn with a red and white striped *cotón* fringed at both ends. Infants were dressed in gathered white cotton bonnets with a self-fabric fringe and dense multicoloured embroidery on a red base. The sources used here do not describe footwear.

### Architecture

Houses were traditionally built of wattle and daub or of lumber, usually with thatched roofs. Beyond that, the sources used for this profile say nothing about Tzotzil house form, interior arrangement or architectural decoration; what they do record is that the built environment is religiously charged rather than ornamented — ceremonial circuits are walked counterclockwise around the four corners of houses and fields and end at the centre, and the ceremonial centre of a settlement holds the "navel," a mound of earth at the middle of a world conceived as a square. Any Tzotzil who takes mud for house walls or limestone for lime is held to owe the Earth Lord, who owns all land and its resources, compensation in a ceremony.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The most distinctive Tzotzil pottery form in these collections is the *incensario*, a pottery incense burner on a circular base that tapers to a cylindrical stem and flares out into a bowl, with three sets of three prongs on the rim; examples survive with an orange slip, with a beige slip, and plainly fire-stained, one with its lower half painted orange and the bowl striped and dotted. Spinning equipment — a double-pointed wooden spindle with a pottery whorl — and the wooden backstrap loom with its plaited *mecapal* are the other recurring household objects. Figurative work includes a pottery figure wearing a glazed pottery sombrero, a blue cloth tunic and a red plastic sash, and dolls made from maize leaves with cloth heads and braided corn-silk hair tied with red and yellow yarn. The sources record no Tzotzil metalwork.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used here do not cover Tzotzil jewelry or body adornment; the only hair practice they document is indirect — figures made for nativity scenes are given black wool hair braided and decorated with fuchsia, blue and yellow wool.

## Music & performance

Musical documentation in these sources is slight but concrete. A Tzotzil guitar carved from a single hourglass-shaped wooden body, with a central soundhole, a glued-on bridge and neck, wooden frets, twelve wooden tuning pegs and twelve metal strings, carries two leather loops — one on the body, one on the neck — for a strap, so it was played standing and in procession. The language itself preserves a word for flute, *'ama*. Recordings of Tzotzil made between 1964 and 1972 for Victoria Bricker's Mayan Languages Collection, now at the Archive of the Indigenous Languages of Latin America, include elicited humorous narratives, songs and prayers, as well as live recordings of ritual humor at the fiestas of Saint Lawrence, Christmas, New Year's Day, Epiphany and Saint Sebastian — which places song and prayer firmly inside the festival cycle rather than on a concert stage. Tzotzil-language programming is broadcast today by the CDI radio stations XEVFS, from Las Margaritas, and XECOPA, at Copainalá. The sources name no Tzotzil ensemble type or song genre beyond this.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used for this profile do not describe named Tzotzil dances or dramatic forms; the nearest documented performance genre is the ritual humor recorded at the township fiestas of Saint Lawrence, Saint Sebastian, Christmas, New Year's Day and Epiphany, and the nativity scene, or *nacimiento*, built as a tableau of maize-leaf figures mounted on wooden pegs — a kneeling shepherd with a painted face and an apron over shirt and trousers, and a cowled Wise Man in a dark robe with a red wool sash, holding a nut shell in both hands as his offering.

## Festivals & rituals

The documented festival calendar is the Catholic one as kept in Tzotzil townships: Saint Sebastian, Epiphany, New Year's Day, Christmas and the fiesta of Saint Lawrence, each an occasion for prayer, song and licensed ritual joking. Carved wooden or plaster images and pictures of Catholic saints are revered, dressed in a mixture of colonial and Zinacanteco-style clothing. Alongside this runs a body of ritual that is not Catholic in origin. Priests walk ceremonial circuits counterclockwise around the four corners of houses and fields, ending at the centre with offerings. Anyone drawing on water holes, trees, mud or limestone owes a compensating ceremony to the Earth Lord, pictured in modern times as a large, fat, rich Ladino living underground. The inner soul, *ch'ulel*, is composed of thirteen parts, and a person who loses one or more needs a curing ceremony performed by a shaman to recover them — soul loss being caused by the fright of a fall or of seeing a demon at night, by punishment from the ancestral gods, or by witchcraft that sells the soul to the Earth Lord. The second soul, the *wayhel*, is shared with a wild animal companion, the *chanul* — jaguars, ocelots, coyotes, squirrels, opossums — penned by the ancestral gods in four corrals inside the Senior Large Mountain in the east; if a *chanul* is released, its person is in mortal danger until a long ceremony rounds the animal up and returns it. At death the inner soul goes to *Katibak*, the world of the dead at the centre of the earth, and relives its life in reverse before being assigned to a newborn of the opposite sex; baptized infants and women who die in childbirth go instead to *Winajel*, in the Sun.

## Foodways

The sources used for this profile do not describe Tzotzil cooking, dishes or drink. What they document is production and trade: the Tzotzil made salt from wells near Ixtapa and traded it throughout the Chiapas highlands both before and after the Spanish Conquest, and just before the Conquest they exported quetzal feathers and amber to the Aztec capital of Tenochtitlán. Maize is present in the collections only as a material — the leaves of the plant are the body of dolls and of nativity figures, and corn silk their hair.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tzotzil narrative, as these sources preserve it, is cosmological. The god Manojel-Tojel created human beings by leading them out of the caves of the original hills, and each patron god then "installed himself in a hill, by order of the gods of the four corners of the earth," so that every town has its sacred mountain. Yahwal Balamil lives inside the earth, rides a deer with serpent bridles, releases the water-filled clouds through caves, and announces himself with the croaking of frogs. The ancestral couples, the *Totilme'il* or "Fathers–Mothers," inhabit local hills and are the most important gods; the world rests on the shoulders of the *Vashak*. A Spanish chronicler recorded that the ancestors of Zinacantán "discovered a stone bat and considered it God and worshiped it." Spoken genres were documented on tape between 1964 and 1972: humorous narratives, songs, prayers, and didactic lessons and texts. Written resources begin with Otto Stoll's word-lists in *Zur Ethnographie der Republik Guatemala* (1884) and run through the Smithsonian Institution's 1975 dictionary of some 30,000 Tzotzil–English entries to the INALI writing norm of 2011 and a Tzotzil New Testament.

## Language & religion

Tzotzil is a Mayan language of the Tzeltalan sub-branch, most closely related to Tzeltal; with Tzeltal and Ch'ol it is among the most widely spoken languages of Chiapas after Spanish, and many speakers are monolingual. Six dialects are named for their regions: Chamula, Zinacantán, San Andrés Larráinzar, Huixtán, Chenalhó and Venustiano Carranza. Word order is verb–object–subject and agreement is ergative–absolutive; counted nouns take numeral classifiers matched to the shape of the thing counted. Some primary schools and a secondary school in central Chiapas teach in Tzotzil. Religion is syncretic: under Catholic influence the Sun, "Our Father Heat," has been identified with God the Father or Jesus Christ and the Moon, "Our Holy Mother," with the Virgin Mary, while Venus is "Sweeper of the Path" because it precedes the Sun. In 2013 Pope Francis approved Tzotzil translations of the prayers for Mass and the sacraments.
