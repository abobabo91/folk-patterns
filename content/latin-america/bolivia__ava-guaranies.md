## Overview

The Ava Guaraní, formerly known as Chiriguanos or Chiriguano Indians, are an Indigenous people of southeastern Bolivia, in the foothills between the high Andes and the Altiplano and the flat plains of the Gran Chaco. They lived mostly at elevations between 1,000 metres and 2,000 metres, in a region of steep ridges and deep river valleys. They speak Ava Guarani and Eastern Bolivian Guaraní. A 2001 census counted 81,011 Guaraní, mostly Chiriguanos, over 15 years of age living in Bolivia; a 2010 census counted 18,000 Ava Guarani in Argentina. Their history includes resistance to the Inca Empire, the Spanish Empire, and independent Bolivia, culminating in their subjugation in 1892.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The Chiriguanos initially lived in very large longhouses in villages. They later came to live in small dispersed settlements of individual houses, probably for defense.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that the Chiriguanos acquired horses and guns from the Spanish, but that their preferred method of fighting was on foot with bow and arrow.

## Music & performance

They record that the Chiriguanos resisted missionary attempts to convert them to Christianity until the 19th century, and that a Jesuit mission in 1767 had 268 Chiriguano converts.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention the Ghost Dance in the United States and the Boxer Rebellion in China only as comparisons for the movement led by Apiaguaiki Tumpa, not as Ava Guaraní traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe religious and political events connected with resistance to Christianity and missionary authority. The 1892 rebellion began in January at the mission of Santa Rosa de Cuevo and was led by Chapiaguasu, who styled himself Apiaguaiki Tumpa and said that he had been sent to earth to save the Chiriguanos from Christianity and the Franciscan missionaries. The sources also state that the Chiriguanos were impervious to missionary conversion attempts until the 19th century, while Franciscan missions achieved greater success beginning in 1845.

## Foodways

The Chiriguanos were agricultural people who cultivated maize and other crops. In the 18th century, Chiriguano farmers who grew corn came into conflict with Spanish and Mestizo ranchers whose cattle encroached upon their territory; the cattle destroyed settlements and corn fields, while the Chiriguanos killed cattle and often ranchers. During a drought from 1839 to 1841, Chiriguano maize crops failed and the people increased raids on cattle herds, both eating the cattle and killing them to halt the advances of Hispanic ranchers.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record the name *Candire*, described as a mythical “land without evil,” rich with gold and other wealth, which may have attracted some Ava Guaraní peoples toward the eastern Andes during the Spanish conquest in the 1530s. The sources also describe the 1892 movement led by Apiaguaiki Tumpa, who claimed to have been sent to earth to save the Chiriguanos from Christianity and the Franciscan missionaries. No contemporary literary preservation project is documented.

## Language & religion

The Ava Guaraní speak Ava Guarani and Eastern Bolivian Guaraní, languages associated with the wider Guarani-speaking population of Paraguay, Argentina, and Brazil. Eastern Bolivian Guaraní was spoken by 33,000 people in Bolivia, 15,000 in Argentina, and a few hundred in Paraguay. The Chiriguanos called themselves *ava*, meaning humans. The sources state that missionaries described the Chiriguanos as lacking religion and government, but this description appeared in Spanish accounts that justified wars and enslavement. Jesuit, Dominican, and Franciscan missions attempted conversion; Franciscan missions enjoyed greater success from 1845, partly because some Chiriguanos sought protection from internal disputes, Creole ranchers and settlers, the Bolivian government, and other Indigenous peoples.
