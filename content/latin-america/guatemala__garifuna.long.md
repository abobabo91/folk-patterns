## Overview

The Garifuna (plural *Garínagu*) are an Afro-Indigenous people of mixed free African and Amerindian ancestry whose homeland is the Caribbean island of Saint Vincent, known to them as *Yurumein*. They descend from Indigenous Arawak and Kalinago (Island Carib) people and Afro-Caribbean people, and they speak Garifuna, an Arawakan language. After the Second Carib War the British deported more than 5,000 of them in 1797; about 2,500 survived the voyage and landed on Roatán, Honduras, on 12 April 1797, and from there the population spread along the Caribbean coast of Central America — north into Belize and Guatemala, south into Nicaragua. Garifuna organisations in the United States estimated the population at roughly 400,000 in 2011, most of them in Honduras and the United States; Guatemala has about 20,000 speakers of the language. By 1981 some 65,000 Garifuna lived in fifty-four fishing villages across Guatemala, Belize and Nicaragua. In 2001 UNESCO proclaimed Garifuna language, dance and music a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The objects this atlas shows are Garifuna pieces collected in Belize and held by the British Museum; their catalogue records are the only textile documentation in the sources used here, and they describe garments and plaited fibre work rather than loom weaving.

**Gudu** — a woman's red gingham skirt, worn with a wrap-around tie belt; the catalogue gives the Garinagu name *gudu* for the form.

**Dashiki** — a short-sleeved smock or tunic, made in men's and boys' versions, called *dashiki* in Garinagu. The boys' examples are green gingham with white trim at the neck, sleeves and back and white applied pockets edged in green gingham; the men's is mint green with white trim on chest, sleeves, back and pockets. Both are slit at the sides.

**Gown** — a v-necked shift dress, called *gown* in Garinagu, with a white top and a red gingham skirt, pairing the same checked cloth as the *gudu* with a plain bodice.

**Jippy jappa plaiting** — a hat plaited from "jippy jappa" palm-like fibre, with a rounded crown, open-work sides, a band of plaited triangles around the crown, an open-work rim and a blue silk ribbon tied at the base of the crown.

**Tie-tie basketry** — household vessels plaited from "tie-tie" and "basket tie-tie" palm fibre: a flat circular sifter with a wooden rim wrapped in fibre, and a wicker-work basket with a round flat bottom, vertical sides and a fibre-wrapped rim.

**Dance head-dress** — a festival head-dress built from cardboard: a folded strip sewn into a circle with an arch at the front, covered in white cotton printed with pink stripes and blue hearts, hung with multicoloured tissue-paper flowers, with four feathers (one red, one yellow, two green) on the arch and cloth ties at each side.

**Motif vocabulary.** Gingham check in red and green; plaited triangles and open-work bands on fibre hats; the Garifuna flag in yellow, white and black; a toucan on a branch; a thatched building with drums hanging from the eaves; a named drummer, Ronald Ray McDonald, shown playing before a house; the re-enactment performed on Garifuna Settlement Day; pink stripes and blue hearts on printed cotton; the painted inscription "Warasa, Belize".

### Clothing & dress

Everyday and festival dress in the collected set is built from checked cotton cloth cut into a small number of named forms: the *gudu* skirt with its wrap-around tie belt, the *gown* shift dress, and the *dashiki* tunic worn by men and boys, trimmed in white at neck, sleeves, back and pockets and slit at the sides for movement. Headgear includes the plaited "jippy jappa" hat finished with a blue silk ribbon. Masquerade dress is sharply distinct from daily wear: wire-mesh dance-masks with solid metal borders, moulded as human faces and painted pink with black eyebrows, moustache and beard, white eyes outlined in black and red lips, are held on by a blue cotton strip sewn to the top corners. A cloth doll of a male dancer records the rest of that costume — a white cotton tunic and pants, a pink silk ribbon crossed over the chest, a green ribbon at the knees topped with white cloth sewn with white shells, a pink scarf wrapped round the head, a fuchsia wire-mesh mask, and a yellow, green and burgundy striped bandana set with a red glass jewel, two silver foil circles and feathers glued inside. The sources do not describe footwear.

### Architecture

The sources used here do not describe Garifuna house forms, construction or ornament. The single architectural image among them is painted rather than built: a calabash gourd drinking bowl carries a scene of a thatched building with drums hanging from the eaves, inscribed "Warasa, Belize" around the rim, and a painted *sisira* shaker shows a drummer performing in front of a house.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

No pottery appears in the sources; the household vessels are gourd and wood. **Calabash bowls** serve as drinking vessels and as painted surfaces, one bearing the thatched-house-and-drums scene described above. A **grater** is carved from a single rectangular block of mahogany, slightly curved along its long sides, with a knob at one end for a handle, a hole pierced through it for hanging, and one face inlaid with stones. Plaited palm fibre supplies the sifter and the wicker-work basket. Metal enters mainly through the masquerade: the dance-masks are wire mesh bordered in solid metal, and the drums are tensioned with nails, vine, twisted polyethylene rope and wooden dowels. The segunda drum shell is hollowed Santa María hardwood; other drums are hollowed logs with deer-skin heads.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used here do not cover Garifuna jewelry, tattooing or hair practice. The only adornment they record is on costume: white shells sewn to the cloth above a dancer's knee-ribbons, and a red glass jewel with two silver foil circles fixed to a striped head bandana.

## Music & performance

Garifuna music fuses African, Arawak and Kalinago elements, and was proclaimed with the language and dance by UNESCO in 2001. Drums carry it. The principal instrument is the **segunda** (bass drum), called *Segundo* in the museum record: a shell of hollowed Santa María hardwood, the head of white-tailed deer skin held by a circle of vine, tied down with twisted polyethylene rope and tightened with wooden dowels threaded through the rope, with knotted string crossing the head to alter its timbre. Other drums are cylinders hollowed from logs with deer skin nailed to a wooden hoop and sinew or wire stretched across the head. Drum shells are made from mahogany or mayflower wood and headed with deer, goat or peccary skin; during construction they are rubbed with cassava wine and blessed with the smoke of *buwe*, a sacred herb — a combination that marks the instrument as both African and Amerindian in descent. An ensemble of drummers is a *dangbu*. The second instrument family is the shaker: **maracas** made from hollowed round gourds on nailed wooden handles, and the *sisira*, a gourd shaker filled with canna seeds and painted with the Garifuna flag, a toucan, or a portrait of a drummer. Secular genres are *punta*, *paranda* and *punta rock*, with songs tied to work, play, dance, prayer and ritual. Ritual drumming in the *dügü* is played in triple meter with song and dance, and most *dügü* song texts address ancestors as female; older women predominate as organisers, ritual participants and composers. Named performers include Sofía Blanco, Pen Cayetano, Paul Nabor, Aurelio Martínez, Andy Palacio and Umalali; Palacio's album *Watina* and *Umalali: The Garifuna Women's Project* were released on the Belizean label Stonetree Records.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name Garifuna dance as a UNESCO-proclaimed element and record its costume in detail, but do not name individual dances. What they document is a masquerade: wire-mesh masks painted as pale, bearded male faces; a flower-and-feather cardboard head-dress tied under the chin; and a full male dancer's dress of white tunic and pants crossed with silk ribbons, shell-trimmed knee bands, a head scarf and a jewelled striped bandana, preserved in a cloth doll. A segunda drum painted with a scene of the re-enactment performed on Garifuna Settlement Day places this costumed performance in a commemorative drama of the 1797 arrival. Ritual dance also belongs to the *dügü*, where drumming accompanied by song and dance is held to calm the person possessed by the ancestral spirits (*gubida*).

## Festivals & rituals

**Garifuna Settlement Day**, 19 November, is named in the museum record and is marked by a re-enactment performance, painted onto the side of a segunda drum beside the Garifuna flag. The ritual calendar proper is organised around the ancestors. **Adügürühani**, also called *dügü*, is a healing ceremony held when a person falls ill: a shaman consults the *gubida*, the ancestral spirits held to have inflicted the illness, and drumming in triple meter with song and dance is performed over several days. **Arairaguni** is an invocation to determine the nature of an illness. **Amuyadahani** is a ritual in which family members make offerings to their ancestors. **Achuguhani**, or *chugú*, is the "feeding of the dead". All sit within a wider body of practice honouring the ancestors and *Bungiu* (God), also called *Sunti Gabafu* ("All Powerful"), with the *buyei* — the shaman — at the head of all Garifuna traditional practice. Of life-cycle rites the sources record only beliefs around birth: a baby boy and his father are held to be spiritually attached, and the father is expected to set aside some of his duties to be with the child, a period in which women take on more authority in the household. In 2005 the First Garifuna Summit was held in the Corn Islands, Nicaragua, with participation by other Central American governments.

## Foodways

The sources used here do not describe Garifuna cooking or dishes. What they do record is the food economy and a few ingredients. Garifuna communities long relied on farming for income, but much of that land was taken by fruit companies in the twentieth century and later sold on to mestizo farmers; men now work for foreign-owned companies collecting timber and chicle for export, or as fishermen, and by 1981 the Central American population was concentrated in fifty-four fishing villages. Three food materials appear in the object records and ritual descriptions: cassava, in the form of the cassava wine with which new drums are rubbed; canna seeds, used as the rattling fill of the *sisira* shaker; and the calabash gourd, hollowed into drinking bowls. A mahogany grater inlaid with stones is the one food-processing tool in the collection.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources treat Garifuna oral tradition chiefly as the vehicle of the people's own history: oral tradition traces their African ancestry to West and Central African groups, and the oral history of the Garínagu contradicts the French colonial documents on how African and Carib families joined. Ritual song is the documented verbal art — *dügü* texts composed largely by older women and addressed to grandmothers and great-grandmothers, even when the ceremony honours a man. One moral formula is recorded: *Au Bun, Amürü Nu*, "I for you, you for me", a communal principle of mutual care, reciprocity and collective responsibility, holding that personal and communal well-being are inseparable. Contemporary production includes the first feature film in the Garifuna language, *Garifuna in Peril* (2012), and the work of the Garifuna Heritage Foundation and the Garifuna Research Institute. The sources name no epic cycle.

## Language & religion

Garifuna, also called Karif, belongs to the Arawakan family, unusually for a language spoken outside northern South America, and descends from Island Carib (Kalinago in name, largely Arawak in substance). It carries heavy borrowing from Carib and from French, English, Dutch and Spanish; numerals from three upward are French-derived and vigesimal (*ürüwa* 3, *gádürü* 4, *wein* 20, *gádürü wein* 80). Word order is verb–subject–object, and the language is agglutinative, marking possession with personal prefixes (*ibágari* "life", *n-ibágari* "my life"). A diglossic split survives in a handful of words used differently by men and women, the men's forms generally Cariban (*au* "I") and the women's Arawakan (*nugía*). Almost all Garínagu are bilingual or multilingual, usually with Spanish or English as a first language. Most Garínagu have been Catholic since early encounters with Jesuits, Dominicans and Catholic colonial powers, alongside the ancestor practices led by the *buyei*.
