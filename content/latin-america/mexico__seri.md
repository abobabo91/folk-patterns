## Overview

The Seri, or Comcaac, are an Indigenous people of Sonora, Mexico, whose principal communities are Punta Chueca (*Socaaix*) and El Desemboque (*Haxöl Iihom*) on the mainland coast of the Gulf of California. Their traditional territory also included Tiburón Island (*Tahejöc*) and San Esteban Island (*Cofteecöl*, sometimes *Hast*). The Seri maintain a close relationship with the sea and the land and have preserved their language and cultural practices despite contact with Spanish and Mexican cultures. Their language, *cmiique iitom*, is generally considered a language isolate. The population is small: the Seri-language article gives a figure of between 716 and 900 speakers. Fishing, ironwood carving, basket making, necklace work, songs, oral history, and knowledge of the local flora and fauna are central subjects in the available descriptions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Ironwood carving** — Traditional ironwood carvings are identified as one of the main sources of income for the Seri.

**Basket making** — Elaborate basket making is described as an important Seri economic activity.

**Necklace work** — Necklace work is listed with fishing, ironwood carving, and basket making among the principal income-generating activities.

### Clothing & dress

 They do mention that traditional houses and festivals are connected with the material life of the Seri, but they provide no account of dress associated with those occasions.

### Architecture

Seri villages mainly contain houses made of concrete blocks with concrete or asbestos roofs. These houses generally have a kitchen, dining room, bathroom, and one or two bedrooms. The federal and state governments promoted and supported this type of housing between 1974 and 1984. Some houses may also be made of cardboard.

In fishing areas, traditional shelters remain possible to find. These are built from an ocotillo frame (*Fouquieria splendens*) covered with grasses and other materials; loggerhead shells were traditionally among the materials used. Each shelter houses a single family and suits a nomadic lifestyle. The same construction is used during traditional festivals.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do describe everyday and economic objects connected with fishing and domestic life, including the *panga*, from which fish may be requested under the customary right called *canoaa an hant cooit*. Traditional shelters use ocotillo frames, grasses, and, historically, loggerhead shells. Ironwood carvings, baskets, and necklaces are also identified as important products.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Necklace work is identified as one of the main sources of income for the Seri. They provide no information about tattoos, henna, hair practices, or other forms of body adornment.

## Music & performance

Seri music has preserved much Indigenous material and is described as an important distinction between the Seri and almost all other ethnic groups in Mexico. Instrumental music and most traditional instruments are less commonly used, while songs remain central. Foot drums and rattling gourds may accompany quick, repetitive singing, and dancers may provide rhythm and percussion.

Singing is practiced by Seri of many ages and describes the surrounding world. Songs can preserve knowledge of the ecological environment and the ethnobotanical culture that helped the Seri live under difficult environmental conditions. Because few written records were kept, songs functioned as repositories of history, beliefs, and cultural knowledge. Songs are often repeated, and many are sung in quantities of four.

The sources name several song genres: *icoosyat*, “songs of the giants”; *iquimooni*, “songs of victory”; *icooha*, “songs of mourning”; *hacáatol cöicoos*, “songs of the shaman”; *cmaam cöicoos*, “love songs to women”; *icocooxa*, lullabies; *xepe án cöicoos* and *hehe án cöicoos*, songs of nature; and *icoos icooit*, songs for dance. Songs for dance are the most visible in public cultural events but are described as the least typical type of Seri song. The Seri are reluctant to record many songs of mourning because they are considered personal or unsuitable outside their proper context.

## Dance & theatre

 They state that dancers may provide rhythms and percussion for songs and identify *icoos icooit* as songs for dance, which can be heard at cultural events. No further description of dance movements, costumes, ceremonial distinctions, or theatrical performance is provided.

## Festivals & rituals

The Seri did not develop a complex system of religious governance. Their interpretation of the world, rites, festivals, and other cultural expressions are closely connected with nature and with the biological and social dimensions of the group.

The main traditional rites are linked to the onset of puberty and death. Traditional shelters made from grasses and an ocotillo frame are used for traditional festivals.

Songs and stories concern the sea, animals, and the ancient feats of heroes and warriors. The sources state that the Seri were not formally evangelized during the colonial period and therefore did not acquire the Catholic elements found in many other Indigenous groups. In the Seri area there are no Catholic churches or priests. Two Protestant temples of the Apostolic Church of the Faith of Jesus Christ are present, and many people in the community belong to that church.

## Foodways

The Seri have historically depended on marine resources and the flora and fauna of their arid environment. Fishing, especially the harvesting of crabs and scallops, is identified as a principal source of income. The Seri are also described as the only people known to have harvested the seeds of the maritime seagrass species *Zostera marina* before their nutritional value was discovered in recent times.

A customary system of food distribution is called *quiimosim*. It gives every community member the right to request part of the food being consumed in the community without a prior invitation. Another system, *canoaa an hant cooit*, is the right to ask for fish from any *panga* returning from the sea. Assets are also shared between families through a more complex system.

The sources state that the Seri never practiced agriculture because their territory was unsuitable for it. Their diet and patterns of consumption changed with the former nomadic lifestyle, and diabetes is identified among the resulting health problems.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Seri maintain an extensive oral tradition that preserves their history and culture. Songs and stories revolve around the sea, animals, and the ancient feats of heroes and warriors. Songs preserve knowledge of ecological conditions, ethnobotany, history, beliefs, and cultural practices. Only a very small part of this oral tradition has appeared in written form.

 They state that a growing body of Seri literature is being published, including earlier stories that were recorded, transcribed, and published and are now being re-edited. New material is also being prepared by several writers. Essays by three Seri writers appear in an anthology of Native American literature published by the University of Nebraska Press, and recent literature has appeared as Android applications, often with accompanying audio.

The Constitution of Mexico has been translated in its entirety into Seri by the Instituto Nacional de Lenguas Indígenas.

## Language & religion

Seri, called *cmiique iitom* within the community, is spoken in Punta Chueca and El Desemboque. It is generally considered a language isolate, although attempts have been made to connect it with the theoretical Hokan family, the Yuman family, and the extinct Salinan language of California. No concrete evidence for such connections has been found. The language is viable in daily community life but is vulnerable: its use is reduced in primary and secondary school, parts of local church services, and communication with Spanish speakers outside the community.

The Seri were formerly associated with three mutually intelligible dialects. The dialect of the northern, southern, Tiburón Island, and desert bands is the only surviving variety and is the ancestor of modern Seri. The dialect of the *Xnaamotat* band and that of the *Xiica hast ano coii* band are extinct.

Seri is written in the Latin script. Its alphabet was developed in the 1950s by Edward W. and Mary B. Moser and later revised by a committee of Seri men and women working with Stephen Marlett. The sources describe religious practices as closely related to nature, rites, festivals, and social life rather than to a complex religious governance system.
