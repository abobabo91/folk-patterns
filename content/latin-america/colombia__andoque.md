## Overview

The Andoque, also known as Andoke, are an Indigenous people of Colombia who live along the Aduche tributary of the Japurá River. They refer to themselves as *Pʌʌsiʌʌ́ hʌ*, meaning “People of the Axe.” The Andoque language is spoken by a few hundred people and is in decline. There were 10,000 speakers in 1908, compared with 370 a century later, of whom at most 50 were monolingual. The remaining speakers live in four residential areas in the region of the Anduche River, downstream from Araracuara, Solano, Caquetá, Colombia. Andoque is treated as a language isolate by some linguists, while other classifications place it within the Witótoan family. Its documented folk-culture significance includes sacred plants, the Yuruparí ritual, and oral accounts concerning bees, wasps, tapirs, and ancestral beings.

## Music & performance

The Yuruparí ritual is described as concerning the Andoque people’s transcendent vision of cosmology. In this ritual, men initiate themselves by “dying” and being “reborn” as members of the tribe.

## Festivals & rituals

The documented Andoque ritual is *Yuruparí*. It concerns a transcendent vision of cosmology and makes men initiates “die” and then be “reborn” as members of the tribe.

## Foodways

One oral account says that tapirs of various colors originated from “the star people, who are bees and wasps,” after they ate the body of a honey-drinking old man who “fell into a trap” dug by his own son.

## Oral tradition & literature

Andoque oral history includes an account in which various bee species originated from the nasal bone of Heron-of-the-Center, who was consumed by fire while wearing a jaguar-skin. Another account explains the origin of tapirs of various colors through “the star people, who are bees and wasps,” who ate the body of a honey-drinking old man after he fell into a trap dug by his own son. The sources also identify the study “Bees and Wasps: Ethno-Entomological Notions and Myths among the Andoke of the Caquetá River,” by Fabio Jara.

## Language & religion

Andoque is spoken by a few hundred Andoque people in Colombia and is in decline. It is no longer spoken in Peru, and most speakers have shifted to Spanish. The language has been classified differently: Kaufman’s 2007 Bora–Witótoan stock includes it in the Witótoan family, while Richard Aschmann considers it an isolate. It may be related to the extinct Urequena language, also called Urekena or Arequena, which is known from a single 19th-century wordlist. Andoque has ten consonants and nine vowel qualities in one analysis, while other studies report different numbers. Its vowels have low or high phonological tones, and its grammar includes noun classifiers, case suffixes, evidentials, and the focus marker *-nokó*. Religious culture values “sacred plants” and the Yuruparí ritual.
