## Overview

The Tzʼutujil are an Indigenous people and one of the Maya ethnic groups of Guatemala. Approximately 100,000 Tzʼutujil live in the area around Lake Atitlán, especially in San Juan La Laguna, San Pablo La Laguna, San Marcos La Laguna, San Pedro La Laguna, Santiago Atitlán, Panabaj, and Tzanchaj, with fewer in San Lucas Tolimán. Their pre-Columbian capital was Chuitinamit, and their earlier capital Chiaa stood on the hill of Chuitinamit. The Tzʼutujil language belongs to the Mayan language family and has Eastern and Western dialects. Weaving, traditional songs, farming, and Atiteco religious practices are important elements of their folk culture. Tzʼutujil culture also includes cofradía organizations, spiritual traditions involving Nawals, and distinctive textile symbolism.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Backstrap-loom weaving** — Tzʼutujil women use a backstrap loom. Traditionally, they used *x’cajcoj zut*, a cloth made from brown cotton material; *x’cajoj* refers to the brown cotton, while *zut* indicates that the cloth is longer than it is wide.

**Contemporary Tzʼutujil weavings** — Women increasingly use commercially produced yarns, producing a greater variety of colors. The weavings consistently contain a yellow stripe through the center and six silk tassels.

**San Juan dyeing** — The weavers of San Juan are among the Indigenous artisans who make their own dyes for yarns, using locally grown plants. San Juan artists also use the international genre of *Arte Naif* to express cultural traditions, beliefs, ceremonies, and daily activities.

**Motif vocabulary.** Yellow stripe — the “Abundance Road,” referring to the sun’s path between 6 AM and 6 PM; corner tassels — the sun rising and setting during the solstices; long sides of the cloth — the sun’s path between noon and midnight; *yujkut* — the rope in the loom, symbolically representing the umbilical cord.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe Tzʼutujil weaving. They state that women use a backstrap loom and traditionally worked with *x’cajcoj zut* fabric. The cloth is brown when made from *x’cajoj*, although contemporary commercially produced yarns provide a wider range of colors. Tzʼutujil weavings retain a yellow central stripe and six silk tassels. Four tassels are placed at the corners, with one tassel on each shorter side.

### Architecture

 They do describe Chiaa, an earlier capital established on the hill of Chuitinamit near Lake Atitlán, and state that the Tzʼutujil pre-Columbian capital near Santiago Atitlán was Chuitinamit. In the traditional religious conception of Santo Mundo, the center is understood as a central plaza and “the Heart,” an intersection of four roads containing a sixteenth-century church.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum catalogue records supplied for this atlas contain no objects. They mention looms, loom ropes, and loom sticks in connection with weaving.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 They mention silk tassels attached to woven cloth. Four tassels are placed at the corners of the cloth, and one tassel is placed on each shorter side.

## Music & performance

Traditional songs, known as *b’ix*, are historically and religiously significant among the Tzʼutujil. They are sung by an *ajb’ix*, meaning songman, and serve as a means of connecting with the spiritual world. The songs express gratitude to spirits, protect people from illness and witchcraft, and are used to cause pain to enemies. Courting songs also exist; these compare sexual organs and desires with the way rain fertilizes crops. Traditional songs are connected with prayer, sacrifices, offerings, spiritual protection, courtship, and relations with the Nawals. The sources state that weaving and traditional songs have historically had religious significance, and that Tzʼutujil cultural practices continue to include traditional Atiteco religious forms.

## Dance & theatre

 They mention ceremonies and daily activities as subjects represented by San Juan artists working in *Arte Naif*.

## Festivals & rituals

Tzʼutujil religious life combines traditional practices with institutions introduced during the colonial period. In the sixteenth century, Franciscan Friars Francisco de la Parra and Pedro de Betanços moved the capital to Santiago Atitlán and constructed a monastery. In 1533, the Spanish implemented cofradía organizations in Guatemala as a means of Christianization. Cofradías were dedicated to particular Catholic saints, but Tzʼutujil participants also engaged in ritual activity that followed Indigenous practices.

Traditional customs are associated with Nawals, or “Ancient Ones,” understood as ancient Tzʼutujil people who gained divine status. Their guardian spirit was Old Mam, described as a trickster deity who taught the customs now belonging to the Tzʼutujil. Through prayer, sacrifices, and offerings, people seek health, luck, and good weather for agriculture. Since the arrival of the Spanish, Nawals have been worshipped in cofradías. They describe solstices in relation to the sun’s rising and setting and explain that the central plaza and four corners of Santo Mundo correspond to the center and four corners of the cosmos. They also describe a cyclical concept of life, *Jaloj-K’exoj*, in which individual change and transition between generations are represented through the life cycle of maize.

## Foodways

Agriculture remains important to Tzʼutujil life. Many Tzʼutujil continue traditional farming of coffee and maize, the two main crops identified for the region. They also farm beans, fruit, and vegetables. Farming takes place on volcanic plains, and crops are traded for commodities from Indigenous groups in other towns. In the religious conception of Santo Mundo, the Holy World is understood as a body that carries water and grows trees, flowers, and food. Santo Mundo is also seen as a womb that holds the Maize God. The sources connect maize with the cycle of human life: when a maize plant dies, it scatters seed, corresponding to human birth; its growth symbolizes aging, and the scattering of seed begins the process again.

## Oral tradition & literature

 Traditional songs known as *b’ix* connect singers with the spiritual world and include religious, protective, hostile, and courting functions.

The Tzʼutujil language article states that, as of 2012, the Community Library Rijaʼtzuul Naʼooj in San Juan La Laguna featured storytelling for children in Tzʼutujil. Bilingual children’s books were also available. The language article lists grammatical and dictionary works, including *Tzutujil Grammar*, *Rukeemiik ja Tzʼutujiil Chiiʼ: Gramática tzʼutujiil*, and *Diccionario Tzʼutujil*.

## Language & religion

Tzʼutujil is a Mayan language spoken south of Lake Atitlán in Guatemala and is closely related to Kaqchikel and Kʼicheʼ. The 2002 census found that 60,000 people spoke Tzʼutujil as their mother tongue. The language has Eastern and Western dialects. Most Tzʼutujil speakers also speak Spanish as a second language, although many older people and people in remote areas do not speak Spanish.

Traditional Tzʼutujil religion includes Santo Mundo, the Holy World and cosmos, and spirits believed to control nature, other spirits, and human destinies. Jawal spirits include guardian spirits called *chalbeij* and Spirit Lords called Martins. The highest spirit is known as Dioʼs. Catholic saints, or “Santos,” are understood in cofradías as manifestations of the Spirit Lords. Some Tzʼutujil also practice Evangelical Protestantism or Roman Catholicism.
