## Overview

The Kiliwa (*Ko’leeu*) are an Indigenous people of Mexico living in northern Baja California. Historically, their territory lay between the Cochimí to the south and the Paipai to the north, extending from San Felipe on the Gulf of California to San Quintín on the Pacific coast. They currently live in the Ensenada Municipality, including settlements such as Arroyo de León, Valle San Matías, Ensenada, Guadalupe, Valle de la Trinidad, and Rancho las Pinzas. Their traditional language is Kiliwa, a member of the Yuman family. A 2020 census recorded 76 people reporting Kiliwa as their language, while a 2018 count found only four speakers remaining. Kiliwa culture is documented through hunting and gathering, seasonal mobility, distinctive household technologies, life-cycle ceremonies, music, games, and oral narratives.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Kiliwa clothing was generally limited. Men generally lacked clothing, while women wore deer-hide aprons and reed caps. Both sexes wore sandals made from agave fiber. Rabbitskin blankets provided warmth, and capes made from human hair were used in ceremonies. Cradles were used to transport infants. The sources therefore document materials and uses.

### Architecture

Kiliwa structures included semi-subterranean houses made with willow poles and thatching, as well as ramadas and sweat houses. The sources describe the group as seasonally mobile and state that its traditional material culture was not highly elaborate.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Kiliwa processing equipment included manos and metates for grinding seeds, wooden drills and hearths for making fire, and pottery and basketry for cooking, storing, and transporting. Hunting equipment included willow bows, arrows with carrizo reed shafts, wooden foreshafts, stone points, wooden throwing sticks, and nets made from agave fiber.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They do record that human hair capes were used in ceremonies and that boys’ initiation included the nose-piercing ceremony called *mipípŭsá*.

## Music & performance

Kiliwa music was produced through singing and through instruments including flutes, rattles, clappers, and bullroarers. Music formed part of social recreation and ceremonial life.

Social recreation included several games: racing with balls, shinny, top spinning, archery, dice, a guessing game, and, most importantly, peón. The sources identify these activities.

The available material therefore documents a general instrumental and vocal tradition, together with games that formed part of community recreation.

## Dance & theatre

They mention singing, instruments, games, ceremonies, and oral narratives.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record seasonal subsistence activities: in the fall, harvesting acorns and pine nuts from higher-elevation parts of Kiliwa territory was a major activity. Treks to San Felipe were made to harvest fish and shellfish and to collect salt. These activities are presented as subsistence practices rather than as named festivals.

Life-cycle ceremonies are more fully documented. Birth involved taboos or special requirements and was primarily a private family matter; the father observed a couvade. Boys’ initiation took place for groups of boys aged around 15 years and included *mipípŭsá*, the nose-piercing ceremony, along with other activities extending over two months. Girls’ initiation at the time of first menstruation involved being baked in ashes for five days, washed in cold water for five days, and required to observe prescriptions and taboos.

Marriage involved little ceremony beyond the groom giving gifts to the bride’s parents. Marrying up to two wives was permitted, and either party could obtain a divorce. Death was the focus of the most elaborate Kiliwa ceremonies, both immediately after death and in later collective mourning observances. Formal actions included talking to the dead and burning the deceased person’s possessions.

Shamans were believed to cure or cause illness and injuries through magic, preside over some religious ceremonies, transform themselves into animals or birds, and bring rain.

## Foodways

Aboriginal Kiliwa subsistence was based on hunting and gathering natural animals and plants rather than agriculture. At least two dozen plants were food resources, while other plants were used for medicine, construction, or craft products. Pit-roasted agave, identified as mezcal and called *maal*, was the most important plant food. Acorns and pine nuts were harvested in the fall from higher-elevation portions of Kiliwa territory.

Rabbits and deer were the most important animal foods. Other hunted animals included pronghorn antelope, bighorn sheep, mountain lions, many small mammal species, birds, reptiles, fish, and shellfish. Jackrabbits and quail were hunted communally by driving them into nets. Journeys to San Felipe provided fish, shellfish, and salt. Wild honey became another highly valued food resource during the historic period.

Cooking and processing used pottery and basketry, as well as manos and metates for grinding seeds. Crop growing and stock raising were introduced during the historic period.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kiliwa oral literature includes myths, legends, tales, and oral histories. The creation myth differs conspicuously from those of other Yuman-speaking groups. The Kiliwa name for the creator is *Metipá*, a figure whose name recalls similar figures in other Yuman myths.

The sources identify narratives recorded from Kiliwa speakers, including myths narrated by Emiliano Uchurte and José Espinosa around 1928–1936, and myths and legends narrated by Rufino Ochurte and Braulio Espinosa after 1966. Mauricio J. Mixco published *Kiliwa Texts*, *Kiliwa Texts: “When I Have Donned My Crest of Stars”*, and *Kiliwa Mountain Sheep Traditions*. Peveril Meigs recorded narratives in *The Kiliwa Indians of Lower California*. Jesús Ángel Ochoa Zazueta also published an account of Kiliwa traditions, although the source notes that its authenticity has been questioned by Mixco.

## Language & religion

Kiliwa, also called Kiliwi, Ko’lew, or Quiligua, is a Yuman language spoken in Baja California in the far northwest of Mexico. In Kiliwa it is called *Koleeu ñaja*. The language is considered endangered. A 2000 census reported 52 speakers, a 2018 count found four speakers remaining, and 76 people reported their language as Kiliwa in a 2020 census. Kiliwa was historically oral and unwritten; it is now written with a modified Roman alphabet.

Kiliwa is the southernmost representative of the Yuman family and is described as the most distinct from the remaining languages, which constitute Core Yuman. The sources describe historical bilingualism in Paipai and a debated account of the separation of Kiliwa from Core Yuman, with glottochronology suggesting a date of about 2,000–3,000 years ago.

Kiliwa spiritual life included shamans, religious ceremonies, beliefs concerning magical cures, transformation into animals or birds, and the ability to bring rain. Traditional narratives describe the creator *Metipá*.
