## Overview

The Chochos, formerly Chochones and known in Chocho as *Ngiwa*, are an Indigenous people of the Mexican state of Oaxaca. *Ngiwa* is their name for themselves. The Spanish and English names “Chochos” and “Chochones” derive from the Nahuatl exonym *Chochon*, plural *Chochontin*. The Mixtec term is *tay tocuii*, also spelled *tocuij* or *tocuiy*. Chocho belongs to the Popolocan branch of the Oto-Manguean language family. In 1998, it had 770 speakers. Chochos also speak Spanish, described in the source as the dominant language of Mexico. The sources identify the group, its names, its language family, its language distribution, and its population figure.

## Language & religion

Chocho, also called Chocholtec, Chocholteco, Chochotec, Chochon, or Ngigua, is spoken in Mexico and belongs to the Popolocan branch of the Oto-Manguean language family. In 1998, it had 770 speakers according to the Ethnologue survey cited in the source. Chochos also speak Spanish.

Chocho is spoken in communities of Oaxaca including San Miguel Chicahua, Teotongo, San Miguel Huautla, Santa Magdalena Jicotlán, San Pedro Nopala, San Miguel Tequixtepec, San Francisco Teopan, Ocotlán, Santa María Nativitas, San Juan Bautista Coixtlahuaca, and San Miguel Tulancingo. The source also names settlements associated with these places, including Llano Seco, El Progreso, El Tecomate, Guadalupe, La Luz, Ocotlán, San Mateo Tlapiltepec, Santiago Tepetlapa, Los Batos, Concepción Buenavista, Santiago Ihuitlán Plumas, Tepelmeme Villa de Morelos, Tlacotepec Plumas, Boquerón, San Antonio Nduayaco, Tierra Colorada, Unión Palo Solo, Barrio Nicolás, Barrio Santiago, El Mirador, El Porvenir, Loma del Tepejillo, Pie del Cordoncillo, Primera Sección (Santa Cruz), San José Monte Verde, San Pedro Buenavista, Santa María Nativitas, El Capulín (Sección Primera), El Tepozón (Sección Segunda), El Zapotal (Sección Tercera), La Mulata, Santa Catarina Ocotlán, Agua Dulce, Buena Vista, El Coatillo, El Español, Gasucho, Loma Larga, Rancho Marino Sánchez, and San Miguel Tulancingo.

Chocho distinguishes low, mid, and high tones. Vowel sounds /a e i/ can have lax allophones of [ɑ ɛ ɪ]. Carol Mock analyzes Chocho as an active–stative language. In an active or voluntary transitive phrase, the agent or subject is marked by the active suffix “-á” and the patient by the inactive clitic “-mī”. In an active or voluntary intransitive phrase, the subject is marked by the same suffix. In an involuntary or inactive intransitive phrase, the subject or patient is marked with the inactive clitic “má”. The source describes this as suggesting a Fluid-S type active language, because some intransitive verbs can use either active person suffixes or the inactive enclitic.
