## Overview

The Chimilas, also called *Ette Ennaka*, are an Indigenous people of the Andes in north-eastern Colombia. At the time of Spanish colonization, they were established through most of the Cesar River basin and its valley, including Valledupar in the Cesar Department, between the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta and the Serranía del Perijá mountain ranges and along the Magdalena River. At the time of the Spanish Conquest, the Ariguaní River valley was the strategic centre of their territory. Their language belongs to the Chibcha language family, and there were estimated to be around 1000 speakers in 1998. Chimila history includes large settlements, a hierarchical society, agriculture, hunting, fishing, mining, goldsmithing, trade, musical rites, and prolonged resistance to the Spanish Empire.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources used mention Chimila settlements. The city of Thamara, identified with present-day Tamalameque, was described by Spanish scribes as a very large city with more than a thousand huts. Another major city was Upari, named after the powerful chief, the cacique Upar.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Chimila social structure included artisans, miners, goldsmiths, and merchants. The sources do describe *Gynerium sagittatum*, a cane harvested for producing bows and arrows, as well as musical instruments including drums, flutes, a guacharaca, and maracas.

## Music & performance

The Chimilas practiced musical rites using drums of many sizes, flutes with a wax tip and between two and five holes, an instrument called *guacharaca*, and maracas. The guacharaca was used to imitate the singing of rufous-vented chachalaca birds. These details identify instruments and a form of bird imitation.

The sources also mention the Vallenato Legend Festival in the “See also” section of the Wikipedia article. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription is supplied for this people or for a Chimila musical tradition.

## Dance & theatre

 They mention musical rites but provide no information about associated movements, choreography, performers, or ceremonial and recreational distinctions.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe Chimila musical rites.

The Spanish Conquest and the later history of resistance are important historical contexts. Around 1720, the Chimilas began a guerrilla war against the Spanish Empire, and violence continued well into the twentieth century. In 1990, a reservation called Issa Oristuna was created.

## Foodways

The Chimilas cultivated maize in a technical manner and, to a lesser extent, other local species of vegetables and fruits for consumption. They also cultivated certain trees near their huts to produce shade and freshen the area.

Fishing and hunting were occupations within Chimila society, alongside artisan work, mining, goldsmithing, and trade.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They do record historical names and explanations connected with Chimila territory. A Chimila cacique lent his name to the city of Chimichagua, while the cacique Upar lent his name to Valledupar through the Spanish expression “valley of Upar.” The name of the Cesar River and Cesar Department is described as an adaptation of the Chimila word *Chet-tzar* or *Zazare*, meaning “calm water,” into Spanish.

## Language & religion

The Chimila language is part of the Chibcha language family, with around 1000 speakers estimated in 1998.

Chimila belief included an immortal figure called Masirguta, and the Chimilas were monotheist believers in the god Narayajana, also referred to as Yao.

The Chimila social order was described as pyramidal, with a working class divided into fishers, hunters, artisans, miners, goldsmiths, and merchants. The sources also state that the Chimilas used Arhuacos as slaves. The Yukpas, living on the Serranía del Perijá mountains, were described as part of the Chimila confederation of tribes.
