## Overview

The Yaqui are an indigenous people of northwestern Mexico whose ceremonial life fuses Jesuit Christianity with older belief without surrendering either. Their culture centers on music, dance and ritual tied to Lenten and Easter observance. The deer dance, held all night, thanks the deer for allowing itself to be killed so people may live. Many Yaqui families continue these ceremonies even while living as migrant farm workers in Arizona.

## Material culture

Yaqui material culture combines pottery, gourd, wood and woven textiles to create both everyday objects and ceremonial regalia.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Yaqui weavers made woollen belts for dancers and decorative shawls.

- **Matachin belt** (*matachin*) — A woollen belt worn by every matachin dancer, woven by community men, marking lifelong membership in the dance society.
- **Chal shawl** (*chal*) — A black woven shawl with white flecks, about 9 feet long and wide enough to wrap the body, with densely plaited warp threads ending in tasselled fringes.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress is little documented, but ceremonial regalia includes carved masks, feather wands and ankle rattles.

- **Pascola mask** (*pascola*) — A carved wooden mask painted black with red and white, tufted with white horsehair pegged into eyebrows, cheeks and chin, worn by deer dancers.
- **Tenabaris** — Ankle rattles made of dried cocoons sewn in pairs along two cords with small stones inside, ending in bright tassels, worn by deer dancers and pahkolam to honour the insect world.
- **Wooden cross** (*corona*) — A head ornament or wooden cross placed before Yaqui houses, particularly during Lent, reflecting Christian adoption.

### Architecture

Traditional Yaqui houses have three rectangular rooms with reed walls and mud-covered roofs.

- **Portal** — The living room of a Yaqui house where much of the day is spent, especially in hot months, with branches worked into its structure to allow air circulation.
- **Eight pueblos** (*Hiakim*) — The eight traditional Yaqui towns established by Jesuits: Bácum, Benem, Cócorit, Huiribis, Pótam, Rahum, Tórim and Vícam, remaining the frame of public life.
- **Arizona barrios** — Yaqui neighborhoods in Tucson like Barrio Libre and Pascua, built from scrap lumber and railroad ties; Guadalupe was established in 1904 and New Pascua in the late 1960s.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Yaqui instruments combine pottery, gourd and wood for music and subsistence.

- **Water drum** — Three parts—a glazed earthenware pot with two handles, a half-gourd bowl floated in it, and a wooden stick to strike with, representing the deer's heartbeat.
- **Rasp** (*hirukiam or kuta*) — A three-part instrument of a half gourd sounding board, a grooved stick laid against it, and a round stick drawn across the grooves, representing the deer's breathing.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Yaqui beadwork and stone ornaments use glass beads and mother-of-pearl in formal necklaces and amulets.

- **Five-row necklace** — A necklace of mostly white glass beads with black and red, carrying six mother-of-pearl ornaments notched to form X patterns, tied at the nape with a red cotton band.
- **Wooden amulet** — A rectangular wooden plaque incised with an X, set with a polished blue stone at centre flanked by white stones, worn on a blue cotton thread.

## Music & performance

Deer songs dominate Yaqui music, mixing Native American and Catholic thought with harp, violin, rasp, drum and rattles.

- **Deer songs** (*maso bwikam*) — Songs sung by deer singers (masobwikamem) in an elevated register resembling elders' speech, falling into two stanza-like parts: u vat weeme sung many times, and the concluding u tonua.
- **Messenger songs** (*suru bwikam*) — A genre of Yaqui song, one of several including corn wine songs, fly songs and coyote songs.
- **Kantoras** — The women singers among Yaqui ritual societies, documented in recordings made by Frances Densmore in 1922.

## Dance & theatre

The deer dance and pascola complex honour the deer, while matachines represent European-influenced ritual.

- **Deer dance** (*maso*) — Usually held all night, thanking and honouring little brother deer (saila maso) for coming from the flower world and allowing itself to be killed so people may live.
- **Pascola** — A deer dancer who performs all night; the word comes from Spanish pascua (Easter), and the role includes acting as ritual clown, giving sermons and hosting the gathering.
- **Pahkolam** — Ritual clowns who dance, give sermons, host the gathering by providing water, joke and perform comedic skits such as pretending to be coyotes.
- **Matachines dance** (*matachin*) — A dance with strong European influence, usually performed inside churches, accompanied by gourd rattles and violins, with a leader called the monarca; men enter this society for life.

## Festivals & rituals

Yaqui ceremonial life turns on Lenten and Easter rituals believed to maintain the world and perfect overlapping worlds.

- **Waresma** — The Yaqui name for Lent, during which many ceremonies are performed by named societies including prayer leaders, church assistants and dancers.
- **Ritual societies** (*aniam*) — Named societies distribute ceremonial labour: Kiyohteis (female church assistants), Vanteareaom (female flag bearers), Anheiltom (angels), Kohtumvre Ya'ura (fariseo society), Kantoras (female singers), Pahko'ola (deer dance society) and others.
- **Flower symbolism** (*sewa*) — Flowers sprang from drops of blood at the crucifixion and are the manifestation of souls; a man greets a close male friend with Haisa sewa? ('How is the flower?').

## Foodways

Old agriculture of beans, maize and squash was supplemented by Jesuit-introduced wheat, cattle and horses.

- **Four crops** — Traditional beans, maize and squash grown together with Jesuit-introduced wheat since the 120-year mission period.
- **Corn wine songs** (*vachi vino bwikam*) — A ceremonial drink documented only through its music in the Yaqui song tradition.

## Oral tradition & literature

The deer song corpus is the literary tradition with its own conventions and elevated register distinct from everyday speech.

- **Deer song form** (*maso bwikam*) — A fixed two-part form with an elevated register, archaic vocabulary (such as seyewailo for flower world) and syntax resembling elders' speech.
- **Greeting formula** (*Lios em chania*) — A four-phrase formal greeting used even among close friends: Lios em chania ('God preserves you!'), answered by Lios em chiokoe ('God pardons you!'), Empo allea ('May you rejoice!') and Kettu'i ('How kind!').
- **Language revitalization** — Since 2009 the Pascua Yaqui Tribal Council and University of Arizona have run a programme where tribal elders teach the language to families.

## Language & religion

Yoem Noki is an agglutinative Uto-Aztecan language; Yaqui Religion syncretizes older belief with Jesuit teaching through nine overlapping worlds.

- **Yoem Noki** — An Uto-Aztecan language with agglutinative grammar, generally subject–object–verb order, and about 16,000 speakers; one of two surviving Cahitan languages.
- **The aniam** (*aniam*) — Nine or more overlapping worlds in Yaqui cosmology: flower world (sea ania), enchanted world (yo ania), dream world (tenku ania), night world (tuka ania), wilderness (huya ania), corncob world (nao ania), mountains (kawi ania), underwater (vawe ania) and sky upward (teeka ania).
- **Yaqui Religion** — A syncretism dated from 1500 to present, considered the earliest revitalization reform movement in Native American religions, working through song, music, prayer and dance.

## Glossary

- *Hiaki* — Yaqui people; also Yoeme, from yoemem ('people')
- *Hiakim* — The Yaqui homeland in the lower Río Yaqui valley, Sonora
- *Yoem Noki* — The Yaqui language, an Uto-Aztecan tongue with about 16,000 speakers
- *maso bwikam* — Deer songs, the dominant genre of Yaqui music and literature
- *maso* — Deer; little brother deer in Yaqui belief
- *saila maso* — Little brother deer
- *pascola* — Deer dancer or ritual clown, the word from Spanish pascua (Easter)
- *pahkolam* — Ritual clowns who perform alongside deer dancers
- *matachin* — A dancer in the matachines dance, a society joined for life
- *monarca* — The leader of the matachines dance
- *tenabaris* — Ankle rattles made of dried cocoons worn by deer dancers
- *Waresma* — Lent in Yaqui, the central ceremonial season
- *seyewailo* — The flower world in Yaqui cosmology
- *aniam* — Overlapping worlds in Yaqui cosmology; also ritual societies distributing ceremonial labour
- *portal* — The living room of a traditional Yaqui house
- *Kiyohteis* — Female church assistants in Yaqui ritual societies
- *Kantoras* — Female singers among Yaqui ritual societies
- *masobwikamem* — Deer singers who perform in an elevated register
- *hirukiam* — A rasp used in Yaqui music (also kuta)
- *Lios em chania* — A formal Yaqui greeting meaning 'God preserves you!'
- *sewa* — Flower; used in the greeting 'How is the flower?'
- *u vat weeme* — The first part of a deer song, sung many times
- *u tonua* — The concluding part of a deer song, often turning on antithesis
- *yo ania* — The enchanted world in Yaqui cosmology
