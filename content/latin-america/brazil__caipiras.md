## Overview

The Caipira people are an ethnocultural group originally from the state of São Paulo, Brazil. They are also distributed mainly across Goiás, Minas Gerais, Mato Grosso, Mato Grosso do Sul and Paraná, and are historically associated with the colonization of the mountainous regions of Rio Grande do Sul and Santa Catarina. The territories where Caipira culture was introduced are grouped into Paulistânia, a cultural and geographical concept that gained prominence in the 20th century. The group developed from the old Paulista society and Bandeirante culture. Its early explorers were the Bandeirantes, who travelled from São Paulo into the backlands in search of metals and precious stones. The Caipira language preserves elements of the Paulista General Language and Galician-Portuguese language.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe clothing associated with the traditional June Festivals, or *Festas Juninas*, as simple countryside attire generally stereotyped as representing the Caipira people.

## Music & performance

The sources mention *Música Caipira* in the bibliography, under the title *Música Caipira, da roça ao rodeio*, but the supplied material does not describe its instruments, song genres, performance contexts, or musical techniques. The sources also mention Caipira music as a related subject but provide no further account of it. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription concerns this ethnic group.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources mention the traditional June Festivals, or *Festas Juninas*. During these festivals, people are described as wearing simple countryside attire generally stereotyped as representing the Caipira people.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe several literary and fictional representations of Caipiras. In *Urupês*, Monteiro Lobato portrayed the caipira through stereotyped expressions including “old plague,” “parasite caboclo,” “parasite of the earth,” “unimportant people,” “seminomadic,” “unadaptable to civilization,” and “urumbeba.” The term *urumbeba* is described as being used in the state of São Paulo for a naive and gullible person who is easy to deceive. The same source identifies Jeca Tatu as a Caipira character created by Monteiro Lobato and Chuck Billy, known as Chico Bento in Brazil, as a Caipira boy created by the cartoonist Mauricio de Sousa.

## Language & religion

During the colonial period, the main means of communication among the Caipiras was the Paulista General Language, which the Bandeirantes spread to other regions. Today, the group has its own dialect, preserving elements of the Paulista and Galician-Portuguese language. The sources give several proposed explanations for the word “Caipira.” Baptista Caetano d’Almeida described it in the 19th century as a combination of “cai,” meaning burnt, and “pira,” meaning skin; in Tupi, this was presented as a possible reference to the tanned or dark skin of Caipira colonizers. Luís de Câmara Cascudo proposed “caá,” meaning jungle, combined with “pora,” meaning inhabitant, producing “inhabitant of the jungle” in Tupi.
