## Overview

The Chʼortiʼ people, also called Chʼortiʼ Maya or Chorti, are an Indigenous Maya people living primarily in communities and towns of northeastern Guatemala, northwestern Honduras, and northern El Salvador. In Guatemala, many Chʼortiʼ live in the municipalities of Camotán, Jocotán, Esquipulas, Quetzaltepeque, San Juan Ermita, Olopa, and La Union. Chʼortiʼ is their indigenous language and is described as a survival of Classic Maya, the language of the inscriptions in Copan. It is the first language of approximately 15,000 people, although most present-day Chʼortiʼ speakers are also bilingual in Spanish. Their cultural center was Copán, also known as Oxwitik, which was the headquarters of the ancient Mayan civilization. Chʼortiʼ culture includes distinctive musical instruments, dance, foodways, dress customs, and nature-centered religious rituals.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that traditional customs of dress have been maintained.

## Music & performance

Chʼortiʼ musical traditions are expressed through instruments including *teponangas*, drums, whistles, *chinchins*, *sonajas*, and horns.

The sources describe the Chʼortiʼ as maintaining musical traditions alongside traditional customs of dress. Their music is presented as part of the culture of a people whose communities extend across Guatemala, Honduras, and El Salvador.

## Dance & theatre

The most traditional Chʼortiʼ dance is called the *dance of the giants*.

## Festivals & rituals

Chʼortiʼ religion is based on admiring and adoring nature through rituals dedicated to the gods of the Earth and the Wind.

## Foodways

Chʼortiʼ food is based mainly on corn and beans. Corn is prepared in several forms, including drinks such as *chilate*, *pozol*, sweet *atole*, and sour *atole*, also called *chuco*. Other corn-based foods named in the sources are tortillas, tamales, and *totoposte*. The sources also identify *chicha*, an alcoholic drink.

## Language & religion

Chʼortiʼ is the indigenous language of the Chʼortiʼ people and is described as a survival of Classic Maya, the language of the inscriptions in Copan. Approximately 15,000 people speak it as a first language, while the majority of present-day Chʼortiʼ speakers are bilingual in Spanish. The Chʼortiʼ belong to the Meridional Mayans and are closely related to the Mayans in Yucatán, Belize, and Northern Guatemala; they are also somewhat related to the Choles of Chiapas.

In Copan, Chʼortiʼ people speak Spanish, while most Chʼortiʼ speakers are in Guatemala. The sources connect this linguistic situation to the political division between Honduras and Guatemala and to movement restrictions between the two countries. Chʼortiʼ religion centers on nature and rituals dedicated to the gods of the Earth and the Wind.
