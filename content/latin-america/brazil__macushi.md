## Overview

The Macushi are the largest Indigenous group in northeastern Roraima, Brazil, and also live in Guyana and Venezuela. They speak a Cariban language and are part of the broader Pemon cultural group. Their material culture centers on cassava farming, cotton weaving, featherwork and metalwork that circulated widely in the region. They live in uxorilocal villages organized around family groups and have maintained oral traditions, ritual practices and a blend of indigenous and Christian beliefs.

## Material culture

Macushi material culture reflects their mastery of plant fibers, feathers and metalwork, with objects designed for both everyday use and ritual performance.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Woven cotton is the foundation of Macushi textile arts, from hammocks to dance ornaments.

- **Cotton hammock** — A woven cotton hammock dyed in bright colors with synthetic dyes or left plain, finished with tasselled fringe, traded widely in the region.
- **Shoulder cloak** — A man's dance ornament made from a woven cotton band with five hanging twisted cords, each ending in a ball of down.
- **Featherwork cape** — A cape made of egret or macaw feathers on twine, fastened with cotton cord, worn by medicine men or chiefs.
- **Plaited palm tray** (*ite-palm*) — A serving tray woven from ite-palm leaf, used in food preparation and serving.
- **Cassava squeezer** (*matape*) — A plaited cane device used to squeeze poisonous juice from grated cassava.

### Clothing & dress

Macushi dress emphasizes featherwork and ornament for ceremony and dance rather than everyday wear.

- **Feathered headdress** — Head-dresses of feathers mounted on wood with twine and cotton, or a crown of feathers and reed.
- **Feather tippet** — A red macaw-feather collar worn by a chief or dancer at feasts, marking office and performance.
- **Palm sandals** — Sandals cut from split palm-branch with the foot held to the convex side by lengths of twisted fibre twine.
- **Cotton anklet** — A woven cotton band worn around the ankle as body ornament.
- **Body paint container** — A small cane-and-cotton container used to hold body paint for adornment.

### Architecture

Macushi villages are organized around a central courtyard with houses linked by paths, built by family groups in uxorilocal settlements.

- **Village courtyard layout** — Houses are built around a central courtyard, reflecting the kin group that assembled them.
- **Raposa Serra do Sol Indigenous Territory** — The largest and most populated Macushi territory in Brazil.
- **San Marcos Indigenous Territory** — A second major Macushi territory block in Brazil.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Macushi households are organized around cassava processing, with objects of clay, wood and plant fibers designed for food production.

- **Red clay cooking pot** — A coil-built vessel collected in 1912 at Aynaymong, used for cooking.
- **Wooden manioc grater** — A wooden grater set with small stones and striped with black lines, used to grate cassava root.
- **Chief's club** — A carved wooden club used as a badge of office, knobbed at one end and triangular at the other with red and white incised work.
- **War club** — A wooden club bound with cotton cord and carved with four bird-headed figures.
- **Calabash bowl** — A gourd bowl with a blackened interior used for food storage and preparation.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver nose ornaments are the signature Macushi jewelry, often paired with necklaces of teeth, seeds and beads.

- **Silver nose ornament** — A crescent-shaped silver piece with pierced projections above for suspension and teeth cut along the lower edge.
- **Beaded nose ornament** — A semicircular silver plate strung above with fourteen glass beads alternating red and white.
- **Acouri-tooth necklace** — A plaited fibre cord tied with numerous knots and threaded with about 150 acouri teeth, worn by men.
- **Seed necklace** — A man's necklace of seeds strung on a double cotton cord.

## Music & performance

Macushi musicians play bone flutes, reed pipes, drums and rattles, with rattles tied to dancing and ritual.

- **Bone flute** — A flute made of bone with three finger holes and cotton-cord wrappings, or finished with cotton tassels.
- **Double reed flute** — A flute in which a longer tube is stopped at the upper end while a shorter tube is open, used to create multiple notes.
- **Sampura drum** (*sampura'*) — A drum hollowed from a section of palm trunk with skin stretched over each end and a cord strung across one head with a wood piece beneath it for tone variation.
- **Dance rattle stick** — A stick of wood or cane ornamented with zigzag lines, carrying plaited cotton bands hung with thevetia seeds and used to beat time in dancing.
- **Conjuring wand** — A stick about four feet long with cords strung with large halved sibeni seeds, rattled by piaiman at their dances.

## Dance & theatre

Macushi dancing involves rattle-sticks and specialized costumes that distinguish ritual performance by specialists from festive dancing.

- **Piaiman dancer** (*piaiman*) — A medicine-man and spiritual leader who rattles a long seed-hung wand at his dances, marked as a ritual specialist.
- **Rattle-stick dance** — Dancing accompanied by wood or cane rattle-sticks hung with thevetia seeds on plaited cotton bands to beat time.
- **Feast dancing** — Chief or community members dance in feather tippets and cotton-and-down shoulder cloaks at feasts.

## Festivals & rituals

Macushi ritual life centers on the piaiman's authority and the dangerous kanaima spirit, with feasts and life-cycle practices marking community standing.

- **Piaiman** — A medicine-man and spiritual leader who wears feather capes, dances with a conjuring wand and holds ritual authority.
- **Kanaima spirit** (*kanaima, keinaimi*) — An evil outsider spirit that brings death and misfortune through shape-shifting into animals and attacking people caught alone.
- **Feast** — A ceremonial gathering where chiefs and dancers wear feathered ornaments and tippets marking their status.
- **Uxorilocal marriage** — After marriage the couple settles in the wife's family's village, making the father-in-law a figure of great importance.
- **Cassava farming success** — Village standing is measured by success in cassava cultivation.

## Foodways

Macushi subsistence rests on bitter cassava cultivation and processing, with hunting and fishing providing protein and trade goods.

- **Bitter cassava** — The staple crop, cleared by men and tended and harvested by women, supporting traditional Macushi life.
- **Cassava bread** (*cassava bread, farine*) — Processed cassava products made by women during the preparation chain.
- **Curare poison** (*curare*) — A potent arrow and dart poison produced and traded by the Macushi in the nineteenth century.
- **Fish-poisoning** — A hunting technique using poison to take fish from rivers and streams.
- **Cassava Mother** (*kisera yan*) — A supernatural being who oversees cassava cultivation in Macushi lore, where cassava was created to be farmed.

## Oral tradition & literature

Macushi oral history traces descent from the sun's children and records territorial struggles against colonial settlers and land forgers.

- **Insikiran** (*Insikiran, Inshkirung*) — The benevolent sun-child who, with his malevolent brother Makunaima, created the world and discovered the Tree of Life.
- **Makunaima** (*Makunaima, Negi*) — The malevolent brother of Insikiran who created fire and diseases.
- **Washacá** — The Tree of Life, discovered by the sun-children in Macushi creation narrative.
- **Life principle** (*stkaton*) — A spiritual essence that comes from the sun in Macushi belief.
- **New Testament translation** — The New Testament translated into Macushi in 1996 and printed in Latin script, marking the start of written literature.

## Language & religion

Macushi is a Cariban language with OVS word order, marked as vulnerable to language loss, and the community blends indigenous piaiman beliefs with Catholic and Anglican mission Christianity.

- **Macushi language** (*Macushi*) — A Cariban language of the Macushi-Kapon group, the most populous in its family, written in Latin script with high and low pitch marking distinctions.
- **OVS word order** — The basic word order of Macushi places the object before the verb and subject, with SOV used to foreground the subject.
- **Language endangerment** — Macushi is listed as vulnerable and potentially endangered, having been disparaged as slang beside official Portuguese.
- **Piaiman complex** (*piaiman*) — A ritual system centered on the medicine-man and the kanaima spirit, blended with long-established Catholic and Anglican mission presence.

## Glossary

- *Macushi* — An Indigenous people of the Brazil-Guyana-Venezuela borderlands
- *matape* — A plaited cane squeezer for expressing cassava juice
- *ite-palm* — A palm species whose leaves are plaited into trays and fans
- *piaiman* — A medicine-man and spiritual leader who conducts rituals and dances
- *kisera yan* — The Cassava Mother, a supernatural being overseeing cassava cultivation
- *stkaton* — A life principle or spiritual essence that comes from the sun
- *Washacá* — The Tree of Life discovered by the sun-children
- *sampura'* — A drum made from a palm trunk section with skin heads and a tone-modifying cord
- *thevetia seeds* — Seeds used as rattles on dance sticks and conjuring wands
- *sibeni seeds* — Large seeds halved and strung on a conjuring wand
- *curare* — A potent poison used on arrows and darts for hunting
- *farine* — A processed cassava product made by women
- *wo* — A cassava drink made by women
