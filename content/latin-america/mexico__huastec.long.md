## Overview

The Huastec, who call themselves *Téenek* — a contraction of *Te' Inik*, "people from here" — are an indigenous people of northeastern Mexico living in the region known as the Huasteca, which spans parts of Hidalgo, Veracruz, San Luis Potosí and Tamaulipas, concentrated along the route of the Pánuco River and the Gulf of Mexico coast. The Huasteca reaches from the limestone ranges of the Sierra Madre Oriental across the coastal plain and the Otontepec hills to the Gulf, and contains the northernmost patches of tropical moist forest and cloud forest in the Americas. Counts of speakers vary between sources: one gives roughly 66,000, two-thirds of them in San Luis Potosí and one-third in Veracruz; the 2005 population census reports about 200,000, some 120,000 in San Luis Potosí and some 80,000 in Veracruz. The language belongs to the Mayan family, though it separated from the rest of it earlier than any other branch. Huastec civilization was one of the Mesoamerican cultures of the Pre-Columbian era, and other Mesoamerican peoples admired the Huastecs for their musical abilities.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The catalogued objects shown here document weaving in agave fibre rather than in cotton, and pattern in them is carried by dyed stripes and by stamped dye rather than by brocade or embroidery.

**Zapupe weaving** — *zapupe* is the agave fibre from which the Huastec objects in this collection are woven, and it appears in undyed form and dyed in fuchsia, green, yellow, turquoise and purple. A set of twenty-one loom-woven cloth samples records the range: one carries three purple stripes and one green stripe against an undyed ground, with occasional turquoise and purple weft fibres crossing it. A loom piece survives alongside them — the end bar cut from *zapate* wood, with the agave warp fibres tied onto it with string, some of them dyed.

**Morral** — the shoulder bag, *morral* in Spanish. One example is a single piece of agave-fibre cloth folded in half and sewn up the sides, with braided cord sewn to each top corner for the strap and a triangular flap that folds over the top and ties at the side. Its decoration is stamped rather than woven: four-petalled flowers in turquoise and fuchsia aniline dyes, five along the top and three at the bottom of each face, with three more on the flap. A second shoulder bag is loom-woven with fuchsia, green and yellow stripes, closed by a green twisted cord on the flap that ties to a cord on the front.

**Carrying-strap and tumpline** — both are agave-fibre weaving put to load-bearing use. The *tumpline* is woven from undyed *zapupe*, with a braided cord threaded through the looped warp ends to form the strap and a strip of leather sewn around the centre of the woven area to rest on the head. The carrying-strap is undyed except for turquoise fibre run along its edges, its warp yarns gathered and knotted into a twisted cord made from multicoloured fibre.

**Palm plaiting** — palm fibre is the second great fibre of these collections, worked both by plaiting and by coiling with a binder that leaves an open mesh. Baskets are built on ovoid, rectangular and circular bases with stiff vertical sides and looped handles formed from two loops with fibre open-knotted between them; one has a rectangular lid hung on palm-fibre hinges. Fans come in pairs, roughly tear-drop shaped, stitched across with diagonal lines and edged with a sewn-on border of braided palm fibre. Mats — *petate* in Spanish — are plaited from the same fibre.

**Motif vocabulary.** Four-petalled flower (stamped in aniline dye on the *morral*, and painted on the inside bottom of a pottery bowl), rim triangles in darker orange, and warp-direction stripes in purple, green, fuchsia, yellow and turquoise.

### Clothing & dress

The catalogued garments here are hats, and the palm-fibre hat is documented in unusual depth, including two examples left unfinished so the sequence of work is visible: in one the crown has been plaited with the fringe around its outer edge still to be worked into the brim; in the other crown and part of the brim are done and the outer fringe still waits. Finished hats fall into two constructions. Plaited hats have a tall flat crown with horizontal stripes and a wide brim, finished with a black velvet hatband and an interior leather band stamped "EXTRA FINO XXXXX". Coiled-and-sewn hats of braided palm fibre have a tall crown with a concave top crossed front to back by a raised bar, and a wide, slightly curled brim whose edge is folded over a piece of string tied at the back and left hanging as a tassel. The most worked example adds cut-leather decoration: a strip of black leather folded over the brim with square holes cut through it to expose white beneath, making alternating black and white squares around the edge, and a black leather hatband with white scalloped trim at the base of the crown, again cut through to the white leather below. A man's hat uses the coiling-with-binder open mesh, its flat-topped crown higher on one side than the other and its brim tied to the crown side with a loop of palm fibre. A machete sheath of folded and stitched leather, carried on a leather strap with a metal buckle and decorated with rivets, belongs with this dress.

### Architecture

The sources used here do not describe Huastec houses, building materials or settlement form. What they do record of the pre-Columbian built environment is that Huastecs constructed temples on step-pyramids.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Huastec pottery in this collection is lightly fired and fire-stained, and most of it is burnished. The commonest form is a circular pot tapering at the neck and flaring out for the lip; several are splashed and streaked with a brown substance that the catalogue suggests is maize gruel, one dotted rather than streaked with it, and two painted grey and orange under the same brown markings. A bowl is a flattened-bottomed semicircle painted orange outside with darker orange triangles along the rim and a flower on the inside bottom. One burnished pot is shaped like a bottle-gourd — a small circle above a large flat-bottomed one with a thin neck between — painted with white flowers and the word "CALABAZO". The metalwork shown is a machete with a blade straight on one side and curved on the sharp side, its metal handle wrapped in leather. Of pre-Columbian production the sources record elaborately painted pottery, independently-standing carved sculpture, and work in shell: pots, gaming stones, platform pipes and sculptures, made into human heads, engraved shell gorgets, fan headdresses and hunchbacked human figures.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources name engraved shell gorgets and fan headdresses among pre-Columbian Huastec production, and associate tooth mutilation with the "yoke-palm-axe" carved iconographic complex found from Jaina Island in coastal Campeche to the Huasteca. They describe no other jewelry or body adornment.

## Music & performance

The sources record that other Mesoamerican peoples admired the Huastecs for their musical abilities, but name no instrument, song genre or performance occasion. Huastec-language broadcasting is carried out by the CDI's radio station XEANT-AM, based in Tancanhuitz de Santos, San Luis Potosí.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used do not cover Huastec dance or dramatic traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not describe a Huastec festival calendar or life-cycle rites. Mexico has no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription listed here that concerns this group. The one ritual practice the sources touch on is pre-Columbian and known through carved iconography: the "yoke-palm-axe" complex, found from Jaina Island in coastal Campeche through Aparicio in Veracruz to the Huasteca, in association with the *pelota* ballgame, decapitation and tooth mutilation — though this distribution may reflect coastal trade contacts made after the Huastecs were established in the Huasteca rather than a shared inheritance.

## Foodways

The sources name no Huastec dishes or cooking methods. Two material traces bear on food: the brown streaks and splashes on several burnished pots, which the British Museum catalogue reads as possibly maize gruel, and the bottle-gourd-shaped pot labelled "CALABAZO". The Aztecs knew the Huasteca for its fertile abundance. From the region's deeper history the sources record basalt *manos* and *metates* of Panuco type among artifacts of the Ojite phase at Santa Luisa, and note that the proto-Huastecs borrowed *ciw*, "squash", from proto-Mixe–Zoque while passing through the southern Gulf lowlands. In the late nineteenth century a cross-Gulf steamship trade carried salt from Campeche to the Huastec port of Tuxpan and sugar from Tuxpan to Campeche.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources name no Huastec epic, folktale cycle or proverb tradition. Documentation exists: the Huasteco Collection of Barbara Edmonson, held in the Archive of the Indigenous Languages of Latin America, is an archive of recordings of narratives, words and rituals. The linguistic record begins with Andrés de Olmos, a friar who produced the first grammatical and lexical description of Huastec accessible to Europeans, having also written the first such grammars of Nahuatl and Totonac. One deep fact about Huastec literacy comes out of comparative linguistics: the word "to write" is different in proto-Huastec (*θuc-*) and in the other Maya branch (*c'ib*), the split having occurred at least a millennium before the rise of classic Maya culture.

## Language & religion

Huastec, also spelled Wasteko or Huasteco and now commonly known by the endonym *Téenek*, is a Mayan language, remarkable among them for having tone, like its Otomanguean and Totonac neighbours. Proto-Huastecan was the first branch to split from Mayan proper; estimates of the date range between 2200 and 1200 BCE, with McQuown suggesting 1500 BCE, Manrique Castaneda 1800 BCE and Dahlin 2100 BCE. Morris Swadesh contrasted Huastec *inik*, "man", with *winik* in the other Maya languages. Its only sister, Chicomuceltec (also called Cotoque), once spoken in Chiapas near Comitán, is extinct. Three dialects, with a time depth of no more than 400 years, are spoken: Western (Potosino), about 48,000 speakers across nine San Luis Potosí towns; Central (Veracruz), about 22,000 in Tempoal and Tantoyuca; and Eastern (Otontepec), about 12,000 across seven northern Veracruz towns, though Ana Kondic reports only about 1,700 there in 2012. The sources describe no present-day religious practice.
