## Overview

The Otavalo are an indigenous people of the Andean highlands of Imbabura Province in northern Ecuador, centred on the city of *Otavalo*, which lies at an elevation of 2,532 metres (8,307 ft) in high, cool country north of Quito. In 1990 their number was estimated at 45,000 to 50,000 in the Otavalo area, with a further 5,000 to 8,000 living elsewhere in Ecuador or abroad. They speak *Kichwa*, the Quechua dialect of Ecuador, and Spanish, having lost their earlier language — probably a Barbacoan one — sometime after the sixteenth century. Commerce and handcrafts are their principal economic activities, and they enjoy a higher standard of living than most indigenous groups in Ecuador and many mestizos of their area. In folk-culture terms they matter as weavers and as traders: Otavalo-styled textiles woven with alpaca or llama wool are exported to North America, and tourism draws outsiders to the area for the culture and local dress.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources for this atlas record no vernacular Otavalo names for the weaves below; each is described here as the catalogue describes it.

**Woman's woven waist-band** — A cotton band worked so that the two faces differ, one pink and the other red, with added decoration in pink, red and green wool. A second example is bright pink with yellow and white bands on the outer face and tassels at one end, likewise decorated with wool. The reversible two-colour construction is the distinguishing feature of the type as catalogued.

**Warp-patterned band** — A long, narrow band in warp-rips technique, the pattern carried entirely by a densely set warp in undyed white and blue against a sparser weft. The warp begins and ends in loops; the weaving runs to the very end on one side while the other side is left as fringe.

**Embroidered woollen shawl** — A white wool shawl worked in embroidery, catalogued as belonging to the Otavalo costume and to an Otavalo family.

**Embroidered girl's dress** — A white dress with floral decoration in pale blue, thinly worked at the neck and across the shoulders, and lace around the neck opening and the sleeve openings.

**Export weaving** — Textiles in Otavalo style woven with alpaca or llama wool, made for the textile industry and sent to North America; some Otavalo businessmen and women run these operations.

**Motif vocabulary.** Twist-stripes picked up into lozenges (diamond figures set in banded grounds); lengthwise striping concentrated along the selvedges; flower sprays in pale blue on white embroidered dress linen.

### Clothing & dress

The local dress of the Otavalo is itself an attraction for visitors to the area, and the museum records treat garments as parts of a named ensemble, "the Otavalo costume," rather than as isolated pieces. Documented women's wear comprises a white embroidered wool shawl, fastened at the breast with a long pin made for that purpose; a white felt hat, specified in the catalogue as a woman's hat; woven waist-bands of cotton, one face pink and the other red, or bright pink banded with yellow and white and finished with tassels at one end; multiple strands of glass-bead necklaces; and a pair of slippers made of cloth with a stiffening inside. For girls there is a white dress with pale blue floral embroidery at the neck and shoulders and lace at the neck and sleeve openings. The sources used here do not describe men's dress, and they do not distinguish ceremonial from everyday clothing.

### Architecture

The sources used for this profile say nothing about Otavalo house forms, building materials or built decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The household objects documented here are modest and mostly items of costume and carriage. A metal pin serves specifically to fasten the shawl of the Otavalo costume. Cloth slippers, catalogued as a pair, are stiffened internally. A photograph in the Finnish Heritage Agency collection shows an Otavalo woman carrying a large pot on her back, with Lake San Pablo behind her — the only vessel form the sources attest. Several of the objects shown, including beadwork and a bamboo flute, were bought in the market at Otavalo, which the catalogue entries name repeatedly as the point of purchase; market exchange is thus visible in the collection record itself, consistent with commerce being a principal Otavalo activity.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Beadwork is the best-documented adornment. One necklace consists of small gold-coloured glass beads threaded on fourteen separate cords; another is built of four cords strung with large gold-coloured glass beads; a third is a single cord of red glass beads, long enough to be wound. All three were bought in the market at Otavalo. The shawl pin, though functional, is worn at the front of the costume. The sources used here do not mention tattooing, body painting or hair practices.

## Foodways

What the sources give is subsistence rather than cuisine, and it is mostly historical. The Otavalo area was densely populated and farmed under irrigation; fields called *camelones*, or *Waru Waru* raised fields, were built to regulate the water supply to crops. Maize and potatoes were the important highland products, with coca and other tropical and sub-tropical crops grown at lower, warmer altitudes. Domesticated guinea pigs were an important source of animal protein, alongside wild game such as deer. Unlike Andean peoples further south in Peru and Bolivia, the Otavalo apparently kept no domesticated llamas or alpacas before the Incas. Long-distance traders called *mindaláes* brought cotton, coca, salt and dried fish from the Pacific coast up into the highlands, trading especially with people on the western slopes of the Andes in the Mira River valley, about 75 kilometres (47 mi) north of the city of Otavalo. Today most Otavalo are subsistence farmers who cultivate crops such as potatoes and also raise livestock. The sources used here name no prepared dishes, ceremonial foods or drink traditions.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used for this profile do not cover Otavalo folktales, epic or storytelling. They do record that the Otavalo and other Andean peoples of northern Ecuador are often identified with the pre-Columbian Cara people and Cara culture, and treated as descendants of the semi-mythical Quitu culture, from which the name of Quito comes.

## Language & religion

Before Quechua, the language of the area was *Caranqui* or *Cara* (Kara), an extinct language of Ecuador generally classed as Barbacoan on the strength of similarities to Pasto and Tsafiki, though the evidence is not conclusive because the documentation is poor; it was replaced by Quechua, perhaps surviving as late as the eighteenth century, and appears in turn to have influenced Imbabura Quechua. The Otavalo, the Caranqui, the Cayambe and the Cochasquí were small-scale chiefdoms north of Quito, similar in artistic technique, subsistence, settlement pattern and language, with a combined pre-Inca population estimated at 100,000 to 180,000. The Otavalo today speak Kichwa and Spanish. On religion the sources record only the colonial context: vineyards owned by the Dominican Order were called "fields of blood" for the Otavalo labourers who died working them.
