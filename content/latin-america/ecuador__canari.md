## Overview

The Cañari, also called *Kañari* in Kichwa, are an Indigenous ethnic group of Ecuador traditionally associated with the modern provinces of Azuay and Cañar. Their historic territory extended from the limits of Azuay to Saraguro, from the Gualaquiza mountains to the Narajal beaches and the coasts of the Jambelí canal; another account places their territory between the southern border of modern-day Chimborazo Province and the Jubones River in Loja Province. The Cañari descended from an independent pre-Columbian tribal confederation and were particularly noted for resistance against the Inca Empire. Their population today numbers in the thousands and includes many mestizos. Their cultural history includes federative political organization, circular architecture, a lunar calendar, an extinct or nearly extinct language, oral creation traditions, and the survival of Cañari and Inca archaeological remains.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Cañari architecture is associated with circular or moon-like buildings and temples. At Ingapirca, round Cañari buildings stand alongside rectangular Inca buildings, while stones described as “calendars” contain holes placed at different positions and angles; water filled in the holes reflects celestial bodies at different times and gives dates. The Cañari primarily occupied the Tumebamba area, present-day Cuenca, where construction reportedly rivaled that of the Inca capital, Cuzco. Tumebamba was referred to as the “second Cuzco.” The largest known ruins of the Cañari-Inca confederation are **Ingapirca**, **Pumapungo**, **Chobshi**, and **Yacubiñay**. Túpac Yupanqui renamed Guapondelig as Tumebamba and ordered the construction of the palace of Pumapungo. During the Inca conquest, he also ordered fortresses at Achupallas and Pumallacta, constructed bridges, and commissioned religious and non-religious buildings.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do describe stone “calendars” at Ingapirca, made from stones drilled with holes in different positions and angles.

## Music & performance

They state that the Cañari had an oral tradition of a massive flood as part of their creation stories. The sources also mention stories and designs associated with the snake and macaw, which were understood either as ancestral beings or as sacred animals. No specific musical practice is identified.

## Festivals & rituals

They describe a creation story involving a massive flood in which everyone perished except two brothers who had taken refuge on a high mountain. After the flood, the brothers found prepared food in their repaired and provisioned hut. They eventually learned that two female beings with macaw faces had prepared it; one brother took the younger being as his wife, and the world was repopulated through their offspring.

The sources describe Cañari political and social practices rather than named festivals. The Cañari are presented as a confederacy whose tribes could unite and choose a single leader during natural disasters or wars. Some tribes had matriarchal societies or kinship systems.

## Foodways

In the Cañari creation story, two brothers repeatedly found prepared food in their hut after returning from the mountain, and they later learned that two female beings with macaw faces had prepared it.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Cañari had an oral tradition of a massive flood as part of their creation stories. The account resembles flood traditions associated with the Bible and Gilgamesh: a giant flood destroyed everyone except two brothers who had perched on a high mountain. Afterward, two female beings with macaw faces supplied prepared food, and one brother married the younger of them. Their offspring repopulated the world.

Another traditional story concerns the founding of the location associated with the Peleusis. The caciques Tenemaza and Carchipulla are said to have founded it; their surnames continued to exist in the province. The sources also explain the name Cañari through *kan*, meaning “snake,” and *ara*, meaning “macaw.” According to the account, this interpretation relates either to belief in these animals as ancestors or to their sacred status, expressed through stories and designs.

## Language & religion

Cañari is described as a poorly attested extinct language of the Marañón River basin in Ecuador. It was the original language of the Cañari people before replacement by Kichwa and later Spanish. The language is described as difficult to classify, apparently related to Puruhá and possibly Barbacoan. During the Inca conquest, the Cañari learned Quechua, also called Kichwa, and this language absorbed vernacular words for objects and places such as rivers and mountains.

The Cañari language continued into the colonial period: in 1594, Bishop Luis López de Solís ordered Christian doctrines to be translated into Cañari. Missionaries also worked on a catechism, but no copy survives. Spanish rulers ordered the Cañaris to learn Kichwa, contributing to the disuse of Cañari. The language is now believed to be practically extinct. As many as ten Cañari dialects may have survived into the 21st century, but surviving speakers are few.
