## Overview

The Selkʼnam — known by the exonym *Ona* — are an Indigenous people of Isla Grande de Tierra del Fuego, at the southern end of South America, whose territory straddles today's Chilean and Argentine sides of the island; the museum records for the objects shown here are catalogued simply as *Eldslandet*, Chile/Argentina, with the Argentine province given as Provincia de Tierra del Fuego, Antártida e Islas del Atlántico Sur. They inhabited the northeastern part of the archipelago for thousands of years before Europeans arrived, and were nomads known as "foot-people" because they hunted on land rather than going to sea. Their language, Selkʼnam, belongs to the Chonan family. The sources used give no population figure; they record that the late 19th-century Selkʼnam genocide by European immigrants, fatalities from disease and the disruption of Selkʼnam society ended the community that made these objects, that the last full-blooded Selkʼnam, Ángela Loij, died in 1974, and that Selkʼnam communities are now revitalizing the language.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used do not document any loom-woven or patterned textile tradition for the Selkʼnam; the only plaited fibre work in the collection is basketry, and the only cloth mentioned in a catalogue entry is an undescribed strip attached to a sling.

**Flätad korg (plaited basket)** — Two plaited baskets are held, one acquired in 1929 by exchange with the Museo Missionario-Etnologico del Laterano in Rome and one 9 cm high, in the same plaiting technique, given in 1964; neither record names the technique or the plant used.

### Clothing & dress

Dress in the catalogued material is made from the skins of locally hunted animals, above all the guanaco. A *medicinmansmössa* — a medicine man's cap — is made from skin taken from the guanaco's throat, bought from the Selkʼnam by W. A:son Grebst in 1906 and the one explicitly ritual garment among the objects. A photograph taken by Martin Gusinde at Tierra del Fuego in February 1920, of two Selkʼnam men named Ambrosio and Tomas, is catalogued for its fur and fur cap as well as for the bow one of them carries. Guanaco hide is also the material of a knife's binding and of a tool bag, and fox skin of a needle sheath, so the same skins served for dress and for equipment. A leather mask, acquired in 1929 from the Laterano collection, belongs to ceremonial rather than daily dress, as does the body paint photographed at the ceremonies described below. The sources name no Selkʼnam-language garment terms, and say nothing about footwear, belts or women's dress specifically.

### Architecture

The sources used do not describe Selkʼnam dwellings; the only built environment in the material is a camp scene on an early postcard, catalogued as *lägerscen*, with no description of its shelters. One piece of camp furniture is documented in detail: a *vagga*, a cradle built of two long and four short wooden staves lashed together with guanaco sinew, shaped like a ladder and pushed into the ground, on which the child was tied while its mother was occupied.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

No ceramics appear in the collection, and the only metal is introduced: an iron needle with a bone handle, carried in a fox-skin sheath bound with guanaco sinew. Hunting equipment dominates. Bows are made of *Fagus antarctica*, one of them strung; arrows are of wood tipped with worked glass; quivers are of sea-lion skin (one 71 cm long and 10–14 cm wide) and of seal fur. A sling is made of leather with sinew cords 86 cm long ending in a ball of the same material. Three bone staves were used one at a time to break arrowheads out of bottle glass, work the 1906 collector recorded as extremely difficult, and which he said was why even the spurious arrows sold to visitors at Punta Arenas carried genuine heads. Containers include a bark vessel, a large lidded wooden box, and leather bags — a red one for carrying an edible fungus and a guanaco-hide bag for tools.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Necklaces are the one ornament type documented, and all of them are strung from materials taken from animals rather than traded in. Four are of bone: one of small bone pieces, one of bone pieces threaded on sinew, and one of lengths of bird bone. Two are of shell, and for one of these the shells were identified for the museum by Dr Nils Odhner of Stockholm as *Photinula violacae* King. Body painting is documented photographically rather than verbally: a Gusinde photograph in the collection is catalogued for body painting and masks together, from his plates of the men's ceremonies. The sources used say nothing about tattooing, hair practices or the occasions on which the necklaces were worn.

## Music & performance

The sources used do not cover Selkʼnam instruments, song genres or musical occasions.

## Dance & theatre

The one performance tradition the sources document is the masked impersonation of spirits by men. The mythology recorded by Gusinde and Anne Chapman holds that *Xalpen*, the goddess of the underworld, and her subordinate *soorts* were inhabitants of the underworld who were represented by men in the *Hain* ceremony. The collection holds a leather mask and a Gusinde photograph, published in the plate atlas of his *Die Feuerland-Indianer* (1931), captioned as the representation of demons at the secret Selkʼnam manhood ceremonies, with body painting noted as part of the same image. The sources name no dances and no secular or entertainment theatre.

## Festivals & rituals

The *Hain* is the only named Selkʼnam ceremony in the sources, catalogued by the museum as a secret manhood ceremony and attested by Gusinde's photographs of men costumed and painted as the underworld *soorts* of *Xalpen*. Xalpen has seven named companions — Sate, Yoisik, Wakus, Keyaisl, Talen, Pawus and Sanu — besides many unnamed subordinate soorts. Selkʼnam ritual life also had specialists: the guanaco-throat-skin cap in the collection is identified as a medicine man's cap. No annual or seasonal festival calendar appears in the sources, although the four skies of Selkʼnam cosmology are each tied to a season. For life-cycle rites the sources give only the manhood ceremony and, obliquely, the staked cradle on which infants were secured in camp; birth, marriage and funeral practices are not covered. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage element has Chile listed for this group.

## Foodways

Two foods are named in the sources, both through the objects used to get or carry them. Guanaco was hunted with bow and arrow, and an early postcard in the collection shows Selkʼnam hunting guanacos with a dog; guanaco sinew and hide then went into cradles, bags, needle sheaths and knife bindings, so the same animal supplied both food and raw material. The red leather bag collected in 1906 is recorded as the kind of bag in which an edible thread fungus was carried. Sea-lion and seal skins used for quivers show that marine mammals were taken as well, though the records do not say how they were eaten. Another photograph is catalogued as women carrying water. The sources used name no dishes, cooking methods, staple plant foods or drink traditions.

## Oral tradition & literature

What survives in the sources is a cosmology and a body of myth, known today primarily from the Austrian ethnologist Martin Gusinde and the Franco-American ethnologist Anne Chapman. The cosmos is divided into four *shóʼon*, infinite skies corresponding to the cardinal directions: *Kamuk* in the north, standing for spring and summer; *Kéikruk* in the south, for winter; *Kenénik* in the west, for autumn; and *Wintek* in the east, the most important of the four, the residence of *Temáukel*, the source of all that exists, and associated with all the seasons and possibly with time itself. Below Temáukel are the *howenh*, mythological ancestors who had lived as humans before becoming the great forces of nature: *Kenos*, the first of them, sent by Temáukel to organize the early Earth and create the other ancestors; *Kwányip* and *Čénuke*; *Kojh* of the sea; *Kren* of the sun; *Kre* of the moon; *Josh* of the snow; and *Shenrr* of the wind. The sources record no epic or proverb tradition, and no modern literary revival apart from work on the language.

## Language & religion

Selkʼnam is one of the Chonan languages of Patagonia; within Southern Chon it is closest to Haush, also spoken on Tierra del Fuego, and there is speculation that Chonan and the Moseten languages of Bolivia form a single Moseten-Chonan family. It had three vowels, /a, ɪ, ʊ/, and 23 consonants, with a simple tone system analysed as high and low tone by Najlis (1973) and as pitch accent by Martini (1982); its word order is object–verb–subject, used as a default by only 1% of languages, and it has just two word classes, nouns and verbs. Loans include *kʼlattítaŭ* 'cat', from Spanish *gatito*. The last fluent native speakers died in the 1980s. Luis Miguel Rojas-Berscia of Radboud University wrote a reference grammar working with Herminia Vera Ilioyen, a semi-speaker who spoke Selkʼnam until the age of eight and died in 2014, and with Joubert "Keyuk" Yanten Gomez, of mixed Selkʼnam and Mapuche ancestry, who taught himself the language after learning at eight that he was part-Selkʼnam and was believed at the time to be its only fluent, though not native, speaker. Selkʼnam religion is usually called polytheistic because of its many named figures, but since only Temáukel is held to be a god and the rest are ancestors, it is better described as henotheistic.
