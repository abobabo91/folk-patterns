## Overview

The Warao are an Indigenous people of northeastern Venezuela's Orinoco Delta, where waterways dominate the landscape and canoes are the main transport. Their name means 'the boat people,' reflecting their deep connection to water. Most of their material culture comes from delta vegetation: dugout canoes, plaited containers, bark-cloth dress, and houses built on stilts over the water. Venezuela's second-largest Indigenous group, they speak Warao, a language unrelated to any other in the region.

## Material culture

The Warao built their entire material world from delta vegetation—canoes, containers, clothing, and houses adapted to water and marsh.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Warao plaiting dominated their textile tradition, with decorated containers showing distinctive snake designs.

- **Twilled cane box** — A lidded basketry box in twilled plaited cane with a lid carrying a snake design.
- **Cokeriti-leaf box** — A box with a telescoping lid plaited from cokeriti leaf, often made by boys.
- **Plaited palm-leaf scoop** — An open-work food-processing scoop made of plaited palm leaf.
- **Snake motif** (*orolli*) — A decorative design worked on container lids.

### Clothing & dress

The only documented Warao garment is a woman's dress made from tree bark.

- **Bark dress** — A complete woman's dress made from tree bark and finished with strings of fiber.

### Architecture

Warao houses are built on stilts over the water, a distinctive form that inspired the name Venezuela.

- **Stilt house** — Houses raised on stilts over rivers and marshes, the iconic Warao dwelling.
- **Platform burial** — A raised platform structure for the dead, documented in an 1881 image from the delta.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The signature Warao object is the dugout canoe, built through a deliberate seasonal sequence using fire and stone or shell tools.

- **Large canoe** (*bongo*) — Holds up to five people, made by felling a large tree and hollowing it with fire and tools, with knowledge passed from the eldest woman to her daughters.
- **Small canoe** — Seats three and used for daily travel to food sources.

### Jewelry & body adornment

No jewelry, body painting, or adornment traditions are documented in the sources.

## Music & performance

Warao shamans perform music including rain dances and songs.

- **Rain dance and songs** — Shamanic performances that invoke rain.
- **Gone with the River** — A 2015 Venezuelan film spoken in Warao by director Mario Crespo depicting Warao culture and river life.

## Dance & theatre

The only named dance is the rain dance performed by shamans; no masked or dramatic tradition is documented.

- **Rain dance** — A shamanic dance accompanied by songs.

## Festivals & rituals

Warao seasonal life centers on crab feasts and the long process of making canoes; hunting is avoided by cultural taboo.

- **Crab season** — A communal food event in July and August when crabs move into the delta from the beach.
- **Canoe-making ceremony** (*bongo*) — A seasonal ritual sequence where household heads choose a tree at the dry season's start and fell it at the end.
- **Hunting taboo** — A cultural prohibition against hunting keeps terrestrial game out of the diet.
- **Childhood water skills** — Warao children learn to swim before they learn to walk and hold tight to their mothers' necks while paddling.

## Foodways

The Warao diet centers on fish and delta plants, with seasonal crabs and palm products, while hunting is culturally prohibited.

- **Fish** — The primary protein in the Warao diet.
- **Moriche palm** — Supplies grubs eaten occasionally and pith added to cassava bread.
- **Cassava bread** — A staple prepared with palm pith as an added ingredient.
- **Crabs** — The seasonal delicacy of July and August.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Warao origin story tells of a primordial hunter who descended from the sky world to Earth through a hole made by a fallen bird.

- **Primordial hunter narrative** — The Warao descend from a hunter who shot a bird in the sky world, fell through the hole it made, and lowered himself to Earth on a rope of heavenly cotton.
- **Gone with the River** — A 2015 Warao-language film by Mario Crespo depicting the choice between tradition and education among Creoles.

## Language & religion

Warao is an endangered agglutinative language isolate with an unusual object-subject-verb word order, spoken by about 33,000 people; religion centers on shamanism and the primordial-hunter origin narrative.

- **Warao language** — An agglutinative isolate with about 33,000 speakers, featuring rare object-subject-verb word order and a small consonant inventory with five oral and five nasal vowels.
- **Shamanism** — Religious practice based on shamanic rain dances and songs.

## Glossary

- *orolli* — A snake design worked on container lids
- *bongo* — A large dugout canoe that holds up to five people
- *cokeriti* — A leaf used to plaited boxes with telescoping lids
- *moriche palm* — A delta palm that supplies grubs and pith for food
- *Warao* — The boat people; an agglutinative language isolate
- *Orinoco Delta* — The landscape of streams, rivulets and marshes where the Warao live
- *cassava bread* — A staple food prepared with palm pith
- *plaiting* — The textile technique of interlacing strips, dominant in Warao tradition
- *shamans* — Religious practitioners who perform rain dances and songs
- *agglutinative* — A language that builds words by joining morphemes together
- *language isolate* — A language unrelated to any other known language
- *object-subject-verb* — A rare word order in Warao grammar
