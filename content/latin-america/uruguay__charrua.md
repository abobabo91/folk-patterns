## Overview

The Charrúa are an Indigenous people or Indigenous Nation of the Southern Cone, associated with present-day Uruguay and adjacent areas in Argentina, including Entre Ríos, and Brazil, including Rio Grande do Sul. They were semi-nomadic and sustained themselves mainly through hunting and gathering, moving in response to seasonal resources, rain, drought, and other environmental factors. In summer, they moved toward the shore to fish and gather clams, fruits, and roots; in winter, they moved inland to hunt deer, rheas, and smaller game. The sources state that approximately between 160,000 and 300,000 individuals in Uruguay, Argentina, and Brazil are believed to be descendants of surviving Charrúa. Their language, Charrúa, is extinct and poorly attested.

They do mention the documentary *Perambulantes* (Brazil, 2009), by Giancarla Brunnetto and Karine Emerich, which concerned Acuab, described as the most notable Charrua chief of Rio Grande do Sul and the first female cacique of the Charruas.

## Festivals & rituals

They describe the Massacre of Salsipuedes, also called *La Campaña de Salsipuedes*, a genocide campaign organized in 1831 under the administration of Fructuoso Rivera. The campaign involved attacks at “El Paso del Sauce del Queguay,” “El Salsipuedes,” and “La Cueva del Tigre.” The sources state that the campaign was launched on 11 April 1831 by a group led by Bernabé Rivera, nephew of Fructuoso Rivera.

The sources also record the capture of four Charrúas at Salsipuedes: Senacua Sénaqué, a medicine man; Vaimaca-Pirú Sira, a warrior; and the young couple Laureano Tacuavé Martínez and María Micaëla Guyunusa. They were taken to Paris in 1833 and exhibited to the public. The sources state that all four soon died in France, together with a baby daughter born to Sira and Guyunusa and adopted by Tacuavé.

## Foodways

They state that the Charrúa sustained themselves mainly through hunting and gathering. In summer, they moved to the shore to fish and gather clams, fruits, and roots. In winter, they moved inland to hunt deer, rheas, and smaller game. During the 18th and 19th centuries, cattle exploitation altered their way of life, causing famine and forcing them to rely on cows and sheep, which were increasingly privatized.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that the life of the Charrúas before contact with Spanish colonists remains largely a mystery because most knowledge about them comes from Spanish contact. The only surviving documents concerning the Charrúa are described as those of Spanish explorers, archaeologists, and anthropologists. A new body of literature is emerging about Charrúa oral history, contemporary ethnogenesis, and activism.

The sources mention a legend concerning the first attack of the Salsipuedes campaign: Rivera knew the tribal leaders and called them to his barracks by the river, later named “Salsipuedes,” claiming that he needed their help to defend territory. They state that the Charrúas were attacked after becoming drunk and off their guard. The sources also mention *Tabaré*, an epic poem published in 1888 by Juan Zorrilla de San Martín about a Charrúa and his love for a Spanish woman.

## Language & religion

Charrúa is an extinct Charruan language historically spoken by the Charrúa people in southern Uruguay. It is poorly attested and known from only a few wordlists; the sources state that it has been extinct since the 19th century, although speakers were reported as late as 1964. It was spoken between the Montevideo area and the Uruguay River.

The sources describe disagreement over its classification. Daniel Garrison Brinton classified Charruan as an independent language family, while later classifications grouped it with Tupian, Guaicuruan, Gününa Küne (Puelche), or Araucanian (Mapudungun). Andrey Nikulin claimed that the three Charruan languages have little similarity and are unrelated to one another, while Lyle Campbell classified them as belonging to the same language family.

Teodoro Vilardebó recorded Charrua vocabulary in 1842. José Pedro Rona and Eugenio Petit Muñoz recorded Charrua data in 1964 from a speaker in Villaguay, Argentina. The sources state that the Charrúa people were also thought to have spoken Guarani and Güenoa by the time of Uruguayan independence.
