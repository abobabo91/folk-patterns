## Overview

The Wayampi, also known as Wayãpi and by several other names, are an Indigenous people living in the south-eastern border area of French Guiana and in Brazil, especially around the Camopi and Oyapock rivers and the Amapari and Carapanatuba river basins. Approximately 2,171 people belong to the group: approximately 950 live in French Guiana and 1,221 in Brazil. In Brazil, most live in the Terra Indígena Waiãpi, an autonomous district established in 1996. The Wayampi speak a Tupi–Guarani language belonging to the Tupian language family. Their folk-culture profile in the sources centers on riverine settlement, slash-and-burn agriculture, hunting, fishing, baskets, cotton-woven hammocks, trade, and the continued use of their language.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Cotton-woven hammocks** — Cotton-woven hammocks are among the local manufactures that continued after Western goods replaced many other products from the late 1970s.

**Baskets** — Baskets are another local manufacture that remained in use despite the increased circulation of Western goods.

### Architecture

They state that Wayampi communities include villages, hamlets, and subsistence farms, and that the Wayampi historically built only temporary canoes.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Wayampi traditionally traded cotton thread, hunting dogs, and feather crowns mainly for tools. Since the late 1970s, ammunition, fishhooks, pans, and glass beads have increasingly been traded, while baskets and cotton-woven hammocks have remained local manufactures.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention feather crowns as objects exchanged in trade.

## Festivals & rituals

They report that Jesuit missionaries opened a mission near the current town of Camopi in 1738, that the missionaries brought European diseases, and that the Jesuits left in 1763, after which most of the population dispersed.

## Foodways

Wayampi subsistence is based primarily on slash-and-burn agriculture, with cassava, sweet potatoes, yams, and bananas as important crops. Hunting is most important among groups of the Amapari and upper Oyapok rivers, while bow-and-arrow fishing is predominant for the northernmost group. In the early 21st century, the Wayampi began building hamlets with subsistence farms several kilometres from the main settlements; by 2010, there were 45 hamlets.

## Oral tradition & literature

They record several stages of linguistic documentation: a wordlist collected by Adam de Bauve and published from 1833 to 1834, later material by M. Leprieur, a wordlist compiled in Carl Friedrich Philipp von Martius’s 1867 book, and Jules Crevaux’s 93-word list recorded in 1875 and published in 1882.

## Language & religion

Wayampi, also called Wayãpi, is a Tupi–Guarani language and a member of the Tupian language family. It is spoken in French Guiana and Brazil. The sources distinguish Amapari Wayampi and Guianese Wayampi; the Wayampi article also identifies Amapari Wayampi, Jari, and Oiyapoque Wayampi. The language is written phonetically using the International Phonetic Alphabet rather than French or Portuguese orthography. Its literacy rates are low.
