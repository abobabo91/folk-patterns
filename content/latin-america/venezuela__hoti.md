## Overview

The Hodï, also called Jotï, are a small Indigenous people living in the Amazon rainforest in Venezuela. The 2011 census registered 982 people identifying as Hodï, while a 2016 estimate counted around 1200. They speak the Hodï language and are closely related to the Piaroa people, although linguistic connections between the two groups have not reached consensus among scholars. The Hodï were among the last Indigenous peoples in Venezuela to make contact with non-Indigenous settlers. Their presence in the Sierra de Maigualida, between the Amazonas and Bolívar states, was first attested by European sources in 1913 through accounts from Ye'kuana people, who called them Waruwadu. They are primarily hunter-gatherers with seasonal nomadic patterns.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Two major Hodï settlements are identified: San José de Kayamá, established by Catholic missionaries, and Caño Iguana, established by Evangelical missionaries.

## Foodways

The Hodï are primarily hunter-gatherers and also practice agriculture. Crops named in the source include plantain, maize, cassava, yam, and sweet potato. Overall, the Hodï grow more than 67 plant species; 36 are used for food, 20 for ritualistic or medicinal purposes, and 11 for constructing artifacts.

## Oral tradition & literature

The earliest attestation discussed is European documentation from 1913, based on accounts from Ye'kuana people, who referred to the Hodï as Waruwadu.

## Language & religion

The Hodï speak the Hodï language. The source describes them as closely related to the Piaroa people, but states that linguistic connections between the two groups have not reached consensus among scholars. They do identify San José de Kayamá as a settlement established by Catholic missionaries and Caño Iguana as one established by Evangelical missionaries, and state that some cultivated plants are used for ritualistic or medicinal purposes.
