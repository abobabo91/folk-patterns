## Overview

The Kalapalo are an Indigenous people of Brazil and one of seventeen tribal groups inhabiting the Xingu National Park in the Upper Xingu River region of Mato Grosso. Their communities are associated with the confluence of the Tanguro and Kuluene, tributaries of the Xingu, and with villages near the Leonardo station. The Kalapalo speak Amonap, a Cariban language, and had a population of 569 in 2010. Their cultural life includes collective fishing, a strict code of ethics, oral traditions, ceremonial dances, dietary rules, bow-and-arrow competitions, wrestling, and participation in the *Jogos Indigenas*. Their social organization centers on villages and household groups, whose membership can change over time.

## Material culture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that the Kalapalo gather shellfish for art and craft-making purposes and describe fishing nets, basket traps, bows, arrows, firearms, fishing gear, and razors among their objects and tools.

## Music & performance

They state that dances take place during certain ceremonies and that each dance has a particular value for the Kalapalo. The type of ceremony depends on whether it occurs during the dry season or the wet season, and animal spirits are believed to protect the living when people dance. The Archive of the Indigenous Languages of Latin America contains recordings of Kalapalo narratives, instrumental music, and text transcriptions from AILLA.

## Dance & theatre

Kalapalo ceremonial dancing is connected to spiritual practice. The Kalapalo believe that animal spirits protect the living when people dance during certain ceremonies, and the ceremony varies according to the dry or wet season. At every ceremony, either males or females lead, depending on the time of year. When it is the males’ turn, females may not look at the males, and corresponding consequences apply when it is the females’ turn.

## Festivals & rituals

They describe ceremonies associated with the dry and wet seasons, during which dances have spiritual value and animal spirits are believed to protect the living. They also state that the *Jogos Indigenas*, described as the Indigenous Games, take place about once a year. People from different tribes gather for competitions, and Kalapalo participants take part in bow-and-arrow competitions and wrestling. In wrestling, hosts compete with guests from other tribes; this practice is known as *egitsu* to the Kalapalo.

## Foodways

Fishing is the Kalapalo’s main source of food. During the dry season in the Upper Xingu region, which lasts from May to September, food is abundant. The Kalapalo use nets, basket traps, and bait to bring fish to the surface, where they shoot them with a bow and arrow; since contact with outsiders, some have also used firearms, fishing gear, and razors. They grow piqui fruit, maize, peppers, beans, and sweet manioc. When planting or harvesting manioc, they often bathe three or four times a day. Every adult is expected to contribute food to the supply, but people who do not or cannot contribute are still allowed a share. Dietary restrictions are connected to spiritual practice: the Kalapalo refrain from eating land animals and eat aquatic animals, including fish. They believe that eating only aquatic animals brings moral beauty.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kalapalo oral traditions include legends describing encounters with white people and Christian rituals. These stories suggest that the Kalapalo encountered explorer Colonel Percy Fawcett and may have been the last people to see his expedition alive. The sources also connect oral traditions among the Kalapalo and related tribes with the history of Cariban-speaking Indians in the Xingu region. In collaboration with linguist Bruna Franchetto, the Kuikuro have created a library of recordings of Kuikuro stories, and the Archive of the Indigenous Languages of Latin America preserves Kalapalo recordings of narratives, instrumental music, and text transcriptions.

## Language & religion

Amonap is a Cariban language spoken by the Kalapalo and is also known as Kuikúro, Upper Xingu Cariban, and Apalakiri. It is spoken by the Kuikuro and Kalapalo peoples in seven villages along the Culuene River in the Xingu Indigenous Park of Mato Grosso. The language has an estimated 1,100 native speakers as of 2006, including 600 Kuikúro and 500 Kalapálo speakers, who speak the same language but are ethnically distinct. The language is described as threatened, although bilingualism in Brazilian Portuguese is prevalent among community men. The sources describe spiritual beliefs involving animal spirits, ceremonial dances, and dietary restrictions.
