## Overview

The Arhuaco are an Indigenous people of northern Colombia, living in the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta and known also as Aruaco, Bintucua, Bintuk, Bíntukua, Ica, Ijca, Ijka, Ika and Ike. Their settlements occupy the upper valleys of the Piedras, San Sebastian, Chichicua, Ariguani and Guatapuri rivers; their capital is Nabusimake. They speak Arhuaco, commonly called Ikʉ, a Chibchan language with some 8,000 speakers, 90% of them monolingual, and they are descendants of the Tairona culture. Their traditional territory was once larger, and the lower slopes lost to colonisation still hold sacred sites they visit to leave offerings. For folk-culture study they matter above all because their material world — woven bags, bast caps, reed flutes, clay cooking pots — is still made rather than remembered, and because they hold that the Sierra Nevada is the heart of the world on whose well-being everything else depends.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Arhuaca mochila** — The handmade bag that is the people's best-known craft. Made for household use and also for sale in the lower lands, it has become fashionable in Bogotá and other large Colombian cities among both men and women, and is one of the few Arhuaco products, alongside coffee, made for the market rather than the family.

***Mákke*** — A woman's mantle of cotton, described in the Bolinder collection as drawn through at top and bottom with thread of a different kind, which gives the finished garment its contrasting bands.

***Posa*** — A child-carrying cloth of wool with black stripes. The Arhuaco carry children in it until they are quite large and able to walk well, so the piece is worn hard and made accordingly.

***Si-sittjå*** — A second kind of child-carrier, made not of wool but of bast, showing that the same object is made in two fibre traditions side by side.

***Totto*** — A bag worked in bast and coloured with aniline dye, the technique recorded as *méria-teknink*. It places imported dyestuff inside an entirely local looped-bast structure.

The men of the community produce the traditional clothing entirely, and cotton and wool are both grown or raised at home: cotton in the mid-lands, sheep among the livestock of the higher slopes.

**Motif vocabulary.** The one motif the sources name is on a honey gourd, *yoámsi*: a circle with a dot at its centre, the dot standing for the Iku people and the circle for their land, *Kauseránkua*, with its boundary drawn as the black line.

### Clothing & dress

Men make all of the traditional dress, although modern clothing is now worn as well. The men's head covering is the ***tóttosåma***, a cap made of bast — the fibre recorded as *becha* — and the collection holds both new and used examples. A second bast cap of the same name appears as *tottasåma*, so the term covers the everyday male cap generally. Ceremonial headgear is separate and unmistakable: the ***kákko*** is a cotton cap with feather ornaments, made for the secret religious dance, and it should properly be made from the feathers of every bird on earth; its name is the same as the name of the sun. Women wear the *mákke*, a cotton mantle. A man's costume in the collection includes its belt, and the British Museum holds Arhuaco trousers and a poncho — the latter a long rectangular piece woven of cotton and wool with a slit at the centre for the head. Infants are carried in the *posa* or the bast *si-sittjå* rather than dressed in miniature adult clothing.

### Architecture

Nabusimake, the capital and the most important gathering place of the twenty-two Arhuaco sections, is composed of fifty square-shaped houses and circular temples called ***kankurwa***, one for men and one for women. The population is dispersed across the valleys and comes together in these towns for reunions and ceremonies. Alongside the square dwellings the sources record a distinct round form: a photograph taken by Gustaf Bolinder in 1914 shows the round grass hut of a *mama*, the spiritual authority, at Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta — a separate building type for a separate office. Domestic architecture is duplicated by altitude rather than elaborated: each family owned two houses, one in the cool high lands and one in the warmer lower lands, and moved between them with the agricultural year. Since the expropriations of the colonial period and after, only the high-land house remains usable for cultivation. Furniture is sparse and consistent: the wooden stool *kankávoe* occurs in several examples in every hut, used for sitting at weaving, cooking and similar work.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Cooking pottery is the ***mandtjiki***, a clay vessel for boiling. Old examples are found frequently in the graves of the Indigenous dead and are valued highly, being reckoned better than the modern ones, and were used particularly by the medicine men; a separate pot is kept for cooking at religious feasts. Clay vessels are dried on a tray, the ***kampátiru***. For coca chewing there is the ***dtjåborrå***, a vessel for holding lime, taken up on a stick, mixed with saliva and coca juice, and turning yellow in the process. Wooden and plaited household goods include the carved spoon *kantiru*, the large lidless basket *kákkå*, the square palm-leaf fire fan *gäjjvaue*, a carved horn for storing resin, and a honey gourd with lid, *yoámsi*. Metal appears mainly as the iron point of the lance *garåttja*. Hunting and boys' gear: the bow *kámme* of guava wood, the bow *kammäjj*, the bird arrow *tina* tipped with resin, the boy's arrow *tinö* with a blunt wax point, and the sling *sipätå* for bast cords.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Necklaces are worn in quantity: of the strands of imported glass beads — the green ones called *tjikrúsa* — at least several dozen are worn at a time. A simpler ornament, the ***minágro***, is strung from seeds and is recorded as the plain adornment of poor girls, which makes bead necklaces a marker of means as well as of dress. A third named necklace type, *tjamitj*, is in the collection. Stone is used for amuletic rather than decorative ends: *tumas* is a polished stone bead described as magical. The feathered *kákko* cap belongs to ceremonial adornment rather than jewelry.

## Music & performance

The instruments in the Bolinder collection form a compact but well-documented ensemble. Two reed flutes share the name ***tjáro***: one with five finger holes and a mouthpiece of quill and resin, which is always blown as a duet together with its companion, and one with a single hole. That paired playing — an instrument catalogued as never sounded alone — is the most specific performance fact the sources give. The panpipe ***ponnkri*** appears in several forms, including one of nine reed pipes. Percussion and shaken instruments include the ***tjambår***, a wooden drum with a skin stretched over it; the rattle ***maráka***, a dried fruit shell with the seeds left inside; and ***marákka***, the same idea made from a gourd. A conch trumpet completes the set. Performance context is named only once and obliquely, but decisively: the feathered *kákko* cap was made for the secret religious dance, and one clay pot in the collection is reserved for cooking at religious feasts — so music, masked or feathered dress and communal eating belong to the same ceremonial occasions, held in the towns where the dispersed population gathers.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name one dance and name it only by its function: the secret religious dance for which the feathered *kákko* cap is made, whose headgear should be assembled from the feathers of every bird on earth and whose name is shared with the sun. Nothing further about choreography, occasion or any dramatic tradition appears in the sources used.

## Festivals & rituals

Ritual life is organised around the *mamo* (also *mamü*), the spiritual authority of Arhuaco society, and around the sacred sites of the Sierra. Each *mamo* is chosen from among candidates aged eight to ten and trained for a minimum of nine years, fifteen on average, staying in a cave for nine years while the elders teach him; afterwards he is free to decide whether to continue. *Mamos* specialise — philosophy, priestly office, medicine, or practical counselling of individuals and the community — and their influence in society is decisive. The framework they serve is *Kunsamü*, the single sacred law of nature and society, immutable and pre-existent, which is represented by a boy, Mamo Niankua; the material world may cease to exist while the law continues unaltered. Offerings are made at sacred sites, including those on the lower slopes now outside the recognised territory, which the Arhuaco still travel to. Mountain peaks are themselves sacred, which is why the television communications tower imposed on Mount Alguacil in 1962 caused such outrage. Feasts have their own material equipment — a clay pot kept for cooking at religious festivals, the feathered cap for the secret dance.

## Foodways

Farming is subsistence agriculture, traditionally worked by each family on its own parcel beside the house, and the crop list is split sharply by altitude. The high lands give potatoes, onions, cabbages, lettuce, blueberries, tamarillos, pumpkins, garlic and wheat. The mid-lands give corn, beans, yuca, arracacha, malanga, coca, cotton, pineapple, papaya, guava, passion fruit, sweet granadilla, oranges and limes. Chickens, cattle, sheep and goats are raised. Coffee is grown for sale only, alongside the *mochilas* and other crafts exchanged in the lower lands for what the high lands do not yield. Since the expropriation of land the mid-altitude cultivation has largely been lost, so the two-altitude system that the crop list describes no longer operates as it did.

Coca chewing is the best-documented consumption practice, and it is traditional and non-commercial, distinct from the cocaine the drug traffickers began producing by chemical process in the Sierra from the 1970s. Lime is kept in the *dtjåborrå*, lifted out on a stick, and mixed with saliva and coca juice, colouring yellow. A honey gourd, *yoámsi*, holds a dark brown hard paste — a strong seasoning decocted from a thick-leaved plant said to contain a painkilling agent — of which a little is taken out on the fingernail and chewed as a flavouring with the coca leaves. Cooking is done in the clay *mandtjiki* over a fire fanned with the plaited *gäjjvaue*, eaten with the carved wooden spoon *kantiru*.

## Oral tradition & literature

What the sources record is cosmology transmitted orally rather than a named narrative corpus. The Arhuaco believe in a creator or "father", Kakü Serankua, who engendered the first gods and material living things, along with other "fathers" such as the sun and the snowy peaks and "mothers" such as the earth and the moon. Their account of the origins of matter and of its evolution, equilibrium, preservation and harmony is *Kunsamü*, the universal law, represented by the boy Mamo Niankua. The transmission mechanism is explicit and unusually long: nine years in a cave with the elders, and nine to fifteen years of training in all, covering philosophy among other specialisms. Literacy figures show why the tradition remains spoken — 1 to 5% literacy in Arhuaco, 15 to 25% in Spanish. The request the Arhuaco made of the Colombian government in 1916 was for teachers of reading, writing and mathematics; what arrived were Capuchin friars who forbade the children to learn about their own culture. Documentation efforts named in the sources include an Ika dictionary and an Ika-language version of the National University of Colombia's Faculty of Humanities site.

## Language & religion

Arhuaco, called Ikʉ by its speakers, is a Chibchan language of the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta with about 8,000 speakers, 90% monolingual, and use of it is vigorous. It has 17 consonant phonemes; syllables may open with up to two consonants, the second a glide, and close in a small set of consonants. Stress normally falls on the penultimate syllable with secondary stress on alternate preceding syllables, and some affixes and enclitics do not count for stress. Word order is subject–object–verb. Unusually, person marking on the verb has come to function as a marker of epistemic modality or evidentiality. Religion is the people's own: a creator *Kakü Serankua*, the sacred law *Kunsamü*, the Sierra Nevada as the heart of the world, and the *mamo* as spiritual authority. The Capuchin mission imposed from 1916 was expelled by the Arhuaco in 1982–1983.
