## Overview

The Tzeltal are the largest Indigenous group in Chiapas, living in the highlands in distinct communities that each maintain their own dialect, clothing style, and patron saints. They base their economy on agriculture—maize, beans, squash, and chili—supplemented by handcrafts, particularly weaving and pottery. Their religious life blends Catholic saints' festivals with Indigenous shamanic practice, organized through a system of community sponsors. Each settlement is centered on a main village divided into neighborhoods with their own local authorities.

## Material culture

Tzeltal material culture centers on textiles woven on backstrap looms and slip-painted pottery, both made for home use and regional trade.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Handwoven textiles in traditional Mayan designs dominate Tzeltal crafts, with the strongest weaving centers in Tenejapa, Pantelhó, Larráinzar and Chenalhó.

- **Woman's huipil** (*huipil*) — A sleeveless garment made from two white or off-white cotton panels joined at the sides and open at the center for the head, often decorated with weft-brocaded wool in bands of dog's-paw and geometric patterns.
- **Brocaded huipil in commercial cotton** (*hilaza*) — A version woven with commercial cotton thread and brocaded with diamonds in orange, turquoise, pink, green and yellow on a burgundy ground, with colored triangles framing the neck.
- **Man's sash** — A brocaded band of indigo-dyed and white cotton with orange X patterns in black cross-hatching, finished with multicolored stitching.
- **Embroidered blouse** (*blusa bordada*) — A white factory-made cotton top with a black neck opening embroidered in multicolored flowers in acrylic yarn, showing the blend of industrial and handmade textiles in modern dress.

### Clothing & dress

Dress is strongly gendered and marks community identity, with women wearing black skirts and embroidered blouses while men have largely abandoned traditional attire.

- **Woman's black skirt** — A black skirt worn with a wool belt, part of the standard female wardrobe.
- **Hair ribbons and cloth covering** — Women tie their hair with ribbons before covering it with cloth as part of daily dress.

### Architecture

Communities are organized around a central town or village with dependent settlements, each divided into neighborhoods called barrios or calpuls with their own authorities and sometimes patron saints.

- **House construction** (*pasna*) — The Tzeltal term for house and its construction, from the roots for 'make' and 'house.'
- **Neighborhood division** (*barrios or calpuls*) — Local subdivisions within community settlements, each with its own authorities and sometimes its own patron saint.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Painted pottery with slip-decorated designs is the most prominent ceramics tradition, ranging from functional vessels to figurative pieces.

- **Slip-painted pot** — Round-bodied pots with flattened bottoms, tapering necks and looped handles, covered in ivory slip and painted with brown and red zigzags, spirals, triangles, and curvy lines.
- **Painted jug with spout** — A slip-painted vessel with a bent spout opposite the handle, decorated with the same motifs as standard pots.
- **Animal figurines** — Pottery molded as a four-antlered deer with a candle tube on its back and red chevrons, a slotted money-box animal with spots, and a dog figurine with curled tail.
- **Painted candlestick** — A tapering stem with three tubes topped in curvilinear designs and hashmarks, used for lighting.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Body adornment consists primarily of cloth and hair decoration rather than metal ornament.

- **Wool belt** — Worn with the black skirt as part of women's standard dress.

## Music & performance

No traditional Tzeltal instruments or musical performance genres are documented in available sources, though radio programming carries spoken-word content.

- **Tzeltal-language radio** (*XEVFS*) — A CDI radio station broadcasting from Las Margaritas, Chiapas, that carries Tzeltal-language programming.

## Dance & theatre

Dance and dramatic traditions are not documented in available sources.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual year follows the Catholic saints' calendar, with ceremonies organized by community sponsors and paralleled by active shamanic and magical practice.

- **Saints' festival system** (*mayordomos*) — Annual ceremonies tied to saints' days, organized by sponsors called mayordomos with assistants called alfereces, who often also serve as secular village leaders.
- **Shamanic healing** — Ritual practice based on the idea that illness is a loss of harmony, treated through restoration of balance with the saints, gods, or community rather than symptom alone.

## Foodways

Agriculture centers on the Mesoamerican complex of maize, beans, squash and chili peppers, supplemented by wage labor and handcraft income.

- **Maize tortilla** (*waj*) — The primary staple, made by hand, with the verb for the process, pakʼwaj, literally meaning 'tortilla baking' from roots meaning 'strike with the hand' and 'tortilla.'
- **Mesoamerican field crops** — Maize, beans, squash, chili peppers, wheat, manioc, sweet potatoes, cotton, chayote, fruits and coffee grown in the highlands.
- **Domestic animals** — Pigs, donkeys, cattle and fowl raised for food and labor, with cattle-raising particularly emphasized in lowland jungle settlements.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Tzeltal identify themselves as bearers of the original Maya word, though specific folktales and storytelling traditions are not documented in available sources.

- **Maya oral tradition** (*batzilʼop*) — One of the Tzeltal self-designations, meaning 'those of the original word,' reflecting their role as carriers of Maya tradition.
- **Tzeltal liturgical translations** — Approved by Pope Francis in 2013, covering prayers for Mass, marriage, baptism, confirmation, confession, ordination and anointing of the sick for use in the regional diocese.

## Language & religion

Tzeltal is a Mayan language with five vowels and twenty-one consonants, showing vigor in speech though little use in schools, paired with a syncretic Catholicism blended with Indigenous belief.

- **Tzeltal language** (*Tseltal*) — A Mayan language forming with Tzotzil the Tzeltalan branch, with two main traditional dialects—highland (Oxchuc) and lowland (Bachajonteco)—and agglutinative, ergative–absolutive structure.
- **Dialect divisions** — Four regional variants: north, central (Oxchuc), south and southeast, with the southeast dialect now spoken only by scattered elderly speakers.

## Glossary

- *huipil* — Woman's sleeveless garment made from two woven cotton panels open at center for the head
- *hilaza* — Commercial cotton thread used in brocaded weaving
- *blusa bordada* — Embroidered blouse made from factory cloth with multicolored acrylic yarn flowers
- *pasna* — House construction
- *barrios* — Local neighborhoods within a community seat, each with its own authorities
- *calpuls* — Alternative term for barrios, community subdivisions with local authority
- *waj* — Tortilla, the primary maize staple
- *pakʼwaj* — Tortilla baking, the handmade daily process
- *pakʼ* — Strike with the hand
- *mayordomos* — Community sponsors who organize saints' festivals and ceremonies
- *alfereces* — Assistants to mayordomos in organizing ceremonies
- *batzilʼop* — Those of the original word, a Tzeltal self-designation referring to carriers of Maya oral tradition
- *Winik atel* — Working Men, the primary Tzeltal self-designation
- *Tzeltal* — Mayan language of Chiapas, also written Tseltal
- *Tzeltalan* — Subgroup of Mayan languages including Tzeltal and Tzotzil
- *Oxchuc* — Highland Tzeltal dialect
- *Bachajonteco* — Lowland Tzeltal dialect
