## Overview

The Munduruku are an Indigenous Amazonian people centered historically on the Tapajós River valley and today spread across three Brazilian states. They live on forest margins of navigable rivers and traditional savannah clearings. They are known worldwide for their featherwork and basketry in European collections, and speak a Tupi language with distinctive features like the absence of number words above five.

## Material culture

Munduruku material culture centers on two major traditions: featherwork assemblies built on cotton string and coiled vine basketry, with lesser documentation of everyday objects and architecture.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Two main textile traditions are documented: feather assemblage on cotton string and coiled vine basketry.

- **Featherwork regalia** — Feathers mounted on cotton string or cloth forming head-dresses, crowns, caps, belts, skirts, and limb ornaments.
- **Feathered sceptres and staffs** — Plumed rods combining feathers with cane or reed, using deliberate colour triads of blue, red, yellow or red, yellow and black.
- **Cipó spiral basketry** (*cipó*) — Baskets coiled from vine in spiral technique, including tall and low conical shapes and shallow plaited dishes for carrying and serving.

### Clothing & dress

Munduruku dress as documented consists almost entirely of feather ornaments worn on the head, body, and limbs.

- **Head-dresses and caps** — Open head-dresses of feathers and string, and closed caps or hoods including one made entirely in black feathers.
- **Feather crown** — A separate head ornament of feathers on cotton string.
- **Feather belts and skirts** — Belts and girdles of feathers on cotton string, and a feather petticoat or skirt for the body.
- **Limb ornaments** — Armlets, leg-bands, leglets and paired pendants all made of feathers mounted on cotton string.

### Architecture

Munduruku villages organize households by sex and age rather than family bonds, situated on river margins and savannah clearings.

- **Male house** — A single communal house where all males over thirteen years old live together.
- **Female house** — A communal house housing all females and boys under thirteen.
- **Tapajós fields** — Savannah clearings inside the rainforest where traditional Munduruku villages are situated.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Munduruku everyday objects are documented primarily as basketry rather than pottery or metalwork.

- **Cipó baskets** (*cipó*) — Spiral-coiled vine baskets in tall and low conical forms for storage and carrying.
- **Basket-plaited dish** — A shallow plaited vessel for serving food.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Body adornment is feather-based, worn on limbs and head, with one documented practice of preparing reduced heads for ritual use.

- **Limb ornaments** — Armlets, leg-bands, leglets and paired pendants of feathers on cotton string.
- **Feather crowns and caps** — Head ornaments of feathers mounted on cotton string.
- **Reduced head** — The skin of a deceased man's head dried and prepared for religious purposes, a pre-Columbian practice now abandoned.

## Music & performance

One Munduruku instrument is well documented: a side-blown trumpet that represents a distinct construction type in the region.

- **Side-blown trumpet** — A 95 cm long trumpet held by the British Museum, distinguished from the more common end-blown trumpets of the region.

## Dance & theatre

No sources cover Munduruku dance or dramatic traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

Munduruku ritual life centers on hunting and respect for the Supernatural Gamekeepers who protect game animals.

- **Supernatural Gamekeepers** — Spirit mother deities who watch over each hunted creature and ensure the animals return to the world after death.
- **Hunting ritual obligations** — Rules of respect toward game that maintain the rebirth cycle and ensure the survival of species.

## Foodways

Hunting is central to Munduruku life and governed by elaborate ritual obligations to the spirit mothers of game.

- **Game hunting** — Central to Munduruku subsistence and governed by ritual beliefs in Supernatural Gamekeepers.

## Oral tradition & literature

Munduruku oral history explains their name and their own self-designation reflects a division of the world into people and prey.

- **Munduruku etymology** (*Munduruku*) — The exonym means 'red ants' from enemies the Parintintin, referring to the Munduruku tactic of attacking in masses.
- **Wuy Jugu** — The Munduruku's own name for themselves, meaning 'people' or 'our people'.
- **Daniel Munduruku** — An educator, author and museum director, the group's best-known literary figure.

## Language & religion

Munduruku is a Tupi language with distinctive phonetic features and an unusual absence of number words above five, while religion centers on Supernatural Gamekeepers.

- **Munduruku language** (*Munduruku*) — A Tupi family language with sixteen consonant phonemes, no consonant clusters, and four phonemic accents per syllable.
- **Number limit** — Munduruku lacks number words above five, but speakers map numbers logarithmically and can still calculate larger quantities.
- **Pariwat** — The Munduruku term for anyone outside the group, using the same word that also means 'prey'.

## Glossary

- *Munduruku* — The people's exonym, meaning 'red ants' in reference to their mass-attack tactics
- *Wuy Jugu* — The Munduruku's own name for themselves, meaning 'people' or 'our people'
- *pariwat* — Munduruku word for anyone outside the group, also meaning 'prey'
- *Tapajós fields* — Savannah clearings inside the rainforest where traditional villages are situated
- *cipó* — Vine used in spiral-coiled basketry for carrying and serving vessels
- *Supernatural Gamekeepers* — Spirit mother deities who protect hunted animals and ensure their rebirth
- *Parintintin* — A people historically hostile to the Munduruku
