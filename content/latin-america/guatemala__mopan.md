## Overview

The Mopan are an Indigenous Maya sub-ethnic group living in Petén, Guatemala and Belize. Their recent history includes forced movement by British and Guatemalan officials across the 18th and 19th centuries, eventually settling in San Antonio around 1889. Because they have no written tradition, their culture is passed down orally, making their embroidered cloth, basketry and dance masks in museums especially important as evidence of their traditions.

## Material culture

Mopan material culture centers on embroidered textiles, coiled and woven basketry, carved wooden masks and figurines, and pottery with spiritual purposes.

### Textile & pattern traditions

White cotton blouses embroidered in black cross-stitch with geometric and animal motifs are the primary documented textile tradition.

- **Cross-stitch embroidered blouse** — A white cotton blouse with open-weave embroidery cloth at the neck and sleeves, embroidered in black cross-stitch with birds, flowers and geometric patterns, with horizontal tucks and vertical machine stitching at the shoulders.
- **Pleated and tucked skirt** — An orange cotton skirt decorated with pleats, white lace and rows of tucks, finished with a narrow cream waistband and long ties.
- **Backstrap-loom bag** — A woven acrylic carrying bag on a yellow base with multi-coloured vertical stripes, woven on a backstrap loom with a purple striped handle.

### Clothing & dress

Women wear a two-piece outfit of an embroidered white blouse with a coloured pleated skirt, with stiffened shoulders and decorative elements in the hair and ears.

- **Women's blouse and skirt ensemble** — A white cotton blouse with black cross-stitch embroidery at neck and sleeves worn with a pleated cotton skirt in a strong colour under a narrow tied waistband, with rows of horizontal tucks held down by machine stitching on the shoulders.
- **Hair braiding and yarn ties** — Hair braided down the back and tied with multicoloured yarn, as shown on cloth dolls made by Mopan makers.
- **Wire earrings with glass beads** — Wire hoop earrings hung with green glass beads, worn at the sides of the head.

### Architecture

No information is available about Mopan house form, building materials or construction.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Lightly fired pottery includes figurines and ceremonial vessels, while palm-fiber baskets and carved wooden objects serve everyday household purposes.

- **Hanging incense-burner** — A semicircular pottery bowl with a slightly flattened bottom and three pottery strips rising from the sides with bumps, meeting at a knob pierced for hanging.
- **Coiled palm-fiber basket** (*jippy jappa*) — Lidded baskets coiled from jippy jappa palm fiber with a wrapped fiber wavy line around the rim, finished with an openwork star and central knob, sometimes decorated with plastic beads and buttons.
- **Wicker-work basket** (*basket tie-tie*) — Open baskets made in wicker-work technique from basket tie-tie palm fiber with rounded bottoms, used as containers for shelled corn.
- **Animal figurines** — Modelled pottery figurines including a toucan with incised wings and circles for eyes, and a human figure standing on a four-legged turtle with a geometric bordered shell.
- **Wooden bird trap** — A trap built log-cabin style from slats lashed at the corners with coloured string, propped open with two loose sticks.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Necklaces combine pottery beads and seeds in fixed patterns with modelled pendants, while beadwork and earrings use glass beads on wire.

- **Pottery and seed necklaces** — Necklaces strung on fishing line combining small round pottery beads, larger oblong pottery beads and oblong red seeds in repeating orders, ending in modelled pottery pendants shaped as snakes or triangles with incised patterns.
- **Glass beadwork bracelet** — A rectangular bracelet of flat beadwork showing an orange and fuchsia diamond pattern on a green ground, fastened with a metal hook and eye.
- **Wire earrings with glass beads** — Wire hoop earrings hung with green glass beads, worn on the sides of the head.

## Dance & theatre

Masked dance is the best-documented performance tradition, using carved wooden human masks and animal-head equipment for dancers.

- **Human dance mask** — Carved wooden human face masks with holes for eyes and mouth, threaded with string at the temples for hanging, in two colour schemes: pink faces with black eyebrows and white eyes with black pupils, or black faces with pink around eye and mouth openings.
- **Carved deer dance equipment** — A deer's head and upper torso with a slit mouth, indented eyes and separately carved antlers, painted pink with black antlers tipped in white, with a hole and string for hanging.
- **Carved dog head dance equipment** — Dogs' heads and upper torsos with pointed ears and slit mouths, painted black with a white patch running from between the ears to the end of the snout, with a hole and string for hanging.

## Festivals & rituals

Ritual life centres on Maya-Catholic spirituality with the cacao tree as central to religious celebrations, though Protestant evangelical influence since the 1970s has declined traditional practice.

- **Cacao spiritual practice** (*cucu*) — The cacao tree and beans are believed to contain spirits, and cacao beverages are drunk at religious celebrations as part of Maya-Catholic ritual.
- **Hanging incense-burner** — A semicircular pottery bowl with three pottery strips and a pierced knob, used as ceremonial equipment in religious practices.
- **Protestant evangelical influence** — Since the 1970s many Mopan villagers have left Maya-Catholic faith for Protestant groups and reject beliefs about spiritual aspects of the natural world.

## Foodways

Mopan livelihood rests on agriculture with smallholding independent farmers cultivating maize, beans and rice as cash crops, and cacao as both a financial and ritual crop.

- **Maize, beans and rice cultivation** — Maize and beans are cultivated and rice is planted, serving as cash crops for Mopan farmers.
- **Cacao cultivation and trade** — Cacao has been financially rewarding, with Mopan growers building relationships with buyers including the Hummingbird Highway Hershey Company and Green and Black's.
- **Cacao ritual consumption** — Cacao beverages are drunk at religious celebrations, making it central to both economic and spiritual life.
- **Wicker-work corn basket** (*basket tie-tie*) — A wicker-work basket made from basket tie-tie palm fiber, specified as a container for shelled corn.
- **Plaited hearth-fan** — A plaited fiber fan whose handle splits into strips plaited into a round blade, used for working a cooking fire.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Mopan have no written tradition and depend on oral transmission for preserving their culture, though colonial-era written Mopan uses Latin script with recently standardized spelling.

- **Oral transmission** — The preservation of Mopan culture depends entirely on oral transmission, as they have no written tradition.
- **Standardized written Mopan** — Since the colonial era the language has been written in Latin script with competing orthographies, recently standardized by the Guatemalan Academy of Mayan Languages (ALMG).
- **Mopan New Testament** — A Mopan New Testament was published in a 2004 edition.
- **Mopan Maya–Spanish–English Dictionary** — Charles Andrew Hofling's scholarly documentation of the language, published in 2011.

## Language & religion

Mopan belongs to the Yucatecan branch of Mayan languages with three to four thousand speakers in Guatemala and six to eight thousand in Belize, with distinctive grammar features and a growing Protestant minority alongside traditional Maya-Catholic practice.

- **Yucatecan language branch** — Mopan belongs to the Yucatecan branch alongside Yucatec, Lacandon and Itzaʼ, having diverged from them at least a thousand years ago.
- **Noun classifiers** (*ix, aj*) — Two noun classifiers mark gender: ix (feminine) and aj (masculine), used irregularly for most nouns, do not require agreement elsewhere and do not always follow natural gender.
- **Numeral classifiers** (*-kuul, -tzʼiit, -pʼeel, -tuul*) — Every numeral takes a classifier suffix describing the thing counted: -kuul for round objects, -tzʼiit for long thin ones, -pʼeel for inanimate things and -tuul for people and animals.
- **Verb-object-subject word order** — Basic word order is verb-object-subject, though subject-verb-object is also common.
- **Maya-Catholic religion** — The traditional religion is Maya-Catholic with a growing Protestant minority since the 1970s.

## Glossary

- *cucu* — The cacao tree, believed to contain spirits and central to Maya-Catholic ritual
- *jippy jappa* — A palm-like fiber used for coiled baskets
- *basket tie-tie* — Palm fiber used for wicker-work baskets
- *ix* — Feminine noun classifier in Mopan language
- *aj* — Masculine noun classifier in Mopan language
- *-kuul* — Numeral classifier suffix for round objects
- *-tzʼiit* — Numeral classifier suffix for long thin objects
- *-pʼeel* — Numeral classifier suffix for inanimate things
- *-tuul* — Numeral classifier suffix for people and animals
- *Petén* — Department of Guatemala where Mopan people live
- *Maya Mountains* — Region in Belize where Mopan people live
- *Yucatecan* — Branch of the Mayan language family including Mopan, Yucatec, Lacandon and Itzaʼ
- *ALMG* — Guatemalan Academy of Mayan Languages, which standardized Mopan spelling
- *Maya-Catholic* — Syncretic religion blending indigenous Maya beliefs with Catholicism
- *San Antonio* — Town founded by Mopan around 1889
