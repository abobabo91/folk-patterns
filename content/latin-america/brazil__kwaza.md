## Overview

The Kwazá, also written Kwaza, Coaiá, Koaiá, Koaya, or Quaiá, are an Indigenous people of Brazil. Most live with the Aikanã and Latundê in the Tubarão-Latundê Indigenous Reserve in Rondônia, while others live in the Terra Indígena Kwazá do Rio São Pedro. Their population was 40 in 2008, compared with 25 in 1998. The Kwaza language is endangered and unclassified; it is also described as a language isolate. Kwaza speakers traditionally lived in the high forest of the Amazon, settling around rivers, and oral tradition places them along the São Pedro and Taboca rivers and the headwaters of the Pimenta Bueno River. Historical documentation is limited, and the people experienced displacement, epidemics, enslavement, and cultural destruction after increasing contact with Brazilian society.

Kwaza body adornment included shells, earrings, teeth, and painted body decoration using various dyes.

## Music & performance

Van der Voort’s documentation includes translated songs among the texts collected in *A Grammar of Kwaza*.

The Kwaza historically lived with the Aikanã, Kanoê, Tuparí, Sakurabiat, Salamãi, and other culturally similar groups. These groups intermarried, battled, and joined in festivals.

## Festivals & rituals

Before contact with the “national society,” Kwaza practices included several-month isolation of young girls, anthropophagy, and the sport of head-ball.

Kwaza groups historically joined festivals with Aikanã, Kanoê, Tuparí, Sakurabiat, Salamãi, and other neighboring peoples.

## Foodways

Some Kwaza still plant bananas, manioc, peanuts, yams, and tobacco. They also gather fruits and keep monkeys, birds, and pigs as pets.

The sources connect Kwaza settlement to the high forest of the Amazon and to river environments, including the São Pedro and Taboca rivers and the headwaters of the Pimenta Bueno River.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that oral tradition and sporadic documentation place the Kwaza along the São Pedro and Taboca rivers and the headwaters of the Pimenta Bueno River.

Van der Voort’s *A Grammar of Kwaza* includes texts translated from Kwaza to English. These comprise traditional tales, historical personal accounts, modern personal accounts, and translated songs. The work was intended to bring attention to Brazilian Indigenous culture and support preservation by making the language available for others to learn, including non-Indigenous people.

## Language & religion

Kwaza is an endangered, unclassified Amazonian language spoken by Kwaza people in Brazil. It is also described as a language isolate because research has not established its connection to another language family. It has grammatical similarities with Aikanã and Kanoê. Kwaza also has lexical similarities with several language families attributed to contact.

As of 2005, 25 Kwazá people spoke Kwazá. In 2004, the language was reported as spoken on a day-to-day basis by 54 people in southern Rondônia. Three families speak Kwaza today: two are scattered among Aikanã in different Indigenous territories, and one lives in a Portuguese-speaking town. The language is transmitted to younger generations in two families.

Kwaza has eight oral and seven nasal vowel phonemes and 19 consonant phonemes. Its word order is relatively free, with SOV and SVO structures prominent.
