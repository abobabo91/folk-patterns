## Overview

The Wixárika, also known as Huichol, are an Indigenous people of Mexico who live in the Sierra Madre Occidental range, especially in Nayarit, Jalisco, Zacatecas, and Durango. They also have considerable communities in California, Arizona, New Mexico, and Texas. The three main Wixárika communities belong to the municipality of Mezquitic, Jalisco: San Sebastián Teponohuastlan, Santa María Cuexcomatitlán, and San Andrés Cohamiata. Other communities include Guadalupe Ocotán, Santa Catarina, and Tuxpán de Bolaños. Some 13,000 live in other places within Mexico and the United States. The Wixárika speak Wixarika, a Uto-Aztecan language of the Corachol branch related to Cora and closely related to the Nahuan group. Their folk-culture significance is especially visible in ritual art, beadwork, yarn painting, pilgrimage, peyote ceremonies, and sacred landscape traditions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Embroidery** — Embroidery is listed among the crafts of the Huichol.

**Beadwork** — Huichol beadwork is made with glass, plastic, or metal beads pressed onto wooden forms covered in beeswax. Common forms include masks, bowls, and figurines, and the work depicts patterns and symbols associated with Huichol religion.

**Yarn painting** — Yarn painting developed among urbanized Huichols as a detailed and elaborate modernization of the *nieli'ka*, a ritual tablet. Yarn is pressed into beeswax and pine resin, and modern commercial yarn allows finely spun, colorful, highly detailed compositions.

**Cuchuries** — Cuchuries are woven or embroidered bags included among Huichol crafts.

**Nieli'ka** — A *nieli'ka* is a small square or round tablet with a hole in the center. One or both sides are covered with beeswax and pine resin into which threads of yarn are pressed. Nieli'kas occur in house shrines, temples, springs, and caves.

**Prayer arrows** — Prayer arrows are named among Huichol crafts.

**Weaving** — Weaving is named as a Huichol craft.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name prominent patterns and symbols associated with Huichol religion.

### Clothing & dress

They identify sombreros as Huichol crafts and describe embroidery, woven or embroidered bags, and beadwork.

### Architecture

Extended Huichol families traditionally live together in rancho settlements. These communities consist of individual houses belonging to nuclear families, with a communal kitchen and a family shrine called a *xiriki*, dedicated to the ancestors of the rancho. The buildings surround a central patio. Individual houses are traditionally built of stone or adobe and have grass-thatched roofs. A district of related ranchos is called a temple district, and temple districts belong to a larger community district governed by a council of kawiterutsixi, elder men who are usually also shamans.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention wooden forms covered in beeswax for beadwork, wooden prayer arrows, chimales described as “woods of oration,” and nieli'ka ritual tablets made with beeswax, pine resin, and yarn.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They describe small beaded eggs, jaguar heads, masks, bowls, and figurines as forms or subjects of beadwork.

## Music & performance

The sources describe ritual performance primarily in connection with peyote and ancestor spirits. Huichol peyote practices involve singing, weeping, and contact with ancestor spirits. During the annual journey to Wirikuta, the participants assume the roles of gods along the trail, usually traveling by foot. After reaching Wirikuta, they hunt for the first cactus, share it among everyone, harvest enough peyote for the year, and then eat enough of the plant to have visions. The shaman is described as being able, according to Huichol belief, to speak to the gods and ensure the regeneration of the Wixárika souls.

Ritual singing is the only specifically identified musical practice.

## Dance & theatre

The source names deer dances as part of the ceremonial importance of Cerro Quemado, also called Leunaxü.

## Festivals & rituals

The central annual ritual described in the sources is the pilgrimage to Wirikuta to collect peyote, also called hikuri in Wixarika. Some Wixárika journey once yearly to San Luis Potosí, which they regard as their ancestral homeland, to perform Mitote Peyote ceremonies. Wirikuta is located in the region of Real de Catorce in San Luis Potosí and is central to traditional Wixárika religion.

Before reaching Wirikuta, pilgrims pass the sacred springs of Tatéi Matiniéri, meaning “Where Our Mother Lives,” identified as the house of the eastern rain goddess. They cross steppes known as the Cloud Gate and Where the Clouds Open. The pilgrimage is understood as a return to where life originated and as a means of healing oneself. At Wirikuta, the first peyote cactus found is shared among the participants, after which enough is gathered for the year.

The sources also describe family and community ritual spaces. Each rancho has a family shrine, or *xiriki*, dedicated to the ancestors. Nieli'ka tablets are placed in house shrines, temples, springs, and caves. Through ceremonies, Huichols interact with primal ancestor spirits associated with fire, deer, and other elements of the natural world.

## Foodways

The Wixárika diet consists mainly of tortillas made from Blue, Red, Yellow, or White Sacred corn, beans, rice, pasta, chili peppers, and occasional chicken or pig. From pigs they make chicharrones. Wild fruits and vegetables supplement the diet, including colorines, a legume gathered from trees, and ciruelas and guayabas. In summer, when the rain comes, families live on their ranchos and make cheese from cattle milk. Cattle are usually slaughtered and eaten only during celebrations.

Peyote, or hikuri, is consumed in religious rituals rather than described as ordinary food. During the annual pilgrimage to Wirikuta, participants harvest enough peyote for the year and eat it to produce visions. The source identifies peyote as a hallucinogenic cactus whose effects are associated with psychoactive alkaloids such as mescaline.

## Oral tradition & literature

Wixárika oral history is an important source for accounts of migration and historical identity. It states that the Wixárika came from the region of San Luis Potosí and that, before migration to the Bolaños Canyon region, they considered themselves part of the Guachichil ethnic group. Oral traditions also describe the region’s earlier inhabitants and are presented alongside Tepecano oral history concerning villages such as Santa Catarina.

The sources describe extensive mythology concerning the Sun, rain, maize, children, animals, deer, fire, and ancestor spirits. Named figures include Tayaupá, the Sun; Nacawé, the Rain Goddess; Kacíwalí, the maize goddess; Komatéame, a goddess of midwives; Otuanáka; Stuluwiákame; Na'alewáemi; Tatéi Kükurü 'Uimari, Our Mother Dove Girl; Tatéi Wérika; and Tatéi Niwetükame.

## Language & religion

Wixarika is a Uto-Aztecan language in the Corachol branch and is related to Cora. The source states that it is closely related to the Nahuan group and has features typical of the Mesoamerican language area. It describes four word types according to inflection. Type I words, principally verbs, are inflected for person and mode; type II words, principally nouns, can be inflected for number and possession; type III words include quantifiers and are inflected for case and optionally gender and person; and type IV words are uninflected.

Traditional Wixárika religion centers on the trinity of Corn, Blue Deer, and Peyote, together with the Eagle, all descended from the Sun God, Tao Jreeku. Religious practice includes peyote pilgrimage, ceremonies, shamanic mediation, ancestor spirits, sacred mountains, springs, caves, and house shrines. Most Huichols retain traditional beliefs and resist change. The sources also describe Catholic and evangelical Protestant influence, continuing religious-freedom conflicts, and discrimination connected with Indigenous religious practice.
