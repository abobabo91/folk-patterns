## Overview

The Barasana are a Tucanoan people living in the eastern part of the Amazon Basin, principally in Colombia’s Vaupés Department around the Apaporis River and the Pirá-piraná river basin, with a further population in Brazil. They refer to themselves as the *jebá-baca*, or people of the jaguar, and *Jebá* is their mythical ancestor. At least 500 Barasanas lived in Colombia in 2000, although estimates in the source reach 1,950; a further 40 lived on the Brazilian side. Barasana belongs to the Eastern branch of the Tucanoan language family. Their culture is described through multilingual social organization, language-group exogamy, cassava cultivation, hunting, fishing, longhouse settlement, and Yurupari ritual traditions.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

 They state that ritual ornaments were destroyed during missionary campaigns and that secret masks were displayed to women and children, who had previously been forbidden to look at them.

### Architecture

Barasana families live in isolated settlements in multifamily longhouses. The sources state that these settlements contain units of four to eight families.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention blowguns and shotguns in connection with hunting, and beeswax in connection with ceremonial use.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 They mention ritual ornaments and secret masks in accounts of missionary suppression of Yurupari practices.

## Music & performance

The sources describe Yurupari as a central Barasana cult and culture-hero tradition consisting of a series of rituals rather than a divinity. The Barasana have seven exogamous phratries and five sibs, whose traditional ritual functions were ranked hierarchically. The Koamona were ritual chiefs; the Rasegana were dancers and chanters; the Meni Masa were warriors; the Daria were shamans; and the Wabea were cigar lighters. These functions were restricted to distinct sibs and survive mainly as ideology.

The sources also describe Yurupari rites as reinforcing the separation of men and women. Men and women entered dwellings by different doors and passed much of their lives in separation. Missionaries regarded the central Yurupari cult as the work of the Devil, contributing to the burning of ceremonial houses, destruction of ritual ornaments, and public display of secret masks.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name dancers and chanters as the ritual role of the Rasegana sib and describe Yurupari as a series of rituals. Alfred Russel Wallace recorded the rites of the Yurupary cult in 1850.

## Festivals & rituals

 They describe Yurupari rites as central ceremonial practices and state that these rituals contributed to the separation of men and women. The Barasana have seven exogamous phratries and five sibs, traditionally descended from the Yebi Meni Anaconda people and assigned distinct ritual functions.

The sources also describe jaguar shamanism and messianic shamanism. Messianic shamanism declined with the establishment of Catholic missions in the first decades of the 20th century. Missionary campaigns caused ceremonial houses to be burnt, ritual ornaments to be destroyed, and secret masks to be shown to women and children.

## Foodways

Barasana subsistence combines slash-and-burn swidden horticulture with hunting and fishing. Bread made from bitter cassava is the staple food, and Barasana culture is described as grounded in cassava cultivation. The Barasana also harvest maize, bananas, cooking plantains, yams, sweet potatoes, pineapples, sugarcane, and fruits gathered in the forest or from cultivated trees such as *Pithecellobium dulce*, known as the “Madras thorn” or *mene*.

Fishing supplies most of the protein, supplemented by game, rodents, and birds. Woolly monkeys and peccaries are also hunted. Traditionally, blowguns were used, while shotguns were used more recently. Honey is gathered occasionally, whereas beeswax is highly valued for ceremonial purposes. From cassava leaves the Barasana extract a native chicha. They cultivate coca and tobacco, prepared as cigars or snuff, and prepare the entheogenic drink ayahuasca, which they call *yagé*, from *Banisteriopsis caapi*, a liana known locally as the “vine of the soul” or “vine of the ancestors.”

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe Barasana and wider Tukanoan myths of origin as referring to a westward upstream migration from Brazil. They also identify *Jebá*, “jaguar,” as the mythical ancestor of the *jebá-baca*, or people of the jaguar. The Yurupari tradition is described as a culture-hero cult and a series of rituals rather than a divinity. They state that missionary hostility toward traditional religion contributed to ethnocide and damage to native culture.

## Language & religion

Barasana is a Tucanoan language of Colombia, belonging to the Eastern branch of the Tucanoan family. It is spoken in the Amazonian region, especially along the Pira Paraná River and in the Vaupés River Basin. A 1993 census recorded about 1,990 native speakers in one source and approximately 1,900 speakers in another. Barasana is mutually intelligible with Taiwano, and the two are described as dialect variations of one another, although Barasana and Eduria are also treated as separate languages by their speakers.

Barasana has 23 phonemes, including 11 consonants and twelve vowels, and uses OV word order in basic declarative clauses. It is classified as an OVS language. The language has dialect names including Southern Barasano, Come Masa, Comematsa, Janera, Paneroa, and Yebamasa. Traditional religion includes Yurupari, jaguar shamanism, and messianic shamanism. Catholic and Protestant missionaries entered the region in the last decades of the 19th century, and Catholic missions were established in the first decades of the 20th century.
