## Overview

The Tucano — also spelt Tukano, and calling themselves *ye'pâ-masɨ* (man), *ye'pâ-maso* (woman), *ye'pâ-masa* (plural) — are an Indigenous people of the northwestern Amazon, living along the Vaupés River and the country around it. Most are in Colombia, with a smaller number in Brazil; their language, Tucano, belongs to the Tucanoan family and is spoken in Amazonas on both sides of the border. They are often described as a cluster of separate tribes, but that flattens a social and linguistic order built on marriage between language groups. The objects in this atlas were collected along two tributaries of the Rio Uaupés — the Rio Papurí and the Rio Tiquié — by Theodor Koch-Grünberg on his expedition of 1903–05 and by Douglas Melin on the Swedish Amazon Expedition of 1923–1925. The sources used give no population figure.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Bark cloth** — A rectangle of cloth beaten from bark, its surface left with a wavy texture and its front decorated with geometric designs in red pigment; the British Museum also holds a plain sample of the same material. It is the main patterned flat textile recorded for the group.

**Painted bast dance skirts** — Dance skirts (*dansskört*) of bast cloth carrying painted ornament, three of them collected at Taracuá, at the confluence of the Rio Tiquié and the Rio Papurí. The decoration is applied to the cloth rather than woven into it.

**Beaded dance skirts** — A dance skirt made entirely of glass beads, also from Taracuá, and a further skirt from San Bernardo on the Rio Papurí, which came to the museum with a conservation record attached.

**Feather-and-fibre headbands** — A headband woven of vegetable fibre and mounted with two rows of yellow, red and white feathers, with a fibre tumpline attached for carrying or securing it.

**Palm-leaf plaiting** — A lidded box woven from palm leaves (borassus palm), the body and the close-fitting lid each tied together with string, the lid with *tucum* string. Combs are plaited from plant fibre in the same idiom.

The sources name no motifs beyond the general terms "geometric" and "painted ornament," so no motif vocabulary is listed here.

### Clothing & dress

Documentation is almost entirely of festival dress; the sources say nothing about everyday clothing. Head ornaments are the most fully recorded class: a straw ring set with feathers; a spray of fine heron feathers fixed at the back of a feather browband called *uká*; a feather ornament covered with white down, pushed in at the lower edge of the same browband; and a feather head-dress from Taracuá. To the back of a festival head-dress was fastened a rope of monkey hair finished at each end with tassels of palm-fruit shell, which hung down the wearer's back. Dance skirts of painted bast cloth or of glass beads were worn with these. A photograph from the *bacába* festival at Juquirára on the Rio Uaupés shows the chief in full parade dress standing with his nephews, and another, taken at Pary-Cachoeira on the Rio Tiquié during the 1903–05 expedition, catches a breakfast pause in the middle of a dance feast. Nothing in the sources describes footwear or belts.

### Architecture

Wikipedia records only that a Tucano household is a longhouse, and that on marriage women move into their husbands' households or longhouses, so that several languages are spoken under one roof. Houses appear in the background of Melin's group photographs from Juquirára, but the sources used describe neither their construction, their materials nor their roofing, and no named building type is given.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery is black and unglazed: a vessel from the Rio Papurí waisted in at the middle, still holding remains of *carayarú* pigment (*Bignonia chica*), a paint credited with healing and magical power; a clay pot from San Bernardo; and a black bowl collected on the Colombian side. Woodwork includes a stool with a painted surface. Hunting equipment is represented by a quiver coated with pitch, holding 13 poison darts, and by a case made of sections of cane in which *samaúma*, a tree silk, was kept for winding round the butt ends of blowgun darts. A double-sided comb with a plaited plant-fibre centre once carried feather decoration at its short ends, now lost. The sources record no metalwork.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Necklaces are strung from seeds, and one neck ornament is a drilled cylinder of carved quartz threaded on a fibre cord hung with seed cases. Arm ornaments from the Rio Papurí were photographed together with museum objects from neighbouring peoples. A wristlet of feathers carries a pendant of feather and shell. The most elaborate recorded piece is a dance ornament of four beaded cords hanging from a loop of fibre cord, each cord ending in a cut black conical fruit shell into which a bunch of multicoloured parrot feathers is fixed; it was first catalogued as Tecuna and later corrected to Tucano. Body painting is attested only indirectly, through the pot of *carayarú* colour. The sources say nothing of tattooing or hair practice.

## Music & performance

The instruments collected are small and mostly made from what the forest and the hunt supply. There are pan flutes from the Rio Tiquié and from the Rio Papurí, one of the latter described as a pan flute for a boy; end-blown flute pipes cut from deer bone and from monkey bone; and a bone whistle from Taracuá. Percussion runs from a dance drum collected on the Rio Papurí to a dance rattle made from a calabash, now cracked, and a rattle of fruit shell, both from the Rio Tiquié. The British Museum holds a tortoise shell used as a musical instrument, one end closed with beeswax. Performance context comes from the expedition photographs rather than from any description of repertoire: dancing at the celebration of the ripening of the *bacába* palm fruit, photographed by Melin on 22 May 1924 and published in his *I Amazonas urskogar* (Uppsala, 1929), and a dance feast at Pary-Cachoeira photographed by Koch-Grünberg and published in his account of 1909. The sources name no song genres and give no words or melodies.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is the best-documented performance form, entirely through its equipment and through two days of photography. A dancing stick was hung over the left shoulder while dancing: it is carved from a V-shaped piece of wood, with a flat blade lashed to the shorter arm of the V with vegetable fibre. Two separate ornaments made for dance staffs were collected at Taracuá. Dancers wore painted bast or beaded skirts, feather head-dresses and rattles. At the *bacába* festival Melin photographed fruit bearers dancing without their baskets, and a "ring game" played as part of the same festival at Juquirára. A later museum photograph shows a mask phallus belonging to a dance mask of the neighbouring Wanána alongside a Tucano arm ornament, which places Tucano dance ornament in a regional field of masked performance without describing Tucano masks themselves. No dramatic or puppet tradition appears in the sources.

## Festivals & rituals

One festival is documented in detail: the celebration held when the fruit of the *bacába* palm ripens, recorded at Juquirára on the Rio Uaupés on 22 May 1924. Its photographed elements are a procession of fruit bearers who dance, a ring game, and the appearance of the chief in full ceremonial dress accompanied by his brothers' sons. A separate dance feast, long enough to require a meal break in the middle of it, was photographed at Pary-Cachoeira on the Rio Tiquié between 1903 and 1905. Ritual power is attributed in the catalogue to one substance: *carayarú* paint, held to heal and to work magic.

The central life-cycle rule is marriage. Tucano descent groups practise linguistic exogamy: no man may have a wife who speaks his language, a match that would be regarded as a form of incest, so men marry women from neighbouring groups speaking other languages, and the wife moves into her husband's longhouse. Children are therefore born into a household where the father's language, the mother's language, the languages of the other in-married women and a regional trade language are all in daily use. The sources describe no birth, initiation or funeral rite.

## Foodways

The Tucano are swidden horticulturalists, clearing patches of forest to grow manioc and other staples, and they hunt, trap, fish and gather wild plants and animals. The material evidence matches that account: blowgun darts stored with their poison in a pitch-coated quiver and their tree-silk wadding in a cane case; black clay pots and a black bowl for cooking and serving; the fruit of the *bacába* palm gathered in quantity and carried in baskets to a festival. A photograph from the Rio Uaupés border country in 1924 shows fishermen with a large catfish, taken among the Arapáso, whom Melin treated as a branch of the Tucano. The sources name no prepared dish, no ceremonial food and no drink.

## Oral tradition & literature

The outstanding document is a series of twelve watercolours by the painter Feliciano Lana in the British Museum, each one part of a contemporary rendering of the Tukano narrative of origin from the Upper Rio Negro in Amazonian Brazil — an origin story told in paint by a Tucano artist rather than transcribed by an outsider. Alongside it stands the everyday verbal art of the region: speakers move between languages within a single conversation so readily that they are barely conscious of doing it, and must be prompted before they can count the languages they speak or rank how well they speak each one. Documentation of speech and song is held in the Tucanoan Languages Collection assembled by Janet Chernela, with audio recordings, transcriptions, translations and field notes from the 1970s and 1980s, archived at AILLA. The sources name no epic cycle, proverb collection or riddle genre.

## Language & religion

Tucano (also Tukano or Tucana), whose own name for itself is *yeʼpâ-masa yee uúku͂sehé*, is a Tucanoan language of Amazonas in Brazil and of Colombia; many speakers of the endangered Tariana language are shifting to it. Its phonology turns the voiced stops into nasals between nasal vowels and prenasalises them after one. Because descent groups marry outside their own language, most Tucano speak two, three or more Tucanoan languages, and a longhouse commonly hosts several. The named descent groups include **Bara Tukano**, **Barasana**, **Cubeo** (who do not practise exogamy), **Desana**, **Macuna**, **Wanano** and Tucano proper; catalogue records add the group name Dasea, also written Dachsea or Daxsea. The sources do not describe the religious landscape beyond the healing and magical power ascribed to *carayarú* paint.
