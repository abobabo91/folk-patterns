## Overview

The Kaiabi, also called Kayabí, Caiabi, Parua, Maquiri, Kawaiwete and by other romanizations of *Kaiabi*, are an Indigenous people of Brazil. They inhabit the northern state of Mato Grosso, primarily living in the Xingu Indigenous Park and the Apiaká-Kayabi Indian Reservation south of Pará. Approximately 1300 Kayabí live in the Xingu Indigenous Park. The Kaiabi language is a Tupian language and is spoken in the Xingu Indigenous Park and Apiaká-Kayabi Indigenous Territory. Their folk-cultural importance in the sources lies in their diversified horticulture, woven objects, naming practices, cosmology, shamanic traditions and continued connection to ancestral lands. Their material culture includes sieves, baskets, hammocks, slings and tucum-palm collars.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Hammocks** — Women make hammocks from cotton grown and harvested in their own fields. The sources describe them as woven objects.

**Slings** — Women also make woven slings from cotton grown and harvested in Kaiabi fields.

**Tucum-palm collars** — The main items of present-day produce include collars made from tucum palm. They may be smooth or designed with animal-inspired figures, and the sources identify women as their makers.

**Motif vocabulary.** Animal-inspired figures are the only named decorative motif category in the sources.

### Clothing & dress

They state only that women manufacture tucum-palm collars, which may be smooth or designed with animal-inspired figures.

### Architecture

They mention self-reliant villages and settlements in the Xingu Indigenous Park, but provide no architectural details.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The most notable self-manufactured Kaiabi item identified by the sources is the sieve. Different types include the *apàs*. The sources also describe baskets woven and designed by the men of the village. No ceramics, metalwork, wooden objects or other named household tools are documented in the supplied material.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources document collars made from tucum palm. These are manufactured by women and may be smooth or decorated with animal-inspired figures.

## Music & performance

They describe shamans as important members of society who act as intermediaries between humans and the supernatural world. A shaman’s responsibilities include advising, helping, healing and teaching for the common good of the community.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe agricultural activities organized through four main seasonal stages: cutting down shrubs and trees in May, clearing debris in June, burning the cleared area in August, and planting in September and October. These are agricultural practices rather than named festivals in the supplied material.

Naming is a significant life event. Kaiabi individuals acquire several names at birth and throughout their lifetime. Names may represent personal events or accomplishments, including a rise in status or life-altering experiences. Names can also be taken from ancestors or occurrences within the community, such as market day, the one day of the week or the second day of the week. The child is responsible for making the name known to the rest of society.

The sources also describe beliefs concerning ancestors. About 200 Kayabi continue to live in their traditional homelands outside the Xingu Reserve because their ancestors are buried there. Kaiabi believe that spirits exist after bones have turned to dust and that these spirits can be communicated with and sought for blessings and protection.

## Foodways

Kaiabi foodways are closely connected to diversified agriculture, fishing and hunting. Their two basic types of cultivation field are poly-variety manioc fields and poly-cultural fields created through slash-and-burn techniques. Manioc is planted almost exclusively in the first type and is used to produce flour, bread and porridges.

Poly-cultural swiddens contain maize, cotton, peanut, potato, yam, banana, beans, sugarcane, pumpkin, watermelon and other staples. The sources state that the Kaiabi diet includes seafood and wild game. A typical meal may consist of flour from the cassava root, bread made from that flour and a fish dish. Juices made from fruit pulp, corn, peanuts and other products are also consumed.

The agricultural cycle includes cutting down shrubs and trees, clearing debris, burning the cleared area and planting.

## Oral tradition & literature

They describe a cosmological account in which the universe is divided into overlapping segments inhabited by powerful beings, animal chiefs, cultural heroes, Gods and shamans of the sky. Cultural heroes are believed to have taught the Kaiabi their way of life.

The sources state that the *ai’an* is the soul possessed by every animal, including humans. Humans are not given souls at birth but receive them when they are given names. Names reflect the community in which a person lives and may be associated with the day of birth and whether the child is the first, second or third child of the parents. The supplied material does not describe a contemporary literary revival or preservation project.

## Language & religion

Kayabí, also called Caiabi and Kawaiwete, is a Tupian language spoken by the Kayabí people of Mato Grosso, Brazil. It is spoken in the Xingu Indigenous Park and Apiaká-Kayabi Indigenous Territory. Although the Kayabi call themselves Kagwahiva, their language is not part of the Kagwahiva language. The supplied language source notes that /f/ can also be heard as the bilabial fricative [ɸ].

Kaiabi cosmology includes overlapping segments of the universe inhabited by powerful beings. Animal chiefs include the “anyang and mama’e,” who are said to have the power to take human souls. The sources also describe Gods and shamans of the sky, as well as cultural heroes who taught the Kaiabi their way of life.

Shamans act as intermediaries between humans and the supernatural world. They are associated with divination, healing, advice, protection of tradition and teaching. The sources state that shamans gained the gift of divination through an encounter with grave sickness, creating a link between the supernatural and human worlds.
