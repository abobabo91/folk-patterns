## Overview

The Matlatzinca are Indigenous peoples associated with the Toluca Valley in the central highlands of Mexico, in the state of México. The name refers both to the people of Matlatzinco and to their language, although historical usage could also include speakers of Otomi, Mazahua, or Nahuatl who lived in the valley. The Matlatzinca share cultural similarities with the Otomi and Mazahua. Their language belongs to the Oto-Pamean subgroup of the Oto-Manguean family and is severely endangered. Matlatzinca is spoken mainly in San Francisco Oxtotilpan, while Ocuiltec or Tlahuica is spoken in villages of the municipality of Ocuilan. Their history includes the regional power of Teotenango, the political center at Calixtlahuaca, Aztec domination, and later territorial loss.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources mention the archaeological site of Calixtlahuaca and ceremonial centers, sanctuaries, hills, and caves.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The name Matlatzinca is associated in one explanation with *matlatl*, meaning “net,” because the Matlatzinca used nets to shuck corn; another explanation connects the name with *tematlatl*, meaning “sling.”

## Music & performance

The sources do describe ritual activity within the prehispanic political and religious system: the nobility governed, collected taxes, carried out rituals and justice, and confirmed local rulers after their selection in the towns. The political system was theocratic and organized through a distinction between nobility and commoners. Three high lords were superior to the other rulers. No instrument, song, or musical form is named.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe a prehispanic cosmology in which the universe was divided into four directions and a center, with upper and lower planes. The sun, masculine, sky, day, and man were associated with the upper plane; the moon, feminine, earth, night, and woman were associated with the lower plane. The Matlatzinca worshipped the sun and moon, emphasizing the sun because they were described as a warlike people. Earth, air, fire, and water were associated with protector gods.

Ceremonial centers in towns formed sacred spaces, while sanctuaries could also be located in hills and caves. Named deities include Tlamatzincatl, probably associated with the hunt; Quequex, another name for Otontecuhtli, the Otomi fire god; Oztoteotl, compared with Tlaloc and Tezcatlipoca; Coltzin, an agriculture god important in Toluca; Mixcoatl; and Quetzalcoatl. The number six had symbolic importance in accounts concerning lords accompanying King Xolotl and Matlatzinca lords who left the Toluca Valley for Chalco.

## Foodways

The name Matlatzinca is explained in one account through *matlatl*, meaning “net,” because the people used nets to shuck corn, but no broader foodways are described.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do mention Aztec native historical sources and Spanish chroniclers, while noting that references to “the Matlatzinca” are often ambiguous because they may concern speakers of the Matlatzinca language, peoples of the Toluca Valley, or inhabitants of Calixtlahuaca.

Contemporary documentation and revitalization efforts are described for the Matlatzinca language. These efforts focus on recorded audio-visual interviews, Spanish-translated materials, transcriptions, glosses, and annotated materials. A database assembled in 2019 by Enrique L. Palancar and Leonardo Carranza provides materials intended to support future revitalization, although it is not described as a revitalization project exactly.

## Language & religion

Matlatzinca is an Oto-Manguean language belonging to the Oto-Pamean subgroup, alongside Otomi, Mazahua, Pame, and Chichimeca Jonaz. It has two mutually unintelligible subgroups or dialects: Ocuiltec or Tlahuica, and Matlatzinca proper. The language is also called *bot'una*, *fot'una*, or *mbot’una*, and the sources list additional names including Botuna, botuná, k’a futuna, futuna, maklasinca, maklatzinca, matéame, matlaltzinga, matlatzinca, matlatzinca de San Francisco de los Ranchos, and matlazinca.

The language is endangered. The sources state that it was spoken by around 100 mostly elderly people in San Francisco Oxtotilpan, while Ocuiltec or Tlahuica was spoken by between 50 and 100 people in San Juan Atzingo and Santa Lucía del Progreso. INEGI recorded 1,245 Matlatzinca speakers country-wide in 2020. The language is recognized as an official language of Mexico alongside Spanish and other native languages.

The prehispanic religion described in the sources resembles other Mesoamerican religions, including those of the Otomi and Nahua. Matlatzinca was originally one language, but its varieties are now often considered separate languages.
