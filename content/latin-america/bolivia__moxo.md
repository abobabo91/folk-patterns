## Overview

The Moxo, also known as Mojo, Moxos, Moxeños, or Mojeños, are an Indigenous people of Bolivia living in south central Beni Department, on both banks of the Mamore River and across the marshy plains west of it, known as the Llanos de Mojos. The Mamore is a tributary of the Madeira River in northern Bolivia. Traditionally, they were hunter-gatherers, farmers, and pastoralists. Their present ethnic identification developed through encounters among pre-existing peoples in the Jesuit mission environment. Distinct identities include Mojeño-Trinitarios, Mojeño-Loretanos, Mojeño-Javerianos, and Mojeño-Ignacianos. They numbered some 30,000 in the first decade of the 20th century. Their languages belong to the Arawakan family.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The Moxo built irrigation canals, crop terraces, ritual sites, artificial hills, and rectangular ponds. The artificial hills reached up to 60 feet high, while the ponds reached up to three feet deep; canals connected ponds and rivers in the flood-prone region. The sources attribute these hydraulic works to the ancestors of the present-day Moxeños and date the great hydraulic works of their ancient empire to ca. 250 CE.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery pieces found in the countryside of Santa Cruz Department and in the present-day precinct of Santa Cruz de la Sierra indicate an Arawak tribe known as the Chané with a ceramic-making culture. The Moxos also brought a tradition of incised ceramics during migrations from the lowlands.

## Music & performance

The sources name many rich dances as practices that renew the life of the community and the universe. They also describe a wider Amazonian cosmos in which the sky, earth, and underworld form a tripartite world, with spiritual forces connected to places and things. In Moxos, the principal *dueños* are the spirits of the jungle, connected with the tiger, and the spirits of the water, connected with the rainbow.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe religious rituals involving prayers and natural remedies. According to the account of Amazonian cultures, slipping from the norm brings about a spiritual sickness, which is addressed through a communal search for its cause and through these rituals. The sources also describe ritual sites built by the ancestors of the Moxos and state that the Jesuits accepted Indigenous names for God in their catechism while attempting to embrace cultural elements not contrary to Christian faith or custom.

## Foodways

The Moxo were traditionally farmers as well as hunter-gatherers and pastoralists. The sources mention irrigation canals, crop terraces, and systems of cultivation.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They state that a dictionary of Ignaciano-Mojeño has been published and that the New Testament was translated into the language in 1980. A Mojeño Trinitario DoReCo corpus contains audio recordings of narrative texts with transcriptions, translations, and, for some texts, time-aligned morphological annotations.

## Language & religion

Moxo is also known as Mojo and is a subgroup of the Arawakan languages spoken by the Moxo people of the Llanos de Moxos in northeastern Bolivia. The two extant Moxo languages are Trinitario and Ignaciano; the extinct Magiana was also distinct. The languages have an active–stative syntax and are National Languages of Bolivia. Moxo was the primary lingua franca used in the Jesuit Missions of Moxos. Many Mojeño people also speak Spanish. The sources describe belief in God as father and creator, Indigenous spiritual beings called *dueños*, and Catholic conversion during the Jesuit mission period.
