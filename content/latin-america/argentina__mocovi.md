## Overview

The Mocoví, who call themselves *moqoit*, are an Indigenous people of the Gran Chaco region of South America and one of the ethnic groups belonging to the Guaycuru peoples. In the 2010 Argentine census, 22,439 people self-identified as Mocoví. They speak the Mocoví language, a Guaicuruan language of Argentina spoken by about 3,000 people, mostly in Santa Fe, Chaco, and Formosa provinces. Before the Spanish arrived, they were nomadic and lived from fishing, hunting, and gathering. They hunted deer and rhea, slept on animal skins and flimsy shelters, and did not farm because of poor soil conditions and flooding. After the Jesuits taught them to farm with cattle, they became sedentary.

## Language & religion

Mocoví is a Guaicuruan language of Argentina. The Mataco-Guicurú language family is described as a group of 11 indigenous languages spoken in Argentina, Bolivia, Brazil, and Paraguay, with approximately 100,000 speakers distributed in the Bermejo, Pilcomayo, and Paraguay river basins. In 2010, the province of Chaco declared Mocoví one of four provincial official languages alongside Spanish, Qom, and Wichí. In Santa Fe it is used mostly by the elderly Mocoví population; bilingualism is widespread among adults, while Spanish is preferred by young people. Writing in Mocoví was non-existent until the 1950s, when a writing system developed for the Toba language was later adapted to Mocoví for a Bible translation by Alberto Buckwater.
