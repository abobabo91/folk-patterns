## Overview

The Palikur are an Indigenous, Arawak-speaking people living in the riverine areas of the Brazilian state of Amapá and in French Guiana, particularly near the north bank of the Oyapock River. Their ancestral territory is around the Urucaua River, while settlements in Brazil include Kumenê, Kuahi, Ywawka, Flecha, Mangue 1, Mangue 2, Tawari, Amomni, Kwikwit, Pwaytyeket, Kamuywa, and Urubu. In French Guiana, many live at Trois-Palétuviers and Saint-Georges, with others in Régina, Roura, Lamirande near Balata, and l’îlet Malouin in Cayenne. In 2015, their estimated population was 2,300, including 1,400 in Brazil and 900 in French Guiana. They are socially organized in clans and are regionally known for basket-ware.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

 They state that commercial relations with Europeans involved exchanges of river and forest products for clothes and glass beads, and that the Palikur manufacture objects from cotton seed.

### Architecture

 They state that a Portuguese expedition in the late 18th century burned all Indian villages in the territory and deported the Palikur into the interior of Brazil.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Palikur manufacture objects of wood, bone, feathers, and cotton seed. Their economy has included bow and arrow fishing, hunting, horticulture, and the use of tools, harpoons, shotguns, cotton fishing lines, and other goods acquired through commerce.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention glass beads in commercial exchange.

## Music & performance

 They state that the Palikur were involved in a shamanic war in the early 1960s, but provide no further description of its performance or ritual form.

## Festivals & rituals

 They state that the Palikur resisted missionary activities, that part of the Brazilian community relocated to French Guiana after a shamanic war in the early 1960s, and that conversion to Pentecostalism contributed to greater responsiveness to the Brazilian government from the late 1960s.

## Foodways

Manioc is the main cultivated plant. It is roasted or used to prepare flat cakes and beer. The Palikur subsist largely on bow and arrow fishing, supplemented by hunting and horticulture. They also cultivate sweet potatoes, sugarcane, peppers, gourds, cotton, papayas, mangoes, coffee, and citrus trees. Until the end of the 19th century, roasted manioc flour was their main commercial surplus.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They cite a work on Palikur oral tradition: *La Cote d’Amapa, de la bouche de l’Amazone å la baie d’Oyapock å travers la tradition orale palikur*, by Françoise and Pierre Grenand, published in 1987.

## Language & religion

The Palikur Nation, or *naoné*, is Arawak-speaking. The main language is Palikúr, used on both the Brazilian and French sides; French Guianese Creole serves as a common language between tribes and with the local population, while knowledge of French and Portuguese is common. Palikúr is considered endangered in French Guiana and vulnerable in Brazil. The language has dependent and independent personal pronouns, marks the verb’s object with suffixes, and marks the noun complement with a possessive prefix. The sources state that the Palikur resisted missionary activities, that many began converting to Pentecostalism in the late 1960s, and that a shamanic war contributed to a community division in the early 1960s.
