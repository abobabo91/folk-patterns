## Overview

The Otomi are Mexico's fifth-largest Indigenous people, spread across discontinuous regions of central Mexico. Their territory and settlements were shaped by dependence on the maguey plant, which provided both fiber for weaving and pulque for sustenance. They are known especially for two crafts: backstrap-loom weaving and embroidery in natural fibers, and the cut-paper spirit figures of San Pablito. Despite colonial pressure and language shift toward Spanish, Otomi communities continue to practice weaving, paper-working, shamanism, and maintain older spiritual beliefs alongside Catholicism.

## Material culture

Otomi material culture centers on textiles woven on backstrap looms from maguey fiber and cotton, decorated with embroidery and geometric or figurative patterns, and on ritual objects cut from bark paper.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Otomi weaving combines backstrap-loom technique with bold embroidery in cotton and wool, featuring animals, stars, flowers, and geometric patterns.

- **Quechquemitl** — A shoulder garment made of two woven rectangles forming a diamond shape, worn with the points to the sides.
- **Huipil** — A rectangular blouse of commercial cotton with a neck slit, embroidered on yoke and shoulders in multicolored wool.
- **Faja and belt** — Backstrap-loom waist sashes in cream cotton with black stripes of stylized birds and animals, and red diamond patterns.
- **Ayate** — An open-mesh carrying cloth woven from agave fiber, two webs seamed into a square, often embroidered with the Mexican eagle.
- **Morral** — A shoulder bag of agave fiber with designs of roosters, birds facing trees, and stars on the flap.

### Clothing & dress

Otomi women wear wrapped skirts, embroidered blouses, shoulder garments, and plaited palm hats; ceremonial dress includes silk ribbons and lace.

- **Blusa bordada** — An embroidered woman's blouse cut from factory calico with floral and geometric designs in running stitch on yoke and shoulders.
- **Wrapped skirt** — Backstrap-woven from two webs of brown wool with blue or red geometric stripes, sewn together with raw and selvedge edges.
- **Palm-fiber hat** — A plaited hat with tall indented crown, wide brim with white spiral stitching, and black plastic band.

### Architecture

Little is documented, but in the Mezquital Valley dwellings were built low and concealed from maguey leaves.

- **Maguey-leaf huts** — Dispersed, low dwellings built from maguey leaves in the Mezquital Valley, so concealed they could blend into the landscape.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Otomi ceramics are lightly fired vessels with geometric and floral designs painted in pitch; plaited baskets are purchased at market.

- **Geometric jug** — A lightly fired, fire-stained jug with three looped handles and designs painted in pitch above the handles.
- **Plaited basket** (*canasta*) — A diagonally plaited basket bought at market in Ixmiquilpan, Hidalgo.
- **Bark-beater stone** — A rectangular stone with a ridge used to beat bark into amate paper.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment is documented only on paper votive figures, which wear pink glass earrings and black-and-white braided strings.

- **Glass earrings and braided strings** — Pink glass earrings and black-and-white braided strings as shown on earth deity paper figures.

## Music & performance

Prayer songs in Otomi are still heard in parts of Guanajuato and Hidalgo, though sources describe few performance traditions.

- **Prayer songs** — Prayer songs in Otomi language continue to be heard in Guanajuato and Hidalgo.

## Dance & theatre

The volador pole dance is documented through a museum model and ethnographic literature, but details of Otomi performance are sparse.

- **Volador** — A pole dance represented by a model in the collection; documented through Helga Larsen's and Gösta Montell's ethnographic studies.

## Festivals & rituals

Otomi ritual practice blends prehispanic polytheism with shamanism and Catholicism, centered on cut-paper spirit figures representing crops and deities.

- **Cut-paper spirit figures** — Paper figures with hands raised and plants growing from head and limbs, representing spirits of crops like long beans and chiles.
- **Paper votive figures** — Dressed figures representing La Madre Tierra (Mother Earth) and her male consort, made from dark bark paper.
- **Nagualism** — A prehispanic belief practice, documented among Otomi shamans and spiritual practitioners.
- **Moon goddess** (*Zäna*) — An important prehispanic Otomi deity patronized at Xaltocan, Metztitlán, and San Bartolo Tutotepec.
- **Fire-death god** (*Otontecuhtli*) — The most important prehispanic Otomi god, also called Cuecuex, patronized by the Tepanec.

## Foodways

The Otomi subsisted on the maize-beans-squash complex, but the maguey plant was decisive, providing both fiber and pulque.

- **Pulque** — Fermented unfiltered juice of the maguey, central to both Otomi economy and nutrition.
- **Maguey** — The century plant, grown for fiber (henequen and ixtle) and for fermented juice; shaped Otomi settlement and diet.
- **Tortillas** — Staple food made from maize, often eaten with pulque and available fruit.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Otomi had no pre-Columbian writing system but developed a substantial written literature in the colonial period; oral storytelling continues among elders.

- **Codices of Huichapan and Jilotepec** — Colonial-period secular and religious documents in Classical Otomi, the best-known surviving texts.
- **Amate paper books** (*amate*) — Modern books made of bark paper and signed by living Otomi authors like Antonio López Maya.
- **Arte de la lengua othomí** — A grammar written by Pedro de Cárceres, perhaps as early as 1580, documenting Classical Otomi.

## Language & religion

Otomi is a complex of nine closely related tonal languages that split from Mazahua around the eighth century; religious practice combines Catholicism with shamanism and prehispanic beliefs.

- **Otomi language varieties** (*Otomi*) — Nine recognized varieties grouped into Northwestern, Southwestern, and Eastern dialects, all tonal.
- **Shamanism** — A spiritual practice that persists among Otomi communities alongside Catholicism.
- **Ley General de Derechos Lingüísticos** — A 2003 law recognizing Otomi and 61 other Indigenous languages as national languages of Mexico.

## Glossary

- *quechquemitl* — A shoulder garment made of two woven rectangles forming a diamond
- *huipil* — A rectangular blouse or dress, typically embroidered
- *ayate* — An open-mesh carrying cloth woven from agave fiber
- *morral* — A shoulder bag woven from agave fiber
- *blusa bordada* — An embroidered woman's blouse
- *maguey* — The century plant, grown for fiber and fermented juice
- *ixtle* — Fiber extracted from the maguey plant, used in textiles
- *henequen* — Fiber from the maguey plant
- *pulque* — Fermented unfiltered juice of the maguey
- *amate* — Bark paper made by beating bark
- *canasta* — A plaited basket
- *volador* — A pole dance performance
- *Zäna* — An important prehispanic Otomi moon goddess
- *Otontecuhtli* — The most important prehispanic Otomi fire-death god
- *Nagualism* — A prehispanic belief and spiritual practice
- *ñätho* — The Otomi language variety spoken in the Toluca Valley
- *hñähñu* — The Otomi language variety spoken in the Mezquital Valley
