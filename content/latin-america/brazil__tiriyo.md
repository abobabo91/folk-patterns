## Overview

The Tiriyó, also called Trio or *tarëno*, are an Amerindian ethnic group native to northern Brazil, Suriname, and Guyana. In 2014, approximately 3,640 Tiriyó lived in the three countries, mainly in villages in the border zone between Brazil and Suriname. In Brazil, they live in the Tumucumaque Indigenous Park; in Suriname, communities include Kwamalasamutu, Tepoe, and Paloemeu. The Tiriyó language belongs to the Cariban family, and *tarëno* means “people from here” or “local people.” The sources document a history of movement, contact with Ndyuka and European explorers, missionary concentration into larger villages, and continuing tension between traditional tribal government and national governments.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Tiriyó settlements consist of major and minor villages in a difficult-to-access border region. Until 1957, a village could accommodate up to 50 people, and villages commonly lasted three to six years; the death of a captain or chief could lead to abandonment. Newly acquired agricultural techniques later allowed larger populations. In 1967, Alalapadu exceeded 500 inhabitants, after which surrounding soils became depleted and the settlement was abandoned, although some Tiriyó remained nearby. A similar process occurred at Kwamalasamutu.

The sources describe linguistic research and ethnographic documentation. They mention Protásio Frikel’s writings and Peter Rivière’s ethnographic work, including *Marriage Among the Trio*, but the supplied material does not describe music or performance in those works.

## Festivals & rituals

 They state that missionary activity attempted to convert Tiriyó communities to Christianity, but provide no detailed account of religious ceremonies or ritual practice.

## Foodways

 The language source gives examples of Tiriyó words for “bread-like food,” “fish bait,” “fruit,” “leaf, contents,” and “tree, plant,”.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources document Tiriyó linguistic research rather than a corpus of oral literature. The first recorded European contact occurred in 1843 between a “Drio” village and Robert Schomburgk. Jules Crevaux compiled the first Tiriyó wordlist in 1882, containing 31 entries and two sentences in Ndyuka-Tiriyó, a pidgin language. De Goeje later produced a short grammar and a wordlist of around 500 entries.

## Language & religion

Tiriyó, also called *tarëno ijomi*, is the Cariban language used in everyday life by the Tiriyó, most of whom are monolingual. It is spoken on both sides of the Brazil–Suriname border, and its level of endangerment is described as low because it is spoken by the entire Tiriyó population. It has been classified within the Taranoan group of the Guianan sub-branch of Cariban, together with Karihona and Akuriyó. The sources identify two principal dialects: Eastern or Tapanahoni basin, also called K-Tiriyó, and Western or Sipaliwini basin, also called H-Tiriyó. H-Tiriyó is spoken in Kwamalasamutu and along the Western Paru and Marapi rivers; K-Tiriyó is spoken along the Eastern Paru river and in Tepoe and Paloemeu. Missionaries attempted to convert Tiriyó communities to Christianity.
