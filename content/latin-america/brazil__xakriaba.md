## Overview

The Xakriabá are an Indigenous people of Brazil associated with Minas Gerais and the Tocantins and São Francisco river regions. They are one of the Gê peoples and formerly spoke the Xakriabá dialect of the Akwe language, an Akwẽ language belonging to the Ge and Macro-Ge language families. Today, the Xakriabá speak Portuguese. As of 2010, 9,196 Xakriabá people lived in Minas Gerais. In pre-colonial times they lived in the valley of the Tocantins River, in Goiás, and along the São Francisco River. Their documented history includes forced migration, missions, settlement in reservations, and conflict with ranchers over territory.

## Festivals & rituals

The historical sources mention Roman Catholic missions and a statue of St. John of the Indians, in Portuguese São João dos Índios, which appeared at one of the missions and became a patron saint in the region.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Xakriabá language is known through two short wordlists collected by Augustin Saint-Hilaire and Wilhelm Ludwig von Eschwege. The sources also state that only a few elders presently remember some phrases in Xakriabá.

## Language & religion

The Xakriabá formerly spoke Xakriabá, also called Chakriaba, Chikriaba, Shacriaba, or Shicriabá. It is described as a dormant Akuwẽ, or Central Jê, language within the Jê and Macro-Jê groupings. The language was formerly spoken in Minas Gerais and was originally spoken along the São Francisco River near São Romão before 1712. The Xakriabá were forced to migrate after being defeated by Matias Cardoso de Almeida and other Paulistas from 1690 onwards. In 1819, Saint-Hilaire reported that Xakriabá in the Triângulo Mineiro region spoke a Xerente dialect. Today the Xakriabá speak Portuguese. The sources document Roman Catholic missions and the regional importance of São João dos Índios.
