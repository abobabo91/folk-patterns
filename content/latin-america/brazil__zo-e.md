## Overview

The Zoʼé are an Indigenous people of Brazil who live in the State of Pará, Municipality of Óbidos, on the Cuminapanema River. They are a Tupi–Guarani people, and their language belongs to Subgroup VIII of the Tupi-Guarani language family. They are also known as the Poturu, Poturujara, or Buré; the term “Zoʼé” means “us,” in contrast to non-Indians or enemies. They describe the Zoʼé as semi-nomadic hunters and gatherers living in a largely unexplored rainforest between the Amazon River and Suriname. Their documented cultural distinctiveness includes the *poturu* wooden lip plug, distinctive marriage practices, hunting and gathering, and the processing of manioc into flour.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe the *poturu* as a wooden plug piercing the bottom lip, worn by all Zoʼé.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources identify *poturu* as a type of wood used to make the *embe'po* labrets worn by the Zoʼé. They also describe manioc as a root plant that must be processed into flour because of its poisonous properties.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The principal documented form of Zoʼé body adornment is the *poturu*, a wooden plug piercing the bottom lip. The term *poturu* also refers to the wood from which the *embe'po* labrets are made. The sources further describe a practice in which new fathers have the backs of their calves cut with the “tooth of a small rodent.” No jewelry, tattoos, henna, hair practices, or other adornments are described.

## Music & performance

The sources mention *The Salt of the Earth*, a film documenting the work of photographer Sebastião Salgado, including his photographs of the Zoʼé.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe Zoʼé marriage rituals as complex and not fully understood. One or more husbands may be “learning husbands”: young men learning how to be good spouses in exchange for hunting for the rest of the family.

The sources also describe a practice involving new fathers: the backs of their calves are cut with the “tooth of a small rodent.” No annual festivals, seasonal ceremonies, funerals, initiation rites, coming-of-age ceremonies, ritual dates, or broader religious ceremonies are documented.

## Foodways

The Zoʼé are described as semi-nomadic hunters and gatherers. Their favorite prey is monkey, which is plentiful in the region during the dry season. During the rainy season, they rely on a root plant called *manioc*. Because manioc has poisonous properties, it must be processed into flour before it can be used.

## Oral tradition & literature

They mention *The Salt of the Earth*, which documents Sebastião Salgado’s photographs of the Zoʼé, but this is a film about photographic work rather than a documented Zoʼé oral or literary tradition.

## Language & religion

Zoʼé, also called Zoʼé, Zoé, Joʼé, Buré, Poturu, Poturujara, and Tupí of Cunimapanema, is a Tupian language spoken by the Indigenous Zoʼé people of Pará, Brazil. It belongs to Subgroup VIII of the Tupi-Guarani language family and is described as close to the Emerillon language. The language has oral vowel qualities with nasal vowel counterparts.
