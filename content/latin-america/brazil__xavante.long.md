## Overview

The Xavante — also written Shavante or Chavante, and known by the self-designations *Akuen*, *A'uwe*, *Akwe*, *Awen* and *Akwen* — are an Indigenous people of eastern Mato Grosso state in Brazil, numbering about 30,000. Their language, Xavante, belongs to the Central (Akuwẽ) branch of the Jê family within the Macro-Gê stock; a 2014 count put the population at roughly 18,380 people across 170 villages, with 9,600 speakers, some 7,000 of them monolingual. The Xavante originally lived east of the Araguaia River in what was then the province of Goiás, withdrawing westward into Mato Grosso between roughly 1830 and 1860. In folk-culture terms they are known for a dual clan organisation that structures marriage and ceremonial competition, for male initiation rites, and for a body of plaited palm-fibre, cotton, feather and bark objects represented in this atlas by a British Museum collection registered in 1959.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

What the sources do document is plaiting in vegetable fibre and the spinning of native cotton, recorded object by object in the British Museum catalogue rather than under tradition names.

**Plaited vegetable-fibre basketry** — Open carrying baskets with twisted handles, some with the handle itself plaited from vegetable fibre, others with a twisted handle of native cotton thread; lidded storage baskets whose cotton-thread handle passes through a separate plaited lid and is attached to the end of the basket; and baskets made of plaited palm fronds. One carrying basket entered the museum holding eleven unbaked pottery sherds.

**Shallow plaited bowls** — Flat bowls combining plaited fibre with string, a recurring form in the collection.

**Palm-fibre matting** — A sleeping mat woven of palm fibre, finished with a fringe running along its length at the bottom.

**Native cotton thread** — Hand-spun cotton appears throughout the collection as a structural and decorative element: binding the two bamboo slivers of composite combs, binding bundled feather-holders, forming the strings, tassels and tufts of necklets, and dyed with *urucu* for a rattle band and for necklets.

**Motif vocabulary.** The one surface treatment the sources name is the burned-on speckled design on a wooden war club; *urucu* colouring of cotton, bark and grass is a colourant rather than a named motif.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday and ceremonial dress in the catalogued material centres on bark, palm fibre and feather work rather than woven cloth. A **penis sheath** is made from the inner bark of the *palmito* palm. Paired white bands worn round the ankles and wrists are cut from the inner bark of a tree. A **headband** catalogued as religious or ritual equipment is a strip of bark decorated with *urucu*. For dance there is a **cape of palm fibre**, hung by a suspension loop at the top, from which a stout bundle of plaited fibres wrapped with stripes descends, with a deep fringe at the bottom; and a **dance mask** made from a bundle of long grass strands bound at the top with white native cotton and decorated with *urucu*. Combs are composite, their teeth — thirty-three in one example, fifteen in another — set between two slivers of bamboo bound with native cotton. Wikipedia records one further element of male dress: small wooden sticks inserted into the earlobes at the age of fourteen, the adornments being enlarged over the course of a man's life.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery appears in the collection only as raw material: eleven unbaked sherds that arrived inside a plaited carrying basket. No metalwork is recorded. The everyday and weapon forms documented are wooden and bamboo. A **war club** is cylindrical, tapering to a point and curved in side-section, carrying a burned-on speckled design. A **child's arrow** is bamboo, sharpened to a point and fletched with two black flight feathers. A **fibre carrying bag** has a painted handle. Household containers are the plaited baskets, lids and shallow bowls described above, together with the fringed palm-fibre sleeping mat. Composite bamboo-and-cotton combs complete the personal kit.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment in the catalogue is built from cotton string, wax, feathers, seed beads and animal hoof and nail. Three **necklets** survive in related forms: one of native white cotton strings with tassels at each end and cotton tufts, a feather fixed at the centre with wax; a second of the same construction; and a third of cotton coloured with *urucu*, with single-strand tassels at each end, strung with beads and terminating in a deer's hoof with a feather insert — at its centre, fixed with wax, a feather with tassels, one of which ends in four deer's hooves with feather inserts suspended on bead strings. A fourth necklet of *urucu*-coloured cotton carries two strings of seed beads with animal toe-nails and feathers attached to the pendant. A set of four bamboo **feather-holders**, bound with white cotton, holds a small bunch of red feathers at the end, one of them a set of yellow and blue feathers. Bark bands at wrist and ankle and the *urucu*-decorated bark headband complete the recorded body adornment.

## Music & performance

The sources document sound-producing objects rather than song genres. Two **men's ceremonial whistles** are composite, made from seed pods with one end cut off and the other waxed and holding a small string of beads, with pale pink feathers inserted; one is noted as small. A **dance rattle** is made of deer's hooves attached to a cotton band painted with *urucu*, and the hoof-and-bead pendants of the necklets described above would sound in the same register. Beyond these objects, the sources record one modern episode: in 1996 the Brazilian heavy metal band Sepultura stayed and recorded with the Xavante, who feature on the album *Roots* — Xavante singing can be heard in the track "Itsari". A small number of Xavante travelled to São Paulo in 1998 to take part in Sepultura's *Barulho Contra Fome* ("Noise Against Hunger") concert, which opened the tour for the following album, *Against*, and their presence was featured in the video for the song "Choke". The Xavante Language Collection of Laura Graham, held at the Archive of the Indigenous Languages of Latin America, contains one recorded narrative.

## Dance & theatre

No named dances are recorded in the sources used here, but the material evidence for dance is substantial: the palm-fibre **dance cape** with its striped bundle and deep fringe, the **dance mask** of bound grass strands decorated with *urucu*, the deer-hoof **dance rattle** on its *urucu*-painted cotton band, and the men's ceremonial whistles. The one named competitive performance the sources describe is the **log race**, in which the two clans race each other carrying palm tree trunks weighing as much as 80 kg to a defined point.

## Festivals & rituals

Brazil has no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions listed in the sources supplied for this profile, and none would in any case concern the Xavante specifically.

The ritual life the sources do describe turns on the dual organisation of the society. Two clans, the **Âwawẽ** and the **Po'reza'õno**, compose Xavante society, and marriage between members of the same clan is not permitted. Relations between the two are enacted in the traditional log races, where the clans compete to carry palm trunks of up to 80 kg to a fixed point. The Xavante are also known for complex **male initiation rituals**: at fourteen, small wooden sticks are inserted into a boy's earlobes, and the size of these adornments is increased for the rest of his life. Betrothal creates a formally marked relationship, *dasiwasini*, between the couple arranged to marry, who must then use respectful grammatical forms to one another; conversation between a man and his father-in-law is likewise marked by honorific variants used by both parties. A group of Xavante men trained in new forms of pre-existing words, the *a'ama*, are addressed by other members of the clan with a specific set of morphological forms. The one item in the atlas explicitly catalogued as ritual equipment is the *urucu*-decorated bark headband.

## Foodways

The sources record only the surrounding conditions: disease and famine during the twentieth-century relocations, forest burned to pasture and wasteland by land-grabbers and squatters on lands the Xavante later recovered, and monocultures such as entire fields of rice planted on land transferred to settlers.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources name no epic or folktale cycle, but they do record the documentation of Xavante speech. The Programa de Documentação de Culturas Indígenas (PRODOCULT), run by J. R. Welch and R. Costa, maintains a permanent Xavante collection of images, text and cartography at the Museum of the Indian. The Xavante Language Collection of Laura Graham at the Archive of the Indigenous Languages of Latin America holds a recorded narrative. Lexicography began with the *Pequeno Dicionário: Xavante-Português, Português-Xavante* compiled by Joan Hall, Ruth McLeod and Valerie Mitchell in 1987 and revised by the same three authors in 2004; Myriam Lapierre and colleagues deposited Xavante field materials with the Survey of California and Other Indian Languages in 2019. Speech itself carries strong social marking: children are scolded if they fail to use respectful grammatical forms to parents and grandparents as they grow older.

## Language & religion

Xavante is a Central (Akuwẽ) Jê language of the Macro-Gê stock, grouped with Xerente, Xakriaba and Akroa; it has been written as Chavante and Shavante and is also called Akuen, Akwen, A'uwe Uptabi, A'we, Crisca, Pusciti and Tapuac. It has nine vowel qualities, long and short, four of them nasalizable, and only ten consonants — remarkable for lacking velar consonants apart from /w/, a loss traced to a historical shift from velars to glottal stops. Word order is ergative object–agent–verb, and morphology encodes honorific and endearment relations. Speakers of all ages use it vigorously and hold positive attitudes toward it. On religion the sources record only that missionaries invaded Mato Grosso during the Vargas era attempting to convert the Xavante to Catholicism and were attacked in return for the unsolicited intrusion. Non-Xavante people are called *waradzu*, and distrust of them follows from enslavement in the eighteenth century and the massacres and epidemics of the National Integration Program years, 1946–1957.
