## Overview

The Abipones were an Indigenous people of Argentina’s Gran Chaco region and Paraguay who spoke Abipón, an extinct Guaicuruan language. They initially occupied the lower portions of the Bermejo River and were seasonally mobile hunters, gatherers, fishers, and, to a limited extent, farmers. After obtaining the horse from Spanish settlers by 1641, they abandoned farming and increasingly relied on mounted hunting, cattle and horse raiding. By 1768, disease had reduced their numbers to below 5,000, and the expulsion of the Jesuits further disrupted their communities. The Abipones ceased to exist as an independent ethnic group in the early 19th century; survivors assimilated into Argentine society, and the last speaker of Abipón is believed to have died in the 19th century.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do record that men in battle wore armour fashioned from tapir’s hide, over which a jaguar’s skin was sewn. The sources also state that women’s faces, breasts, and arms were tattooed with black figures of various designs according to age and social status. These markings are described as bodily adornment rather than as garments.

### Architecture

During the five-month-long flood season, the Abipones lived on islands or in shelters built in trees.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They identify weapons made from a local hardwood known as *netergé–*: the bow and arrow, spear, and club. Boleadoras are also named among their weapons.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources describe several forms of body modification and marking. Women tattooed their faces, breasts, and arms with black figures whose designs varied according to age and social status. The lips and ears of both genders were pierced. Men plucked the hair from the forehead to the crown as a tribal mark.

## Dance & theatre

They mention religious rites in which women held considerable power, without describing movement, choreography, or dramatic representation.

## Festivals & rituals

They describe the Abipones as sharing most customs of the Guaycurú, including the couvade, but provide no further explanation of that practice. The sources state that they rarely married before the age of 30 and describe a marriage arrangement in which a man bargained with a girl’s parents about the price; they also report that the girl could reject the arrangement, run away, and hide. Infanticide was common, no more than two children were reared in one family, and young children were suckled for two years.

## Foodways

Before the introduction of the horse, the Abipones subsisted through hunting, fishing, food gathering, and a limited amount of agriculture. After acquiring horses, they shifted away from agriculture and toward mounted hunting of wild cattle, rhea, guanaco, deer, and peccary, as well as cattle and horse raiding.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Abipón language is described as extinct; it was also known as Abipone, Callaga, and Apibon. The sources list historical linguistic works and a phonological inventory project.

## Language & religion

Abipón was a Guaicuruan language formerly spoken in Argentina by the Abipón people. It is extinct, and its last speaker is thought to have died in the 19th century. The language was also known as Abipone, Callaga, and Apibon. From 1750, Jesuit missions were established among the Abipones, mainly through Martin Dobrizhoffer, who had been a missionary in Paraguay for eighteen years; by that period they had been largely Christianized and turned sedentary. Their colonies experienced continuing conflict with Spanish settlers and raids by the Tobas and Mocovís, described as hostile Guaycuru peoples.
