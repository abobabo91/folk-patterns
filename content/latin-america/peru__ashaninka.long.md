## Overview

The Asháninka are an Indigenous people of the Amazonian rainforests of central-eastern Peru — the regions of Junín, Pasco, Huánuco and part of Ucayali — and of the Brazilian state of Acre. Their settlements follow the rivers: the Lower Apurímac, Ene, Tambo, Satipo, Perené, Pichis, Lower Urubamba, Upper Ucayali, Pachitea and Yurúa and their tributaries, together with the interfluvial grassland of the Gran Pajonal. Estimates of their number range from 25,000 to nearly 100,000, of whom rather more than a thousand live on the Brazilian side. Their language belongs to the Arawakan (Maipurean) family. In folk-culture terms they are known above all for a woven cotton robe made entirely within the household — spun, dyed and woven by women on looms — and for a body-painting practice using the crushed red seeds of the annatto tree.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The catalogue records of the objects shown here, and the Wikipedia articles, together describe a cotton-weaving tradition but name no separate regional weaves or named techniques. What they do document:

**Robe** — the central Asháninka garment: cotton that women collect, spin, dye and weave on looms. The dye is either brown or a bright royal blue, the shoulders are ornamented with seeds, and a full-length robe can take up to three months to finish.

**Striped woven cloth** — the museum pieces show the stripe as the recurring structure of the woven cotton: a sleeveless tunic, pale brown with dark brown vertical stripes; a shoulder bag of woven cotton with vertical stripes in beige and brown, its strap sewn down the edges with medium and dark blue geometric designs and tasselled at the end; and a hood of cotton cloth with brown stripes.

**Painted cloth** — a second decorative register, applied rather than woven. Several of the smocks in the collection are painted, and two of the woven cotton bags carry painted decoration or painted designs.

**Baby-carriers** — cotton textile carriers, hung with ornaments: pendant incised bones on some, seeds and nuts on others. They are the one object type in this collection that combines woven cloth with worked bone and forest seeds as a matter of course.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no motifs. They describe only vertical stripes in the woven cloth and geometric designs on a bag strap.

### Clothing & dress

The everyday garment for both sexes is a cotton robe (the museum records enter comparable pieces as smocks and as a sleeveless tunic). Women wear their hair long and over the shoulder; men wear theirs short, or in "bowl" cuts below the ear. Necklaces and bracelets are worn in quantity around the neck, made with seeds, with the teeth of tapir, peccary and monkey, and with brightly coloured feathers. Men, women and children paint their faces in a variety of designs with the crushed bright red seeds of the achiote fruit (*Bixa orellana*, annatto). Ceremonial dress separates the sexes: for ceremonies men wear woven circles of palm leaves decorated with feathers on their heads, and women wear a woven cotton head-dress. The collection holds several pieces that match this description — a hood of cotton cloth decorated with parrot feathers along the bottom, a head-cover or hood of textile and feathers, cane rings recorded as possible head-dresses, and belt fringes made of feathers, probably parrot, and seeds.

### Architecture

The sources used here — the Wikipedia articles on the Asháninka and their language, and the catalogue records of the objects in this gallery — say nothing about Asháninka house forms, building materials or settlement layout, beyond noting that residential groups of around five nuclear families lived dispersed along the river valleys under a local chief, or *kuraka*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources describe no Asháninka pottery or metalwork. What they do record of everyday equipment is subsistence gear and containers. Hunting and fishing are carried out primarily with bows and arrows or with spears. Cultivation is by the slash-and-burn method, clearing land for yucca roots, sweet potato, corn, bananas, rice, coffee, cacao and sugar cane. The carried objects in the collection are all textile: bags of woven cotton cloth, some plain, some with painted decoration or painted designs; a shoulder bag with a sewn, tasselled strap; and the cotton baby-carriers with their bone, seed and nut pendants. Cane appears as a worked material in the ring-shaped pieces recorded as head-dresses.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment draws on the forest rather than on metal. Necklaces and bracelets are made from seeds, from the teeth of tapir, peccary and monkeys, and from brightly coloured feathers. One necklace in the collection is sixteen strings of black seeds tied together at each end with a string to fasten it round the neck; a second is likewise of seeds. Feathers — parrot feathers where the museum identifies them — are worked into belt fringes together with seeds, sewn along the bottom edge of a cotton hood, and combined with textile in a head-cover. Seeds are also the ornament sewn to the shoulders of the robe. Body painting is face painting: the crushed red annatto seed, applied by men, women and children alike in a variety of designs. The sources record no tattooing.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used here give no festival calendar and no account of birth, initiation, marriage or funeral rites. The only ceremonial practice they record is dress: for ceremonial purposes men wear woven circles of palm leaves decorated with feathers on their heads, and women wear a woven cotton head-dress. Face painting with crushed annatto seed is described as a general practice of men, women and children rather than as tied to a particular occasion.

## Foodways

Subsistence agriculture is the base. Fields are cleared by slash-and-burn and planted with yucca roots, sweet potato, corn, bananas, rice, coffee, cacao and sugar cane, in what the source describes as biodiversity-friendly techniques. This is supplemented by hunting and fishing — with bows and arrows or spears — and by gathering fruit and vegetables in the jungle. Biodiversity is described as the foundation of the Asháninka way of life, treated as natural capital, which is why the threat to the forest is also a threat to the food base. The sources name no prepared dishes, no cooking methods beyond the clearing and planting cycle, and no ceremonial food or drink.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used here record no folktales, epics or storytelling occasions. What they do document is the state of the language as a spoken and written medium: literacy in Asháninka runs between 10% and 30%, against 15% to 25% literacy in Spanish as a second language, and attempts to unify the closely related varieties around a single written standard have not succeeded. Language use within the community is nonetheless described as vibrant. From 1988 a programme in Peru began teaching Spanish to Indigenous people. The one Asháninka author named in the sources is not a writer but an activist: Luzmila Chiricente Mahuanca, named among those who have defended Indigenous rights and territories.

## Language & religion

Asháninka belongs to the Arawakan family, in its Campa or Pre-Andean group, alongside Gran Pajonal Campa, Ashéninka, Axaninca, Machiguenga and Nomatsiguenga; Matsiguenga, Yine, Caquinte, Yanesha and Nomatsiguenga are named as further Arawakan relatives. Whether these closely related systems count as dialects or as separate languages is a social and political question rather than a linguistic one. About 25,000 people speak it in Peru, in Junín, Pasco, Huánuco and Ucayali and in adjacent areas including Cusco, and it is an official language where it is spoken. The alternative name *Campa* is offensive to its speakers: it derives from Quechua *thampa*, "ragged and dirty". The language is classed as vulnerable, its speakers' forest home under threat from logging. The sources describe no religious system; they record only that Roman Catholic missions from the 17th to the 19th centuries were resisted with some success, especially near the Cerro de la Sal (Salt Mountain) and the Gran Pajonal.
