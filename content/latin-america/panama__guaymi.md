## Overview

The Guaymí, more commonly called Ngäbe in the sources, are an Indigenous people of Panama and Costa Rica. Most live in the Comarca Ngäbe-Buglé, a semi-autonomous Indigenous reservation encompassing parts of Bocas del Toro, Chiriquí and Veraguas provinces in western Panama. Other Ngäbe live in Indigenous territories in southwestern Costa Rica, including Coto Brus, Abrojos Montezuma, Conte Burica, Guaymí de Alto Laguna de Osa and Altos de San Antonio. The Ngäbere language belongs to the Chibchan language family and is distinct from Buglere. The 2010 Panamanian National Census recorded 260,058 people who identified themselves as Ngäbe; the early 21st-century number of Ngäbere speakers is given as approximately 200,000–250,000. Their folk culture includes plant-fiber weaving, geometric dress patterns, balsería, life-cycle customs and a distinctive oral language tradition.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Kra** — *Kra* are handmade bags made from plant fibers. Ngäbe women make them for household and family use and also sell them as an additional source of income.

**Nagua** — *Nagua* is the Ngäbe women’s full-length, short-sleeved dress. It extends from the neck to the ankles and is usually decorated with geometric patterns at the sleeves, neckline, waist and lower skirt.

**Pintura** — *Pintura*, meaning “paint,” is the name for the classic Ngäbe geometric pattern used on women’s dresses.

**Dientes** — *Dientes*, meaning “teeth,” is another name for the classic geometric dress pattern. The sources associate its forms with mountains, animal teeth, river ripples or dragon scales.

**Plant-fiber hats** — Ngäbe men weave hats from plant fibers; farmer’s hats are also made from pita leaves.

**Beaded necklaces and bracelets** — Ngäbe women make beaded necklaces and bracelets, which may be sold beside roads in Panama.

**Motif vocabulary.** Mountains, animal teeth, river ripples, dragon scales.

### Clothing & dress

Ngäbe women commonly wear *naguas*, full-length short-sleeved dresses extending from the neck to the ankles. The dresses carry geometric decoration at the sleeve and neck lines, waist and bottom of the skirt. The patterns are called *pintura* or *dientes*. Since roughly the 1960s, these dresses have been worn as women’s characteristic clothing; the sources state that Catholic missionaries are widely believed to have introduced them for modesty, while Ngäbe women traditionally wore loincloths and little else.

Men typically wear collared cotton-and-polyester-blend shirts with polyester trousers. Some wear farmer’s hats made from pita leaves, although baseball caps adopted from urban residents are also common. Both men and women generally wear *chancletas*, or sandals, or rubber boots because of Panama’s heavy rainfall and the lack of infrastructure in the Comarca. During balsería, attendees wear traditional Ngäbe clothing and colors, feathers, animal skins and sometimes entire animals on their backs. Some men wear the woman’s *nagua* to conceal their legs during the match.

### Architecture

 They state that many Ngäbe formerly lived in dispersed villages and family units, while roads, schools, clinics and other infrastructure built in designated points encouraged the formation of larger communities.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do mention handmade bags from plant fibers, plant-fiber hats, beaded ornaments, knotted ropes used to count down the days before balsería, animal horns and improvised trumpets.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ngäbe women make beaded bracelets and necklaces, both for personal and family use and for sale. During balsería, participants may wear feathers, animal skins and entire animals on their backs.

## Music & performance

 They describe several sound-making practices associated with social life and public events. In the weeks before balsería, people in each participating town blow animal horns and other makeshift trumpets to announce that the festival is approaching. Horns, whistles and improvised trumpets are widely used during the event.

Ngäbere-speaking people may greet one another or pass the time while working by making a noise known in Spanish as a *saloma* or *grito*. The sources also mention songs translated into the Guaymi dialect by the Methodist missionary Ephraim S. Alphonse. Alphonse translated the four Gospels of the New Testament and many hymns, devised a written form of the language, and created a Guaymi Grammar and a dictionary in Guaymi, Spanish and English.

## Dance & theatre

 Balsería is described as a traditional sport and four-day festival rather than as a dance or theatrical form. Its public procession, costumes, horns, whistles and improvised trumpets form part of the event’s performance context.

## Festivals & rituals

**Balsería** is a four-day festival and traditional Ngäbe sport. Two players take turns throwing four-foot-long balsa sticks at one another’s legs, aiming below the knees. Before the match, opponents decide how many sticks will be thrown; ten is considered few, forty a lot and twenty average. There is no tournament structure, and individual matches begin through challenge and request.

A town invites another town to host and challenge it. A good harvest or a reciprocal obligation to host may prompt the event. Once the date is selected, the host presents the challenged town with a knotted rope, each knot representing a day in the countdown. On the first two days, the hosts receive the visitors and provide food and drink, generally fermented corn, banana and palm-leaf liquor. The third day begins at dawn with a procession led by the best balseros to a predetermined location. The fourth day is when the challenged visitors leave, while the hosts care for those who are inebriated or injured.

Ngäbe life-cycle customs include pregnancy, birth and funeral practices. Pregnancy is considered a state of being “sick,” or *bren*, and is almost never discussed, even during birth. Traditionally, many women gave birth at home with assistance from their mother or a midwife. After a death, close family members stay awake with the body for several nights, drinking cacao and coffee, eating and talking, while candles remain lit. The grave has a tunnel or shelf for the casket, and the mound is covered with *otoe de lagardo* leaves. The deceased’s possessions are placed on the grave. Some families abstain from salt and sugar for four days after the funeral.

Girls’ puberty ritual is called *Mägän* in the sources. It involved isolation and ritual bathing, followed by instruction from an older woman about how to behave as a woman and how to make *chácaras*. The boys’ puberty ritual is called *Grä* and involved being taken into the wilderness by an elder, who taught them how to be men and make hats. The sources state that *Mägän* is no longer practiced frequently.

## Foodways

Ngäbe subsistence farming includes rice, corn, yucca, otoy, ñame and several species of beans on the Pacific slopes. Small-scale livestock production includes chickens and pigs. In higher elevations, livestock is supplemented by hunting where permitted. Green bananas are the primary crop on the Atlantic slopes. Many Ngäbe also pick coffee or work on cattle ranches and banana plantations.

The sources mention fermented corn, banana and palm-leaf liquor as food and drink provided during the first two days of balsería. They also describe *dö*, an alcoholic beverage usually made from corn and identified in Spanish as *chicha fuerte*. After a funeral, some families avoid salt and sugar for four days and then end the fast by eating a spoonful of cooked bananas with salt and sugar. After death, relatives and visitors drink cacao and coffee while keeping vigil.

Coastal Ngäbe men, including those living on the Bocas del Toro islands or on Punto Valiente, rely on ocean resources. They spearfish and dive for lobster, both consuming and selling their catch according to circumstances.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ngäbere is traditionally an oral language. The sources describe vagueness and long silences as normal and acceptable in communication, and state that it is considered impolite not to give another person enough time to consider their words before speaking. Word play is common, with double and triple meanings. A greeting or work-related sound may take the form of a *saloma* or *grito*.

Ngäbere contains many polysemic words. For example, *kä* can mean name, earth, year, climate or place depending on context; *sö* can mean moon, month or tobacco; *kukwe* can mean language, word, topic, issue, roast or burn; *kri* can mean tree or large; *tö* can mean mind, intelligence, to want or summer; and *tare* can mean pain, difficulty or love. Ephraim S. Alphonse’s written Guaymi materials, including his grammar, dictionary, Gospel translations and hymns, are identified as an important documentation effort; copies are held in the archives of the Smithsonian Institution in Washington, DC.

## Language & religion

Ngäbere, also known as Guaymí, Movere, Chiriquí and Valiente, is a Chibchan language spoken by the Ngäbe people in Panama and Costa Rica. Buglere is a related but mutually unintelligible language spoken by the Buglé people. Ngäbere varieties are grammatically identical and mutually intelligible, although pronunciation and vocabulary vary by region. The language was traditionally oral, and a writing system using the Latin script was created only recently. Spanish is the language of education and government and the lingua franca for contact with outsiders.

Spanish missionaries introduced Roman Catholicism in the early 1600s. Since the late 19th century, Protestant, Mormon and Evangelical missionaries have also worked among the people. The sources name Jehovah’s Witnesses, the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-Day Saints, the Church of Christ, Cuerpo de Cristo, Methodism, Evangelicalism and Seventh-day Adventism. *Noncomala* is identified as the name of a traditional deity.

*Mama Tada* is a Ngäbe religion dating from September 1961. The sources describe a vision in which the Virgin Mary, and possibly Jesus, appeared to a Ngäbe woman named Besikö, thereafter more commonly known as Mama Chi, over the Fonseca River. Its principles included disengagement from Latino society, abolition of fences and alcohol, treatment of fellow Ngäbe as “brothers and sisters,” opposition to fighting and maltreatment, and Saturday rest and prayer. The movement combined these commands with Christian principles.
