## Overview

The Molise Croats, also called Molise Slavs, are a Croat community in the Molise province of Campobasso in Italy. They constitute the majority in Acquaviva Collecroce (*Kruč*), San Felice del Molise (*Filić*) and Montemitro (*Mundimitar*). The community originated from Dalmatian refugees fleeing the Ottoman conquests in the late 15th and 16th centuries, although evidence places their arrival in the early 16th century, including documented arrival in Stifilić in 1518. About 1,000 active and 2,000 passive speakers use the Slavomolisano dialect. It belongs to the Western Shtokavian dialect of Ikavian accent and preserves many features and lexemes of Chakavian. Its cultural importance lies in the preservation of an archaic language, old folk songs, migration traditions, personal names, toponyms and customs within an Italian-language environment.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Home lacemaking** — Upon settling in their current area, Molise Croats practiced home lacemaking and traded the resulting goods.

**Grain production** — Subsistence agriculture, mostly producing grain, formed part of the community’s economy after settlement.

**Vine-cultivation** — Vine-cultivation was among the agricultural activities practiced by Molise Croats.

**Olive and olive oil production** — In modern times, olive and olive oil production are also cultivated.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no documented textile motifs or pattern vocabulary.

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that common customs include living and dressing. They provide no documented garment names, head coverings, belts, footwear, jewelry or distinctions between men’s and women’s dress.

### Architecture

They do record settlements associated with the community, including Acquaviva Collecroce, San Felice, Montemitro and Palata, and mention a church inscription in Palata stating that residents of Dalmatia first settled the town and founded the church in 1531.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention agriculture, animal husbandry, lacemaking, olive production and olive oil production.

## Music & performance

Molise Croat culture is associated with preserved old folk songs and Croatian folk poetry. The sources connect this folk poetry with the community’s early history: the absence of Turkish words in folk poetry is presented as evidence supporting an early-16th-century dating, while the unity of elements in Croatian folk poetry from diverse regions is associated with the end of the 15th and 16th centuries. The sources also state that the language is important because of its archaism, preserved old folk songs and tradition.

Local amateur associations preserve folklore and language.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention folklore and common customs.

## Festivals & rituals

The community is adherent to Catholicism.

The sources record an oral tradition of migration and several identity formulas connected with ancestry and community belonging. These include *naša krv* (“our blood”), *naša čeljad* (“our dwellers”), *braća naša* (“our brothers”) and *na našo* (“on our way”). Another tradition describes the community as having settled “z one ban(d)e mora” (“from the other side of the sea”). In 1904, Josip Smodlaka recorded the testimony *su z' Dalmacije pur naši stari* (“from Dalmatia are our ancestors”).

## Foodways

The sources state that, after settling in the area, Molise Croats practiced subsistence agriculture, mostly producing grain, together with some vine-cultivation and other kinds of agriculture. Animal husbandry was also practiced. In modern times, olive and olive oil production are cultivated. These details indicate the principal documented agricultural foundations of the community’s food economy.

## Oral tradition & literature

The community’s oral tradition includes accounts of migration and descent from Dalmatia. The formula *su z' Dalmacije pur naši stari*, recorded by Josip Smodlaka in 1904, presents Dalmatia as the homeland of the ancestors. Folk poetry also preserves historical and linguistic evidence: the absence of Turkish words was used in dating the community’s arrival, and Croatian folk poetry contains elements from diverse regions at the end of the 15th and 16th centuries.

They do document literary and cultural preservation efforts. *Slavenska knjižnica* (“Biblioteca Slava”) was founded in Acquaviva Collecroce in 1904. The journal *Naš jezik* appeared from 1968 and was published again between 1986 and 1988 as *Naš život* in the Slavomolisano dialect. From 2002, the journal *Riča živa* has been released. The Agostina Piccoli Foundation was founded in 1999 and officially recognized by Italy in 2002 for preserving and protecting Molise Croats culture and tradition.

## Language & religion

The Molise Croat language is called the Slavomolisano dialect. It is classified as Western Shtokavian with an Ikavian accent and contains many features and lexemes of Chakavian. Its vocabulary has been compared with the language of Sumartin on Brač, Sućuraj on Hvar and Račišće on Korčula, as well as with several settlements in southwestern and western Istria. The language is considered important for its archaism and preservation of old folk songs and tradition.

They state that the community is adherent to Catholicism. They also describe identity through common language, shared ancestry and physical appearance, personal names and toponyms, common customs, and an oral tradition of migration.
