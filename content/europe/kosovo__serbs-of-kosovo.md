## Overview

Kosovo Serbs are a recognized ethnic minority community in Kosovo and the second-largest ethnic group there after Albanians. Estimates place their population at 95,000 to 100,000, or 5% to 6% of Kosovo’s total population. Around half live in the compact area of North Kosovo and around half in Serb enclaves across central and southeastern Kosovo. They form an ethnic majority in ten municipalities, including North Mitrovica, Leposavić, Zvečan, Zubin Potok, Gračanica, Štrpce, Novo Brdo, Ranilug, Parteš, and Klokot. Kosovo has been described in Serbian historical interpretation as a political, religious, and cultural core of the medieval Serbian state. Its monasteries, churches, oral epic poetry, music, and religious gatherings remain central subjects in accounts of Kosovo Serb folk culture.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Serbian Orthodox monasteries and churches are widespread in Kosovo. Gračanica Monastery, Patriarchate of Peć Monastery, Visoki Dečani Monastery, and Our Lady of Ljeviš form part of the Medieval Monuments in Kosovo listed as a World Heritage Site. The sources also state that churches and monasteries were destroyed, damaged, converted into mosques, or used as sources of building material during periods of Ottoman rule and conflict.

## Music & performance

Serbian folk music includes a large number of songs from Kosovo. The sources identify the performances of Jordan Nikolić and Mara Đorđević as especially important in preserving these songs.

Music also appears in social gatherings among Kosovo Serbs around churches and monasteries. During gatherings called *Sabori*, held in connection with *Slava* and *Hram*, people were expected to dance to instrumental accompaniment. According to the source, this practice was connected to a belief that everyone had to dance in order to gain and secure good health.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources mention *Slava* and *Hram* in connection with social gatherings around Kosovo Serb churches and monasteries. These gatherings were called *Sabori*. The source describes a belief that everyone had to dance to instrumental accompaniment during them in order to gain and secure good health.

The sources also describe the Serbian Orthodox religious landscape through the Serbian Patriarchate of Peć, the Serbian Archbishopric, monasteries, churches, and holy sites. The Serbian Patriarchate of Peć was re-established in the 16th century, officially abolished in 1532, restored in 1557, and abolished again by the Ottomans in 1766.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Kosovo Myth is described as a Serbian national myth and a major subject in Serbian folklore and literary tradition. It has been cultivated through oral epic poetry and portrays Kosovo as the spiritual and cultural heart of Serbia. The narrative has historically been used to emphasize Serb identity in the region.

The Battle of Kosovo, fought near Pristina at Gazimestan on 28 June 1389, is central to this tradition. The source presents the battle as one of the most pivotal events in Serbian history and identifies Prince Lazar, Sultan Murad I, and the Serbian knight Miloš Obilić in its account.

## Language & religion

The sources identify Kosovo Serbs as a Serbian ethnic community and provide Serbian forms for several names, including Косовски Срби and Kosovski Srbi.

Serbian Orthodox Christianity is central to the historical and cultural account. The Serbian Archbishopric received autocephaly in 1219, and the centre of the Serbian Church moved to Peć from Žiča by the end of the 13th century. In 1346, the archbishopric at Peć was upgraded into a Patriarchate, although it was not recognized until 1375. Churches, monasteries, holy sites, *Slava*, *Hram*, and *Sabori* are the principal religious and folk-cultural institutions described by the sources.
