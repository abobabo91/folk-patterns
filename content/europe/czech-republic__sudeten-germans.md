## Overview

Sudeten Germans, traditionally also called German Bohemians, are ethnic Germans who lived in the Czech lands of the Bohemian Crown, including Bohemia, Moravia and Austrian Silesia. German settlers arrived from Bavaria, Franconia, Upper Saxony and Austria from the Middle Ages, especially during the Ostsiedlung, or “Settling of the East.” Before 1945, more than three million German Bohemians constituted about 23% of the population of the whole country and about 29.5% of Bohemia and Moravia. Their settlements included the borderlands later called the Sudetenland, areas along the Bavarian frontier, northern and southern Moravia, and German-speaking towns surrounded by Czechs. German and Czech bilingualism and code-switching were common. Most were Roman Catholics, and their communities were shaped by German-language institutions, industrial work, and long interaction with Czech society.

## Foodways

The sources mention agricultural areas in southern Bohemia and the economic importance of chemical works, lignite mines, textile, china and glass factories, but provide no ethnographic account of foodways.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources identify one culturally significant example of German Bohemian prose from the Middle Ages: *Der Ackermann aus Böhmen* (“The Ploughman from Bohemia”), written in Early New High German by Johannes von Tepl, who lived approximately from 1350 to 1414 in Žatec, also called Saaz, and probably studied liberal arts in Prague.

## Language & religion

The sources describe German-speaking communities in Bohemia, Moravia and Silesia and distinguish the German terms Deutschböhmen, Deutschmährer and Deutschschlesier. German and Czech bilingualism and code-switching were common, particularly in mixed cities such as Prague. Separate German and Czech schools, churches and public institutions developed during the second half of the 19th century, although many Germans understood Czech and many Czechs spoke German. German Bohemians were mostly Roman Catholics, a legacy of centuries of Austrian Habsburg rule, with relatively few Protestants.
