## Overview

Tver Karelians are a Karelian people inhabiting regions of Tver, Saint Petersburg, and Moscow. They migrated from Karelia, mostly Kexholm County, to the Tver region during the 16th and 17th centuries, settling largely around the city of Tver in villages abandoned because of war and plague. Their dialect belongs to the Balto-Finnic linguistic sphere and is notable for centuries of geographical isolation from other Balto-Finnic languages. The population was about 14,633 in 2002, 7,394 in 2010, and 2,764 in 2020. Their cultural history includes migration, rural settlement, language loss, and the decline of inter-generational cultural knowledge such as “Babkin tradition,” described as traditional craftsmanship.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe migration connected with war, taxation, forced religious conversion, and the Treaty of Stolbovo in 1617. They state that loss of religion to atheism was among the factors associated with a decrease in national identity during the twentieth century.

## Oral tradition & literature

They identify “Babkin tradition” as inter-generational cultural knowledge connected with traditional craftsmanship.

## Language & religion

The Tver Karelian dialect is described as distinct from other languages and dialects because it did not borrow from other Balto-Finnic languages during centuries of geographical isolation. It continues in an archaic form and is considered probably close to a Karelian proto-language. Its vocabulary was influenced by the language of the medieval Egonskoy villages, which no longer exist. Reliable information about the origin of writing in Tver Karelian is unavailable; by 1930, Karelian was commonly written in Cyrillic and or the Latin script. The historical account describes forced conversion from the Orthodox religion to Lutheranism under Swedish rule, while the later decline of religion to atheism is associated with decreasing national identity.
