## Overview

The Bretons (*Bretoned* or *Vretoned* in Breton) are a Celtic people native to Brittany, the north-western peninsula of France. The demonym originally designated Brittonic speakers who emigrated from south-western Great Britain — particularly Cornwall and Devon — in waves from the 3rd to the 9th century, most heavily between 450 and 600, settling the western part of Armorica, which was subsequently named after them. Brittany's population was estimated at 4,365,500 in January 2007, a figure that appears to include the department of Loire-Atlantique, which the Vichy government separated from historical Brittany in 1941; France does not collect statistics on ethnicity, so the number of Bretons as such cannot be established. The traditional language, *Brezhoneg*, belongs to the Brittonic branch of Celtic, alongside Cornish and Welsh. Brittany has historically been counted as one of the six Celtic nations, and its festival, pilgrimage and music traditions remain among the most actively practised folk cultures in France.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources available for this profile — the Wikipedia article on the Bretons and the catalogue records of the Metropolitan Museum of Art objects shown here — describe Breton textiles only through the garments themselves, not through named weaving or embroidery traditions. What the catalogue records establish is a consistent 19th-century repertoire of wool garments worked with coloured wool thread, metal thread, glass beads and metal spangles or paillettes:

**Beaded and metal-thread woollens** — The Met's Breton holdings are jackets, vests, bodices and skirts of wool, decorated with wool thread, metal thread and glass beads; one 19th-century set comprises a matching jacket, bodice and skirt. Several late-19th-century jackets and vests use glass beads together with metal paillettes rather than metal thread.

**Silk-lined jackets and vests** — A separate group of 19th-century jackets and vests pairs wool with silk, without beadwork.

**Decorative ribbons** — Silk ribbons worked with metal spangles (19th century) and with metal (late 19th–early 20th century) form part of the same costume complex, entering the collection alongside the garments they trimmed.

No motif names appear in the sources, so no motif vocabulary is given here.

### Clothing & dress

The dress documented for the Bretons in these sources is the 19th- and early-20th-century costume represented by the Metropolitan Museum's objects. Women's dress is attested by bodices and skirts of wool worked in wool thread, metal thread and glass beads, assembled as matching ensembles with a jacket. Jackets and vests occur in both the beaded and metal-spangled form and in a plainer wool-and-silk form. Belts are represented by a single named object: a *gouriz* of leather and brass, dated about 1834. Silk ribbons with metal spangles trimmed these garments. Beyond this, the sources describe no head coverings, no footwear, and no distinction between everyday and ceremonial wear — though they do note that numerous people appear in traditional costume at the pardon of Locronan (*Lokorn*), so the costume complex remains in ceremonial use.

### Architecture

The sources used for this profile do not describe Breton house forms, building materials or vernacular ornament. The only built features they name are the shrines and parish churches of the seven founder saints — at Saint-Pol-de-Léon (*Kastell-Paol*), Tréguier (*Landreger*), Saint-Brieuc (*Sant-Brieg*), Saint-Malo (*Sant-Maloù*), Dol-de-Bretagne (*Dol*), Vannes (*Gwened*) and Quimper (*Kemper*) — which anchor the *Tro Breizh* pilgrimage, and the memorial at Saint-Columban in Carnac commemorating the Irish saint Columbanus.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources name no Breton ceramic or woodworking tradition. The one metalwork object they document is the *gouriz*, a leather belt with brass fittings dated about 1834, held by the Metropolitan Museum of Art. Metal otherwise appears in these records as costume material rather than as objects in its own right: metal thread couched onto woollen jackets, bodices and skirts; metal paillettes sewn alongside glass beads on late-19th-century jackets and vests; and metal spangles on silk ribbons. Brittany's other widely recognised emblematic objects named in the sources are not household goods but heraldic and symbolic devices — the ermine of the Duchy's blazons and of the chivalric *L'Ordre de l'Hermine*, and the triskele, a Celtic symbol Bretons use to assert their Celtic descent.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used for this profile do not describe Breton jewelry, tattooing, henna or hair practices; the only body-worn metal they document is the brass of the *gouriz* belt and the metal thread, paillettes and spangles applied to garments and ribbons.

## Music & performance

Breton music divides into two main types: a choral a cappella tradition called *kan ha diskan*, and instrumental music. The characteristic traditional instruments are the *bombard*, similar to an oboe, and two kinds of bagpipe — the *veuze* and the *binioù kozh*. Diatonic accordion, clarinet and, less often, violin and hurdy-gurdy are also commonly found. The basic clarinet, called *treujenn-gaol*, had very nearly disappeared before regaining popularity in recent years. After the Second World War the Great Highland bagpipe and the *binioù bras* became commonplace through the *bagadoù*, Breton pipe bands, and in many settings displaced the *binioù kozh*.

The principal performance context is the *fest-noz*, a festival built around dancing. Although many of its dances are old, some reaching back to the Middle Ages, the *fest-noz* as an institution dates from the 1950s; UNESCO inscribed it on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity on 5 December 2012. Festoù-noz are now also held well outside Brittany, carrying the regional repertoire with them — a Breton association in China organises a "Fest-noz de Pékin". Contemporary groups work in registers from rock and jazz (Red Cardell) through ethno-rock (Diwall, Skeduz) to punk, and some *fest-noz* bands, among them Strobinell, Sonerien Du, Les Baragouineurs and Plantec, use electronic keyboards and synthesisers.

## Dance & theatre

The best-known Breton dances are the *gavottes*, the *An Dro*, the *hanter dro* and the *plinn*. At a *fest-noz* most dancing is done in a chain or a circle, with participants linking little fingers; there are also couple dances and choreographed dances built from set sequences and figures. Processional movement is central to Breton religious practice as well: the pardon of Locronan includes a *troménie*, a procession twelve kilometres long, walked by large numbers of people in traditional costume. The sources describe no Breton dramatic or theatrical tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The Breton festival year is organised around *pardons*, each the feast day of a parish's patron saint. A pardon typically opens with a procession, followed by mass in the saint's honour, and is frequently accompanied by a small village fair. The three most celebrated are **Sainte-Anne d'Auray** (*Santez-Anna-Wened*), **Tréguier** (*Landreger*) in honour of St Yves, and **Locronan** (*Lokorn*) in honour of St Ronan, the last with its twelve-kilometre *troménie*.

The major pilgrimage is the *Tro Breizh*, the "tour of Brittany", in which pilgrims walk from the grave of one of the Seven Founder Saints to the next; today the circuit is usually completed over several years. In 2002 the *Tro Breizh* included a pilgrimage to Wales, symbolically reversing the journey made by the Welshmen Paul Aurelian, Brioc and Samson. Breton religious tradition holds that anyone who fails to make the pilgrimage once in life must make it after death, advancing only the length of their coffin every seven years. Attendance at Sunday mass fell through the 1970s and 1980s, but pilgrimage has revived.

The "national day" falls on 1 August, the Feast of Saint Erwann (Saint Yves); the largest Breton national event, the *Gouel Breizh*, takes place each year in the week of 19 May, the day Saint Yves died.

## Foodways

Breton cooking draws much from the wider French culinary tradition, with a set of local specialities the sources name directly. *Krampouezh* — crêpes or galettes — are thin pancakes made from either wheat or buckwheat flour, usually eaten as a main course rather than as a sweet. *Kouign-amann* is a butter pastry. *Fars forn*, also called *far breton*, is a sweet suet pudding with prunes. *Caramel au beurre salé*, salted butter caramel, rounds out the sweet repertoire. Three drinks belong to the same list: *sistr*, cider; *lambig*, an apple eau de vie; and *chouchenn*, a Breton mead. Pardons are accompanied by village fairs, and the *fest-noz* is a festival in its own right.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources name no Breton epic or folktale cycle, but they do identify the central figure of Breton folk belief: the *Ankou*, the "Reaper of Death", described as the most powerful folk figure surviving from pre-Christian tradition in Brittany. Alongside it stands the body of legend around the seven founder-saints of Brittany and the numerous records of Celtic Christian missionaries migrating from Britain during the second wave of settlement — a hagiographic tradition so pervasive that, as in Cornwall, many Breton towns are named after these early saints. Written Breton culture is sustained today by a periodical press: *Al Lanv*, published in Quimper, along with *Al Liamm*, *Louarnig-Rouzig* and *Bremañ*. Breton-language broadcasting includes Arvorig FM, France Bleu Armorique, France Bleu Breizh-Izel, Radio Bro Gwened, Radio Kerne and Radio Kreiz Breizh on radio, and Brezhoweb, France 3 Breizh, France 3 Iroise, TV Breizh and TV Rennes on television. The national anthem, *Bro Gozh ma Zadoù*, is based on the Welsh *Hen Wlad Fy Nhadau*; the Dukes of Brittany's motto was *Kentoc'h mervel eget bezañ saotret* — "death before dishonour".

## Language & religion

Breton is an Insular Celtic language of the Brittonic branch, closely related to Cornish and more distantly to Welsh, and more distantly still to extinct Continental Celtic languages such as Gaulish. It has four main dialects — *Gwenedeg* (Vannes), *Kerneveg* (Cornouaille), *Leoneg* (Léon) and *Tregerieg* (Trégor) — of varying mutual intelligibility; a standard orthography was devised in 1908 and extended to Gwenedeg in the reform of 1941. Breton is now spoken mainly in Lower Brittany, by around 206,000 people as of 2013; a 2024 study found 2.7% of those surveyed, about 107,000 people, spoke it very well or fairly well. Eastern Brittany speaks *Gallo*, a Romance langue d'oïl. Breton was banned from French schools from 1880 until the Deixonne Law of 1951. Bretons are predominantly Catholic, with minorities in the Reformed Church of France and non-religious people.
