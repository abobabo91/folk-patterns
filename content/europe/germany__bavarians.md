## Overview

Bavarians are an ethnographic group of Germans native to Bavaria, a state in Germany. Their dialect or language is known as Bavarian and is native to Altbayern (“Old Bavaria”), approximately the territory of the historic Electorate of Bavaria in the 17th century. Culturally Bavarian areas comprise Upper Bavaria (*Oberbayern*), Lower Bavaria (*Niederbayern*) and the Upper Palatinate (*Oberpfalz*); Bavarian Franconia and Bavarian Swabia are excluded from this cultural definition. Bavarians are traditionally Catholic, and in much of Altbayern Catholic Church membership remains above 70%. Modern Bavaria had 12.5 million inhabitants in 2012, while Altbayern or Bavaria proper had 6.7 million.

## Material culture

### Architecture

 The sources identify Munich as the capital and cultural center of Bavaria and state that it was founded in the high medieval period.

## Music & performance

The sources identify several Bavarian musicians and composers. Christoph Willibald Gluck is listed as a composer; Richard Strauss as a composer, conductor and pianist; Carl Orff as a composer and music educator; Franzl Lang as a singer and actor; and Jonas Kaufmann as an operatic tenor.

## Dance & theatre

 Karl Valentin is listed as a comedian, while Gerhard Polt is listed as a writer, filmmaker, actor and cabaret artist.

## Festivals & rituals

 The sources state that Bavarians are traditionally Catholic and that Christianization of Bavaria was gradual throughout the 7th century and into the 8th. Saint Corbinian was sent by Pope Gregory II to work toward the evangelization of Bavaria and became the first bishop of Freising. The Bishopric of Regensburg was founded in 739 by Boniface.

## Foodways

The sources used document one Bavarian food-related item: Johanna Händlmaier is listed as the creator of a recipe for a sweet Bavarian mustard. The sources also mention *Weisswurstäquator*, German for the “White Sausage Equator,” as a humorous term for the dividing line between Southern Germany, including Bavaria, and Northern Germany.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources list several Bavarian writers and literary figures. Ludwig Thoma is listed as a writer, Emerenz Meier as a folk poet, Michael Ende as a writer, W. G. Sebald as a writer and academic, and Patrick Süskind as a writer and screenwriter.

## Language & religion

The Bavarian language is divided into three main dialects. Upper Palatinian (*Oberpfälzisch*) is spoken in northern Bavaria, including the Bohemian Forest and Regensburg. Danube Bavarian (*Donaubairisch*) is spoken in central and south-eastern Bavaria and in Central and Lower Austria, including Munich, Salzburg and Vienna. Alpine Bavarian is spoken in south-western Bavaria, southern Austria and South Tyrol.

There is no linguistic distinction between Bavarians and Austrians in the source’s account. Bavarians are traditionally Catholic, and Catholic Church membership remains above 70% in much of Altbayern. The sources describe Bavaria as nominally Christian from the 6th century under Christian dukes, with population-wide Christianization continuing through the 7th and 8th centuries.
