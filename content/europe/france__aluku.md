## Overview

The Aluku are a Bushinengue ethnic group living mainly along the riverbank in Maripasoula, in southwest French Guiana. They are also called Boni, after the eighteenth-century leader Bokilifu Boni. Their ancestors were African slaves who escaped from Dutch plantations in what is now Suriname during the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries. After intermarrying with Native Americans, they initially settled east of the Cottica River and were known as Cottica-Maroons. Aluku settlements are concentrated along the Lawa River and include Maripasoula, Papaichton, Kormontibo, Assissi, Loca, Tabiki, Nouveau Wakapou, Agoodé, and Cottica. The urban Aluku population was estimated at 3,200 people in 2018. Their language, Aluku or Aluku Tongo, is a creole related to Pamaka and Ndyuka.

## Material culture

### Architecture

They state that many Aluku formerly lived in smaller villages with farms around main settlements, while modern developments contributed to concentration in larger villages and the near abandonment of smaller settlements.

## Festivals & rituals

The traditional religion of the Maroon people was Winti, described as a synthesis of African religious traditions. Among the Aluku, the absence of missionary activities preserved Winti as the dominant religion. Odun is identified as the main god, and four pantheons of mystical spirits are distinguished as part of everyday life. Funeral rites are described as extensive and capable of lasting many months.

The Aluku also maintained political and social rituals through treaties and customary leadership. In 1815, the Aluku and Wayana became blood brothers. In 1860, the Ndyuka signed a peace treaty with the Aluku in Albina and allowed them to settle in Abouna Sounga. In February 2018, the Grand Conseil coutumier des Populations Amérindiennes et Bushinengué was established with six Aluku Captains and two Aluku leaders among its members.

## Foodways

Traditionally, the Aluku lived by subsistence farming, hunting, gathering, and fishing. They obtained necessities through trade with neighbouring Maroon and Indigenous tribes, and there was a moral obligation to share among the clan. Farms were established around main settlements, with smaller villages helping prevent depletion of the soil. They state that the Aluku later participated in the market economy and consumption society.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do preserve a statement attributed to an Aluku elder during the 1891 discussion over nationality: “Sir, when you have a chicken whom you never feed, and your neighbour takes care of the animal would you think that the animal would stay with you, or do you think that it will leave and go to your neighbour? Well, the same applies to us.”

## Language & religion

Aluku, also called Aluku Tongo, is the eponymous term for the Aluku language. Richard Price estimated about 6,000 speakers in 2002, and many speakers are bilingual in French. The language is a creole of English, Dutch, a variety of African languages, and more recently French. It derives from Plantation Creole, identified in the source as Sranan Tongo, although its branch diverted around 1712 and developed separately. Aluku is related and mutually intelligible with the languages spoken by the Pamaka and Ndyuka peoples; the principal differences concern phonology and lexicon.

Winti is identified as the dominant traditional religion among the Aluku. It is described as a synthesis of African religious traditions. Odun is the main god, while four pantheons of mystical spirits play an integral role in everyday life.
