## Overview

The Bashkirs (Bashkir *Başqorttar*) are a Turkic people indigenous to Russia, concentrated in the republic of Bashkortostan and in the wider historical region of Badzhgard, which straddles both flanks of the Ural Mountains where Eastern Europe meets North Asia. Smaller communities live in Tatarstan, Perm Krai and the Chelyabinsk, Orenburg, Tyumen, Sverdlovsk and Kurgan oblasts, with a sizeable minority in Kazakhstan. The 2021 Russian census recorded 1,571,879 Bashkirs, of whom 1,268,806 — 31.5% of the republic's population — live in Bashkortostan. Their language belongs to the Kipchak branch of Turkic. In folk-culture terms the Bashkirs matter as a formerly half-nomadic people of the Ural steppe and mountain pastures whose herding, horse-based diet, wild-hive beekeeping and Tengrist-rooted epic poetry survived into the written record, and whose museum-held material culture is dominated by archery gear, felt and linen dress, and turned and carved wooden vessels.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here document Bashkir textiles chiefly through the catalogued garments rather than through named weaving or printing traditions, so only what those records describe is given.

**Embroidered linen shirt** — A man's shirt of natural cream tabby linen, cut from a single full loom width (about 39 cm) at centre front and back with a further full width at each side and four added panels, producing an A-line body; sleeves are set in at right angles over large diamond-shaped gussets of red tabby linen. The slit neckline is faced inside with a linen panel and trimmed outside with applied red tabby cotton.

**Felted men's headwear** — A domed man's hat with points at either side, built from several pieces of cream-grey coarse felted cloth and hand stitched, its bottom edge bound with dark red tabby cotton. The combination of coarse undyed felt with a contrasting red cotton binding is the distinguishing treatment in the catalogued examples.

**Motif vocabulary.** The one motif the sources name is a symmetrical stylized leaf, embroidered in outline on the shirt; the decorative contrast of red cotton or linen against undyed cream cloth runs through both the shirt and the hat.

### Clothing & dress

For dress the sources are limited to the three catalogued garments and to what the written sources say about livelihood, so the picture is partial. Men's dress is represented by the cream tabby linen shirt described above, whose neckline is further decorated with white and green glass beads grouped in threes, with a single group of yellow; by the domed, side-pointed felt hat edged in dark red cotton; and by boots made of leather and horse skin. Horse skin for footwear and the presence of a riding whip among the same body of objects both point to a wardrobe built around mounted herding life: the half-nomadic Bashkirs travelled through the mountains or the steppes with their cattle, and recruitment into the Russian army and heavy taxation under the Russian Empire pressed many of them towards a settled life and away from that nomadic pastoralism.

### Architecture

The sources used for this profile do not cover Bashkir house forms, building materials or architectural ornament.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The catalogued Bashkir objects are overwhelmingly of wood, bone, leather and horn rather than clay or metal. Archery equipment comes as a set: a bow of wood and horn, a leather bow case, a leather quiver and a group of arrows, alongside a knife with a bone blade and a leather sheath. Household and dairy equipment is turned and carved: wooden spoons, a wooden soup ladle, wooden basins made for *kumis*, and the *chelak*, a wooden honey barrel. Two specialised wooden forms stand out — the holiday scoop, fitted with a chain, of which several examples are held, and a bee-collecting ladle, the direct material trace of the wild-hive beekeeping practised in the Burzyansky District near the Kapova Cave. A riding whip completes the equestrian kit.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used describe no Bashkir jewelry as such; the only body adornment attested is the beadwork on the man's shirt, where white and green glass beads are grouped in threes along the applied red neckline trim, with one group of yellow beads.

## Music & performance

The central Bashkir instrument is the *quray*, an end-blown flute made from a reed found only in the Ural Mountains; it is the most important instrument in the Bashkir ensemble and is played in both traditional and modern popular music, among others by Zahir Bayiq. Song genres sit inside a broader oral repertoire that the sources describe as including ritual, epic and lyrical song alongside epic and fairy tales, legends, traditions and riddles. The documentation of this music began with the ethnographer S. G. Rybakov, whose *Music and Songs of the Ural's Muslims and Studies of Their Way of Life* appeared in 1897 as the first major study of music in Bashkortostan; L. N. Lebedinskiy collected numerous folk songs in the republic from 1930 onwards. Institutional support followed: the Ufa State Institute of Arts, founded in 1968 and later named for Zagir Ismagilov, sponsored research and teaches a dedicated Bashkir music faculty, and a network of conservatories and colleges — Ufa College of Arts (1921), the Bashkir Republican College of Culture and Art at Sterlitamak (1933), Salawat College of Music (1961), the music college at Oktyabrsky (1969), Uchaly College of Arts and Culture (1972) and Sibay College of the Arts (1997) — carries the training further. The National Symphonic Orchestra of the Bashkortostan Republic was founded in 1992 under Tahir Kamalov and became one of the major symphonies of late twentieth-century Russia. Music in Bashkortostan is shared with the Russians, Tatars, Chuvash, Mari, Udmurts and Ukrainians who live there.

## Dance & theatre

The sources record the professionalisation of Bashkir dance rather than a repertoire of named dances: G. Gaskarav, the first artistic leader of Bashkir folk theatrical choreography, founded a professional folk dance ensemble in 1939, and the company has since toured widely. Theatre is present institutionally, as a faculty of the Ufa State Institute (Academy) of Arts. The sources used name no individual dances, mask or puppet traditions, and do not distinguish ceremonial from entertainment forms.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not set out an annual Bashkir festival calendar, but they describe several occasions in detail. *Kaz-oemahe* is a work-and-feast gathering: the hostess invites her neighbours to help process geese, and at the end of the day's work treats everyone to fresh goose. Weddings carry the densest ritual food obligations. The bride prepares *chak-chak*, and after the ceremony, with washed hands, places a piece of it in the mouth of every guest — an obligatory part of the wedding rite. Small dumplings called *keyeu-bilmene* are made for the wedding in honour of the groom, and the bride also makes a special noodle, which must be especially thin and crumbly, as a demonstration of her skill. At festive meals it is customary to offer each guest *ulush*, their portion of the game — ram, goose and so on. Hospitality itself is codified: food is served as soon as a guest arrives, flatbread is set out in an odd number and never fewer than the number of guests, the host tastes first and the oldest guest is served first, the host stops eating when the guest stops, and if food is short the host eats as little as possible so the guest is satisfied. Food and drink are taken only with the right hand, fresh bread is not cut with a knife, one takes the poorer food from a shared dish and leaves the best for others, peace and good cheer are kept through the meal, and it closes with a prayer for the host's well-being.

## Foodways

Bashkir cooking rests on cattle breeding and on a semi-nomadic need for food that keeps: boiled, dried and jerked horse meat and lamb, dairy, dried berries, dried cereals and honey. Spicing is deliberately spare — only black and red pepper — and meat is abundant in every hot dish and snack. *Qazı* is a horse sausage, and horse meat is eaten in thick slices with fat, washed down with the sour fermented-milk *qurut* (also *qorot*, a young dry cheese) to cut the richness. Other keeping foods include *qaqlanğan et* (jerky), pastille, *siyəli harı may* (cherry in ghee), *moyıl mayı* (bird cherry oil), *irimsik* (cottage cheese) and *ayran*. The signature dish is *beshbarmaq* (*bishbarmaq*), boiled meat with *salma* or *halma*, coarsely chopped noodles, heaped with herbs and onions and seasoned with *qurut* or sour cream (*qaymaq*). *Boða* is a wheat or oatmeal drink; *üyrə* and *horpa* are soups; *qað*, *qatlama*, *mantı*, *ıvmas aşı* and *bukken* are shared with peoples from the Urals to the Far East. *Qımıð* (koumiss), fermented mare's milk, is the national healing drink, made from the milk of the Bashkir horse, drunk fresh before it sours, and said to have been made on the move in a vessel hung from the saddle. Bashkir honey, from the Burzyan bee, is a point of family honour and no tea party happens without it. Tea is always taken with milk — guests are not asked — and "drinking tea" means a spread of pies, boiled meat, sausage, cheesecake, sour cream, jam and honey substantial enough to replace a meal. Festive tables add *belish*, *gubadia* and obligatory chak-chak. Religious prohibitions bar pork, carnivorous animals and birds of prey such as falcon, kite and hoopoe, snake and frog; swans and cranes are Bashkir totems and not eaten; thyroid glands, spinal cord and spleen are avoided, as are fish without scales.

## Oral tradition & literature

Bashkir folklore is the strongest-documented intangible tradition here, preserving ancient views of nature, wisdom, psychology and moral ideals across epic and fairy tales, legends and traditions, riddles and song. The epics descend from ancient Turkic mythology and are described as a more developed and expanded form of that older epic culture; most were written down and published as books in the early twentieth century, where they form a large part of Bashkir literature. The central work is ***Ural Batyr***, which tells of the hero Ural and gives the Ural Mountains their name. Its world is three-tiered: the heavenly king Samrau lives in the sky with the Sun and the Moon as his wives and the daughters Umay and Aikhylu, who appear as birds or as beautiful girls — Umay first as a swan; people live on the earth, the best of them honouring nature; and beneath lies the underworld of the Devas, snake-formed powers of darkness. Ural destroys the deva and obtains the "living water", a dualistic framing of good and evil rooted in the pre-Islamic Bashkir conception of the world. Other epics central to Bashkir identity are ***Aqbuzat***, ***Qara yurga***, ***Aqhaq qola***, ***Kongur buga*** and ***Uzaq Tuzaq***. Genealogical tradition is recorded in *shezhere*, Bashkir family trees, which preserve material about the sixteenth century that is otherwise lost. A legend of origin explains the ethnonym itself: a wolf sent to guide the first seven Bashkir tribes from the Syr Darya valley to the land granted them by the Tengrist fertility goddess Umay, hence *bash* ("head, leader") and *qurt* (an archaic word for wolf) — "leading wolf".

## Language & religion

Bashkir is a Kipchak Turkic language, co-official with Russian in Bashkortostan, with roughly 1.6 million native speakers and three dialect groups: Southern, Eastern and North-Western. It is agglutinative and SOV, with heavy Russian, Arabic and Persian borrowing, and is set apart from its close relative Tatar by the dental fricatives /θ/ and /ð/ — as in *duś* "friend" or *hüź* "word", where Tatar has *süz* — by word-initial /s/ becoming /h/, and by explicitly written labial vowel harmony. A runic inscription of the ninth century is the first attestation of a Bashkir language. After the adoption of Islam from the tenth century the Bashkirs wrote in Turki, in an Arabic-based script; a purpose-built Arabic alphabet and a literary standard followed in 1923, the Unified Turkic Latin Alphabet in 1930, and an adapted Cyrillic in 1939. Most Bashkirs today are bilingual in Bashkir and Russian, and most are Sunni Muslims of the Hanafi madhhab following the Jadid doctrine; the older Tengrist layer, with its sky god, twelve powers of nature recorded by Ibn Fadlan in 922 and the goddess Umay, survives inside the epic repertoire.
