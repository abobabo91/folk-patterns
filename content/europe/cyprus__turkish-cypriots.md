## Overview

Turkish Cypriots, also called Cypriot Turks, are ethnic Turks originating from Cyprus. A significant part of the population lives in Northern Cyprus, while the majority live abroad in a diaspora formed especially through migration to Turkey and the United Kingdom. Their community emerged through Ottoman settlement after the conquest of the island in 1571, subsequent migration from Anatolia, and conversions to Islam among local Christians. Standard Turkish is the official language of Northern Cyprus, while Turkish Cypriots speak Cypriot Turkish, influenced by Cypriot Greek and English. Their folk-culture significance lies in a distinct Cypriot Turkish identity combining Turkish roots, connections to mainland Turkey, and cultural and linguistic similarities with Greek Cypriots.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe changes in clothing rather than a named Turkish Cypriot costume tradition. During the 1920s, the adoption of western dress became one of the reforms embraced by Muslim Turkish Cypriots alongside the introduction of the new Turkish alphabet, secularization, modernization, and westernization. Most Turkish Cypriot women do not cover their heads.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They state that Turkish Cypriot males are generally circumcised at a young age, although the practice is described as more closely related to custom and tradition than to strong religious motivation.

## Music & performance

Folk music is described as an integral part of Turkish Cypriot social life. They do state that Turkish Cypriot folk dances, music, and art are integral parts of the culture.

Social life is strongly connected with family ties, relatives, neighbourhood relations, and helping people in need. Food is described as a central feature of gatherings, and music and dance form part of this wider social activity. Folk-dancing groups perform during national festivals, weddings, Turkish nights at hotels, and in tourism areas.

## Dance & theatre

Traditional Turkish Cypriot folk dances are divided into five categories: *Karsilamas*, *Sirtos*, *Zeybeks*, *Ciftetellis/Arabiyes*, and topical dances. The topical dances named in the source are *Orak*, *Kozan*, *Kartal*, and *Topal*. Folk-dancing groups perform during national festivals, weddings, Turkish nights at hotels, and within tourism areas. They identify folk dancing as a social and public performance practice, particularly associated with weddings, festivals, hotels, and tourism areas.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources mention national festivals as settings for Turkish Cypriot folk-dance performances. Weddings are also identified as important performance contexts.

The sources describe circumcision among Turkish Cypriot males as a practice generally carried out at a young age. It is connected with religious beliefs but is also presented as a matter of custom and tradition. The sources further record that some former crypto-Christians gave their children both Muslim and Christian names and baptized them according to the Christian faith while also practicing circumcision. They do record the historical existence of crypto-Christians known as *Linobambaki*, or “the linen and cotton” group, who maintained Christianity secretly while formally embracing Islam.

## Foodways

Food is described as a central feature of Turkish Cypriot gatherings, and much of community life revolves around social activities connected with family, relatives, and neighbourhoods.

Alcohol is frequently consumed within the community. The sources also discuss crypto-Christians in Cyprus who were described by foreign travellers as Turks who were secretly Greeks and who observed Greek Orthodox fasting, drank wine, ate pork, and sometimes took Christian wives. These practices are presented in the context of crypto-Christianity rather than as a general description of Turkish Cypriot dietary practice.

The sources describe Turkish Cypriots as regarding themselves as secular Muslims.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record the medieval Cypriot historian Leontios Machairas, who recalled that baptized Turks were not permitted to leave Nicosia when the Mamlukes approached the city after the battle of Khirokitia in 1426. This is historical testimony about Turkish captives and baptized Turks on Cyprus, not a documented Turkish Cypriot oral-literary tradition.

## Language & religion

Standard Turkish is the official language of Northern Cyprus. Turkish Cypriots speak Cypriot Turkish, a variety that developed in relative isolation from standard Turkish and was strongly influenced by the Cypriot Greek dialect. English and Greek have also influenced its vocabulary. The coexistence of Turkish Cypriots and Greek Cypriots produced bilingualism in areas where the two communities lived and worked together. The sources note that some Muslim villages normally used Greek and that Turkish Cypriots historically differed greatly in their command of Cypriot Greek.

Turkish Cypriots generally regard themselves as secular Muslims. According to a 2020 survey, about 70% were religious, most of those—about 98.7%—identified as Sunni Muslims, and about 30% of the total population did not identify with any religion. Religious practice is treated as an individual choice, and many people do not actively practice their religion. The sources also describe crypto-Christianity and the *Linobambaki* as a historical phenomenon in Cyprus.
