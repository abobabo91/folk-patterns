## Overview

The Sorbs, also known as Lusatian Serbs, are a West Slavic ethnic group indigenous to parts of Lusatia in the German states of Saxony and Brandenburg. They traditionally speak Upper Sorbian and Lower Sorbian, languages closely related to Polish, Czech, and Slovak; both are officially recognized minority languages in Germany. Sorbian history includes early medieval political organization, long periods of rule by German and Slavic powers, Germanization, and persecution under Nazi Germany. The group remains divided religiously between Roman Catholicism and Lutheranism. Sorbian cultural institutions, literature, schools, newspapers, music, festivals, and organizations have played important roles in preserving a distinct identity.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources mention Sorbian costume as part of Sorbian culture and customs. Under Nazi Germany, Sorbian costume, culture, customs, and language were wrongly treated as no indication of a non-German origin.

### Architecture

The Sorbian House was built in Bautzen in 1904 and became the most important Sorbian cultural centre. The sources also mention a reconstructed castle at Raddusch in Lower Lusatia, described as the sole physical remnant from the early period of Sorbian history.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention Leipzig group archaeological culture and Tornow group ceramics in connection with scholarly interpretations of the historical separation between Upper and Lower Sorbian territories, but provide no catalogue of objects or craft techniques.

## Music & performance

Sorbian musical culture is documented through song, religious practice, and organized cultural activity. In 1841–1843, Jan Arnošt Smoler and Leopold Haupt published a two-volume collection of Wendish folk-songs in Upper and Lower Lusatia. In 1845, the first festival of Sorbian songs took place in Bautzen. The poet Handrij Zejler established a weekly magazine in 1842, and Sorbian publishing companies began appearing at the same time.

Religious institutions also supported Sorbian performance and literature. Evangelical students founded Sorbian preaching societies in Leipzig and Wittenberg in 1716 and 1749. In 1667, the Evangelical Church supported the printing of Sorbian religious literature, while the Prince of Brandenburg ordered the destruction of Sorbian printed materials and banned masses in Sorbian.

## Dance & theatre

A Sorbian theatre was created in East Germany after the Second World War, as part of a broader range of Sorbian institutions. The Sokol Movement, founded in 1920 as a youth movement and gymnastic organization, was another organized form of Sorbian cultural activity; the Nazi party dissolved it from 1933.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources record the first festival of Sorbian songs, held in Bautzen in 1845. They also document Sorbian religious life, including Roman Catholicism and Lutheranism, Sorbian preaching societies, Catholic priestly education, and religious literature. The Sorbian Seminary, the main centre for educating Sorbian Catholic priests, was founded in Prague in 1706. They state that religious observance, cultivation of tradition, and strong families contributed to the survival of Sorbian national culture.

## Oral tradition & literature

The oldest known relic of Sorbian literature is dated to about 1530 and is identified as the Bautzen townsmen’s oath. In 1548, Mikołaj Jakubica, a Lower Sorbian vicar from Lubanice, wrote the first unprinted translation of the New Testament into Lower Sorbian. The first Sorbian book was printed in 1574: Albin Mollers’ songbook. In 1688, Jurij Hawštyn Swětlik translated the Bible for Catholic Sorbs.

From 1706 to 1709, the New Testament was printed in Upper Sorbian in a translation by Michał Frencel and in Lower Sorbian in a translation by Jan Bogumił Fabricius. Jan Bjedrich Fryco translated the Old Testament into Lower Sorbian; it was published in 1790. Other Sorbian Bible translators included Jakub Buk, Michał Hórnik, and Jurij Łušćanski.

Sorbian literary and cultural revival continued through publishing, newspapers, folk-song collections, and literary movements. In 1767, Jurij Mjeń published the first secular Sorbian book. In 1875, Jakub Bart-Ćišinski and Karol Arnošt Muka created a movement of young Sorbians that influenced Lusatian art, science, and literature. In Lower Lusatia, a similar movement formed around Mato Kósyk and Bogumił Šwjela.

## Language & religion

Sorbian is a West Slavic language tradition represented by Upper Sorbian and Lower Sorbian. These languages are closely related to Polish, Czech, and Slovak, and both are officially recognized minority languages in Germany. The two languages developed from dialects associated in the sources with Upper and Lower Sorbian territories. Sorbian was used in family, business, village, and religious contexts, although restrictions on its use appeared repeatedly. By the end of the 19th century, almost the entire Sorbian population was bilingual.

The community is divided religiously between Roman Catholicism, described as the majority, and Lutheranism. Sorbian religious life supported schools, preaching societies, seminaries, Bible translation, and printed literature. The sources also describe periods when Sorbian religious practice and institutions were suppressed, including under Nazi Germany and during East German policies affecting the church.
