## Overview

Inari or Aanaar Sámi are a group of Sámi people who inhabit the area around Lake Inari in Finland. Their traditional homeland includes the shores of Lake Inari and surrounding areas bordering Saariselkä, the Ivalo River, Lemmenjoki, Vaskajoki, Aksujärvi, Syysjärvi, Säytsjärvi and Iijärvi. There are an estimated 700–900 ethnic Inari Sámi in Finland, of whom approximately 300–400 speak Inari Sámi. Inari Sámi belongs to the eastern Sámi languages and is the only Sámi language spoken exclusively in Finland. Fishing and hunting have been important cultural practices, while the Inari Sámi costume remains an important part of identity.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Inari Sámi costume** — The costume has been used in the Inari area uninterruptedly and is regarded as an important part of Inari Sámi identity. Its background is black or dark-blue, with red, yellow and green used in the decoration.

**Peski** — *Peski* was a winter garment made from reindeer coat. It has largely been replaced by modern snowmobile suits.

### Clothing & dress

The Inari Sámi costume is an important part of identity and has been used in the Inari area without interruption. The sources describe its background as black or dark-blue, with red, yellow and green used in the decoration. They also identify *peski* as a traditional winter garment made from reindeer coat.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources mention artifacts connected with early inhabitants, most of which were linked to hunting and fishing. They also record one discovery of bronze items from Inari, found on Lusmansaari in Lake Inari and dated to 900–700 BC, and one finding of an iron item from the early Iron Age.

## Music & performance

They mention that Mikkâl Morottaja, whose stage name is Amoc, uses Inari Sámi in rap songs and published the first full-length Inari Sámi rap CD in the world on Sámi National Day in 2007. The sources also state that Amoc’s brother Petter Morottaja served as editor-in-chief of the Inari Sámi newspaper *Kierâš*.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe holy sites called *sieidi*, often located on islands of Lake Inari. The most important of these was Äijih. Only men were allowed to go to these holy sites, although women could enter if they dressed as men.

The sources also describe the spread of Christianity among the Inari Sámi in the 17th century and state that the first church in the area was built in 1642. During the Christian mission, many older traditions disappeared, and some shamans were executed for practicing witchcraft.

## Foodways

The sources identify fishing and hunting as important features of Inari Sámi culture. The main hunting game was Finnish forest reindeer, while fishing was especially significant before Lake Inari was overfished in the 20th century. Inari Sámi moved between winter and summer homes, although this practice disappeared quite early.

## Oral tradition & literature

Inari Sámi mythology included some of the same gods as Finnish mythology. The most important was Äijih, who had characteristics similar to Finnish Ukko. Other gods included Piäiváž and Čäcialmai. Holy sites called *sieidi* were often located on islands of Lake Inari.

The first book in Inari Sámi was *Anar sämi kiela aapis kirje ja doctor Martti Lutherus Ucca katkismus*, written and translated by Edvard Wilhelm Borg in 1859. The written history of modern Inari Sámi is said to begin with Lauri Arvid Itkonen’s translation of the history of the Bible in 1906. Later publications were associated with linguists including Frans Äimä and Erkki Itkonen.

Language preservation has included the Anarâškielâ servi, founded in 1986, its publications, language immersion programs, the *Kierâš* newspaper, and the Inari Sámi version of Wikipedia, launched in 2020. In 2023, *Anarâš Aavis*, described as the only daily newspaper written in Inari Sámi, was founded.

## Language & religion

Inari Sámi, also called *anarâškielâ* or *aanaarsämikielâ*, is a Sámi language spoken by the Inari Sámi of Finland. It belongs to the eastern Sámi languages and is classified as seriously endangered because few children learn it, although more children are learning it in language nests. As of 2018, it had approximately 400 speakers, while the Sámi Parliament of Finland recorded 269 people using it as their first language.

Inari Sámi is written using the Latin script. Its current alphabet became official in 1996. Inari Sámi mythology included Äijih, Piäiváž and Čäcialmai, while Christianity began spreading among the Inari Sámi in the 17th century.
