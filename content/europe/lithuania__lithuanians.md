## Overview

Lithuanians are a Baltic ethnic group native to Lithuania, where around 2,378,118 people live; another two million form a diaspora, largely in the United States, United Kingdom, Brazil and Canada. Their native language is Lithuanian, one of only two surviving members of the Baltic language family, alongside Latvian. According to the 2021 census, 84.6% of Lithuania’s population identified themselves as Lithuanians. The Lithuanian nation developed from eastern Baltic tribes, with its language and culture forming from the first centuries CE through the early Middle Ages and its ethnos consolidating between the 9th and 13th centuries. Lithuanians retain regional identities associated with Žemaitija, Aukštaitija, Dzūkija, Suvalkija and Lithuania Minor, expressed through dialects, seasonal customs, folklore, clothing and song.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources used provide only limited information about clothing. The Dzūkai are described as having worn folk clothing exceptionally long among Lithuanian ethnic groups, with colorful skirts and aprons still worn until the 1940s. Regional costumes are characterized by bright colors and decorative patterns among the Suvalkiečiai.

### Architecture

The sources describe wooden architecture as associated with the Aukštaičiai. They also identify wooden churches and historic towns such as Telšiai and Plungė as symbols of Žemaitian cultural identity.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

## Music & performance

Music and song are associated with several regional Lithuanian identities. Aukštaičiai are described as known for folk songs with rich melodies, while Dzūkija has the nickname “the land of mushrooms and songs.” Folk poetry, dance and polyphonic singing remain parts of Dzūkai cultural expression. The sources also state that the regional identities of Lithuanians are distinguished through traditions, dialects and historical divisions.

The Lietuvininkai are associated with a literary tradition and with the publication of Lithuanian-language books, including Martynas Mažvydas’ *Catechism* in 1547.

## Dance & theatre

The sources state that folk poetry, dance and polyphonic singing remain vital parts of Dzūkai cultural expression.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources associate Aukštaičiai agrarian customs with seasonal festivals such as **Jorė** and **Rasos**. No dates for these festivals are supplied. The sources describe Christianity as an important part of Lithuanian historical experience: Lithuania was definitively Christianized in 1387, while Žemaitija was officially baptized in 1413 and is described as the last region of Europe to accept Christianity.

The Lithuanian National Revival began in the 19th century in the context of Russification, including a 40-year ban on public speaking and writing in Lithuanian. The sources also describe the Reformation among the Lietuvininkai as strengthening literacy and encouraging the publication of Lithuanian-language books.

## Oral tradition & literature

Lithuanian origin traditions include the Roman, or Palemon, theory, which claimed that Lithuanians originated from Roman patricians led by a nobleman named Palemon, who fled Rome. This legend was repeated in 16th–17th century chronicles to strengthen noble authority and political legitimacy. Other versions linked Lithuanians to the Goths, Heruli, Greeks or Hittites. The Palemon legend was especially influential in the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, where it appeared in chronicles and genealogical works.

The sources describe Lithuanian folklore as an important part of regional identity. Aukštaitija is associated with regional folklore and ethnographic museums, while Dzūkai traditions include folk poetry and song. The Lietuvininkai developed a literary tradition within the German-dominated Prussian state, and the Reformation fostered the publication of Lithuanian-language books. Martynas Mažvydas’ *Catechism*, published in 1547, is identified as the earliest printed work in Lithuanian.

## Language & religion

Lithuanian belongs to the Baltic branch of the Indo-European language family and is one of only two surviving Baltic languages, the other being Latvian. The standard Lithuanian language developed partly from Aukštaitian varieties. The Samogitian dialect is described as highly distinctive in pronunciation, grammar and vocabulary and is sometimes considered a separate language. The Dzūkai speak a sub-dialect of Southern Aukštaitian characterized by the use of *dz* sounds. The sources also describe the historical suppression of Lithuanian under Russian occupation, including a 40-year ban on public speaking and writing in Lithuanian.

Most Lithuanians belong to the Catholic Church. The Lietuvininkai were mostly Lutherans and were early adopters of the Reformation. Lithuania’s Christianization was completed in the late medieval period: the country was definitively Christianized in 1387, and Žemaitija was officially baptized in 1413.
