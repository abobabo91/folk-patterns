## Overview

Carpathian Germans are an ethnic German group in Central and Eastern Europe. Since the First World War, the name has commonly referred to the Germans of Slovakia, including the Zipser Germans, and to Germans of Carpathian Ruthenia in Ukraine. In Slovakia, their principal settlement areas were around Pressburg and in language islands of Szepes County and Hauerland. German settlers arrived in the northern territory of the medieval Kingdom of Hungary from the 12th to the 15th centuries, especially after the 1241 Mongol invasion of Europe. They were attracted by kings seeking craftsmen, miners, and other specialists, and often settled in older Slavic market and mining settlements. The German population of Slovakia declined sharply after the Second World War. National censuses recorded 6,108 Germans in Slovakia in 2007, compared with approximately 128,000 in 1938.

They do record that Carpathian Germans often lived in older Slavic market and mining settlements and that their principal settlement areas included Pressburg, Szepes County, and Hauerland.

## Music & performance

They do record two German-language media assisted financially by the Slovak government: *Karpatenblatt*, a monthly publication, and *IKEJA news*, an Internet publication.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record several historical public and political events involving the group. On 28 October 1918, the National Council of Carpathian Germans in Késmárk declared loyalty to the Kingdom of Hungary, while a Slovak group declared the area part of Czechoslovakia two days later. In 1945, the Přerov incident involved 265 Carpathian Germans from Dobšiná who were murdered while passing through Horní Moštěnice near Přerov railway station. On 2 August 1945, Carpathian Germans lost the rights of citizenship under Beneš decree no. 33 and were interned in camps including Bratislava-Petržalka, Nováky, and Krickerhau Handlová.

## Foodways

The sources do identify mining and lumberjacking as important occupations in some Carpathian German settlements. In Maramureș and Bukovina, Carpathian and Zipser German communities were associated especially with mining areas; in Bukovina, Zipsers were also miners and lumberjacks. No corresponding food practices are described.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record institutional efforts to maintain traditions. A Carpathian German Homeland Association was created for this purpose, and since 2005 a museum of the culture of Carpathian Germans has existed in Bratislava. After the war, Carpathian Germans living in Germany and Austria also founded cultural associations.

## Language & religion

Carpathian Germans speak German varieties, but isolation from Germany, Austria, and, to some extent, Switzerland allowed obscure German dialects to continue in Slovakia, many of them endangered. In the Upper and Lower Zips regions, and later in Romania, Zipser Germans spoke *Zipserisch*. A community in Hopgarten speaks *Outzäpsersch*, also called *Altzipserisch*, literally “Old Zipserish.” In Dobsina, speakers used *Dobschauisch* or *Topschauisch*. In Metzenseifen, now Medzev, they spoke *Mantak*, which only a few dozen people speak today. German schools were closed after the Second World War in former German-speaking towns, and children were required to learn Slovak. German was not to be used in the workplace or publicly on the street.
