## Overview

The Poleshuks, also called Paleshuks, Polishchuks or Polesians, are described as the indigenous population of Polesia, also known as Polesie and Polissia. The region includes Belarusian, Polish and Ukrainian parts. Their native speech forms a dialect continuum between Belarusian and Ukrainian and includes recently codified West Polesian, alongside many local variations and sub-dialects. Since the interwar period, the Poleshuks developed a sense of identity influenced by the ethnic politics of the Second Polish Republic and the Polesie Voivodeship. The group is considered one of the distinct cultural and ethnic identities of the area, although much of the population has assimilated with Belarusian, Polish, Ukrainian or Russian identities.

## Festivals & rituals

They identify *Kvasovka* as a wedding dish made from boiled millet with dried cherries, apples and plums, but provide no further description of wedding ritual.

## Foodways

Poleshuk cuisine is characterized by products obtained through fishing and gathering, including fish, berries, mushrooms and grass. The fish named in the source are tench, crucian carp and perch. Rye flour is used for pies, pancakes and various kinds of bread. A traditional Polesian dish is buckwheat varenyky with soured milk, while *kvasha* is prepared from soured rye and buckwheat flour.

Festive dishes include borshch, kissel, thick pancakes with sour cream, cottage cheese with milk and millet kasha. The source also records several named dishes and preparations. *Bukhty* are wheat-flour pastries with raisins and berries. *Hartanachka* is millet porridge with meat and potatoes, cooked in a large pot. *Hurky* are braised cucumbers with carrots, onions, poultry or pork. *Kalamukha* is made from boiled cherries, sour cream and flour. *Krupnyk* is a soup containing millet, mushrooms and whey. *Matsyk*, also called *kavruk*, *kirukh*, *matsiok*, *kniushok* or *bohuk*, is salted cured meat kept for several months in a linen bag or pig stomach or bladder.

Other recorded foods are oven-baked cabbage, called *kapusta*; *Tomachi*, made from mashed potatoes; and *Volok*, also called *voylok*, consisting of boiled or braised beetroot, goosefoot or nettle leaves seasoned with sour cream, whey, seed oil or garlic. Blueberries may be dried and mixed with sugar, flour or poppyseed, or eaten fresh with bread and milk or sour cream.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record a cultural and linguistic revival associated with Nikolai Shelyagovich, who created the public-cultural association “Polesie” in 1988. The association sought the revival of the Western Polesie language and recognition of local residents as a national minority, while also supporting national and cultural autonomy or independence of Western Polesie. Its activities gradually tapered off after the Belarusian referendum in 1995.

## Language & religion

Poleshuk speech forms a dialect continuum between the Belarusian and Ukrainian languages. It includes recently codified West Polesian as well as many local variations and sub-dialects. A 1923 Polish statistical document described the speech of Poleshuks as East Slavic dialects transitional between Ukrainian and Belarusian, sometimes identified as a separate Polesian language. The 1931 Polish Census used mother tongue rather than ethnicity as its relevant question; 62.5% of the population identified their language as *tutejszy*, meaning “local.”
