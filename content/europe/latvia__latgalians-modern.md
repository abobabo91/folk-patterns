## Overview

The Latgalians are an ethnographic group living in the Latgale region of Latvia. They speak Latgalian and Standard Latvian, and their distinct culture sets them apart from other Latvians. Modern Latgalians developed after Latgale developed separately from other parts of contemporary Latvia following its division between Sweden and Poland after the 1621 Truce of Altmark. The eastern part of Latvia came under Polish control as Inflanty Voivodeship, and Roman Catholicism became the main religious denomination in Latgale, unlike the traditionally Lutheran remainder of Latvia. They record that 164,500 people in Latvia spoke Latgalian daily in 2011.

## Music & performance

They state that Latgalian was used in books, periodicals, radio and television programming, internet culture, and digital content. Examples named in the source include the internet culture magazine **Lakuga.lv**, versions of local newspapers, radio and television programming, and digital content by the Public Broadcasting of Latvia. The source also records that some political periodicals were published in Latgalian during the Soviet period, including **Ludzas Taisneiba**, also called **Ludzys Taisneiba**, meaning “Truth of Ludza,” from the end of the 1940s to the 1960s.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document historical and cultural periods connected with Latgalian public life. A period of Latgalian reawakening began after the ban on Latgalian written in Latin script was lifted in 1904. Publishing in Latgalian resumed in Latvia during the Third National Awakening in the late 1980s and has continued.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe Latgalian publishing and literary use. The terms *latgalīši* and *latgaļi* have traditionally been used in Latgalian as synonyms for ancient and contemporary Latgalians as part of the same continuum. The term *latgalīši* prevailed in Latgalian literature and mass media during the first decades of the 20th century, while *latgali* was consistently used in Latgalian literature and media published during the 1920s and 1930s in Latvia and from 1940 to 1988 in exile. Since the cultural revival of Latgalians in 1988, Latgalian literature and media have shown a tendency to follow Latvians in using both names.

The sources record a history of restriction and revival. During the Russification period beginning in 1865, Latgalian written in Latin script was forbidden. The ban was lifted in 1904. During the Soviet period, publishing in Latgalian was generally banned, although some political periodicals were published. Latgalians continued publishing books and periodicals abroad among the Latvian diaspora, and publishing in Latvia resumed during the late 1980s.

## Language & religion

Latgalians speak Latgalian and Standard Latvian. The sources describe Latgalian as a dialect of Latvian with many regional varieties and state that local dialects underwent a sound change probably caused by Slavic influence. The language was written in Latin script, which was forbidden during the Russification period beginning in 1865; the ban ended in 1904. During the Soviet period, Russian and Latvian were the only recognized languages.

Latgalian is most frequently used at home in rural areas, while Latvian is overwhelmingly used in official contexts and urban areas. The Official Language Law of 2000 states that the state shall ensure the maintenance, protection, and development of the Latgalian written language as a historic variant of Latvian. The main religious distinction described by the sources is that Roman Catholicism became the principal religious denomination in Latgale, while the rest of Latvia is traditionally Lutheran.
