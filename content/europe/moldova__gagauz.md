## Overview

The Gagauz are an Oghuz Turkic ethnic group native to southern Moldova, especially Gagauzia, Taraclia District, and Basarabeasca District, and to southwestern Ukraine, particularly Budjak. They are also present in Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan, Bulgaria, Greece, Romania, Brazil, Turkmenistan, Belarus, Estonia, Latvia, Georgia, Turkey, and the Russian region of Kabardino-Balkaria. According to the 2014 census, 126,010 Gagauz lived in Moldova, representing 4.57% of the population without Transnistria. Gagauz belongs to the Oghuz branch of the Turkic languages and is especially close to Balkan Turkish dialects. Most Gagauz are Eastern Orthodox Christians. Their folk culture combines pastoral and agricultural practices, Turkic language, Balkan connections, and a distinctive regional cuisine.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Toward the end of the 19th century, a Gagauz woman’s clothing in good weather consisted of a canvas shirt, a sleeveless dress, a smock, and a large black kerchief. In winter, women wore a dress with sleeves, a cloth jacket, and a sleeveless fur coat. Men’s traditional clothing included a shirt, cloth pants, a wide red sash or belt, and a hat. The winter cap was made of Karakul sheep wool. A shepherd’s clothing consisted of a shirt, sheepskin pants with the fleece turned in, a sleeveless fur coat, and a short sheepskin jacket, sometimes decorated with red-on-green stitching.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do record agricultural and pastoral activities, including animal husbandry, sheep raising, grain cultivation, market gardening, and viticulture.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Women’s required ornaments included earrings, bracelets, and beads. Wealthy Gagauz women wore necklaces of gold coins.

## Music & performance

The sources used provide little information about Gagauz music. In 2013, Ludmila Tukan represented Gagauzia in the territory’s debut at the Turkvision Song Contest with the song Вернись любовь, translated in the source as “Come back love.” The Gagauz language article also identifies a Gagauz song titled “Yaşa, Halkım!” by Andrey İVANOV. No traditional instruments, song genres, musical ensembles, performance contexts, or ritual musical practices are described.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record family holidays and rituals connected with the baking of wheat bread, including leavened loaves such as *kalaches* and unleavened flatcakes. The dish *kurban* is described as a traditional ritual food combining bulgar wheat porridge with a slaughtered or sacrificed ram.

## Foodways

Grain is the staple food of Gagauz cuisine, and the sources connect its importance with the community’s farming lifestyle. The Gagauz maintain a method of processing milk and preserving meat, curds, and sheep milk cheese in animal skins. A layered pie filled with sheep’s milk cheese and soaked with sour cream before baking is identified as a favorite dish. Other recorded foods include pies with crumbled pumpkin and sweet pies made with the first milk of a cow that has just calved.

The traditional ritual dish *kurban* combines bulgar wheat porridge with meat from a slaughtered or sacrificed ram. Head cheese, or cold cuts in jelly prepared from the heads and feet of livestock, is served during holidays and is described as an indispensable component of holiday meals. Gagauz meat dishes are often served with peppered sauces. One sauce combines onion and finely granulated porridge, while another is tomato-based. Red house wine or red wine is served with dinner and supper, and the cuisine article states that red wine is served with most meals.

## Oral tradition & literature

They record that Gagauz surnames can derive from professions, street nicknames, and the father’s name, and that the Gagauz language has been supported through newspapers and other printed materials. *Ana Sözü* is the largest local newspaper in Gagauzia, the only local newspaper still written entirely in Gagauz, and the first newspaper of any kind published in the Gagauz language. Todur Zanet, its editor-in-chief, has encouraged instruction in Gagauz and contributed to efforts to standardize and increase access to the language.

## Language & religion

Gagauz is a Turkic language belonging to the Oghuz branch, alongside Azerbaijani, Turkmen, and Turkish. It is spoken by the Gagauz people of Moldova, Ukraine, Russia, and Turkey and is an official language of the Autonomous Region of Gagauzia in Moldova. The modern language has central, or “Bulgar,” and southern, or “maritime,” dialects. It was established as a written language in 1957, although it was not used in schools until 1959. Cyrillic was used from 1957 until 1993; on May 13, 1993, Moldova’s parliament adopted a Latin-based alphabet, subsequently amended in 1996. The alphabet is modeled on the modern Turkish alphabet and includes ä, ê, and ţ.

The vast majority of Gagauz are Eastern Orthodox Christians. The sources describe competing theories about Gagauz origins and note that their religion is Eastern Orthodox Christianity. In Turkey, Muslim Gagauz are called Gajal.
