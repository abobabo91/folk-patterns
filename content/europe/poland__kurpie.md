## Overview

Kurpie are the people associated with an ethnic culture and region in Poland. The region lies on the lowland plain called the Mazovian Region, or *Mazowsze*, formerly covered by the Puszcza Zielona (the Green Wilderness) and Puszcza Biała (the White Wilderness). Together these forests are often called the Puszcza Kurpiowska (Kurpie Forest). Kurpie culture developed through centuries of isolation and is noted for distinctive traditional costumes, dances, architecture, and livelihoods. Today the region extends across the Masovian, Podlaskie, and, to a small extent, Warmian-Masurian regions. Populated areas include Myszyniec, Ostrołęka, Czarnia, Dylewo, Jednorożec, Kadzidło, Lipniki, Łyse, and Zbójna.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Wycinanki* are Kurpie paper cut-outs made by women. The sources describe them as representations of animals, geometric designs, flowers, and other forms.

Linen and cloth weaving was an important domestic activity: the women of the Kurpie forests became accomplished in weaving linens and cloths.

**Motif vocabulary.** Animals, geometric designs, flowers.

### Clothing & dress

Kurpie clothing differed between the northern and southern parts of the region. In the north, women wore red skirts, a green vest, and a white linen blouse with some trim, together with a necklace made of amber. In the south, women wore green skirts and used more elaborate embroidery and needlework. Blouses could also be red or white.

Northern men wore long brown coats tied at the waist with a red sash, white linen shirts, and white trousers fastened at the bottom with straps from their Kurpie shoes. Northern men wore dark brown top hats, while men from the south wore small black caps. Trousers could be grey or white. The sources do not distinguish everyday clothing from ceremonial dress beyond these regional variations.

### Architecture

Traditional Kurpie homes were made of timber and had thatched roofs. Crosses or flags were mounted on the gable roof. The roof fascia and bargeboards often carried carved decorative ornaments, and the ridge was usually topped with animal heads. Interiors were sparse, with wooden floors and windows containing six panes of glass. Shutters and doors were painted.

In 1927, Adam Chetnik established an open-air museum in Nowogród displaying traditional Kurpie homes and other elements of Kurpie culture.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that men used forest materials to create wood products and that amber was polished at home.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Amber necklaces formed part of the northern women’s costume.

## Music & performance

Kurpie music is described as very different from that of neighboring regions. More than one thousand original Kurpie songs were recorded by the Polish priest Władysław Skierkowski in his book *Puszcza Kurpiowska w pieśni*.

Kurpie music also influenced concert music: the first part of Henryk M. Górecki’s third symphony, titled “Symphony of Sorrowful Songs,” was inspired by it.

## Dance & theatre

Kurpie has traditional dances including the “horse” (*konik*), in which dancing men imitate a horse and rider. The Kadzidlańskie Wedding includes dances, songs, chants, and wedding ceremonies based on ancient wedding rituals.

## Festivals & rituals

Kurpie Palm Sunday involved making tall Easter Sunday palms from small trees and decorating them with flowers and other ornaments. The stated purpose was to protect houses from ghosts.

At Easter, Kurpie women were known for decorated Easter eggs and cookies shaped like an Easter lamb. During *Smigus-Dingus*, people sprinkled water over one another as an expression of affection, with men generally taking the lead in sprinkling women.

A religious procession known as the God’s day celebration visited various altars. Participants wore traditional costumes, and the procession leaders blessed the Kurpie lands. In August, Kurpies collected grain and flowers and took them to church to be blessed.

On 6 December, a winter celebration praised the good fortune that cattle had not been eaten by wolves. At Christmas, an embossed wafer (*opłatek*) depicting a religious scene was shared with family members and cattle. Women placed hay under the table, while children sang Christmas carols in the streets in return for treats.

For New Year’s celebrations, cookies shaped like animals, called “bulls,” and circular cookies showing a bird, called “years,” were hung from the ceiling. The Kadzidlańskie Wedding was a named wedding tradition involving dances, songs, chants, and ceremonies.

## Foodways

These include Easter cookies shaped like an Easter lamb and New Year’s cookies called “bulls” and “years.”

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record a preservation effort by Władysław Skierkowski, who recorded over one thousand original Kurpie songs in *Puszcza Kurpiowska w pieśni*.

The sources also mention Henryk Sienkiewicz’s historical novel *The Deluge*, whose beginning of Chapter 55 gives an account of the Kurpie region and its people during the 17th-century Swedish invasion. Chapter 111 contains an account of the Kurpie peasant attack on the Swedish army. Sienkiewicz also describes Kurpie people in Chapters XX and XXI of *The Teutonic Knights*.

## Language & religion

They describe religious practices including processions to altars, the blessing of Kurpie lands, the blessing of grain and flowers at church, and the sharing of the religiously depicted *opłatek* at Christmas.
