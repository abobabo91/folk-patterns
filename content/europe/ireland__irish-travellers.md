## Overview

Irish Travellers are a traditionally peripatetic indigenous ethno-cultural group originating in Ireland. They refer to themselves in Shelta as *Mincéirí*, or in Irish as *an lucht siúil*, meaning “the walking people”; they are also known as Pavees. Irish Traveller communities are located in Ireland, the United Kingdom, the United States and Canada. In 2016, there were 32,302 Travellers within Ireland, representing 0.7% of Ireland’s population. The group is predominantly English-speaking, though many Travellers also speak Shelta, a language of mixed English and Irish origin. Most are Roman Catholic. Their folk-cultural significance includes oral storytelling, itinerant metalworking, distinctive clothing, beady pockets, music, family networks, life-cycle customs and traditions associated with travel.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Beady pockets* are flat, pocket-sized pouches associated with traditional Irish Traveller attire. They are characterised by intricate embroidery and beadwork and are identified in the source as a distinctive feature of Irish Traveller clothing.

## Motif vocabulary.

The sources name embroidery and beadwork.

### Clothing & dress

Irish Travellers have clothing traditions distinct from those of the wider Irish population. Beady pockets are a documented element of traditional attire: these are flat, pocket-sized pouches decorated with intricate embroidery and beadwork.

### Architecture

The sources describe barrel-roofed horse-drawn wagons, tents and mobile homes or trailers. Around 60% of Irish Travellers lived in barrel-roofed horse-drawn wagons at the time discussed in the source, while almost 40% used tents in summer and fewer used them in winter. The sources also state that only an estimated 15–20% still live in mobile homes or trailers, often in substandard and overcrowded conditions.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Irish Travellers worked with metal and travelled throughout Ireland making ornaments, jewellery and horse harnesses to earn a living. They were consequently referred to as “tinkers” or “tinklers”, terms meaning “tin smiths” that later became regarded as derogatory.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources identify ornaments and jewellery among the objects made by Irish Travellers, as well as beady pockets decorated with embroidery and beadwork.

## Music & performance

Irish Traveller music is described as known for lively and virtuosic melodies and as a significant and influential part of Ireland’s musical landscape.

The sources describe storytelling as the primary method through which the Traveller community disseminates its own history and culture.

## Festivals & rituals

Irish Traveller culture is described as including family networks, birth, marriage and burial rituals, taboos and folklore.

Irish Traveller Ethnicity Day is an annual event held on 1 March. It commemorates the Government of Ireland’s recognition of Irish Travellers as a distinct ethnic minority on 1 March 2017. On that date, Taoiseach Enda Kenny announced the recognition, describing Irish Travellers as “a people within our people”. Educational institutions have since launched events relating to the day, including the Atlantic Technological University, University of Galway, University College Dublin and NUI Galway.

The UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions supplied for this profile contain no entries for Irish Travellers.

## Foodways

The sources state that hedgehogs are, or at least used to be, eaten by Irish Travellers. Irish scones are also popular among Irish Travellers in the United States. A typical meal is described as coddle or spuds and cabbage with Irish soda bread.

## Oral tradition & literature

Irish Travellers appear to have no written records of their own in the account supplied. Oral tradition through storytelling is described as the primary method through which the community disseminates its history and culture. The sources mention folklore and taboos.

## Language & religion

Irish Travellers predominantly speak English, while many also speak Shelta. The source describes Shelta as a language of mixed English and Irish origin, and identifies two dialects: Gammon, or Gamin, and Irish Traveller Cant. Shelta has been dated to the 18th century but may be older. Cant derives from Irish and is described as a combination of English and Shelta.

Shelta is a cryptolect, or secret language. Irish Travellers use the term “Buffers” for outsiders or non-Travellers, and speakers may disguise Shelta’s structure when speaking before them. The language is made up mostly of Irish lexicon and has English-based grammar.

The majority of Irish Travellers are Roman Catholic. Travellers practise their Catholic faith at holy wells and shrines across Ireland and pay particular attention to faith healing. The sources also describe a strict code of behaviour influencing some moral beliefs and actions. In 2022, the Irish Bishops Conference asked every parish to welcome Irish Travellers to towns and villages throughout Ireland.
