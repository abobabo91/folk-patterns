## Overview

The Masurians, also called Mazurs, are a Polish ethnic group originating in Masuria, within the modern-day Warmian-Masurian Voivodeship, Poland. Historically known also as Prussian Masurians, they lived especially in the southern parts of East Prussia. They number around 5,000 to 15,000 people; in the 2011 Polish census, 1,376 individuals declared themselves Masurian as a first or secondary identification. Most are descendants of colonists from Mazovia, with some admixture of the Old Prussians. They spoke Masurian dialects and many were bilingual in German and Polish. Today, most Masurians live in modern-day Germany and elsewhere.

## Oral tradition & literature

Masurian folk songs were collected by Gustaw Gizewiusz during the 1840s and later included in Oskar Kolberg’s *Dzieła Wszystkie*. Masurians referred to themselves in the 19th century as “Polish Prussians” or *Staroprusaki* (Old Prussians). The sources also mention the poem “O Mazurach,” published by Wojciech Kętrzyński in 1872.

## Language & religion

The Masurians spoke Masurian dialects. High German was increasingly used from the mid-19th century, and many Masurians were bilingual in German and Polish. Like most of the East Prussian population, Masurians favored Protestantism and adopted Lutheranism in 1525, when Albert, Duke of Prussia, secularized the duchy and converted. This distinguished them religiously from neighboring Roman Catholic Warmiaks and Masovians. After World War II, conflict between leaving Masurians and incoming Kurpie was associated partly with Protestant–Catholic divisions.
