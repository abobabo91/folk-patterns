## Overview

The Manx are an ethnic group originating on the Isle of Man, an island in the Irish Sea in Northern Europe. According to the 2011 interim census, the Isle of Man had 84,655 inhabitants, including 26,218 in the capital, Douglas. The Manx are governed through Tynwald, a legislature introduced by Viking settlers over a thousand years ago. Their heritage language, Manx Gaelic, is a Goidelic Celtic language related to Irish and Scottish Gaelic. Manx culture combines Celtic and Norse influences with later English influence. Its folk-cultural significance is especially visible in the survival and revival of Manx language, music, dance, mythology, foodways and political traditions.

## Music & performance

Manx music reflects Celtic, Norse and other influences, including those from Scotland, Ireland, England and Wales. Little can be determined about music before the 15th century, although carved crosses depict two musicians: a lur player and a harpist. The song *Reeaghyn dy Vannin*, the Manx sword dance, is described as similar to a lullaby from the Hebrides and as possibly having been a ritual dance during the Scandinavian era.

Church music is the most documented Manx music of the 19th century. The first collection of Manx church songs was printed in 1799. During the 1870s and 1880s, drawing-room ballads, religious songs and choral arrangements became increasingly common. Manx music-hall and dance-hall songs and dances also benefited from the growth of tourism.

The 1970s folk revival began after the death of Ned Maddrell, the last native speaker of Manx, in 1974. A music festival called *Yn Çhruinnaght* was held in Ramsey. Musicians associated with the revival include King Chiaullee, Skeeal, The Mannin Folk, Mactullagh Vannin and Moot. Other documented performers include Emma Christian, a singer, harpist and recorder player, and Charles Guard, a harpist and producer.

Manx carvals are related to the medieval English carol and were sung to popular Manx tunes. They were formerly performed in parish churches on Christmas Eve, or *Oie'l Verrey*. The traditional song *Arrane Oie Vie* was sung on the way home from church. The melody *Mylecharane* is described as a traditional Manx melody and the Manx National Air. Culture Vannin supports Manx music and dance through educational and community work.

## Dance & theatre

*Reeaghyn dy Vannin* is the named Manx sword dance. It is compared with a lullaby from the Hebrides and is also described as a ritual dance during the Scandinavian era. Cammag, the national sport of Mann, is similar to Irish hurling and the Scottish game of shinty; it ceased to be played by the beginning of the 20th century and was later revived through an annual match at St John's.

They do identify the Gaiety Theatre and Opera House in Douglas as a theatre and opera house forming part of the Villa-Gaiety complex.

## Festivals & rituals

The Manx Music Festival is an annual festival held at the end of each April in Douglas. It was founded in 1892 by M. L. Wood after music classes were included in the Fine Arts and Industrial Guild. The festival includes singing, instrumental, drama and public-speaking classes. At its close, winners of the individual voice categories compete for the Cleveland Medal, first donated in 1923 by the Cleveland Manx Society. The Manx National Anthem was first performed at The Guild in 1907.

The 1970s folk revival was associated with *Yn Çhruinnaght*, a music festival in Ramsey. The Manx sources also describe Christmas Eve church customs. On *Oie'l Verrey*, people brought candles to parish churches, where prayers, hymns and carols were performed. Church decorations included holly and ivy. After the parson left, members of the congregation sang carols in turn, and peas could be thrown at bachelor friends. The traditional drink was hot ale flavoured with spice, ginger and pepper.

Manx mythology associates *Midsummer Eve*, given as 24 June, with a traditional payment demanded by Mannan: a bundle of coarse marsh-grass like rushes.

## Foodways

The traditional national dish of the Isle of Man is *spuds and herrin*, boiled potatoes and herring. It is associated with subsistence farmers who cultivated the land and fished the sea. Seafood has traditionally formed a large part of the local diet. Manx kippers, or smoked herring, are produced by smokeries in Peel, which also produce smoked salmon and bacon.

Crab, lobster and scallops are commercially fished. The queen scallop, known as “queenies,” is especially well regarded for its light, sweet flavour. Cod, ling and mackerel are angled for the table, while trout and salmon are taken from local rivers and lakes. Cattle, sheep, pigs and poultry are commercially farmed, and Manx lamb from hill-farms is a popular dish. The Loaghtan is described as the indigenous breed of Manx sheep, with rich, dark meat.

Manx cheese includes smoked and herb-flavoured varieties. It received bronze medals at the 2005 British Cheese Awards and sold 578 tonnes over the year. The Peel flapjack is a popular dessert. Beer is brewed commercially by Okells Brewery, Bushy's Brewery and Hooded Ram Brewing Company. The island has a beer purity law resembling the German Reinheitsgebot, dating from 1874. As of 2019, the Isle of Man also had a whiskey distillery operated by the Manx Whiskey Company.

## Oral tradition & literature

Manx folklore belongs to the wider group of Celtic mythology. Named figures include Manannán mac Lir, the Mooinjer veggey, the Buggane, Lhiannan-Shee, Ben-Varrey and the Moddey Dhoo. Stories also describe the Fenodyree, Glashtyn, tarroo-ushtey or Water-Bull, cabbyl-ushtey or water horse, Jimmy Squarefoot and the Arkan Sonney or Arc-Vuc-Soney.

An Irish folktale attributes the formation of the Isle of Man to Fionn mac Cumhaill, who hurled a mass of clay and rock from Northern Ireland into the Irish Sea. Another tradition connects the island’s name with Manannán mac Lir. The ballad *Mannanan beg mac y Leirr; ny, slane coontey jeh Ellan Vannin*, dated to 1507–22, tells of Mannan’s rule and his use of mist to conceal the island.

The earliest datable Manx text is a poetic history of the Isle of Man dating at least to the 16th century. Religious writing was more common than secular literature. The first printed work in Manx, *Coyrle Sodjeh*, dates from 1707. The first Manx Bible was printed between 1771 and 1775. The language revival produced new literature, including Brian Stowell’s Manx translation of *Alice in Wonderland*, published in 1990, and the first full-length Manx novel, *Dunveryssyn yn Tooder-Folley*, published in 2006.

## Language & religion

Manx, also known as Manx Gaelic, is a Goidelic language of the insular Celtic branch of the Celtic language family. Its endonyms include *Gaelg*, *y Ghaelg*, *Gailck* and *y Ghailck*. It is closely related to Irish and Scottish Gaelic and developed in relative isolation on the Isle of Man. Two historical dialects are attested: Northern Manx and Southern Manx.

Manx is considered critically endangered. Ned Maddrell, the last native speaker to grow up in a Manx-speaking community environment, died in 1974. The language revival has included recordings, school teaching, bilingual signs, radio programmes and the Manx-medium primary school Bunscoill Ghaelgagh at St John's. In the 2011 census, 1,823 of 80,398 residents claimed knowledge of Manx.

The predominant religious tradition is Anglican Christianity. The island’s ancient Christian Church is part of the Church of England, and the Diocese of Sodor and Man traces its history to 1154. Methodist, Roman Catholic, Baptist, Pentecostal, Jewish and Muslim communities are also documented. Manx folklore includes Christian ballads, church songs and stories of supernatural beings.
