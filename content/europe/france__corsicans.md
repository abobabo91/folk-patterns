## Overview

The Corsicans (Corsican, Italian: *Corsi*; French: *Corses*) are a Romance ethnic group native to the Mediterranean island of Corsica, a territorial collectivity of France. Corsica had a population of 322,120 inhabitants according to a January 2013 estimate. Corsican (*Corsu*) is a Romance language belonging to the Italo-Dalmatian branch and is akin to medieval Tuscan. Corsican culture includes distinctive first names, supernatural beliefs, vendetta practices, Catholic and liturgical singing, polyphonic song, proverbs, and cuisine based on game, seafood, pork, cheeses, chestnuts, and Corsican wines. The island’s population has also been shaped by connections with Tuscany, Liguria, Sardinia, France, and other Mediterranean regions.

## Music & performance

Catholicism has directly influenced Corsican traditional music, symphonies, and songs, particularly through liturgical songs and singing practices. The Corsican language is described as a key vehicle for Corsican culture and is notably rich in proverbs and polyphonic song.

## Festivals & rituals

They describe Catholicism as being at the source of Corsican culture and mention Catholic and liturgical songs.

## Foodways

Corsican foodways draw on mountain, plain, and sea environments. Game such as wild boar, called *Cignale* or *Singhjari*, is popular, while mouflon, *muvra*, was consumed in earlier times. Seafood and river fish such as trout are also part of the cuisine. Corsican pork, *porcu nustrale*, is used to make *figatellu*, *coppa*, ham called *prizuttu*, and *lonzu*. Goat’s or sheep’s milk is used for cheeses including *Brocciu*, *casgiu merzu*, described as the Corsican version of Sardinian *casu marzu*, and *casgiu veghju*. Chestnuts are the main ingredient in *pulenta*. Drinks include *aquavita*, red and white Corsican wines called *Vinu Corsu*, muscat in plain or sparkling forms, and *cap corse*, produced by Mattei.

## Oral tradition & literature

Corsican culture is described as notably rich in proverbs and polyphonic song. Corsican supernatural beliefs include *L’ochju*, the Corsican version of the Evil Eye. A *signatore* is described as a faith healer who removes *l’ochju* through prayers and rituals. The *Mazzeru* is a sorcerer who predicts human death in dreams by hunting and killing a wild beast whose face becomes that of a loved one who will soon die. The sources also record the vendetta as a major social practice associated with honor, dishonor, and revenge for murder; in some cases, a mediator can bring the vengeance to an end.

The sources name several works concerning Corsica and the Corsicans, including *Justification of the Corsican Revolution* (1758), written in standard Italian by Gregoriu Salvini; *An Account of Corsica* (1768) by James Boswell; and Jean-Jacques Rousseau’s *Constitutional Project for Corsica*. Fictional works listed include *The Corsican Brothers* by Alexandre Dumas, *Mateo Falcone* and *Colomba* by Prosper Mérimée, and *Asterix in Corsica* by René Goscinny and Albert Uderzo.

## Language & religion

Corsican (*Corsu*) is a Romance language of the Italo-Dalmatian branch, akin to medieval Tuscan. French (*Français*) is the official language throughout France, while Corsican is the other most widely spoken language on the island. Corsican was long the vernacular language alongside Italian (*Italiano*), which retained official status in Corsica until 1859. Italian was replaced by French as the island’s traditional prestige language following the annexation of Corsica by France in 1768. By the Liberation in 1945, all islanders had a working knowledge of French. In 1990, an estimated 50% of islanders had some degree of proficiency in Corsican, while perhaps 10% used it as a first language. The sources also name the Ligurian dialect *bunifazzin* in Bonifacio and the Corsican variety Gallurese in the extreme north of Sardinia.

The Corsicans are in the extreme majority Roman Catholics, with their own pre-Christian contributions. Catholicism is described as central to Corsican culture and traditional music. Earlier Corsican Muslims followed Sunni Islam; these Muslims were established in North Africa and were often freed slaves.
