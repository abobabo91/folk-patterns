## Overview

The Volga Tatars, also called Volga-Ural Tatars or simply Tatars, are a Turkic ethnic group native to the Volga-Ural region of western Russia. They are primarily found in Tatarstan, where they make up 53.6% of the population, and they also live in other regions of Russia and in diasporas across the globe. Their native language is Tatar, a Turkic language of the Kipchak–Bulgar branch, and they are primarily followers of Sunni Islam. The cultural center of the Idel-Ural Tatars is the Republic of Tatarstan. Their history is connected with Volga Bulgaria, the Golden Horde, the Khanate of Kazan, and later Russian rule. Their folk culture includes a distinct language, Islamic celebrations, Sabantuy, wrestling, horseback riding, literature, theater, and music.

## Music & performance

Professional Tatar music culture took shape during the Soviet era. The sources name several composers who became famous in the mid-20th century: Cəwdət Fəyzi, Salix Səydəşev, Mansur Mozaffarov, and Nəcip Cihanov. The largest center of Tatar folk music is identified as the Musa Dzhalil Opera and Ballet Theater.

The first Tatar opera, *Saniyə*, was performed in 1925. It was composed by Soltan Ğəbəşi in collaboration with Vasily Vinogradov. Fərit Yarullin composed the first Tatar ballet, *Şürəle*. Sofia Gubaidulina is described as one of the “most significant Russian composers of the late 20th century.” The works of compositor Rashid Kalimullin have been performed worldwide, and Aida Garifullina performed together with Robbie Williams in the 2018 FIFA World Cup.

Named Tatar folk singers include Rəşit Wahapov, Xəydər Bigiçev, and Rafail İlyasov. Modern Tatar musicians named by the source include İttifaQ, Usal, Tatarka, K-Ru, Taraf, Saida Mukhametzyanova, Malsi Music, and Dyshat. Başkarma is described as a “legendary Tatar folk-rock group” from Finland.

## Dance & theatre

They do state that Sabantuy includes competitive sports such as wrestling and horseback riding, and that the wrestling genre *körəş* is widely known among Turkic peoples.

The first published Tatar play was written by Ğabdraxman İlyas in 1887 and was called *Biçara qız*, meaning “The Unhappy Girl.” A professional Tatar theater group called *Səyər* emerged in early 1907 in Uralsk. This group is thought to have formed the basis of the Galiaskar Kamal Tatar Academic theatre in Kazan, Tatarstan. Its repertoire mainly includes plays in the Tatar language, alongside some plays written by Russians and others. Translation has been arranged for people who do not speak Tatar. Named playwrights include Mirxəydər Fəyzi, Kərim Tinçurin, Ğəliəsğar Kamal, Ğayaz İsxaqi, and Zölfət Xəkim.

## Festivals & rituals

The traditional celebrations of the Tatar people include ancient Turkic and common Islamic celebrations. The Persian New Year *Nowruz* is also celebrated among Tatars. The best-known Tatar cultural holiday is *Sabantuy*, also called *Saban tuyı*. It dates back to pre-Islamic times and began as a harvest festival for farmers. Today, Sabantuy is a national holiday in Tatarstan. Its program includes competitive sports, including wrestling and horseback riding. The celebration is also found among neighboring nations and in Kazakhstan.

The Islamic roots of the Volga region are connected in the source with Volga Bulgaria and the year 922. In September 2010, Eid al-Fitr and 21 May, identified as the day the Volga Bulgars embraced Islam, were made public holidays in Tatarstan. The annual celebration of the Bulgar conversion to Islam is also mentioned.

## Foodways

They state only that the Tatars are primarily followers of Sunni Islam and that the first halal food production facility in Tatarstan opened after negotiations concerning Islamic banking in September 2010.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tatar literature has an ancient history. Before printing, ancient Tatar books written in Arabic script were copied by hand. Manuscripts of the Koran, other spiritual literature, and educational books were widely distributed. One of the earliest works of national Tatar literature named by the source is *Qissa-i Yosıf*, a poetic work written at the beginning of the 13th century by the Volga Bulgar poet Qol Ğəli. Its title is glossed as “Tale of Yusuf.”

The first printed edition in the Tatar language was the Manifesto of Peter I on the occasion of the Persian campaign, published in 1722. The Golden Horde and the Khanate of Kazan were described as periods of major development for Turkic-Tatar literature. Nasreddin bine Borhaneddin Rabğuzi is named among the writers of the Golden Horde, and Möxəmməd Yar as a notable poet of the Khanate of Kazan.

Ğabdulla Tuqay is often considered the founder of the modern Tatar literary language. Tatar literature of the 20th century included works by Ğabdulla Tuqay, Fatix Əmirxan, Dərdemənd, and Ğəliəsğar Kamal. Later literature addressed Soviet politics, World War II, and the independence of the Tatar people.

## Language & religion

Tatar is a Turkic language belonging to the Kipchak–Bulgar sub-branch of the Kipchak languages. The source identifies two main dialects: Central, also called *Urta* or Kazan, and Western, also called *Könbatış* or Mishar. Some linguists consider Siberian Tatar a third dialect, while Crimean Tatar is identified as a different language within the Kipchak group.

Tatar has been influenced by Uralic languages spoken in the Volga River area, as well as Arabic, Persian, and Russian. The language has used Arabic script, and the source gives an example in the Latin script. Tatar names have mostly been rooted in Arabic since the spread of Arabic influence through the adoption of Islam, although Turkic-Persian names remain in use.

Volga Tatars are primarily Sunni Muslims. The Islamic roots of the region are traced to Volga Bulgaria, whose adoption of Islam is associated with 922. Islam is currently the majority religion in Tatarstan. The source also discusses a historically Sufi-influenced tradition, Jadidism, the Naqshbandi order, and changing religious attitudes after the Soviet period.
