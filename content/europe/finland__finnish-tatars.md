## Overview

The Finnish Tatars are a Tatar ethnic group and minority in Finland consisting of approximately 600–700 people. They practice Sunni Islam and speak the Turkic Tatar language, specifically its western Mishar dialect. The community formed between the late 1800s and the early 1900s, when Mishar Tatar merchants emigrated from the Nizhny Novgorod Governorate of the Russian Empire and settled in Finland. Many originated in neighboring villages in Sergachsky District, especially Aktuk. The community’s main congregation building is in Helsinki, and cultural associations have also been founded in other cities. Finnish Tatars are described as the oldest Muslim community in Finland. Their cultural history includes migration, commercial life, religious organization, language change, connections with Tatarstan and Turkey, and the preservation of a distinct Tatar identity.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources state that Tatar merchants sold fabrics, furs, clothes, cotton products, silk fabrics, carpets and soap. In Vyborg halls, cotton products, silk fabrics, carpets and furs were among the goods sold, while fabric was often the main product in Tampere.

### Clothing & dress

They record that Tatar merchants sold clothes, fabrics, furs, cotton products, silk fabrics and carpets. The sources also state that many Mishar Tatars had worked as farmers in their home villages before becoming merchants because of lack of income. No further clothing traditions are documented.

### Architecture

Finnish Tatars have their main congregation building in Helsinki. The Finnish-Islamic Congregation’s main building is in Fredrikinkatu, Helsinki, and the congregation also owns a wooden mosque in Järvenpää. A separate Tampere Tatar congregation has its space in Hämeenkatu. The first registered Tatar and Islamic formation in Finland was founded in 1915 as “Helsingin musulmaanien hyväntekeväisyysseura,” or “Helsinki Moslems’ Charity Club.” An actual congregation was founded in 1925 after Finnish legal approval of the relevant practices in 1922.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention commercial goods including fabrics, furs, clothes, cotton products, silk fabrics, carpets and soap, but provide no catalogue of objects used in domestic life or identify particular forms as Finnish Tatar.

## Music & performance

They record that Finnish Tatars received Bashkir artists as guests in 1967, shortly before contacts with Kazan Tatars strengthened. The Tampere Tatar congregation also received musicians including Haydar Bigichev.

The community’s cultural associations and religious congregations organized cultural events and publishing. The Helsinki-based Suomen turkkilaisten seura, or Association of Finnish Turks, and Tampereen Turkkilainen Yhdistys, or Tampere Turkish Society, were established in 1935. The Turun Turkkilais-Tataarilainen Yhdistys, or Turku Turko-Tatar Association, followed two years later. These organizations mainly focused on cultural events and publishing.

## Festivals & rituals

The Finnish Tatar community undertook its first pilgrimage to Mecca in the 1920s, and members have continued making pilgrimages to Mecca since the 1920s. The sources identify this as a religious practice.

The community organized “a day of Tatar culture” in Järvenpää in 1974, an event open to Finns. The Tatar community of Tampere organized a three-day celebration for Ayaz Ishaki in February 1937. Finnish Tatars also organized a 20-year memory celebration of the Idel-Ural state in Warsaw in 1938, where seven Finnish Tatars were present.

They state that the Finnish Tatars are Sunni Muslims and that their community did not receive the right to wed its members until 1932, because authorities believed this could lead to polygamy, although polygamy was not practiced among the Tatars.

## Foodways

They state that Finnish Tatars practice Sunni Islam.

The sources mention soap among the goods sold by Tatar merchants, together with fabrics, furs and clothes.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record a history of publishing, education and translation. Cultural associations and the religious congregation organized publishing, and associations arranged language instruction for children.

A Finnish Tatar history book by Dr. Ramil Belyaev, imam of the Finnish-Islamic Congregation, was translated into Turkish and released in Ankara in 2024. The sources also describe efforts to produce a Finnish translation of the Quran. In 1924, Finnish Tatars Zinnetullah Ahsen and Imad Samaletdin traveled to Berlin to meet Tatar activist Ayaz İshaki and Bashkir activist Zeki Velidi Togan and discuss these efforts.

## Language & religion

Finnish Tatars speak the Turkic Tatar language, more specifically its western Mishar dialect. It differs from the dialect of the Kazan Tatars, identified in the sources as standard Tatar, and also differs from the language of Mishars in Russia. The Finnish Tatar dialect does not use the letter X and uses soft H instead.

Before adopting the Latin alphabet, Finnish Tatars used the Arabic script and modified it to fit their language. Abdullah Battal, later Battal-Taymas, adapted spelling in a more phonetic direction and worked to make Tatar vowels clearly and consistently visible in Arabic script while standardizing the marking of consonants. From the 1930s onward, the community gradually changed to the Latin script. By the 1950s, Finnish Tatar publications had mostly adopted it, and children were taught with the Latin alphabet during the 1960s. The first Latin script was borrowed directly from Turkish and later modified.

Finnish Tatars are Sunni Muslims. Their religious institutions include the Finnish-Islamic Congregation in Helsinki, the Tampere Islamic Congregation, founded in 1942, and the wooden mosque in Järvenpää. Finland became the first western country to give Muslims official recognition. The congregations accept only Tatars as members.
