## Overview

Burgenland Croats (*Gradišćanski Hrvati*) are ethnic Croats living chiefly in the Austrian state of Burgenland, with related communities in neighboring Hungary and Slovakia. Between 87,000 and 130,000 residents of Austria are Burgenland Croats. Their settlements include villages south and northeast of Lake Neusiedl, east of Mattersburg, and in the southern and northern parts of Burgenland. The group emerged through migrations from Lika, Krbava, Kordun, Banovina, Moslavina and Western Bosnia during the Turkish wars of 1533–1584. They preserved Croatian language varieties, developed their own orthography, and maintained cultural institutions, schools, churches, newspapers, literature and tamburica orchestras. Their cultural history also reflects Magyarization, persecution during World War II, and a cultural renaissance following the Austrian Treaty of Independence of 1955.

## Music & performance

Tamburica is identified as part of Burgenland Croats’ cultural identity. Around 40 tamburica orchestras were active among them, and the orchestra *Tamburica Štikapron* was established in 1961.

The Croatian Culture Association in Burgenland has organized Festival klapov, described as a “Klapa festival,” since 2011. The sources establish the festival’s connection with Burgenland Croatian cultural organization.

Music also appears in the group’s institutional and linguistic life. Croatian was used in Church wherever a sufficiently large minority existed, and Croatian-language cultural associations, newspapers and literary organizations supported public cultural activity.

## Dance & theatre

Burgenland Croats’ Folklore ensemble *Kolo Slavuj* is known for choreography of Burgenland Croats folklore and created this choreography in collaboration with Ivan Ivančan.

## Festivals & rituals

The Croatian Culture Association in Burgenland has organized Festival klapov, or “Klapa festival,” since 2011. This is the only named festival in the sources. No annual festival calendar, seasonal observance, harvest festival, religious feast, coming-of-age rite, wedding custom or funeral ritual is described.

The historical sources do document religious and communal institutions. Refugee Croats were given independent ecclesiastical rights by Austrian King Ferdinand I. In 1922, Austria founded the Apostolic administration of Burgenland, and in 1960 the Diocese Eisenstadt, also called Željezno, was founded. Croatian was used in Church wherever a large enough minority existed.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources connect the beginnings of Burgenland Croatian literacy with *Klimpuški misal* (*Klimpuški Missal*) from 1501, S. Consul Histrianus and Anton Dalmatin’s *Postila* (*Postil*) from 1568, *Duševne pesne* (*Spiritual songs*) from 1609, and Grgur Mekinić Pythiraeus’s *Druge knjige duševnih pesan* (*Other books of the Spiritual songs*) from 1611. By the mid-19th century, Burgenland Croatian literature had a predominantly religious character and was intended mostly for peasants; priests and nuns were its main writers.

In the second half of the 19th century, teachers began writing, leading to the production of school textbooks and calendars. The sources list writers including J. Mulih, Godefrid Palković, L. Bogović, E. M. Kragel, M. Laáb, J. Ficko, M. Drobilić, T. Jordan, G. Glavanić, M. Naković, I. Mušković, M. Borenić, Ivan Čuković, P. Jandrišević, I. Blažević, Mate Meršić Miloradić, Ignac Horvat, Martin Meršić, A. Blazović, Franz Probst, N. Benčić, Ivan (Lav) Sučić, Mathilda Bölcs, J. Čenar, P. Tyran and H. Gassner. F. Sedenik wrote the first book about the history of Burgenland literature, *Naši pisci i književnost* (*Our writers and literature*), in 1912.

## Language & religion

Burgenland Croats preserved Croatian and dialects associated with the Croatian regions from which they originated. Burgenland Croatian combines Chakavian, Shtokavian and Kajkavian elements, but its principal basis is the Chakavian dialect. It also contains phrases no longer used in standard Croatian and words taken from German and Hungarian. Almost all Burgenland Croats are fluent in German. Names are often written according to Hungarian orthography because of Magyarization policies during the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

The sources describe independent ecclesiastical rights, the use of Croatian in Church, the Apostolic administration of Burgenland founded in 1922, and the Diocese Eisenstadt founded in 1960.
