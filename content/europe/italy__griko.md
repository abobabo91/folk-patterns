## Overview

The Griko are an ethnic Greek community of Southern Italy, found principally in Calabria and Apulia, especially the Salento peninsula. In Calabria, Greek-speaking settlements lie in mountainous terrain around the southern Aspromonte massif, while in Apulia the principal enclave is Grecia Salentina, in the province of Lecce. Two small Italiot Greek-speaking communities survive today: one in Calabria and one in Apulia. The total population of Grecia Salentina is around 40,000, while the Calabrian Greek region has around 2,000 inhabitants. The Griko preserve two endangered dialects, Griko in Salento and Grecanico in Calabria. Their significance in folk culture lies particularly in their oral poetry, songs, dances, foodways, and the survival of Greek cultural forms within Italy.

## Music & performance

The Griko possess an extensive oral tradition of folk music and lyric poetry. Their repertoire includes songs in Griko and Grecanico, performed by traditional and neo-folk ensembles in Italy and Greece. Named groups associated with Salento include Canzoniere Grecanico Salentino, Ghetonia, Arakne Mediterranea, and Officina Zoè; named Calabrian groups include Astaki and Nistanimera. The Griko musical tradition has also been performed or recorded by George Dalaras, Haris Alexiou, Maria Farantouri, Dionysis Savvopoulos, and the Greek ensemble Encardia.

A prominent musical form is *Pizzica*, an ancient fast-tempo courtship and healing dance connected to tarantism. Another is *moroloja*, funeral dirges belonging to the oral poetic tradition. The annual Notte della Taranta festival in Melpignano presents traditional Salentine music and Griko poetry and draws over 100,000 visitors. The documentary *Encardia, the Dancing Stone* presents the shared musical heritage of Greece and Southern Italy.

## Dance & theatre

*Pizzica* is the principal named Griko-associated dance in the sources. It is described as a fast-tempo courtship and healing dance connected to tarantism. The sources also describe Griko songs, music, and poetry as popular in Italy and Greece, with performances by ensembles such as Canzoniere Grecanico Salentino, Ghetonia, Aramirè, and Encardia.

In Calabria, contemporary literary activity has included theatrical plays.

## Festivals & rituals

The best-documented annual festival in the sources is the Notte della Taranta, held in Melpignano. It presents traditional Salentine music and Griko poetry and draws over 100,000 visitors.

The sources document several ritual contexts through song and food. *Moroloja* are funeral dirges, traditionally chanted by women during wakes. In Calabria, *strine* are rhyming songs performed during festive seasons. The sources also identify *Aggute*, a brioche-like Easter bread decorated with whole boiled eggs, *Cuddhure*, Easter ring pastries, and *Scardateddhi*, anise-scented fried pastry rings served at Calabrian weddings.

## Foodways

The traditional gastronomy of Grecia Salentina and Bovesia is based on Mediterranean agriculture, especially cereals, legumes, wild greens, and olive oil. The sources name several regional preparations. *Lestopitta* and *Pitta* are traditional flatbreads of the Calabrian Bovesia. *Ciceri e ttrìa* consists of fresh tagliatelle served with seasoned chickpeas and fried pasta ribbons in Salento. *Cranu stompatu* is hulled wheat that is soaked, pounded, and cooked with tomato and pecorino.

*Sceblasti* is a rustic yeast bread baked on a stone hearth in Zollino and prepared with squash, olives, and onions. In Calabria, *Aggute* is an Easter bread decorated with whole boiled eggs and compared in the source with Greek tsoureki. *Cuddhure* are Easter ring pastries related to Greek *koulouri*. *Scardateddhi* are anise-scented fried pastry rings coated in honey and sugar and served at Calabrian weddings.

## Oral tradition & literature

Griko vernacular literature was preserved primarily through oral poetry. *Moroloja*, from Greek μοιρολόγια, are funeral laments chanted by women during wakes. They retain classical Greek metric structures and mourning imagery. In Calabria, oral literature also includes *strine*, rhyming songs performed during festive seasons, and fairy tales called *paramythe*. Nineteenth-century philologists including Domenico Comparetti and Giuseppe Morosi transcribed some of these materials.

A major revival figure was Vito Domenico Palumbo (1854–1918) of Calimera. He collected Griko folklore, oral poetry, and songs, composed original lyric verse in Griko, and translated classical and modern authors into the dialect. In Salento, Rocco Aprile, Brizio Montinaro, and Franco Corlianò expanded vernacular writing during the mid- and late twentieth century. Corlianò’s poem and song “Klama” (“Andramu pai”) concerns post-war Italian emigration to Swiss and German industrial centers. In Calabria, writers and philologists included Filippo Violi, Filippo Condemi, Angelo Maesano, and Salvino Nucera.

## Language & religion

Griko and Grecanico are the two Italiot Greek dialects. Griko is spoken in Grecia Salentina in Apulia, while Grecanico, also called Calabrian Greek, is spoken in Bovesia in southern Calabria. The dialects contain archaic vocabulary, phonology, and grammar derived from ancient Doric and Koine Greek, together with Byzantine Greek syntax and long lexical exchange with neighboring Salentino and Calabrian Romance dialects. Griko and Standard Modern Greek are partially mutually intelligible. UNESCO data from 2011 classify both dialects as severely endangered; the sources state that approximately 20,000 speakers remain in Salento and fewer than 1,000 in Calabria.

The Greek communities historically followed the Byzantine Rite under the jurisdiction of the Ecumenical Patriarchate of Constantinople. Following the Norman conquest, Latin bishops gradually replaced Greek hierarchs, and the communities later transitioned under Roman papal authority while retaining Greek liturgical practices. Today most Griko are Catholics of the Latin Rite.
