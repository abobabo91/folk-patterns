## Overview

The Arvanites are a population group in Greece of Albanian origin. Their traditional settlement areas include Attica, southern Boeotia, the north-east of the Peloponnese, the south of Euboea, the north of Andros, and islands of the Saronic Gulf, including Salamis, Hydra, Poros, Agistri, and Spetses. Other settlements existed in Phthiotis and parts of the Peloponnese, while Albanian settlers also reached Kea, Psara, Aegina, Kythnos, Skopelos, Ios, and Samos. Albanians were first recorded as settlers in southern Greece in the late 13th and early 14th century. Arvanites traditionally spoke Arvanitika, an Albanian variety related primarily to other Tosk varieties, alongside Greek. They are significant in the cultural history of Greece because of their role in the Greek War of Independence, their distinctive language, songs, descent organization, clothing, and changing relationship with Greek national identity.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Chemise shirt** — Arvanite women were known for wearing a chemise shirt that was heavily embroidered.

**Foundi** — The *foundi* was a heavily embroidered gown-like garment, embroidered in silk.

**Sigouni** — On the Greek mainland, women wore the *sigouni*, a thick white woolen coat.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional Arvanite clothing differed according to region and gender. Arvanite men on the Greek mainland wore the *fustanella*, a pleated skirt-like garment or kilt. Men living on some Aegean islands wore baggy breeches associated with seafaring Greeks. Arvanite women wore heavily embroidered chemise shirts and the heavily embroidered *foundi*, a gown-like garment made with silk embroidery. On the mainland they also wore the *sigouni*, a thick white woolen coat. Women on the Aegean islands wore silk gowns with Turkish influences. The sources state that terms for Arvanite female clothing were in Arvanitika rather than Greek.

## Music & performance

Traditional Arvanite folk songs are identified as an important source of information about the social values and ideals of Arvanitic societies. They do state that in Hydra, men spoke both Albanian and Greek, with Albanian used to speak with each other and sing songs at sea. Up to the early 20th century, Albanian in the form of the Arvanitika dialect was the main language of the Greek naval fleet because a high proportion of its sailors came from Albanian-speaking islands of Greece.

The relationship between language and performance is especially visible in the description of Hydra. Men used Albanian among themselves and for singing songs in the sea, while many women spoke only Albanian. Arvanitika was almost exclusively a spoken language and was not used by Arvanites in writing or media. Since the sources give no instrument names or further performance contexts, no fuller musical classification can be made from them.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do describe several historical and communal contexts connected with Arvanite identity. Arvanites participated in the Greek War of Independence, often fighting on the Greek side against the Ottomans, and many became regarded as Greek national heroes. They also participated in the 1821 Tripolitsa massacre. Their common Christian Orthodox religion with the rest of the local population contributed to increasing assimilation. In the 20th century, state policies and social pressure encouraged the abandonment of Arvanitika in favor of Greek, especially during the Greek military junta of 1967–1974.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources identify traditional Arvanite folk songs as valuable evidence for the social values and ideals of Arvanitic societies.

Arvanitic communities preserved genealogical records connected with the *fara*. In an Arvanitic village, each *phara* was responsible for keeping genealogical records, which have been preserved as historical documents in local libraries. The sources describe *fara* as a descent model similar to the Albanian tribal system of *fis*. Arvanites were organized in *phares* mainly during the Ottoman Empire; the *phara* was named after an apical ancestor who was a warlord. Several *phares* could exist in one village, and they were sometimes organized into phratries.

Since the 1980s, organized efforts have sought to preserve Arvanite cultural and linguistic heritage. The largest organization promoting Arvanitika is the “Arvanitic League of Greece.” The sources also describe the propagation of the Pelasgian theory in post-dictatorial Greece as a counter-discourse intended to give Arvanites a positive image in Greek history; modern scholars have rejected that theory and regard it as a myth.

## Language & religion

Arvanitika is a dialect of the Albanian language, sharing similar features primarily with other Tosk varieties. Its name and native equivalents are connected with the ethnonym Arvanites and the toponym Arbëna, which in the Middle Ages referred to a region in what is today Albania. Arvanitika is currently in a state of attrition because of language shift toward Greek, internal migration to cities, intermarriage and intermingling with the wider Greek population, government policies, and public indifference. It has no legal status in Greece and is not available at any level of the educational system.

Arvanites are bilingual, traditionally speaking Arvanitika and Greek. During the 20th century, almost all Arvanites came to self-identify as Greeks and did not consider themselves Albanian. The common Christian Orthodox religion shared with the rest of the local population was one of the main factors in their assimilation.
