## Overview

Anglo-Irish people are an ethnic, social and religious grouping consisting mostly of the descendants and successors of the English Protestant Ascendancy in Ireland. They were predominantly members of the Church of Ireland, although some belonged to English Dissenting Churches such as Baptists, Presbyterians and the Methodist Church, while others were Roman Catholics. The group was chiefly associated with Ireland, including Dublin and the southern three provinces, and with political, commercial and cultural connections across Great Britain and the British Empire. They describe the group as a professional and landed class from the 17th century until Irish independence in the early 20th century, and as a community prominent in administration, military service, literature, science, business and the arts.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources identify the *Big House* as a large country house associated with the Anglo-Irish landed class in Ireland. These houses became symbols of the class’s dominance in Irish society. They also mention Slane Castle as a property associated with the 8th Marquess Conyngham.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do mention businesses associated with the group, including Jacob's Biscuits, Bewley's, Beamish and Crawford, Jameson's Whiskey, W. P. & R. Odlum, Cleeve's, R&H Hall, Maguire & Patterson, Dockrell's, Arnott's, Goulding Chemicals, the Irish Times, the Irish Railways and the Guinness brewery.

## Music & performance

The sources name several Anglo-Irish composers of art music: Michael William Balfe, John Field, George Alexander Osborne, Thomas Roseingrave, Charles Villiers Stanford, John Andrew Stevenson, Robert Prescott Stewart, William Vincent Wallace and Charles Wood.

The sources also identify the Gate Theatre in Dublin as a setting associated with the 6th Earl of Longford, who was an impresario there in the 1950s.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention the Gate Theatre in Dublin through the 6th Earl of Longford, described as an impresario there in the 1950s. They also identify the playwrights Brendan Behan, J. M. Synge, Bernard Shaw, Augusta, Lady Gregory, Samuel Beckett, Giles Cooper and William Trevor among prominent Anglo-Irish writers or associated figures.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe religious and political circumstances affecting the group. The Church of Ireland was the established Church of Ireland until 1871. Under the Penal Laws, in force between the 17th and 19th centuries, Roman Catholic recusants faced restrictions on public office, education, professions and landed property. The sources also describe the group’s political responses to Irish independence and Home Rule.

The Anglo-Irish, as a class, were mostly opposed to Irish independence and Home Rule, and many supported continued political union with Great Britain. During the Irish War of Independence (1919–1921), many Anglo-Irish landlords left Ireland because of arson attacks on family homes. The burnings continued during the Irish Civil War, when sectarian murders were carried out by the Anti-Treaty IRA. The reaction to the Anglo-Irish Treaty was mixed. In December 1921, the Most Rev. John A.F. Gregg, Church of Ireland Archbishop of Dublin, expressed support for offering the Irish Free State loyalty and cooperation.

## Foodways

The sources mention Jacob's Biscuits, Bewley's, Beamish and Crawford, Jameson's Whiskey, the Guinness brewery and other businesses owned or controlled by Anglo-Irish people at the beginning of the 20th century.

The sources also state that Anglo-Irish families participated in English sports, particularly racing and fox hunting, and that many members of the class held land or business interests across the British Isles. These references do not provide evidence for a distinctive Anglo-Irish cuisine.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources identify a substantial Anglo-Irish literary presence. Prominent poets, writers and playwrights include Oscar Wilde, Maria Edgeworth, Jonathan Swift, George Berkeley, Sheridan Le Fanu, Oliver Goldsmith, Laurence Sterne, George Darley, Lucy Knox, Bram Stoker, J. M. Synge, W. B. Yeats, Cecil Day-Lewis, Bernard Shaw, Augusta, Lady Gregory, Samuel Beckett, Giles Cooper, C. S. Lewis, Lord Longford, Elizabeth Bowen, William Trevor and William Allingham. The writer Lafcadio Hearn was of Anglo-Irish descent on his father’s side and was brought up as a Catholic by his great-aunt.

They do record memorable characterizations by Brendan Behan, who defined an Anglo-Irishman as “a Protestant with a horse,” and by Elizabeth Bowen, who described the experience as feeling “English in Ireland, Irish in England.” W. B. Yeats delivered a eulogy for his class in the Irish Senate in 1925, naming Burke, Grattan, Swift, Emmet and Parnell among the group’s political and literary predecessors.

## Language & religion

Hiberno-English, or Irish English, is the set of English dialects native to the island of Ireland. English is the first language in everyday use in both the Republic of Ireland and Northern Ireland, alongside Irish, while Ulster Scots is another local language or dialect in Northern Ireland. Irish English has influences from the Irish language and, in the north, the Scots language.

The sources describe Ulster or Northern Irish English, Western and Southern Irish English, Dublin English and a supraregional standard accent. Dublin English includes local Dublin English and advanced Dublin English. Irish English is characterized in the sources by features including widespread rhoticity, distinctive vowel systems, th-stopping outside Ulster, and vocabulary borrowed from Irish. Examples of official Irish loan words include Taoiseach, Tánaiste, Oireachtas and Dáil Éireann.

The Anglo-Irish were predominantly associated with the Anglican Church of Ireland. Others belonged to Baptists, Presbyterians or the Methodist Church, and some were Roman Catholics. The sources also describe Anglo-Irish families of English, Welsh, Old English, mixed-British and Gaelic Irish descent, including families that conformed to the established Church of Ireland while retaining their lands and privileges.
