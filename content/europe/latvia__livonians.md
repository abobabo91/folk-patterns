## Overview

The Livonians, or Livs, are a Finnic people indigenous to the Livonian Coast in northwestern Latvia and southwestern Estonia. Historically, they lived in two separate areas of Latvia: Livonia and the northern coast of Courland. The Daugava River, called *Vēna* in Livonian, was an important trade route connecting them with Gotlanders, Russians, Finns, Germans, Swedes and Danes. Livonians historically spoke Livonian, a Finnic language closely related to Estonian and Finnish. In 2011, 250 people claimed Livonian ethnicity in Latvia; the modern article states that 176 people identified themselves as Livonian. The group’s folk-culture significance is closely tied to its coastal fishing society, language revival, village history and survival through war, foreign rule, repression and assimilation.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Livonian settlement history is recorded through villages and named community buildings rather than through a documented house type or architectural system. In the Middle Ages, Albert von Buxhövden forced Livonian leaders at the mouth of the Daugava River to give him land for a Christian settlement; building began in 1201, and the city of Riga grew from this settlement. A church was supposedly built in the Livonian village today known as Kolka, and a new Russian Orthodox church, grammar school and navy school were later built in Kolka and Mazirbe. The Livonian Community Centre in Mazirbe, called *Irē* in Livonian, was founded in 1939 with subsidies from the Estonian and Finnish governments, later expropriated during the Soviet period, and eventually returned and transformed into the House of the Livonian People.

## Music & performance

Music formed an important part of the Livonian cultural revival between the two World Wars. A Livonian language choir was founded, and Livonian song festivals were held along the entire Livonian Coast. The Livonian Society, established on April 2, 1923, represented the Livonian people and used the Livonian name *Līvõd Īt*; the sources also give its Latvian name as *Līvu savienība*. In the early 1970s, Livonian singers were allowed to found a choir named *Līvlist*, meaning “The Livonians,” in the western Latvian city of Ventspils.

The sources state that this was the first time in the history of that event that a song with Livonian lyrics was featured.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources record cultural events rather than a complete Livonian annual festival calendar. During the interwar cultural revival, Livonian song festivals were held along the entire Livonian Coast. The Livonian Society was established on April 2, 1923, and the Livonian Community Centre in Mazirbe was founded in 1939 with support from the Estonian and Finnish governments.

The Latvian government created the protected cultural historic territory *Līvõd rānda*, the Livonian Coast, on February 4, 1992. The territory included twelve Livonian villages: Lūžņa, Miķeļtornis, Lielirbe, Jaunciems, Sīkrags, Mazirbe, Košrags, Pitrags, Saunags, Vaide, Kolka and Melnsils. In 2023, the University of Latvia Livonian Institute, the UNESCO Latvian National Commission and the Latvian National Cultural Center proclaimed Livonian Heritage Year, during which various events were held by individuals and institutions.

They do record that Viktors Bertholds was buried on 28 February 2009 in the Livonian village of Kolka in Courland.

## Foodways

They state that Livonians historically supported themselves mainly by fishing, as well as by agriculture and animal husbandry.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record several forms of literary and language revival. In the 1930s, the first Livonian language reader, poetry collections by several Livonian writers and a monthly magazine in Livonian called *Līvli*, “The Livonian,” were published.

In October 2022, Jānis Mednis and Renāte Medne published *Kūldaläpš. Zeltabērns*, “Golden Child,” a book in Livonian and Latvian for children and parents. The sources state that subsequent books and an audio version were planned. The Livonian language is also being revived through education, research and public cultural activity.

## Language & religion

Livonian is a Finnic language closely related to Estonian and Finnish. The sources give the Livonian names *rāndalist*, “coast dwellers,” and *kalāmīed*, “fishermen,” as earlier self-descriptions; from the 1920s and 1930s, Livonians increasingly called themselves *līvõd*, *līvnikad* or *līvlist*. The language has eastern and western contexts in the historical account, and the last known speaker of the eastern Livonian dialect died in 1864. In 2020, Kuldi Medne was reported as the only living person who spoke Livonian as a first language; as of 2011, approximately 210 people had some knowledge of the language at an A1 or A2 level.

Christianization began during the Middle Ages, when missionaries from Western Europe sought to convert pagan Livonians. In the early 20th century, many local Livs converted to the Russian Orthodox faith.
