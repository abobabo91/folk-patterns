## Overview

Gottscheers are German settlers of the Kočevje region, also known as Gottschee, in Slovenia. Until the Second World War, their main language of communication was Gottscheerish, a Bavarian dialect of German. The settlement developed after Counts of Ortenburg brought German peasants from East Tyrol and Carinthia into their fiefdom between 1330 and circa 1400. Gottscheer communities remained isolated subsistence farmers, supplemented by peddling throughout the Holy Roman and later Austro-Hungarian and German Empires. Their population reached around 26,000 between 1869 and 1878, but emigration, discrimination, wartime resettlement, and displacement reduced the community. Today, most Gottscheers and their descendants live in the United States, mainly in New York City and Cleveland, with smaller numbers in Canada and Europe.

## Festivals & rituals

They record a 600-year celebration of Gottschee held in 1930. The sources also describe the community’s wartime resettlement: after Adolf Hitler and Benito Mussolini reached a resettlement treaty on October 1, 1941, Gottscheers were moved from the Italian-annexed territory to the Rann Triangle, while Slovenes from that region were deported to eastern Germany for potential Germanisation or forced labor. The sources describe this as a political and wartime process rather than a ritual tradition.

## Language & religion

Gottscheerish was the main language of communication among Gottscheers until the Second World War and is described as a Bavarian dialect of German. The community is also associated with German and Slovene names: Gottscheer is German, while Kočevarji and kočevski Nemci are Slovene. The sources describe severe pressure on German-language education and public life in the Kingdom of Yugoslavia. On January 1, 1919, German teachers and civil servants were dismissed; the Gymnasium of Gottschee became a Slovene-language institution, and the town’s trade school was shut down. By 1939, only five German classes were offered in the region’s elementary schools. The sources mention a Roman Catholic priest among notable Gottscheers.
