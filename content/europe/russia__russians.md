## Overview

Russians (Russian: *русские*, romanized: *russkiye*) are an East Slavic ethnic group native to Eastern Europe. Their mother tongue is Russian, the most spoken Slavic language. Most Russians adhere to Orthodox Christianity, a pattern established since the Middle Ages. Russians live mainly in Russia, while notable minorities live in Belarus, Kazakhstan, Moldova, Ukraine, the Baltic states, Germany, Poland, China, Japan, South Korea, Mexico, Brazil, Argentina, Australia, Canada, and the United States. A Russian diaspora has been estimated at 25 million people. Russian cultural ancestry is based in Kievan Rus', and Russian culture has developed through literature, painting, music, cinema, science, and historical communities such as the Cossacks, Pomors, Lipovans, and Old Believers.

## Music & performance

Until the 18th century, music in Russia consisted mainly of church music and folk songs and dances. In the 19th century, musical life was shaped by the tension between the classical composer Mikhail Glinka and members of The Mighty Handful on one side, and the Russian Musical Society, led by Anton and Nikolay Rubinstein, on the other. Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky continued the Romantic tradition into the 20th century, followed by Sergei Rachmaninoff.

The sources name Alexander Scriabin, Alexander Glazunov, Igor Stravinsky, Sergei Prokofiev, Dmitri Shostakovich, Georgy Sviridov, and Alfred Schnittke among the world-renowned composers of the 20th century. Soviet and Russian conservatories produced internationally known performers, including violinists David Oistrakh and Gidon Kremer, cellist Mstislav Rostropovich, pianists Vladimir Horowitz, Sviatoslav Richter, and Emil Gilels, and vocalist Galina Vishnevskaya.

Soviet popular music included the balladeers Vladimir Vysotsky and Bulat Okudzhava and the performer Alla Pugacheva. Jazz flourished despite sanctions from Soviet authorities, and the Ganelin Trio was described by critics as the greatest ensemble of free-jazz in continental Europe. Rock music became popular across Russia by the 1980s, with bands such as Aria, Aquarium, DDT, and Kino. Pop music continued to flourish from the 1960s, including t.A.T.u. and, more recently, Little Big.

## Oral tradition & literature

Russian literature can be traced to the Middle Ages, when epics and chronicles in vernacular Old East Slavic were composed. During the Age of Enlightenment, literature included works by Mikhail Lomonosov, Denis Fonvizin, Gavrila Derzhavin, and Nikolay Karamzin. From the early 1830s, Russian poetry, prose, and drama underwent what the source calls the Golden Age of Russian Poetry. Vasily Zhukovsky and Alexander Pushkin came to the fore, followed by Mikhail Lermontov, Nikolay Nekrasov, Aleksey Konstantinovich Tolstoy, Fyodor Tyutchev, and Afanasy Fet.

Nikolai Gogol is described as the first great Russian novelist. Ivan Turgenev, Fyodor Dostoevsky, Leo Tolstoy, and Ivan Goncharov became major novelists, while Mikhail Saltykov-Shchedrin wrote prose satire and Nikolai Leskov was known for shorter fiction. Anton Chekhov excelled in short stories and became a leading dramatist. Other 19th-century writers included Ivan Krylov, Vissarion Belinsky, Aleksandr Griboyedov, and Aleksandr Ostrovsky.

The beginning of the 20th century brought the Silver Age of Russian Poetry, with poets including Alexander Blok, Anna Akhmatova, Boris Pasternak, Konstantin Balmont, Marina Tsvetaeva, Vladimir Mayakovsky, and Osip Mandelstam. After the Russian Revolution of 1917, Russian literature divided into Soviet and white émigré parts. Socialist realism became predominant in the 1930s, with Maxim Gorky as a leading figure. Mikhail Bulgakov, Vladimir Nabokov, Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, Victor Pelevin, and Vladimir Sorokin are also identified as important writers.

## Language & religion

Russian is an East Slavic language and the official and predominantly spoken language in Russia. It is the most-spoken native language in Europe, the most geographically widespread language of Eurasia, and the world's most widely spoken Slavic language. Russian is also one of the six official languages of the United Nations and one of two official languages aboard the International Space Station.

The majority of Russians adhere to Orthodox Christianity. The state of Kievan Rus' adopted Christianity from the Byzantine Empire in 988. The sources also identify groups of Old Believers, including Kamenschiks, Lipovans, and Semeiskie. The Russian language distinguishes *русские* (*russkiye*), generally meaning ethnic Russians, from *россияне* (*rossiyane*), meaning people of Russia regardless of ethnicity or religious affiliation.
