## Overview

Icelanders (Icelandic: *Íslendingar*) are an ethnic group and nation native to the island country of Iceland. They speak Icelandic, a North Germanic language. Iceland was first permanently settled around 874, and the Alþingi was established at Þingvellir in 930. Icelanders’ folk-culture significance in the sources lies especially in their sagas, poetry, religious traditions, cuisine, epic singing, and musical life. Their historical background includes settlement by people primarily of Norwegian, Irish, and Gaelic Scottish origin, followed by long political connections with Norway and Denmark and the establishment of the Republic of Iceland on 17 June 1944.

## Music & performance

The earliest indigenous Icelandic music named in the sources is *rímur*, described as epic tales from the Viking era often performed a cappella. Christianity influenced the development of Icelandic music, and many hymns were written in the local idiom. Hallgrímur Pétursson, identified as a poet and priest, wrote many such hymns in the 17th century.

Iceland’s relative isolation helped its music maintain a regional flavor. The first pipe organs, which were prevalent in European religious music, appeared on the island in the 19th century. The sources describe Icelandic music as containing vibrant folk and pop traditions and name Voces Thules, The Sugarcubes, Björk, Sigur Rós, and Of Monsters and Men among Icelandic groups and singers.

The national anthem is “Ó Guð vors lands,” translated in the source as “Our Country’s God.” Matthías Jochumsson wrote the text and Sveinbjörn Sveinbjörnsson composed the music. The song was written in 1874, when Iceland celebrated its one thousandth anniversary of settlement, and was originally published under the title *A Hymn in Commemoration of Iceland’s Thousand Years*.

## Dance & theatre

They describe *rímur* as epic tales often performed a cappella but do not classify them as dance or theatre.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe Iceland’s adoption of Christianity in approximately 1000 in an event called the *kristnitaka*. Lutheranism is identified as the predominant religion, and the Lutheran church is said to claim approximately 84% of the total population. Iceland is described as mostly secular in observance but predominantly Christian culturally.

The sources also identify 17 June 1944 as the date on which the monarchy was abolished and a republic was established. This occurred on Jón Sigurðsson’s 133rd birthday. Iceland’s first national assembly, the Alþingi, was established at Þingvellir in 930, discontinued in 1798–99, and restored in 1844.

They mention Pietism, a religious movement imported from Denmark in the 18th century, and the legally recognized revival of the pre-Christian Nordic religion called Ásatrúarfélag.

## Foodways

Icelandic cuisine is described as consisting mainly of fish, lamb, and dairy. Fish was once the principal part of an Icelander’s diet but later gave way to beef, pork, and poultry.

Traditional foods are collectively called *Þorramatur*. The named foods include smoked and salted lamb, singed sheep heads, dried fish, smoked and pickled salmon, and cured shark. Fermented shark fin is also identified as a form of *Þorramatur*.

## Oral tradition & literature

Icelandic literature includes Eddic poetry, skaldic poetry, and saga literature. Eddic poetry consists of heroic and mythological poems. Poetry praising someone is described as skaldic poetry or court poetry. Saga literature is prose ranging from pure fiction to fairly factual history.

The elder or Poetic Edda, the younger or Prose Edda, and the sagas are identified as major pieces of Icelandic literature. The Poetic Edda is described as a collection of poems and stories from the late 10th century, while the Prose Edda is a manual of poetry containing many stories of Norse mythology. The sagas tell of events in Iceland in the 10th and early 11th centuries and are described as the best-known pieces of Icelandic literature.

Written Icelandic has changed little since the 13th century, allowing modern readers to understand the sagas.

## Language & religion

Icelandic is the official language of Iceland in practice and belongs to the North Germanic branch. Its inflectional grammar is compared with Latin, Ancient Greek, Old English, and Old Norse.

Iceland embraced Christianity in approximately 1000 through the *kristnitaka*. Lutheranism is predominant, while the country is described as mostly secular in observance but predominantly Christian culturally. Small Protestant Free Churches and Catholic communities are present, alongside a Muslim community composed of immigrants and local converts. The sources also mention Ásatrúarfélag, a legally recognized revival of the pre-Christian Nordic religion of the original settlers.
