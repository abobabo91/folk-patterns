## Overview

The Yenish (German: Jenische; French: Yéniche, Taïtch; Luxembourgish: Jënesch) are an itinerant group in Western and Central Europe, living mostly in Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Luxembourg, Belgium, and parts of France, roughly centered on the Rhineland. Their origins are unknown. One historical theory connects them with marginalised and vagrant poor classes of the early modern period; other accounts describe the incorporation of members of itinerant communities, including Romani and Jewish individuals. The group emerged as distinct by the early 19th century, while most Yenish became sedentary between the mid-19th and 20th centuries. Their folk-cultural significance in the sources lies especially in mobility, occupational traditions, language, persecution, and the preservation of Yenish identity.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources describe Yenish settlement and mobility. Yenish people who did not become sedentary by the late 19th century took to living in trailers, while recently established settlements of Yenish, Sinti, and Roma in Germany were called “gypsy colonies” (*Zigeunerkolonien*).

## Music & performance

They identify occupations associated with earlier Yenish mobility, including showpeople, tinkers, and door-to-door salesmen.

The sources mention several Yenish people involved in music and performance. Stephan Eicher is identified as a Swiss musician who is Yenish on his father’s side. Oliver Kayser is described as a Luxembourgish musician and variété performer. The film and television list includes *Es geschah am hellichten Tag* from 1957, *Das gefrorene Herz* from 1979, *Kinder der Landstrasse* from 1992, *Hunkeler macht Sachen* from 2008, *Fog in August* from 2016, *Dove cadono le ombre* from 2017, and *Ruäch — eine Reise ins jenische Europa* and *Lubo* from 2023.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document historical and political events affecting Yenish families. In Switzerland, the program *Kinder der Landstrasse* separated children from their parents from the 1920s until the 1970s; the program was discontinued in 1973 after public criticism in 1972. The Swiss government formally acknowledged in February 2025 that forced removals and assimilation efforts targeting Yenish, Manouche, and Sinti people under the program constitute a crime against humanity under international law.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do, however, describe the Yenish language as containing unusual metaphors and metonyms that replace standard German words. The sources also identify literary and documentary work concerning Yenish experience. Mariella Mehr is described as having documented the plight she suffered under the *Kinder der Landstrasse* project in the 1970s, contributing to its discontinuation. The film *Fog in August* is based on a 2008 book of the same name.

## Language & religion

Yenish is a variety of German spoken by the Yenish people, formerly nomadic populations living mostly in Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Alsace, Luxembourg, and other parts of France. It has been documented since the 18th century. Speakers generally use their local German dialect enriched by Yenish vocabulary, which derives in part from Rotwelsch and includes influences from Yiddish, Romani, Hebrew, French, and other minority languages of the region. Yenish grammar is borrowed from German, while individual varieties can have distinct names, including *Masematte*, *Lepper Talp*, and *Heenese Vlek*. The sources give examples of Hebrew-origin words such as *laf*, meaning “no,” and *Schuck*, meaning “market.” In 1997, Switzerland gave Yenish the status of a “territorial non-tied language” under the European language charter.
