## Overview

Cham Albanians, or Chams, are a subgroup of Albanians associated historically with Chameria, a region along the Ionian coast from Konispol to the Acheron valley. Chameria included parts of southern Albania and the Greek regional units of Thesprotia and Preveza, within the larger region of Epirus. The region contains the Thyamis and Acheron rivers, mountainous terrain, valley farmlands, and hilly areas. Cham communities now mostly exist in Albania, the United States, and Turkey, following the expulsion of the Muslim Cham population from Greece after World War II; a minority remains in the region. A 2007 estimate placed the Cham diaspora at 400,000. Chams speak Cham Albanian, a Southern Tosk Albanian dialect, and have a distinct cultural identity within Albanian society.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do record religious and social distinctions that affected communal identity. Under Ottoman rule, Muslim and Orthodox communities were generally classified as “Turks” and “Greeks” respectively, regardless of ethnic origin, while religion defined social classification more strongly than ethnicity. Muslim Chams were especially associated with the Bektashi order after the 18th century, while Orthodox Chams lived in areas including Fanari, Louros, and Thesprotiko. These classifications concern religion and administration rather than documented dress.

### Architecture

They do identify the main settlements historically associated with Chams: Paramythia, Filiates, Igoumenitsa, Parapotamos, Syvota, Sagiada, Perdika, and Margariti. Preveza and Ioannina also had significant Cham Albanian communities. Orthodox Chams originally resided in Fanari, Louros, and Thesprotiko. After the expulsion from Greece, many Muslim Chams settled in the outskirts of Vlorë, Durrës, and Tirana, while others moved into existing coastal villages such as Borshi or established villages such as Vrina.

## Music & performance

They do, however, describe cultural organizations and the preservation of language and traditions. During the Albanian National Awakening, several Chams led cultural clubs and patriotic organizations connected with the establishment of an independent Albanian state. Thoma Çami was a founder and first chairman of Bashkimi, described as the best-known cultural club of the National Renaissance. He also wrote the first scholarly history book for Albanian schools.

The sources characterize Cham identity as a mixture of Albanian and Greek influences together with specifically Cham elements. In Albania, the Cham dialect and other traditions have been preserved. In Greece, the sources state that linguistic rights and Orthodox Cham heritage were suppressed in public space and subjected to assimilation policies, with Albanian use relegated to the household. These statements provide evidence for cultural preservation and restriction.

## Dance & theatre

They mention Osman Taka as a distinguished Cham personality of the Albanian National Renaissance, but the supplied material does not describe a dance or performance associated with him.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe religious change and communal organization. Chams were still predominantly Christian in the region at the end of the 16th century, while by the end of the 17th century urban centers had largely adopted Islam. The process of Islamization began in the 16th century and reached major proportions in the 18th and 19th centuries. Muslim Chams were mostly followers of the Bektashi order, especially after the 18th century.

The sources also record political and historical gatherings rather than ritual festivals. A meeting was organized in Preveza in January 1879, and a petition was signed there on 28 February 1879. Chams had delegates at the Vlora Congress of 1912, when Albanian Independence was proclaimed. Four representatives from Chameria and two from Ioannina took part. These were political events, not religious or seasonal festivals.

## Foodways

They do record religious divisions that shaped social classification: Islam became widespread among Chams under Ottoman rule, while Orthodox communities remained in southern areas, including the area south of the Acheron and down to Preveza.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record literary and educational activity during the Albanian National Awakening. In 1870, the despot of Paramythia, Grygorios, translated the New Testament into Albanian because his followers could not understand Greek well. Thoma Çami wrote the first scholarly history book for Albanian schools, although he died before the declaration of independence.

The sources identify the Cham Albanian dialect as a Southern Tosk Albanian dialect and state that it has been preserved in Albania together with other traditions. They also describe the restriction of Albanian in Greece, where its use was relegated to private household space. Since the fall of Communism in Albania, Chams have campaigned for the right of return to Greece and the restoration of confiscated properties.

## Language & religion

Cham Albanian is a dialect of the Albanian language and is described as a Southern Tosk Albanian dialect. It is one of the two most conservative Southern Tosk dialects identified in the source, the other being Arvanitika. The Albanian ethnic endonym shqiptar became prevalent after the 18th century and was also used by Christian Albanian-speakers in the region; in the present context described by the source, it is used mostly to distinguish them from Greeks and Vlachs.

Religiously, Chams include Muslim and Orthodox communities. Islamization began in the 16th century, while major changes occurred in the 18th and 19th centuries. Muslim Chams were mostly followers of the Bektashi order, particularly after the 18th century. In northern Chameria, the vast majority became Muslims, while south of the Acheron and down to Preveza Albanians remained Orthodox. Under Ottoman administration, religion rather than ethnicity generally determined communal classification. The sources state that Orthodox Cham communities remaining in Epirus are now assimilated and identify entirely with the Greek nation, while other accounts include Orthodox people among Cham Albanians.
