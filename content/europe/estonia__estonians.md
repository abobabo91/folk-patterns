## Overview

Estonians, also called Estonian people, are a Finnic ethnic group native to the Baltic Sea region in Northern Europe, primarily Estonia. Approximately 1.1 million ethnic Estonians live worldwide, with the majority residing in Estonia. Estonian is related to Finnish, Karelian and Livonian, and South Estonian is often considered a separate language. Estonians can also be classified according to dialects, including Võros and Setos, although internal migration and extensive urbanisation have made these divisions less pronounced. Estonian communities also formed in Finland, the United States, Sweden, Canada and the United Kingdom. Their history includes Baltic Finnic origins, Scandinavian and German rule and settlement, an Estonian national awakening, emigration during World War II and renewed independence in 1991.

## Festivals & rituals

 The sources do record the Estonian national awakening during the 19th century and the first Estonian World Festival, held in Toronto in 1972. They also record World War II, the Soviet occupation of Estonia in 1944 and the nation’s regained independence in 1991, without providing associated folk rituals.

## Oral tradition & literature

The oldest known endonym of the Estonians is *maarahvas*, meaning “land people” or “country folk”; it was used until the mid-19th century and was gradually replaced by *Eesti rahvas*, meaning “Estonian people”. The sources state that the Wanradt–Koell Catechism, the oldest known book in Estonian, was printed in 1535, while the oldest known examples of handwritten Estonian appear in 13th-century chronicles. Bible translations into Estonian appeared in 1739. The number of books and brochures published in Estonian increased from 18 in the 1750s to 54 in the 1790s, and by 1800 more than half of adult Estonians could read.

## Language & religion

Estonians primarily speak Estonian, a Finnic language related to Finnish, Karelian and Livonian. Estonians can be divided into dialect groups such as Võros and Setos; South Estonian is also often considered a separate language. Historically, the area was connected with Baltic Finnic peoples, and Estonians identify as part of the Finno-Ugric peoples while also having a Baltic identity. Their oldest known endonym was *maarahvas*, later replaced by *Eesti rahvas*. The sources state that Estonians have strong ties to the Nordic countries because of cultural and religious influences during Scandinavian and German rule and settlement.
