## Overview

Galicians are an ethnic group primarily residing in Galicia, in the northwest Iberian Peninsula. Historical emigration resulted in Galician populations in other parts of Spain, Europe, and the Americas. They possess distinct customs, culture, language, music, dance, sports, art, cuisine, and mythology. Galician is a Romance language derived from the Latin of ancient Roman Gallaecia and is a primary cultural expression. It shares a common origin with Portuguese, and Galicians are closely related to the Portuguese people. Galicia also maintains literary and oral traditions of songs, tales, and sayings.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Galicia contains archaeological building traditions associated with the Neolithic, Bronze Age, and Iron Age. Neolithic structures included *menhirs*, *barrows*, and *cromlechs*. During the Late Bronze Age, ceremonial henge-like ring structures approximately 50 metres in diameter were built throughout Galicia. From the Bronze Age–Iron Age transition, communities constructed fortified hill-forts, including Chandebrito in Nigrán, Penas do Castelo in A Pobra do Brollón, and O Cociñadoiro in Arteixo. These settlements used walls, ditches, ramparts, towers, and heavily fortified gates. Houses were first built with perishable materials and later with stone walls, sometimes reaching two storeys. Longhouses of north European tradition were later replaced in much of Galicia by roundhouses.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources describe a material culture involving copper metallurgy, bronze weaponry, bronze axes, cauldrons, sacrificial hatchets, gold torcs, and stone carving. Hill-fort communities produced or controlled metalwork connected with local gold, tin, and Atlantic commerce. Other documented objects include ear pendants, round hand mills, petroglyphs, warrior sculptures, carved stones, and stone heads that may have been placed near fort gates.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention gold torcs, of which more than a hundred exemplars are known, as well as bronze ear pendants.

## Music & performance

Galician culture includes music and an oral tradition of songs, tales, and sayings. In an account attributed to Silus Italicus, Galicians are described as chanting rude songs in their fatherland’s tongues. The same account describes men making the ground tremble with alternating foot movements while clashing their *caetra*.

## Dance & theatre

The sources describe an early performance practice in which men made the ground tremble with alternating foot movements while clashing their *caetra*. The sources used do not distinguish ceremonial dance from entertainment.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document ritual and ceremonial features of earlier archaeological cultures. Bronze cauldrons, richly figured sacrificial hatchets, and gold torcs had ritual use and great value. Saunas of probable ritual use were known in hill-forts. In the Castro Culture, urns with ashes were exceptionally buried at foundational sites, probably acting as protectors. The sources also describe the deposition or hoarding of prestige items, frequently in aquatic contexts, during the Late Bronze Age.

## Foodways

They state that agriculture and husbandry arrived with a new population approximately 6,500 years ago and that woodland was later cleared for pasture and farmland. In the account attributed to Silus Italicus, women are described as adding seed to the furrow and working the ground with the plough, while other work was associated with women except for hard war.

## Oral tradition & literature

Galicia maintains a significant literary and oral tradition of songs, tales, and sayings. These traditions contributed to the spread and development of the Galician language and share commonalities with Portugal. Medieval or Old Galician, also known linguistically as Galician-Portuguese, developed a notable literary tradition from the late 12th century.

## Language & religion

Galician is a Romance language belonging to the Western Ibero-Romance branch and deriving from Latin. It holds official status in Galicia and is also spoken in bordering areas of Asturias and Castile and León. Medieval Galician-Portuguese evolved in the northwest Iberian Peninsula from Vulgar Latin and became the written and spoken language of medieval Galicia and Portugal. From the 15th century, it diverged into Galician and Portuguese. Galician developed primarily as a regional spoken language influenced by Castilian Spanish, while Portuguese became an international language.
