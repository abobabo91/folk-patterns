## Overview

The Crimean Goths were a Germanic-speaking people who lived around the Black Sea, especially in Crimea, from about the 3rd to the 18th centuries. Their precise origins and the date at which they ceased to exist as a distinct culture remain uncertain. They may have been connected with the Greuthungi or with peoples speaking West Germanic languages. Their former capital was Doros, present-day Mangup. The Principality of Gothia, or Theodoro, developed around this stronghold and included Crimean Goths among a population that also comprised Greeks, Alans, Bulgars, Kipchaks and other groups. Crimean Gothic is poorly attested, but the people are important to the study of historical language contact, Christian communities and the archaeology of Crimea.

## Music & performance

The sources mention a song reported in the late 16th century. Joachimus Cureus wrote that, during a voyage in the Black Sea, he encountered a man singing a song in which he used “German words”; the man reportedly said that his home was nearby and that his people were Goths.

A three-line song is among the very limited surviving evidence for the language of the Crimean Goths, but it has never been conclusively translated. The sources also report that the Crimean Goths normally used Greek, Tatar or Hungarian when dealing with outsiders. No further Crimean Gothic musical genres or performance traditions are documented.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record that the Crimean Goths followed Orthodox Christianity in the Principality of Gothia or Theodoro and that earlier Gothic peoples practiced forms of Gothic paganism before being nominally Christianised from the 4th to 6th centuries AD.

The Vita of Saint Cyril, Apostle to the Slavs, reports that “Goths” in Crimea read and praised the Christian God “in their own language.” Joseph Justus Scaliger later claimed that the Goths of Crimea read both the Old and New Testaments in the letters of Wulfila’s alphabet. These are the only reports identified by the source as referring to a written form of Crimean Gothic.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources provide several reports about Crimean Gothic speech but do not preserve a documented body of folktales, epic poetry, proverbs or riddles. Cureus reported a man singing a song containing “German words.” Ogier Ghiselin de Busbecq recorded a Gothic-Latin dictionary containing about a hundred Germanic words, some sharing traits with the ancient Gothic language. Engelbert Kaempfer also described German words spoken in the Crimea and referred to Busbecq’s collection.

Only 101 certain independent forms of Crimean Gothic survive, few of them phrases, together with a three-line song that has never been conclusively translated. In 2015, five Gothic graffiti inscriptions were found on stone plates excavated in Mangup in 1938; they were deciphered by Andrey Vinogradov and Maksim Korobov, although later Greek graffiti made their reading difficult.

## Language & religion

Crimean Gothic was a Germanic language, but its exact relationship to other Germanic languages is disputed in the source. Some scholars connect the Crimean Goths with the Ostrogoths, while others propose the incorporation of peoples speaking West Germanic or North Germanic languages. Busbecq stated that Crimean Gothic did not strongly resemble the well-attested Gothic language. The language is poorly attested, with 101 certain independent forms, few phrases and a three-line song.

The Principality of Gothia or Theodoro followed Orthodox Christianity, and many Crimean Goths spoke Greek. The first report of the Crimean Goths appears in the Vita of Saint Cyril, which describes “Goths” praising the Christian God in their own language. The sources also mention the letters of Wulfila’s alphabet and earlier forms of Gothic paganism. The last known record comes from Stanisław Bohusz Siestrzeńcewicz, who visited Crimea at the end of the 18th century and described people whose language and customs differed greatly from those of their neighbours.
