## Overview

The Mari are a Finno-Ugric people of Eastern Europe who have traditionally lived along the Volga and Kama rivers in Russia. They live mainly in the Mari El republic, with significant minorities in Bashkortostan, Perm Krai, Tatarstan and Udmurtia. Mari make up 40.1% of the population of Mari El. Their two principal ethnographic subgroups are Meadow Mari, living on the right bank of the Volga, and Hill or Mountain Mari, living on the left bank; Eastern Mari form a third subgroup associated especially with Bashkortostan, Sverdlovsk Oblast and Perm Krai. The Mari language belongs to the Uralic family and has Hill Mari, Meadow-Eastern Mari, Eastern Mari and Northwestern Mari varieties. Mari folk culture is especially documented through its mythology, indigenous religion, songs, instruments and relationship with nature.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources describe the *kudo* as a small wooden building used for domestic tasks and, historically, for family ritual. Its use has become rare more recently.

## Music & performance

Mari musical traditions include a many-stringed zither called the *kusle*, also called *karsh*, and a bagpipe called the *shyuvr* or *shuvyr*. Other instruments named in the sources are the *tumyr*, a drum; a birch-bark horn; and accordions. Mari music is generally pentatonic. Mari songs are usually short and lyrical, with recurring themes including the Volga river and love of nature, especially forests and rainbows.

The sources connect music with the wider Mari relationship to the natural world. Mari culture is described as having musical lyrics that mention the Volga and express Mari adoration of forests and rainbows.

## Festivals & rituals

Mari indigenous religion connects people closely with nature, which is regarded as sacred, powerful and living. Mari rituals take place either in sacred groves or in the home. During periods of persecution, worshippers prayed in groves at night or moved worship into their homes to avoid authorities. Private family worship is led by an elder; if no elder is present, a *kart* is invited to conduct the ritual.

Historically, family ritual took place in a *kudo*, a small wooden building also used for domestic tasks. In autumn, poultry, mainly geese, are sacrificed as thanksgiving for a good harvest and as a petition for a good winter. The goose is killed and eaten by close family members, while all remaining parts are burnt.

The sources describe the reformist movement *Kugu-sorta*, meaning “Great Candle,” which began in the 1870s and gained influence into the early 20th century. Public prayers took place regularly until 1887. In the 1980s and 1990s, Mari traditional religion experienced another revival; in 1991 the Center for Mari Traditional Belief, also called *Oshmarii-Chimarii*, was registered and the first worship centre was established in Moscow. Public rituals that had ceased under Soviet leadership began again.

## Oral tradition & literature

Mari mythology is a collection of myths belonging to Mari folk heritage and shares features with the mythologies of neighbouring Komis, Udmurts and Mordvins, as well as with other Finno-Ugric peoples. The principal literary work named in the sources is the Mari epic *Jugorno*, written in Russian by Anatoli Spiridonov in 2002. A Mari translation by Anatoli Mokejev was published alongside the original, and in 2015 the epic was translated into Estonian by Arvo Valton.

Mari creation mythology begins with an eternal ocean in which a duck searches for land on which to lay eggs. The first egg produces Jum, probably an initial form of the sky god Kugu Jumo, while the second produces Jõn, probably an initial form of the god of death Kijamat; Targõltõš later emerges from the third egg. The sky was believed to revolve around the Northern Star, supported by the world pillar Kava Menge. The end of the world lies in the north beyond the rivers Tupsa, Perža and Voja.

Named mythological figures include Oš Kugu Jumo, the “Great White God”; Onar, the mythological giant and ancestor of the Maris; Mlandava, the earth mother; Čodõra-kuva, the forestmother and goddess of hunting; Kijamat, god of the dead and underworld; and Küdõrčö, god of thunder. Mari folklore also mentions Chotkar and the semi-legendary king Chumblat. The sources describe contemporary revival efforts involving Mari language, literature and traditional religion.

## Language & religion

Mari belongs to the Uralic language family and is spoken primarily in Mari El, along the Vyatka river basin and eastwards to the Urals, with speakers also found in Tatarstan, Bashkortostan, Udmurtia and Perm regions. Its principal varieties are Hill Mari, Northwestern Mari, Meadow Mari and Eastern Mari. Mari uses modified Cyrillic script. Hill Mari and Meadow Mari have literary written standards, while both language forms have official status in Mari El alongside Russian. Mari is agglutinative, lacks grammatical gender and does not use articles.

Mari religion is an ethnic religion also called Mari paganism. Its traditional belief is closely connected with nature, and its supreme deity is Kugu Yumo, also called Osh Poro Kugu Yumo, the creator of the laws of the universe and protector of humanity. Other figures include Keremet, associated with earth and water; Shochyn-Ava, goddess of birth; Sukso, defender of faith and protector of humans; and Piyambar, associated with divination and predestination. Mari paganism includes Chimari, Marla vera and Kugu sorta: respectively unbaptised Mari who generally reject Christianity, baptized Mari who combine ethnic and Christian practices, and neopagan revivalists. Russian Orthodoxy and Islam are also recognised as traditional religions in Mari El.
