## Overview

The Bessarabia Germans (German: *Bessarabiendeutsche*; Romanian: *Germani basarabeni*; Ukrainian: *Бессарабські німці*, romanized as *Bessarabs'ki nimtsi*) were a German ethnic group who lived in Bessarabia between 1814 and 1940. Bessarabia is today part of the Republic of Moldova and south-western Ukraine. They migrated to the Russian governorate of Bessarabia from German areas including Baden, Württemberg, Bavaria, some Prussian areas of modern-day Poland, and Alsace, France. Their history in the region lasted 125 years, and they were overwhelmingly rural. Before their remigration to Greater Germany, they numbered 93,000 people and formed about 3% of the population. Their settlements, churches, schools, farms, agricultural practices, and German-language religious life are the principal folk-culture subjects covered by the supplied source.

### Architecture

Bessarabia German settlement architecture was shaped by planned rural colonies. The settlers were placed on treeless steppe surfaces in Southern Bessarabia, also called Budjak or *Budschak*. The settlements were usually laid out in valleys with gently sloping hills. Farms could be up to 50 m wide and were bordered by acacias; properties were about 20 metres wide at the roadside and extended up to 250 metres in depth.

The elongated, single-storey houses stood with their gables facing the road. Their whitewashed walls were built of loam bricks or natural stone. A farmyard included stables, threshing-rooms, a stockroom, and a wine cellar. Fruit and vegetable gardens and vineyards occupied the rear part of an estate. In the earliest period, dwellings were usually earth houses with reed roofs. In newly founded villages, places of worship were among the first communal facilities. Larger municipalities had churches for congregations of up to 1,000 people, while smaller ones had prayer houses that also contained the sexton’s accommodation and the village school.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record religious institutions and practices as central to communal life. Many settlers had emigrated for religious reasons, and newly founded villages treated places of worship as their first communal facilities.

The majority of approximately 150 German settlements were organized into 13 *Kirchspielen* (parishes) and three *Pfarrgemeinden* of Lutheran denomination. There was also a Reformed parish in Schabo and a Roman Catholic church district with four municipalities: Balmas, Emmental, Krasna, and Larga. These belonged to the diocese Cherson, created on July 3, 1848; the diocese was soon renamed Tiraspol. Its seat moved to Saratov and later to Odessa.

They do record the maintenance of churches, schools, sextons, and teachers by the colonists, and they state that the German language was preserved in Bibles and hymn books.

## Foodways

The Bessarabia Germans lived in the Bessarabian black earth belt, whose soil was considered among the best farming land in Europe. Each German family received 60 *desyatinas*, described as about 65 hectares, from the state. Because of the soil’s fertility, fertilisation was not needed. The principal crops were wheat and corn.

Viticulture was conducted widely in some colonies, while most farms produced only enough wine for their own needs. Animal husbandry was limited because manure was unnecessary in the fertile soil. Dung was usually dried and used as winter fuel. Shepherding was more widespread, particularly the raising of fine-wooled Karakul sheep. Poultry farming for self-sufficiency was customary on each individual farm.

Their foodways are documented here only through farming, wine production, poultry farming, shepherding, and the use of agricultural products within rural households.

## Language & religion

The Bessarabia Germans were a German ethnic group, and German was their colloquial language. Russian was the official language in the settlements. Their ancestors had often left their German homeland for religious reasons, and church and religion shaped communal life. German continued to be used in Bibles and hymn books.

Most of the approximately 150 German settlements belonged to 13 Lutheran *Kirchspielen* and three Lutheran *Pfarrgemeinden*. A Reformed parish existed in Schabo, and a Roman Catholic church district included Balmas, Emmental, Krasna, and Larga. The Roman Catholic communities belonged to the diocese Cherson, later called Tiraspol.
