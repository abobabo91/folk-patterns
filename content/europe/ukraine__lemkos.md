## Overview

Lemkos are an ethnic group associated with the Lemko Region, an ethnographic region in the Carpathian Mountains and foothills spanning Ukraine, Slovakia, and Poland. They are often considered a subgroup of Rusyns, although Ukraine categorizes them as an ethnic subgroup of Ukrainians rather than a separate ethnicity. Their homeland historically extended from the Poprad River to the valley of the Oslawa River, primarily in present-day Poland. The Lemko language is variously described as a language in its own right, a dialect of Ukrainian, or a dialect of Rusyn. Estimates in the source vary by place and date: the Polish section of Lemkivshchyna had around 10,000–15,000 Lemkos at the time described, while around 50,000 lived in western and northern Poland. Lemko culture is especially documented through its language, Byzantine Christianity, wooden churches, displacement history, and cultural revival efforts.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Lemko wooden churches have a distinctive architectural arrangement: the highest cupola is placed at the entrance, with the roof sloping downward toward the sanctuary. This contrasts with the wooden church style associated in the source with the Boykos, who place the highest cupola in the middle. Both styles have three cupolas and numerous eaves.

## Music & performance

They mention festivals of Lemko culture in Zdynia, Ukraine, Svidník, Slovakia, and Bentinck Township, Ontario.

## Festivals & rituals

A Lemko festival is held annually in Zdynia. Festivals of Lemko culture also take place in Svidník, Slovakia, and in Bentinck Township, Ontario. In Ukraine, Lemko festivals have been organized in Zymna Voda, Volia Yakubova near Drohobych, near Monastyryska, and in Nahirne near Sambir. Lemko youth activists organize summer camps, and public initiatives support and popularize Lemko culture and history.

## Foodways

The source mentions an agrarian economy in the Lemko region, alongside ox grazing and sheep herding.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do document a modern literary and educational tradition. Metodyj Trochanovskij developed a Lemko primer, *Bukvar: Perša knyžečka dlja narodnŷch škol*, published in 1935, and a first reader, *Druha knyžečka dlja narodnŷch škol*, published in 1936. In 2013, Petro Krynyckij translated *The Little Prince* into Lemko. The source identifies several Lemko writers and poets, including Bohdan Ihor Antonych, Aleksander Dukhnovych, Seman Madzelan, Radoslav Rochallyi, and Petro Murianka, also identified as Piotr Trochanowski. The Lemko Portal in Ukraine, Lemko Portal in Poland, and other listed organizations are associated with cultural revival and preservation.

## Language & religion

Lemko speech belongs to the Slavic dialect continuum of Central Europe and shares features with other Carpathian varieties often grouped by outside linguists under the Rusyn language. It has also been strongly influenced by neighboring Polish and Slovak languages. Its status is debated: it has been described as a separate language, a dialect of Ukrainian, or a dialect of Rusyn. The language has the ISO 639-3 code rue. The Lemko dialect was introduced as a language of instruction in schools in 1934, while in the school year 2010–2011 it was taught as a first language in twenty primary schools and interschool groups and ten junior high schools and interschool groups, with 188 students attending.

Lemko religious life is centered on Byzantine Christianity. Most Lemkos today are Eastern rite or Byzantine-rite Catholics, while substantial numbers belong to the Eastern Orthodox Church. In Poland, Lemkos belong to the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church or the Ruthenian Catholic Church; in Slovakia, the source also refers to the Slovak Greek Catholic Church. The Union of Brest with the Roman Catholic Church took place in the 17th century. Eastern Orthodoxy was reintroduced to many Lemko areas in the early 20th century through the efforts of Father Maxim Sandovich, who was canonized by the Polish Orthodox Church in the 1990s.
