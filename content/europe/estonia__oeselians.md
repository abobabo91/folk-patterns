## Overview

Oeselians, also called Osilians, were the people who lived on the Estonian island of Saaremaa, known historically as Ösel, before the Northern Crusades in the 13th century. Written records also called the island Oeselia or Osilia. The sources describe the Oeselians as a maritime population whose strength lay in ships and whose activities included raiding surrounding lands. They appear in Viking Age literature under the name “Vikings from Estonia,” and their history is recorded in sources including the writings of Henry of Livonia, Saxo Grammaticus, the Livonian Rhymed Chronicle, and the Chronicle of Hermann von Wartberge.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources mention strongholds and stone forts. In 1222, the Danish king Valdemar II established a stone fortress on Saaremaa; the Oeselians besieged it, and the castle was later leveled to the ground. After 1261, the Livonian Order established a stone fort at Pöide.

## Music & performance

One historical episode records the words “Laula! Laula! Pappi!”—“Sing! Sing! Priest!”—spoken by Oeselians during the torture of the captured Christian missionary Frederick of Zelle. Henry of Livonia recorded this encounter during the 13th century. The expression is described as Finnic and has been used to support the identification of the Oeselians as a Finnic language group at that time.

The sources also preserve descriptions of maritime activity. The Livonian Rhymed Chronicle states that the Oeselians used two kinds of ships, the *piratica* and the *liburna*. The *piratica* was a warship, while the *liburna* was mainly a merchant ship. A *piratica* could carry approximately 30 men and had a high prow shaped like a dragon or a snakehead, together with a quadrangular sail. These vessels are described as instruments of warfare, raiding, and movement across the Baltic Sea rather than as elements of a documented musical tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record religious practices and periods of conflict connected with Christianity. In 1227, after the destruction of Muhu Stronghold and the surrender of Valjala Stronghold, the Oeselians formally accepted Christianity. After the defeat of the Livonian Brothers of the Sword in the Battle of Saule in 1236, military action on Saaremaa began again. In 1241, the Oeselians accepted Christianity again by signing treaties with the Livonian Order’s Master Andreas de Velven and the Bishopric of Ösel-Wiek; these treaties included penalties for pagan rituals.

In 1261, the Oeselians again renounced Christianity and killed all the Germans on the island. On 24 July 1343, during St. George’s Night Uprising, the Oeselians killed all the Germans on the island, drowned the clerics, and besieged the Livonian Order’s castle at Pöide.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oeselians appear in several historical and literary records. In Viking Age literature, they were often included under the name “Vikings from Estonia,” including in the writings of Saxo Grammaticus in the late 12th century. The earliest known written use of the Latinised form “Oeselians” is attributed to Henry of Livonia in the 13th century.

The Livonian Rhymed Chronicle describes the Oeselians as neighbors to the Kurs, surrounded by the sea, confident in their ships, and active in raids during summers when sea travel was possible. The Chronicle of Henry of Livonia describes a fleet of sixteen ships and five hundred Oeselians ravaging the area that is now southern Sweden, then belonging to Denmark. Saxo Grammaticus describes a battle on Öland in 1170 in which the Danish king Valdemar I mobilised his entire fleet against incursions by Estonian Vikings and Couronians.

The sources also preserve a religious legend concerning Tharapita. According to the chronicle, Tharapita was born on a forested mountain in Virumaa, or Vironia, on mainland Estonia, and flew from there to Saaremaa.

## Language & religion

The sources identify the Oeselian expression “Laula! Laula! Pappi!” as Finnic and state that it has been used to support identifying the Oeselians as a Finnic language group.

Henry of Livonia described Tharapita as the superior god of the Oeselians. The name Taarapita has been interpreted as “Taara, help!” or “Taara keeper,” and Taara is associated with the Scandinavian god Thor. The story of Tharapita’s or Taara’s flight from Vironia to Saaremaa has been associated with a major meteor disaster estimated to have happened in 660 ± 85 B.C. and to have formed Kaali crater in Saaremaa. The sources also document repeated conversions, renunciations of Christianity, and treaties that imposed penalties for pagan rituals.
