## Overview

The Danube Swabians are an ethnic German population whose ancestors settled in the middle and lower Danube basin of east-central Europe during the 17th and 18th centuries, primarily through recruitment by the Habsburg monarchy. Settlers came from Swabia, Alsace, Austria, Bavaria, Franconia, Hesse, the Palatinate, and other regions of the Holy Roman Empire. Their communities developed in Hungary, Romania, and Yugoslavia, especially around the Danube valley, the Pannonian plain, Buda, the Bakony and Vértes mountains, Lake Balaton, the Banat, Bačka, Satu Mare, and the Vojvodina. The group preserved German language, customs, and folklore while developing regional influences from Hungarian, Serbian, Croatian, Romanian, and other neighboring cultures. Before World War II, approximately two million ethnic Danube Swabians lived in the region.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources describe regional clothing-related vocabulary in the Danube Swabian language.

### Clothing & dress

They state that loanwords from Hungarian, Serbian, and Romanian concern dress as well as cuisine, agriculture, politics, place names, and sports. No museum catalogue records are supplied for clothing or other dress objects.

### Architecture

Danube Swabian architecture is described as neither southern German nor Balkan, but as distinctive to itself. Houses were often built with stamped mud and straw walls or mud bricks, and such houses were widespread throughout the Vojvodina region.

The Danube Swabians also participated in the construction and rebuilding of settlements. German farmers drained marshes near the Danube and the Tisza, rebuilt farms, and constructed roads and canals. A fortress representing Temeschburg, or Timișoara, appears on a coat of arms adopted by many Danube Swabian cultural organizations.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention *Ulmer Schachteln*, boats used by many settlers who traveled from Ulm down the Danube River to their destinations, and state that the Austro-Hungarian Empire provided funds to build these boats for transport. No museum catalogue records are supplied.

## Music & performance

The sources do describe the preservation of the Danube Swabian language through poetry. Sepp Stumper, who grew up in Ravni Topolovac, wrote numerous poems in dialect about Danube Swabian life. In 2006, the poems were recorded to preserve the language for future generations. This is the only specifically documented performance or recording activity in the supplied sources.

The sources also state that Danube Swabian dialects contain elements from Swabian, East Franconian, Austro-Bavarian, Pfälzisch, Alsatian, and Alemannic German, together with Austro-Hungarian administrative and military jargon and regional loanwords from Hungarian, Serbian, Romanian, Turkish, English, Russian, and other Balkan and South Slavic languages. These linguistic contacts are documented.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document religious and settlement history. The Stifoller, also called Stiffolder, practitioners of Folk Catholicism, settled in villages in Baranya County and Tolna County between 1717 and 1804, mostly in 1720. Their ancestors came from the Diocese of Fulda at Fulda and the surrounding Rhön Mountains in Germany. The Crown generally permitted German settlers to retain their language and religion, and many Danube Swabians were Roman Catholic. Under Joseph II, Holy Roman Emperor, Lutheran Germans from Hesse, the Palatinate, and Lower Saxony were also allowed to settle in Hungary and other parts of the Habsburg empire.

## Foodways

Danube Swabian cuisine includes recipes brought from Germany as well as regional dishes adopted into the Danube Swabian repertoire. The sources name chicken paprikasch, goulash, *spätzle*, *kipfel*, caramel and walnut wafers called *Oblaten*, *sarma*, apple strudel, pumpkin strudel, cheese strudel, *schaum rolle*, *djuvec*, stuffed peppers, plum dumplings, and distinctive *Banater bratwurst*.

Paprika is described as heavily employed in the food, contributing to the German nickname *Paprikadeutsche*. The cuisine therefore combines southern German regional customs with Hungarian-Croatian influence. The sources identify cuisine and agriculture as areas in which Hungarian, Serbian, and Romanian loanwords are especially common.

The supplied material does not describe staple grains, cooking vessels, kitchen architecture, ceremonial foods, tea or coffee customs, dietary rules, fasting practices, or the preparation of any particular dish.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do describe the use of dialect poetry as a form of cultural preservation. Sepp Stumper wrote numerous poems in dialect about the life of the Danube Swabians, and these poems were recorded in 2006 for future generations. The sources also state that Danube Swabian communities maintained their language and customs through societies and clubs in diaspora communities.

Danube Swabian folklore is mentioned as part of the customs preserved by early German communities.

## Language & religion

Danube Swabian speech is described as only nominally Swabian German and is also locally called Shwovish. It contains elements from several German dialects, especially Swabian, East Franconian, Austro-Bavarian, Pfälzisch, Alsatian, and Alemannic. It also includes administrative and military jargon from the Austro-Hungarian period and loanwords from Hungarian, Serbian, Romanian, Turkish, English, Russian, and Balkan and South Slavic languages.

The language varies considerably between communities. Speakers could distinguish inhabitants of neighboring villages by vocabulary, including different words for marmalade. In Baranya, Stiffolderish Shwovish is identified as its own dialect. In the 1920s, Danube Swabians from widely separated villages sometimes used standard high German to communicate. In Esseg, also called Osijek, the local Shwovish or “Essekerish” was so strongly assimilated with Croatian that it could be understood only by people who also spoke Croatian or Serbian.

The sources describe Roman Catholic, Folk Catholic, and Lutheran communities.
