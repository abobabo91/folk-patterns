## Overview

Kryashens are a subgroup of the Volga Tatars and are frequently referred to as one of the minority ethnic groups in Russia. They are mostly found in Tatarstan, Udmurtia, Bashkortostan and Chelyabinsk Oblast. Kryashens are Orthodox Christians, and some regard themselves as different from other Tatars. Most Kryashen dialects differ only slightly from the central dialect of the Tatar language and do not differ from the accents of Tatar Muslims in the same areas. The 2010 census recorded 34,882 Kryashens in Russia. Their history is connected with the voluntary and violent Christianization of Muslim and Animist Volga Tatars between the 16th and 19th centuries, while their written culture includes Arabic, Cyrillic and Latin scripts.

## Festivals & rituals

The history of the Kryashens is associated with Christianization, the adoption of Christianity and the continued presence of Orthodox Christianity. They state that many converts continued secretly to practice Islam and that some Kryashens were crypto-Muslims.

## Oral tradition & literature

The earliest Kryashen works and literature were written using the Arabic script. An unaltered Cyrillic script was also used to translate religious material to Tatar, and a modified Cyrillic script was adopted in 1862. By the early 20th century, over 100 books had been published using this script. In 1922, a modified Arabic script for writing Tatar was introduced, although Cyrillic continued in use until 1928, when both scripts were replaced by the Latin script. Early literature was mainly religious, but around the 1910s secular works began to increase. A newspaper for the Kryashen community was published in Kazan from 1928 to 1929 before ceasing to exist.

## Language & religion

Kryashens speak Kryashen dialects of the Tatar language. Most dialects differ only slightly from the central dialect of Tatar and do not differ from the accents of Tatar Muslims in the same areas. Kryashens are Orthodox Christians, although their historical formation involved the Christianization of Muslim and Animist Volga Tatars. The earliest writing used the Arabic script; Cyrillic was used for religious translations, a modified Cyrillic script was adopted in 1862, a modified Arabic script was introduced in 1922, and both were replaced by the Latin script in 1928. A Tatar school for converts was established in 1863, and the first seminary was founded in 1872.
