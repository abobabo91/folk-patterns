## Overview

The Chuvash are a Turkic ethnic group and a branch of the Oğurs. They inhabit an area stretching from the Idel-Ural region to Siberia, with most people living in the Russian republic of Chuvashia and surrounding areas; Chuvash communities are also found throughout Russia and in Central Asia. Chuvash is a Turkic language spoken primarily in the Chuvash Republic and adjacent areas, and it is the only surviving member of the Oghur branch of Turkic languages. The Chuvash have distinctive embroidery patterns in their traditional clothing, preserve pre-Christian traditions in cultural activities, and have experienced Russian, Tatar and neighboring Uralic influences. Most Chuvash are Eastern Orthodox Christians, while minorities are Sunni Muslims or practitioners of Vattisen Yaly.

## Festivals & rituals

The Chuvash retain some pre-Christian and pre-Islamic shamanism traditions in cultural activities. They pray in parallel at shrines called *keremet* and sacrifice geese there; one of the main shrines is located in the town of Bilyarsk.

The Chuvash also have Vattisen Yaly, described in the sources as a contemporary revival of the ethnic religion of the Chuvash people.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe educational, literary and linguistic publication in Chuvash during the 18th and 19th centuries, together with the establishment of schools and other programs. A special written script for Chuvash was created in 1871. A Chuvash cultural revival began in 1989, and the language later returned to educational, public and political life.

## Language & religion

Chuvash is a Turkic language and the only surviving member of the Oghur branch of the Turkic language family. It is spoken in European Russia, primarily in the Chuvash Republic and adjacent areas, and is an official language of Chuvashia. The Chuvash writing system is based on Cyrillic and adds four letters to those used in the Russian alphabet: Ӑ, Ӗ, Ҫ and Ӳ. The modern Chuvash alphabet was devised in 1873 by school inspector Ivan Yakovlevich Yakovlev and underwent significant modification in 1938.

Chuvash has two to three dialects. Its classification within the Oghuric family cannot be determined exactly because surviving literary records for Bulgar and possibly Khazar are scant. The language is distinguished from Common Turkic languages by r corresponding to Common Turkic z and l corresponding to Common Turkic š. Russian, Mari and Volga Tatar heavily influenced Chuvash, while Mongolian, Arabic and Persian also influenced it. Chuvash is considered endangered by UNESCO because Russian dominates most spheres of life and few children learning Chuvash are likely to become active users.

Most Chuvash are Eastern Orthodox Christians and belong to the Russian Orthodox Church. Minorities are Sunni Muslims or practitioners of Vattisen Yaly. Following Russian subjugation in the 16th century, Christianization began, although most Chuvash were not converted until the mid-19th century. The Chuvash retain some pre-Christian and pre-Islamic shamanism traditions in cultural activities. Some Chuvash communities influenced by Tatars became Muslim while retaining their language and several Chuvash customs.
