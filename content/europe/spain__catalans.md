## Overview

Catalans are a Romance-speaking ethnic group native to Catalonia who speak Catalan. The current official category includes citizens of Catalonia, a nationality and autonomous community in Spain, and inhabitants of the Roussillon historical region in Southern France, also called Northern Catalonia and Pays Catalan in French. The word is also extended by some authors to people from Andorra, Valencia, the Balearic Islands, eastern Aragon, and Alghero in Sardinia, where Catalan is spoken. The vast majority of Catalans live in Catalonia, in the northeast of Spain; at least 100,000 Catalan speakers live in the Pays Catalan in France. Catalan culture includes distinctive clothing, foodways, festivals, music, religious traditions, and forms of collective identity shaped by the history of the Crown of Aragon, France, and Spain.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Traditional Catalan dress is now practically used only in folkloric celebrations. Men’s clothing included the *barretina*, a woollen, long cap usually red or purple, and the *faixa*, a wide belt. Women wore the *ret*, a fine net bag used to contain hair. Traditional footwear was the *espardenya*, or espadrille. Other items associated with women’s folk costume include the *pubilla* dress, the *catalana*, also known as the *pagesa*, and the *gandalla* as headwear. The sources distinguish these folkloric garments from ordinary contemporary clothing.

### Architecture

The *masia*, or *mas*, is described as a defining characteristic of the Catalan countryside. It includes a large house, land, cattle, and an extended family. The tradition is in decline as the nuclear family has largely replaced the extended family, as elsewhere in western Europe.

## Music & performance

Catalan music has one of the oldest documented musical traditions in Europe. Music is nevertheless identified as an important component of Catalan celebrations, where songs and bands accompany food, processions, dances, contests, and festive gatherings.

## Dance & theatre

Catalan celebrations include dances, processions, performances of Passion Plays, and representations of mythological creatures. The sources also identify the *castells*, or human towers, as a key element of Catalan celebration. These practices occur within festivities alongside music, songs, bands, animals, food, and festive floats.

## Festivals & rituals

Fire is used in many important Catalan traditional festivals, which are described as deriving from pagan roots. The sources mention the Flame of Canigó and the Bonfires of Saint John. The *Diada de Sant Jordi* is held on 23 April; men give women roses and women give men a book.

Historical memory is another axis of celebration in Catalonia, where Catalan people reunite with their date of birth as a people. Religious celebrations include St. George’s Day, the celebrations of Saint Vincent Martyr and Saint Anthony Abbot, Easter processions, and Passion Plays. Carnival and the Dances of Death have a complicated relationship with religion. Christmas traditions include the *Tió de Nadal* and the *caganer* in Nativity scenes.

Other elements of Catalan celebration include food, pig slaughter and harvest festivals, contests, the choice of major and festive floats, music, songs, bands, processions, dances, animals, bulls, and representations of mythological creatures. The Patum of Berga has been declared a World Heritage Site by UNESCO.

## Foodways

The Catalan diet is part of the Mediterranean diet and includes olive oil. Catalans are described as eating veal, *vedella*, and lamb, *xai*. Daily meals traditionally included a very light morning breakfast, an afternoon main meal, and an evening meal. Breakfast could include fruit or fruit juice, milk, coffee, or *pa amb tomàquet*, “bread with tomato.” It was often divided into an early first breakfast and a second breakfast between 10:00 and 12:00.

The main meal, roughly from 13:00 to 14:30, usually comprised three dishes: pasta or vegetables, meat or fish, and fruit or yogurt. The evening meal, roughly from 20:00 to 22:30, was more substantial than breakfast but lighter than lunch, often consisting of one main dish and fruit; moderate quantities of wine were commonly consumed.

*Embotits*, a wide variety of Catalan sausages and cold meats, are important in Catalan gastronomy. These include pork sausages such as *botifarra* and *fuet*. Bread, once central to the diet, is now used mainly in the morning and as a supplement to the noon meal. In the past, poor people ate soup every day and rice on Thursday and Sunday. Abstaining from meat during Lent was once strong but is now practiced only in rural areas. Spicy food is rare, although *allioli* and *romesco* are notably garlicky sauces.

*Escudella* is a soup containing chickpeas, potatoes, green cabbage, celery, carrots, turnips, *botifarra*, pork feet, salted ham, chicken, and veal. In Northern Catalonia it is sometimes called *ollada*. Other dishes include *calçots*, onions similar in shape to leeks that are often grilled and eaten with *romesco* sauce, and *escalivada*.

## Language & religion

Catalan is a Romance language and is described as the language closest to Occitan. It also shares features with Spanish, French, Portuguese, Aragonese, and Italian. Catalan has several linguistic varieties regarded as dialects, including Central Catalan, the dialect group with the most speakers. The total number of Catalan speakers is given as over 9.8 million in 2011, including 5.9 million residing in Catalonia; native speakers number about 4.4 million, including more than 2.8 million in Catalonia. Most Catalan speakers in Spain are bilingual in Catalan and Spanish. In Roussillon, French is the majority language after a continued process of language shift. Aranese, an Occitan dialect, is spoken in the Aran valley.

The traditional religion in Catalonia is Roman Catholicism, although the region has undergone several waves of secularization. The sources describe Catholic, Christian, agnostic, atheist, Muslim, Evangelical, Buddhist, and Orthodox Christian populations. Religious traditions remain visible in St. George’s Day, the celebrations of Saint Vincent Martyr and Saint Anthony Abbot, Easter processions, Passion Plays, Carnival, and Christmas customs.
