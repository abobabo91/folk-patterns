## Overview

The Kalmyks are the only Mongolic people found in Europe, residing in the easternmost part of the European Plain. Their ancestors were Oirats, or Western Mongols, who migrated from Dzungaria to Eastern Europe in early modern times and established the Kalmyk Khanate in the 17th century. The Kalmyks are primarily descended from the Torghut subgroup of Oirats, with smaller populations of Dörbets, Khoshuts and Dzungars; a group that settled in the Don region among the Don Cossacks became known as the Buzavas. They have retained distinguished sub-ethnic groups and remain separated from geographical neighbours in Russia and northeast Caucasus. The Kalmyks are the only traditionally Buddhist ethnic group located inside Europe. Small Kalmyk communities have also been established in the United States, France, Germany, and the Czech Republic.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The Oirats, the ancestors of the Kalmyks, pitched yurts while moving across the grassy plains of western Inner Asia. The sources describe these communities as nomadic pastoral societies whose movements extended between Lake Balkhash in present-day eastern Kazakhstan and Lake Baikal in present-day Russia north of central Mongolia. In the lower Volga region, the Torghut and Dörbet Oirats encamped on the east and west banks of the Volga River. During the period of the Kalmyk Khanate, the Oirats governed themselves as a pastoral nomadic society, while Erdeni Batur designed and built a capital city called Kubak-sari on the Emil River near the modern city of Tacheng.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Kalmyk livestock and its products were exchanged at Astrakhan, Tsaritsyn and Saratov for brick tea, grain, textiles and metal articles. Erdeni Batur also sought to build small industry, including metal works, to supply the Dzungar military with weapons. No Kalmyk ceramic forms, household objects, tools, or locally distinctive metalwork are described.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They record *Ulan Zalata*, translated as the “red-buttoned ones,” as another generally accepted name for Kalmyks.

## Music & performance

The sources name one Oirat epic song, **“The Rout of Mongolian Sholoi Ubashi Khuntaiji.”** The song recounts the Oirat victory over the Altan Khan of the Khalkha in 1587 and preserves the back-and-forth struggle between the Oirats and the Altan Khanate in narrative form.

The sources also describe the political and religious setting in which Oirat culture developed. The Four Oirat were a decentralized alliance of Khoshut, Choros, Torghut and Dörbet tribes. Their society was organized at the tribal level, and the Oirats maintained a pastoral nomadic lifestyle.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record the religious and political significance of the Gelug school of Tibetan Buddhism. The Oirat summit near the Tarbagatai Mountains in 1640 ratified the “Great Code of the Nomads” while seeking unity under the banner of the Gelug school. Güshi Khan formed the Khoshut Khanate to protect Tibet and the Gelug from internal and external enemies, and the Khoshut rulers Baibagas Khan and Güshi Khan were the first Oirat leaders to convert to that school.

## Foodways

They do mention pastoral livestock and historical exchange. The Oirats kept herds of cattle, flocks of sheep, horses, donkeys and camels, while Kalmyk livestock and its products were exchanged for brick tea, grain, textiles and metal articles at Astrakhan, Tsaritsyn and Saratov.

## Oral tradition & literature

The principal named oral tradition in the sources is the Oirat epic song **“The Rout of Mongolian Sholoi Ubashi Khuntaiji.”** It recounts the Oirat victory over the Altan Khan of the Khalkha in 1587. The sources also preserve historical explanations associated with names and origins. Paul Pelliot translated “Torghut” as *garde de jour* and connected the name either with the guard of Genghis Khan or with the Keraites and an earlier *garde de jour*. The name “Kalmyk” was supposedly given to the Oirats by their Muslim neighbours and was later used by the Russians, while the Oirats continued to identify themselves through tribal, clan, or other internal affiliations.

## Language & religion

The Kalmyks are a Mongolic people, and the sources identify their ancestors as Oirats, or Western Mongols. They describe Oirat and Kalmyk identities through the names Torghut, Dörbet, Khoshut, Dzungar, Choros, Khoit, Bayid, Mingat and other tribal affiliations.

The Kalmyks are the only traditionally Buddhist ethnic group located inside Europe. Oirat leaders converted to the Gelug school of Tibetan Buddhism, and the Gelug became an important religious and political affiliation among the Oirats. The sources state that the Oirats adopted the “Great Code of the Nomads” in 1640 under the banner of the Gelug school, and that Ayuka Khan maintained close contacts with the Dalai Lama in Tibet.
