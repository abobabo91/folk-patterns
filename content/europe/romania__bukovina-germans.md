## Overview

The Bukovina Germans, also called Buchenland Germans or Bukovinian Germans, are a German ethnic group associated with Bukovina, a historical region at the crossroads of Central and Eastern Europe. Their main demographic presence lasted from the last quarter of the 18th century, when Bukovina was annexed by the Habsburg Empire, until 1940, when nearly all were forcefully resettled. German communities remain in Suceava County, especially in Suceava and Rădăuți, as well as in smaller rural settlements. In the 1930 census, approximately 75,000 ethnic Germans were registered in Bukovina. Their culture developed through several German-speaking regional groups, including Austrian, Bohemian-Bavarian, Palatine Rhine Franconian, Swabian, and Zipser communities.

## Material culture

### Architecture

German communities in Bukovina transferred local architectural heritage, including churches, to Romanian communities free of charge and without conditions during World War II. The sources also state that historical heritage, including churches and houses, was given to local Romanian communities after most Bukovina Germans departed.

## Music & performance

The sources mention *Volkssagen aus der Bukowina*, described as a compilation of folk songs from the entire region of Bucovina, collected and written by Ludwig Adolf Staufe-Simiginowicz. The sources also state that he collected fairytales from Bucovina. No instruments, song genres, performance contexts, dances performed with music, or musical institutions are described.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources state that local German culture-based festivals have been held in Suceava County, with members of the Bukovina German diaspora returning on such occasions, especially in Suceava. These festivals are compared with Haferland week of the Transylvanian Saxons, but no specific Bukovina German festival names, dates, seasonal calendar, ritual sequence, or associated ceremonies are given.

## Foodways

The regional culture and cuisine of the Bukovina Germans is described as similar to the cultures and cuisines of Central Europe, particularly German and Austrian cuisine, and also to German-Bohemian cuisine.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ludwig Adolf Staufe-Simiginowicz wrote *Volkssagen aus der Bukowina*, described as a compilation of folk songs from the entire region of Bucovina, and collected fairytales from Bucovina. Bukovina German or Bukovina-associated German-language writers named in the sources include Gregor von Rezzori, Alfred Kittner, Alfred Margul Sperber, Paul Celan, Ludwig Adolf Staufe-Simiginowicz, Ernst Rudolf Neubauer, Rose Ausländer, and Olha Kobylianska. German-language literature flourished in Bukovina during the first round of the 20th century.

## Language & religion

The Bukovina Germans speak German dialects associated with different regions of origin. Historically, some developed a dialect called *Buchenländisch*. Austrian High German was spoken in urban centres including Cernăuți, Rădăuți, Suceava, Gura Humorului, Câmpulung, and Siret. Bohemian-Bavarian German was spoken by woodsmen in several settlements; Palatine Rhine Franconian and Swabian German were spoken in farming villages; and Zipser German was spoken by mine workers and their descendants. Before World War II, the vast majority of Bukovina Germans were Roman Catholic, with Lutherans in the minority.
