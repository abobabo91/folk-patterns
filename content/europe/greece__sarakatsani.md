## Overview

The Sarakatsani are an ethnic Greek population subgroup traditionally associated with transhumant shepherding. They are native to Greece, with smaller communities in neighbouring Bulgaria, southern Albania, and North Macedonia. Historically, their life was centred on the Pindus mountains and other mountain ranges in continental Greece; Sarakatsani populations have also been found in Central Greece, central Euboea, the northern Peloponnese, Greek Thrace, Greek Macedonia, and the mountains near Olympus and Ossa. In Bulgaria, communities are concentrated around the Balkan range, Mount Rila, and northeastern Bulgaria. The Sarakatsani speak Sarakatsanika, a northern Greek dialect containing archaic Greek elements. Their importance for folk culture lies in their pastoral organisation, seasonal migration, settlements, social institutions, decorative art, song, dance, poetry, embroidery, and distinctive relationship to Greek cultural identity.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Embroidery on traditional costumes** — Embroidery is one of the distinctive Sarakatsani folk arts. The designs on their traditional costumes resemble the geometric art of pre-classical Greece and have been discussed in relation to the group’s decorative art.

**Decorative art** — Sarakatsani decorative art is associated with geometric forms. Angeliki Hatzimihali pointed to similarities between Sarakatsani decoration and the geometric art of pre-classical Greece.

**Shorn wool** — Wool from sheep was prepared, spun, and dyed by women. The work formed part of the household economy and supplied material for clothing and other domestic uses.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe distinctive Sarakatsani dress and costumes. Clothing formed part of the elaborate ritualisation of Sarakatsani life and contributed to the group’s recognition as a distinct social and cultural group within Greek heritage. Women’s traditional costume is described in connection with photographs of women outside branch-woven huts. The sources also state that Sarakatsani people in Evia were known for elaborate costume while working as resin gatherers. No detailed distinction between everyday and ceremonial garments is provided.

### Architecture

The most characteristic Sarakatsani dwelling was a domed hut, framed from branches and covered with thatch. A second form was a rectangular, wood-beamed structure, also thatched. In both types, the centre of the dwelling was a stone hearth. Floors and walls were plastered with mud and mule dung.

Traditional settlements were built near grazing lands in both summer and winter locations. They consisted of cooperating houses, generally connected through kinship or marriage, arranged in a cluster on flat land close to the pasturage. Supporting structures stood nearby. This settlement complex was called *stani* (στάνη), a term that also referred to the cooperative group sharing leased land. Since the late 1930s, national registration requirements led many Sarakatsani to adopt villages associated with summer grazing lands as their legal residences, and many later built permanent houses there.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do describe the stone hearth at the centre of the dwelling, the construction of sheepfolds and goat pens, and the household use of wool, vegetable gardens, chickens, eggs, herbs, honey, and lamb’s blood.

## Music & performance

Song, dance, and poetry are identified as distinctive Sarakatsani folk arts.

Sarakatsani oral and expressive culture was studied by Carsten Høeg, who travelled to Greece between 1920 and 1925 and examined the group’s dialect and narrations. His work is described as influential in discussions of Sarakatsani language and culture. The sources also state that Angeliki Hatzimihali studied Sarakatsani life and emphasised the importance of pastoral life, social organisation, and art forms. These accounts connect expressive culture with a wider social order centred on kinship, honour, cooperation, and the needs of the flocks.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is listed among the distinctive Sarakatsani folk arts. They provide no separate account of ceremonial and entertainment dancing.

## Festivals & rituals

The Sarakatsani are Greek Orthodox Christians affiliated with the Church of Greece. Their ritual calendar includes the feast days of Saint George and Saint Demetrius, which occur shortly before the seasonal migrations in late spring and early winter. The migration to the mountains traditionally began on the eve of Saint George’s day in April, while the return migration began on Saint Demetrius’ day, on 26 October.

Saint George’s feast was especially important. A family feasted on lamb in the saint’s honour, and the ritual also marked Christmas and the Resurrection of Christ. Easter week was the most important ritual period in Sarakatsani religious life. Weddings and funerals were additional ceremonial occasions outside the formal Christian calendar. Funerals involved not only the immediate family of the deceased but also members of the wider kindred, and funerary practice followed that of the church. Mourning was most marked among women, especially the widow.

The sources also describe ritualised social values surrounding honour. The behaviour of each family member reflected on the entire family, while men were responsible for protecting the family’s honour and observing the conduct of household members.

## Foodways

The sources describe a pastoral food economy centred on sheep, goats, chickens, eggs, herbs, honey, and lamb’s blood. At Saint George’s feast, families ate lamb in the saint’s honour. Women maintained household vegetable gardens, and wild herbs were used to supplement the family diet. Eggs from chickens are described as women’s only source of personal income within the traditional household economy.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that the Sarakatsani possess song, dance, and poetry traditions. Carsten Høeg studied Sarakatsani narrations alongside the dialect during visits to Greece between 1920 and 1925. The sources also report that Sarakatsani communities have been the subject of extensive anthropological fieldwork.

The group’s oral culture is connected to a life organised around seasonal pastoral movement, kinship, family honour, and cooperation. There is no detailed account in the supplied sources of contemporary literary revivals or specific preservation projects beyond general efforts to preserve Sarakatsani cultural heritage.

## Language & religion

The Sarakatsani speak Sarakatsanika (Σαρακατσάνικα), a northern Greek dialect containing archaic Greek elements that have not survived in other varieties of modern Greek. Carsten Høeg found no significant traces of foreign loan words and no foreign elements in its phonological or grammatical structures. The dialect contains a few words related to pastoralism that are of Aromanian origin; the sources attribute these influences to more recent contacts and economic dependencies between the two groups.

In Bulgaria, Sarakatsani use Bulgarian and Greek to the same extent in their families and communities, although they usually speak Bulgarian in most situations. Their identities there are described as complex, involving Sarakatsani, Greek, and Bulgarian affiliations.

The Sarakatsani are Greek Orthodox Christians and affiliated with the Church of Greece. Their religious life includes the feasts of Saint George and Saint Demetrius, Easter week, Christmas, the Resurrection of Christ, weddings, and funerals.
