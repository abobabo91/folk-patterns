## Overview

Silesians are the inhabitants of Silesia, a historical region in Central Europe divided among Poland, Germany, and the Czech Republic. The region includes Lower and Upper Silesia and is associated with the river now called Ślęza and Mount Ślęża, which served as a place of cult for pagans before Christianization. Silesians include people connected with Polish, Czech, and German historical populations. In the 2021 Polish census, 596,224 people declared Silesian nationality, while 467,145 declared Silesian to be their native language. The Silesian language belongs to the West Slavic branch and is often classified by Polish linguists as a regional dialect of Polish, although many Silesians regard it as a separate language. Silesian identity matters in folk-cultural study because it developed through contact among Slavic and German-speaking populations and through major boundary and population changes.

## Language & religion

Silesian is described as a Slavic language spoken by the Silesian ethnographic group in Polish Upper Silesia. It belongs to the West Slavic branch of Slavic languages, together with Polish and other Lechitic languages. Most Polish linguists consider it a prominent regional dialect of Polish, while many Silesians regard it as a separate language. In July 2007, the Library of Congress and SIL International officially recognized it and attributed the ISO code SZL. German is also spoken in Silesia, particularly among a sizable minority in the Opole Voivodship in Poland.

Silesian identity has developed within a region historically inhabited by Polish, Czech, and German populations. Slavic tribes entered the depopulated territory in the first half of the 6th century, and settlement stabilized in the early 9th century. The 9th-century Bavarian Geographer recorded the tribal names Opolanie, Dadosesani, Golenzizi, Lupiglaa, and Ślężanie; the 1086 Prague Document also mentioned the Bobrzanie and Trzebowianie. Later sources classified these groups as Silesian tribes and as part of the Polish tribes because of their fundamentally common culture and language.

In the Middle Ages, Silesia was part of Great Moravia, then the Kingdom of Bohemia, and finally the Piast monarchy of Poland. In 990, the first Polish state was created by Duke Mieszko I, and King Boleslaw I established the Bishopric of Wrocław in Lower Silesia in 1000. From 1177 onward, Silesia was divided into smaller duchies. Between 1327 and 1348, its duchies came under the suzerainty of the Crown of Bohemia, which passed to the Habsburg monarchy of Austria in 1526.

From the 13th century, Germans from various German regions settled in Silesia. German dialects became widely used throughout Lower Silesia and some Upper Silesian cities, while Polish remained predominant in Upper Silesia and parts of Lower and Middle Silesia north of the Odra river. The German authorities therefore issued some official documents in Polish or in German and Polish. The Polish-speaking territories of Lower and Middle Silesia were mostly Germanized in the 18th and 19th centuries, except for areas along the northeastern frontier.

In 1742, most of Silesia was seized in the War of the Austrian Succession by King Frederick the Great of Prussia. Cieszyn Silesia remained in the Austrian Empire. The Prussian part constituted the Province of Silesia until 1918 and was later divided into the Prussian provinces of Upper and Lower Silesia. German and Moravian neighbors called Slavic Upper Silesians Wasserpolen, while Germans in Upper Silesia were called Rajchy. The terms Prusocy and Cesarocy also developed from the division between Prussian and Austrian Silesia; Prusocy was later replaced by Hanysy.

After the Silesian Uprisings, the eastern part of Upper Silesia became part of the newly restored Poland. Most of the land ruled by the Habsburgs after the 1742 war went to Czechoslovakia, while Lower Silesia and most of Upper Silesia remained in Germany. World War II and its aftermath intensified ethnic polarization. The Polish-speaking group was the largest, the German-speaking group was smaller, and a third group supported separatism and an independent Silesian nation-state.

After World War II, most of former German Silesia was incorporated into Poland, while smaller regions remained under the control of the German Democratic Republic. Czechoslovakia obtained most of Cieszyn Silesia. Millions of Silesians, mostly of German ethnicity, were forcibly expelled. Between 1955 and 1959, some remaining Silesians emigrated to West and East Germany to reunite with their families. Until 1989, nearly 600,000 Silesians emigrated to Germany. Between 1945 and 1949, millions of ethnic Poles from former eastern Poland and central Poland moved into Silesia, particularly Lower Silesia.

In the 2011 Polish national census, about 209,000 people declared themselves pure Silesians, 376,000 declared joint Silesian and Polish nationality, and 471,000 declared only Polish nationality from Silesia. About 126,000 people declared membership in the German minority. In the 2021 Czech census, 31,301 people declared Silesian nationality, including 18,850 who declared two nationalities. In the 1991 Slovak national census, 6,361 people declared joint Silesian and Moravian nationality. Over 85% of the population in the Polish part of Upper Silesia declared themselves Poles, and in the Czech part, Czechs.
