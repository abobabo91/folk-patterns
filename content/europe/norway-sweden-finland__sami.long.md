## Overview

The Sámi are the only people recognised as indigenous within the European Union, occupying a homeland — *Sápmi* — that stretches across four states from the Norwegian Atlantic coast through the Swedish and Finnish interior to the Kola Peninsula, and which has never corresponded to any national border. Their languages belong to the Sámi branch of Uralic, distantly related to Finnish and Estonian and more distantly to Hungarian; they form a dialect chain of nine or ten living tongues whose ends are mutually unintelligible, so that a speaker from Kautokeino and one from Jokkmokk may share more vocabulary with the herding trade than with each other. Population figures are estimates rather than counts, since most states stopped recording ethnicity: 80,000 to 100,000 is the conventional range, of whom perhaps half live in Norway. The historical economy was not a single one but several — coastal fishing and small-scale farming, river salmon fishing on the Deatnu (Tana) and Tornio, inland hunting of wild reindeer with pitfall lines and fenced drives, and from roughly the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the intensive reindeer pastoralism, with seasonal migration between inland winter lichen pastures and coastal or mountain summer calving grounds, that has since come to stand for Sámi culture as a whole. Only a minority of Sámi herd reindeer today, but the herd remains the material foundation of the crafts: antler, bone, sinew, hide and fur supply almost every object, joined to birch, birch burl, root and bark from the taiga edge and to pewter and silver acquired through centuries of trade with Scandinavian and Russian merchants. Kautokeino (*Guovdageaidnu*) in Norwegian Finnmark, a high inland village of reindeer-owning families, functions as the cultural capital of this world: the site of the Sámi high school and *duodji* college, of the Beaivváš Sámi National Theatre, and of the Easter festival at which the year's weddings, races and song competitions concentrate. The signature Sámi contributions to the world's material culture are the *gákti* with its coded bands of colour, the *duodji* tradition of antler and root carving, the pewter-thread embroidery of reindeer leather, the conical *lávvu* tent, and the painted shaman drum that missionaries burned by the hundred and museums now hold as the most eloquent surviving record of a suppressed cosmology.

## Material culture

Sámi material culture is the material culture of the reindeer: antler, bone, sinew, rawhide and fur worked in combination with birch, root and traded metal, in objects light enough to be carried on a migration and durable enough to outlast several of them.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Sámi textile work centres not on figurative pattern but on woven bands and on stitched metal thread; the decorative load of a costume is carried by its edgings, collars and ties.

- **Pewter-thread embroidery** (*tinntråd*) — Tin-rich pewter wire drawn repeatedly through the graded holes of a reindeer-antler draw-plate until hair-fine, hammered flat, then couched onto soft reindeer leather with sinew thread in tight spirals, zigzags and running scrolls.
- **Band weaving** (*vuoddaga fanas*, *bandgrind* weaving) — Narrow warp-faced bands woven on a rigid heddle of carved reindeer antler or on tablets, in red, yellow, green and blue wool, producing the ties, belts and shoe-bands that finish every garment.
- **Shoe bands** (*vuoddagat*, *komagbånd*) — Long woven bands, sometimes several metres, wound over the instep and up the ankle to seal the soft boot against snow; pattern and width commonly signal district and family.
- **Collar band** (*koltakrage*) — The stiffened standing collar of the tunic, faced in coloured broadcloth and often beaded or pewter-stitched, above which the neck brooch sits.
- **Woollen coverlet weaving** (*grene*) — Coarse striped wool blankets woven on upright or horizontal looms, used as bedding on birch-twig floors, as sledge wraps and as tent lining.

### Clothing & dress

The tunic known as *gákti* in North Sámi and *kolt* in Swedish and Norwegian is the single most legible Sámi object: its cut, hem length, colour banding and button arrangement identify the wearer's home district and often family, and reading them correctly is a social competence in itself.

- **Tunic** (*gákti*, *kolt*) — Straight-cut wool or broadcloth tunic, most commonly deep blue with bands of red, yellow and green at hem, cuff and shoulder; shorter and belted for men, longer for women, with regional cuts from the flared Kautokeino form to the narrow South Sámi one.
- **Hooded shoulder cape** (*luhkka*) — Short circular hooded poncho of wool, thrown over the tunic against wind and driving snow and worn without fastening.
- **Fur coat** (*beaska*) — Winter overcoat of reindeer hide with the hair outward, cut wide and closed with a belt, the warmest garment in the wardrobe and still worn by herders.
- **Fur boots** (*skaller*, *gállohat*, *nutukas*) — Reindeer-leg-skin boots with a sharply upturned toe that hooks the ski strap, soled in hard forehead or hoof skin and insulated with dried sedge grass (*suoidni*, *sennegress*) rather than socks.
- **Horn hat** (*ládjogahpir*) — Nineteenth-century women's cap built over a curved wooden board rising behind the head, condemned by pietist clergy as a seat of the devil, abandoned by the late 1800s and deliberately revived by artists and craftspeople in recent decades.

### Architecture

There is no Sámi monumental architecture and no masonry tradition; the built repertoire is a graded series of shelters and stores matched to how long a family intends to stay, from a night's tent to a turf house occupied for generations.

- **Turf hut** (*goahti*, *gåetie*, *kåta*) — Semi-permanent dwelling on a frame of paired curved birch poles (*bealljat*) clad in birch bark and turf, with a central hearth and smoke hole and, in the rear, the sacred *boaššu* space where meat and hunting gear were kept.
- **Conical tent** (*lávvu*) — Portable tent of straight poles leaned into a tripod and covered formerly in reindeer hide or woollen cloth, now canvas; erectable in minutes and the standard migration dwelling.
- **Pole store** (*njalla*) — Small gabled storehouse raised on one or two peeled stumps and reached by a removable ladder, keeping dried meat, fish and flour beyond the reach of bears, wolverines and foxes.
- **Timber house** (*stuollu*, *stuga*) — Log cabins at fixed fishing and winter sites, adopted from Scandinavian and Finnish neighbours from the eighteenth century onward and now the ordinary dwelling.
- **Sacred sites** (*sieidi*) — Not buildings but marked places: distinctive rocks, cliffs, lake islands and springs that received offerings of antler, bone, fat and coins, many of which remain known and respected.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Sámi produced no pottery — clay firing suits neither the migration nor the sub-arctic fuel budget — so vessels are carved from wood, sewn from hide or beaten from traded metal, and the craft complex as a whole is called *duodji*.

- **Burl cup** (*guksi*, *kuksa*) — Drinking cup carved from a birch burl, the grain left to show, often fitted with an antler or bone handle plate and a leather thong for the belt; boiled in salt brine to season it against cracking.
- **Milking vessel** (*náhppi*) — Shallow carved wooden bowl with a broad handle, used to milk reindeer cows in the summer pen and to make the sour reindeer milk from which *juobmu* preserve was prepared.
- **Antler carving** (*čoarvi duodji*) — Reindeer antler sawn, filed, bent in hot water and incised with fine geometric bandwork, then rubbed with soot or bark dye; used for knife hilts and sheaths, spoons, buttons, needle cases and belt fittings.
- **Needle case** (*nállogoahti*) — Hollow antler tube, its name literally "needle house", hung from a woman's belt on a chain or thong with scissors, awl and thimble, and among the most densely decorated of all Sámi objects.
- **Root basketry and knives** (*vesse*, *niibi*) — Coiled spruce- and birch-root containers for cheese and flour, and the long-bladed working knife whose sheath of stiffened leather and antler is the male counterpart to the needle case.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Sámi ornament is silver and pewter rather than gold, and much of it entered the tradition through trade with Scandinavian silversmiths before being reworked to Sámi taste; the brooch and the belt are the two loaded sites.

- **Neck brooch** (*risku*) — Concave silver disc, pierced and stamped, fastening the tunic's collar and hung with loose spoon- or leaf-shaped danglers that chime with movement; large ceremonial examples are heirlooms passed between generations.
- **Chest brooch sets** (*silbabáktaris*) — Several graded brooches worn together on a woman's chest, historically also a portable store of household wealth.
- **Belt** (*boagán*) — Leather or woven belt studded with pewter or silver plaques and rosettes, carrying knife, needle case, pouch and cup, and cut differently for men and women.
- **Leather bracelet** (*sámi bracelet*, *tinntrådsarmband*) — Soft reindeer-leather band embroidered in pewter thread and closed with an antler button and a sinew loop; now the most widely sold single *duodji* object and, in this form, largely a twentieth-century development.
- **Hair and headwear** — Women's caps vary sharply by district, from the red four-winds cap of Kautokeino to the embroidered Skolt cap, and headgear commonly marks marital status.

## Music & performance

Sámi music is dominated by a single vocal genre, the *joik*, which is sung not *about* its subject but *of* it: a person, a reindeer, a mountain or a grief has its own melody, and to joik it is to call it into presence.

- **Yoik** (*luohti*, *vuolle*, *juoigos*) — Short cyclic melody, pentatonic or freer in pitch, without fixed beginning or end, often with few or no lexical words; personal yoiks are composed for individuals, frequently at birth, and remain attached to them after death.
- **Skolt narrative song** (*leu'dd*) — The distinct Skolt Sámi genre, long and text-heavy, recounting individual biographies, marriages and migrations, and treated as a form of historical record.
- **Angelica pipe** (*fadno*) — Reed instrument made from the hollow stalk of angelica, attested historically and reconstructed by contemporary musicians; the pre-Christian Sámi otherwise used almost no melody instruments.
- **Shaman drum** (*goavddis*, *meavrresgárri*, *runebomme*) — Oval frame or bowl drum with a hide head painted in red alder-bark juice with sun, deity, dwelling and animal figures, struck with an antler hammer by the *noaidi*; also read as a divining device by the movement of a brass ring across the figures.
- **Contemporary Sámi music** — Nils-Aslak Valkeapää, Mari Boine, Wimme Saari, Sofia Jannok and Ande Somby carried the yoik into art music, rock and jazz; Frode Fjellheim's South Sámi *vuelie* setting opened Disney's *Frozen* to a global audience.

## Dance & theatre

There is no large documented Sámi dance corpus, and the honest reason is historical: Laestadian revivalism in the nineteenth century condemned dancing along with yoik and the drum, and whatever secular dance existed in the western communities was largely abandoned.

- **Skolt dances** (*Sä´mmlaž tanss*) — The Skolt Sámi, insulated from Lutheran pietism by Orthodox Christianity, retained couple and set dances including quadrille forms absorbed from Russian and Finnish neighbours.
- **Ring and game dances** — Scattered accounts and a few revived sequences suggest simple circle and imitation dances, but the record is too thin to reconstruct a regional system.
- **Beaivváš Sámi Nášunálateáhter** — The Sámi national theatre, based in Kautokeino and founded in 1981, performing in Sámi languages and touring internationally; its repertoire mixes adapted oral narrative with new political drama.
- **Sámi cinema** — Nils Gaup's *Ofelaš* (*Pathfinder*, 1987), the first feature in North Sámi, and Amanda Kernell's *Sameblod* (*Sami Blood*, 2016) on the boarding-school era have done much of the work a staged tradition might otherwise have carried.
- **Festival stage culture** — Riddu Riđđu in Kåfjord and Márkomeannu in the Ofoten borderlands function as the commissioning venues for contemporary Sámi performance.

## Festivals & rituals

The Sámi ceremonial year is organised around the herding calendar and around markets and church gatherings that historically drew dispersed families together; several of these have become the principal public expressions of Sámi nationhood.

- **Sámi National Day** (6 February) — Commemorates the first Sámi congress, held in Trondheim in 1917 and convened largely through the work of Elsa Laula Renberg; marked across Sápmi with the Sámi flag, designed by Astrid Båhl and adopted in 1986, and the anthem *Sámi soga lávlla*.
- **Kautokeino Easter Festival** (*beassážat*) — The great spring gathering at the end of the winter pastures, with weddings, confirmations, reindeer racing, lasso throwing and the Sámi Grand Prix song and yoik competition.
- **Jokkmokk winter market** — Swedish Sámi market held since 1605 in the first week of February, originally a taxation and trading occasion imposed by the crown and now a craft and reunion fair.
- **Bear ceremonialism** — The most elaborate documented pre-Christian rite: a regulated hunt, a euphemistic vocabulary for the animal, ritual purification of the hunters, and burial of the bear's bones in anatomical order so that it might return.
- **Offering at sieidi** — Seasonal offerings of antler, fat, bone and later metal at named rocks and lake islands, made for fishing and herding luck; the practice persisted quietly long after nominal conversion.

## Foodways

Sámi cooking is a cuisine of one animal and of what the tundra adds to it. Reindeer supplies boiled meat (*vuoššanbiergu*) cut from bones in water with little more than salt; *suovas*, cold-smoked and thinly sliced; *gurpi*, minced meat and fat sewn into an intestine casing and smoked into a dense travelling sausage; blood puddings, blood pancakes and *márfi* blood sausage; and marrow, tongue and heart, all of which are eaten rather than discarded because a herding economy wastes nothing. Freshwater and anadromous fish are the other pillar: Atlantic salmon taken from the Deatnu with weirs and drag-nets, whitefish and arctic char dried on racks, and cod and halibut among the Sea Sámi of the fjords, where a cow, a boat and a small hayfield rather than a herd defined the household. Reindeer were milked into the *náhppi* into the twentieth century, the milk soured or used to preserve *juobmu*, a paste of boiled mountain sorrel or angelica that supplied vitamin C through winter. Bread is *gáhkku* or *láibi*, a soft unleavened wheat or barley round baked on a griddle or in hearth ashes, split and eaten with butter and meat broth. Cloudberries (*láttak*), lingonberries and crowberries are gathered in quantity and keep with little or no sugar. Boiled coffee, taken very strong, is the standard hospitality of both tent and kitchen, and *bidos* — a thick reindeer stew of meat, marrow bone, potato and carrot — is the dish that marks a wedding.

## Oral tradition & literature

Before the twentieth century Sámi knowledge moved by voice: genealogies, pasture rights, route descriptions, weather signs and cosmology all travelled as narrative, and much of it is inseparable from the yoik that names its subjects. The mythological corpus is populated by *Beaivi*, the sun, whose sons the South Sámi priest Anders Fjellner rendered in the nineteenth century as an epic of the Sun's Son courting the daughter of a giant; by the great mother *Máttaráhkká* and her daughters Sáráhkká, Juksáhkká and Uksáhkká, who governed conception, birth and the threshold; by *Horagalles* the thunderer; and by the *stállu*, a large, rich and stupid ogre who is regularly outwitted by a Sámi boy, and the *čáhcerávga*, a water being that drowns the careless. Written Sámi literature begins in earnest with Johan Turi's *Muitalus sámiid birra* (1910), a reindeer herder's own account of Sámi life and the first secular book written in a Sámi language by a Sámi, and reaches its modern high point in Nils-Aslak Valkeapää's *Beaivi, áhčážan* (1988), a book-length poem interleaved with archival photographs that won the Nordic Council Literature Prize. The generations since — Rauni Magga Lukkari, Ailo Gaup, Sigbjørn Skåden, Ann-Helén Laestadius — have written the school era, the language loss and the land conflicts directly into Nordic literary mainstream.

## Language & religion

The Sámi languages form a chain of nine living members: South, Ume, Pite, Lule and North Sámi on the Scandinavian side, Inari and Skolt in Finland, and Kildin and Ter on the Kola Peninsula, with Akkala recently extinct and Ume, Pite and Ter critically endangered. North Sámi, with perhaps 20,000–25,000 speakers, carries most published literature, education and broadcasting. The vocabulary is famously fine-grained where the economy demands it — many distinct terms for snow conditions, ice states, reindeer coat colour, antler form and herd behaviour — and correspondingly thin in domains the language was long excluded from, which recent terminology work has addressed. That exclusion was policy: Norwegianisation (*fornorsking*) from the mid-nineteenth century to the mid-twentieth, with parallel programmes in Sweden and Finland, put children in boarding schools where Sámi was punished, a history examined by Norway's Truth and Reconciliation Commission, which reported in 2023. Religiously, almost all Sámi are Lutheran, and the dominant idiom is Laestadianism, the austere revival begun by Lars Levi Laestadius — himself of Sámi descent — which both gave Sámi congregations a preaching tradition in their own language and drove the yoik, the drum and dancing out of respectable life. The Skolt Sámi, resettled from Petsamo to the Sevettijärvi area after 1944, are Russian Orthodox. The pre-Christian system, with its *noaidi* mediating between worlds by drum and trance, survives mainly in missionary records, confiscated drums and place names, though the sacred geography of the *sieidi* is still recognised on the ground. Politically the Sámi are represented by three Sámi Parliaments (Finland from 1973, Norway's Sámediggi from 1989, Sweden's from 1993) and by Norway's ratification of ILO Convention 169 in 1990, the first in the world; the Alta dam protests of 1979–81 and the Norwegian Supreme Court's 2021 Fosen ruling on wind turbines in reindeer pasture bracket the modern land-rights struggle.
