## Overview

The Sámi are the Indigenous people of Sápmi, the region spanning northern Norway, Sweden and Finland and Russia's Kola Peninsula — formerly called Lapland, a name the Sámi reject along with the exonyms *Lapp* and *Laplander* in favour of their endonym *Sámit* or *Sápmelaš*. Core areas include Finnmark, the Tana and Karasjok river valleys, the Swedish mountain parishes of Jokkmokk and Gällivare, Kautokeino, Inari, and the Lofoten and Vesterålen coasts. Population estimates run to roughly 80,000–100,000. Their languages form a branch of the Uralic family, first developed south of Lake Onega and Lake Ladoga and spread northwest from there. In folk-culture terms the Sámi matter as the only Indigenous people of the European Union, with an unbroken material tradition — *duodji* — that encodes family, region and marital status in the cut of a coat and the curve of a pewter-thread line.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Gákti** — the Sámi coat, in Northern Sámi *gákti*, in Swedish and Southern Sámi usage *kolt*. Wool broadcloth (formerly reindeer hide), cut straight and gathered, with bands of coloured felt or ribbon appliqué at collar, cuffs and hem. Unlike a generic Nordic folk coat, the gákti is a legible document: the pattern, shoulder width, hem length and colour combination identify the wearer's home siida and family, so that a Kautokeino gákti is not interchangeable with a Jokkmokk one.

**Duodji** — the umbrella term for Sámi handicraft, covering both the hard crafts in reindeer antler, bone and wood and the soft crafts in hide, sinew and cloth. Duodji is not decoration applied to an object; the ornament follows the working logic of the material, so a band's proportions are set by the width of the loom card and an antler knife handle's incisions follow the grain.

**Tinntrådsbroderi / pewter-thread embroidery** — spun tin-and-lead wire (*tinntråd*, Northern Sámi *dádjá*) laid in tight scrolls onto tanned reindeer hide and couched down with sinew, used on pouches, belts, knife sheaths and the collars of hide garments. The technique is distinctively Sámi within Fennoscandia; the drawn wire is flattened, coiled around a thread core and stitched in double curves and spirals.

**Band weaving — komagbånd, vuoddagat and belt bands** — narrow warp-faced bands woven on rigid heddles or carved bone/antler band-weaving heddles. *Vuoddagat* (*komagbånd* in Norwegian) are the long shoe-bands wound over the instep and up the ankle to close the *skaller*; they are woven in red, yellow, green and blue geometric repeats and often run several metres.

**Grene weaving** — coarse woven woollen blankets and rugs from the Sámi and northern Nordic tradition, woven in broad stripes and used as bedding in the *goahti* and *lávvu* and as sledge covers.

**Beaska and luhkka** — the *beaska* is the winter overcoat of reindeer hide worn with the fur out, belted, with a high standing collar; the *luhkka* is a hooded shoulder poncho in wool that sheds snow and wind, worn over the gákti and needing no fastening.

**Koltakrage** — the collar piece of the Swedish-Sámi *kolt*, a separate ornamented band of appliqué and braid closed at the throat with a *risku* brooch.

**Motif vocabulary.** Straight and hooked bands (*bånd*) in stacked primaries; the eight-pointed star; lozenge and diamond repeats on woven bands; running double spirals and S-curves in pewter thread; chevron and zigzag courses on shoe-bands; sun and reindeer-antler figures from the drum repertoire; the concentric-rhomb "eye" on antler; ribbon-and-bar hem borders on the gákti.

### Clothing & dress

Daily and festival dress are the same garment system in different registers. Both sexes wear the *gákti*, belted at the waist; women's versions are longer and gathered, men's shorter with a flared skirt, and in some districts men's gákti carry more collar and shoulder ornament than women's. Over it goes the *luhkka* or, in winter, the fur *beaska*. Footwear is the *skaller* or *nutukas* — reindeer-hide boots with an upturned pointed toe, stuffed with dried sedge grass (*sennegress*) instead of socks and bound with woven *vuoddagat*. Belts are broad leather, tooled or studded with pewter and hung with a sheathed knife, needle case (*nállogoahti*) and pouch; a man's belt is part of his working kit, not an accessory.

Headgear is the strongest regional marker. The Kautokeino men's *four-winds hat* has four stiffened points; Swedish-Sámi men wear a round blue cap with red bands. The *ládjogahpir* — the tall "horn hat" built over a wooden crescent — was the ceremonial women's cap of the north until Laestadian clergy condemned it in the 19th century as the devil's seat; it has been revived since the 2010s as a deliberate act of reclamation. Married women in many districts wear a fringed shawl closed with a *risku*.

### Architecture

The Sámi built environment is a set of relocatable and semi-permanent forms suited to seasonal movement. The *lávvu* is the portable conical tent — a tripod of forked poles with further poles leaned in, covered formerly with reindeer hide and later with heavy cloth, with the smoke hole at the apex and the hearth directly below; the floor is divided by convention, with the *boaššu* behind the hearth as the space where food and hunting gear are kept. The *goahti* (Norwegian *gamme*) is the heavier winter and summer-settlement dwelling: a frame of paired curved poles carrying an oval, dome-like structure covered with birch bark and turf, so that it reads from outside as a low green mound. Both share the central hearth and the same internal geography. The *njalla* is the storehouse raised on one or more peeled posts, reached by a removable ladder, keeping dried meat and fish above scavengers — the single most recognisable Sámi structure in the landscape. German scorched-earth destruction in northern Norway and Finland in 1944–45 levelled nearly all standing Sámi buildings in the war zone, and Norwegian law into the 1970s limited the size of houses Sámi people could build.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Sámi are not a pottery culture; the vessel tradition is in wood, antler, bone and hide. The *guksi* (Swedish *kåsa*) is the drinking cup carved from a birch burl, its bowl following the grain of the wood and its handle often pierced or incised, sometimes inlaid with reindeer antler and carried on the belt. Reindeer-antler carving supplies knife handles, spoons, needle cases, band-weaving heddles, button toggles, *nállogoahti* needle houses and scoops, cut with fine incised geometric fields sometimes rubbed with pigment. The *nappe* is a hide bag or cover in the soft-craft repertoire. Tin and pewter appear as drawn wire and cast buttons rather than as vessels. The *runebomme* (*goavddis*/*meavrresgárri*) — the oval or bowl drum of reindeer hide painted with alder bark in red figures of sun, reindeer, *noaidi* and dwelling — is the central ritual object; most surviving examples are in museums because missionaries burned them.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The defining ornament is the *risku*, the large flat silver brooch that closes the gákti collar or shawl, its disc pierced and hung with small loose silver pendants that chime as the wearer moves; risku are worn singly or stacked several deep, and their accumulation marks wealth and inheritance. Sámi silver reached the north through trade with Norwegian and Swedish silversmiths and through tribute exchange, and the pendant-hung disc form is closer to Finno-Karelian than to Scandinavian brooch types. Contemporary *duodji* silversmiths and antler carvers make bracelets of tanned reindeer hide couched with pewter thread and closed with an antler button — the *Sámi bracelet* now sold across Sápmi. Chains, chatelaine hooks and pewter-studded belt fittings complete the set. Tattoo and henna traditions are not part of Sámi practice.

## Music & performance

Sámi vocal art is dominated by *joik* (Northern Sámi *luohti*, Southern Sámi *vuolle*, Skolt *leu'dd*), a genre with no close parallel in Europe. A joik is not a song *about* its subject but a sonic likeness *of* it: a person, a reindeer, a mountain, a lake each has its own joik, often composed for a newborn and carried for life. Joiks are typically short, cyclic, pentatonic or narrower in range, sung without fixed beginning or end and with sliding, ornamented attacks; many have no words, or only a name embedded in vocables. The Skolt Sámi *leu'dd* is more narrative, recounting biographies and family histories at length. Laestadian revivalism from the 19th century condemned joik as heathen singing, and in many districts it survived in private or moved into the hymn repertoire — one reason the loss of joik and the loss of the drum are told as the same story.

The *runebomme* drum accompanied the *noaidi*, the Sámi ritual specialist, who drummed to reach a trance state; the drum was struck with a T-shaped antler hammer and read by the movement of a brass or antler ring (*árpa*) across the painted figures. Confiscation and burning of drums during the 17th–18th-century Christianisation campaigns destroyed the practice as a living institution.

Since the 1970s joik has been the raw material of a modern Sámi music scene — Nils-Aslak Valkeapää's fusion of joik with concert composition, Mari Boine's joik-rooted songwriting, and the Sámi grand-prix song contest at the Easter festivals.

## Dance & theatre

The Sámi have no large indigenous repertoire of named set dances; the performance tradition is vocal and narrative, and ring and couple dances current in Sápmi are largely shared Nordic and Finnish forms adopted through church and market gatherings. Skolt Sámi communities on the Finnish side retained a distinct social dance culture in which the *leu'dd* singer and the dancers occupy the same evening event. The modern theatrical tradition is young and consequential: *Beaivváš Sámi Nášunálateáhter*, founded in Kautokeino in 1981, works in Northern Sámi and stages joik-driven productions, and the Sámi presence at Riddu Riđđu and other Indigenous festivals has built a shared circumpolar performance circuit.

## Festivals & rituals

The Sámi festival year hangs on the reindeer calendar and on the church calendar fused to it. The great gathering is **Easter week in Kautokeino** (*Sámi Easter Festival*) and at Karasjok, when herders arrive before the spring migration for weddings, confirmations, the reindeer-racing championships, lasso-throwing competitions, the Sámi Grand Prix song contest and the market — the single largest concentration of gákti in the world. **Jokkmokks marknad** in Swedish Sápmi, held the first Thursday of February since 1605 and thus one of Europe's oldest continuous winter markets, is the equivalent midwinter event, with reindeer caravans and a duodji fair in −30 °C. **Sámi National Day** falls on 6 February, the anniversary of the first pan-Sámi congress at Trondheim in 1917; the flag and the anthem *Sámi soga lávlla*, both adopted in August 1986, are raised across all four countries. **Riddu Riđđu** at Manndalen in July is the summer Indigenous arts festival.

Life-cycle rites run through the Lutheran church but keep Sámi content: a child receives a personal joik and a name inherited from a relative; baptism gowns and confirmation gákti are made new by the family's own duodji makers; a wedding brings the full silver, with stacked *risku* and the bride's shawl. The autumn reindeer separation (*gárdi*), when herds are gathered into corrals, marked and divided between owners, functions as a working ritual and a social season. Pre-Christian practice centred on the *sieidi* — a natural rock or stone formation given offerings of antler, bone and fat — and on the sacred mountains and lakes of each siida; many sieidi sites are known and respected even where offerings stopped generations ago.

## Foodways

Sámi cooking is meat, fish and dairy with almost no grain of its own — the plague of 1349 spared the interior Sámi partly because they ate reindeer and fish rather than the wheat and rye that carried plague fleas north in barrels. Reindeer is the centre: *suovas*, cold-smoked and salted reindeer meat hung in the smoke of the *goahti*; *gurpi*, minced reindeer and fat wrapped in fat membrane or intestine and smoked; *bidos*, the reindeer stew with potato and carrot that is the wedding and festival dish of Finnmark; dried strips (*goikebiergu*); and reindeer blood used in blood pancakes and sausage. Marrow bones are split and the marrow eaten as a delicacy; the tongue and heart are prized rather than discarded. Coastal Sea Sámi cooking runs on cod, halibut, salmon and Arctic char, dried and wind-cured on racks, plus cod liver and roe. *Bidos* and boiled reindeer are ladled from a single pot with a shared ladle.

The land supplies the rest: cloudberries (*luomi*), lingonberries and crowberries eaten with reindeer milk products or preserved in fat; Angelica stalks and sorrel as the two significant plant foods; and *gáhkku* / *láibi*, the unleavened flatbread baked on a pan over the hearth, eaten with butter and dried meat. Coffee is the fixed social ritual — boiled strong in a kettle over the fire, drunk from the *guksi*, offered to any arrival, sometimes with a piece of cheese dropped into the cup.

## Oral tradition & literature

Sámi oral tradition has no single long epic; its backbone is the *máinnas* — the folktale — and the joik, which does narrative work that elsewhere falls to sung epic. The most widespread cycle concerns the *Stállu* (*Stallo*), a large, slow, dangerous ogre-like being outwitted by a clever Sámi, and the *Čáhcerávga*, the water-being that drowns the careless. Tales of the **Čudit** — hostile raiders from the east — anchor a historical memory of raiding, and the Kautokeino tale of the boy who leads pursuing raiders over a cliff became the source of Nils Gaup's 1987 film *Ofelaš* (*Pathfinder*). Place-name lore is itself a genre: fishing grounds and seafloor landmarks were named topographically and the knowledge transmitted generationally, so that the naming system doubled as the navigational chart.

The modern literature is inseparable from the political revival. Johan Turi's *Muitalus sámiid birra* (1910) was the first secular book in a Sámi language by a Sámi author. Nils-Aslak Valkeapää's *Beaivi, áhčážan* (1988) won the Nordic Council Literature Prize. Elin Anna Labba's account of the 1919–20 forced displacements of the northern Sámi appeared in English in 2023 as *The Rocks Will Echo Our Sorrow*.

## Language & religion

The Sámi languages are a branch of Uralic, comprising nine or ten written languages in three groups: Western (South, Ume, Pite, Lule, Northern), Eastern (Inari, Skolt, Kildin, Ter) and the extinct Akkala. Northern Sámi has by far the most speakers; Pite and Ume have a handful each; Ter Sámi is effectively gone. All use Latin script with extended characters, standardised in the 20th century. School language bans — seven compulsory years from 1889 in Norway, and Norwegianisation policies at their harshest between 1900 and 1940 — broke transmission for entire generations, and the current position is one of active recovery through language nests and Sámi-medium schools.

Religion is Lutheran, strongly shaped by **Laestadianism**, the revival movement founded by Lars Levi Laestadius in the 1840s, which reached deep into Sámi communities and simultaneously preserved Sámi-language preaching and suppressed joik, the drum and the *ládjogahpir*. Older cosmology — the *noaidi*, the drum, the *sieidi* offering-stone, the bear-burial rites, and deities including *Beaivi* the sun and *Máttaráhkká* with her daughters — survives as documented tradition, place-name evidence and, increasingly, as reclaimed practice.
