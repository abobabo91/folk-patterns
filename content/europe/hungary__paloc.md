## Overview

The Palóc are a subgroup of Hungarians living in Northern Hungary and southern Slovakia. They have retained distinctive traditions, including a divergent dialect of Hungarian, while generally being regarded as ethnic Hungarians. Their settlement area includes the Ipoly Valley, the Cserhát, Mátra and Bükk mountains, and the basin north of these areas. Palócföld, or Palócland, formerly belonged to Hont and Gömör and Kishont counties and now extends partly across Slovak and Hungarian areas, including Pest, Nógrád, Heves and Borsod-Abaúj-Zemplén counties. The region includes nearly 150 settlements. The village of Hollókő was proclaimed a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987 for preserving traditional Palóc architecture and land use.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Májfa** — The installation of the *Májfa* is identified as a famous Palóc tradition. Fáy Elek argued that it had an Avar origin and compared it with a similar Avar ritual; the source states that the custom became popular throughout the region in the early 16th century.

**Motif vocabulary.** “Sun cross,” the circle “variga” or “vár,” and folk motifs associated with the Sun and Fire are named in discussions of Palóc and Avar symbolism.

### Architecture

The village of Hollókő was proclaimed a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987 because of the preservation of traditional Palóc architecture and land use.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The only named object is a set of mother-of-pearl knives recorded in 1656 by the notary from Nagykőrös, who wrote: “I bought ten pairs of mother of pearl knives from the Palócs.”

## Festivals & rituals

The *Májfa* installation is identified as a famous Palóc tradition. Fáy Elek argued that it had an Avar origin because the Avars practiced a ritual with a similar meaning. The source states that a comparable tradition was not found among other ethnic groups in the region until the early 16th century, when it became popular throughout the region.

The sources also describe Palóc folk customs as combining remnants of ancient inner Asian beliefs with Christianity. They refer to traditions concerning the Sun and Fire and to remnants of Palóc paganism, which Pintér Sándor associated with Avar or Hunnic religion.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Palóc folk legend of the *Palóc Herceg*, or “Palóc Prince,” is named as support for a theory of Palóc connection with the Avars. The sources also mention Palóc folklore, folk symbolism, superstitions, folk motifs and traditions concerning the Sun and Fire.

Kálmán Mikszáth gave new prominence to the Palóc in 1882 through his work *The Good People of Palóc*.

## Language & religion

The Palóc are generally regarded as ethnic Hungarians and have retained a distinctive, highly divergent dialect of Hungarian. The sources state that they can be further grouped according to dialect. They describe western and eastern, or Barkó, Palócs, whose customs include remnants of ancient inner Asian beliefs and Christianity.

The sources discuss proposed connections with the Khazar, Kabar, Pecheneg, Cuman and Avar tribes. They also describe interpretations linking Palóc folk symbolism and traditions to Avar or Hunnic religion, particularly through symbols and practices associated with the Sun and Fire. These interpretations are presented through the work of Bakó Ferenc, Magyar Adorján, Fáy Elek and Pintér Sándor.
