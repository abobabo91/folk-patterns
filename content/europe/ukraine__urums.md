## Overview

Urums are Turkic-speaking Greek Orthodox people native to Crimea, northeastern Turkey and Transcaucasia. Their identity emerged and developed from the 13th to the 17th centuries through the interaction of Crimean Greeks, Greek-speaking Crimean Alans and Crimean Goths, and other indigenous groups. In Ukraine, the North Azovian Urums live in the region adjacent to Crimea, around Mariupol and in present-day Donetsk Oblast. They are distinguished from the Greek-speaking Romaioi, whose dialect is known as Rumeíka or Mariupol Greek. The North Azovian Urums speak a Turkic language belonging to the West Kipchak branch, while the Tsalka Urums of Georgia speak an Oghuz Turkic language. The sources identify them as an important example of Greek Orthodox communities whose languages and collective identities developed through long contact with Turkic-speaking populations.

## Oral tradition & literature

A legend common among the Urums of Georgia states that, before leaving Turkey, Orthodox Greeks were forced to choose between their language and their faith; they chose their Orthodox faith and relinquished their language. The article describes this account as a myth according to most historians.

The sources mention a documentation project on the language of the Caucasus Urum people. It compiled a basic lexicon, sample translations for grammatical study and a text collection, and its website contains further information about the language and language community.

## Language & religion

Urum identity includes several Turkic-speaking Greek Orthodox groups. North Azovian Urum belongs to the West Kipchak branch of the Turkic languages and is considered by some to be a dialect of Crimean Tatar. It has been written with the Greek alphabet, with Yañalif between 1927 and 1937, and with Cyrillic after 1940. Tsalka Urum belongs to the Oghuz branch and resembles Anatolian Turkish dialects while also showing similarities with Russian.

North Azovian Urum religious vocabulary includes Turkic-origin words such as *Allah*, meaning “God,” and *cänäm*, meaning “hell.” The Tsalka Urums call their language *bizim dilja*, “our language,” or *moussourmanja*, “Muslims’ language.” The majority of Tsalka Urums now speak Russian. Urums generally practice Eastern Orthodox Christianity, although there are no liturgical practices in the Urum language. The sources report that 60% of native Urum speakers surveyed used Urum in prayer.
