## Overview

Serbs of Montenegro, also called Montenegrin Serbs, are a recognized ethnic minority in Montenegro. According to the 2023 census, 205,370 people identified as ethnic Serbs, representing 32.9% of the population and making them the country’s second-largest ethnic group after Montenegrins. A further 0.5% identified as either “Serbs-Montenegrins” or “Montenegrins-Serbs.” Serbian was declared the mother tongue by 269,307 people, or 43.2% of the population. Serbs in Montenegro belong to Eastern Orthodoxy and are adherents of the Serbian Orthodox Church. Their cultural identity is connected in the source material with Serbian historical traditions, Orthodox institutions, Montenegro’s political history, and debates over Montenegrin and Serbian ethnic and linguistic identity.

### Clothing & dress

The sources used document one item of dress: the *Montenegrin cap*, worn by both Montenegrins and Montenegrin Serbs. It originally had the shape of a flat cylinder with a red upper surface called *tepeluk*. According to the source, Prince-Bishop Petar II Petrović Njegoš surrounded it with a black rim called *derevija*. The cap was wholly red before this change. The black rim was interpreted as a sign of grief for occupied Kosovo, while the red surface was associated with the bloody defeat at the Battle of Kosovo. The source also describes five small stripes on the top as representing the remaining parts of the once greater Serbian realm. Within the stripes is an angled six-pointed star representing Montenegro as the last free part. A version bearing the Serbian cross in place of the star became popular among ordinary people during the growth of nationalism.

### Architecture

They do identify religious buildings and monasteries associated with Serbian Orthodox history, including the monastery of Holy Archangel Michael on Prevlaka, the Đurđevi Stupovi Monastery, Morača, Praskvica, Vranjina, and the 17th-century Ostrog Monastery. The source states that the Ostrog Monastery is the single most visited pilgrimage destination of the Serbian Orthodox Church worldwide.

## Festivals & rituals

It does describe several politically and historically significant events. The unification of Serbia and Montenegro was proclaimed in 1918 by the Podgorica Assembly, and the Christmas Uprising followed it. On November 26, 1918, the Great People’s Assembly, later known as the Podgorica Assembly, convened in Podgorica and formalized Montenegro’s incorporation into Serbia and the new Yugoslav state, declared on December 1, 1918. An insurrection followed in 1919. These events are presented as parts of political history rather than as recurring folk festivals or life-cycle rituals.

The source also describes the 2006 Montenegrin independence referendum, whose result was 55.5% for independence and 44.5% against. In 2019 and 2020, protests were held against the Law on Freedom of Religion or Belief and the Legal Status of Religious Communities; the law was repealed in 2021.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do identify the Kosovo Myth as an important element of collective identity. According to the source, the myth became entrenched in the collective psyche during the periods of the Prince-Bishopric of Montenegro, the Principality of Montenegro, and the Kingdom of Montenegro. The source also records Milovan Đilas’s description of Montenegro as the spiritual homeland of Serbs, the place where the soul of the Serbs resides, and the cradle of Serbian myths and aspirations for the unification of Serbs.

The source material further describes the role of the Montenegrin cap in expressing historical memory: its black rim was associated with grief for occupied Kosovo, its red surface with the Battle of Kosovo, and its stripes with the remnants of the Serbian realm. No literary works, preservation projects, or contemporary revival movements are named.

## Language & religion

Serbs in Montenegro speak Serbian, and 269,307 people, or 43.2% of the population, declared Serbian their mother tongue in the 2023 census. The source identifies two sub-dialects of the Shtokavian dialect of the Serbian language in Montenegro: the Eastern Herzegovinian dialect in the western half of the country and the Zeta-Raška dialect in the eastern half. Serbian was the official language in Montenegro until 2007. The Constitution of Montenegro then designated Montenegrin as the sole official language while Serbian received the status of a language in official use.

Serbs in Montenegro belong to Eastern Orthodoxy and adhere to the Serbian Orthodox Church. Four eparchies of that church have jurisdiction over Montenegro: the Metropolitanate of Montenegro and the Littoral, the Eparchy of Budimlja and Nikšić, the Eparchy of Mileševa, and the Eparchy of Zachlumia, Herzegovina, and the Littoral. The Montenegrin Orthodox Church remains canonically unrecognized.
