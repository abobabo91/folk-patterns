## Overview

The Prussian Lithuanians, or *Lietuvininkai* (singular: *Lietuvininkas*), are Lithuanians who formerly inhabited northeastern East Prussia, a territory known as Prussian Lithuania or Lithuania Minor. Their historical area lay between the rivers Alle and Neman and included places such as Königsberg, Memel, Tilsit, Ragnit, Karalene and Insterburg. Unlike most Lithuanians, most Prussian Lithuanians became Lutheran-Protestants after the Reformation, while their language remained Lithuanian. The Prussian census of 1890 recorded 121,345 speakers of Lithuanian. Prussian Lithuanians contributed substantially to written Lithuanian, and Lithuanian-language literature appeared earlier in the Duchy of Prussia than in the Grand Duchy of Lithuania. Their culture developed through contact with German language and institutions while preserving Lithuanian traditions and folk culture.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources do state that Prussian Lithuanians preserved Lithuanian traditions and folk culture while adopting cultural values and social conventions associated with the German state.

### Architecture

They record that Prussian Lithuanians lived in villages until the mid-19th century and that those who moved to Königsberg and Memel usually became bilingual and eventually Germanized. They also state that churches and cemeteries in the former Memel Territory were destroyed after World War II.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

One household practice is recorded: portraits of the rulers of the House of Hohenzollern were widely hung in homes.

## Music & performance

The Pietist congregational movement was important among Prussian Lithuanians. Evangelical fellowships, called *Stundenhalter* in German and *Surinkimininkai* in Lithuanian, were active in Prussia, and about 40 percent of Lithuanians belonged to such fellowships. Their members lived according to ascetic principles.

Church services for Prussian Lithuanians were held in the Lithuanian language by the will of Albert after he became duke of Prussia and converted to Protestantism. Lithuanian-language church services in Tilsit and Ragnit continued until the evacuation of East Prussia in late 1944. Pastors including A. Keleris, J. Pauperas, M. Preikšaitis, O. Stanaitis, A. Trakis and J. Urdse later gathered people from Lithuanian parishes and reorganised the Lithuanian church in the western zones of Allied-occupied Germany.

## Dance & theatre

They mention the poem *The Seasons* by Kristijonas Donelaitis and the activity of Lithuanian churches and evangelical fellowships.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record religious divisions and practices. Most Prussian Lithuanians became Lutheran-Protestants, and religious belief was one of the three principal criteria of self-identification, alongside loyalty to the German state and the mother tongue.

Evangelical fellowships known as *Stundenhalter* or *Surinkimininkai* attracted large numbers of Prussian Lithuanians. Their members followed ascetic principles. Church services in the Lithuanian language were held in Prussian Lithuanian communities, including services in Tilsit and Ragnit until late 1944.

## Foodways

The sources mention that villagers were fed on unwinnowed bread and pallid buttermilk in lines from Kristijonas Donelaitis’s *The Seasons*, but they do not present these as a documented food system or describe their preparation and social use.

## Oral tradition & literature

Prussian Lithuanian literary culture is especially associated with Lithuanian-language writing. The first book in Lithuanian was published in Königsberg in 1547 by Martynas Mažvydas, an émigré from the Grand Duchy of Lithuania. The first Lithuanian book in the Grand Duchy of Lithuania was printed in 1596 by Mikalojus Daukša. The first major Lithuanian poet, Kristijonas Donelaitis, came from East Prussia and represented the Prussian Lithuanian lifestyle in his works.

Donelaitis’s poem *The Seasons* depicts the life and outlook of Prussian Lithuanian villagers. It criticizes the adoption of German ways when these are associated with decadent noblemen and urges Lithuanians to work, fulfil their duties and avoid idleness. The poem reflects the feudal mentality of the largely rural population before the mid-19th century.

Other writers associated with Lithuanian-language literature in Prussia include Michael Märlin, Jakob Quandt, Wilhelm Martinius, Gottfried Ostermeyer, Sigfried Ostermeyer, Daniel Klein, Andrew Krause, Philipp Ruhig, Matttheus Praetorius, Christian Mielcke, Adam Schimmelpfennig and others. The first newspaper in Lithuanian, *Nuſidawimai apie Ewangēliôs Praſiplatinima tarp Źydû ir Pagonû*, was published by Prussian Lithuanians.

## Language & religion

Prussian Lithuanians were originally Lithuanian-language speakers and, since the end of the 18th and beginning of the 19th century, were typically bilingual. Their spoken German belonged to the Low Prussian dialect of Low German, specifically the *Mundart des Ostgebietes* subdialect, while High German was used in institutions. Their Lithuanian language included Samogitian and Aukštaitian dialects and contained more German loanwords and translations than the Lithuanian spoken in the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, which was more strongly influenced by Polish and Ruthenian.

Prussian Lithuanian orthography followed the German style rather than the Polish-based style used in the Grand Duchy. Prussian Lithuanians used German Gothic script; the letters ſ, ß and ʒ appeared in their writing, and nouns began with capital letters. The first Lithuanian book was published in Königsberg in 1547. The Prussian Lithuanian newspaper *Naujaſis Tilźes Keleiwis* continued in Gothic style until 1940.

Most Prussian Lithuanians became Lutheran-Protestants, while most Lithuanians elsewhere remained Roman Catholic. Religious difference contributed to antagonism between the two communities.
