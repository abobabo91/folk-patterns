## Overview

The Transylvanian Saxons are a Germanic people who settled in Transylvania in successive waves from the 12th-century Ostsiedlung to the mid-19th century. Their ancestors came mainly from the Low Countries, Alsace, the Moselle and Lorraine river valleys, and Luxembourg, with later settlers from regions including Thuringia and Bavaria. Their settlements developed around places such as Hermannstadt, today’s Sibiu, the Nösnerland around Bistritz, today’s Bistrița, and the Weinland near Mediasch, today’s Mediaș. Their native dialect, Transylvanian Saxon, is closely related to Luxembourgish. The population has declined through emigration; the 2011 Romanian census recorded 11,400 people of Transylvanian Saxon descent, while the article states that approximately 12,000 remained in Romania at another point in its historical overview. Their folk-culture significance is especially visible in fortified churches, medieval settlement forms, and the survival of a distinctive German dialect.

## Material culture

### Architecture

**Kirchenburgen** are fortified churches built by Transylvanian Saxon rural communities. They combined religious and defensive functions, with extensive inner and outer walls and fortified watch towers intended to protect settlements during foreign attacks. In standard German they are also called **Wehrkirchen**. The sources state that approximately 300 villages had such fortified churches in the Middle Ages, and that more than 150 villages in southeastern Transylvania still contain fortified churches in good shape. Seven are included in the UNESCO World Heritage under the name Villages with fortified churches in Transylvania.

The towns were also fortified after the Mongol invasion of 1241–42. Stone castles and economically developing urban settlements became characteristic of the medieval landscape. The historical towns associated with the name Siebenbürgen include Nösen or Bistritz, Hermannstadt, Klausenburg, Kronstadt, Mediasch, Mühlbach, and Schässburg or Schäßburg. Other notable urban Saxon settlements included Heltau, Rosenau, and Reps.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record religious and institutional developments: the earliest religious organization of the Saxons was the Provostship of Hermannstadt, founded on 20 December 1191, and the first superintendent of the Transylvanian Saxons’ Lutheran Church, Paul Wiener, was elected at a synod on 6 February 1553. Under the influence of Johannes Honterus, the great majority embraced the creed of Martin Luther during the Protestant Reformation.

## Foodways

They state only that the settlers were charged with developing agriculture and bringing more agriculture to the region.

## Oral tradition & literature

They mention a common interpretation of the Pied Piper of Hamelin tale, dated to 26 June 1284, according to which the story could relate to an emigration event connected with the Ostsiedlung; a minor alternative theory suggests settlement in Transylvania. The article also records a statement by former federal German president and professor doctor Theodor Heuss that the history of the Transylvanian Saxons is “a piece of German history as a whole.”

## Language & religion

Transylvanian Saxon is the group’s native dialect and is close to Luxembourgish; it is also described as sharing many lexical similarities with Ripuarian. The medieval language spoken by the settlers became locally known as *Såksesch*, more fully *Siweberjesch-Såksesch*. Local names include *Såksen* or *Soxen* in Transylvanian Saxon, *Soxn* or *Soxisch* among the Transylvanian Landler, *Sachsen* in German, *sași* in Romanian, *(erdélyi) szászok* in Hungarian, and Саси in Bulgarian.

The Transylvanian Saxons were initially strongly Roman Catholic. After the Protestant Reformation, almost all became Lutheran Protestants, with very few Calvinists, while smaller segments remained Catholic. The Lutheran Church in Transylvania became a de facto *Volkskirche*, or “national church,” of the Transylvanian Saxons.
