## Overview

The Istro-Romanians (Istro Romanian: *rumeri* or *rumâri*) are a Romance ethnic group native to or associated with the Istrian Peninsula. Historically, they also inhabited the western side of the island of Krk until 1875. Today they are reduced to eight settlements on the Croatian side of Istria, where they do not form the majority. Their language belongs to the Eastern Romance family alongside Romanian, Aromanian and Megleno-Romanian, all descending from Proto-Romanian. The group has experienced assimilation, emigration and severe demographic decline. Its folk-culture significance lies in the survival of a distinctive language, village-based identities, costumes, dances, songs, pastoral history and a small Istro-Romanian literature.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that Istro-Romanian culture has costumes, dances and songs with many similarities to those of Romania.

### Architecture

They state that houses and especially churches in Istro-Romanian villages were destroyed during the last phase of the Second World War, and that Žejane was occupied by German-Italian forces on 5 May 1944, when many houses and farms were burned.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do record that Istro-Romanians historically used caravans to sell dairy products and transport other goods, and that they traded cheese and salt.

## Music & performance

The sources state that Istro-Romanian culture includes songs. No specific wedding, funeral, religious or community performance tradition is described.

The language itself has been preserved in written and spoken forms through a small literary tradition. Ireneo della Croce recorded Istro-Romanian in a work about Trieste from 1698, including thirteen single nouns, eight nouns with determiners and two sentences with Italian translations. This was described as the first attestation of the language apart from earlier toponyms and personal names. The first book entirely in Istro-Romanian was *Calindaru lu rumeri din Istrie* (“Calendar of the Romanians of Istria”), written by Andrei Glavina in collaboration with Constantin Diculescu and published in 1905. In 1928, Alexandru Leca Morariu published *Lu frati noștri: libru lu rumeri din Istrie* (“To our brothers: book of the Romanians of Istria”).

## Dance & theatre

The sources state that Istro-Romanian culture includes dances with many similarities to those of Romania.

## Festivals & rituals

Religious and communal institutions appear in the historical record. During Austro-Hungarian rule, church services were given in Latin and Croatian, and the sources state that priests hindered the cultural development of parishioners. In the Italian period, Andrei Glavina promoted Istro-Romanian identity through education and public activity. He led the school of Frascati-Susgnevizza, named *Împăratul Traian*, and worked to preserve the language and culture of the Istro-Romanians.

## Foodways

The sources connect Istro-Romanian livelihood and foodways mainly with pastoralism, dairy production, agriculture and trade. Historically, the people were peasants and shepherds. During the 14th century, they used caravans to sell dairy products and transport other goods. In Ragusan trade, *caseus vlachescus* or *vlachiscus*, identified in a document from 1357 as *brença* (“cheese”), was important enough to serve as a payment method, with its price set by the authorities. They also traded salt on the Adriatic coast.

The sources mention *copra* (“goat”) and *lapte* (“milk”) among the Istro-Romanian words recorded by Ireneo della Croce in 1698. In the 19th century, pastoralism declined, zootechnics were neglected and agriculture was poorly productive. Istro-Romanians cultivated vineyards, but the vines were destroyed by the grape phylloxera bug. They also cultivated culinary sage, especially in Šušnjevica, as a means of increasing income.

## Oral tradition & literature

Istro-Romanian popular stories describe the arrival of the people in Istria during the Middle Ages. Local legends speak of seven caravans coming from Transylvania: six settled south of the Učka and one north of it. The sources present these stories as part of the discussion of Istro-Romanian origins, alongside competing scholarly theories, none of which is universally accepted.

Istro-Romanian literature is small. The first book was published in 1905, when Andrei Glavina and Constantin Diculescu produced *Calindaru lu rumeri din Istrie*. Morariu’s *Lu frati noștri: libru lu rumeri din Istrie* followed in 1928. Preservation efforts have included associations and projects supported by the Croatian and Romanian governments, although the language has little use today in education, media and religion.

## Language & religion

Istro-Romanian is an Eastern Romance language, closely similar to Romanian and related to Aromanian and Megleno-Romanian. Speakers use several local names. Those from Šušnjevica use *șușnevți* for the people and *șușnevski* or *šušnjevski* for the language; people from Nova Vas use *novosani* and *novosanski* or *novošånski*; those from Kostrčani use *costărčånți*; and people from the Brdo area use *brijånski*. In the villages south of the Učka mountain range, *vlåš* and *vlaška* or *vlaški* are also used. In Žejane, *jeianți* or *žejånci* denotes the people, while *jeianski*, *žejanski* and *po jeianski cuvinta* refer to the language.

The term “Istro-Romanian” was created in the middle of the 19th century and is not used by the people themselves for self-identification. They state that religious services were given in Latin and Croatian, while Croatian later became dominant in education, media, religion and other domains.
