## Overview

The Kashubians—also called Cassubians or Kashubs—are a West Slavic ethnic group native to the historical region of Pomerania, including Pomerelia, in north-central Poland. Their settlement area is known as Kashubia, whose largest city is Gdańsk (Kashubian: Gduńsk), while Gdynia contains the largest proportion of people declaring Kashubian origin. Kashubian is a separate language closely related to Polish and is classified within the Lechitic West Slavic languages, although its status as a language or dialect remains debated. Estimates of the Kashubian population range from approximately 500,000 to 567,000, while the 2021 census recorded 179,685 people in Poland claiming Kashubian ethnic-national identity. Kashubian identity is expressed through language, agriculture and fishing, pottery, embroidery, seasonal customs, and regional foodways.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Kashubian embroidery* is identified as a famous Kashubian craft, and the Kashubian embroidering Zukowo school is described as important intangible cultural heritage.

*Pottery* is described as one of the ancient Kashubian crafts that has survived to the present day.

### Architecture

The sources state that old Kashubian culture has partially survived in architecture.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery is identified as an ancient Kashubian craft that has survived to the present day. The sources also mention amber-working, sculpturing, glasspainting, and plaiting among folk crafts in which old Kashubian culture has partially survived.

## Music & performance

They mention Kashubian-language religious songbooks and biblical publications connected with the Lutheran Church in the 16th and 17th centuries. Simon Krofey, a pastor in Bütow, published a religious song book in 1586, written in Polish but containing some Kashubian words. Michael Brüggemann, also known as Pontanus or Michał Mostnik, published a Polish translation of works of Martin Luther containing Kashubian elements. Other biblical texts were published in 1700 by J. M. Sporgius, pastor in Schmolsin.

The sources also identify later literary and cultural organizations connected with the preservation of Kashubian expression. Aleksander Majkowski founded the Society of Young Kashubians in 1912 and started the newspaper Gryf. The Young Kashubian movement was associated with the paper Zrzësz Kaszëbskô and the “Zrzëszincë” group, which contributed to the development of Kashubian literary language. No instrumental tradition or performance context is supplied.

## Festivals & rituals

Kashubian seasonal customs include practices associated with Palm Sunday and Easter Monday. Because palm leaves were not obtainable in Kashubia, pussy willows were used as an alternative in Palm Sunday celebrations. Priests blessed the branches, after which parishioners whipped each other with them while saying *Wierzba bije, jô nie bijã. Za tidzéń wiôldżi dzéń, za nocë trzë i trzë są Jastrë*, translated in the source as “The willow strikes, it’s not me who strikes, in a week, on the great day, and in three and three nights, there is the Easter.”

The blessed pussy willows were treated as sacred charms. They were believed to prevent lightning strikes, protect animals, encourage honey production, and bring health and good fortune. Swallowing one pussy willow bud on Palm Sunday was traditional and was associated with good health.

On Easter Monday, Kashub boys traditionally chased girls and gently whipped their legs with juniper twigs. This was intended to bring good fortune in love. The practice could be accompanied by the chant *Dyngus, dyngus – pò dwa jaja, Nie chcã chleba, leno jaja*, translated as “Dyngus, dyngus, for two eggs; I don’t want bread but eggs.” Girls gave boys painted eggs.

## Foodways

Kashubian cuisine contains elements from the wider European culinary tradition. The sources name several local specialities. *Czarnina*, also called *Czarwina*, is a soup made from goose blood. *Brzadowô zupa* is a sweet soup made with ingredients such as apples. *Kaszëbsczi kùch marchewny* is Kashubian carrot cake. The sources also list *Plińce* and *Prażnica* as local dishes.

No UNESCO inscription concerning Kashubian foodways is identified in the supplied material.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe several Kashubian literary and language-preservation efforts. Florian Ceynowa documented Kashubian alphabet and grammar by 1879 and published a collection of ethnographic-historic stories about Kashubian life, *Skórb kaszébsko-slovjnckjé mòvé*, in 1866–1868. He is identified as the first activist of the Kashubian national movement.

Hieronim Derdowski was an early writer in Kashubian. His writing included the statement “There is no Cassubia without Polonia, and no Poland without Cassubia,” which later Kashubian activists used in discussions of Kashubian and Polish identity. Aleksander Majkowski led the Young Kashubian movement and wrote for *Zrzësz Kaszëbskô* as part of the “Zrzëszincë” group. Bernard Sychta, who lived from 1907 to 1982, is identified as another important Kashubian writer.

They do, however, identify the collection of ethnographic-historic stories published by Ceynowa as part of the written preservation of Kashubian culture.

## Language & religion

Kashubian is a West Slavic language closely related to Polish. From a diachronic perspective, it is grouped with Slovincian, Polabian, and Polish as a Lechitic West Slavic language; from a synchronic perspective, it is sometimes treated as a group of Polish dialects. Its classification remains controversial, and no standard Kashubian language exists. Instead, several dialects are spoken, differing significantly from one another, with vocabulary influenced by German and Polish.

Kashubian is legally protected in Poland as an official regional language. It received this status through an act of the Polish Parliament on 6 January 2005. In the 2021 Population Census, about 87,600 people declared Kashubian as the language used at home, compared with 108,100 in the 2011 Census.

Pomerelia retained Polish as the church language through its connection with the Kuyavian Diocese of Leslau. The Slovincians adopted Lutheranism in 1534, while Kashubes in Pomerelia remained Roman Catholic. Kashubian was introduced into the Lutheran Church through religious songbooks and biblical texts associated with Simon Krofey, Michael Brüggemann, and J. M. Sporgius.
