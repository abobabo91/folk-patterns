## Overview

The Udmurts (Udmurt: Удмуртъёс, Udmurtjos) are a Permian (Finno-Ugric) ethnic group in Eastern Europe who speak the Udmurt language. They mainly live in the republic of Udmurtia in Russia, with smaller groups in Kirov Oblast, Perm Krai, Bashkortostan, Tatarstan, and Mari El. The Russian Census reported 552,299 Udmurts in 2010, down from 637,000 in 2002 and 746,562 in 1989; Udmurts make up 24.1% of the population of Udmurtia. Formerly semi-nomadic forest dwellers living in riverside communities, most Udmurts now live in towns. Their folk-culture significance in the sources includes the national epic *Dorvyzhy*, distinctive musical instruments, clan-based cultural traces, and a red-haired people’s festival held annually in Izhevsk since 2004.

### Architecture

The sources state that Udmurts formerly lived in riverside communities and were semi-nomadic forest dwellers.

## Music & performance

The Udmurts’ national musical instruments include the *krez*, a zither similar to the Russian gusli, and the *chipchirghan*, a pipe-like wind instrument. A chapter in the 1776 book *Description de toutes les nations de l'empire de Russie* describes the Wotyak people, and James George Frazer mentions a rite performed by the people in *The Golden Bough*, but the supplied material does not provide details of the musical or performative practices involved.

## Festivals & rituals

A festival celebrating red-haired people has been held annually in Izhevsk since 2004. The sources also state that James George Frazer mentions a rite performed by the people in *The Golden Bough*. No further annual festival calendar or life-cycle rites are covered by the supplied sources.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Udmurts have a national epic called *Dorvyzhy*. The supplied material also lists Udmurt fairy-tale publications, including *Сто сказок удмуртского народа* [A hundred fairy tales from the Udmurt people], published in Ижевск by the Удмуртское книжное издательство in 1961, and *Udmurtskije narodnyje skazki*, published in Izevsk in 1948. Further discussion of Udmurt folk beliefs and fairy tales appears in Isidor Levin’s 1962 study of the Volkserzählungen der Wotjaken (Udmurten) and in Galina Shushakova’s 1995 discussion of earthly and unearthly worlds in Udmurt fairy-tales.

## Language & religion

The Udmurt language belongs to the Uralic family. The sources identify the Udmurts as a Permian (Finno-Ugric) ethnic group and state that they are closely related to Komis to their north, both linguistically and culturally. The supplied material does not describe Udmurt dialects, historical scripts, the current religious landscape, syncretism, or specific spiritual practices. It states only that James George Frazer mentions a rite performed by the people in *The Golden Bough*.
