## Overview

The Veps, or Vepsians, are a Baltic Finnic people who speak Vepsian, a Finnic language in the Uralic language family. In modern times they live between Lake Ladoga, Lake Onega and Lake Beloye, in the Russian Republic of Karelia, Leningrad Oblast and Vologda Oblast. Their settlements include areas along the Oyat River and districts around the Prionezhsky, Podporozhsky, Lodeynopolsky, Tikhvinsky, Boksitogorsky, Vytegorsky and Babayevsky districts. The 2002 Russian census recorded 8,240 Veps in Russia; the 2001 Ukrainian census recorded 281 Veps in Ukraine, of whom 11 spoke Vepsian. Their historical importance in folk culture is closely connected with language preservation, rural communities, folklore collection and the development of Vepsian writing and education.

## Material culture

### Architecture

 They do record a Vepsian rural community formed in East Karelia in 1994, encompassing 8,200 square kilometers of land and 3,373 inhabitants, 42% of them Vepsian.

## Music & performance

 They do identify Ryurik Lonin as a collector of Veps folklore and writer. The sources also record a broader cultural and linguistic revival: early Soviet nationality politics supported Vepsian national development, the alphabet and written language were developed, and teachers began instructing in Vepsian in some elementary schools. These details concern education and cultural preservation rather than musical performance.

## Festivals & rituals

 They state that Soviet authorities began to oppress Vepsian culture in 1937, after which all national activities were stopped and the national districts were abolished. The sources also record later institutional efforts connected with cultural preservation, including the formation of the Vepsian rural community in East Karelia in 1994 and the granting of some budgetary autonomy by the authorities of the Republic of Karelia in 1996. The federal authorities abolished that autonomy in 2006.

## Oral tradition & literature

Veps folklore is represented in the sources through Ryurik Lonin, described as a collector of Veps folklore and writer. They do record a modern written and publishing history. The Vepsian primer *Abekirj* and other elementary school books were published in Petrozavodsk in 1991, while *Kodima*, a newspaper in Vepsian, has been published since 1993. Nikolay Abramov is identified as a Vepsian-language poet, translator and writer. The sources also identify Raisa Lardot as a Veps-born Finnish writer.

## Language & religion

Vepsian belongs to the Finnic branch of the Uralic languages; the article also places it in the Northern Finnic branch. It is divided into Northern Veps, spoken in the Prionezhsky District in the Republic of Karelia; Central Veps, spoken in the central parts of Podporozhsky District in Leningrad Oblast and Babayevsky District in Vologda Oblast; and Southern Veps, spoken in the southern parts of Podporozhsky District in Leningrad Oblast and Babayevsky District in Vologda Oblast. Vepsian self-designations include *vepsläižed*, *vepslaine*, *bepslaane*, *lüdinik* and *lüdilaine*. Almost all Vepsians are fluent in Russian, while the younger generation in general does not speak Vepsian, although many understand it.

Vepsians were historically known in medieval Russian chronicles as *ves* and in some Arabic sources as *Wisu*. The existence of the Vepsian people was not widely known until the mid-19th century, and Vepsian was one of the last Uralic languages to be recognized. Alexander Svirsky is identified as an Eastern Orthodox saint.
