## Overview

The Komi are a Permian ethnic group indigenous to northeastern European Russia, living primarily around the basins of the Vychegda, Pechora and Kama rivers. The Komi-Permyaks are the southern Komi, associated especially with the Kama River basin and Perm Krai; the Komi also live in the Komi Republic, Murmansk Oblast, Khanty–Mansi Autonomous Okrug and Nenets Autonomous Okrug. The Komi are divided into northern Zyryans and southern Permyaks, with smaller groups traditionally named after the rivers where they live. At the beginning of the 21st century, 256,000 Komi lived in the Komi Republic, while the 2010 census recorded 228,235 people identifying as Komi. Their language belongs to the Permian branch of the Uralic family. Their folk culture is expressed through river-based settlement, wooden architecture, regional clothing, hunting and fishing foodways, and mythology combining older beliefs with Christianity.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Komi clothing is regionally diverse. Men’s clothing remained relatively similar across Komi territories, apart from winter costumes, while women’s clothing varied by region in embroidery technique, fabric and ornamentation. Southern and central Komi dress closely resembles the clothing of Northern Russians and other Finno-Ugric groups. The clothing of the Izhma Komi has features in common with Nenets dress.

### Architecture

Komi settlements traditionally consisted of large, multi-courtyard churchyards and villages built along or near rivers. Because the Komi territories are densely forested, wood was the principal material for houses and farm buildings. Komi dwellings resemble North Russian houses in their internal structure.

Two major house forms are identified: the *Sysol house type* (Сысольский тип) and the *Vym house type* (Bымский тип). The Sysol home is square-shaped and divided internally into a commercial section and a private section for residents. The Vym house is similar, with differences in its windows and internal arrangement. The Izhma Komi, living in sparsely wooded areas, use *chum* tents. Church construction developed alongside the efforts of Stephen of Perm to convert the Komi to Orthodoxy. Many churches used a tented-roof style associated with Northern Russia and Pomorye; most were wooden, although some churches and monasteries were built of stone.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that houses and farm buildings were traditionally made of wood and that Komi dwellings had regionally distinct internal arrangements.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe the historical Christianisation of the Komi. Stephen of Perm was tasked with converting the region to Christianity in 1365, and his mission led to the creation of the eparchy of Perm in 1383. The duke of Perm accepted baptism in 1470 and was given the Christian name Mikhail. The sources also state that pre-Christian Komi religion included shamanism, paganism and many deities, whose wooden images were kept in cult sanctuaries or, for domestic deities, in Komi dwellings.

## Foodways

Hunting, gathering and fishing were long-standing main sources of food. Komi diets commonly included meat, fish, berries and mushrooms. Meat dishes were more frequent among northern Komi, while berry-based dishes were more common in the south.

Named foods include grain pies with fish, various porridges, *Serbanka*, other sour soups, and cold soups based on bread and kvass. The Russian dumpling dish *pelmeni* is described as likely originating in the cuisines of the Komi and Udmurt peoples. In both languages, its name, *пельнянь* (*pel'n'an'*), means “ear bread.”

## Oral tradition & literature

Komi mythology includes stories of shamanism and paganism. The most widespread creation myths describe a struggle between two gods, *En* (Ен) and *Omöl'* (Омöль). Both are regarded as creator-gods, although En vivifies the life they create. After Christianisation, representations of En and Omöl' came to resemble God and Satan, with Omöl' portrayed as obstructing En’s creative work.

Tales of *chudins*, described as pagans who flee the new order into the forests, became widespread. Named mythological characters include *Jirkap* (Йиркап) from Sindor, who is thought by the Komi to have invented skis; *Joma* (Ёма), regarded as a Komi equivalent of Baba Yaga; and *Pera*, a Komi-Permyak tale character known for courage.

## Language & religion

The Komi language belongs to the Permian branch of the Uralic family and has limited mutual intelligibility with Udmurt. Its two main dialects are Zyrian in the Komi Republic and Permyak in the Komi-Permyak Okrug. They have traditionally been treated as separate languages but are mutually intelligible and can also be understood as two regional standards of one language.

Until the 18th century, Komi was written in the Old Permic script, *Важ Перым гижӧм* (*Važ Perym gižöm*), also called Anbur. Saint Stephen of Perm created it in the 14th century. Cyrillic was used from the 19th century, and the Latin alphabet briefly replaced it between 1932 and 1936. Modern Komi Cyrillic adds Іі and Ӧӧ for vowel sounds absent from Russian. A vaccination manual was the first book printed in Komi, appearing in 1815.

Komi religion combines Christianisation with older shamanic and pagan traditions. Formal research into pre-Christian religion began in the later half of the 19th century. Christian legends are relatively few, while older mythological figures and stories remain prominent in Komi folklore.
