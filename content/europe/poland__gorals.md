## Overview

The Gorals (Polish: Górale; Goral ethnolect: Górole) are an ethnographic group with historical ties to the Vlachs. Their traditional areas lie in southern Poland, northern Slovakia—especially Orava, Spiš and Zamagurie—and Cieszyn Silesia in the Czech Republic. In Poland, they inhabit Podhale in the Tatra Mountains and parts of the Beskids, including Cieszyn Silesia, the Silesian Beskids and the Żywiec Beskids. Their principal towns include Nowy Targ, Zakopane and Żywiec. A Goral diaspora exists in Bukovina, western Ukraine, northern Romania and Chicago. The Goral dialects belong to the West Slavic family and contain influence from Eastern Romance languages, Slovak, Hungarian and other Balkan languages. Their cultural profile includes distinctive mountain pastoral traditions, architecture, music, costume and embroidery.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Parzenica** — *Parzenica* embroidery developed from simple string loops used to reinforce cuts in the front of cloth trousers. The loops originally protected the cloth from fraying; later, red or navy blue string, woollen yarn and embroidery transformed the practical feature into colourful trouser ornamentation.

**Cucha** — The *cucha* is outerwear on which clasps were worn as ornaments in Zakopane. The article describes clasps as formerly functional fasteners that later became decorative elements attached or sewn onto garments.

**Goral folk costume** — Goral folk costumes are documented in the National Museum of Ethnography in Warsaw, the Tatra Museum in Zakopane, the Ethnographic Museum of Kraków and the City Museum of Żywiec.

**Motif vocabulary.** Thistle and edelweiss, embroidered on corsets; parzenica, the named trouser ornament.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe Goral clothing chiefly through its decorative elements. In the second half of the 19th century, corsets in Podhale were adorned with depictions of thistle and edelweiss. These motifs were especially popular in the early 20th century; later, the “Kraków style” introduced shiny sequins and glass beads. Clasps were originally used to fasten shirts, but buttons caused them to fall out of practical use. By the early 20th century, they remained mainly among senior and young shepherds, and in the 1920s and 1930s became collector’s items sought by tourists. In Zakopane they were worn ornamentally on the *cucha*, sweaters and occasionally leather bags. Today, Podhale highlanders usually attach or sew them onto clothing rather than use them as fasteners.

### Architecture

The Zakopane Style is identified as a Goral tradition established at the end of the 19th century. It draws on local architecture and the vernacular architecture of the Carpathians and is widespread in the Podhale region.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They identify the shepherd’s axe as a named object in Cieszyn Silesia and northern Slovakia, where it and elements of folk costume are termed Vlach: Polish *wałaska* or *wałaszczaki*, Slovak *valaška*. Clasps, once shirt fasteners and later ornaments, are also documented as objects associated with Goral dress.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources describe adornment rather than a separate jewellery tradition. Clasps became ornaments on the *cucha*, sweaters and occasionally leather bags, and are now attached or sewn onto clothing. Podhale corsets were decorated with thistle and edelweiss, followed by shiny sequins and glass beads under the influence of the “Kraków style.”

## Music & performance

Goral music is identified as part of the group’s material and cultural life, but the sources provide limited information about instruments, song genres and performance contexts. Kazimierz Dobroslowski is reported to have identified similarities between the Podhale dialect and Romanian and Albanian languages, including elements of belief systems, music and material culture. The article also states that the Gorals have belief-system elements, music and material culture similar to those of the Vlachs and related groups, including Moravian Vlachs.

Two contemporary musical groups are named. Zakopower is a popular folk-pop musical group from Zakopane. The Trebunie-Tutki folk musical group, also from Zakopane, blends traditional Goral music with reggae.

## Dance & theatre

They mention the Polish national opera *Krakowiacy i Górale* (Cracovians and Gorals), composed by Wojciech Bogusławski and premiered in 1794, but do not present it as a Goral theatrical tradition. The sources also mention a film about the Kostka-Napierski uprising, *Podhale w ogniu*, produced in 1956 and distributed in many languages across the Eastern Bloc.

## Festivals & rituals

The annual *Gorolski Święto* festival is held in Jablunkov in the Czech Republic. It is described as a showcase of local Polish Goral traditions, organized by the PZKO (Polish Cultural and Educational Union). The festival preserves the traditions of the Polish nationality group in Trans-Olza and is the largest cultural and folklore festival in Trans-Olza, gathering thousands of spectators each day of festivities.

The sources also describe religious traditions connected with the Roman Catholic Church. The Sanctuary of Our Lady of Ludźmierz is of particular significance to the Gorals and is identified as the oldest shrine in the Podhale region. Numerous Catholic religious cults and traditions are connected with the church.

## Foodways

They mention that Goral social and economic life resembled that of Vlach shepherd culture, but provide no specific foodways. No museum catalogue records are supplied for food-related objects.

## Oral tradition & literature

Goral folk tradition recognizes *zbojniks*, members of local robber bands in the western Carpathians, as heroes who helped exploited Gorals by stealing from the rich and giving back to the poor. The most famous was Juraj Jánošík from Terchová in the Žilina region on the Slovak side of the Carpathian mountains. As a youngster, he fought with the Kuruc insurgents against the Habsburg monarchy and later formed his own band of *zbojniks*. The phenomenon became widespread in the mid-16th century and disappeared in the 19th century with the death of Wojtek Mateja, considered the last *zbójnik*.

## Language & religion

The various Goral dialects belong to the West Slavic family and have influence from surrounding linguistic groups, especially the Eastern Romance languages. The Podhale dialect, Polish *gwara podhalańska*, is of Polish origin and belongs to the Lesser Poland dialect group, while also being considerably influenced by Slovak. Goral dialects contain vocabulary from Slovak, Hungarian and other Balkan languages. The Podhale dialect is the de facto standard literary Goral ethnolect, although most Gorals speak closely related dialects and commonly refer to them as *Górolski*.

Most Gorals adhere to the Roman Catholic Church. A notable portion are Augsburg Confession Lutherans, concentrated around Wisła, the main centre of Protestant Gorals. In Slovakia, significant portions of Goral settlements fall under the territorial jurisdictions of the Roman Catholic Diocese of Spiš.
