## Overview

The Võros (*võrokõsõq*) are a Finnic ethnographic group of Estonia, native to the Võro-speaking area of historical Vana-Võromaa in the southeast of the country. Their historical region covers approximately 4,200 km² across present-day Võru and Põlva counties and parts of Valga and Tartu counties, with a current population of around 57,000. The Võros descend, together with the Setos, from speakers of South Estonian, a divergent branch of the Finnic languages. The 2021 Estonian census recorded 97,320 residents speaking a dialect of the Võru group, including 25,080 speakers of the Seto sub-variety and roughly 72,240 speakers of Võro proper. The group is significant in folk culture through its language revitalisation, regional media, song festival, and the smoke sauna tradition of Vana-Võromaa.

## Music & performance

The sources identify **Uma Pido**, a regional song festival held in Võru every two to three years since 2008. Estonian Public Broadcasting has aired a weekly five-minute Võro news bulletin since 2005, as well as Võro-language television documentaries and drama series between 2005 and 2017. The sources also mention the annual **Kaika Suvõülikuul**, or Kaika Summer University, which began in 1988.

They likewise do not describe a musical tradition comparable in the sources to shashmaqam or gamelan.

## Festivals & rituals

The annual **Kaika Suvõülikuul**, or Kaika Summer University, began in 1988. The regional song festival **Uma Pido** has been held in Võru every two to three years since 2008.

The most fully described cultural practice is the **smoke sauna tradition of Vana-Võromaa**, or *savvusann*. It comprises bathing customs, the making of bath whisks, the construction and repair of saunas, and the smoking of meat. The tradition was inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2014.

They do record a contemporary political gathering: on 22 April 2023, 126 elected delegates from the eight historical parishes of Vana-Võromaa convened the inaugural Congress of the Võro People in Võru. The Congress declared the Võros an indigenous people of Estonia, adopted its declaration by 122 votes in favour to one against with three abstentions, elected an eleven-member Council of Elders, and called for recognition of Võro as an indigenous regional language together with bilingual schooling, media, and public signage.

## Oral tradition & literature

A distinct South Estonian literary tradition emerged in the 17th century. Early printed works in the language included the Catholic prayer book *Agenda Parva* from 1622, a translation of the New Testament titled *Wastne Testament* from 1686, and the first Estonian-language newspaper, *Tarto maa rahwa Näddali-Leht*, from 1806.

South Estonian literary use declined in the late 19th century as standardised North Estonian became the basis of national literary Estonian. Primary instruction in South Estonian ceased in the 1890s. During the Soviet period, systematic documentation of the dialect continued at the Estonian SSR Academy of Sciences’ Institute of Language and Literature in Tallinn. Paul Ariste founded the journal *Sovetskoye finno-ugrovedenie* in 1965, while dialectologists including Mari Must and Hella Keem assembled the corpus and text editions on which subsequent Võro scholarship rests.

The Võro revitalisation movement emerged in the late 1980s alongside Estonia’s second national awakening. The Võro Institute was established in 1995. The fortnightly newspaper *Uma Leht* has been published in Võro since 2000, and the children’s monthly magazine *Täheke* has published an annual Võro-language issue since 2005.

## Language & religion

Võro, or *Võro kiil*, is one of the two surviving South Estonian varieties, together with Seto. South Estonian diverged from the rest of the Finnic family between roughly 1000 and 600 BC. Võro has vowel harmony, which was lost in North Estonian; a glottal stop in the nominative plural; a different third-person singular indicative form; and a post-verbal negative particle. Approximately 19,000 South Estonian words are not used in North Estonian, and the shared core vocabulary between Võro and Standard Estonian has been estimated at around one fifth.

The 21st edition of *Ethnologue* classifies Võro as “threatened,” while the UNESCO Atlas of the World’s Languages in Danger classifies it as definitely endangered. A Võro–Estonian dictionary was produced in 2002 with 15,000 entries, followed by an Estonian–Võro dictionary in 2014 with 20,000 entries, both compiled at the Võro Institute. The Estonian Language Act treats Võro as a “regional form” of Estonian rather than a separate language, and the Population Register does not permit residents to register Võro as their mother tongue.

The Võro population was predominantly Lutheran, like the rest of historically south-eastern Livonia, in contrast to the Eastern Orthodox tradition of the Setos.
