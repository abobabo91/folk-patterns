## Overview

Swabians are a German ethnographic group native to Swabia, a region mostly divided between Baden-Württemberg and Bavaria in southwestern Germany. Their historical name derives from the medieval Duchy of Swabia, whose Germanic inhabitants were called Alemanni or Suebi. In modern ethnography, Swabia roughly corresponds to the Swabian Circle of the Holy Roman Empire during the Early Modern period. Swabian German belongs to the Alemannic group of German dialects and is traditionally spoken in the upper Neckar basin upstream of Heilbronn, along the upper Danube between Tuttlingen and Donauwörth, and on the left bank of the Lech, in an area centered on the Swabian Alps and stretching from Stuttgart to Augsburg. SIL Ethnologue estimated 819,000 Swabian speakers in 2006. Swabian culture retains elements of Alemannic tradition, especially carnival traditions associated with Swabian-Alemannic Fastnacht.

## Music & performance

The sources describe Swabian cultural identity through dialect, song, and carnival tradition. Swabian-Alemannic Fastnacht is identified as a carnival tradition shared with Alemannic neighbors. The songwriter Ralf Bendix praised the economic recovery of Germany after the Second World War in the 1964 song *Schaffe, schaffe Häusle baue / Und net nach de Mädle schaue*. The first line became a common summary of Swabian stereotypes and was translated in the source as “[let’s] work and work, and build a house / and not look out for girls.”

## Festivals & rituals

Swabian culture retains carnival traditions forming Swabian-Alemannic Fastnacht.

The sources describe several historical institutions through which Swabian cultural identity and a sense of cultural unity survived: the Swabian League of Cities in the 14th century, the Swabian League of 1488, and the establishment of the Swabian Circle in 1512. These are presented as political and cultural formations rather than as annual festivals or ritual observances.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources identify *The Seven Swabians* (*Die sieben Schwaben*) as one of the *Kinder- und Hausmärchen* published by the Brothers Grimm. The tale contributed to stereotypes portraying Swabians as stingy, overly serious, prudish, petty-bourgeois, and simple-minded. The same stereotype could also portray them positively as frugal, clever, entrepreneurial, and hard-working.

Ludwig Aurbacher is described as a teacher and writer best known for his stories about *The Seven Swabians*.

## Language & religion

Swabian German is a branch of the Alemannic group of German dialects. The source states that Swabian is “40 percent intelligible” to speakers of Standard German. Swabians are closely related linguistically to Badeners, Alsatians, and German-speaking Swiss. The dialect is traditionally spoken in the upper Neckar basin upstream of Heilbronn, along the upper Danube between Tuttlingen and Donauwörth, on the left bank of the Lech, and across an area centered on the Swabian Alps extending from Stuttgart to Augsburg.

The sources discuss religious migration. During the 17th and 18th century, substantial numbers of Swabians moved to the Dutch Republic in search of work or religious freedom, which the source associates with the republic’s wealth and religious tolerance.
