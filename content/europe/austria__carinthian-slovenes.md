## Overview

Carinthian Slovenes or Carinthian Slovenians are a minority of Slovene ethnicity living in the Austrian state of Carinthia, which borders Slovenia. Their present settlement area lies mainly in the south and east of the state, including the Jauntal (*Podjuna*), Rosental (*Rož*), lower Lavanttal (*Labotska dolina*), the Sattniz (*Gure*) mountains between the Drau River and Klagenfurt, and the lower Gailtal (*Ziljska dolina*). Köstenberg and Diex are approximately the most northerly points of current Slovene settlement. In 2001, Zell had 89% Carinthian Slovenes, Globasnitz 42%, and Eisenkappel-Vellach 38%. Their dialects belong to the Carinthian group of Slovene dialects, and their cultural importance in the sources is especially connected with language, bilingual education, literature, political organization, and resistance during World War II.

## Music & performance

The sources describe a Slovene music school rather than a traditional instrumental or performance repertoire. The Slovene music school, *Kärntner Musikschule* or *Glasbena šola na Koroškem*, was founded in 1984 through a private initiative and received public funds from 1998, when a cooperation agreement was concluded with the State of Carinthia. The school has continued its operations with help from contributions from the Republic of Slovenia.

The sources also identify Matija Ahacel as a collector of folk songs, and mention Milka Hartman, Anton Kuchling, Gustav Januš, Andrej Kokot, Erik Prunč, and Karel Smolle as lyric poets.

## Festivals & rituals

They do mention Roman Catholic parishes, bilingual schools, and the historical importance of the Roman Catholic Church in the Slovene compulsory school system.

The Carinthian Slovenes initially enjoyed minority rights that included bilingual schools and parishes, Slovene newspapers, associations, and representatives in municipal councils and the Landtag assembly. In 1848, the Ministry of Education decreed that compulsory school pupils should be taught in their respective native language. Between 1855 and 1869, the Slovene compulsory school system was administered by the Roman Catholic Church. On 3 October 1945, a law introduced bilingual education for all children in the traditional area of settlement, regardless of ethnic group. Bilingual education took place during the first three school years, after which Slovene became a compulsory subject.

## Oral tradition & literature

Carinthian Slovene literature after the Second World War is described as an emancipated literature free from provincialism in the 2000s. In early 1981, Florjan Lipuš’s novel *Der Zögling Tjaž* appeared in a German translation by Peter Handke. Handke later translated Gustav Januš. Other writers named in the sources include Mirko Kumer, Kristo Srienc, Valentin Polanšek, Janko Messner, Jože Blajs, Martin Kuchling, Kristijan Močilnik, Janko Ferk, Maja Haderlap, Franc Merkac, Jani Oswald, Vincenc Gotthardt, Fabjan Hafner, Cvetka Lipuš, Rezka Kanzian, and Tim O. Wüster.

The literary periodical *Mladje* provided a focus for a group that included Janko Ferk, Maja Haderlap, Franc Merkac, Jani Oswald, Vincenc Gotthardt, Fabjan Hafner, and Cvetka Lipuš. Johann (Janez) Strutz’s *Profile der neuen slowenischen Literatur in Kärnten* was published in 1998 in a revised and extended edition and is described as a respected standard work on the sociology, theory, and history of Carinthian Slovene literature.

The sources also identify Matija Ahacel as a collector of folk songs and Urban Jarnik as an ethnographer.

## Language & religion

Carinthian Slovenes traditionally speak four dialects belonging to the Carinthian dialect group: the Jaun Valley dialect, the Rosen Valley dialect, the Gail Valley dialect, and the Ebriach dialect. The Carinthian Slovene dialect area extends beyond the present borders of Carinthia, through Slovenian Carinthia, into the Pohorje Mountains and along the upper Drava Valley in Slovenian Styria. A Carinthian Slovene dialect is also spoken in Rateče in Slovenia, while Kranjska Gora has a transitional dialect between Carinthian and Upper Carniolan.

The dialects have experienced reciprocal influence with German in tone and vocabulary. The sources describe Slovene as a South Slavic language and record German influence on a Slovene dialect with a large number of borrowed German words. The term Windisch has been used for Slovene dialects spoken in Carinthia, but it is rejected by a large part of the Carinthian Slovene population because of its historical associations and German nationalist use.

Roman Catholic institutions have played an important role in education and community life. The sources mention the Roman Catholic Church, Roman Catholic parishes, a secondary school operated by the Church in St Peter in Rosental, and a public-funded bilingual primary school in Klagenfurt operated by the Church.
