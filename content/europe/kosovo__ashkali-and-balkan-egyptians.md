## Overview

The Ashkali and Balkan Egyptians are Albanian- and Slavic-speaking, mainly Muslim ethnic cultural minorities recognized as communities. They mainly inhabit Kosovo and southern Serbia, and also live in Albania, Montenegro, and North Macedonia. Most Ashkali live in Kosovo, particularly in its central and eastern regions, including Ferizaj, Fushë Kosova, and Lipjan. Kosovo’s Egyptian community is concentrated mainly in the west, including Gjakova, Istog, Peja, and Deçan. Most Balkan Egyptians are thought to live in North Macedonia and Albania rather than Kosovo. Before the Kosovo War of 1999, Balkan Egyptians and Ashkali registered themselves as Albanians. Their cultural distinctiveness is expressed through contested identities, language, community organization, and circumcision celebrations.

## Material culture

### Architecture

They mention archaeological structures in modern Ohrid and Bitola that some Balkan historians attribute to temples of the Goddess Isis.

## Music & performance

The history source mentions *Khener*, described as an Ancient Egyptian dance group, in connection with an origin theory that cites Herodotus. The sources also mention that no television or radio channels are dedicated to Kosovo’s Ashkali or Egyptian minority audiences.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record circumcision celebrations for sons as a community-organized rite. No further details are given about the timing, sequence, participants, food, music, clothing, or religious interpretation of these celebrations.

The sources describe several historical and political events affecting community identity. In 1990, an Egyptian association was formed in Ohrid with representatives from different Balkan countries. During the Kosovo War, many Ashkali were sent to refugee camps with Romani people in Kosovo. After the war ended in 1999, some reaffirmed their identity as Ashkali. The first Ashkali party, the Democratic Ashkali Party of Kosovo, was formed in 2000 under Sabit Rrahmani.

## Foodways

They identify the communities as mainly Muslim.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record several origin narratives constructed by Ashkali community leaders. The Persian narrative places their origin in Persia in the 4th century; the Italian narrative, based on folk etymology, presents them as colonists from ancient Rome; and the Semitic narrative connects them with the town Ashkelon in Biblical Philistia.

The sources also describe competing accounts of Balkan Egyptian origins. Some Balkan historians trace them to the Iron Age and references in Herodotus, while other accounts connect them with Egyptian soldiers who came to Albania after the Ottoman–Egyptian invasion of Mani. Historians cited in the source maintain that during the Ottoman era Balkan Egyptians and other Balkan Roma were part of a single community called by the Ottomans *Kıbti*, meaning “Copts.” A 14th-century placename, Агѹповы клѣти (*Agupovy klěti*), in the Rila Charter of Ivan Alexander of Bulgaria was thought by some authors to be related to the Balkan Egyptians.

## Language & religion

The Ashkali and Balkan Egyptians are Albanian- and Slavic-speaking and mainly Muslim. Some Ashkali speak Romani, while Egyptians do not. The two groups are not clearly delineated and have often been grouped with Roma under the acronym RAE, meaning Roma, Ashkali, and Egyptians. The sources state that Ashkali and Balkan Egyptians reject having a relationship with the Roma, while Ashkali and Roma both claim Egyptians as their own and Ashkali and Egyptians dispute each other’s background.

They mention the Goddess Isis and the Mysteries of Isis only in connection with historical interpretations of archaeological structures and do not present them as contemporary Ashkali or Balkan Egyptian religious practices.
