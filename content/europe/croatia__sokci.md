## Overview

Šokci are a South Slavic ethnic group native to the historical regions of Baranya, Bačka, Slavonia, and Syrmia, which today span eastern Croatia, southwestern Hungary, and northern Serbia. They primarily self-identify as a subgroup of Croats and are not considered a separate ethnicity in Croatia and elsewhere. They are considered a native population of Slavonia and Syrmia in Croatia. In Serbia, 302 people declared as ethnic Šokci in the 2022 census. Outside Slavonia and Syrmia, Šokci live in Bački Monoštor, Sonta, Sombor, Bački Breg, and Hercegszántó. Their culture is associated with an old-Shtokavian Slavonian sub-dialect, Roman Catholic faith, village life, folk songs and dances, tambura music, distinctive clothing, and foodways connected with the fertile Pannonian plain.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Lace decorations** are part of the white linen *rubina*.

### Clothing & dress

The traditional Šokac outfit is called the *rubina*. It is made of white linen cloth with lace decorations, and its main part is a blouse called *oplećak i krila*. Women mostly wear the entire outfit only in summer and replace it with a wool skirt in winter. Gold coins known as *dukati* are described as the most esteemed decoration of a Šokac outfit. A wealthy Šokac girl could have a large number of them woven onto her chest as both decoration and a sign that she came from a wealthy family.

### Architecture

Šokac villages often have one main street, called *šor*. Each family house has auxiliary buildings and a spacious yard. The central street is bordered on both sides by water channels, with small crossings leading to the houses.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources describe *dukati*, gold coins woven onto the chest of a Šokac outfit. They function as decoration and as a visible sign that a girl came from a wealthy family.

## Music & performance

The most recognisable feature of Šokci culture is music played mostly on the *tambura*. Many tambura bands achieved nationwide fame in Croatia. The body of the *tambura* was traditionally made from maple, poplar, or plum wood; today it is mostly made from spruce or fir. The sources also name the bagpipe as an instrument used in the past.

Each Šokac village has a cultural society where folk songs and dances are cultivated. Traditional wedding festivities receive considerable attention and can attract the attention of entire villages.

## Dance & theatre

The sources state that each Šokac village has a cultural society where folk songs and dances are cultivated. They name *bećarac* as a popular folk custom.

## Festivals & rituals

A popular folk custom is *bećarac*. Šokci also hold a yearly festivity called the *Šokačko sijelo*. It lasts nine days in February and is described as the largest traditional event in the area of Posavina near Županja, featuring local culture and traditions.

Traditional wedding festivities receive considerable attention and can attract the attention of entire villages. The sources also describe traditional autumn slaughtering, during which pork products such as ham, sausages, *kulen*, and bacon are customarily obtained.

## Foodways

Šokci live in the fertile Pannonian plain, where they cultivate grains and corn in large fields surrounding their villages. Families often keep poultry, particularly ducks and geese, but pigs are described as the main source of meat and are almost inevitably kept by a Šokac. Pork-based products include ham, sausages, particularly *kulen*, and bacon. These products are customarily obtained through traditional autumn slaughtering.

Plums are the most common fruit, partly because they are often fermented into a liquor called *rakija*.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that Šokac villages cultivate folk songs and dances through cultural societies. They identify *bećarac* as a popular folk custom and describe the *Šokačko sijelo* as an event featuring local culture and traditions.

## Language & religion

Šokci speak an old-Shtokavian Slavonian sub-dialect that is almost exclusively spoken by Šokci. The Slavonian dialect has a mixed Ikavian and Ekavian accent. Ikavian predominates in Posavina, Baranja, Bačka, and the Slavonian sub-dialect enclave of Derventa, while Ekavian is used in Podravina. There are also enclaves with one accent in the territory of the other, as well as mixed Ekavian–Ikavian and Jekavian–Ikavian accents. In some villages in Hungary, the original Slavic yat sound is preserved.

The Šokci are Roman Catholic by faith and follow the Latin Rite.
