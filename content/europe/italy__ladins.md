## Overview

The Ladins are a Romance ethnolinguistic group of northern Italy whose communities are distributed across several valleys collectively known as Ladinia. These include the valleys of Badia and Gherdëina in South Tyrol, Fassa in Trentino, and Livinallongo—also known as Buchenstein or Fodom—and Ampezzo in Belluno. Their native language is Ladin, a Rhaeto-Romance language related to Swiss Romansh and Friulian. Ladinia lies in the historical region of Tyrol, and Ladins share that region’s culture, history, traditions, environment and architecture. The Ladin people constitute 4.53% of the population of South Tyrol.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ladin culture is promoted by the government-sponsored cultural institute Istitut Ladin Micurà de Rü in the South Tyrolean municipality of San Martin de Tor. The sources also identify Micurà de Rü’s first attempt to develop a written form of the Ladin language.

## Language & religion

Ladin is a Rhaeto-Romance language related to Swiss Romansh and Friulian. Around 1000 AD, the Rhaeto-Romance language area extended from the Swiss Alps in the west to the Julian Alps in modern-day Slovenia. Germanic and Slavic migrations fragmented this cultural territory, and the Ladin people were pushed into high-altitude, isolated mountain valleys, separating them from their linguistic cousins in Switzerland and northeastern Italy. Ladins developed a formal national identity in the 19th century. The sources mention no Ladin religion, denomination, spiritual practice or religious landscape.
