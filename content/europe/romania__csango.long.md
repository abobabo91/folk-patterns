## Overview

The Csángós (Hungarian *Csángók*, Romanian *Ceangăi*) are ethnic Hungarians living mostly in the Romanian region of Moldavia, above all in Bacău County, in the area known as Csángó Land. Smaller communities live in Transylvania around the Ghimeș-Palanca Pass and in the so-called Seven Villages (*Hétfalu*), and in the village of Oituz in Northern Dobruja. Their traditional speech, Csángó, is a Hungarian dialect now used by only a minority of the group; research by the folklorist Vilmos Tánczos between 2008 and 2010 put the number of speakers at roughly 43,000, with the most archaic Northern Csángó form known to about 4,000 people, almost all over the age of fifty. Most Csángós are Roman Catholic, in a region where the majority church is Orthodox. The Council of Europe has described them as associated with ancient traditions and a great diversity of folk art and culture of exceptional value for Europe.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The catalogue records of the objects shown here are the sources for this section; they describe the pieces technically rather than naming regional weaving schools, so the entries below follow the vernacular terms the records themselves give.

**Katrinca** — a woman's rectangular skirt wrap, woven in 2/2 twill from wool by hand, with the selvedges worn at the waist and hem. A solid black centre field about 66 cm wide is bordered by two panels of roughly 35.5 cm in which brown wool alternates with multicoloured stripes of pink, green, yellow and blue wool and pale pink silk, woven as bands.

**Öv** — a man's wide belt of four red stripes and three groups of blue, yellow and green stripes, with cream, pink and grey among them. It is twill woven in a herringbone pattern from a mixture of wool and cotton yarns, with three narrow bands towards each warp end and the warp ends left unfinished. A woman's under belt in the same collection is instead warp-faced tabby in high-twist multicoloured wool and cotton, its stripes laid out as a mirror image about a yellow cotton centre line.

**Kerpa ruva** — a woman's outer headscarf: a long, diaphanous rectangle in two shades of orange, tabby woven, the centre field carrying regularly spaced fine white bands grouped in twos. Each warp end has an identical wide patterned border of two bands of large laid-in diamonds framed by gauze weave and further laid-in work on the diagonal.

**Falvedö** — a rectangular wall-cover or protector woven as a multicoloured chequered wool tabby, with red, black and yellow warps against green, pink, orange, blue, yellow, black and white cotton wefts; the warp ends are turned and stitched.

**Traistă** — a woman's bag made from a single piece of predominantly red cloth folded in half weftwise, twill woven on a black cotton warp with a wool weft, banded in repeating polychrome stripes. Printed cotton loops of pink and blue floral cloth carry a doubled, plaited pink woollen handle.

**Batistă** — a long cotton handkerchief with twill-woven polychrome bands arranged as a mirror image about the centre line, in maroon, green, yellow, blue, orange, purple and red, the patterning growing denser and thicker at each end, with gold-coloured tinsel thread added.

**Household linens** — pillowcases and towels of hand-spun, handwoven cotton banded in red and black; cushion covers in commercially made chequered cotton with supplementary-weft diamond and zigzag stripes worked in wool and a little gold metal-wrapped thread; and floor runners of 2/2 twill chequered hemp and wool in cream, grey, brown and black.

**Motif vocabulary.** Framed circular motifs; straight and meandering linked leaf motifs; diamonds, both laid-in and in trellis arrangements; zigzag stripes; snowflake or geometric floral motifs worked on the diagonal; and simple stripe and chequer fields.

### Clothing & dress

The women's costume documented here is built from a white blouse of tabby-woven cotton, made up from three full weaving widths and hand stitched, with a gathered neckline edged in blue blanket stitch, a V-shaped opening overstitched in black wool, and a black rectangular embroidered panel with diamond patterning on the upper sleeve. Over it go the *katrinca* skirt wrap and a narrow warp-faced under belt; an unfinished sleeve kept as an embroidery sample shows the same diagonal snowflake and trellis patterning being worked in wool and tinsel thread. The head is covered by the *kerpa ruva*. For outerwear there is a woman's sleeveless sheepskin waistcoat, two tones of brown fleece inside and cream leather outside, embroidered in multicoloured wool with circular and leaf motifs, edged with solid black wool bands at hem, armholes, neckline and centre front, and fastened with three leather toggles with pom-poms and loops.

Men wear a long cream cotton shirt reaching to just above or just below the knee, cut with a straight front and back panel plus extra panels and small diamond gussets to give an A-line, finished with a V-neck, a small collar and self-colour or geometric embroidery in yellow, pink and lilac cotton, and with drawn-thread work. Summer trousers are hand-woven tabby cotton, two full loom widths for the legs and a folded diamond of cloth for the crutch, gathered on a white cotton drawstring. The wide herringbone *öv* is the man's belt. The sources do not describe Csángó footwear.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The objects recorded here are domestic textiles rather than pottery or metalwork: a *falvedö* wall-protector, a long banded interior cloth in twill with diamond patterning and tinsel thread, a kerchief woven for interior decoration with wide polychrome borders at each warp end, pillowcases and towels in hand-spun cotton, a cushion cover, and a twill floor runner in hemp and wool. Several show their use plainly — the towel carries two stains at the fold where it hung, the *katrinca* is worn through in its black centre field. The sources say nothing about Csángó ceramics, metalwork or woodworking.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Three necklaces are recorded, all of threaded glass beads tied off with twisted wool. One is formed of six double-twisted strands of graduated length in yellow glass, fastened with twisted purple and pale blue wool; a second has five double-twisted strands of red glass tied with twisted red wool; the third uses beads made as two-colour cylinders of silver cased in clear glass, tied with orange, green and purple wool, and has one strand broken. The sources also note that Csángó village elders could be recognised by their traditionally long hair and beards. Nothing further on tattooing, hair practice or metal jewellery appears in the sources used.

## Music & performance

Csángó music shows the characteristic features of Hungarian music, and the words of the songs are mostly Hungarian with some dialect differences; the anthem of the Csángós refers to Csángó Hungarians repeatedly. The sources used do not name individual instruments, song genres or performance occasions.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used give no festival calendar, but they do describe the ritual frame within which Csángó life was lived. The Roman Catholic faith, influenced by Orthodox rites, has been one of the two pillars of Csángó self-identity for centuries, alongside the Hungarian spoken in the family; it is what distinguished the group from the majority Greek Orthodox society around it. Sixteenth-century observers record the same tension: after 1562 the humanist Johann Sommer noted that Despot Vodă punished breaches of the sanctity of marriage among the Hungarians and Saxons of Moldavia, and a Habsburg report of April 1562 describes Alexandru Moldoveanul forcing all nations to be rebaptised into the religion of the Moldavians before Despot appointed the Polish bishop Ian Lusenius over the Saxons and Hungarians and had the confiscated churches rebuilt. Bartolomeo Brutti wrote from Iaşi on 14 January 1587 that the few Franciscans in the country spoke neither German nor Hungarian and so could not give spiritual care to the fifteen thousand Catholics there. The question of language in worship is still live: at the time of the Council of Europe's 2001 report the Vatican expressed the hope that the Csángós would be able to celebrate Catholic mass in Csángó, their liturgical language.

## Foodways

The sources used do not describe Csángó dishes or cooking. They record only the agricultural base: the Csángós were historically a rural and agricultural people who raised stock such as sheep and cows and farmed corn, potatoes and hemp — the last of which reappears as the probable fibre of the twill floor runner in the collection shown here.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used do not record Csángó folktales, epics or storytelling occasions. What they do document is a written and scholarly record around the dialect. The Munich Codex, a Hussite translation of the New Testament into Hungarian, is dated in its text to 1466 in Moldavia. A Jesuit report from Roman in 1588 by Stanisław Warszewicki observed that the Hungarians and Saxons of the region's fifteen towns and surrounding villages mostly could not read and did not recognise the letters. The Csángós took no part in the language reforms of the Age of Enlightenment or the bourgeois transformation that produced modern national consciousness, and had neither a noble stratum nor an intelligentsia to shape a Hungarian consciousness for them. Very few Csángós today can write in their mother tongue, since there is no teaching of the language in the Csángó villages; the Csángós make no political demands beyond recognition as a distinct culture and education and church services in their dialect. Vilmos Tánczos's fieldwork of 2008–2010 is the most recent documentation of the dialect's decline.

## Language & religion

Csángó is a dialect of Hungarian; the Hungarians originated on the Asian Steppe as a tribal federation of Ugric and Turkic peoples. The most archaic form is Northern Csángó, used regularly by about 4,000 speakers, all above fifty. Applying the UNESCO framework for measuring language vitality, the dialect falls into the "Severely Endangered" category. The Council of Europe estimated 60,000–70,000 speakers in 2001; Tánczos found roughly 43,000 by 2010. The majority of Csángós are Roman Catholic, with Orthodox influence on the rites, and that Catholicism is what kept the group distinct from the surrounding Greek Orthodox majority. Hungarian and international scholarship agrees the Csángós are of Hungarian origin, with assimilated Romanian, German, Polish and Italian elements; the Romanian delegation to the Council of Europe dissented, arguing that the Bacău and Roman groups are Romanians from Transylvania who joined Moldavia's Catholic population, and that only the group settled in the Trotuș and Tazlău valleys is of Szekler origin.
