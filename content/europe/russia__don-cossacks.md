## Overview

Don Cossacks, also called Donians, are Cossacks who settled along the middle and lower Don. Historically, they lived within the former Don Cossack Host, an independent or autonomous democratic polity in present-day Southern Russia and parts of the Donbas region of Ukraine from the end of the 16th century until 1918. In the general census of 1884, the male population of the Don Cossacks was reported to number 425,000. Their historical territory was associated with the Don River steppes, later the Don Host Land and Don Host Oblast, and their capital moved from Cherkassk to Novocherkassk in 1805. Their importance in folk culture is connected particularly with military customs, uniforms, communal institutions, music, song, dance, and the preservation of Don Cossack traditions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Don Cossack uniform** — The distinguishing colour of the Don Cossack Host until 1914 was red, used on cap bands and wide trouser stripes on a dark-blue uniform of loose-fitting cut. Blue riding breeches with broad red stripes remained characteristic of the Don Host during active service in both World Wars.

**Khaki field tunic** — In 1908, a khaki field tunic replaced the dark-blue coats and white summer blouses previously worn for ordinary duties. The change concerned field clothing, while the blue riding breeches with broad red stripes continued to be worn.

**Tsar’s green uniform** — The Don Cossack Battery of the Imperial Guard wore a “Tsar’s green” uniform, a dark shade common to the army, with black and red distinctions identifying artillery.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe men’s military and ceremonial clothing rather than a complete everyday dress system. Before 1914, the Don Cossack Host used a dark-blue, loose-fitting uniform with red cap bands and broad red trouser stripes. Tall lambskin hats could be worn, with red cloth tops edged in white lace. Silver metal scrolls on the headdress marked the distinguished conduct of individual regiments. Officers wore silver braiding on collars and epaulettes, together with silver-and-black girdles. Other ranks had shoulder-straps matching the dark-blue caftan. A whip was used instead of spurs. In 1908, the khaki field tunic became standard for ordinary duties, while blue riding breeches with broad red stripes continued in active service. In 1939, some regiments received traditional Cossack uniforms in ceremonial and field-service versions.

### Architecture

They mention the first records of Cossack villages, called *stanitsas*, dating from 1549, but provide no architectural description of them.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention silver metal scrolls on military headdresses, silver braiding on officers’ collars and epaulettes, silver-and-black girdles, horses and harness, and communally owned clothing factories.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The only documented adornment is military: silver metal scrolls on headdresses and silver braiding, epaulettes, and girdles worn by officers.

## Music & performance

Don Cossack musical culture is documented through the Don Cossack Choir Serge Jaroff. The choir consisted of former officers of the Russian Imperial Army who had fled after the defeat of their army in the Crimea and were discovered singing in Çilingir, near Constantinople. They made their formal concert debut in Vienna in 1923 under their founder, conductor, and composer Serge Jaroff.

The men dressed as Cossacks and sang a cappella. Their repertory included Russian sacred and secular music, army songs, folk songs, and art songs. The choir became popular in America, Japan, and Europe, touring the world in the 1930s, 40s, and 50s, and continuing to the present. Cossack dancing was later added to its programme.

The sources also identify an anthem, *Всколыхнулся, взволновался православный Тихий Дон*, written by Fedor Anisimov in 1853. The article gives its title in Russian.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention Cossack dancing as part of the programme eventually added by the Don Cossack Choir Serge Jaroff.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe the Common Assembly, or *Казачий Круг*, as an important institution of Cossack society. The assembly made the most important decisions and elected temporary authorities, including atamans.

Several public and political events are recorded. The Don Cossacks assembly in Novocherkassk inaugurated the Don Cossacks flag on 4 May 1918 under the leadership of Ataman Pyotr Krasnov. The flag had blue, yellow, and red colours. The Coat of Arms of the Don Cossacks was known from the 17th century and was adopted as a symbol of the Don Republic on 15 September 1918.

## Foodways

They state that the Don Cossacks’ early occupations included hunting and fishing, and that only later did they settle and work on the land, but provide no further foodways information.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources identify Mikhail Sholokhov’s monumental work, *And Quiet Flows the Don*, as a literary work dealing sympathetically with the Don Cossacks and depicting the destruction of their way of life as a result of World War I and the Russian Civil War.

A musical oral-performance tradition is represented by the Don Cossack Choir Serge Jaroff, whose repertory included Russian sacred and secular music, army, folk, and art songs.

## Language & religion

The sources give Russian and Ukrainian names for the Don Cossacks and their historical host. They state that the name Cossack derives from the same etymological root as “Kazakh,” an unrelated Central Asian Turkic people, and report that Don Cossack life and language were heavily influenced by “Tatars from Meshchera.”

Most Don Cossacks are Russian Orthodox and consider themselves guardians of the faith. A large percentage were Starovers. In 1903, a minimum of 150,000 from a total of 2,500,000 parish members of the Don Eparchy were Starovers. Ataman count Matvei Platov belonged to a Popovtsy Old Believers Family. The sources state that Don Cossacks were tolerant of other religions.
