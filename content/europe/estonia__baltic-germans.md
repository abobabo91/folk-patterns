## Overview

Baltic Germans are a historical community of German speakers associated principally with Estonia and Latvia. In Estonia, their settlements included the northern part of the country, known historically as Estland, with major towns including Reval, Narwa, Wesenberg, Weissenstein and Hapsal. German-speaking traders, crusaders, clergy, craftsmen and soldiers began settling in the eastern Baltic in the 12th and 13th centuries. They became prominent in urban commerce, education, government and the clergy, while a wealthy minority formed the Baltic nobility and acquired rural estates. German was used in official documents, commerce, education and government. The community declined after Estonia achieved independence in 1918, and by 1934 there were 16,346 Baltic Germans in Estonia, 1.5% of the total population.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that Baltic Germans used German language, customs and family names.

### Architecture

Baltic German architecture is represented in the sources principally by rural manor houses and urban religious and civic buildings. The Baltic German nobility acquired large rural estates, and many new manor houses were built on country estates during the period of Russian rule. After Estonian land nationalization, most grand manor houses were taken over by schools, hospitals, local administration and museums. The sources also mention German churches and cathedrals in Tallinn and Riga. Tallinn Cathedral was given to an Estonian congregation in 1927; St. James's Cathedral in Riga was lost after the 1923 referendum, and Riga Cathedral was taken away after another referendum in 1931.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do state that former German owners in Estonia and Latvia were permitted in some circumstances to retain farm equipment after agrarian reforms.

## Music & performance

They do record the importance of German schools and societies, German-language education, urban professional life and churches. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription or museum catalogue record supplied for this profile concerns Baltic German music.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record the community’s association with Catholicism during the medieval period and with Protestantism in Livonia during the Reformation. German congregations possessed churches.

The sources also describe political and institutional ceremonies rather than folk rituals. Livonian and Estonian nobles delivered a note of independence to Soviet representatives in Stockholm on January 28, 1918. On April 12, 1918, Baltic German representatives from all Baltic provinces met in Riga and called on the German Emperor to annex the Baltic lands. A proposed United Baltic Duchy had a regency council that met on November 9, 1918, but it collapsed with the German Empire.

## Foodways

They mention commerce, urban occupations, rural estates, farming and manor houses. The museum catalogue records supplied contain no food-related objects.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do identify German-language newspapers, schools and learned institutions. In Latvia, Paul Schiemann was editor-in-chief of the *Rigasche Rundschau* newspaper, while in Estonia Axel de Vries led the German party from 1926 and edited *Revaler Bote*. The Herder Institute in Latvia was a private German university with faculties of Theology, Jurisprudence and Political Science and Philosophy. These sources establish German-language literary and educational institutions.

## Language & religion

Baltic Germans were a German-speaking community. In the eastern Baltic, Low German became the dominant language of official documents, commerce, education and government after the decline of Latin; it was later replaced gradually by High German. The early settlers included Catholic German traders and crusaders, and the majority of medieval Catholic settlers and their German-speaking descendants lived in the towns of medieval Livonia. Livonia became mostly Protestant during the Reformation. German religious and educational institutions remained important: the Academia Gustaviana, now University of Tartu, was founded in 1632 and became an intellectual focus of the Baltic Germans. German schools continued in Estonia, and the country’s Cultural Autonomy and National Minorities Act of February 12, 1925 provided some cultural autonomy for Germans.
