## Overview

The Gorani, also called Goranci, are a South Slavic ethnic group native to the Gora region, a triangle between Kosovo, Albania, and North Macedonia. In North Macedonia, Gorani-inhabited villages are located in the Polog region, specifically Jelovjane and Urvič. The group is estimated at 20,000 people and speaks a transitional South Slavic dialect called Goranski, also known as Našinski. The vast majority adhere to Sunni Islam, and many practice Sufism. Their folk culture is documented through village-specific women’s clothing, male garments, confectionery and baking, gunsmithing, circle dancing, oil wrestling, coffee customs, and traditions that retain both Islamic and Orthodox Christian elements.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no Gorani motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional Gorani dress resembles Albanian clothing. Men’s attire consists of the *plis*, a white felt cap; *Dzamadan* or *Xhamadan*, a vest; *Benevrek* or *tirq*, woolen trousers; and *openke* or *opinge*, traditional leather shoes. Women’s clothing differs regionally. In the Albanian part of Gora, women’s dress is identical to that of the neighboring Luma region. In Kosovo, women’s dress varies from village to village. Historical examples indicate earlier similarities with Luma clothing, followed by the development of distinct local variants.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

During the Ottoman period, Gorani communities were known as gunsmiths. The craft was particularly associated with Borje, Oreshkë, Cërnelevë, Pakisht, Shishtavec, Krushevo, Zlipotok, Baçka, Orgjost, Vranishta, and Rapça. It continued until the beginning of the 20th century and, in some places, until the Second World War; Rapça was identified as the place where the craft survived longest.

## Music & performance

Traditional Gorani folk music includes *oro*, a two-beat circle dance centered on foot movements. It begins on the right foot and proceeds anticlockwise. The dance is usually accompanied by *curlje*, *kaval*, *čiftelija*, or *tapan*. Singing is used less frequently in Gorani dancing than in the dances of Albanians and Serbs. The sources also connect *curlje* and *tapan* with performances of *Pelivona*, described as the “national” sport of the Gorani.

## Dance & theatre

The named Gorani dance is *oro*, a two-beat circle dance in which performers focus on foot movements, begin with the right foot, and move anticlockwise. *Oro* is accompanied by *curlje*, *kaval*, *čiftelija*, or *tapan*.

## Festivals & rituals

Gorani religious life combines Sunni Islam, Sufi practice, and the continued observance of certain Orthodox Christian traditions. Halveti and Bektashi Sufi orders are widespread. The Gorani also observe *Slavas* and *Đurđevdan*, and know the *Onomastik*, or saints’ days. The sources state that Islamization began in Gora during the 18th century and associate it with the Ottoman abolition of the Archbishopric of Ohrid and Serbian Patriarchate of Peć in 1766/1767. The last Christian Gorani, Božana, died in the 19th century, and later accounts collected by Russian consuls treated her as part of Gorani Christian heritage.

## Foodways

The Gorani are described as “the best confectioners and bakers” in former Yugoslavia. Turkish coffee is a popular drink, served in small cups with a glass of water. Tasseography using the residue of Turkish coffee is popular among Gorani.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record the Gorani–Albanian dictionary published in 2007 by Albanian-Gorani scholar Nazif Dokle. The dictionary contains 43,000 words and phrases and was sponsored and printed by the Bulgarian Academy of Sciences. Dokle defined the language as related to Bulgarian dialects spoken in the northwest of North Macedonia.

## Language & religion

Gorani speech is a South Slavic dialect known as *Našinski* or *Goranski*. It forms part of the wider Torlakian dialect area, associated with Southern Serbia, Western Bulgaria, and part of North Macedonia. Albanian speakers call the dialect *Gorançe*. Within the Gorani community, the dialect is recognized as closer to Macedonian than to Serbian; other classifications place it within Serbian or Bulgarian dialect areas. It contains Turkish and Arabic loanwords associated with Islam and also has Albanian influence. Gorani speakers use Serbo-Croatian in school.

The Gorani are predominantly Sunni Muslims, and many practice Sufism, especially through the Halveti and Bektashi orders. They also retain some Orthodox Christian customs, including *Slavas*, *Đurđevdan*, and the *Onomastik*. The sources describe the group’s ethnic and national classification as contested, with interpretations linking the Gorani to Serbs, Bulgarians, Albanians, Bosniaks, Macedonians, or a distinct community.
