## Overview

The English are an ethnic group and nation native to England, the largest and most populated country of the United Kingdom. They speak English, a West Germanic language of the Indo-European family, and share a common ancestry, history, and culture. The English identity began with the Anglo-Saxons, whose name for themselves was *Angelcynn*, meaning “Angle kin” or “English people.” English ancestry includes West Germanic peoples such as the Angles, Saxons, and Jutes, Romano-British Brittonic speakers, later Danes and other Norsemen, Normans, French Protestants, and migrants from the Commonwealth and elsewhere. The sources give no single population figure for the English ethnic group. English culture has influenced literature, art, architecture, music, cuisine, science, technology, and political life across the world.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Opus Anglicanum** — “English work” was recognised as the finest embroidery in Europe. The sources describe it as an Anglo-Saxon and later English art form.

**Bayeux Tapestry** — This large, English-made embroidered cloth depicts events leading up to the Norman conquest and dates to the late 11th century. It was commissioned by a Norman patron from English artists working in the traditional Anglo-Saxon style.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name religious, literary, and genre subjects in English art.

### Clothing & dress

The sources mention clothing as part of Regency decorative culture and describe the Regency style as including elegant furniture, vertically striped wallpaper, and clothing. For men, the period is associated with the dandy Beau Brummell; for women, it is associated with the Empire silhouette.

### Architecture

English architecture includes prehistoric standing-stone monuments such as Stonehenge, Avebury, Devil’s Arrows, Rudston Monolith, and Castlerigg. Roman building introduced basilicas, baths, amphitheatres, triumphal arches, villas, temples, roads, forts, stockades, and aqueducts; Roman cities and towns included London, Bath, York, Chester, and St Albans. Hadrian’s Wall crosses northern England, while the Roman Baths are located at Bath, Somerset.

Anglo-Saxon ecclesiastical architecture survives in at least fifty English churches. These buildings are generally made of stone or brick and can show reused Roman work, pilaster-strips, blank arcading, baluster shafts, and triangular-headed openings. After the Norman Conquest, Romanesque, or Norman, architecture superseded Anglo-Saxon architecture. Later English Gothic architecture developed through the Early English, Decorated, and Perpendicular periods.

Medieval England produced castles, churches, palaces, universities, parish churches, and large stone houses. Named examples include Windsor Castle, Bodiam Castle, the Tower of London, Warwick Castle, Westminster Abbey, Canterbury Cathedral, Salisbury Cathedral, and York Minster. Tudor, Elizabethan, Jacobean, English Baroque, Queen Anne, Palladian, Georgian, Neoclassical, Regency, and Victorian forms are also identified in the sources. The English country house, stately home, garden, and landscape park are recurring features of English built and designed environments.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Anglo-Saxon metalwork and jewellery from Sutton Hoo are identified as major achievements of early English art. Metalwork was the most highly regarded artistic form among the Anglo-Saxons, whose work favoured brightness and colour. Other surviving or documented objects include the Fuller brooch, the Franks Casket, glass and enamel work, illuminated manuscripts, Nottingham alabaster reliefs, stained glass, and monumental tomb sculpture.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources describe Anglo-Saxon metalwork and jewellery, particularly the objects associated with Sutton Hoo, and identify the Fuller brooch as an example of Anglo-Saxon metalwork. They also describe English portraiture, portrait miniatures, and clothing represented in art.

## Music & performance

The sources describe English folk music as centuries old, with distinct regional variations and peculiarities. They identify sea shanties, jigs, hornpipes, and dance music as important genres or forms. Ballads featuring Robin Hood, printed by Wynkyn de Worde in the 16th century, are described as an important artefact, and John Playford’s work is also named.

The sources state that more English people attend live music performances than football matches. The supplied material also does not document a UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription for England or the English.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name jigs, hornpipes, and dance music as forms within the traditional folk music of England. English literature and theatre are represented through dramatic works by William Shakespeare, including *Hamlet*, *Romeo and Juliet*, *Macbeth*, and *A Midsummer Night’s Dream*.

## Festivals & rituals

They do mention the RHS Chelsea Flower Show, held every year by the Royal Horticultural Society, and identify gardening and visiting gardens as typically English pursuits.

Christian pilgrimage is discussed as a medieval practice. Pilgrims visited churches where saints’ relics were interred, seeking spiritual aid, blessing, or healing. Named pilgrimage centres include St Albans Abbey, Ripon, Durham, Ely, Westminster Abbey, Chichester, Winchester, and Canterbury. Canterbury became a major pilgrimage destination because of the shrine and relics associated with Thomas Becket, who was assassinated in 1170; in the 13th century, Canterbury was second only to Santiago de Compostela as a place of pilgrimage.

## Foodways

English cuisine includes cooking styles, traditions, and recipes associated with England. Traditional foods named in the sources include bread and cheese, roasted and stewed meats, meat and game pies, boiled vegetables, broths with herbs or spices, freshwater and saltwater fish, gingerbread, roast beef, tea with scones, fish and chips, Cornish pasties, crumpets, Yorkshire pudding, shortbread, Lancashire hotpot, steak and kidney pudding, jellied eels, and clotted cream.

The 14th-century cookbook *The Forme of Cury*, associated with the royal court of Richard II and dating to around 1390, records recipes using nutmeg, ginger, cinnamon, cardamon, and cloves. Medieval dishes often had the texture of purées or stews, with sauces thickened by bread or almonds. Named dishes include “mortruys,” “pyany,” and “hyppee.” Later cookbooks include Richard Pynson’s 1500 *Boke of Cokery*, Gervase Markham’s *The English Huswife* from 1615, and Hannah Glasse’s *The Art of Cookery made Plain and Easy*, published in 1747.

English cooking absorbed foreign ingredients and methods. Curry was adapted from Indian cooking from the eighteenth century, while French cuisine influenced English recipes, especially during the Victorian era. Tea, coffee, and chocolate originated outside Europe but became established in England. Elizabeth David’s 1950 *A Book of Mediterranean Food* influenced English cooking after the Second World War. Anglo-Indian dishes named in the sources include kedgeree, Mulligatawny soup, chicken tikka masala, and balti cuisine.

## Oral tradition & literature

English mythology is often described in the sources as a misnomer because England has no unifying or popularly understood national mythology. English-associated mythological material includes ancient Celtic mythology, Anglo-Saxon religion, Breton, Cornish, and Welsh mythology, medieval Welsh literature, and Arthurian legends in the Matter of Britain.

The *Matter of England* is a corpus of chivalric romances written in the 13th and 14th centuries. It includes the legends of Guy of Warwick, Bevis of Hampton, King Horn, Havelok the Dane, and later Robin Hood. These works are connected by English settings and characters but are centred on human activity and difficulties rather than creation myths, cosmic battles, or pre-Christian gods.

*Beowulf* is an Old English epic poem written between the 8th and 11th centuries. It is associated with English cultural memory and Anglo-Saxon audiences, although its story takes place in Denmark and Sweden and its protagonist is a Geatish hero. Robin Hood is described as a heroic outlaw of English folklore, traditionally shown as an archer and swordsman who robs from the rich and gives to the poor. William Shakespeare, Geoffrey Chaucer, Jane Austen, William Blake, Charles Dickens, George Orwell, Agatha Christie, J. R. R. Tolkien, and J. K. Rowling are among the English writers named in the sources.

## Language & religion

English belongs to the Indo-European language family and the West Germanic branch of Germanic languages. Old English emerged from West Germanic dialects spoken by the Anglo-Saxons and was spoken from approximately 450 to 1150. It developed into Middle English after the Norman Conquest in 1066 and later into Modern English. Old English was first written with runes before the adoption of a Latin-based alphabet. The language was influenced by Old Norse and by the Old French spoken by the Norman ruling class. Middle English borrowed vocabulary extensively from French and Latin.

The sources describe Anglo-Saxon religion, medieval Christianity, the veneration of saints, pilgrimage, and the cult of saints’ relics. They also describe the English Reformation, which brought the destruction of almost all wall paintings.
