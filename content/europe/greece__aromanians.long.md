## Overview

The Aromanians (*Armãnji*, *Rrãmãnji*) are an ethnic group native to the southern Balkans who speak Aromanian, an Eastern Romance language descended from the Vulgar Latin of the Romanized Balkans. In Greece they live mainly in Epirus, Macedonia, Thessaly and Aetolia-Acarnania — in the Pindus Mountains, around Olympus and Vermion, and near the Prespa Lakes on the Albanian and North Macedonian border — in upland settlements such as Samarina (*Samarina*, *Xamarina* or *San Marina*), Perivoli (*Pirivoli*) and Smixi (*Zmixi*), and in towns including Metsovo (*Aminciu*), Ioannina (*Ianina*, *Enina*, *Enãna*), Veria (*Veryia*), Grevena (*Grebini*), Trikala (*Trikolj*), Katerini, Agrinio (*Vrachori*) and Thessaloniki (*Sãruna*). Estimates of their number in Greece range from 40,000 to 300,000; the 1923 Treaty of Lausanne put it at 150,000–200,000, while the 1940 and 1951 censuses recorded 26,750 and 22,736 Vlachs. Thede Kahl estimates fewer than 100,000 fluent speakers. They are widely known by the medieval exonym "Vlachs", and are recognised in Greece as a linguistic rather than an ethnic minority.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The objects in this atlas are a woman's festive costume and a few household weavings, and they define what the sources let us say about Aromanian pattern.

**Tapestry-woven aprons** — Aprons built from two equal loom-widths of wool joined along a selvedge to give a horizontal seam, woven tabby (weft-faced) and in tapestry technique. One is predominantly dark red, with isolated elongated diamonds in the centre field and a deep border of linked zigzags; another is dark blue with diamond and zigzag bands at the sides and lower half. Both carry blue, green, maroon and black woollen yarns worked together with gold- and silver-metal-wrapped thread, and all four sides are finished with applied commercial wool braiding and a braided tie.

**Plain-ground wool aprons** — A near-square apron of deep yellow hand-spun, hand-woven wool with a single band of white geometric ornament near the base, its edges bordered in white wool stitching, the hem carrying an added knotted cotton fringe and the ties plaited from yellow and white wool.

**Patterned knitted socks** — Knee-length women's socks knitted in the round in a slight variant of western stocking stitch, in bands of white, red, blue and black wool with gold-coloured metal-wrapped thread, the top third left in natural cream and closed with three rows of garter stitch and plied yarn ties. A second, plainer pair is hand-knitted from hand-spun black and blue wool, each blue band framed by a darned line of gold-coloured metal-wrapped thread that stops short of the sole.

**Panelled fulled-wool outer garments** — Sleeveless coats and waistcoats cut from heavily fulled twill wool and assembled from very large numbers of separate panels — fourteen at the back of one black coat, twenty-two in a dark red waistcoat falling just below the knee — to produce a flared, heavy skirt. The square or deep U-shaped necklines carry dense couched wool braiding, predominantly maroon or black with blue, green and orange wool and yellow and green silk highlights; braiding is repeated at armholes, hem and pocket openings, and one waistcoat has an inscription above the pleated skirt.

**Beaded bib (plastron)** — A rectangle of natural cream twill cloth gathered onto a blue cotton velvet neckband, decorated in a V-shape at the centre top with small transparent, blue, green and red glass beads, gold and black faceted beads, a line of silver-coloured metal sequins, blue and black rickrack and polychrome tubular glass beads.

**Striped hand-spun household weaving** — Utility cloth in undyed and dyed sheep's wool: a narrow bag with red and black stripes, recycled from a larger shepherd's bag and fitted with a plaited wool carrying string, and a tabby-woven napkin of natural undyed wool with narrow stripes of supplementary weft in red and dark blue, edged all round in red wool blanket chain stitch and tied with a twisted red-and-white cord.

**Motif vocabulary.** Isolated elongated diamonds (apron centre fields), linked zigzags (deep apron borders), diamond-and-zigzag bands (apron sides), vertical lines with dashes (apron side borders), a band of white geometric ornament (yellow apron), narrow supplementary-weft stripes (napkin, bag), and the V-shaped beaded panel of the plastron.

### Clothing & dress

In Aromanian rural areas clothing differed from city dress, and the shape and colour of a garment, the volume of the headgear and the shape of a jewel could indicate both cultural affiliation and the specific village a wearer came from. Women's festive attire, as represented here, is layered: a calf-length long-sleeved chemise of beige tabby cotton muslin with natural cream seersucker stripes, cut from four full 36.5 cm weaving widths for a straight line, opening vertically at the centre front to below the bust and trimmed with cream, black and dark blue crocheted silk; over it the beaded plastron at the throat, a pleated tapestry-woven apron, patterned knee-length socks, and a panelled sleeveless coat or waistcoat of fulled wool. Summer footwear is *opanci* — shoes of sewn and plaited dark brown, tan and red-brown leather, a one-piece sole-and-toe sewn up the centre of the toe, black straps across the front and a deep red strap threaded through a suede heel rim and fastened with a metal buckle. For men, the sources record the *fustanella*, whose use among Aromanians can be traced to at least the 15th century — notably on the Aromanian *stećak* of the Radimlja necropolis — and which Aromanians claim as their ethnic costume.

### Architecture

The sources used here say nothing about Aromanian house form, building materials or architectural decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources document no Aromanian ceramic or metalworking tradition; the everyday objects they record are textile. Hand-spun, hand-woven wool serves both carriage and table: a long narrow striped container cut down from a full-sized shepherd's bag, with a small plaited wool carrying string at one side, and a single-loom-width napkin of undyed sheep's wool hemmed top and bottom and finished with blanket chain stitching and a twisted cord. Metal enters the costume rather than the household — gold- and silver-metal-wrapped thread woven into aprons and darned onto socks, silver-coloured sequins on the plastron, and a buckle on the *opanci*.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources note only that the shape of a jewel, like the cut of a garment or the bulk of a headdress, could mark cultural affiliation and village origin; they name no jewelry types, materials or ritual functions, and record no tattooing, henna or hair practices. The nearest documented adornment is sewn rather than worn: the glass, faceted and tubular beads, sequins and rickrack massed in a V on the festive plastron and repeated along its velvet neckband.

## Music & performance

Polyphonic music is common among the Aromanians and follows a common set of rules. The sources name no instruments, song genres or performance occasions, and note that the Aromanian language is almost totally absent from television and that there are no exclusively Aromanian newspapers; the largest Aromanian cultural gatherings in the world are the festivals held at Metsovo, which regularly draw over 40,000 participants.

## Festivals & rituals

The Aromanians are predominantly Orthodox Christians and follow the Eastern Orthodox liturgical calendar; the sources describe no life-cycle rites, though the museum records repeatedly distinguish "festive attire" and "festive costume" from everyday dress, implying a calendar of occasions at which the beaded plastron, metal-threaded aprons and heavy panelled coats were worn. The one dated observance is national rather than agrarian. The Ottoman Empire recognised the Aromanians as a distinct millet, the Ullah millet, on 22 May 1905, with freedoms of worship and education in their own language; the date of the announcement of the irade, 23 May 1905, has in recent times been adopted as **Aromanian National Day** (*Dzua Natsionalã a Armãnjilor*) by Aromanians in Albania, Australia, Bulgaria and North Macedonia — but notably not in Greece or among Aromanians in the Greek diaspora. In Romania the same event is marked on 10 May as Balkan Romanianness Day, an observance extended to the Megleno-Romanians and Istro-Romanians as well. Greek Aromanians took part instead in the Greek Struggle for Independence and are commemorated through that national history; the Metsovo festivals are the principal recurring Aromanian cultural event the sources record.

## Foodways

Aromanian cuisine is strongly influenced by Mediterranean and Middle Eastern cuisine. The sources used name no staple grains, dishes, ceremonial foods or drink traditions.

## Oral tradition & literature

A literature in the Aromanian language exists, and its written tradition is bound up with the Aromanians' position inside the Greek-dominated Rum millet: the first book written in Aromanian used the Greek alphabet and was aimed at spreading Greek among Aromanian-speakers. The Latin-derived character of the language began to be studied in grammars and language booklets in the early 19th century; the work many scholars have called the most valuable for translating the ethnic name is the grammar printed in Vienna in 1813 by Mihail G. Boiagi, titled *Γραμματική Ρωμαϊκή ήτοι Μακεδονοβλαχική / Romanische oder Macedonowlachische Sprachlehre* ("Romance or Macedono-Vlach Grammar"). The term "Aromanian" itself was coined by Gustav Weigand in *Die Aromunen* (1894). Preservation work in Greece is recent and contested: since 1994 the Aristotle University of Thessaloniki has offered beginner and advanced courses in "Koutsovlach"; in 1997 the Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe passed Recommendation 1333 urging Balkan states to address the "critical situation" of Aromanian culture and language, after which President Konstantinos Stefanopoulos publicly urged Greek Aromanians to teach the language to their children. Of more than 200 Vlach cultural associations active in Greece as of 2002, many do not even carry the term "Vlach" in their titles and only a few work on the language.

## Language & religion

Aromanian is an Eastern Romance language, very similar to Romanian, with many slightly varying dialects; it developed from Common Romanian, the shared stage of the Eastern Romance varieties that evolved out of the Vulgar Latin spoken by Paleo-Balkan peoples. It shares features with Albanian, Bulgarian and Greek and has many Greek, Slavic and Turkish loanwords, but its lexicon remains majority Romance. Thede Kahl divides speakers into two main groups with sub-groups: the **Rrãmãnji** (Muzãchiars, Fãrshãrots, Moscopolitans) and the **Armãnji** (Pindeans of the Pindus, Gramustians of the Gramos mountains). Greek Aromanians, nicknamed *Koutsovlachs*, are predominantly Orthodox Christians following the Eastern Orthodox calendar; before the 19th century they were subsumed, as Orthodox Christians, into the Rum millet under the Patriarchate of Constantinople, and most still consider themselves part of the broader Greek nation (*Elini*).
