## Overview

Ukrainians are an East Slavic ethnic group native to Ukraine, where most ethnic Ukrainians live and constitute over three-quarters of the population. Ukrainian communities also exist in Russia, North America, Brazil, Kazakhstan, Moldova, Argentina, Germany, Italy, Belarus, Uzbekistan, the Czech Republic, Spain, Romania, and many other countries; the diaspora is present in more than 120 countries. At around 46 million worldwide, Ukrainians are the second largest Slavic ethnic group after Russians. Their native language is Ukrainian, an East Slavic language descended from the colloquial Old East Slavic language of Kievan Rus’. Most Ukrainians adhere to Eastern Orthodoxy. Their folk-cultural profile is connected in the sources with regional diversity, dialect, dress, folk traditions, religious history, and the continuing importance of Cossack history and symbolism.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources identify dress as one of the particular features associated with several Ukrainian ethnic sub-groups, including Hutsuls, Volhynians, Boykos, and Lemkos.

## Music & performance

The sources identify the Ukrainian national anthem as an important example of modern Ukrainian identity and symbolism, particularly through the frequent emphasis on Cossacks.

Music is mentioned indirectly through the broader cultural history of Ukraine. The sources state that Ukrainian culture developed under Eastern European influence, with some Central European influence, especially in the western region, and with movements brought by the Byzantine Empire and the Renaissance. They also describe a strong Christian culture and a long history of conflict and interaction among Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and Islamic spheres of influence. No particular musical tradition is associated in the supplied material with these cultural influences.

The Cossacks are frequently emphasised in modern Ukrainian identity and symbolism. The Cossack Hetmanate, the Zaporozhian Host, the Zaporozhian Sich, and the Cossacks of Zaporizhzhia are described as important historical elements. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription is supplied for Ukrainian music.

They do state that Hutsuls, Volhynians, Boykos, and Lemkos possess particular areas of settlement, dialect, dress, and folk traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe a religious history centred on the Christianisation of Kievan Rus’. Ukraine was inhabited by pagan tribes until Byzantine rite Christianity was introduced by the turn of the first millennium. The emerging state became influenced by the Byzantine Empire by the 10th century. The first known conversion was that of Princess Saint Olga, who came to Constantinople in 945 or 957. Several years later, her grandson, Prince Vladimir, baptised his people in the Dnieper River. This began a long history of the dominance of Eastern Orthodoxy in Ruthenia, identified in the source with Ukraine.

The supplied material does identify religious communities and historical religious change. Ukrainians are described as majority Eastern Orthodox Christians. The Orthodox Church of Ukraine, headed by Metropolitan Epiphanius, is identified as the most common church, while the Ukrainian Orthodox Church, described as being under the jurisdiction of the Moscow Patriarchate, is more common in small areas of the country. In Halychyna, the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church has strong membership. The sources also mention Protestant churches, including Baptists, Evangelism, and Pentecostalism, as well as Seventh-day Adventists, Jehovah’s Witnesses, Crimean Tatars practising Islam, and Jews and Karaites practising Judaism.

## Foodways

They do state that Ukrainian culture developed through Eastern European influence, with Central European influence especially in the western region, and through historical interaction among Byzantine, Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and Islamic spheres. The supplied material does not connect those influences with particular foods or culinary practices.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources connect the development of Ukrainian identity with language, national revival, historical memory, and literary publication. The publication of Ivan Kotliarevsky’s *Eneyida* (*Aeneid*) in 1798 is described as helping establish the modern Ukrainian language. Together with the subsequent Romantic revival of national traditions and culture, this contributed to the increasing prominence of the ethnonym Ukrainians and the notion of a Ukrainian language at the beginning of the 19th century.

The sources also describe the Ukrainian National Revival in the early 19th century as a period when Ruthenians changed their name because of the regional name. They state that the active development of a concept of a Ukrainian nation and Ukrainian language began during this period. In the 1920s, the Ukrainisation policy pursued by the national Communist leadership of Mykola Skrypnyk encouraged a national renaissance in Ukrainian culture and language as part of the Soviet-wide policy of Korenisation.

## Language & religion

Ukrainian is the sole official language in Ukraine. It belongs to the East Slavic branch of the Slavic languages and is written using the Ukrainian alphabet, one of several alphabets based on the Cyrillic. The language is described as a lineal descendant of the colloquial Old East Slavic language of medieval Kievan Rus’. That language first split into Ruthenian and Russian, while the Ruthenian languages later evolved into modern Ukrainian, Belarusian, and Rusyn.

Historically, Ukrainian was known in Western and Central Europe by the exonym “Ruthenian.” State-enforced Russification saw Ukrainian banned as a subject in schools and as a language of instruction in the Russian Empire. Linguistic oppression continued in various ways during the Soviet period, although Ukrainian continued to be used throughout the country, especially in western Ukraine. In modern Ukraine, most of the population is also fluent in Russian, and many use Russian as their native tongue.

Ukrainians are majority Eastern Orthodox Christians. The sources also identify Ukrainian Greek Catholics, Protestants, Latin Catholics, Jews, Karaites, and Crimean Tatars practising Islam. A 2020 Razumkov Centre survey found that 81.9% of Ukrainian populations adhered to Christianity, while 16.3% claimed no religious affiliation and 1.7% adhered to other religions.
