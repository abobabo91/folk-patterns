## Overview

The Maniots, also called Maniates, are the native Greek inhabitants of the Mani Peninsula in the southern Peloponnese region of Greece. The peninsula has historically also been known as Maina, and the people as Mainotes. Maniots have been known as fiercely independent warriors who engaged in piracy and blood feuds and lived mainly in fortified villages and tower houses. They claim descent from the ancient Spartans and identify with a militaristic culture centered on preserving liberty with arms. They describe the Maniots as speakers of a variety of Modern Greek and document distinctive architecture, dances, cuisine, folklore, clan organization, piracy, and vendetta practices.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Maniots traditionally lived in fortified villages and *tower houses*, which were built as defenses against “Frankish” and Ottoman invaders. The sources connect these buildings closely with Maniot vendettas: families involved in a feud locked themselves in their towers, while other families in the village also took shelter in their towers to avoid the fighting.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention raiding ships in connection with piracy.

## Music & performance

No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription concerns this ethnic group.

## Dance & theatre

Two dances are identified as coming from Mani: *Palio Maniatiko* (Παλιό Μανιάτικο), meaning “Old Maniot,” and the Modern Maniatiko. The Palio Maniatiko is described as an ancient dance found only in Mani. The Modern Maniatiko is its modern version and includes certain aspects of the Kalamatiano dance. Like the Palio Maniatiko, it is performed only in Mani.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that families involved in long-running vendettas sometimes agreed to a temporary *treva* (τρέβα), meaning “truce,” so that harvests could take place and religious ceremonies could be attended. A universal treva was also understood to begin whenever the community faced a Turkish threat. The longest known treva was announced by the Mavromichalis clan when war was declared on the Ottoman Empire in March 1821, beginning the Greek War of Independence. Local Eastern Orthodox Church priests blessed raiding ships before departure, and sometimes joined pirate crews.

## Foodways

The sources list the following local specialities: *Hilopites*, *Kolokythokorfades*, *Paspalas*, *Regali*, a lamb soup; *Tsouchtí*, a pasta with egg dish; *Syglino*, a pork meat coldcut; *Dakos*; *Lalagides* or *Lalagia* (Λαλαγγίδες); and *Diples*, a dessert.

## Oral tradition & literature

Maniot folklore has a strong history of superstition. The most common stories concern witches, demons, vampires, and ghosts. The sources describe a story recorded during Henry Herbert, 3rd Earl of Carnarvon’s tour of Mani in 1839: after finding a fresh egg by the side of the road, he offered it to a Maniot soldier, who refused because a hag might have enchanted it and forced him to marry her. The sources also state that Maniots believed certain areas were haunted by demons.

## Language & religion

Maniots have historically spoken a variety of Modern Greek, described in the sources as either a dialect or an idiom. One feature shared with Tsakonian and with dialects spoken around Athens until the 19th century is the divergent treatment of historical /y/, written <υ>: where the sound merged to /i/ elsewhere, these varieties have /u/ instead. Other features include the palatalization of velar consonants before /i, e, j/. In Outer Mani, family names end in *-eas*, while surnames of Inner Mani end in *-akis*, *-akos*, or, less frequently, *-oggonas*. The sources mention the local Eastern Orthodox Church and its priests.
