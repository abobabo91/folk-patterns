## Overview

The Irish are an ethnic group and nation native to the island of Ireland, sharing common ancestry, history and culture. Ireland consists of the Republic of Ireland, officially called Ireland, and Northern Ireland, part of the United Kingdom; people in Northern Ireland hold Irish, British, or combined national identities. The Irish were primarily a Gaelic people for most of recorded history, although Viking, Norse–Gael, Anglo-Norman, English, Lowland Scots, French Huguenot and other influences shaped the island. The population of Ireland is about 6.9 million, while an estimated 50 to 80 million people worldwide have varying degrees of Irish ancestry. Irish culture includes distinctive languages, music, dance, sports, cuisine, mythology, literature, religious practices, festivals and visual arts.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name Celtic crosses, spiral designs and intricate interlaced patterns of Celtic knotwork in Irish art.

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that the Irish historically had their own style of dress. Irish Travellers are described as historically residing in ornamented barrel top wagons and maintaining rural traditions. The Twelfth of July in Northern Ireland is associated with Orange Banners and sashes, but these are described as elements displayed during parades rather than as a general dress system.

### Architecture

Irish architecture in the sources includes prehistoric monuments, monasteries, churches, castles and urban settlements. Newgrange is a Neolithic megalithic tomb in the Brú na Bóinne complex in County Meath. The stone high cross was a distinctive insular monument, originally painted, and many examples survive. Irish missionaries founded monasteries outside Ireland, including Iona Abbey, the Abbey of St Gall in Switzerland and Bobbio Abbey in Italy. The Viking influx contributed to the founding of important towns including Cork, Dublin, Limerick and Waterford, although earlier Gaelic settlements at these sites did not have the urban character of the later Norse trading ports. Medieval Ireland also saw the development of churches and castles in English-influenced styles.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Irish art includes prehistoric goldwork, bronze objects, illuminated manuscripts, stonework and metalwork. Gold personal ornaments were produced around 2000 BC, including crescent-shaped disks known as *lunulae*, thin decorated gold discs, bands and plaques, earrings, torcs, bangles and collars. The Petrie Crown, Loughnashade Trumpet and Broighter Hoard are named examples of early Irish objects. The Ardagh Chalice and Tara Brooch exemplify Insular art, alongside the Book of Kells. In domestic life, the cauldron, or *coire*, was the main cooking utensil in Gaelic Ireland. A wooden cup with two or four handles, called a *mether* or *meadar* in Irish, served as a communal drinking vessel.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources document Irish personal adornment chiefly through archaeological metalwork. Early gold objects include *lunulae*, torcs, bangles, collars, decorated discs, bands, plaques and objects interpreted as earrings. Celtic brooches in precious metal were important examples of Insular art; the Tara Brooch is described as the most spectacular of about fifty elaborate Celtic brooches found in Ireland. The sources also mention the Petrie Crown and ornamental gold objects from the Bronze Age.

## Music & performance

Irish traditional music, also called Irish folk music, remained vibrant through the 20th and 21st centuries despite emigration and exposure to music from Britain and the United States. It includes drinking songs, ballads and laments, performed unaccompanied or with instruments. Traditional dance music includes reels, hornpipes and jigs; the common double jig is in 6/8 time. The polka arrived at the start of the 19th century, while set dancing may have arrived in the 18th century. Later imported dance signatures included the mazurka and the highlands.

The Irish sources name the harp, fiddle, great Irish warpipes, bagpipes, button accordion, concertina and tiompan. Harp musicians were patronised by the aristocracy until the collapse of Gaelic Ireland in the 18th century. Turlough Carolan, who lived from 1670 to 1738, is described as the best known of these harpists, with more than 200 known compositions. Edward Bunting collected Irish harp tunes at the Belfast Harp Festival in 1792. An older singing style called *sean-nós*, meaning “in the old style,” remained associated mainly with highly poetic songs in Irish.

A revival of Irish traditional music occurred around the turn of the 20th century, and a second revitalisation took place in the 1960s. Seán Ó Riada, The Chieftains, The Clancy Brothers, The Dubliners, Sweeney’s Men, Planxty, The Bothy Band and Clannad are among the named performers and groups associated with these developments. The annual Fleadh Cheoil na hÉireann is described as the largest festival of Irish music in Ireland. Irish traditional music also influenced country and roots music in the United States and later entered rock, punk, electronic and world-music fusions.

## Dance & theatre

Irish traditional dance is associated with céilís, organised competitions and some country houses where local and itinerant musicians were welcomed. Irish dancing was supported by the educational system and patriotic organisations.

Riverdance is a musical and dancing interval act that originally starred Michael Flatley and Jean Butler and featured the choir Anúna. It was performed during the Eurovision Song Contest in 1994, after which an entire musical revue was developed around it. The sources also describe Irish theatre and opera, including works based on Irish legends. The tragedy of Deirdre became source material for plays by William Butler Yeats and John Millington Synge. Opera has been performed in Ireland since the 17th century, while Wexford Festival Opera is a major international festival held every October and November.

## Festivals & rituals

The Irish festival calendar includes Christian, pre-Christian and civic observances. Saint Patrick’s Day is the national holiday in the Republic of Ireland and Northern Ireland. It falls on 17 March and is marked by parades and festivals across the island and by the Irish diaspora worldwide. The festival commemorates Saint Patrick, one of Ireland’s three patron saints. Pious legend associates Patrick with banishing snakes and teaching the concept of the Trinity through the shamrock.

St. Brigid’s Day is observed on 1 February and is associated with St. Brigid, one of Ireland’s patron saints. The sources state that it does not originate in Christianity but is another religious observance superimposed at the beginning of spring; it is the only official public holiday in Ireland named after a woman. Other pre-Christian festivals whose names survive as Irish month names are Bealtaine in May, Lúnasa in August and Samhain in November. Samhain is associated with Halloween, which is celebrated on the night of 31 October. Traditional Halloween customs include disguising children, going door to door requesting food or coins, carving turnips into lanterns, apple bobbing, lighting bonfires and displaying fireworks.

Christmas traditions include nativity scenes, known as “cribs,” and Wrenboys on 26 December, Saint Stephen’s Day. In Northern Ireland, the Twelfth of July, or Orangemen’s Day, commemorates William III’s victory at the Battle of the Boyne and includes parades organised by the Orange Order. Life-cycle practices are less fully documented: baptisms, funerals and weddings are usually conducted by a priest of the local parish.

## Foodways

Irish cuisine developed through crops and animals farmed in a temperate climate, together with fresh fish and seafood from the Atlantic Ocean. Historically important foods included oats, wheat, barley, bread, milk, butter, cheese, beef, pork, sheep, fish, shellfish, hazelnuts, berries and potatoes. Before the potato arrived in the 16th century, grains such as oats, wheat and barley formed the staple diet, cooked as porridge or bread. Oat flatbread was common, while wheat bread was regarded as a luxury of the aristocratic class. Traditional porridge was made from oats, barley or wheat meal mixed with water, buttermilk or new milk and accompanied by butter or honey.

In Gaelic Ireland, the main meal was eaten in the afternoon or evening. A daytime meal was called *díthat*, while a night meal, especially a celebratory one, was called a *feis* and was often accompanied by beer. Meals consisted of bread, fresh milk or fermented milk products such as *bainne clabhair*, yoghurt or cheese, with *anlann* or *tarsunn*, a relish or condiment usually made from vegetables, salted meat or honey. The cauldron, or *coire*, was used for broths and stews.

The potato was introduced in the second half of the 16th century and became the main field crop of tenant and labouring classes. By the 21st century, representative dishes included Irish stew, bacon and cabbage, boxty, brown bread, soda bread, coddle and colcannon. Traditional cuisine also includes the Irish breakfast and potato bread. The sources describe *fulachtaí fia* as crescent-shaped mounds of burnt stones associated with cooking sites, using heated stones to boil, steam or bake food.

## Oral tradition & literature

Irish mythology was originally passed down orally in the prehistoric era. Christian scribes wrote down and revised many myths in the early medieval period. The mythology is conventionally grouped into cycles: the Mythological Cycle, Ulster Cycle, Fianna Cycle and Kings’ Cycle. Important works include the *Lebor Gabála Érenn*, the *Cath Maige Tuired*, the *Aided Chlainne Lir*, the *Táin Bó Cúailnge*, the *Acallam na Senórach* and *Buile Shuibhne*.

The Ulster Cycle concerns heroes including Cú Chulainn, Conchobar mac Nessa and the Ulaid, with much of its action centred on Emain Macha, known in English as Navan Fort. The Fianna Cycle concerns Fionn mac Cumhaill and the Fianna, professional warriors associated with hunting, fighting, poetry and the spirit world. Irish folklore also includes the leprechaun, pooka, banshees, Cailleach and Oilliphéist.

Folktales were preserved through traditional storytelling, monastic scribes, bards and families. The Irish Folklore Commission gathered folktales from the Irish populace from 1935 onward. The Gaelic revival encouraged renewed interest in Irish language, folklore, mythology, sports, music and arts. The Gaelic League, or *Conradh na Gaeilge*, was established in 1893 and promoted Irish in everyday life through gatherings, conversation meetings, publications and education.

## Language & religion

Irish, or *Gaeilge*, is a Celtic language within the Indo-European family. It was the first language of the majority of the population until the 19th century, when English became dominant. Irish has three major dialects: Connacht, Munster and Ulster Irish. Its writing system, Ogham, dates to at least the 4th century AD and was gradually replaced by Latin script from the 5th century. Irish is the national and first official language of the Republic of Ireland, an official language of Northern Ireland and one of the official languages of the European Union.

Irish and English are the most widely spoken languages in Ireland. Irish remains a first language in Gaeltacht regions, and Shelta is spoken widely by Travellers. The sources describe Roman Catholicism as the majority religion on the island, alongside Anglican, Presbyterian, Methodist, Orthodox and other Christian communities, as well as Buddhist, Hindu, Baháʼí, Pagan, Islamic and Jewish communities. Popular Catholic practices include roadside shrines and grottoes dedicated to the Virgin Mary, prayers for the intercession of saints and making the sign of the cross when passing a church or graveyard.
