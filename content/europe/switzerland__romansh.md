## Overview

The Romansh people are a Romance ethnic group native to the Swiss canton of Grisons (Graubünden), where Romansh is spoken predominantly in the Romansh-speaking valleys. Their language belongs to the Romance languages and is classified within the Gallo-Romance branch; its relationship to Ladin and Friulian as a possible Rhaeto-Romance subgroup remains disputed. As of 2017, about 45,000 Romansh people lived in Switzerland, including about 30,000 inhabitants of Grisons. In the 2019 assessment, 40,074 Swiss residents primarily spoke Romansh. The language area includes the Vorderrhein (Rein Anteriur) and Hinterrhein (Ragn Posteriur) valleys, the Gelgia and Albula/Alvra valleys, the Upper and Lower Engadine, and Val Müstair. Romansh culture is especially documented through its regional dialects, written literature, language movement, and continuing negotiation with German and Italian.

## Music & performance

The sources identify several written and religious works connected with Romansh language history. In the 16th century, Gian Travers wrote the first surviving work in Romansh, *Chianzun dalla guerra dagl Chiaste da Müs*, in the Putèr dialect. Written in 1527, the epic poem describes the first Musso war, in which Travers himself had taken part. Travers also translated numerous biblical plays into Romansh, although only the titles survive for many of them.

Giachem Bifrun, another early writer working in Putèr, produced the first printed book in Romansh, a catechism published in 1552. In 1560 he published a translation of the New Testament, *L'g Nuof Sainc Testamaint da nos Signer Jesu Christ*. In 1562, Durich Chiampel published *Cudesch da Psalms*, a collection of church songs in the Vallader dialect. These works indicate the importance of religious writing and song within the documented literary history of Romansh.

The sources also mention *Igl Vêr Sulaz da pievel giuvan* (“The true joys of young people”), a series of religious instructions for Protestant youths published by Steffan Gabriel in 1611, and a Catholic catechism, *Curt Mussament*, published in 1615 by Gion Antoni Calvenzano.

## Festivals & rituals

Religious instruction and church writing are documented, particularly in the 16th and 17th centuries.

The documented religious works include the Vallader collection of church songs *Cudesch da Psalms*, published in 1562; the Sutsilvan catechism *Curt mussameint dels principals punctgs della Christianevla Religiun*, published in 1601; the Protestant instructions *Igl Vêr Sulaz da pievel giuvan*, published in 1611; the Catholic catechism *Curt Mussament*, published in 1615; and the complete translation of the Bible, *Bibla da Cuera*, published between 1717 and 1719.

## Oral tradition & literature

Romansh literary history developed through several regional written varieties. The earliest writing identified as Romansh dates from the 10th or 11th century, while major works began to appear in the 16th century. Gian Travers’s *Chianzun dalla guerra dagl Chiaste da Müs*, written in 1527 in Putèr, is identified as the first surviving work in Romansh. Its subject is the first Musso war, in which Travers had participated.

The first printed book in Romansh was Giachem Bifrun’s catechism, published in 1552. The first translation of the New Testament into Sursilvan was published in 1648 by Luci Gabriel, the son of Steffan Gabriel. The first complete translation of the Bible, *Bibla da Cuera*, appeared between 1717 and 1719. Early writing in the Sursilvan and Sutsilvan dialects often focused on religious themes, especially conflicts between Protestants and Counter-Reformers.

During the 19th century, Romansh speakers experienced a literary revival and began a language movement dedicated to halting the language’s decline. In 1919, the Lia Rumantscha was founded as an umbrella organization for regional language societies. A pan-regional written variety, Rumantsch Grischun, was introduced in 1982, but acceptance has remained limited.

## Language & religion

Romansh is a Romance language of the Gallo-Romance and/or Rhaeto-Romance branch. It descends from the spoken Latin brought to the region by Roman soldiers, merchants, and officials after the Roman conquest of the modern-day Grisons area in 15 BC. Before Romanization, inhabitants spoke Celtic and Raetic languages. Romansh retains a small number of words from those languages and has been strongly influenced by German in vocabulary and morphosyntax.

The language is divided into five regional dialect groups, each with its own standardized written language: Sursilvan, Sutsilvan, Surmiran, Putèr, and Vallader. Jauer is a dialect of Val Müstair closely related to Vallader, while Tuatschin is associated with Tujetsch and Val Medel. Romansh is geographically divided into non-adjacent parts because of the spread of German.

Romansh has been recognized as a national language of Switzerland since 1938 and as an official language in correspondence with Romansh-speaking citizens since 1996. It has official status in Grisons alongside German and Italian and is used as a medium of instruction in schools in Romansh-speaking areas. The sources discuss Protestant and Catholic writing.
