## Overview

The Janjevci are a Croat community in Kosovo, inhabiting the town of Janjevo and surrounding villages near Pristina, as well as villages centered on Letnica near Vitia: Šašare, Vrnez, and Vrnavokolo. They are also known as Letničani and are not officially recognised as a national minority group. The community derives its name from Janjevo and is considered among the oldest Croatian diaspora communities. Its members are associated with Catholicism, the Janjevo-Lepenica idiom, folk costumes, songs, oral traditions, goldsmithing, and trade. According to the Kosovan 2011 census, there was a total of ca. 400 Janjevci, of whom 80 remained in the Vitia municipality. During and after the Kosovo War, most of the community fled to Croatia.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Bošča** — A richly decorated apron worn over *dimije* by women. The source describes it as part of women’s dress.

**Šamija** — A headscarf worn by women. It was woolen outside the house and ordinary inside the house.

**Duak** — A veil included in women’s wedding clothing.

**Rubije** — Ducats worn as head jewelry in women’s wedding clothing. The source specifies two rows of *rubije*.

### Clothing & dress

Adult men’s clothing consisted of a mixture of oriental and Bulgarian costumes. Men wore a *šubara* on the head, a shirt, *čakšire*—long trousers with a long tour and narrow legs that fasten on the sides—and a *mintan* or *palta*, described as a coat. A belt was tied around the middle of the body, and *kondure* or *jemenije* were worn on the feet.

Women retained an oriental costume influenced by the Ottomans. Married women wore a *fes* or *otos*, a cloth cap sewn from velvet, while unmarried women wore a *kucelj*, or braid, down their backs. A *šamija* was also worn on the head. Women wore a *mintan* or *mintanče*, a jacket, and a *jelek* or *žamadan*, a vest, over a shirt with a lace collar. They wore *dimije* rather than skirts, with a richly decorated *bošča* over them and a *kušak* around the waist. On working days they wore *nalunas*; when leaving the house, they put on *jemenije*. Formal clothing was accompanied by jewelry. Wedding clothing included a *mintanče*, *dimije*, shirts, a fez, *terlik*, *duak*, earrings, a ring, two rows of *rubije*, and one larger Ducat on the forehead.

### Architecture

They do mention *zagrađi* in Letnica: special buildings arranged in a row with a common wall that closed in a circle. Janjevci stayed in these buildings during family pilgrimages connected with the feast of the Assumption.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources do not catalogue museum objects or provide a detailed account of ceramics, wooden objects, tools, or household goods. They record that Janjevci in Croatia are widely known as goldsmiths and merchants and are recognised for their craftsmanship, *kujundžijstvo*. The language section names *džam*, glass; *ćumbe*, stove; *tendžera*, pot; and *duvar*, wall.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Women’s formal clothing was accompanied by *dukati*, gold necklaces, *đinđuves*, described as pearl necklaces, *curaće*, earrings, and rings. Women’s wedding clothing included earrings, a ring, two rows of *rubije* as head jewelry, and one larger Ducat placed on the forehead.

## Music & performance

The sources state that Albanian, Bulgarian, Serbian, and Turkish influences are visible in Janjevci folk costumes, songs, and oral tradition.

## Dance & theatre

They mention a celebration called *rifana*, associated with Saint George’s Day and lasting all night, but describe it as a celebration involving mischievous *bećari* who tried to steal prepared food from girls they loved and cut the rope on the swings on which the girls were swinging.

## Festivals & rituals

Janjevci have maintained their Catholic faith. Most families celebrate Saint Nicholas on December 6 and May 9, as well as Christmas and *Veligdan*, described as “Great Day,” or Easter. Saint Sebastian, Saint Anne, and Anthony of Padua are also celebrated. On the evening before the main feast, a priest blesses traditional *Pogača* bread and a candle, which are used at the family gathering.

Family pilgrimages are made in Letnica for *Gulem Gospođindan*, described as “Great Lady-day,” or the Assumption of Mary; *Mali Gospođindan*, “Small Lady-day,” or the Birth of Mary; Our Lady of Mount Carmel; and St. Anne. Pilgrimages are also made in Pristina for the feast of St. Anthony of Padua. Janjevci went to Letnica for the feast of the Assumption from the feast of St. Ane on July 26, remaining there until August 19. The whole family life then moved to Letnica, where families stayed in *zagrađi*.

The community attends the Feast of St. Blaise in Dubrovnik and participates in its procession in folk costumes. Saint George’s Day, known among Janjevci as *Đurđevdan*, was especially important for teenagers, who prepared *rifana*. The parish church celebrates the Translation of the Relics of Saint Nicholas from Myra to Bari, known as *sveti Nikola ljetni*, *župna slava*, or *dan župe*. On the feast of St. John the Baptist, children jumped over burning straw in a custom called *kalavešnica*, connected in the source with the pre-Christian celebration of the summer solstice.

## Foodways

They mention traditional *Pogača* bread, which is blessed by a priest with a candle on the evening before the main feast and used at the family gathering. During *rifana*, *bećari* tried to steal prepared food from the girls they loved.

## Oral tradition & literature

Janjevci have several legends related to the origin of Janjevo. One tells of a sister and brother living alone in the *dilbokoj planini*, “deep mountain,” after a general famine; they did not know that they were related to the new Janjevo. A second story concerns the terrible fate of people after the war and says that only Jana Palić, called “Nedokoljka,” and one of the Glasnovićs remained alive. Both stories are based on massive deaths and suffering during the Mongol invasion of the area.

The sources also state that Albanian, Bulgarian, Serbian, and Turkish influences are visible in Janjevci oral tradition.

## Language & religion

According to Croatian classification, Janjevci speak the Janjevo-Lepenica idiom, which belongs to the Torlak dialect group. Names for parts of the day borrowed from Turkish include *saba*, morning; *ićindije*, afternoon after sunset; and *akšam*, dusk after sunset. Other Turkicisms include *belendzika*, a thin oriental bracelet in a series of five to six; *džam*, glass; *ćumbe*, stove; *tendžera*, pot; and *duvar*, wall. From Bulgarian *chromid* they took *chromit*, onion. The sources also record Albanisms such as *tota*, grandmother, and *nana*, mother.

Janjevci have maintained their Catholic faith. The source describes family patron-saint observances, pilgrimages, priestly blessing of bread and a candle, and the veneration of St. Nicholas. It also connects *kalavešnica* with a pre-Christian celebration of the summer solstice.
