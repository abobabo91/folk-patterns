## Overview

The Mijaks (Macedonian: Мијаци, romanized: Mijaci) are an ethnographic group of Macedonians living in the Lower Reka region of western North Macedonia, along the Radika river. Their territory, including the Bistra mountain and Radika region, is also known as Mijačija. Their principal settlement is Galičnik, although many Mijak villages became uninhabited after inhabitants left during the twentieth century. The group is estimated at 30,000–60,000 people. The sources describe the Mijaks as predominantly practicing animal husbandry and as especially known for ecclesiastical architecture, woodworking, iconography, masonry, preserved customs, and the characteristic Galičnik dialect of Macedonian.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do document *pečalba*, a deeply entrenched custom of seasonal work in which men in their twenties would leave their village for months or years to work in more prosperous regions and create wealth for their families. This practice contributed to the dispersion of Mijak families and to the desertion or sparse settlement of villages, but the sources do not associate it with a described clothing style.

### Architecture

Architecture is a defining feature of Mijak culture. The Mijaks were regarded as highly skilled masons and helped wealthy Aromanians develop Kruševo into a large, prosperous, and beautiful city in the eighteenth century. Apart from masons from the Kriva Palanka region, the source describes them as the most proficient masons in Macedonia and the Balkans. The Saint Jovan Bigorski Monastery is built in the Mijak style. The Mijaks also produced the intricate wood carving found inside that monastery, which the source describes as the best in North Macedonia.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do document woodworking and woodcarving, including the intricate carving inside the Saint Jovan Bigorski Monastery. A woodcarving school operated for many years in the Mala Reka region, and the Mijaks are described as having mastered the craft of woodcarving.

## Music & performance

The sources used provide little information about Mijak music. They identify Aleksandar Sarievski as a traditional singer among notable Mijak people.

Performance is documented in connection with the Galičnik Wedding Festival. This is an annual traditional wedding and its characteristic ceremony, held on Petrovden, identified in the source as the St. Peter feast day and dated 12 July. During the festival, a couple is chosen to receive the wedding and the event is shown on national television.

## Dance & theatre

The Mijak shepherd dance *Teškoto oro*, whose title is glossed as “the hard one,” is identified as one of the national dances of North Macedonia. The source describes it as a folk dance of the Mijaks.

## Festivals & rituals

The principal named Mijak festival in the sources is the Galičnik Wedding Festival, known in Macedonian as Галичка свадба. It is held annually on Petrovden, the St. Peter feast day, 12 July. The event centers on a traditional wedding and its characteristic ceremony; a selected couple receives the wedding and the ceremony is shown on national television.

The sources also record a historical religious change in Galičnik: its Islamized population was re-Christianized in 1843. A proportion of Mijaks converted to Islam during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and became known as Torbeši.

## Foodways

They state that the Mijaks practiced predominantly animal husbandry. No food tradition can therefore be described from the supplied material.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources connect Mijak history with legends concerning the founding of Galičnik and record several theories about the group’s origins. One theory holds that the Mijaks were the first to settle permanently in the area, where they encountered Vlachs; another presents them as the remains of an old Slavic tribe associated with the Salonica field and cattle breeding.

Georgi Pulevski wrote about the places where the Mijaks were concentrated, their migrations, and the Mijak region. In 1822, Panajot Ginovski wrote an unpublished lexicographical work entitled *Mijački rečnik po našem govoru*, containing 20,000 words. The notable Mijak writers listed in the source include Dimitrija Čupovski, Risto Ognjanovikj-Lonoski, Isaija Mažovski, Georgi Pulevski, and Toma Smiljanić-Bradina.

## Language & religion

The Mijaks traditionally speak the Galičnik dialect and the Reka dialect of Macedonian. The source also refers to *Mijački govor*, or Mijak speech, and gives examples of distinctive forms including *ovde*, *onde*, *kode*, *koga*, *zašto*, and *dojdi*.

Religiously, the sources describe both Christian and Muslim elements in Mijak history. A proportion of Mijaks converted to Islam during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and became known as Torbeši. The Islamized population of Galičnik was re-Christianized in 1843. In 1875, a referendum concerning church affiliation was held among Christians in Debar county; the majority supported accession to the Bulgarian Exarchate, while two villages and twenty houses in Debar supported the Patriarchate of Constantinople.
