## Overview

Serbs are a South Slavic ethnic group and nation sharing Serbian ancestry, culture, history, and language. They live primarily in Serbia and in neighboring Bosnia and Herzegovina, Montenegro, Croatia, and Kosovo, with smaller communities in North Macedonia, Romania, and Hungary, as well as diaspora communities across Europe, the Americas, and Oceania. Serbia’s Serbs number 5.3 million and make up 80.6% of the population; approximately 1.5 million Serbs live in bordering countries and an estimated 1.6 million in the diaspora. They speak Serbian, a South Slavic language and normative variety of Serbo-Croatian. Their folk-culture profile includes icon painting, frescoes, ecclesiastical architecture, epic poetry, gusle performance, and varied traditional music and dance.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Traditional Serbian visual arts and ecclesiastical architecture are strongly influenced by Byzantine traditions, with additional Mediterranean and Western elements.

## Music & performance

Traditional Serbian folk music is described as rich and varied. Its central named instrument is the *gusle*, a bowed single-stringed musical instrument and musical style that is always accompanied by singing. The gusle holds a central place in Serbian epic poetry and accompanies a centuries-old oral tradition of patriotic songs. The vast majority of these epics center on the Ottoman occupation and the Serbian struggle for liberation.

Flutes and trumpets are also used in traditional Serb folk music.

The sources describe Serbian visual culture alongside music: Serbs are renowned for contributions to icon painting, literature, music, dance, and medieval architecture. Traditional Serbian visual arts, especially frescoes and icons, are connected to Byzantine traditions while also incorporating Mediterranean and Western elements.

## Festivals & rituals

Religious history is documented in broader terms. Before Christianization in the 9th century, the Serbs followed Slavic paganism. After the East–West Schism of 1054, they aligned with Eastern Orthodoxy. During the centuries of Ottoman rule, the Serbian Orthodox Church remained the only permitted Serb institution and preserved language, literacy, written history, and national consciousness.

## Oral tradition & literature

Serbian oral tradition is closely associated with the gusle and with epic poetry. The instrument accompanies patriotic songs transmitted through a centuries-old oral tradition. Most of these epics concern the Ottoman occupation and the Serbian struggle for liberation.

The symbolic Kosovo Myth is a central Serbian national myth and a major theme in Serbian folklore and literary tradition. It symbolizes martyrdom and the defense of the Serbian homeland and Christendom against Ottoman Muslims.

Serbs are also described as renowned for contributions to literature.

## Language & religion

Serbian belongs to the South Slavic group of languages and is a normative variety of Serbo-Croatian, making it mutually intelligible with Bosnian, Croatian, and Montenegrin. Serbian is official in Serbia, co-official in Bosnia-Herzegovina and disputed territory of Kosovo, and a recognized minority language in Montenegro, Croatia, North Macedonia, Romania, Hungary, Czech Republic, and Slovakia.

Serbian has active digraphia, using both Cyrillic and Latin scripts. Serbian Cyrillic was devised in 1814 by Serbian linguist Vuk Karadžić according to strict phonemic principles. In Serbia, Cyrillic has constitutional status as the “official script,” while Latin is designated as a script in official use. Serbian Cyrillic is also in official use in Montenegro and Bosnia-Herzegovina alongside the Latin alphabet. Older literary Serbian includes Church Slavonic of the Serbian recension, which continues in the liturgy of the Serbian Orthodox Church.

Serbs are predominantly Eastern Orthodox Christians and are traditionally affiliated with the Serbian Orthodox Church. Smaller Catholic and Muslim Serb communities have existed at various points in history. A substantial number of Serbs converted to Islam during Ottoman rule, particularly in Bosnia and Herzegovina and Sandžak. Historically, some Serbs in coastal Dalmatia belonged to the Catholic Church, including communities associated with the Bay of Kotor and Dubrovnik.
