## Overview

The Székelys, also referred to as Szeklers, are a Hungarian subgroup living mainly in the Székely Land in Romania. The region roughly corresponds to Harghita, Covasna, and central and eastern Mureș counties, where they make up roughly 80% of the population. The Székelys identify themselves as Hungarians while maintaining a somewhat distinct ethnic identity. They were estimated to number about 860,000 in the 1970s and are officially recognized as a distinct minority group by the Romanian government. The Székelys of Bukovina form a culturally separate group, now settled mostly in Vojvodina and southern Hungary. Their cultural significance in the sources lies especially in their historical identity, language, traditions, literature, symbols, and political history.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources do state that the Székelys have preserved traditions to an extent unusual even in Central and Eastern Europe.

## Music & performance

 They mention the folklorist Elek Benedek and several Székely writers, but provide no description of music or musical practice.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources document political commemorations rather than a complete annual ritual calendar. A major peaceful demonstration was held in 2006 in favor of autonomy. In 2013 and 2014, thousands of ethnic Hungarians marched for autonomy on 10 March, identified as Székely Freedom Day, in Târgu Mureș, Romania. The date commemorates the execution in Târgu Mureș in 1854, by the Austrian authorities, of three Székelys who tried to achieve national self-determination. Since 2015, the Székelys have also observed Székely Autonomy Day, celebrated every last Sunday of October.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe historical legends and a substantial literary association with Székely identity. Ancient legends recount that a contingent of Huns remained in Transylvania and later allied with the main Hungarian army that conquered the Carpathian Basin in the 9th century. The thirteenth-century chronicler Simon of Kéza claimed that the Székely people descended from Huns who lived in mountainous lands before the Hungarian conquest.

Áron Tamási, a 20th-century Székely writer from Lupeni, Harghita, wrote novels about the Székely that placed stories of love and self-individuation against the background of Székely village culture. Other named Székely writers are the folklorist Elek Benedek, the novelist József Nyírő, and the poet Sándor Kányádi. In Bram Stoker’s novel *Dracula*, the title character declares himself to be a Székely.

The sources also mention a language tradition. Nicolaus Olahus wrote in *Hungaria et Athila* in 1536 that Hungarians and Székelys shared the same language, while the Székelys had words specific to their nation. In 1558, Mihály Vilmányi Libécz described the language of the ancient Székelys as a model of the purest Hungarian tongue.

## Language & religion

The Székelys speak Hungarian, and research cited in the sources indicates that their dialect does not contain more Bulgaro-Turkish loanwords from before the Hungarian conquest of the Carpathian Basin than standard Hungarian. Toponyms in the Székely settlement area are also presented as evidence of a Hungarian mother tongue. The sources state that, after the Hungarians became Christians in the 11th century, the importance of the Sun and Moon became purely visual and symbolic. They do state that the Székelys were considered a distinct ethnic group, *natio Siculica*, and that they have maintained a distinct identity from other Hungarians.
