## Overview

The Cornish, or *Kernowyon*, are a Celtic ethnic group native to Cornwall, a region of the United Kingdom. Their historical homeland includes Cornwall and the Isles of Scilly, and Cornish communities also exist among emigrant and descendant populations. In the 2021 census, Cornwall, including the Isles of Scilly, had a population of 570,300; within Cornwall, 79,938 people specified a Cornish-only identity and 9,146 specified Cornish in combination with British. Cornish is a Celtic language of the Southwestern Brittonic branch, closely related to Breton and Welsh. Cornish culture is significant in folk-culture studies for its language revival, folk music, processional dances, festivals, mining traditions, folklore, distinctive architecture, cuisine and continuing assertions of a separate national identity.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name Celtic designs in connection with Cornish tin jewellery.

### Clothing & dress

The Cornish music sources mention white bonnets purchased for minstrels in the household accounts of the Arundells of Lanherne, Mawgan-in-Pydar, during the Twelve Days of Christmas in 1466–67, as well as cloth, bells and materials for costumes used in “disgysing,” or mumming and guise dancing. These records concern performance clothing rather than a general dress tradition.

### Architecture

Cornish vernacular architecture uses abundant natural stone, especially Cornubian granite, together with slate and local white lime-washing. Its plain, unadorned simplicity shares cultural and stylistic similarities with Atlantic Brittany, Ireland, Wales and neighbouring Devon. West Cornish towns such as St Ives are particularly distinctive for solid granite and early promontory hermitages associated with Celtic Christianity.

Stone architecture has been used continuously for more than two thousand years. Romano-British enclosed courtyard houses at Carn Euny and Chysauster differ from the largely rectangular, timber-derived architecture of Saxon England and often use rounded or circular forms. These include ringforts, roundhouses and enclosed settlements known locally as *rounds*. The medieval longhouse was typical of early Cornish *Tre* dispersed settlements of farmsteads and associated field systems. It combined human accommodation and livestock under one roof.

Cornish and Devon churches commonly have one or two aisles, a western tower, wagon roofs and arcades with standard types of piers. The typical tower has three stages and buttresses set back from the angles. Norman fonts of the Altarnun type and recognisably Cornish benchend carving are also documented.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources used document Cornish metalwork more fully than ceramics. The Trewhiddle style is an insular style of black-and-white niello metalwork named after a Cornish find from the 9th century. Tin and copper are symbolic of Cornwall’s traditional industries, alongside fish. Cornish tin is highly prized for jewellery, particularly work featuring mine engines or Celtic designs.

The Cornish stove, commonly known as the “slab,” was made of cast iron with brass knobs and was found in most kitchens in west Cornwall. The sources also describe clome, or cloam, ovens: masonry ovens with removable clay or cast-iron doors, commonly built into chimney breasts. The St Ives Museum includes a reconstruction of a traditional Cornish kitchen.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Cornish tin jewellery is associated with mine-engine and Celtic designs. The sources also describe the Duchy of Cornwall shield, consisting of 15 gold bezants on a black field, as a Cornish symbol, although it is not identified as personal adornment.

## Music & performance

Cornwall has a long musical history shaped by 20th-century revivals. Its traditions include Cornish folk music, pipers, brass and silver bands, male voice choirs, classical music, electronic music and popular music. Medieval records describe performances of miracle plays in the Cornish language with considerable musical involvement. Minstrels were hired for saint’s day celebrations, and wealthy families retained their own minstrels.

Traditional instruments include the crwth, or crowd, similar to a violin; the bombarde, or horn-pipe; bagpipes; harp; fiddle; crowdy crawn, a drum; banjo; accordion; and brass and reed instruments. A late-medieval line-up documented in Cornish iconography could include crwth, bombarde, bagpipes and harp. The crowdy crawn with a crwth or fiddle was popular by the 19th century.

Cornish music is closely connected with Brittany. Older songs and carols share roots with Breton tunes, and the Cornish and Breton languages were mutually intelligible until Tudor times. Cornish dance music includes *Troyls*, dance nights comparable to a ceilidh, and *Nozow looan*, comparable to a Breton Fest Noz. Bands associated with the *nos lowen* style follow the Breton style of uncalled line dances.

Named songs include “Sweet Nightingale,” “Little Eyes,” “Lamorna,” “Trelawny,” “Camborne Hill,” “The White Rose,” “Bro Goth Agan Tasow,” “Hail to the Homeland” and “Cornwall My Home.” The Cornish anthem used by Gorseth Kernow is “Bro Goth Agan Tasow,” meaning “The Land of My Fathers,” or literally “Old Country of our Fathers.” Cornish-language music continues through bands including Dalla, Skwardya and Krena, while Radyo an Gernewegva broadcasts Cornish music through community radio stations and online.

## Dance & theatre

Cornish dance includes processional dances, *furry dances*, social set dances, linear and circle dances, and competitive step dances. The Helston Furry is described as the best-known tradition, with “furry” used for a dance or associated tune. The Padstow *’Obby ’Oss* festival also includes a named local tradition of processional performance.

Other dance forms include *Troyls*, which resemble ceilidhs, and *Nozow looan*, which resemble Breton Fest Noz events. The 19th-century social dance *Joan Sanderson*, also called the cushion dance, has 17th-century origins.

Cornwall produced passion plays during the Middle Ages, including the *Ordinalia*, a cycle comprising *Origo Mundi*, *Passio Christi* and *Resurrexio Domini*. Other surviving plays include *Beunans Meriasek* and *Beunans Ke*. Many plays were performed in open-air round theatres known as *plen a gwary*. The theatre company Kneehigh Theatre is active in Cornwall, while the open-air Minack Theatre is well known.

## Festivals & rituals

Cornish festivals combine processional dance, music, seasonal celebration and Celtic connections. The Padstow *’Obby ’Oss* festival takes place on 1 May, the feast of Beltane to Celtic people. The Helston Furry is a major processional dance tradition. In Helston, townspeople dance the Furry Dance through streets, shops and private houses on 8 May.

Golowan festival in Penzance was revived in 1991. It belongs to a wider midsummer tradition in which bonfires were lit on hilltops on Midsummer’s Eve. Midsummer bonfires continue throughout Cornwall, although less extensively than when fires could be seen on every hilltop.

Lowender Perran is held at the end of October in Perranporth and brings together musicians and dancers from the six Celtic nations. Cornwall also hosts Cornish-Breton festivals, including AberFest in Falmouth, or *Aberfal*. The Cornwall Folk Festival has been held annually for more than three decades, and other festivals include Lowender Peran and Golowan.

The sources document ceremonies connected with Cornish language and agricultural or seasonal practice, including Crying the Neck and annual mid-summer bonfires.

## Foodways

Cornish cuisine developed in relation to Cornwall’s peninsula, fishing industry, fertile fishing grounds and mining history. Fish dishes form an important part of historical and modern recipes. Newlyn is described as the largest fishing port in the United Kingdom by value of fish landed.

The pasty is Cornwall’s iconic dish and is associated with mining. Modern pasties commonly contain beef steak, onion, potato and turnip, with salt and white pepper. Other varieties include cheese and onion, Steak and stilton, vegetarian, and pork and apple pasties. Historically, “Turmut, ’tates and mate” described a common filling of swede, potatoes and meat. Pasties could also contain leeks, watercress, parsley, shallots, jam, apple, blackberry, plums or cherries. They are often locally called *oggies*.

*Stargazy pie* is a fish-based pie with fish heads protruding through the crust. It is prepared for celebrations associated with Tom Bawcock’s Eve. Other documented dishes include “gerty milk,” boiled beef, squab pie, “scrowled pilchards,” heavy cake, hog’s pudding and gurty pudding.

Cornish dairy traditions include clotted cream, Cornish fudge, Cornish ice cream and the Cornish cream tea. In Cornwall, clotted cream is served on top of the jam. Named cheeses include Cornish Blue, Cornish Brie, Gevrik, Keltic Gold, Menallack, Nanterrow, Tesyn, Cornish Yarg, Cornish Garland and Tiskey Meadow. Cakes and sweet dishes include saffron cake, heavy or *hevva* cake, fairing biscuits, figgy ’obbin, fuggan, scones and whortleberry pie.

## Oral tradition & literature

Cornish folklore includes tales of giants, mermaids, buccas, piskies, pixies and the *pobel vean*, or little people. Storytelling events may include a *droll teller*. The fairy tale Jack the Giant Killer takes place in Cornwall. Cornish mythology also connects Cornwall with King Arthur, whose birthplace is associated in early British legends with Tintagel, and with the court of King Mark of Cornwall, Tristan and Iseult.

The legend of Lyonesse concerns land supposedly lost to the sea in one night. Other traditions include the Mermaid of Zennor, Tom Bawcock, the Mermaid of Padstow and legends surrounding the Merry Maidens stone circle. The Merry Maidens story says that nineteen maidens were turned to stone for dancing on a Sunday; the Pipers are said to be the petrified musicians who played for them.

Cornish literature began in the Cornish language and includes passion plays, poems, essays, short stories, songs and spoken poetry. Many works were lost through language erosion and the dissolution of Glasney College and Crantock College. The language revival that began in the 20th century led to new Cornish literature, periodicals, children’s books, films and music.

## Language & religion

Cornish, also called *Kernewek* or *Kernowek*, is a Celtic language of the Brittonic subgroup and a Southwestern Brittonic language. It is closely related to Breton and Welsh and descends from Common Brittonic. The language declined after the introduction of the English Book of Common Prayer in 1549 and ceased to function as a community language by the end of the 18th century. A revival began in the early 20th century, particularly after Henry Jenner published *A Handbook in the Cornish Language* in 1904.

Cornish was recognised under the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages in 2002. UNESCO altered its classification in 2010, stating that the former designation “extinct” was no longer accurate. Cornish is taught in some schools, appears on street nameplates and is used in broadcasting, literature, ceremonies and public life.

Celtic Christianity was historically important, and Cornish saints are commemorated in legends, churches and place names. The Cornish have traditionally been non-conformists. Methodism became particularly popular among working-class people in the 18th century, and Methodist chapels served as social centres. The sources also describe renewed interest in Celtic Christianity and the Fellowship of St Piran.
