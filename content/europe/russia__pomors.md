## Overview

The Pomors (Russian: *помо́ры*, romanized: *pomory*) are an ethnographic group traditionally associated with Russian settlers, primarily from Veliky Novgorod, who live along the White Sea and Barents Sea coasts and in nearby regions. Their southern boundary is described as the watershed separating the White Sea basin from river basins draining southward. They primarily live in the coastal areas of Arkhangelsk Oblast and are typically considered a subgroup of the Russian ethnos. Their historical livelihoods included sea mammal hunting, fishing, trade, whaling, salt production, and, in tundra regions, reindeer herding. The historical term refers to northern coastal populations whose livelihoods depended on maritime activities, while modern Pomor self-identification remains a subject of scholarly debate.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name swastika motifs in connection with Mezen painting.

### Clothing & dress

They state that a *raspetukh* was a person of indeterminate sex who could be intersex or a biological male whose appearance, behavior, lifestyle, and occupations were closer to those associated with a woman. According to the source, such people did not have a certain gender, wandered around villages, and were also regarded as possessing magical knowledge and secret skills such as healing.

### Architecture

Pomor houses were traditionally very large and occupied by extended families. Each house had two stories, a gallery and a ramp, cellars, a bathhouse, a stable, granaries, and often a mill, all within the household complex. The doors were traditionally left unlocked, described as one of the oldest customs. Gardens could contain votive or funerary crosses measuring 2–3 meters in height. Until the Soviet Revolution, the dead were buried next to the home. The bathhouse also had religious significance as an archaic sanctuary-temple and stood on the periphery of the settlement, where it was associated with transformation, rebirth, life, and death.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**Mezen painting** is described as a highly symbolic style used for wood and household objects, originally developed at Palashchelye. Items including *tuesa* storage boxes, *polotuhi* flat boxes, and *koroba* baskets were made from birch bark. Pomor pottery was rooted in the village of Timoschel'e, while copper casting was based around Kimzha. Kholmogory bone carving was another important artistic tradition of Pomorye. The sources also mention votive and funerary crosses, mills, granaries, and household bathhouses as elements of the built environment.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They state that people may leave ribbons and coins at places of power as part of modern practices associated with traditional Pomor worldviews.

## Music & performance

They describe the *byliny* tradition as a form of long fairy tale or folk narrative, often connected with the sea, and state that Pomor spells were compared to mantras and were connected with traditional healing practices.

The poet-storyteller, or *starinshchik*, is presented as a keeper of “deified memory” who maintained social balance through mythopoetic expression. Such poets were described as transformed shamans and as interpreters and recreators of the meaning of life through words. They could be associated with world-building practices, including the ability to calm the sea on boats. The sources also state that the *starinshchik* possessed knowledge of ritual turns of speech and sacred formulas. No source used here documents particular instruments, melodies, dances accompanying music, or named musical occasions.

## Dance & theatre

The *byliny* are described as narrative works involving adventures, sea-related action, poor male protagonists, female protagonists, helpers, and treacherous enemies. Their functions included preserving folk history with mythic layers, transmitting values, warning against transgression, and connecting listeners to ancestral memory.

## Festivals & rituals

Pomor ritual life is closely connected in the sources with seasonal transition, the sea, death, rebirth, and cyclical time. The passage from winter to summer was associated with rebirth. A sacrificial vow to the “sea god” Nikola Morsky and a farewell to the sea, celebrated like a funeral, were described as important ritual elements. During the main holiday joining the Old and New Year, a demiurge defeats the bearer of chaos and death each year anew, expressing the concept of cyclical time and eternal return.

Modern Pomors may celebrate the Pomor New Year in September and the Roe Deer Festival. They may also participate in Orthodox pilgrimages or meditate at places of power, where ribbons and coins can be left. The sources describe these practices as part of a fluid and diverse religious life in which traditional holidays, Orthodox practices, local cults, and hierotopic practices can coexist without a clear boundary between sacred and profane.

Islands had a particular role in burial and memorial practices. They were viewed as spaces connecting living people with ancestors, and memorial rites conducted on islands were said to preserve ancestral memory and the sacred traditions of the ethnic group.

## Foodways

They state that Pomors historically traded corn and fish by sea with Northern Norway. Salt production was practiced in Pomorye, including at Sol'-Kamskay, Sol'-Vychegodskay, and Tot'ma. The sources also connect Pomor livelihoods with fishing, sea mammal hunting, whaling, and reindeer herding in tundra regions.

## Oral tradition & literature

Pomor fairy tales, or *byliny*, were strongly influenced by the culture of neighboring peoples. The sources describe popular narratives as long adventure tales whose action is often connected with the sea. A poor man is usually the principal character, while tales with female protagonists are also common. Female characters may share trials equally with men, become wonderful assistants, or appear as innocent victims of treacherous enemies.

The *byliny* preserved folk history through mythic layers, transmitted values, warned against transgression, and connected listeners to ancestral memory. These purposes were pursued through moral examples and the invocation of encounters with spirits. Pomor spells, compared in the source to mantras, were important in daily life and were linked to traditional healing. The *starinshchik* served as a poet-storyteller and keeper of sacred memory, using mythopoetic expression to interpret and recreate the meaning of life.

## Language & religion

The name Pomor derives from *pomorye*, the Russian word for coastland, from *po* (“by”) and *more* (“sea”), literally “by the sea.” The sources describe the historical term as having changed in meaning over time, from “a person who lived near the sea” in the 10th–12th centuries to a designation applied to populations living farther from the sea. The trade with Northern Norway produced a pidgin known as *Moja på tvoja*, or *Russenorsk*, developed from Russian and Norwegian and used from 1750 to 1920.

Pomors are mostly Orthodox Christians. Before the Revolution of 1917, many Pomors practiced Old Belief, and the Pomorian Church is described as still having around 400,000 members. Pomor Christianity traditionally coexisted with animism, expressed through sacral geography and a strong environmental ethic. The beluga whale was classified as holy, and this contributed to resistance to modern fishing techniques in the 20th century. The sources also describe shamanism, ritual practices, sacred winds, the bathhouse, islands, the sea, and seasonal rebirth within a syncretic worldview.
