## Overview

The Norse–Gaels were a people of mixed Gaelic and Norse ancestry and culture who emerged during the Viking Age, when Vikings settled in Ireland and Scotland, intermarried with Gaels, adopted Gaelic, and became Gaelicised. They dominated much of the Irish Sea and Scottish Sea regions from the 9th to 12th centuries. In Ireland, they established kingdoms and settlements in Dublin, Waterford, Wexford, Cork and Limerick, while the term *Ostmen* was used for Norse–Gaelic people living in Ireland. Many were merchants or followed fishing, craft-working and cattle raising. Their influence remained especially visible in the Isle of Man and the Outer Hebrides, where many place names are of Norse–Gaelic origin. They later assimilated into English settler communities or disappeared as a distinct group.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The Norse–Gaels founded kingdoms and fortified settlements, including settlements in Dublin, Waterford, Wexford, Cork and Limerick. In Dublin, the Ostmen lived outside the city walls on the north bank of the River Liffey in *Ostmentown*, a name that survives in corrupted form as Oxmantown.

## Music & performance

The Viking longship influenced the Gaelic *birlinn* and *longa fada*, vessels that were used extensively until the 17th century.

## Festivals & rituals

The Norse–Gaels originally included Norse settlers who intermarried with native Gaels, adopted Gaelic customs and, in many cases, left their original worship of Norse gods and converted to Christianity. The sources used mention Caill Tomair, a sacred grove near Dublin targeted by Brian Boru in the year 1000, and refer to Gaelic monks, known as *papar* or culdees, in Iceland before the Norse settlement.

## Foodways

They record that many Ostmen pursued fishing, craft-working and cattle raising.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources discuss Norse–Gaelic connections with Irish mythology. Heinrich Zimmer, writing in 1891, suggested that the Fianna Cycle came from the heritage of the Norse–Gaels and compared the name of the *fianna* with Old Norse *fiandr*, meaning “enemies.” He also compared Finn’s Thumb of Knowledge with the Norse tale *Fáfnismál*. Ranko Matasović derived *fíanna* from reconstructed Proto-Celtic *wēnā*, meaning “a troop,” while Kim McCone derived it from Proto-Celtic *wēnnā*, meaning “wild ones.”

## Language & religion

The Norse–Gaels used Gaelic languages and developed a range of names for themselves. The Old Irish term was *Gall-Goídil*, the Irish form was *Gall-Ghaeil*, the Scottish Gaelic form was *Gall-Gàidheal*, and the Manx form was *Goal-Gael*, meaning “foreigner-Gaels.” They also called themselves *Ostmen* or *Austmen*, meaning “East-men,” while they called Gaels *Vestmenn*, meaning “West-men.” Other terms included Norse-Irish, Hiberno-Norse, Hiberno-Scandinavian, Norse-Scots and Scoto-Norse. Their religious history included the abandonment of original Norse worship by many settlers and conversion to Christianity, contributing to Gaelicisation.
