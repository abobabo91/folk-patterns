## Overview

The Kursenieki are a nearly extinct Baltic ethnic group associated with the Curonian Spit, a thin strip of land between southwestern Lithuania and the Kaliningrad exclave of Russia. Some autochthonous inhabitants of Šventoji in Lithuania also call themselves *kuršiai*. The group historically lived along the Curonian Spit and, at different periods, in areas of East Prussia, including the Vistulan Spit and Sambia. At the end of the 19th century, their total number was around 4,000 persons; only 219 lived along the Curonian Spit in 1955, most of them in Latvia. The Kursenieki spoke a southwestern dialect of Latvian, known within the group as the “Curonian language,” and were predominantly fishermen. Their importance for folk-culture study lies in the survival of a distinct coastal identity, language, fishing-based social life, and cultural practices shaped by Latvian, German, Lithuanian, Old Prussian, and other regional influences.

They state only that Curonian culture, religion, and architecture were similar to those found in Germany and Sweden.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They identify the Kursenieki as primarily fishermen.

## Music & performance

They state that the Curonian language was used within the family and while fishing, German was used in everyday communication, and German and Lithuanian were used in church services. The sources also mention the 2002 documentary film *Tarp aštuonių vėjų* (“Amidst Eight Winds”), by Arvydas Barysas, which concerns the endangered Kursenieki language and introduces the Curonian Spit. The film discusses three brothers, Sakutis, plural *Sakučiai*, who had moved from the Spit to Sweden about 60 years earlier but still spoke “kuršiškai.” In 2005, the film won the Best Overall Production Award at the second European Heritage Film Festival in Toblach, Italy, under the patronage of Europa Nostra.

## Dance & theatre

The documentary *Tarp aštuonių vėjų* is the only named audiovisual work in the sources, and it is presented as documentation of an endangered language rather than as a Kursenieki theatrical tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that the Kursenieki were predominantly Lutheran, like most former inhabitants of East Prussia, while some ancient pagan customs were preserved. The language of church services was German and Lithuanian.

## Foodways

The sources describe the Kursenieki as primarily fishermen. One named beverage associated with them is *Curonian coffee* (*Kurenkaffee*), a drink made from vodka flavoured with coffee, honey, and other ingredients. It was popular throughout East Prussia.

## Oral tradition & literature

They identify several works concerned with the group’s language and heritage. Paul Kwauka was the first scholar said to have taken an interest in Kursenieki culture and language; his book *Kurisches Wörterbuch* is described as a highly valuable source of information. Richard Pietsch, one of the last remaining Kursenieki, continued the work of describing their heritage. Sample texts in the Kursenieki language appear in Adalbert Bezzenberger’s 1888 monograph *Über die Sprache der preussischen Letten* and Juris Plāķis’s 1927 monograph *Kursenieku valoda*. The sources also mention the 2002 documentary *Tarp aštuonių vėjų*, which presents the endangered language and the Curonian Spit.

## Language & religion

The Kursenieki language is a dialect of Latvian and is also called the Curonian language of the Curonian isthmus. The Kursenieki themselves called their language “Curonian language,” *kursisk valoud*; the language is also given as *kursineeki wahloda*, Latvian *kursenieku valoda*, German *Nehrungskurisch*, and Lithuanian *kuršininkų kalba*. It is distinct from Old Curonian, the language of the Curonian people. The language was influenced successively by Old Prussian, Low German, High German, and Samogitian. A vocabulary published in 1927 recorded 60% of Curonian words as the same as Latvian, 26% as loanwords from German, and 13% as borrowed from Lithuanian dialects. The language was mutually intelligible with Latvian, especially its southwestern dialects. Before World War II it functioned as a sociolect of Curonian fishermen, while German was used in other everyday settings. The Soviet annexation of East Prussia led to its near extinction, and several remaining native speakers lived in Germany after being expelled from East Prussia following World War II. The Kursenieki were predominantly Lutheran, although some ancient pagan customs were preserved.
