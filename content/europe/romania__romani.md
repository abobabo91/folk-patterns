## Overview

The Romani are an Indo-Aryan ethnic group whose ancestors originated in northwestern regions of the Indian subcontinent, particularly present-day Punjab, Rajasthan and Sindh. Their westward migration occurred in waves, and they arrived in Europe through the Balkans during the Byzantine period, with Romani populations later spreading throughout Europe and the Americas. Europe had an estimated 10 million Romani in 2019, including approximately 6 million in the European Union. Romania has a sizable Romani minority, and Roma there include occupationally defined subgroups such as *Lăutari*, *Kalderash*, *Ursari*, *Argintari* and *Aurari*. Romani is an Indo-Aryan macrolanguage influenced by Persian, Armenian, Greek, Turkish and South Slavic languages. Romani culture is expressed through family institutions, oral law, music, dance, cuisine and regional customs.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name *Roma chakra* as an element of the Romani flag.

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that women in Brazil may present the “Romaní dance” while wearing traditional attire. The sources also state that clothes worn by Romani women and men, and clothes covering the upper and lower body, are laundered and dried separately in some communities. In certain communities, the clothing of elderly people is also washed and dried separately as a sign of respect and to prevent possible defilement. No specific everyday or ceremonial garment names are supplied.

### Architecture

They describe a tent in connection with bathing practices: a spacious tub is placed on the ground within the tent, and family members cleanse themselves in an age-based order. The sources also state that Romani people traditionally maintained a nomadic lifestyle in some communities, although many are now settled.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Occupational names identify several Romani metalworking traditions. *Argintari* are “silversmiths,” *Aurari* are “goldsmiths,” *Kalderash* are associated with making buckets, kettles, tinsmithing and tinkering, and *Ungaritza* are blacksmiths and bladesmiths. *Zlătari* are goldsmiths working with the extraction and processing of gold. The sources also mention *Churari*, sieve-makers, and *Lingurari*, spoon makers. No specific object forms, ceramic traditions or decorative techniques are documented.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention jewelry as a gift brought by godparents during baptism among Christian Romani people. The sources also state that godparents may give clothes, jewelry and money to the child, and that godparenthood brings respect within the community.

## Music & performance

Music is a major documented field of Romani cultural expression. Romani music incorporates Indian, Greek, Persian, Armenian, Turkish, Balkan, German and French influences acquired through migration and contact. Lyrics may use one or more Romani dialects, and dance frequently accompanies performance. Romani musical traditions have influenced flamenco, manouche jazz, Russian “romances,” Balkan music, Hungarian czardas and various fusions with jazz, hip-hop, Western art music and European folk genres.

The *Lăutari* are traditional Romani musicians in Romania who perform at weddings, funerals and other events. Their music draws on Romanian, Turkish, Slavic and Romani traditions. The sources identify Taraful Haiducilor as an internationally prominent contemporary performer in the *lăutari* tradition. In Hungary, the Rajkó Orchestra and Folk Ensemble has preserved Romani music, dance and costume culture since 1952. Romani musicians in Turkey are associated with urban music, *fasıl*, the dance music *oyun havası*, and belly-dance motifs. Instruments named in this context include the clarinet, violin, *kanun* and *darbuka*. In France, Django Reinhardt is identified as a Romani jazz guitarist and composer.

Romani folk music is mainly vocal, combining slow plaintive songs and fast melodies. Fast melodies may use tongue-clacking, hand-clapping, mouth-basses, clicking wooden spoons and dancing. The sources describe three vocal or instrumental parts, syncopation, contrasting phrase entrances, frequent minor-key harmony and emphasis on natural vocal abilities.

## Dance & theatre

The sources identify several Romani dance traditions. A snake dance in the eastern Balkans is described as having origins in the Indian subcontinent. Belly dance is performed by Romani people in Turkey, and Romani influence on Turkish *fasıl* includes Ottoman *rakkas* and belly-dancing motifs. Flamenco music and dance came from the Roma in Spain, and the Calé Romani people contributed significantly to the Andalusian musical tradition known as flamenco.

In Brazil, the “Romaní dance” is presented by women parading in traditional attire. The sources describe a related “carnival wedding,” in which a boy is disguised as a bride. In Russia, the Romen Theatre was established in Moscow in 1931 as a public Romani theatre incorporating Romani music and dance into theatrical performances.

## Festivals & rituals

International Romani Day is celebrated in Europe on 8 April, especially in Bulgaria, Hungary, Romania and other parts of Central and Eastern Europe. World Day of Romani Language is celebrated every 5 November. In London, Gypsy, Roma & Traveller History Month is celebrated each June. World Roma Festival is celebrated in Prague. Among Turkish Roma, Ederlezi and Kakava are spring holidays.

Romani Christians celebrate Christmas on 25 December and Easter in either April or May. Adults may fast during these holidays and eat special foods. Romani people in Serbia celebrate Bibi and Bibijako Djive. The sources also describe the pilgrimage for Saint Sarah at Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer. The day honouring Sarah is 24 May, when her statue is carried down to the sea to re-enact her arrival in France.

Marriage is described as an important family institution. Arranged marriage is traditionally desirable, and the groom’s parents customarily pay the bride’s family. Parents help identify a partner, and potential couples are expected to be supervised by an adult. Weddings, engagement parties, baptism ceremonies, circumcision ceremonies, schools, churches and mosques are described as places where prospective partners may be found. The sources also document godparenthood, known as *kirvipen* or *kirvimos*, as a form of kinship among Christian Romani people.

Childbirth is considered “impure” in the practices described, and must occur outside the dwelling place; the mother is considered impure for 40 days.

## Foodways

Romani cuisine varies according to the countries where Romani people have lived and is influenced by Balkan and Turkish cuisine. Food is often designed to be portable, reflecting a nomadic lifestyle. Stews are common, and potatoes, peppers, cabbage and rice are frequent building blocks. Meals may be cooked outdoors in cauldrons over a wooden flame. Bread traditionally forms part of every meal.

Named dishes include *sarma*, *salmaia* or *sodmay*, cabbage stuffed with meat and rice; *pufe*, fried flour; *xaritsa*, fried cornbread; *bogacha*, baked bread; and *xaimoko*, a rabbit-meat meal. *Fusui eski zumi* is a butter-bean soup often made with ham, while *pertia* is made with jellied pig’s feet and pig’s ears. A Romani version of chicken paprikash is called *puyo*. At feasts, the sources list *sarmi*, meats, sauces, celery sticks, salads, *pirogo*, *saviako* and stews.

Cornmeal is a staple for the Kalderash. *Ankrusté* is an unleavened bread made from cornmeal mush and flavoured with cumin and coriander. *Bodag* is a Romani flatbread in Hungary, also called *vakáro*, *vakarcs* or Gypsy bread in some places. *Xevica* or *mamaliga* is porridge made from boiled cornmeal. *Joe Grey* is a British Romani stew usually made with rabbit meat, although eels may also be used.

Garlic, lemon, tomato, peppers and fermented foods such as sauerkraut, pickles and sour cream may be considered auspicious or lucky, *baxtalo*. Foods traditionally considered *marime*, or ritually unclean, include horse, cat and dog meat. Frog and snake meat are considered unlucky by Christian Roma, peacock meat is forbidden, and a menstruating Romani woman cannot cook or serve food to men. Coffee and tea are widely consumed; *kafa* and *chao* may be served with sugar, milk or fruit.

## Oral tradition & literature

Romani folklore, *paramichia*, encompasses folktales, myths, oral traditions and legends. A popular legend among Vlach Roma concerns the hero Mundro Salamon, known among other Roma subgroups as Wise Solomon or O Godjiaver Yanko. Other documented folktales include “Bald Pate,” “The Captive’s Tale and Circumcision,” “The Creation of the Violin,” “Fedor and the Fairy,” “The Foam Maiden,” “The King of England and his Three Sons,” “The Red King and the Witch” and “The Yellow Dragon.”

The sources describe beliefs in the *mulo* or *mullo*, meaning “one who is dead,” the Romani version of the vampire. Roma from Slavic countries are said to believe in werewolves. Romani *chovihanis* may use herbs and amulets for protection, and garlic is identified as a popular protective herb.

The Romani Code, or *Romano Zakono*, is not written and is preserved through oral tradition. The *kris* is a traditional institution for upholding and enforcing it. Its central concepts include *baxt*, meaning honour, and *ladž*, meaning shame.

## Language & religion

Romani is an Indo-Aryan macrolanguage spoken through a number of dialects, including Balkan Romani, Vlax Romani and Sinte Romani. It has considerable influence from Persian, Armenian and Byzantine Greek, while Balkan Romani has significant Turkish influence and other varieties show South Slavic influence. Romani is spoken almost exclusively in Europe and is recognized as a minority language in many countries. It is an official language in the Republic of Kosovo regionally and in the Šuto Orizari Municipality within Skopje, North Macedonia. The language was historically unwritten, and most current literature uses a Latin-based orthography.

Most Romani people are Christian or Muslim. Orthodox Christian Romani in the Balkans were known as Dasikane during Ottoman times, while Muslim or Turkified Romani were known as Xoraxane. Muslim Romani communities in the Balkans include communities in Bulgaria, Romania, Greece, Albania, North Macedonia, Serbia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Montenegro, Croatia and Slovenia. Some Romani people practice Shaktism, and the ancestors of the Romani possibly followed Hinduism in South Asia. Named spiritual concepts include *Kuntari*, *Kintala*, *Dji* and *Marime*. Saints and spiritual figures named in the sources include Saint Sarah, Kali Sara and Blessed Ceferino Giménez Malla.
