## Overview

The Portuguese are a Romance ethnic group and nation indigenous to Portugal, occupying the western side of the Iberian Peninsula in south-west Europe. Portuguese origins include Indo-European, Celtic, Roman, Germanic, Moorish, Jewish and other historical influences. The Portuguese language is a Western Romance language of the Indo-European family, written in the Latin script. Approximately 9.15 million Portuguese-born people live in Portugal, within a total population of 10.467 million. Portuguese culture developed through interactions among prehistoric, Roman, Germanic, Viking, Sephardic Jewish and Moorish populations. Regional culture includes traditions associated with the Minho, Douro, Trás-os-Montes, Alentejo, Algarve, Madeira and the Azores. Portuguese folk culture is expressed through regional dance, fado, foodways, architecture, literature and music.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name flowers, animals and cherubs in the decoration of the Foral of Évora, but do not present them as a textile motif vocabulary.

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that dance apparel is highly varied, ranging from work clothes to Sunday’s best, with distinctions between rich and poor.

### Architecture

Portuguese architecture includes medieval castles, villas and forts associated with Celtic and Roman occupation, ancient church buildings, monasteries, palaces and urban cultural buildings. Two typical elements are *azulejo*, painted tile, and Portuguese pavement. Late-Gothic Manueline architecture is associated with intricate designs attributed to the Age of Discoveries. Johannine baroque developed during King John V’s long reign and included gilded altars, blue-and-white tiles, decorated churches, halls, staircases and gardens.

Named buildings include the Royal Building of Mafra, the Clérigos Church, the Biblioteca Joanina, the Sanctuary of Bom Jesus do Monte in Braga, the Shrine of Our Lady of Remedies in Lamego and the Mateus Palace in Vila Real. Portuguese colonial architecture is also identified in Brazil, Uruguay, Angola, Cabo Verde, São Tomé and Príncipe, Benin, Ghana, Morocco, Guinea Bissau, Zimbabwe, Mozambique, China, India, Indonesia, Malaysia and Timor Leste.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources describe *azulejos* as painted tiles used in architectural and decorative settings, including churches, halls, staircases and gardens. Decorative mural painting and the *brutesco* style were also applied to stucco, carved woodwork and architectural decoration. Portuguese art includes gilded wooden sculpture and altarpieces, particularly during the Baroque period.

## Music & performance

Portuguese music includes classical, traditional or folk, popular, rock, hip hop, jazz, reggae, ska, electronic music, heavy metal and other genres. Folk music and dancing, together with fado, are described as fundamental forms of musical expression.

*Fado* is a musical genre associated with Lisbon and Coimbra. It is characterized by mournful tunes and lyrics concerning the sea, poverty, destiny, love, sadness, longing, sailors and fishermen. Fado performances traditionally included a singer, a Portuguese guitar player and a classical guitar player, although later settings range from a singer and string quartet to a full orchestra. The Lisbon style is the most popular, while Coimbra’s style is described as more refined. According to the sources, audiences in Lisbon clap their hands to applaud fado, while audiences in Coimbra cough as if clearing their throats.

The sources describe fado as having arisen in Lisbon among the urban poor and associate it with a wire-strung acoustic guitar or the Portuguese Guitar. Amália Rodrigues began her career in 1939 and contributed to fado’s international popularity. Contemporary performers named in the sources include Carminho, Mariza, Mísia, Camané, Maria Ana Bobone, Cuca Roseta, Cristina Branco, Ana Moura and Katia Guerreiro.

Regional folk music includes the *danças do vira* of Minho, *Pauliteiros de Miranda*, *Corridinho do Algarve* and *Bailinho* of Madeira. Instruments named in the sources include guitar, mandolin, bagpipes, accordion, violin, drums, Portuguese guitar, *Machete*, *rajão*, *Brinquinho* and *Cavaquinho*. Trás-os-Montes traditions include the *gaita-de-fole transmontana*, a cappella vocals and a musical scale with equal semitones. The sources also identify music in Mirandese and performances by Galamdum Galundaina.

## Dance & theatre

Portuguese folk dances include circle dance, *Vira* of the Minho region, Two-Steps Waltz, *Fandango* of the Ribatejo region, *Schottische* or *Chotiça*, *Corridinho* of the Algarve and Estremadura, *Bailarico*, *Vareirinha*, *Malhão*, *Vareira*, *Maneio*, *Vira de Cruz*, *Vira Solto*, *Vira de Macieira*, *Sapatinho*, *Tau-Tau*, *Ciranda*, *Zé que Fumas*, *Regadinho*, *O Pedreiro* and *Ó Ti Taritatu*. Variations called the *Chamarita* are associated with the Azores.

In Madeira, the *Bailinho da Madeira* is a traditional folklore dance accompanied by local instruments including *Machete*, *rajão*, *Brinquinho* and *Cavaquinho*. The *Pauliteiros* folk dance is popular in Trás-os-Montes, particularly around Miranda de I Douro. Local folklore groups perform traditional dance and song in villages and smaller towns, while local festivities provide seasonal contexts for performance.

Portuguese theatre is represented in the sources through Gil Vicente, described as the father of Portuguese theatre, whose works criticized and satirized society.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe local festivities as particularly popular during the summer season, in villages and cities, alongside beach holidays from July to September.

Music-related cultural traditions include regional folklore in the Azores, Madeira, Minho, Alentejo, Algarve and Trás-os-Montes. The sources state that Cante Alentejano is associated with the Alentejo region. They also identify fado as a major cultural form and describe performances in Lisbon and Coimbra as having distinct audience practices.

Historical religious and state practices mentioned in the sources include the Reconquista, the Portuguese Inquisition, forced conversions, persecution of religious and ethnic minorities, and the creation of a Jewish diaspora. These accounts concern historical religious and political developments rather than a complete description of present-day ritual life.

## Foodways

Portuguese cuisine consists of cooking traditions and practices in Portugal and includes meat, seafood, fresh fish, dried and salted cod, vegetables, legumes, bread, rice, potatoes and desserts. Each region has traditional dishes. Olive oil is used for cooking and flavouring, while garlic, onions, bay leaf, parsley, oregano, thyme, mint, marjoram, rosemary and coriander are common ingredients.

A central food is *bacalhau*, dried and salted cod. The sources state that there are more than 365 ways to cook cod and that it is generally soaked in water or sometimes milk before cooking. Fish may be grilled, boiled, fried, stewed, roasted or steamed. *Caldeirada* is a stew of fish, shellfish and vegetables. Other seafood includes sardines, sea bass, swordfish, mackerel, octopus, squid, crabs, shrimp, prawns, lobster, clams, mussels, oysters and scallops.

Named meat dishes include *cozido à portuguesa*, a stew that may contain beef, pork, charcutaria, potatoes, carrots, turnips, cabbage and rice; *Tripas à moda do Porto*; *dobrada*; *Leitão à Bairrada*; *chanfana*; *carne de porco à alentejana*; *alcatra*; *bife*; *bife com ovo a cavalo*; *bife à casa*; *iscas*; *pregos*; *bifanas* and *espetada*.

Portuguese sausages and preserved meats include *alheira*, *chouriço*, *farinheira*, *presunto*, *morcela*, *linguiça*, *paio*, *salpicão* and others. Common soups include *caldo verde*, made from potato, onion, garlic, collard greens and sometimes *chouriço*, and *açorda*, a bread soup. Breakfast may include fresh bread, butter, ham, cheese, jam, coffee, milk, tea, hot chocolate and *pastel de nata*. Typical alcoholic drinks include Port wine, Madeira wine, *Vinho Verde*, *Licor Beirão* and *Ginjinha*.

## Oral tradition & literature

Portuguese literature developed from the 12th century through lyrical works associated with Galician-Portuguese and oral traditions. The sources name *Cantigas d’amor e amigo* and *Cantigas de escárnio e maldizer*, performed by troubadours and joglars. The *Cancioneiro da Ajuda* is a late-13th-century collection of poems in Galician-Portuguese with miniatures of musicians.

The *Lusiads* or *Os Lusíadas*, by Luís de Camões, is described as a national epic book about the history of Portugal incorporating elements of Ancient Greek mythology. Portuguese literary history also includes chronicles associated with Fernão Lopes and theatre by Gil Vicente. Later authors named in the sources include Almeida Garrett, Camilo Castelo Branco, Eça de Queiroz, Fernando Pessoa, José Régio, Miguel Torga and José Saramago. They state that Portuguese folk music expresses the beliefs of a community and tells its history to later generations.

## Language & religion

Portuguese is a Western Romance language of the Indo-European family, written in the Latin script. It developed from dialects of Vulgar Latin in the medieval Kingdom of Galicia and the County of Portugal. The language has European, Brazilian, African and Asian varieties, with European Portuguese and Brazilian Portuguese identified as two primary standardized varieties. Portuguese is the official language of Portugal, Brazil, Angola, Mozambique, Cape Verde, Guinea-Bissau, São Tomé and Príncipe and Timor-Leste, and is co-official in Macau and Equatorial Guinea.

Native or autochthonous languages in Portugal include *Caló*, associated with the Portuguese-Romani community; *Mirandês*, officially recognized and protected in areas of Miranda do Douro, Vimioso and Mogadouro; *Barranquenhu*; *Minderico*; and Portuguese Sign Language. The sources describe historical Roman Catholic, Jewish, Muslim and Moorish populations, as well as persecution and forced conversion during the Inquisition.
