## Overview

Krymchaks are Jewish ethno-religious communities of Crimea descended from Turkic-speaking adherents of Rabbinic Judaism. They historically lived near the Crimean Karaites, who follow Karaite Judaism. Their language is the Krymchak language, a Jewish ethnolect of Crimean Tatar, which belongs to the Kypchak branch of the Turkic languages. Before the Russian Revolution in 1917, Krymchaks generally used their ethnolect and Hebrew in religious life and written communication. By 2000, about 600 Krymchaks lived in the former Soviet Union, while approximately 600–700 people maintaining their Crimean identity lived in Israel; others lived in the United States and Canada. Their folk-cultural significance lies in the combination of Turkic language and customs with Rabbinic Judaism, distinctive prayer traditions, and a history of interaction with Karaites, Crimean Tatars, and other Jewish communities.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state only that Krymchak dress and customs resembled those of nearby Karaites and Crimean Tatars. The Crimean Tatars referred to Krymchaks as *zuluflı çufutlar*, meaning “Jews with pe'ot,” distinguishing them from Karaites, whom they called *zulufsız çufutlar*, or “Jews without pe'ot.”

### Architecture

They mention that synagogues and yeshivas were closed by government decree under Joseph Stalin, but give no architectural description of these buildings.

## Festivals & rituals

Religious information is limited to the statement that Krymchaks practice Orthodox or Talmudic Judaism and historically used Hebrew in religious life. Under the Crimean Khanate, Jews lived in separate quarters, paid the dhimmi-tax, and possessed limited judicial autonomy under the Ottoman millet system.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that the available summary of folklore materials is incomplete. The sources do identify several written works associated with Krymchak religious and intellectual history. In the 12th century, Rabbi Yehuda haLevi wrote the *Kuzari*, a philosophical work presenting a discussion between a learned Jew and the Khazar king. In the 18th century, David Ben Eliezer Lehno wrote the introduction to the “Kaffa” rite prayer book and *Mishkan David* (“Abode of David”), a work devoted to Hebrew grammar. He also wrote *Devar sefataim* (“Utterance the mouth”), a Hebrew historical chronicle concerning the history of the Crimean Khanate. The sources state that Krymchaks are making efforts to revive their language.

## Language & religion

The Krymchak language is a modified form of Crimean Tatar and a Jewish ethnolect of the Kypchak Turkic language. It contains numerous Hebrew and Aramaic loanwords. It was traditionally written in Hebrew characters and is now written in Cyrillic script. Before the Russian Revolution in 1917, Krymchaks were at least bilingual, using the Krymchak ethnolect and Hebrew for religious life and written communication. They maintained their Turkic patois until World War II, after which they began to lose their linguistic identity. Krymchaks practice Orthodox or Talmudic Judaism. Their distinctive prayer book, *Nusah Kaffa*, emerged during the 16th century; *Kaffa* was a former name of the Crimean city of Feodosia. In 1515, Sephardi, Ashkenazi, and Romaniote traditions were united in a distinctive Krymchak prayer book representing the Romaniote rite.
