## Overview

Bunjevci are a South Slavic ethnocultural group living mainly in Bačka of northern Serbia, as well as in Bács-Kiskun County of southern Hungary, Croatia, and Bosnia-Herzegovina. Their recorded origin is Western Herzegovina, from where migrations led to Dalmatia and later to Lika, the Croatian Littoral, and Bačka. In Serbia, they are represented both by the Bunjevac National Council and by the Croat National Council, reflecting an unresolved dispute over whether they constitute a distinct ethnic group or a Croatian sub-ethnic group. They are mainly Catholic and most speakers use the neo-Shtokavian Younger Ikavian dialect of the Serbo-Croatian pluricentric language.

## Music & performance

They do record a language dispute concerning the status of Bunjevac speech and the official use in schools of the Štokavian dialect with Ikavian pronunciation, described as “bunjevac speech with elements of national culture” (*Bunjevački govor s elementima nacionalne kulture*). The sources also state that most Bunjevci speak the neo-Shtokavian Younger Ikavian dialect of the Serbo-Croatian pluricentric language, with certain archaic characteristics. No musical repertoire, vocal style, instrumental practice, wedding music, funeral music, or professional performance tradition is described.

## Festivals & rituals

They do record several commemorative and political occasions connected with Bunjevac history. The Catholic Church in Subotica celebrates 1686 as the anniversary of the Bunjevac migration, because the largest single migration to Bačka was said to have taken place then. In October 1918, part of the Bunjevci held a national convention in Subotica and decided to separate Banat, Bačka, and Baranja from the Kingdom of Hungary and join the Kingdom of Serbia. This decision was confirmed at the Great National Assembly of Serbs, Bunjevci, and other Slavs in Novi Sad in November 1918. These are historical commemorations and political assemblies rather than documented ritual traditions.

## Oral tradition & literature

They mention folk oral tradition in connection with explanations of the ethnonym. In Littoral and mostly Podunavlje branches, oral tradition preserved the theory that the name Bunjevci derives from the river Buna in central Herzegovina, although linguists and historians generally dismiss that derivation. A related historical account states that Alberto Fortis recorded people on the Velebit as calling themselves Bunjevci because they came from the area of Buna in Bosnia and Herzegovina.

## Language & religion

Bunjevci are mainly Catholic, and the sources describe their historical identity as connected with social, historical, and confessional divisions during the Ottoman period. The majority speaks the neo-Shtokavian Younger Ikavian dialect of the Serbo-Croatian pluricentric language, which has certain archaic characteristics. The status of Bunjevac speech remains politically disputed, particularly in Serbia and Croatia. In 2005, the Government of Vojvodina allowed the official use in schools of the Štokavian dialect with Ikavian pronunciation, under the designation “bunjevac speech with elements of national culture” (*Bunjevački govor s elementima nacionalne kulture*). The first school year used Cyrillic script and the following school years used Latin script. Local schools in the former Yugoslavia used the Serbian version of Serbo-Croatian in Latin script and, during the 1990s, also in Cyrillic script. Bunjevci have variously been classified as Croats, a separate ethnic group, or a former subcategory of Croatian ethnicity. In Serbia, they were officially recognized as a separate ethnicity in 1991 and as a recognized ethnic minority in 1996.
