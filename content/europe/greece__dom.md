## Overview

The Dom, also called Domi, Dūmī, Dūmrī, Ḍom, or Doms, are descendants of the Dom caste, with origins in the Indian subcontinent. Ancient migrations dispersed Dom communities across the Middle East and North Africa, the Eastern Anatolia region, Syria, Iraq, and Azerbaijan. The sources also mention Muslim Roma communities in Komotini (Gümülcine) and the Serres region in Greece, whose traditions describe origins in Ottoman Egypt. The Domari language is an endangered Indo-Aryan language, and the Dom do not identify themselves as Romanies. Their cultural expression includes music, poetry, dance, oral history, cuisine, and historically itinerant occupations such as blacksmithing, horse trading, animal healing, music, and dance.

## Material culture

### Architecture

They state that the Dom historically lived a nomadic life but that the community has adopted a sedentary way of life for more than a century. The sources also describe a small Dom community in the Bab Huta neighborhood of the Old City of Jerusalem, without providing architectural details.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do identify blacksmithing as a historical Dom occupation and describe blacksmiths among the informal workers of Syria before the war.

## Music & performance

Music is an important part of Dom cultural expression. The Dom have an oral tradition through which they express culture and history in music, poetry, and dance. Earlier generations engaged in music as a nomadic profession, and some Domari subgroups were associated with the Ghawazi, who were known for dancing and music as a business. The sources also state that, in Syria before the war, many Dom worked as musicians alongside seasonal laborers, day laborers, scrap dealers, and blacksmiths.

A travel book written by the Ottoman Turkish traveller Evliya Çelebi in 1668 is cited in the source as describing beliefs among the Romani from Komotini (Gümülcine) about ancestral origins in Ottoman Egypt. This concerns Romani communities and does not provide a named Dom musical repertoire.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is identified as one of the principal forms through which the Dom express culture and history. The Ghawazi, among the Domari subgroups mentioned in the source, were known for dancing and music as a business. Earlier Dom generations also engaged in dance as a nomadic profession.

## Festivals & rituals

They do state that Muslim Roma in Baranya and Pécs converted to Christianity and chose the Roman Catholic faith during the years 1686–1713 after the Siege of Pécs (1686), but this account concerns Muslim Roma communities rather than a documented Dom ritual system.

The sources also mention that the sedentary Romani groups from the Serres region in Greece believed their ancestors had been taken from Ottoman Egypt by the Turks after 1517 to Rumelia to work on tobacco plantations. No Dom festival or ritual associated with Greece is described.

## Foodways

Domari cuisine includes Kufta bi Tahineh, described as meatballs in tahini sauce; musakhan, consisting of chicken with caramelized onions and almonds served on flatbread; and danan al aut, described as delicate dumplings. The sources also name stuffed vegetables such as Mahshi Beitinjan and Kusa, dishes influenced by local Arab and Palestinian cuisine.

Domari cooking commonly uses seasonal vegetables, rice, meat, olive oil, yogurt, lemon juice, curry leaves, and dried mint. The sources describe spices as originating in the various regions where nomadic Domari Gypsies lived and as having been handed down through generations. Food is associated with hospitality and sharing meals within the community. The Domari Society, a non-profit organization based in Jerusalem, supports the culinary tradition by offering traditional Domari meals prepared by women from the community.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Dom have an oral tradition and express culture and history through music, poetry, and dance.

The source discusses the travel book *Seyahatnâme*, written by Evliya Çelebi in 1668, because it records beliefs among the Romani from Komotini (Gümülcine) concerning their ancestors. This is documentary evidence about a Romani tradition and is not presented as Dom oral literature.

## Language & religion

Domari is the traditional language of the Dom. It is described as an endangered Indo-Aryan language and as showing Turkic, Kurdish, and Arabic influence. Domari in the Middle East is known as Nawari. The sources state that Domari has a separate origin in India from Romani and that the Dom and Romani peoples departed from the Indian subcontinent at different times and by different routes.

The sources describe many Dom as Muslims, including Dom communities in Palestinian society, while also noting that Muslim Roma in Baranya and Pécs converted to Christianity and chose the Roman Catholic faith during the years 1686–1713.

A separate Wikipedia article titled “Dom language” describes Dom as a Trans–New Guinea language of the Eastern Group of the Chimbu family in Papua New Guinea. It concerns another Dom population and does not describe Domari or the Dom of the Middle East.
