## Overview

The Loma, also called Loghoma, Looma, Lorma or Toma, are a West African ethnic group living primarily in mountainous, sparsely populated regions near the border between Guinea and Liberia. Their population in the two countries was estimated at 330,000 in 2010. They speak Loma, a Southwestern Mande language belonging to the Niger-Congo family, and are closely related to the Mende. Loma cultural life documented in the sources includes shifting rice cultivation, patrilineal and exogamous social organization, traditional religion, Poro secret rites of passage, large wooden masks, and a writing script developed in the 1930s.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used do not document Loma textile or pattern traditions.

**Mask carving** — Loma masks are large wooden forms combining animal and human motifs. The largest are about six feet high, with feather decorations, and are associated with forest spirits and Poro secret rites of passage.

**Blackened wooden masks** — Museum catalogue records describe wooden masks from northern Liberia that were blackened. The records state that women of the Lorma group used them and that they belonged to the Sandisällskapet.

**Wooden mask with leather beard** — A museum record describes a wooden mask from Guinea associated with Toma, or Loma, with a beard made of skin.

**Motif vocabulary.** Animal and human motifs, feather decorations, feather finial, looking-glass eyes, amulets, horns, central spike, claws, black fur, brown mud, twigs, bicycle chain.

### Clothing & dress

The sources used do not document everyday or ceremonial Loma clothing in sufficient detail. One museum record describes a composite hat consisting of a woven textile cap with cowrie shells around the rim, plaster, a pair of horns, numerous tufts of black fur, and lumps of medicine. The same record identifies it as a hat or head-dress, but the sources do not provide a vernacular name, gender attribution, or context of use.

### Architecture

The sources used do not cover Loma architecture, house forms, roofing, settlement design, or named building types.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum records document several wooden and mixed-material objects. One object is an implement or weapon with a wooden handle wrapped in skin and fitted with two iron hooks. A pall from Lofa County, Liberia, is made of wood and has a seat with carved decoration. Other catalogue records describe wooden figures used as religious or ritual equipment. One male figure is thickly encrusted with blood, and another is recorded as its pair.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used do not document Loma jewelry, tattoos, henna, hair practices, or named forms of body adornment. Museum records do mention cowrie shells, amulets, medicine, animal skin, claws, and black fur as components of particular hats and masks.

## Music & performance

The Loma language article records hymns composed in Loma by Billema Kwillia in the 1960s. The missionary Margaret D. Miller recorded them, and the Lutheran Church later adopted them. They first appeared in print in Loma in 1970. The most widely used hymn is “A va de laa”; it was translated into singable English in 2004 and also translated into German. The sources do not name Loma musical instruments, secular song genres, or performance settings beyond this hymn tradition and its connection with the Lutheran Church.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used do not document named Loma dances, dramatic traditions, or theatrical genres. They do document large wooden masks associated with Poro secret rites of passage and museum masks attributed to women of the Lorma group belonging to the Sandisällskapet.

## Festivals & rituals

The Loma have retained their traditional religion and resisted Islamic incursion. The religious conflict with Mandinka people was called a historic “rolling war.” Large wooden masks formed part of Poro secret rites of passage; the largest masks were believed by Loma to have forest spirits. Museum catalogue records also identify male wooden figures as religious or ritual equipment and describe a composite hat with medicine attached to it. The sources used do not provide an annual festival calendar or detailed accounts of birth, marriage, funeral, or other life-cycle ceremonies.

## Foodways

The Loma farm rice in shifting farms. The sources used do not name Loma dishes, cooking methods, beverages, ceremonial foods, or dietary rules.

## Language & religion

Loma is a Mande language spoken by the Loma people of Liberia and Guinea. It belongs to the Southwestern branch of the Mande languages and, more broadly, to the Niger-Congo family. Dialects of Loma proper in Liberia are Gizima, Wubomei, Ziema, Bunde, and Buluyiema. In Guinea, the dialects distinguished as Toma are an official regional language. Loma uses a Latin-based alphabet written from left to right. A syllabary saw limited use in the 1930s and 1940s before falling into disuse. The Loma call their language Löömàgòòi, or Löghömàgòòi in Guinea, and refer to themselves as Löömàgìtì or Löghömagiti. Led by Wido Zobo and assisted by the weaver Moriba, they developed a script containing at least 185 characters. The Loma retained traditional religion and resisted Islamic incursion.
