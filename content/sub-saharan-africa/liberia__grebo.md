## Overview

The Grebo are found mainly in southeastern Liberia and also in southwestern Côte d’Ivoire. Their culture includes masks, shrines, priests, age groups, bush schools, ceremonial clothing, drums, and ancestor practices. Civil-war displacement makes population figures uncertain. Grebo speech varieties form a broad language continuum, and tone is very important.

## Material culture

Grebo material culture includes carved masks, brass ritual objects, large drums, ceremonial cloth, stools, stones, and mortars.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The named textile tradition is the ceremonial lappa, while other patterns are recorded mainly on masks and metal objects.

- **Ceremonial wrap cloth** (*lappa*) — Lappa is a ceremonial wrap-around cloth compared with material made from raffia.
- **Named surface patterns** — Sources name spirals, zig-zag lines, parallel lines, spots, stripes, and incised motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Ceremonial clothing includes lappa and the black attire worn by Bodios and Jides.

- **Ceremonial wrap cloth** (*lappa*) — Lappa is a ceremonial wrap-around cloth, while common men had access to pants.
- **Black priestly attire** — Bodios and Jides are described as wearing black attire.

### Architecture

The main named building is the takae, a secluded residence for a Bodio.

- **Priest’s seclusion house** (*takae*) — A takae was built by Bodio followers and served as the Bodio’s place of seclusion and residence.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Grebo ritual and communal objects include masks, stools, stones, brass rings, drums, horns, chairs, and mortars.

- **Protective brass rings** (*nitien*) — Nitien were brass rings used at shrines for protection, healing, punishment, and sacred power.
- **Large village-protection rings** — Large brass rings were used to protect villages from spirits, sickness, war, and other people’s witchcraft.
- **Bronze ring from Grand Cess** — A museum record describes a bronze ring from Grand Cess with four raised elements, spiral, zig-zag, and parallel-line decoration.
- **Carved wooden masks** — Carved wooden masks were important ritual objects and were decorated with many added materials.
- **Wooden head figure** — Museum records include a wooden human-head figure.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Brass rings and richly decorated masks are the most distinctive forms of Grebo adornment described.

- **Bodio’s ankle ring** — A Bodio often wore a heavy brass ankle ring placed by a blacksmith until death, and it was regularly fed human blood.
- **Cowrie-shell mask** — A wooden mask from Maryland County had a beard, metal inlays around the eyes, and cowrie shells on the forehead.
- **Horn and bead head-dress** — Grebo masks and head-dresses could include horns, beads, cowrie shells, nails, metal studs, fibres, hair, fur, and painted or incised details.

## Music & performance

Large painted drums linked community life with communication, entertainment, battle, and danger signals.

- **Large village drums** — Some Grebo drums are painted and carved, used for towns and war, maintained in villages, and reach six or seven feet in height.
- **Paired tall drums** — The largest drums are commonly used in pairs, sometimes with chairs or mortars used to reach them.
- **Trumpets and horns** — Trumpets and horns were used for signaling and communication with laborers as well as for entertainment.
- **Male and female sound** — The source links a lower hum with male sound and a higher pitch with female sound.

## Dance & theatre

Masked dancers appeared mainly at funerals and dances, where the masks represented spirits and warriors.

- **Spirit-marked masked dancers** (*ku*) — Dancers wearing carved masks were daubed with white clay, marking a ku or spirit.
- **Helmet-like masks** — Most masks sat on basket-like structures above the head and worked more like helmets than face coverings.
- **Warrior masks** — Masks were especially linked with warriors and warfare and were meant to frighten enemies and empower those nearby.

## Festivals & rituals

Grebo ritual life includes bush schools, masked ceremonies, shrines, divination, offerings, sacrifices, and ordeal practices.

- **Men’s bush school** (*poro*) — Poro was a traditional bush school and society for males.
- **Women’s bush school** (*sande*) — Sande was a traditional bush school and society for females.
- **Masked forest ceremonies** — Highly initiated specialists made ritual masks, and performers wore them in forest rituals connected with spirits, funerals, dances, warriors, and warfare.
- **Spirit divination** — Divination was practiced by scrying, and spirits were believed to exist only in natural objects.
- **Sasswood ordeal** (*sasswood*) — A person accused of witchcraft could be forced to drink a decoction made from sasswood bark or vine in a trial by ordeal.

## Foodways

The sources mention food and alcohol only as offerings at shrines.

- **Food offerings** — Food was prepared for spirits at shrine sites to appease them and provide luck and fortune.
- **Alcohol offerings** — Alcohol was poured at shrines to appease spirits and provide luck and fortune.

## Oral tradition & literature

The profile records myth knowledge and oral histories but gives no stories, poems, proverbs, or named literary works.

- **Forest-zone myth culture** — Mask makers retained detailed information about the forest-zone myth culture.
- **Oral histories** — Oral histories were used in classifying Grebo language varieties, but their contents are not recorded.

## Language & religion

Grebo is a tonal Kru language, and religious life centers on gods, ancestors, spirits, shrines, priests, offerings, sacrifices, and divination.

- **Grebo language** — Grebo is a Kru language spoken in southern Liberia, with related languages called Krumen in Côte d’Ivoire.
- **First-person pronoun** (*né*) — Né with a high or high-mid tone means “I.”
- **Singular second-person pronoun** (*nè*) — Nè with a low tone means the singular form of “you.”
- **High priest** (*Bodio*) — The Bodio was a high priest guided by the gods.
- **Priest’s wife** (*Jide*) — The Jide was the Bodio’s wife and faced restrictions on public appearance and contact with objects.

## Glossary

- *lappa* — ceremonial wrap-around cloth
- *takae* — Bodio’s seclusion house
- *nitien* — brass ritual rings
- *Bodio* — high priest guided by the gods
- *Jide* — Bodio’s wife
- *ku* — spirit marked by white clay
- *poro* — men’s bush school and society
- *sande* — women’s bush school and society
- *sasswood* — bark or vine used in ordeal drinks
- *né* — “I” with high or high-mid tone
- *nè* — singular “you” with low tone
