## Overview

The Sakalava live along Madagascar’s western edge. Their society has regional customs and two main dialects. Their history includes Austronesian and Bantu settlement, Arab and European trade, royal dynasties, cattle herding, and royal ancestor worship. Tromba ceremonies connect communities with deceased rulers.

## Material culture

Sakalava material culture includes raffia textiles, thatched shelters, weapons, staffs, charms, beads, and simple string instruments.

### Textile & pattern traditions

A raffia shawl uses bold stripes and ikat patterns showing riders, animals, and geometric designs.

- **Raffia ikat shawl** — The shawl has blue, green, and beige stripes, two bands of ikat designs, and braided fringe.

### Clothing & dress

Recorded clothing includes cotton garments, tied tunics, grass-tied cloth, and a shawl.

- **Waist-tied tunic** — One recorded tunic is tied at the waist with a hide belt.
- **Grass-tied cotton garment** — Another garment is made of cotton cloth tied with grass.
- **Rider’s clothing** (*ombi*) — A recorded man wears head-gear, a tee-shirt, and shorts while riding an ombi, or humped cow.

### Architecture

Sakalava settlements include rectangular thatched shelters, while doany are places for royal ancestor worship.

- **Thatched shelter** — A settlement photograph shows rectangular thatched shelters with palms and eucalyptus trees nearby.
- **Royal ancestor shrine** (*doany*) — A doany is a place of worship with shrines for royal ancestors and can also be a royal gravesite.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Recorded objects include weapons, carved staffs, string instruments, cloth, beads, and warrior equipment.

- **Socketed spear** — Spears have iron or copper heads, hardwood shafts, and sometimes a copper spur at the butt.
- **Carved staff** — Recorded wooden staffs include one with a horse-and-rider handle and another carved with zigzags.
- **Gourd-resonator instrument** — A wooden guitar or zither uses a gourd resonator and vegetable-fibre strings.
- **Warrior rifle and powder-flask** — Model rifles and powder-flasks appear with male warrior figures.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Recorded adornment includes silver earrings and protective charms made from horn, wood, cloth, raffia, clay, and beads.

- **Silver earring** — The catalogue records an earring made of silver.
- **Animal-horn charm** — Some charms are made from animal horn and other materials, including cloth, raffia, wood, and glass beads.
- **Anti-theft charm** — One charm against thieves contains seven wood shavings and chips of wood.

## Music & performance

The named performance tradition is Tromba, a ceremony for communicating with deceased royal ancestors.

- **Tromba ceremony** (*Tromba*) — During Tromba, dady priests divine the spirits of deceased royal ancestors and communicate their words.
- **Wooden guitar or zither** — A recorded instrument is made of wood with a gourd resonator and vegetable-fibre strings.

## Dance & theatre

The sources do not describe named Sakalava dances or theatre forms.

- **Royal processions** — Tromba includes ceremonies and processions for the spirits of deceased royalty, but their movements are not described.

## Festivals & rituals

Sakalava religious life centers on royal ancestors, Tromba, memorial sites, and doany shrines.

- **Traditional royal ancestor religion** (*Fomba Gasy*) — Fomba Gasy centers on royal ancestor worship and the noble dady lineage.
- **Ancestor-assigned tree or stone** (*toñy*) — A toñy is a tree or stone assigned to a spirit in its honour.
- **Blessing limestone** (*tanifoty*) — Visitors to a doany may receive a small piece of limestone called a tanifoty as an ancestor’s blessing.
- **Spirit medium** (*saha*) — During Tromba, deceased rulers may possess mediums known as saha.

## Foodways

The Sakalava have historically kept large herds of zebu cattle, but the sources do not describe their food.

- **Zebu cattle herding** — Pastoralists traditionally allowed large zebu herds to graze freely over grasslands in the northwest.

## Oral tradition & literature

Sakalava history is preserved through local tradition, oral histories, and written documents.

- **Maroseraña founders** (*Maroseraña*) — Local tradition says the founders of the Sakalava kingdom were the Maroseraña princes from Fiherenana, now Toliara.
- **Maroseranana name** (*Maroseranana*) — The Maroseraña are also called Maroseranana, meaning “those who owned many ports.”
- **Ibonia epic** (*Ibonia*) — Ibonia is a Malagasy national epic about a folk hero, but the source does not identify it as specifically Sakalava.

## Language & religion

Sakalava speak two main Malagasy dialects and follow a mix of traditional, Islamic, and Christian beliefs.

- **Northern Sakalava dialect** (*Boina*) — Sakalava of Boina speak a northern Malagasy dialect with features shared with Tsimihety.
- **Southern Sakalava dialect** (*Menabe*) — Sakalava of Menabe speak a southern Malagasy dialect more closely related to Masikoro.
- **Sorabe script** (*Sorabe*) — Sorabe is a local development of the Arabic script that was used before Latin writing.
- **Sunni Islam** — Sunni Islam arrived through Arab traders and was adopted by Sakalava rulers in the eighteenth century.
