## Overview

The Moru are a people of South Sudan living in extended family groups in the southern Mundri County region. They speak Moru, a Central Sudanic language with five main dialect divisions. Most are subsistence farmers growing sorghum and other crops, with livestock kept mainly in the north and east. Their cultural life is notably organized around three seasonal dances keyed to the agricultural calendar, and much of their contemporary literary and religious life centers on the Christian church.

## Material culture

Moru material culture is dominated by iron tools and weapons, with leather and natural materials used for everyday objects; no woven textile tradition is documented.

### Textile & pattern traditions

No woven or dyed textile tradition is documented for the Moru.

- **Waist girdle** — A leather thong hung with bunches of nuts strung on iron rings and a few iron pendants.

### Clothing & dress

Very little is documented about Moru everyday or ceremonial dress.

- **Girdle** — A waist girdle of leather thong with nuts strung on iron rings and iron pendants.
- **Archery guard** — A leather protecting ring worn for archery.
- **Carrying-sling** — A baby-carrier made of antelope skin used to carry an infant.

### Architecture

Moru people live in extended family groups scattered through the landscape rather than in compact villages, with building materials sourced from the bush.

- **Family settlement** — Extended families live in groups surrounded by their gardens, linked into clans under headmen and sub-chiefs.
- **Megalithic grave marker** — Stone or wooden markers with horns attached to the top, sometimes with a shelter built behind them.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Iron is the dominant material in Moru material culture, used for tools, weapons, and currency; no ceramics are documented.

- **Hoe blade currency** — Wide, flat iron hoe blades with handles serving as both agricultural tools and money.
- **Bow** — A wooden bow bound with lizard skin and strung with gut.
- **Arrow** — Long wooden shafts with iron points, some poisoned for hunting.
- **Knife** — A two-edged, leaf-shaped iron blade set in a wooden handle.
- **Wheel trap** — A wooden trap with many spokes bound to two rims with palm leaf, used for hunting.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Iron and brass ornaments are documented; no tattooing, scarification, or hair practices are described.

- **Bracelet** — An iron bracelet ornamented with a double row of short projecting barbs.
- **Finger-ring** — A brass ring with a serrated design.

## Music & performance

Moru music centers on drum ensembles and harps that change with the seasons, plus church singing traditions documented in hymn books.

- **Dugye** (*Lari Endre (mother drum)*) — The largest drum in the Moru ensemble, used in all three seasonal dance cycles.
- **Ruma** — A medium drum that gives its name to the January-April dance.
- **Lari Ngwa** (*Children of the drum*) — Small high-pitched drums used in all seasonal dance cycles.
- **Diri** (*Biri*) — A drum that enters the ensemble during the harvest season.
- **Kudi** — A harp that plays important roles in recreation, cultural values, and male-female engagement.

## Dance & theatre

Three named dances divide the Moru year, each with its own drum ensemble and movements reflecting the season's physical demands and food abundance.

- **Ruma dance** — Danced from January to April when food is abundant and labour is light, using four drums: Dugye, Ruma, and two Lari Ngwa.
- **Dego dance** — Runs from May to August during heavy agricultural work, using three drums and progressing from slow movements to faster Ngala steps.
- **Yelu dance** — The harvest season dance from September to December with five drums, featuring vigorous movements reflecting renewed health and strength.

## Festivals & rituals

Moru ritual life is organized around the three seasonal dances and Christian church practices; burial involves megalithic stone grave markers with horns.

- **Seasonal dance cycle** — The Ruma, Dego, and Yelu dances structure the ritual year around agricultural seasons and food availability.
- **Burial with megalithic markers** — Stone or wooden grave markers with horns attached, sometimes with a shelter behind them.
- **Buku Mätu Ro** — The Book of Common Prayer in Moru, used in Episcopal Church services.

## Foodways

The Moru are subsistence farmers dependent on rain-fed agriculture, with sorghum as the staple crop supplemented by wild foods from hunting, fishing, and gathering.

- **Sorghum** — The staple crop, grown with sesame, cowpeas, bulrush millet, and finger millet in various field types.
- **Kätiri** — A cultivation unit made of close relatives working together in one area, organized around a central marker like a large tree or ant hill.
- **Wild foods** — Vegetables, fruit, fungi, insects (especially termites), and wild relishes gathered from the bush.
- **Livestock** — Sheep, goats, and poultry kept by most households; cattle mainly in the north and east.

## Oral tradition & literature

Moru literature is a growing tradition rooted in church use, with published folk tales, histories, and poetry in the Moru language.

- **Kitoŋwa Tauniʼba yi and Kitoŋwa ʼdi Odoʼba yi** — Two booklets of local folk tales published in Moru Kediro in 1981, one available as a PDF online.
- **Taoye Opi Moru Ro Kyeno Kayi Ro** — A history titled 'Behaviour of the Past Moru Chiefs' published by Scopas M. Elias through the Moru Literature Committee in 1987.
- **Moru–English Dictionary** — Issued by the Moru Literacy Programme in 2007.

## Language & religion

Moru is a Central Sudanic language with six dialects; it has been preserved and developed through Christian church use with Bible, hymn, and school materials in the language.

- **Moru language dialects** (*Agi, Andri, ʼBaliʼba, Kadiro (Lakamaʼdi), Miza, Moruwaʼdi*) — Six recognized dialects of the Moru language, with Moru Miza serving as the vernacular for education and church.
- **Taobaro Toʼdi** — The New Testament and Psalms in Moru, published in 1951.
- **Baibolo Alokado** — The full Bible in Moru, published by the Bible Society in Sudan in 1999.
- **Kito Lusi ro Luka be** — A school primer first published in 1953 and often reprinted.
- **Buzevosite** — An adult literacy primer with a Christian theme, published in the 1970s by New Day Publishers in Juba.

## Glossary

- *Kediro* — One of five main Moru divisions
- *Dugye* — The largest drum, also called Lari Endre (mother drum)
- *Lari Endre* — Mother drum, the largest in the ensemble
- *Ruma* — Medium drum; also the name of the January-April dance
- *Lari Ngwa* — Small high-pitched drums, called children of the drum
- *Diri* — Drum that enters the harvest season ensemble, also called Biri
- *Biri* — Alternate name for the Diri drum
- *Kudi* — A harp used in recreation and male-female engagement
- *Kätiri* — A cultivation unit of close relatives working together around a central marker
- *Ngala* — The faster progression of the Dego dance
- *Ruma* — The January-April dance when food is abundant
- *Dego* — The May-August dance during heavy agricultural work
- *Yelu* — The September-December harvest season dance
- *Buku Mätu Ro* — Book of Common Prayer in Moru
