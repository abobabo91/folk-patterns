## Overview

The Somba, also known as the Tammari, Batammariba, Tamberma, Otamari, or Ottamari, are an Oti–Volta-speaking people of Benin’s Atakora Department and neighboring areas of Togo, where they are officially known as Ta(m)berma. They are also found in towns including Nikki, Kandi, Parakou, Natitingou, and surrounding villages. The Tammari language belongs to the Gur family. The people are described as agronomic herdsmen who inhabit hills and valleys and maintain a strongly clannish social organization. Their cultural identity is especially associated with the fortified earthen dwellings known as *Tata Somba*, or *Takyenta*, as well as with initiation ceremonies, funeral rites, sacred relationships to land, and a religious world centered on underground forces and ancestral powers.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do describe traditional body scarring as a form of visible identification. These marks begin between the ages of two and three and identify a person as belonging to the person’s tribe while also conveying coded personal information. Additional scars are added at puberty, at readiness for marriage, and after childbirth. The marks can occur on the face, belly, and back and are described as lifelong forms of communication.

### Architecture

The most extensively documented material tradition is the *Takyenta*, also spelled *Takienta*, which includes the better-known *Tata Somba*. These dwellings are typically built of mud and surrounded by towers supporting garrets, giving them the appearance of medieval citadels. Their massive, storied construction functions as a protective fortress, designed to keep out invaders and repel spear attacks. It also protects inhabitants from leopards said by village elders to roam in the overgrown bush.

The dwellings have masculine and feminine orientations: the masculine side faces south and the feminine side faces north. Models differ from village to village. The ground floor houses livestock at night, while internal alcoves are used for cooking. The upper floor contains a rooftop courtyard for drying grain, together with sleeping quarters and granaries. The upper level also serves as a living space and safe haven. The houses developed through the addition of an enclosing roof to clusters of huts connected by a wall, a form described as typical of Gur-speaking areas of West Africa.

Building a fortress takes several months and requires skilled labor. Until 2000, parents and children slept in elevated box structures placed along the sides and central areas of the terrace; these also protected inhabitants and guests from midday heat. Nowadays, the fortresses are reserved for ancestral devotional ceremonies. Earthen, cone-shaped altars are associated with ancestral souls, and strangers cannot enter the temple area without permission from the head of the home.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that the upper levels of the traditional dwellings contain granaries and rooftop courtyards used for drying grain, while the ground floor provides shelter for livestock and internal alcoves are used for cooking.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They do document traditional scarification. The marks begin in early childhood, serve as lifelong tribal identification, and communicate additional personal information connected with puberty, readiness for marriage, and childbirth. Scarring can extend across the face, belly, and back.

## Music & performance

They do, however, describe ritual and ceremonial contexts in which cultural knowledge and authority are maintained. Initiation ceremonies and funeral rites are central institutions, and a ritual center is organized around a cemetery, a large initiation house for youth, and a head serpent sanctuary. Those responsible for rituals are selected according to rigorous ethical expectations, especially discretion and self-mastery.

The sources also describe ceremonies as preserving warrior traditions, hunting skills, pride, care for the land, and respect for ancestral obligations. The youngest members of the community, whether schooled or not and whether they leave or remain in the village, very rarely abandon the cycle of initiation tradition. No source used here provides names for musical instruments, repertories, dances accompanied by music, or specialized performance settings.

## Dance & theatre

They describe initiation ceremonies, funeral rites, ancestral devotional ceremonies, and ritual meetings with underground forces.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document a ritual system organized around clans, territorial groups, cemeteries, initiation houses, serpent sanctuaries, and sacred relationships with the land.

Two, four, or six clans can form what the source calls a village, or more precisely a territorial groupement, centered on rituals upheld by each clan. The ritual center is the foundation of the village. Clans recognize family bonds because they descend from the same founding fathers, while the society rejects centralized power and hereditary chieftains. It maintains an age-based hierarchy between elders and younger residents.

Initiation ceremonies are meetings with underground forces and require bravery, self-mastery, and discretion. The Batammariba preserve an important cycle of initiation tradition, including rites connected with gender, martial prowess, clairvoyance, and other ritual qualifications. The traditional religion includes initiation deities such as Fawafa, the python deity of men’s initiations, and Fakuntifa, the lizard deity of women’s initiations.

Funeral rites are described as exceptionally well preserved. The souls of ancestors are associated with earthen, cone-shaped altars in the fortified dwellings. Exterior southern altars contain spirits of animals formerly hunted and killed, while altars may also contain underground spirits with whom ancestors possessing the gift of “sight” made pacts.

## Foodways

They state that the Batammariba are agronomic herdsmen and that their traditional houses contain cooking alcoves, livestock areas, granaries, and rooftop courtyards used for drying grain.

The relationship between food production and sacred land is described through an alliance between the ancestors of the Batammariba and underground forces regarded as the true owners of the region. The ancestors swore to respect agricultural rules and the pieces of land belonging to those forces. In exchange, the forces allowed them to build houses and harvest the soil. The sources therefore connect agriculture with ritual obligations.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do identify the Batammariba as affirming allegiance as “Serpent Children” and describing themselves as descendants of a grand, invisible, underground “Serpent Mother” who bore the first eggs of their ancestors.

The sources also describe the mythological first village, Linaba, where Kuiye first created humans and deities. The Batammariba’s relationship to ancestors, underground forces, serpent sanctuaries, and sacred places forms part of the cultural knowledge maintained through ritual institutions. Researchers and writers listed in the source include Rigobert Kouagou, a specialist in the Tammari language and author of poems and stories translated into French, and Dominique Sewane, whose work has focused on Batammariba funeral and initiation rites.

## Language & religion

The Tammari language belongs to the Gur family, and Batammariba language is described as a strong common link among communities that do not form a fully homogeneous society. The source on Somba language identifies Somba as a dialect of the Somba-Siawari language and places Somba among the Eastern branch of the Oti–Volta languages.

The traditional religion centers on Kuiye, the Supreme Deity, sun god, and creator of gods and humans. Kuiye is described as both male and female and is referred to as “The Sun, Our Father and Our Mother.” Butan, goddess of the Earth and the Underworld, is Kuiye’s complement and governs the earth, vegetal growth, agriculture, game proliferation, and cemeteries. Oyinkakwata, “the Rich Man Above,” is the god of the sky, thunder, lightning, and storms.

The religious world also includes Fawafa, Fakuntifa, Fayenfe, Litakon, and Kupon, associated respectively with initiation, war and death, twins and fertility, and divination. The Batammariba are mostly animists. Their sacred geography includes underground forces incarnated in rocks or trees, ancestral altars, village springs, cemeteries, and serpent sanctuaries.
