## Overview

The Argobba are a Muslim ethnic group inhabiting isolated village networks and towns in the north-eastern and eastern parts of Ethiopia. Communities are found in the Afar, Harari, Amhara, and Oromia Regions, in and along the Great Rift Valley, including Yimlawo, Gusa, Shonke, Berehet, Khayr Amba, Melka Jilo, Aliyu Amba, Metehara, Shewa Robit, and surrounding rural villages. The Argobba traditionally speak Argobba, an Ethiopian Semitic language belonging to the South Ethiopic subgroup of the Afroasiatic language family and closely related to Amharic. Historically associated with trade, Muslim sultanates, hilltop settlements, mosques, marketplaces, and Qur'anic schooling, the Argobba are also documented as an endangered community whose language has declined through economic change, exogamy, destitution, and historical persecution.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do record that Argobba women were subjected to a chant in Harar referring to them as “night-time hyena, day-time human,” but this concerns a social stigma rather than clothing.

### Architecture

Argobba settlements were typically located on hilltops and mountain retreats known as *amba*. Residences consisted of stone-and-wood square houses or huts with earth and hay-thatched roofs. Each village had at least one mosque, a weaver’s workshop, a public square, a marketplace, and a *zawiya* for Qur'anic schooling. In 1902, Oscar Neumann described Argobba houses near Harar as stone buildings with high watchtowers in the centre, surrounded by strong walls. He reported that many had fallen into decay and that old ruins overlooking the Erer valley resembled medieval castles. Straw huts associated with the Ala Galla were also scattered among the ruins.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention stone-and-wood houses, straw huts, marketplaces, and weavers’ workshops, but provide no catalogue of material objects.

## Music & performance

They mention Qur'anic schooling in the village *zawiya* and a chant directed at Argobba women by Harari children.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources identify the Argobba as exclusive adherents to the Muslim faith and describe them as widely believed to have been the first to accept Islam collectively in the Horn of Africa and as vanguards of early Islamic expansion. Villages contained mosques and *zawiya* for Qur'anic schooling.

Historical religious change is documented. After the incorporation of Ifat into Ethiopia, large sections of the local Argobba population embraced Christianity. A 1517 invasion led by Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi was described as having forced inhabitants of Ifat-Argobba to embrace Islam again. The Shonke Argobba reportedly forbid their children from attending school because of possible non-Islamic influence.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources record traditional explanations for the name Argobba. One derives it from the Amharic phrase *Arab gebba*, meaning “Arabs came”; Aklilu Asfaw recorded the explanation *Arab geba*, translated as “Arabs have entered.” Modern Argobba claim an origin in the Arabian Peninsula through Zeila in what is now Somaliland, followed by settlement in Ifat. Argobba people consider the inhabitants of Doba their ancestors.

Harari tradition holds that numerous Argobba fled Ifat and settled around Harar in the Aw Abdal lowlands during their conflict with Abyssinia in the fifteenth century; a gate was named after them and called the gate of Argobba. Harari and Oromo communities in the Harar region are also said to preserve legends concerning Argobba, including accusations of cannibalism and the belief that Argobba are were-hyenas.

## Language & religion

Argobba is an Ethiopian Semitic language within the Afroasiatic language family and belongs to the South Ethiopic languages subgroup. It is closely related to Amharic, but Getahun Amare described it as a separate language rather than an Amharic dialect. Argobba is spoken in several districts of the Afar, Amhara, and Oromia Regions. In the Harari Region, many Argobba are shifting to Oromo, while people in Ankober speak Amharic. The sources state that only a few areas remain where Argobba are not at least bilingual in Amharic, Oromo, or Afar.

The language has six vowel phonemes, eleven plosive consonant phonemes, and five syllable types: V, VC, CV, CVC, and CVCC. It is agglutinative and uses prefixes and suffixes, with four cases: nominative, accusative, genitive, and oblique. Argobba distinguishes singular, plural, and transnumeral forms. Religiously, Argobba are Muslims, and village mosques and *zawiya* supported religious life and Qur'anic schooling.
