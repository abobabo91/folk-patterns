## Overview

The Fulani — *Fulɓe* in their own language, *Peul* in French, *Fula* in English, *Halpulaar* ("speakers of Pulaar") in Senegal — are a semi-nomadic cattle-herding people dispersed in a broad belt from Senegal to Sudan, the most widely spread ethnic group in West Africa. In Senegal they concentrate in the Fouta Toro, the middle Senegal River valley running from Dagana upstream past Podor and Matam, and in the Ferlo, the dry sahelian interior where transhumant herders move between seasonal wells. A second, sedentary branch, the **Toucouleur** (*Tukulor*), farms the river floodplain and shares the Pulaar language with the pastoralists. Fulani represent roughly a quarter of Senegal's population, and about 6% of Nigeria's. Pulaar belongs to the Atlantic (Senegambian) branch of Niger-Congo. Their folk culture matters because it is the material record of a mobile life: everything of value is portable, hung, wrapped, or worn.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Arkilla kerka** — the great marriage blanket of the Fulani weavers of the middle Niger and Senegal valleys, woven by male *maabuuɓe* (weaver-caste) artisans on the narrow horizontal treadle loom in six to eight strips, then sewn edge-to-edge into a panel two metres by four. Wool weft on cotton warp, indigo-blue, madder-red, white and black, with a dense field of interlocking tapestry-woven lozenges. It is hung as a curtain across the entrance of the bridal hut, not laid on a bed — a wall, not a bedspread, which distinguishes it from the flat-lying Manding blankets.

**Khasa** — the thick all-wool blanket woven from the fleece of the sahelian long-haired sheep, in wide bands of undyed cream and dark brown or indigo. It is a herder's object: worn over the shoulder against the cold harmattan nights of the Ferlo, and sold across the Sahel as a trade good. The wool is fulled and beaten after weaving, giving it a felted nap absent in cotton blankets.

**Arkilla jenngo** — a lighter, narrower relative of the *kerka*, with fewer strips and a more open pattern, used as a bed hanging and as a dowry piece of lesser rank.

**Indigo Fulani cloth** — Pulaar and Toucouleur dyers of the river valley work cotton strip-cloth in fermented indigo vats, layering repeated dips until the cloth reaches the near-black, metallic sheen called *gara*, then beating it with wooden mallets to raise a burnished surface. Resist patterns are tied or stitched before dipping.

**Fulani embroidery** — white or ochre cotton thread worked in chain and satin stitch over the chest, neck-opening and sleeve-ends of men's robes and around the yoke of women's wrappers, in registers of triangles and stepped diamonds that echo the weave patterns rather than depicting anything.

**Leather goatskin work** — Fulani leatherworkers (*sakkeeɓe*) cut, dye and plait goatskin into saddle covers, amulet cases, sandals, cushion covers and the fringed and braided straps that carry milk gourds. Geometric panels are tooled and dyed red, green and black, with cut-out negative shapes backed by contrasting hide.

**Motif vocabulary.** Interlocking lozenge (the *kerka*'s dominant field unit); the stepped triangle, read as the hobble or tether of a cow; the chevron band; the concentric diamond "eye"; the cowrie row; the cattle-hoof print; the zigzag "river"; the eight-pointed star of the amulet-case leather; the fringe-and-tassel border that terminates every woven panel.

### Clothing & dress

Men wear the *boubou* (*mbubbu*), a wide sleeveless or wide-sleeved robe over drawstring trousers, in white, indigo or the burnished *gara* cloth, with embroidery massed at the chest pocket. The conical **Fulani hat** — a woven straw or reed cone with a wide brim, bound with dyed leather strips and often topped with a leather knob and pendant thongs — is the single most recognisable Fulani object in West Africa, worn by herders against sun and rain. Herders carry a long staff (*sawru*) and a knife sheathed in tooled leather at the upper arm.

Women wrap the *pagne* over the hips, with a matching shoulder cloth and head-tie; married Toucouleur women of the river towns favour heavily starched, elaborately folded head-wraps. Ceremonial dress for pastoralist women means layering: indigo wrapper, dozens of amber and silver ornaments, and hair dressed into a crest over a padded armature and hung with coins and beads.

At the **gerewol**, the courtship gathering of the Woɗaaɓe (a Fulani sub-group of the eastern Sahel), young men are the ornamented sex: faces painted yellow-ochre and red with a white line down the nose, lips and eyelids blackened to set off white teeth and eyes, ostrich plume in the turban, chest crossed with amulet straps.

### Architecture

Pastoral Fulani build the *suudu hudo*, a demountable grass house: a hemisphere or loaf-shape of bent millet-stalk or acacia arches lashed at the crown and thatched with mats of woven grass, erected by women, struck and loaded on pack-ox or donkey at the move. The mats themselves — rolled reed screens bound with dyed leather thong in banded patterns — are dowry property and the principal decorated surface of the nomadic home. Inside, the *arkilla* hangs at the entrance and the milk gourds are stacked on a wooden rack at the honoured side.

Sedentary Toucouleur villages of the Fouta Toro build rectangular mud-brick (banco) houses with flat or low-pitched thatch roofs, arranged around a swept sandy courtyard with a fenced enclosure of millet-stalk screens. Village mosques of the valley follow the sahelian mud-and-timber manner: massive tapering buttressed walls with projecting toron beams, a low minaret over the mihrab. The compound fence, the cattle corral and the granary on stilts define the built space as much as the houses do.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The **milk gourd** (*kaggu*, or the decorated calabash) is the defining Fulani object. Halved *Lagenaria* gourds are scraped, smoked, and incised or pyro-engraved with fine registers of hatched triangles, zigzags and concentric bands, then rubbed with soot or ochre so the pattern reads dark against the shell. A woman's stack of nested calabashes — displayed on a rack, carried on the head to market, exhibited in full at her wedding — is her wealth, her lineage record and her household inventory in one. Larger gourds are bound with plaited leather netting for transport; some are repaired with stitched fibre, the repairs themselves becoming ornament.

Wooden churns, milking bowls, spoons and the carved *sawru* staff are turned and incised by the *laoɓe* woodworking caste. Blacksmiths (*wayilɓe*) forge knives, hoes, spear blades and the silver ornaments below.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Fulani women's signature ornament is the crescent gold earring, *kwottenai kanye* — a twisted, flared hoop of hammered gold worked in four lobes, so heavy it is supported by a cord of red wool or cotton over the crown of the head. Gold and gilt, rather than silver, mark the high-status pastoralist woman across the Sahel. Silver dominates elsewhere: hollow beads, torques, bracelets, anklets, and the *tumar*-type amulet case — a flat rectangular or cylindrical container of silver or tooled leather holding a folded Qur'anic text, hung at the neck or strapped across the chest. Amber beads, carnelian, coral and glass trade beads are strung in the hair and at the neck. Hair is dressed in high crested coiffures over a shaped pad, parted in fine rows and hung with coins, beads and silver discs. Henna is applied to hands and feet for weddings, and a fine line of dark blue-black tattoo along the lower lip and gum margin is the mark of adult Fulani womanhood.

## Music & performance

Fulani music is caste music: the hereditary bards (*awlube*, singular *gawlo*) are the praise singers and genealogists, attached to noble lineages and paid to recite them. The instrument most identified with the Fulani is the *hoddu*, a three- to five-string plucked lute with a hide-covered wooden body, ancestor of the banjo family and close cousin of the Wolof *xalam*; the *molo* is its smaller one- or two-string relative. Herders play the *fulannu* or *serdu*, a slender end-blown reed flute whose breathy, overblown line is the archetypal cattle-camp sound, often sung simultaneously by the player to produce a two-voice drone-and-melody. Women keep the rhythm on a calabash floated upside-down in water and struck with rings (the water-drum), on inverted gourds struck with the palms, and by clapping.

Song genres divide by function: the *dilluɓe* praise-recitation of lineage; herding songs sung to particular cattle by name, believed to quiet and direct the herd; grinding and butter-churning songs; and the *ɗaɗɗo*, the massed men's chant. In the Fouta Toro the Pulaar repertoire overlaps heavily with Wolof and Soninke music of the Senegal valley, and Senegalese Fulani musicians — Baaba Maal of Podor above all — have carried the *hoddu* and Pulaar praise-form into the international recording repertoire.

## Dance & theatre

The best-documented Fulani performance is the **gerewol**, the male beauty dance of the Woɗaaɓe, held at the end of the rains where clans converge at a temporary camp. Young men in painted faces and ostrich plumes line up shoulder to shoulder and dance for hours on tiptoe, rolling their eyes and baring their teeth — white eyes and white teeth being the judged points of beauty — while unmarried women selected as judges pass along the line and choose. The related **yaake** is the slow, grimacing charm dance; the **ruume** is the welcome dance that opens the gathering. These are courtship rites with a legal outcome: a choice made at *gerewol* can constitute a marriage.

Sedentary Fulani and Toucouleur dance at weddings, naming ceremonies and Muslim festivals in circle formation, with the dancers entering the ring singly to a drum break. There is no masked or puppet theatre tradition; Fulani dramatic expression is in praise-oratory and in the staged competitive display of the herding camp.

## Festivals & rituals

The calendar is governed by the Muslim year and by the movement of cattle. *Korité* (Eid al-Fitr) and *Tabaski* (Eid al-Adha) are the great village festivals of Senegal, the second requiring the sacrifice of a ram per household and new clothes for every member of it. *Gamou* (Mawlid, the Prophet's birthday) draws Toucouleur Sufi adherents to the Tijani centres; the *Magal* of Touba and the Tijani *Gamou* of Tivaouane are national pilgrimages that Halpulaar households join.

The pastoral calendar's own ceremony is the crossing of the Senegal and Niger rivers by the herds at the end of the dry season, and the **gerewol** gatherings of the rains, when dispersed clans reunite, marriages are made and disputes settled.

Life-cycle rites: the naming ceremony (*inde*, Wolof *ngente*) on the seventh day after birth, when the head is shaved, a ram sacrificed and the name pronounced by the imam; circumcision of boys, followed historically by a seclusion period in a bush camp with instruction and songs; marriage in stages — bride-price in cattle, the Islamic contract, the hanging of the *arkilla kerka* and the transfer of the calabash stack, the bride's move to her husband's compound. Funerals follow Muslim rite, with the mourning assembly and the third- and fortieth-day gatherings.

The unwritten code beneath all of this is *pulaaku* — the Fulani ethic of reserve, patience, modesty and self-control, the conduct that makes a person recognisably Fulani.

## Foodways

The diet is built on milk and grain. Fresh milk, soured milk (*kossam*) and butter are the herder's staples; the sedentary diet is millet and sorghum, with rice dominant in the river valley and in Senegalese towns. *Lacciri e kossam* — steamed millet couscous under soured milk, sometimes sweetened — is the dish most identified with Fulani households across the Sahel, and the one a guest is offered. *Thiakry* (*dégué*) is its dessert form: fine millet granules in sweetened curdled milk, served at naming ceremonies and Ramadan breaks.

From the wider Senegalese table, Fulani households share *thieboudienne* (rice with fish and vegetables in tomato), *yassa* (onion-and-lemon sauce over chicken or fish), *mafé* (groundnut stew) and *lakh*, a millet porridge with sour milk. Meat is eaten at festivals rather than daily — the herd is capital, valued for milk and increase, and slaughtered for Tabaski, weddings and funerals.

Tea is *ataya*, green gunpowder tea boiled with sugar and mint and poured from height into small glasses, served in three rounds of descending strength and ascending sweetness, each round a required social unit of the visit. Food is halal; alcohol is absent from observant households.

## Oral tradition & literature

Pulaar oral literature is dominated by genealogy and by pastoral epic. The *gawlo* bards recite lineage chains reaching back generations, and the cycle of **Silâmaka and Poullôri** — the Fulani epic of the Macina hero and his companion, their defiance of the Bambara king of Ségou, and their deaths — is the best-known narrative poem of the Fulani-speaking world, collected in Mali and Senegal from the mid-twentieth century onward. Herding narratives centre on the origin of cattle emerging from water, and on the culture-hero who first brought the herd out of the river.

Alongside the epic run *taali* folktales told at night by women to children, animal trickster cycles shared with Wolof and Mandinka neighbours, riddle games, and praise-names for individual cattle. Written Pulaar exists in *ajami* — Arabic script adapted to Pulaar — used for devotional verse and Sufi poetry in the Fouta Toro since at least the eighteenth century, and Senegal now teaches Pulaar in a standard Latin orthography. The Malian scholar Amadou Hampâté Bâ's collection and transcription of Fulani initiatory texts and epic is the foundational documentation project for this literature.

## Language & religion

Pulaar (Fulfulde further east) is an Atlantic language of the Niger-Congo family, characterised by an unusually large noun-class system — over twenty classes — and by consonant mutation at the word stem. Senegalese dialects divide between Fouta Toro Pulaar and the Ferlo pastoral varieties; it is a national language of Senegal and is written both in Latin orthography and in *ajami*.

The Fulani are overwhelmingly Sunni Muslim of the Maliki school, and the Fouta Toro was one of the earliest and most thoroughly Islamised regions of West Africa — the eighteenth-century *torodbe* clerical movement and the nineteenth-century jihad of El Hadj Umar Tall, a Toucouleur of Halwar, both issued from it. Most Senegalese Fulani belong to the Tijaniyya Sufi order. Pre-Islamic practice persists in the amulet trade, in divination, and in the ritual regard for cattle, and among the Woɗaaɓe of the eastern Sahel an older layer of practice — the *gerewol*, the ritual specialists, the belief in the potency of charms — sits openly alongside Islam.

Beyond Senegal, Fulani communities face significant hostility in several West African states: accusations of complicity with Islamist groups, the herder–farmer land conflicts in Nigeria, and lynchings and massacres targeting Fulani civilians, including the 2019 Ogossagou massacre in Mali. This is context for how Fulani material culture is read and collected today.
