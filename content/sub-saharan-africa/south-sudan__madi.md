## Overview

The Madi or Màʼdí are a Central Sudanic-speaking people living in Magwi County in Eastern Equatoria, South Sudan, and in the districts of Adjumani and Moyo in Uganda. Their territory extends from Nimule at the South Sudan–Uganda border to the Nyolo River, where the Maʼdi mingle with the Acholi, Bari, and Lolubo, and from Parajok/Magwi westward across the River Nile. Maʼdi speakers call themselves Maʼdi, meaning “people,” and call their language *maʼdi ti*, literally “Maʼdi mouth.” Their history is preserved through oral narratives concerning migration, settlement, inter-group relations, chiefs, resistance to the Tukutuku, and migration to Uganda.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources mention Dufile Fort, built on the Albert Nile inside Uganda.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention groundnut, edible fruit called *lugba*, ivory tusks, and the construction of Dufile Fort, but provide no catalogue of Madi objects or production techniques.

## Festivals & rituals

The historical narratives do describe councils of elders, ransom, blood-price, chieftaincy, conflict settlement, and the giving of Abinia as compensation in a dispute between Vuri and Muludia.

In one account, Jukiri brokered peace after Vuri’s son killed Muludia’s son. Vuri was required to pay a ransom, and because he did not have a virgin daughter, Jukiri gave him his daughter Abinia, whom Vuri gave to Muludia. A council of elders then settled the conflict. The sources present this as an element of oral historical narrative rather than as a general description of contemporary Madi ritual practice.

## Foodways

Food appears in historical narratives through groundnut and *lugba*, an edible tree identified in the source as “desert dates” and as *ximenia aegyptiaca*. One narrative says that a Maʼdi man found groundnut and ate it alone, causing a dispute with his Moru brother. Another says that a group moving in a famished state found *lugba*, ate its fruits, and carried some with them.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral history is central to the sources’ account of Madi origins, migrations, settlement, political succession, and relations with neighboring peoples. One narrative says that the Maʼdi came from Nigeria, moved southward to Amadi in southwest Uganda, and later reached the River Nile, where the Maʼdi and Moru separated. The source states that the period of this migration cannot be specified by Maʼdi oral history.

Other narratives explain the names of related groups. The progenitors of the Maʼdi were said to have declared *Muro-Amadri*, “Let’s form a settle here,” while another group eventually declared *ma ʼdi*, “here I am (finally).” The name *lugbara* is connected in one narrative with people asking about *lugba ri*, “the desert dates.”

The sources also preserve narratives about chiefs, founders, migrations, and conflicts. Andruga became the first chief of Bori, followed by his son Milla Akeri and then Kanyara Draru. Manini is named as a possible founder of Guru, later renamed Nimule. Jukiri is described as the founder of the Lukai group, and his chieftaincy as the beginning of the Odrupee rule in Lukai.

## Language & religion

Maʼdi speakers call their language *maʼdi ti*, literally “Maʼdi mouth.” The Madi are described as Central Sudanic-speaking, while the separate article on Madi language identifies Maʼdi as a Nilo-Saharan language of Uganda and South Sudan. Speakers refer to one another as belonging to a *suru*, or tribe, which may be divided into a *pa*, or clan; in some cases, *suru* and *pa* overlap. A Maʼdi can marry only outside their clan but normally marries within the wider group sharing the Maʼdi language.
