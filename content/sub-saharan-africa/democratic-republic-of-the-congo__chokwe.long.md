## Overview

The Chokwe (also *Cokwe*, *Tshokwe*, *Bajokwe*, *Kioko*) are a Bantu people of roughly 1.3 million spread across a single cultural zone that colonial borders cut into three: northeastern Angola, the southwestern Democratic Republic of the Congo between Kinshasa and Lualaba, and northwestern Zambia. Their heartland is the upper Kasai and upper Zambezi watershed — high savanna woodland with a rainy season from October to April and a dry season that dictates when fields are cleared, houses built, and initiation camps opened. They speak Chokwe (*Kichokwe*), a Benue-Congo language of the Niger-Congo family, alongside Portuguese, French, or English depending on the state. In folk-culture terms the Chokwe matter disproportionately: their court sculpture, masquerade, and divination apparatus are among the most systematically studied bodies of Central African art, largely through Marie-Louise Bastin's two-volume documentation of *art décoratif tshokwe*.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Chokwe pattern-bearing work is concentrated less in loom weaving than in fibre construction, mask surfaces, and incised wood — so the "textile" category here is properly a fibre-and-surface category.

**Makishi mask fabric** — the bodies of Chokwe masks are built from plaited vegetable fibre over a wooden or resin armature, then covered with barkcloth, trade cloth, and paper, and painted in the canonical three-colour scheme of black, red, and white. The distinction from neighbouring Lunda and Luvale masking lies in the hard resin-and-fibre faces of the portrait masks, which are modelled rather than carved alone.

**Ngombo divination basket** — the *ngombo ya cisuka*, a coiled lidded basket holding several dozen miniature figures, bones, horns, seeds, and fragments. The diviner shakes it and reads the configuration that surfaces. No neighbouring group has so elaborate a curated object-lexicon inside a basket.

**Pwo and mwana pwo masks** — *pwo* (mature woman) and *mwana pwo* (girl) are near-portrait masks whose surfaces carry the same incised linear designs, *cijingo*, that are scarified and painted onto an initiate's body at the close of *ukule*. The mask and the human skin bear one pattern vocabulary.

**Cikungu mask** — the largest Chokwe mask, property of the *mwanangana* (chief) alone, embodying the collective ancestors. Its lateral wings reference the black stork, *khumbi*; its bands of triangles, *yenge*, are read as the dorsal markings of the Gaboon viper.

**Cikunza headdress** — the tall conical fibre-and-resin headdress of the *mukanda* circumcision masquerade, explicitly read as a grasshopper and as the horns of an antelope: fertility fused with virility.

**Basketry and matting** — coiled and twilled utility baskets, grain containers, and sleeping mats in savanna grasses, with banded geometric registers that recur on carved stool rims.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Yenge* — bands of triangles, the Gaboon viper's back; sawtooth — "the viper of the stork," paired with *khumbi* wing forms; *cijingo* — the linear body-scarification designs shared by skin and mask; concentric lozenges on stool seats and snuff mortars; chevron registers on basket walls; pangolin-scale imbrication, after *nkaka*, the pangolin, whose name is also the word for grandfather; cruciform and cross-hatched fields on tobacco mortars.

### Clothing & dress

Daily dress is now cloth wrappers, but ceremonial dress remains sharply codified. The chief's floor-length skirt is worn by the *cikungu* masquerader, who by rule is the mask's owner — the mwanangana himself — and who carries a *mukwale* sword or, in later practice, a rifle. The *cikunza* masquerader appears with a *mukwale* in one hand and a *citete* branch in the other. Diviners dress the part: costumes are trimmed with fur, feathers, claws, beaks, and bone, and a *cimbanda* may carry genet fur, since the genet's speed and shyness stand for the ability to move unseen at night. Chiefly regalia proper is an ensemble rather than a garment — combs, jewellery, whistles, horns, spears, scepters, and staffs, all carved. At the end of a girl's *ukule* initiation her body itself is the costume: painted with linear designs for the procession through the village in which she dances what she learned in camp and receives gifts from relatives and neighbours.

### Architecture

A Chokwe village is a ring of dwellings around an open central courtyard, occupied by the headman's relatives and their families. Houses are square huts of sun-dried mud brick, formed in wooden moulds at home or bought ready-made, roofed with bunched thick grass under finer grass layers; circular grass-houses also occur. Kitchens and storage rooms are built immediately adjacent to the houses. The defining structure is the *chota*, a cone-shaped shelter of freestanding wooden poles under a grass roof, set in the middle of the courtyard: the place where visitors are received and where men convene. It is a men's house, a court, and a reception hall in one — and it is where the sacrificed goat or rooster of a *cikungu* rite is hung from a pole. Outside the settlement stand two purpose-built ritual enclosures: the *mukanda* compound, screened in bush away from the village, and the *ukule* hut on the village edge, a cone-shaped grass structure in the village's own idiom. Ancestral shrines, *kachipango*, hold sculpture and objects through which spirits are called.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Chokwe object-making covers pottery, basketry, and above all carved wood. The signature form is the **ngunja** — the chief's, headman's, or senior elder's carved chair or joined square stool, which the Chokwe themselves call a throne because it states the owner's rank as keeper of social order. Chair uprights, stretchers, and seat rims carry figural scenes: initiation, drumming, divination, palm-wine tapping, human and animal figures in tiers. Alongside thrones: snuff and tobacco mortars, ceremonial axes, whistles, horns, combs, pipes, staffs, scepters, and lidded containers. Utilitarian carving is done by laymen; commissioned chairs and cult objects are the work of the professional carver, the *songi*, and not every village has one. Household inventory is completed by coiled baskets, grain containers, and grass mats.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The most consequential body art is scarification, and it is surgical and gendered. During *ukule*, an elder scars an initiate's abdomen and lower back by cutting and flaring the skin and rubbing ashes into the wound — a procedure understood as preparing the body for childbirth. At the rite's close the body is painted with linear designs for the public procession. The same idiom of raised keloid lines and geometric fields reappears on the cheeks, temples, and foreheads of *pwo* and *mwana pwo* masks, and on hairline treatments rendered in fibre or incision. Chiefly adornment includes carved combs and jewellery, counted in the sources among the royal arts alongside staffs and horns; divination gear doubles as adornment, with claws, beaks, and genet fur worn on the person.

## Music & performance

Chokwe performance is inseparable from masquerade. The drum is the organising instrument: masked dancing at *mukanda* graduation, at chiefly appearances, and at *hamba* cult events proceeds over drum ensembles, with song led by the assembled village. Two named performance registers structure the repertoire. The *makishi a kuhangana* are dance masks proper — owned privately, performed by their owners, and staged as entertainment; the *cihongo* (originally *cimyanji*) is the best known of these, the spirit of wealth, danced only by the chief, his son, or his nephew, and remembered as having once carried judicial force, able to accuse a spectator of a crime punishable by death. Against these stand the *makishi a ku mukanda*, whose performances belong to the initiation cycle and are not entertainment at all. *Pwo* is danced by a male masquerader whose performance transmits fertility to spectators, and whose choreography imitates a woman's bearing so closely that the mask is treated as a portrait; when the dancer dies, the mask is buried as a person would be. Exorcism ritual has its own sound: the *cimbanda* induces possession in the patient and the sequence is judged complete only at a final scream, taken as the *hamba* leaving through the mouth.

## Dance & theatre

The *makishi* masquerade is the Chokwe theatre, and its categories are strictly maintained. *Cikungu* — ancestral, enormous, winged, performed by the chief alone and only on special occasions. *Cikunza* — the grasshopper-antelope of *mukanda*, whose dramatic function is to seek out the boys due for circumcision, lead them into the bush, and guard them through seclusion. *Cihongo* — the wealth spirit, a dance mask that is simultaneously a *hamba*, and which punishes neglect with infertility in women, illness in men, or failed hunting. *Pwo* and *mwana pwo* — the female portrait masks. *Kalelwa* belongs to the same *mukanda* complex. Women's dance is concentrated at the end of *ukule*, when the initiate performs what she learned in camp in a procession through the village. Before putting on a mask, a masquerader spits into it: purification, offering, and petition to the ancestral spirit at once, and a request for protection against hostile possession.

## Festivals & rituals

The Chokwe ritual year is keyed to the two seasons rather than to a fixed calendar. Fields are cleared at the opening of the dry season; *mukanda* camps open in the dry months and can run from a few months to a full year. The two initiations are the load-bearing rites. *Mukanda*, for boys at puberty, is collective: a screened enclosure in remote bush, circumcision, seclusion under caretakers called *vilombola*, instruction in religion, sexuality, craft, and tradition, and absolute prohibition on returning to the village or approaching women and the uninitiated until graduation — after which the boys are considered reborn. *Ukule*, for girls, is individual rather than collective, with tuition aimed at a marriage that follows soon after, and it closes with scarification, body painting, procession, dance, and gift-giving. The cult of the *mahamba* runs continuously: prayers, sacrifices, and offerings to spirits represented as trees, fragments of termite mound, simplified figurines, or masks. The Chokwe separate *mahamba makulwana*, ancestral spirits embodied in paired termite mounds, from *mahamba yipwiya*, parasitic spirits that possess and sicken and are hardest to appease — the foreign ones most dangerous of all. Illness attributed to a *hamba* triggers a long treatment: induced possession, white clay rubbed over the body as medicine and as a statement of innocence, the terminal scream, then purification by throwing everything the patient touched into bush or river, and initiation into the cult. Meat from cattle, goats, pigs, and chickens is reserved for occasions like wedding feasts.

## Foodways

Chokwe subsistence is dry-season field agriculture on cleared savanna, supplemented by hunting and fishing on a seasonal rhythm. The staples are corn, cassava, and millet. Around these grow sweet potatoes, pumpkins, tomatoes, and okra, with rice, beans, and peanuts in particular districts. Cattle, goats, pigs, and chickens are kept but are not everyday food: they are slaughtered for special occasions, above all wedding feasts, which makes meat a marker of ceremony rather than of diet. Palm-wine tapping is a recurrent subject on carved chair stretchers, which places it firmly in the social repertoire. Kitchens are built directly against the houses, and storage rooms beside them. Two ritual uses of substance are worth recording as foodways-adjacent: the goat or rooster sacrificed with the *mukwale* sword at a *cikungu* rite and then hung from a pole of the *chota*, and the white clay applied as curative medicine in *hamba* exorcism.

## Oral tradition & literature

Chokwe narrative centres on the founding charter of the people themselves: **Chibinda Ilunga**, the hunter-hero who married a Lunda woman and thereby took power, is the single most frequently sculpted figure in the corpus, and the story he carries explains the Chokwe's descent from one of the twelve clans of the Lunda Empire and their eventual overthrow of it. Chokwe, Lunda, Luvale/Lwena, Luchazi, Ovimbundu, and Mbunda chiefly lines all trace back to the sixteenth-century Lunda migrations, and that shared genealogy is transmitted orally. Animal narrative is a second body of tradition, encoded in the symbolic system: the pangolin is *nkaka*, the same word as grandfather; aardvark and pangolin are held to be in contact with underworld ancestors because they live underground; dogs howling in the night are warning sleepers about beings humans cannot see; baboons are favoured because they live in families like people. Objects themselves function as mnemonic devices — the *ngombo* basket's figures are a fixed lexicon read aloud in divination. A published corpus exists as *Contes tshokwé d'Angola* (Karthala, 1992), and the Mofeko project maintains the largest online Chokwe-language resource.

## Language & religion

Chokwe (*Kichokwe*, *Tshokwe*) is a Benue-Congo language of the Niger-Congo family, mutually intelligible in part with neighbouring Lwena/Luvale and Luchazi. Speakers are additionally fluent in Portuguese in Angola, French in the DRC, and English in Zambia. Religion is ancestor-centred: the high god is *Kalunga* or *Nzambi*, and where chiefs exist they are his representative, titled *mwanangana*, "overseer of the land." Carved objects and handicrafts are understood as points of contact between the living, the ancestors, and Kalunga, and are housed in shrines called *kachipango*. Mass conversion to Christianity during the colonial period produced syncretism rather than replacement: spirit ritual, the *mahamba* cults, and the *mukanda*/*ukule* rites of passage all continue. Village courts of male elders still adjudicate land, family disputes, theft, and cases where witchcraft is suspected, below the chiefs' and district courts.
