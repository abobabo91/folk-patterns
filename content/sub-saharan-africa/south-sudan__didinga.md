## Overview

The Didinga, also spelled DiDinga, are a Surmic ethnic group living in the Didinga Mountains region of Budi County, Eastern Equatoria State, South Sudan. Their settlements include valleys, plateaus, slopes and adjacent plains. Neighbouring peoples named in the source include the Toposa, Turkana, Boya, Ketebo, Logir, Ik, Dodos and Dongotona, with whom the Didinga have experienced frequent conflicts connected with economic pressures. Their language belongs to the southwest branch of the Surmic languages. Didinga society has combined cattle herding with farming, education and a continuing attachment to cattle ownership. Their material and intangible culture includes scattered clan homesteads, age-grades, cattle-based foodways, rainmaking rituals, songs, poems and ancestor worship.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Didinga settlements consist of scattered homesteads, with each clan grouped together. Their homes are round and have cone-shaped roofs. During certain seasons and during grazing periods, people also live in rustic camps.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Wikipedia account mentions ancient metal workings traceable through old slag-heaps at Latome, Lotyathe and other parts of the present Didinga country.

## Music & performance

Driberg published a small collection of Didinga songs and poems. The available material therefore identifies songs and poems as part of the documented cultural record.

## Festivals & rituals

Didinga traditional beliefs and religious practices include a tribal rainmaker who performs rituals intended to bring rain. Didinga also worship and sacrifice to spirits and gods, and place great importance on the worship of dead ancestors. These practices are the only explicitly documented rituals in the supplied sources. No annual festival calendar, named festival, ritual date, wedding ceremony, funeral practice or birth rite is described.

The organization of boys into *Nyekerehet* age-grades is an important aspect of Didinga society. Every three to five years, boys around eight years old are placed together to form a new age-grade. They work and play together until they are married.

## Foodways

Cattle are central to Didinga livelihood and foodways. Cattle provide milk, which is consumed daily and made into butter. Didinga also consume fresh blood drawn from the necks of cattle with miniature arrows. Cattle are acquired through the exchange of grain or beer, or with money, and remain important both as wealth and as a food resource.

The Didinga do not fish because eating fish is taboo in their culture. The sources also state that farming is important to contemporary Didinga life. No named beverage, tea or coffee tradition, ceremonial food or feast is documented beyond the references to milk, butter, blood, grain and beer.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Didinga have a strong tradition concerning their arrival in their present habitat from the southeast, travelling through country then inhabited by Dodoth around the southern shore of Lake Rudolph. The date of this migration is described as entirely unknown. Driberg’s 1922 study recorded this tradition, and he published a small collection of Didinga songs and poems.

The supplied sources also preserve an account of the historical relationship among the Didinga, Murle, Tennet and Boya. They are described as having once formed one group during migration to the Didinga Hills. A hunting-party dispute caused the Murle to leave, and a later famine caused the Boya to withdraw, while their language remained the same. No named Didinga epic, folktale cycle, proverb collection, riddle tradition or contemporary literary movement is documented.

## Language & religion

Didinga is a Surmic language spoken by the Chukudum and Lowudo peoples of the Didinga Hills of South Sudan. It is classified as part of the southwest branch of the Surmic languages, and its nearest relative is Longarim. The Didinga language is also referred to as ’Di’dinga, while the related language shared by the Didinga, Boya, Tennet, Murle and Mursi is often called the Murle-Didinga language. A group in southwest Ethiopia also speaks this language.

The New Testament in Didinga was dedicated in March 2018. Traditional religious practices include a tribal rainmaker, rituals for bringing rain, worship and sacrifice to spirits and gods, and the worship of dead ancestors.
