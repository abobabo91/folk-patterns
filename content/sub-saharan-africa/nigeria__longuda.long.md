## Overview

The Longuda, also called Lunguda, are a West African ethnic group living in Adamawa and Gombe States in northeastern Nigeria, with some communities in parts of Borno. They settle mainly in and around the hills of the volcanic Lunguda Plateau and in Guyuk, Adamawa State, as well as Guyuk Local Government Area and Balanga Local Government Area of Gombe State. The language, Lunguda (*Nʋngʋra*), is a Niger–Congo language. The Longuda language has been described as a distinct branch within the Adamawa family and later as a branch of the Bambukic languages. The Longuda are notable in the sources for matrilineal social organisation, dialect diversity, marriage rites, Guinea Corn cultivation, and pottery and other objects recorded in the British Museum.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not document an annual Longuda festival calendar, seasonal festivals, funerals, initiation ceremonies, or religious rituals. They do describe marriage rites. The Longuda are primarily polygamous, and different dialects perform marriage rites differently. Traditionally, a young man courting a woman invited his friends on the night he wished to take her as his bride, without her prior knowledge. The man and his friends abducted the woman to his cottage, often by force. After she spent a night in his cottage, his family and hers considered them married, and the bride price was usually paid afterwards. The other young men in her neighborhood attempted to rescue her, and a fight followed; the intending groom and his companions usually had to win the duel. In recent times, Christianity and cultural assimilation among neighboring societies have altered the practice. A widely used version involves the woman’s consent, an arranged night, the groom’s friends and bride’s friends taking her and her clothing from her parents’ house, and a night spent in the groom’s uncle’s house. A token is left at the place where the couple used to sit during courtship, sealing the union.

## Foodways

Traditionally, the staple crop of the Longuda is Guinea Corn, identified in the source as *Sorghum bicolor*. It was formerly ground on a stone hand mill and cooked into a thick paste called *tuwo*, which was eaten with vegetable soup. Rice, maize, and millet also form part of the staple foods today, while Guinea Corn remains the dominant crop grown by the Longuda. The sources used do not document ceremonial foods, beverages, dietary rules, food taboos, or further cooking traditions.

## Language & religion

Lunguda, also called *Nʋngʋra*, is a Niger–Congo language spoken in Nigeria. The language is spoken mainly in and around the hills of the volcanic Lunguda Plateau in Adamawa State. The sources identify Guyuk and Wala Lunguda, *Nʋngʋra(ma)* of Cerii and Banjiram, *Longura(ma)* of Thaarʋ and Koola, *Nʋngʋra(ma)* of Gwaanda and Nyuwar, and *Nʋngʋra(ma)* of Deele and Jessu within the Longuda dialect cluster. Partly because of word-taboo customs, Longuda dialects have considerable lexical diversity. The sources state that Christianity has influenced marriage practice, but they do not provide a fuller account of Longuda religion, religious institutions, sacred practices, or historical scripts.
