## Overview

The Yaka are an African ethnic group living in southwest Democratic Republic of the Congo, near the Angola border and in the forest and savanna region between the Kwango River and the Wamba River. They number about 300,000 and speak the Yaka language. The Yaka are related to the Suku people and have a matrilineal society that also includes patrilineal lineage as family name. Their villages are led by chiefs, while the paramount chief, or *kiambv*, governs through nobles and village authorities. Yaka cultural life includes farming, hunting, fishing, initiation, divination, ancestor reverence, mask performance, sculpture, basketry, wooden vessels, drums, whistles, and other tools and objects.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Raffia composite masks** — Yaka masks in the museum records combine wood, cane, raffia and raffia cloth. They may have painted faces, patterned caps, projections, cloth strips, geometric designs, or raffia fringes.

**Nkanda masks** — The *nkanda* initiation ceremony uses masks worn by initiates and teachers. The masks may include *mondo*, *ndemba*, *kapita* and *mbala*. They are worn atop the head and are judged for color, creativity and execution.

**Bweni** — The *bweni* is a woven raffia headpiece worn by earth chiefs as a sign of authority. It commonly has between one and three crests running from the front to the back of the head and is worn day and night as a communal charm.

**Tsala** — The *tsala* is a beaded hat worn by nobles. Its upper part forms a cone decorated with a tuft of feathers. *Tsala zi kanaangi* have white feathers and signify a higher rank than *tsala zi nduwa*, which have red feathers.

**Bweni and headrest ensemble** — Because *bweni* were also worn as charms while sleeping, they required a headrest called *musaw* or *m-baambu*. The headrests were carved from one piece of wood and could be decorated with a leopard, antelope, human figure or house with a pitched roof.

**Ceremonial mask coverings** — Museum masks include caps and conical cloth “hats” on fibre frames, sometimes with four projections, painted geometric designs and raffia fibre attached around the rim.

**Raffia cloth and fibre objects** — The catalogue records raffia cloth, vegetable fibre, string, palm leaf and woven fibre in masks, hats, wigs, baskets, purses, load-carriers and other objects.

**Motif vocabulary.** Leopard, antelope, buffalo, human head, human figure, house with a pitched roof, quadruped with pointed snout, crests, projections, geometric designs, upturned nose.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe ceremonial and authoritative head coverings rather than a complete account of everyday clothing. Paramount chiefly authority was signified by coiled rings around the ankle and arm containing fibres and a sacrificial tendon wrapped in copper, called the *kazekedi* or *lukano*. Nobles wore *tsala*, beaded hats with conical tops and feathers. Earth chiefs wore woven raffia *bweni*. Museum records include a chief’s hat made of vegetable fibre, a wig or cap made of fibre, and a male figure with a crested hat or coiffure. The sources also record masks made from wood, cane, raffia and raffia cloth, sometimes with painted faces, caps, cloth strips and raffia fringes. They do not describe ordinary garments, footwear, or a separate everyday dress system for men and women.

### Architecture

The sources do not provide a general description of Yaka houses, villages as architectural forms, roof construction, workshops, or settlement plans. They mention villages governed by chiefs and record headrests carved with imagery of a house with a pitched roof. Many graves are constructed in dwellings or placed in enclosures where *n-saanda* and *n-yoombu* trees are planted.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Yaka material culture includes carved wooden objects, tools, vessels and household goods. Museum records include drinking cups, wooden double cups, vessels with two spouts, boxes, baskets, winnowing trays, fish traps, combs, headrests, load-carriers, pipes, slit drums and whistles. Metal appears in swords, spear points, adze fittings, brass studs, brass nails, copper wire, iron wire and tin-plating. A museum figure has a receptacle for medicine and a glass mirror, while another has a block of fetish material set into the stomach. A *mukoku* is a carved slit drum used in ritual and divination contexts.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources describe authority objects and protective adornment rather than a complete jewelry tradition. Paramount chiefs wore coiled rings around their ankles and arms, made with fibres and a sacrificial tendon wrapped in copper. Noble *tsala* hats used beads and feathers, with white and red feathers indicating rank. Museum figures include beads, metal fittings, cloth bags, cords and pendants. Yaka headrests and other objects could contain or carry charms intended to protect their owners from wicked influences. The sources do not describe tattoos, henna, footwear, or hair practices beyond crested hats and coiffures.

## Music & performance

Yaka musical instruments include drums, slit drums, whistles and miniature flutes. The carved slit drum called *mukoku* is regionally famous and is used in ritual dances. Diviners use *mukoku* slit drums topped with carved heads to provide rhythm for chants. The drums may have compartments used for mixing and serving medicines, may be struck with a baton attached by a cord, and may also be laid horizontally as stools.

Before a hunt, men are signaled several days in advance by a whistle or miniature flute called *kasengo-sengo*. The hunt leader carries an elongated whistle called a *yimbila* to call and signal the hunters. It has a side extension with a hole for altering its pitch, and its lower end is carved as a human head. A more spherical whistle with a similar but shorter side extension is called a *ndemba*. Museum records include ivory and wooden whistles, some surmounted by human heads, as well as friction drums and numerous slit gongs. The sources do not identify named song genres.

## Dance & theatre

Yaka masks are used by dancers and performers during ceremonies and rituals and are believed to communicate with the spirit world. Their sculptures called *mbwoolo* and their carved slit drum called *mukoku* are used in ritual dances. Masks are also central to *nkanda*, the initiation ceremony for young men after circumcision. During the ceremony, the initiates, called *tundansi*, wear masks including *mondo*, *ndemba*, *kapita* and *mbala*. The leading *mbala* mask is designed by the sculptor and wearer to amuse spectators. Masks may have bulging eyes, an upturned nose, an open mouth and exposed teeth, with wooden projections or elaborate headpieces. The sources do not describe a separate theatrical tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do not describe an annual festival calendar or festivals with fixed dates. They do describe *nkanda*, a coming-of-age ceremony for young men. Yaka boys are circumcised at puberty rather than at birth. After circumcision, they become *tundansi* and leave the village for one to three years, during which they learn skills and are taught how to become proper men. They are separated from other villagers and forbidden from seeing women.

The initiation ends when the teachers, called *tulombusi*, and the elders judge the initiates ready. Usually five participants appear, with pairs wearing similar masks and a leader wearing the *kapita*. The *mbala* is worn by the leader of the *tulombusi*. The masks are inspected and judged for color, creativity and execution. The sources also describe ancestor offerings, hunting rites, divination, funerary grave practices and ceremonies connected with reconciliation between Kongo and Lunda groups. No annual festival, wedding rite or birth ceremony is described.

## Foodways

The Yaka farm cassava, sweet potatoes and corn as staple foods and supplement these with fish and game meat. Hunting is also a social and masculine institution. Commonly pursued animals include duiker, antelope, buffalo and wild boar. Yaka hunters use bows and arrows, rifles, lances, snares, traps, long nets and dogs.

Before a group hunt, hunters send palm wine and kola nuts to the earth chief for approval and as offerings to the ancestors. Animals are divided according to hunting rules: the first arrow determines ownership, while owners of other arrows and the dog that tracked the animal also receive portions. The land chief and political chief receive the remaining portions. Skins, teeth or tusks from animals capable of killing humans are given as tribute to the paramount chief. Portions are also divided among dependents and lineage headmen.

The sources mention palm wine, dried meat, mushrooms, fish, game meat, cassava, sweet potatoes, corn and kola nuts. They do not name prepared dishes, cooking methods, tea or coffee traditions, or dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources do not describe Yaka epic poetry, folktales, proverbs, riddles, written literature or contemporary literary preservation efforts. They do describe chants used in divination. During a divination session, the *ngoombu* chants about possible curses, while relatives may repeat the ends of the chants. The diviner later reveals the curse believed responsible for an affliction.

The sources also preserve historical narratives about the establishment of the Yaka kingdom, the movement of Lunda groups westward, relations between Kongo and Lunda communities, resistance to colonial administration, and the later role of the Bayaka in independence movements. These accounts include King Kabamba, the Kwango region, and the political organization of the kingdom.

## Language & religion

The Yaka speak the Yaka language. The language source identifies several languages using the name Yaka, including Yaka language of Congo–Angola, Yaka language of Kivu, Yaka language of Lékoumou, Yaka language of Ubangi, Yaka language of Cameroon, and Yaka as a dialect of West Teke language. It does not provide a language-family classification or historical writing system.

Yaka religious and ritual life includes ancestor reverence, divination, healing, protective charms, initiation and beliefs concerning witches. The *ngoombu* diviner investigates strange phenomena, misfortune, illness and untimely death. *Mbwoolo* statuettes assist with interpreting dreams, communicating with ancestors, healing and protection. The Cleveland Museum describes Yaka figures called *Biteki* as containers for magical ingredients that can become *minkisi*, or power objects; one example is associated with *Phuungu* and protection against witches. The sources do not identify a wider religious affiliation.
