## Overview

The Balanta are an ethnic group found in Guinea-Bissau, Guinea, Senegal, Cape Verde and The Gambia. They are the second largest ethnic group of Guinea-Bissau, representing around a quarter of its population, and mostly reside in the southern and central regions of the country. They are also settled south of the Geba River and in central Guinea-Bissau. Balanta is a group of two closely related Bak languages of West Africa, spoken by the Balanta people. The group is divided into six dialects: Nyacra, Ganja, Naga, Patch, Sofar and Kentohe, with Balanta Kentohe identified as the largest. Their social organisation, initiation rites, farming, music and material culture distinguish them within the region.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe ceremonial clothing associated with the Fanado initiation. A man shown on the back of Guinea-Bissau’s 50-peso note wears the costume of young Balanta men during the “Fanado” or male circumcision ceremony. The costume includes a tortoise shell that is hollowed out and worn on the back. After completing the initiation, an initiate wears a bright red hat to show that he has become *Lante Ndang*, meaning “brave and wise.” Museum catalogue records include a man’s hat with two horns made of wood and palm leaf, and men’s arm-bands made of vegetable fibre and wood saplings.

### Architecture

Balanta houses are normally built based on clans.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum catalogue records include pottery and earthenware objects: water-pots, bowls, pots, and a pot for water or palm wine. One water-pot has a decorated neck. Other recorded objects include a palm-leaf funnel for palm wine, a basket made of vegetable fibre, a wooden and palm-leaf hat, and arm-bands made of vegetable fibre or wood saplings. The Balanta also use the *kebinde*, an agricultural tool used to prepare soil for planting crops, usually rice.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources record men’s arm-bands made of vegetable fibre and wood saplings.

## Music & performance

The Balanta play a gourd lute called a *kusunde*. Its short string is at the bottom rather than the top; the top string is of middle length, while the middle string is the longest, although it is capoed by the middle-length string. The instrument’s tones are described as top string open F#, top string stopped G#, middle string open C#, middle string stopped D#, and bottom drone string A#/B. The Balanta *kusunde* is similar to the Jola *akonting*. The Balanta also play the balafon, which is composed of 24 layers instead of the 12 or 14 found in the Mandingue community.

Music accompanies community celebrations and rites. After the harvest, the Balanta celebrate the *Kussundé*, during which non-initiated men compete in dances. The first day of the Fanado ceremony is a village festival involving eating, drinking and dancing.

## Dance & theatre

The sources describe a Balanta wedding dance presented by the Sunugal Ballet from the village of Mini-Ndame. When the prince comes of age to marry, the kingdom holds a large party to which families bring their daughters. Young women compete through beauty and virtuosic dancing for the prince’s hand, after which he chooses his bride and a celebration follows. A Balanta man must steal a cow to prove his courage to his future wife.

The dance uses a large spirit mask laden with cowry shells, an old woman mask, a young girl mask and a young man mask. These masks represent family and spiritual connection and are used when the community gathers for music and dance.

## Festivals & rituals

The Balanta have initiation rites at different stages of life. From early childhood to age 15, a child belongs to the category of *Nwatch*. Around age 18 to 20, the individual enters the *Fuur*, and around age 25 enters the *Nghaye*. Around age 30, the rites of *Kgness* authorise a man to take a woman.

The most important initiation is *Fanado*. It takes place once every four years and lasts two months in the “sacred woods.” A man may be chosen by his maternal uncle after becoming a landowner and taking on family responsibilities. The first day is a general village festival with food, drink and dancing. Initiates are presented by their maternal uncles before being taken into a secret place in a tropical forest. There they are circumcised, build a hut and undergo tests of endurance while learning how to behave with others, manage a family and live as adults. After two months, the place is burnt and kept secret. Survivors return as mature men, and the most valiant emerge to songs of bravery. The following day, the initiate may serve on the council of elders that manages village life.

The sources also describe marriage, death and cattle sacrifice as important life contexts. Cattle sacrifice is associated with initiation, marriage and death. No annual festival calendar beyond the harvest celebration called *Kussundé* is documented.

## Foodways

The Balanta are farmers, fishermen and herders. They grow millet, rice, peanuts, cashews and fruit for cash crops. Cashew culture is described as a specialty, and the Balanta derive an apple wine called *Cadjou* from the cashew. The export of cashew nuts to India is an important economic force in Balanta villages.

The sources record pottery vessels for water and palm wine, including a pot for water or palm wine and a palm-leaf funnel for palm wine. Cattle are raised as a traditional activity, and their sacrifice is important at initiation, marriage and death. The Balanta use intricate irrigation systems that mix salt and fresh water to maintain soil nutrients and achieve good yields.

## Oral tradition & literature

Balanta oral tradition says that the people migrated westward from the region of present-day Egypt, Sudan and Ethiopia to escape conflict. Another tradition says that the Balanta originated in Sudan. The Balanta claim that the region between the Rios Mansôa and Geba, called *Nhacra*, is their homeland and say that they migrated there from somewhere in the east “in times long past.”

## Language & religion

Balanta is a group of two closely related Bak languages: Balanta-Kentohe and Balanta-Ganja. Balanta-Kentohe is spoken in Guinea-Bissau and The Gambia, while Balanta-Ganja is spoken in Senegal. Balanta-Kentohe is divided into the Kəntɔhɛ dialect in the north and the Fora dialect in the south. Balanta-Ganja has the Fganja and Fjaalib dialects. Balanta has case prefixes and suffixes, and Senegal’s Decree No. 2005-979 provides an orthography for the language.

The Balanta practice indigenous spiritual customs and rites. God is believed to be far away, and communication with the Almighty is established through spiritual practices and traditions. Islam and Catholic Christianity are also practiced; Islam is more prevalent than Christianity and is often practiced alongside Balanta spirit worship. *Djon Cago* is worshipped in Balanta spirituality, and adherents seek what they worship through necromancy and sacrifice.
