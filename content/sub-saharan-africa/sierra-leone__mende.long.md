## Overview

The Mende are one of the two largest ethnic groups in Sierra Leone, constituting 31.2% of the total population. They are predominantly found in the Southern Province and the Eastern Province, with significant populations in Bo, Kenema, Kailahun, and Moyamba. The Mende are mostly farmers and hunters. Their language, Mende, belongs to the Mande language family and is also used as a regional lingua franca in southern Sierra Leone; speakers are found in neighboring Liberia and Guinea. Mende cultural life includes rice cultivation, village-based labor, secret societies, masked performances, wood carving, woven fabrics, body adornment, oral literature, and the Kikakui syllabary. The sources describe both male Poro and female Sande institutions as central to socialization, leadership, ritual knowledge, and community life.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Mende woven fabrics** — The Mende produce woven fabrics that are popular throughout western Africa. Museum records include cotton cloth, display cloth, blankets, and hammocks made from hand-spun or machine-spun cotton, sometimes combined with synthetic fibres. Designs include indigo, white, red, brown, blue, green, cream, and buff areas, supplementary weft patterns, geometric bands, diamonds, lozenges, and fringes.

**Kpoikpoi** — *Kpoikpoi* is a hammock made from hand-spun cotton strips that are hand-woven and hand-sewn together. Museum examples use coloured cotton, including red and two shades of indigo, with supplementary weft patterns and tapestry-weave areas. Other examples have wooden poles carved with birds’ heads.

**Country cloth** — A woven cotton “country cloth” is made from panels stitched together. One museum example has nested borders in natural undyed cotton, dark brown, and sand-coloured dyed cotton, with a repeated hair-comb motif and geometric designs framing a central panel.

**Display cloth** — A *kpoikpoi* display cloth may be composed of ten strips of hand-woven and hand-spun cotton sewn together along the selvedges. Museum records describe white cotton crossed by indigo stripes and supplementary weft floats forming a diamond pattern.

**Mende loom weaving** — Museum records identify looms made from wood, reed, cotton, coarse string, cane, and cardboard, with partially woven or unwoven cotton fibre present. Shuttle forms include boat-shaped wooden shuttles with carved designs or cross-hatching on the bow and stern.

**Motif vocabulary.** Hair-comb motif, geometric design, nested borders, lozenges, diamonds, concentric diamonds, wine-glass shape, birds’ heads.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe Mende clothing mainly through museum records and photographs. A shirt made of vegetable fibre is formed from knotted openwork and dyed with yellow-orange ochre, with ruffs at the neck, waist, and wrists. The records also show cloth garments, tunics, trousers, loincloths, and traditional fabrics. In photographs, women are described as draped in plain cloth and wearing wrapped head-dresses, while men wear tunics and trousers or are draped in cloth. Some people wear European clothing alongside traditional clothing and personal adornments.

Ceremonial clothing is associated with Sande performances. Women wearing masks cover their bodies with masses of raffia or black cloth and must not expose any part of the body. The museum catalogue records fibre head-dresses, woven basketry domes, painted cotton coverings, feather coronas, coral beads, clay-painted surfaces, and fibre fringes. The sources do not document Mende footwear, named everyday garments, or specific belts.

### Architecture

The Mende traditionally live in villages of 70 to 250 residents, situated from 1.5 to 5 kilometers apart. Museum photographs show circular thatched-roof buildings, single-storey buildings with thatched roofs and wooden shutters, and buildings with extended thatched roofs held up by wooden supports. One photograph shows a white-washed building with a thatched roof and wooden shutters. The sources do not provide a vernacular name for these buildings, detailed construction methods, or information about urban architectural traditions.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Mende material culture includes wooden tools, weapons, game equipment, spinning and weaving implements, vessels, and musical or ritual objects. Museum records describe a wooden Leopard society axe with a flat metal blade and curved spikes, an iron spear with a central geometric design, and an iron sword with a curved blade and wooden hilt. Other objects include wooden staffs, combs, palettes, fans, spindles, spindle-whorls, looms, shuttles, mancala boards, and a composite palm-wine vessel made from a calabash with a wooden neck. One mancala board contains 49 white bean counters. The sources do not document Mende ceramics.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Mende jewelry includes gold and silver necklaces, bracelets, armlets, and earrings. Women often wear multiple bells, while Mende hunters often wear a single bell that can be silenced when stealth is necessary. Museum records include a pair of chief’s armlets made of elephant hide inlaid with wire, cowrie-shell rattles, glass beads, coral beads, and a charm wrapped in cloth and vegetable fibre string.

Hair is an important sign of femininity. Thickness and length are admired, and a woman’s hair should be clean, oiled, controlled, and arranged in intricate styles. Loose hair is associated with mourning, while dirty or disheveled hair is associated with insanity and neglect of community standards. Mende masks represent hairstyles, scarification, and neck rings. The neck rings signify health, prosperity, wholesome living, and the concentric ripples associated with the Sowo spirit.

## Music & performance

The sources describe music through songs, drums, rattles, bells, and performance within Poro and Sande institutions. In the 1930s, Lorenzo Dow Turner found a Gullah family in coastal Georgia that had preserved an ancient song in the Mende language, “A waka,” passing it down for 200 years. In the 1990s, Joseph Opala, Cynthia Schmidt, and Tazieff Koroma located a Mende village in Sierra Leone where the same song was still sung. The story is documented in the film *The Language You Cry In*.

Museum records include circumcision drums carved from wood, with skin or hide stretched over the opening or openings and held with rope, leather, or hide strings. They also include rattles made from gourds, cotton string, cowrie shells, seeds, bone, nut-shells, and colored glass beads. Women’s rattles are specifically identified in several records. Necklaces with bells are associated with communication between the worlds of ancestors and the living; women’s multiple bells are linked to community because they ring harmoniously together.

Poro and Sande performances use masks, dancing, and ritual knowledge. The sources do not identify named instrumental ensembles, song genres, wedding music, or court music.

## Dance & theatre

Mende masked performance is closely associated with Sande and Poro. Sande groups conduct masked performances embodying the Sande guardian spirit, associated with water and rivers. The masks represent the collective mind and spirit of the Mende community and express ideals of female morality and physical beauty. They are worn by women, who cover their bodies with raffia or black cloth.

The principal Sande spirit for celebration is *Ndoli jowei*. During initiation, she appears publicly at several events. One event, *yaya gbegbi*, occurs 1–3 days after initiates have been taken into the bush. At this time she comes into town with Sande women, but does not dance because it is not yet a celebration. At the minor feast called *Kpete gbula yombo le* or *Sowo mba yili gbi*, an announcement is made about the date of the *gani* celebration. The new initiates then return to town accompanied by *Ndoli jowei*, and dances are performed by maskers and initiates.

Girls undergo demanding dance training under the direction of *Ndoli jowei*, the dance instructor. *Gonde* is also a dance instructor and provides comic relief, friendship, and encouragement.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do not describe an annual festival calendar. They do document initiation rituals associated with Sande and Poro. All Mende women begin initiation into Sande when they reach puberty. Sande teaches responsibilities of adulthood, hardworking behavior, modesty, and proper relations with elders. The society is also described as a guardian, protector, and guide through life.

A key element of Sande initiation is clitoridectomy, or female genital mutilation. The sources state that the procedure is intended to transform children considered to be of neutral sex into heterosexual, gendered adults and to remove the female’s residue of maleness. Initiates are marked with white *Hojo* clay, showing that they are under the protection and control of Sande. Sande law and punishment apply to objects and people marked with the clay.

Poro is the male equivalent of Sande. Boys are initiated into manhood and trained in knowledge considered essential to community survival. The initiation period lasts seven years. Young men aged 20 are called into the society and trained by the group above them and by elders. Poro prepares men for leadership, responsibility, wisdom, and power. Its rituals include masked dancing, secret language, passwords, work, training, and service.

The sources also mention masks at funerals, arbitrations, and the installation of chiefs. The sources do not document marriage ceremonies, birth rites, annual harvest festivals, or religious calendar dates.

## Foodways

Rice is the principal crop named in the sources. Mende farmers are generally known as growers of rice and several other crops, practicing crop rotation to protect soil productivity. Coffee, cocoa, and ginger are grown as cash crops, while rice, pepper, groundnuts, beniseed, also known as sesame seeds, and palm oil are grown for local consumption. Rice cooperatives have been formed in some rural areas.

Traditional farming is organized through labor groups that move from farm to farm. Men clear land for planting rice, while women clean and pound rice, fish, and weed planted crops. This routine continues during ten months of every year, with a couple of months around the New Year allowing more time in the village for domestic pursuits such as house building.

The museum records include a palm-wine vessel made from a calabash with a wooden neck. The sources do not provide named dishes, recipes, cooking methods, dietary rules, tea or coffee rituals, or ceremonial foods.

## Oral tradition & literature

Mende oral tradition describes the people as a mixture of two groups. The original members were hunters and fishers living in small peaceful settlements, while later leaders brought the arts of war and built larger, more permanent villages. These leaders were almost certainly Mane people who descended into the coastal lowlands from the area near Moussadou, Guinea, in the 16th century. They conquered and mixed with the native Bullom people to form the Mende.

Mende oral literature includes stories, songs, and proverbs. F.W.H. Migeod’s 1908 publication *The Mende Language* contains 17 Mende stories with facing-text English translations and 13 Mende songs. Ralph Eberl-Elber published two Mende tales with English translations in 1935. Marion Dusser de Barenne Kilson collected Mende stories in Sierra Leone and published *Royal Antelope and Spider: West African Mende Tales* in 1976, containing 100 Mende folktales in Mende and English. M. Mary Senior published “Some Mεnde Proverbs” in 1947.

## Language & religion

Mende is a major language of Sierra Leone, with speakers in neighboring Liberia and Guinea. It belongs to the Mande language family and is a tonal language commonly described as having five tones: high, low, rising, falling, and rising-falling. It is also spoken by other ethnic groups as a regional lingua franca in southern Sierra Leone.

Kisimi Kamara invented the Kikakui syllabary in 1921. The script achieved widespread use for a time but has largely been replaced by an alphabet based on the Latin script. The Mende Bible was translated and published in 1959 in Latin script. A different account in the Mende people article dates the invention of the script to 1917 and identifies Muhamad Turay as its inventor and Kisimi Kamara as the person who popularized it.

The sources describe Sande and Poro as secret societies with moral, social, ritual, and political functions. Sande is associated with women, water, rivers, white clay, spiritual protection, justice, love, and harmony. Poro is associated with men, initiation, leadership, responsibility, and secret knowledge. The sources do not describe a broader religious affiliation or denomination.
