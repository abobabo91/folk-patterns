## Overview

The Gurage are a Semitic-speaking Habesha ethnic group inhabiting Ethiopia. They live principally in the Gurage Zone and East Gurage Zone, a fertile, semi-mountainous region in Central Ethiopia Regional State about 125 kilometers southwest of Addis Ababa. Their region borders the Awash River to the north, the Gibe River, a tributary of the Omo River, to the southwest, and Hora-Dambal to the east. Substantial numbers also live in Addis Ababa, Oromia Region, Harari Region, and Dire Dawa. According to the 1994 Ethiopian census, self-identifying Gurage comprised about 2.7% of Ethiopia’s population, or about 1.4 million people. Their folk-cultural profile is especially documented through ensete cultivation, foodways, ritual uses, language diversity, oral traditions, and urban labor and commerce.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do record that, during the nineteenth century, Gurage slaves traveling to Shewa were often nearly naked after Oromo ambushes and that Negus Sahle Selassie gave them new clothes.

### Architecture

They do mention clay pots hung from the walls of Gurage huts, where households kept spiced butter aging.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do describe clay pots used for aging spiced butter and deep pits between rows of ensete plants, where the edible substance extracted from the plant’s root was fermented.

## Festivals & rituals

They do, however, describe ritual and ceremonial uses of ensete. Ensete fronds are used to wrap a corpse after death, and ensete fiber is used to tie off the umbilical cord after birth. Meat is usually eaten when an animal is sacrificed during a ritual or ceremonial event. Ensete also participates in everyday social interactions and can be exchanged as recompense for services rendered.

The sources describe a Gurage proverb concerning butter and illness, and state that different species of ensete are eaten to alleviate illness. They also report that the Gurage regard overeating as coarse and vulgar. It is considered poor etiquette to eat all the ensete passed by a host, and polite to leave at least some ensete bread even after a very small portion has been served.

## Foodways

The principal Gurage crop is ensete, also known as enset, *Ensete edulis*, *äsät*, or “false banana plant.” Its massive stem grows underground, and ensete is involved in daily social life, ritual practice, nutrition, and practical work. It is used for wrapping goods and fireproofing thatch, as well as for wrapping corpses and tying umbilical cords. Gurage households retain large surpluses as protection against famine.

A typical Gurage diet consists primarily of *kocho*, a thick bread made from ensete, supplemented by cabbage, cheese, butter, and grains. To prepare ensete, the Gurage pound its root to extract the edible substance and place it in deep pits between rows of ensete plants in the field. Fermentation makes it more palatable, and the material can be stored for up to several years. Some Gurage also plant teff and eat *injera*, which the sources state they also call injera.

Cash crops include coffee and khat, while livestock is raised mainly for milk and fertilizer. The Gurage raise zebu, primarily for butter. A typical household has a large quantity of spiced butter aging in clay pots hung from the walls of its huts. Butter is believed to be medicinal and is taken internally or used as a lotion or poultice. Meat is not eaten regularly, but is usually consumed when an animal is sacrificed during a ritual or ceremonial event.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used document Gurage oral traditions and proverbs. Oral traditions state that Emperor Amda Seyon established a military colony in northern Gurage, called Aymellel, with soldiers from Akele Guzai led by Azmach Sebhat. The sources also describe historical claims preserved in ancestral lineages, including accounts concerning the Wolane and soldiers of Imam Ahmad al-Ghazi, and several Kistane Gurage clans said to have descended from Amharas and Tigrayans.

A Gurage proverb states: “A sickness that has the upper hand over butter is destined for death.” The sources list Wolf Leslau’s *Gurage folklore: Ethiopian folktales, proverbs, beliefs, and riddles* as further reading. They also cite works by Phillip Lebel on oral tradition and chronicles concerning Guragé immigration, and Worku Nida’s ethno-historical survey.

## Language & religion

The Gurage speak multiple Ethiopian Semitic languages, collectively called Gurage languages, within the Semitic branch of the Afroasiatic language family. Earlier they were treated as dialects of a single Gurage language, but the sources distinguish Northern, Eastern, and Western groups. Western Gurage includes Sebat Bet, with the dialects Inor, Ezha, Muher, Geta, Gumer, Endegegn, and Chaha, as well as Masqan; Mesmes is extinct. Northern Gurage consists of the dialects of Soddo, which might be related to the extinct Gafat. Eastern Gurage includes Silte, Wolane, and Zay, also called Zway.

Gurage languages are strongly influenced by surrounding Cushitic languages and are written from left to right using a system based on the Geʽez script. According to the 1994 census, the largest groups reported in Gurage Zone included Sebat Bet Gurage, Silt’e, Soddo Gurage, Mareqo or Libido, Amhara, and Kebena. The majority of inhabitants were reported as Muslim; 51.97% practised Ethiopian Orthodox Christianity, 15.9% were Protestants, and 1.95% Catholic.
