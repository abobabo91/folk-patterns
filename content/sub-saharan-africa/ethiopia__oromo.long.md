## Overview

The Oromo (Oromoo) are a Cushitic-speaking people of the Ethiopian highlands and the Rift Valley plains, numbering roughly 40 million — about 35.8% of Ethiopia's population and the third most populous ethnic group in Africa — with a further 650,000 in the Kenyan counties of Marsabit, Isiolo and Tana River. Their homeland, Oromia, wraps around central Ethiopia from the Gibe and Didessa valleys in the west through the Shewan plateau and the Bale and Arsi massifs to the semi-arid Borana rangelands on the Kenyan border. They speak *Afaan Oromoo*, the most widely spoken Cushitic language and the fourth-most spoken language in Africa. In folk-culture terms the Oromo matter above all for *Gadaa*, the eight-year generational republic that ordered politics, ritual and craft patronage alike, and for a pastoral material world — milk vessels, horn, hide, beadwork — that survives alongside the highland agricultural traditions the Oromo absorbed after the sixteenth-century expansions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Oromo weaving** — Highland Oromo weavers in Wollega, Shewa and Arsi work cotton on the ground-pit treadle loom, producing the heavy white shawl cloth of the Ethiopian plateau. The distinguishing element is the *tibeb*, the woven-in coloured warp-stripe border, which on Oromo pieces tends toward broad bands of red, green and black rather than the fine multicoloured registers of Amhara and Gurage work.

**Bulluko and gabi** — The *bulluko* is a thick double-layer cotton mantle worn by highland Oromo men in the cold Arsi and Bale uplands; the lighter *gabi* is its everyday equivalent. Both are plain-woven in undyed cotton with the coloured border as the only ornament.

**Oromo leatherwork** — Among the Borana, Guji and Arsi, hide rather than cloth is the primary body covering and container material. Women's *qolloo* skirts and capes of goat or sheepskin are scraped, smoked, softened with butter, and then covered in dense fields of cowrie shells, glass seed beads and metal studs — the pattern is applied, not woven, which sharply distinguishes Oromo dress from neighbouring Amhara textile traditions.

**Oromo basketry** — Coiled grass and *sisal*-fibre baskets, typically lidded and conical, with geometric bands produced by alternating dyed and undyed coil-wrapping. Related to the wider Harari and Somali coiled tradition but with a squatter profile and heavier use of red and black.

**Oromo shield** — The *gaachana*, a round or slightly oval shield of buffalo or hippopotamus hide, boiled and moulded over a form, with concentric raised bosses and radiating ribs tooled into the surface. Carried with the spear by mounted warriors, and the emblem of the *Gadaa* grade holding military office.

**Oromo horo and milk vessels** — *Okolee* and *gorfoo* milk containers of carved wood or woven fibre sealed with butter and smoked with olive-wood, their necks bound in leather thongs and rings of cowrie. Borana camel-milk vessels are the largest and most elaborately banded.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Cowrie* (fertility and wealth, densest at the waist and shoulders); *concentric boss* (shield centre, authority); *radiating rib* (shield surface); *warp-stripe band* (tibeb border, red-green-black); *chevron coil* (basketry, alternating dyed weft-wrap); *triangle field* (leatherwork bead panels); *bokkuu* (the ridged wooden sceptre form, echoed in carving); *odaa* (sycamore fig, the Gadaa assembly tree); *lozenge chain* (metal stud lines on hide capes).

### Clothing & dress

Highland Oromo men wear cotton trousers and tunic under the *bulluko* or *gabi*, and historically the *kalaacha*, a phallic ivory or metal frontlet strapped to the forehead, marked a man who had attained ritual seniority. Nineteenth-century travellers noted the small braided hairstyle worn loose around the head, and the lion's-mane cape and headband awarded for killing large game or an enemy in battle.

Pastoral women among the Borana, Guji and Arsi wear the *qolloo*, a leather skirt and shoulder cape, together with the *callee* — the beaded ornament complex — and the married woman's *siiqqee*, a slender ritual stick of *harooressa* wood carried as the token of female authority and never surrendered. Married Arsi and Borana women dress the hair with butter and ochre in tight braids, and cover it with a black or indigo cloth. Sandals are cut from rawhide. A bride's costume is distinguished chiefly by the density of cowrie and metal and by a fresh application of butter to skin and hair.

### Architecture

The characteristic Oromo dwelling is the round, single-roomed house with mud-and-wattle walls and a conical thatched roof of grass tied to a radiating pole frame, the whole raised on a levelled earth platform with a low internal partition separating the family sleeping space from the calf pen. Borana and Guji pastoralists build a lighter, portable version — a dome of bent saplings covered with grass mats and hides, dismantled and loaded on camels when the herds move.

The most distinctive Oromo built forms are not houses. The *odaa*, a great sycamore fig, is the assembly ground of the *Gadaa* council; the ground beneath it is cleared and ringed with seating stones, and specific trees — Odaa Nabee, Odaa Bultum, Odaa Bisil — are named constitutional sites rather than mere landmarks. Borana territory is organised around the *tula*, nine clusters of deep hand-cut "singing wells" descended by a human chain of men who pass leather buckets upward while singing, a hydraulic and choreographic structure at once. Grave markers in Konso-adjacent and Arsi country include stelae and carved wooden posts set over the burials of prominent men.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Oromo pottery is a woman's craft, hand-built by coiling without the wheel and open-fired: black burnished coffee pots and roasting pans, wide-mouthed water jars, and the tripod bread pan. Woodwork is the male counterpart and carries the highest-status objects: the *bokkuu*, a ridged wooden or metal sceptre held by the *Abbaa Gadaa* and passed to his widow at his death, and the *Oromo headrest* (*boraatii*), a small carved single-piece stool-headrest of hardwood used by herders to sleep without disturbing a buttered hairstyle. Milk and honey containers are carved, or woven and waterproofed with butter. Iron spear blades, knives and the curved *billaawaa* were forged by smiths who, as in much of the Horn, formed an endogamous occupational group.

### Jewelry & body adornment

*Callee* is the general term for the beaded ornament sets that mark a woman's status: strung glass beads, cowries, and amber worn in collars and headbands, with specific configurations reserved for married women and for the mothers of firstborn sons. Silver and copper alloy is worked into heavy neck rings, spiral armlets, ankle bangles and the crescent pendants worn in Wollo and Arsi; the Muslim Oromo of Wollo and Hararghe also wear cylindrical amulet cases holding written Qur'anic texts. Men's ivory or brass *kalaacha* is the emblem of ritual rank. Scarification in small dotted or linear patterns on cheeks and temples, butter dressing of the hair, and henna among Muslim Oromo women complete the repertoire.

## Music & performance

Oromo music is built on a pentatonic system shared with the wider Ethiopian highlands but with its own repertoire and instruments. The *krar*, a five- or six-string bowl lyre, and the *masinqo*, a single-string spike fiddle played with a bow, accompany sung poetry; the *washint*, an end-blown wooden flute, is the herder's instrument. Drums range from the large double-headed *dibbee* used in ritual processions to hand drums accompanying dance.

Song genres are tied tightly to function. *Geerarsa* is the defiant solo boasting-song of men — originally the hunter's and warrior's chant, it became in the twentieth century the principal vehicle for political protest poetry. *Weedduu* covers the broad category of sung verse, including work songs sung at the Borana singing wells, where the rhythm of the song coordinates the chain of men lifting water. *Faaruu* songs accompany cattle herding and milking. Women's *helee* and lullabies form a separate repertoire, and the *ateetee* fertility rite is carried almost entirely by women's collective singing.

The modern Oromo music industry, developed from the 1960s and 1970s around the Mecha and Tulama cultural revival and later in the diaspora, put the *krar* and *masinqo* into electrified band arrangements; singers such as Ali Birra became the central figures of a nationally recognised popular tradition sung in Afaan Oromoo.

## Dance & theatre

Oromo dance is regionally differentiated and largely non-theatrical: there is no indigenous masked or puppet drama. The best-known form is the **shoulder dance** shared across the Ethiopian highlands, in which the torso and shoulder blades move in rapid isolation while the feet remain nearly still; the Oromo variants in Arsi and Wollo are performed by facing lines of men and women, with women additionally whipping the head and butter-dressed braids in circular sweeps — the *sirba* of Wollo is the most virtuosic of these.

Borana and Guji dance is jumping-based rather than shoulder-based, performed in a tight circle of men who leap vertically in turn while the ring claps and sings, close in form to the dances of neighbouring Kenyan pastoralists. Warriors' dances performed with *gaachana* shield and spear reenact raiding and are staged at *Gadaa* power-transfer ceremonies. The *ateetee* and *shanan* women's rites involve processional dance with the *siiqqee* stick held aloft.

## Festivals & rituals

**Irreecha** is the principal Oromo festival, a thanksgiving held at the end of the rainy season in late September or early October at lakeshores and river confluences — most famously Lake Hora Arsadi at Bishoftu — where participants in white dress carry fresh grass and yellow *adey abeba* flowers to the water. A second Irreecha is celebrated on mountain summits (*Irreecha Tulluu*) in spring. The Oromo new year falls in the same season under the *Gadaa* lunisolar calendar, a lunar-stellar system with a named day for each day of the month and no seven-day week.

The **Gadaa power transfer** is the great political ritual: every eight years the outgoing *Abbaa Gadaa* hands the *bokkuu* to his successor at the *odaa* assembly ground, with the Borana ceremony at Me'ee Bokkoo drawing tens of thousands. The *Gumii Gaayoo*, the Borana general assembly, meets every eight years at Gaayoo to review and proclaim law.

**Ateetee** is a women's fertility and grievance rite in which women, carrying the *siiqqee*, assemble to sing against a man who has wronged a woman — a sanctioned mechanism of female legal authority parallel to the male *Gadaa*. Life-cycle rites include naming ceremonies, the passage through the five eight-year *Gadaa* grades for men, marriage by negotiated bridewealth in cattle, and mortuary rites with animal slaughter and, for prominent men, a marked grave. Muslim Oromo observe Ramadan and Eid alongside these; Christian Oromo keep Meskel and Timkat.

## Foodways

The pastoral core of Oromo cuisine is dairy and meat: *baduu*, the curd left after milk is soured and strained, *itto*, a stew of lamb with tomato, potato, ginger and garlic, *ukkaamssa* (*affaanyii*), ground beef stewed with onion, garlic, green chilli and butter, and *foon akaawwii*, seasoned minced roast meat. *Waaddii* is meat grilled outdoors over wood or hot beads. Pork is absent, being taboo for both the Orthodox and Muslim majority.

Grain dishes anchor the highland west. *Anchotte*, a starchy tuber served with spiced butter, and *qoocco* are Wollega specialities; *chechebsaa* is torn flatbread stir-fried with chilli and cheese; *maarqaa* and *affeellama* are porridges of wheat, barley or maize with honey and milk; *qince* is the coarse-ground version of the same; *laaffisoo* is *buddeenaa* bread soaked in stew. *Chororsaa*, a mixture of cheese, butter and yoghurt spread on *chumboo* black-teff bread, is wedding and festival food in Wollega. *Chukkoo* (*micira*) is whole grain sweetened and seasoned with spiced butter; *qorso* (*akaayii*) is roasted barley eaten as a snack.

Coffee is indigenous to Oromo territory and central to hospitality: *buna* is served in the roast-grind-brew ceremony, and *buna qalaa* — whole beans fried in butter — is a ritual offering in its own right. *Hojjaa* is brewed from coffee husks with milk, *quxxii* from the leaves. Fermented drinks are *dadhii*, honey mead, and *farsho*, a barley beer.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oromo verbal art is unwritten and enormous in scope: *mammaaksa* (proverbs), *hibboo* (riddles), *durdurii* (folktales), and the historical-genealogical recitations that preserve the sequence of *Gadaa* office-holders across centuries — a chronology that functions as the Borana historical record. *Geerarsa* boasting-poetry and *weedduu* sung verse carry narrative as much as music. Blessing formulae (*eebba*) delivered by elders open every assembly and ritual. The *Qaalluu*, the hereditary ritual officiants of *Waaqeffannaa*, are the custodians of the cosmological narratives about *Waaqa* and *ayyaana*.

Written Oromo literature begins late and against obstruction. Sheikh Bakri Sapalo devised an original syllabic **Sapalo script** for the language in the 1950s; from 1991 Oromo has been written in *Qubee*, a Latin orthography, and is now a language of primary education and federal websites. The philosopher Claude Sumner's multi-volume *Oromo Wisdom Literature* remains the largest published corpus of the proverbs, riddles and songs.

## Language & religion

*Afaan Oromoo* belongs to the Lowland East Cushitic branch of Afroasiatic, with major dialect clusters corresponding to the Borana–Arsi–Guji south, the Mecha west, the Tulama centre, the Hararghe east and Wollo in the north. It is written in *Qubee* Latin script and is an official working language of Oromia, Harari and Dire Dawa.

Roughly half the Oromo are Sunni Muslim, concentrated in Wollo, Hararghe and near the Somali border; about a third are Ethiopian Orthodox; the remainder are Protestant, following nineteenth- and twentieth-century missionary activity, or adhere to *Waaqeffannaa*. This indigenous monotheism centres on *Waaqa*, the sky-god, and *ayyaana*, the spirit-quality that mediates between *Waaqa* and creation, served by the hereditary *Qaalluu* priesthood whose assembly is one of the three organs of *Gadaa*. Waaqeffannaa practice — above all Irreecha — is now widely observed by Christian and Muslim Oromo as a cultural rather than confessional act.
