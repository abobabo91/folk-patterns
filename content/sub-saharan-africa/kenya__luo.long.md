## Overview

The Luo are a Western Nilotic people of the eastern Lake Victoria basin — *Nam Lolwe* — in the former Nyanza province of western Kenya and the Mara Region of northwestern Tanzania, a tropical, equator-straddling country lying between roughly 3,700 and 6,000 feet above sea level. At 5,066,966 people (2019 Kenyan census, about 10.6% of the national total) they are Kenya's fourth-largest ethnic group, and part of a wider set of Luo peoples spread from South Sudan and southwestern Ethiopia through Uganda into Kenya and Tanzania. They speak *Dholuo*, of the Western Nilotic branch of the Nilotic languages within Nilo-Saharan. Their traditional economy combined cattle pastoralism, seed farming and fishing, supplemented by hunting — a triple base that shows directly in the surviving material record of pots, hoes, fish-country basketry, cattle gear and horn and ivory ornament. They are also the originators of the popular music genres *benga* and *ohangla*.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here — the Wikipedia articles on the Luo and the British Museum catalogue records for the objects shown in this gallery — describe no loom-woven cloth tradition. Pattern in the documented Luo corpus is carried instead by looped and knotted string, plaited leather, imported glass beads, cowrie shell, and impressed or incised surface work on clay, wood and hide. The pattern-bearing object classes the records actually name are these:

**String garment** — a black garment of cords looped and knotted onto a string base and finished with a fringe, catalogued as the dress of a married woman; two examples are held.

**Plaited-leather waist apron** — a plaited leather apron closed with a fringe of red, white and dark blue glass beads, again in more than one example, so that the colour triad is a repeated rather than an incidental choice.

**Cowrie-shell surface work** — cowries are applied as a dense covering rather than as scattered accents: a leather head-dress decorated with cowries, a conical pale-hide cap whose two long ear-pendants are covered entirely with cowrie shell, a leather head ornament filled and covered with cowries, and the sheath of an arm-dagger decorated with cowries and tassels.

**Bead-inlaid woodwork** — a large circular four-legged wooden stool inlaid with glass beads set into geometric decoration.

**Rouletted and cross-hatched earthenware** — a globular two-lugged pot bears rouletted ornament; a tobacco-pipe bowl of black pottery carries a punched band at the rim, three raised vertical bands and two zones of incised cross-hatching.

**Embossed hide** — a composite hide shield retains black fur and an embossed pattern, its handle formed as part of the wooden spine.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Rouletting* (rolled impressions on a globular pot body), *punched rim bands*, *raised vertical bands*, *incised cross-hatching*, *embossing* on hide, and *geometric* bead inlay and bead fringing in red, white and dark blue.

### Clothing & dress

The record divides sharply between everyday cloth and ceremonial or status headgear. In the British Museum's field photographs from Kenya, women potters sit working in front of shelters wearing cloths and necklaces; another portrait shows women in dress, shirt and skirt, one carrying a basket on her head, and men in cloth with wrist ornaments. Distinctively Luo garments in the collection are the black knotted *string garment* with a looped fringe, catalogued specifically as the dress worn by a married woman, and the plaited-leather waist apron with its red, white and dark blue bead fringe. Headgear is where the collection is richest: a basketwork hat of plaited grass whose exterior is plastered with clay and set with plumes of cock's feathers; a second basketwork form with a central boss and upturned rim, daubed with mud or dung; a conical pale-hide cap covered with cowrie shell and trimmed with white feathers and down; a hide head-dress decorated with feathers; a composite head-dress of leather with a zebra-mane crest, conus and cowrie ornament, and buttons; and a leather-and-wood skull cap with chin straps to which two lengthwise-split horns are tied, recorded as warrior's wear. A portrait of two men shows feather head-dresses with animal-tusk decoration worn with arm-rings and wrist ornaments.

### Architecture

Luo building is documented here as a defensive tradition of the savanna. Conflict and raiding in the crowded Lake Victoria basin produced stone-walled enclosures, the best known being **Thimlich Ohinga** in South Nyanza. Neville Chittick, director of the British Institute of History and Archaeology in East Africa, first argued that the site predated the arrival of Luo speakers; that argument is weakly supported, since most of the stone walling dates to within the period of Luo expansion. Luo speakers maintained Thimlich Ohinga and went on building stone-walled fortresses — *ohingni* — and defensive earthworks known as *gunda bur* in both northern and southern Nyanza, the earthworks curving around the living area they enclosed. Some enclosures held several hundred houses, and archaeological and ethnographic analysis shows their internal spatial organisation most closely resembles the layout of the traditional Luo homestead; ceramic analysis confirms continuity between the earliest occupants and Luo speakers. Under colonial administration the enclosures were gradually vacated, families moving out to individual homesteads fenced with euphorbia instead of stone, and by the mid-twentieth century all were abandoned. The field photographs show low open-fronted shelters used as working and sitting space.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery is the best-documented Luo craft and the photographs record it as women's work: women seated on the ground before a shelter, hand-building pots or turning them on what the cataloguer reads as a tournette, with a bowl of water, a lump of clay on a flat container, pottery equipment and unfinished vessels around them, one woman smoothing an upturned pot with a stick, children watching from inside the shelter. The vessels held are earthenware bowls — one with a flat base — and pots, including the globular two-lugged pot with rouletted ornament. Ironwork appears as tools and weapons with wooden fittings: a hoe with separate iron blade and wooden handle, a knife, and an arm-dagger of iron and wood with a cowrie-and-tassel sheath. Woodwork includes a club with a fluted head, a pestle, the bead-inlaid stool, and a carved wooden cowbell hung on a fibre cord. Plant-fibre work covers a cane basket, a grass decoy quail trap, and a basketwork dish lined with cow dung. A horn snuff-box and a tobacco pipe with a bone-and-iron tamping implement tied to the stem round out the household kit, alongside a cattle trapping of tusk or horn, leather-bound and pierced for suspension, carried by the lead bull of a herd.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ornament in this collection is worked from animal material — ivory, tusk, horn, shell and snake bone — rather than metal. Armlets are made of solid ivory, or from two boar tusks bound together with leather thongs. A crescentic forehead ornament is cut from a section of hippopotamus tusk and pierced twice at the centre. A composite neck ornament combines tusk and wood with a leather cap attached to a necklace strung from snake vertebrae. Cowrie shell serves both as ornament in its own right and as a dense facing for leather head ornaments, one of which is filled and covered with cowries and fitted with two small curved antelope horns and recorded as warrior's wear. Conus shell appears with cowrie on the zebra-mane head-dress. As for the body itself, the sources record one permanent modification: the Luo of Kenya and Tanzania are also called *Jonagi* or *Onagi* — singular *Jaonagi* — meaning a person whose six lower teeth have been removed as a puberty rite of passage.

## Music & performance

The Luo are credited as originators of several popular music genres, among them *benga*, described as one of Africa's most popular genres, and *ohangla*. The instruments in this gallery are all from the British Museum's Kenyan Luo material and show a chordophone-and-drum ensemble built from whatever was at hand. A five-string *lyre* has its resonator made from a green enamel bowl closed with a hide membrane, the strings passing over a bridge improvised from a blackened cotton reel — a good illustration of how quickly imported manufactures were absorbed into instrument making. Alongside it are a bamboo *zither* finished with vegetable fibre and dung, and a *musical bow* of wood with a gourd resonator, here broken. Percussion is represented by a composite drum: a wooden body covered at both ends with brown-haired skin, the two heads lashed together. A wooden cattle-bell on a fibre cord belongs to the herding soundscape rather than to performance proper. The simplest item in the set is a musical pipe cut from a stalk of maize, an instrument made from the harvest itself and, by implication, made fresh and discarded.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used for this profile record no Luo dance or dramatic tradition, either named or described.

## Festivals & rituals

There is no annual festival calendar in these sources, and Kenya has no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions to draw on. What the sources do document is a coming-of-age rite and two ritual complexes attached to it. The removal of the six lower teeth at puberty was definitive enough to name the people: *Jonagi* / *Onagi*, "one whose lower teeth have been removed". The same practice became an instrument of cultural incorporation during the Luo expansion into Nyanza. Local Bantu men who wished to marry Luo women were told, in words the sources quote directly, "If you want to marry us you have to stop circumcising and file your bottom teeth, so that you look like us and we can marry you" — and the Bantu groups of the basin, the Suba among them, ceased circumcising as they took on Luo custom. Bridewealth was part of the same calculation: Suba and other Bantu men sought Luo brides because Luo bridewealth was much lower or absent altogether. Ritual authority also operated at the political level. Luo spiritual leaders advised active cooperation with the British after the punitive expeditions of 1896. In 1913 Onyango Dunde of central Kavirondo announced that he had been sent by *Mumbo*, the serpent goddess of Lake Victoria, to spread her teaching; **Mumboism** pledged to drive out the colonisers, condemned their religion, spread among both Luo and Kisii, and was suppressed by deportation and imprisonment through the 1920s and 1930s.

## Foodways

The sources describe the Luo subsistence base but name no dishes. The traditional economy was mixed: cattle pastoralism, seed cultivation and fishing, supplemented by hunting — the same combination attributed to the ancestral Nilotic populations of Bahr el-Ghazal and Eastern Equatoria, and visible as far back as the Kadero site 48 kilometres north of Khartoum, dated to 3000 BC, which preserves cattle pastoralism together with animal domestication, seed cultivation and evidence of fish consumption. In the Lake Victoria homeland the same three legs persist in modern form, with tenant fishing and small-scale farming among the trades named for the Luo today. Bantu-speaking migrants who reached western Kenya by 1000 BC had brought iron-forging and new farming techniques into the region, making the Great Lakes one of Africa's main population centres and earliest iron-smelting zones. The object record carries the food economy without labelling any preparation: a wooden pestle for pounding, earthenware pots and flat-based bowls for cooking and serving, a basketwork dish lined with cow dung to make it hold its contents, a grass decoy quail trap for small game, and an iron-bladed hoe for the seed fields. Maize is present too, obliquely — the musical pipe is cut from a maize stalk. Tobacco and snuff, taken in a horn box and in a pottery-bowled pipe with its own iron tamper, are the only consumption practices the catalogue documents in any detail.

## Oral tradition & literature

Luo oral history is precise enough to have been used as chronology. Oral tradition and genealogical evidence together date four waves of migration into Nyanza: the People of Jok (*Joka Jok*), the first and largest, beginning around 1490–1517 and settling at Ramogi Hill; the People of Owiny (*Jok'Owiny*) and the People of Omolo (*Jok'Omolo*) soon after, in 1598–1625; then a mixed group of Suba, Sakwa, Asembo, Uyoma and Kano. Descent is encoded in the group's own names: *Joramogi* or *Nyikwaramogi*, "Ramogi's heirs", and the prefix *Jo-*, "people of", which builds the names of the 27 traditional Kenya Luo tribes and their clans and sub-clans. The sources also note a bias in the corpus: oral histories dwell on battles rather than on the negotiations, peaceful accommodations and opportunistic identity-switching that were no less real as the Luo moved south. Dholuo was first reduced to writing by Arthur Carscallen, a Canadian Seventh-day Adventist, who produced a grammar textbook and began translating the Bible; a Dholuo print culture followed, including Achieng Oneko's vernacular newspaper *Ramogi*, published by Oginga Odinga's Luo Thrift and Trading Corporation.

## Language & religion

*Dholuo* belongs to the Western Nilotic branch of the Nilotic family, within Nilo-Saharan, and its measured lexical similarity to neighbouring Luo languages traces the migration route: 93% with Dhopadhola, 90% with Lep Alur, 83% with Lep Achol, 74% with Anuak, and 69% with Jurchol (Luwo) and Dhi-Pari. A separate Luo script was invented around 2021 to write the Luo languages. Religiously, the documented landscape is Christian with a nativist strand running through it. Seventh-day Adventist missionaries were among the earliest to work among the Luo — Arthur Carscallen with Peter Nyambo of Nyasaland, the first mission opened in November 1906 at Gendia Hill, Kendu Bay with the help of the German missionary Abraham C. Enns, and the first Luo Adventist converts baptised on 21 May 1911 — followed by stations at Wire Hill, Rusinga Island, Kanyadoto, Karung, Kisii and Kamagambo. Catholic and Anglican Church Mission Society work concentrated on northern Nyanza. Against this, Mumboism's serpent goddess of Lake Victoria supplied an explicitly anti-colonial cosmology in the 1910s–1930s.
