## Overview

The Mano are a forest people of Liberia and Guinea who speak a tonal Mande language. They live in Nimba County, named after Mount Nimba where they once worshipped the creator god Wala. They are known as carvers, musicians, and farmers, and maintain the poro and sande as traditional institutions for men and women.

## Material culture

Mano material culture centers on carved wood, woven textiles, and brass metalwork, often combining multiple materials.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Mano worked textiles and fibers with specific structural techniques, using raphia, indigo, and grass.

- **Plaited grass mat** — A mat with double-strand twill across the field and three-strand twill border, making the edge structurally distinct.
- **Strip-textile apron** — Aprons worn by carved figures made from narrow patterned colored cloth bands assembled together rather than cut from one piece.
- **Raphia and indigo dress** — Fibers used to clothe objects as well as people, such as fringing pottery vessels and adorning masks.

### Clothing & dress

Carved figures show strip-textile aprons at the waist, and masks reveal elaborate headdress costumes combining fur, feathers, and cloth.

- **Patterned strip apron** — Waist covering made from assembled colored textile strips, worn by both male and female figures.
- **Composite mask headdress** — Full head-and-shoulder costume combining yellowish fur, feathers, raphia, hair, and woven cloth rather than a face piece alone.

### Architecture

- **Ganta United Mission** — Founded by American missionary Dr. George Way Harley, it grew into schools, hospital, and colleges including the Winifred J. Harley School of Nursing.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Mano craft includes pottery vessels adorned with fiber, carved wood objects, and brass and iron metalwork.

- **Composite pottery vessel** — A ceramic pot finished with a fringe of raphia and indigo cotton.
- **Penannular brass bracelet** — A hoop made of three strands of two-ply wire coiled around a core and finished with knob terminals.
- **Mancala board** — Wooden game board with two rows of six holes plus one hole at each end for captured pieces.
- **Carved wooden figures** — Standing male and female figures, often wearing aprons and jewelry.
- **Janus heads** — Carved wooden heads with two faces, each mounted on a small stand.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Mano adornment includes brass and composite bracelets, beadwork, and metal studs and bone details on masks.

- **Brass penannular bracelet** — Three-strand two-ply wire wrapped around a core with knob terminals at the open ends.
- **Composite bracelet** — A greenish bracelet covered in string and set with a single cowrie shell.
- **Beaded necklace and bracelet** — Worn by the carved female figure on the left wrist and around the neck.
- **Metal studs on masks** — White metal studs set across the forehead of composite masks.
- **Bone teeth on masks** — Two bone teeth carved onto miniature masks with forward-sloping foreheads and closed eyes.

## Music & performance

The Mano are known as skilled musicians, though specific instruments and songs are not named in available sources.

- **Miniature masks** — A long series of small carved masks, most with bands across the forehead, some with incised details and bone teeth.
- **Full-scale composite mask** — A mask with metal studs on the forehead and a headdress of fur, feathers, raphia, and cloth.

## Dance & theatre

No Mano dance or theatrical traditions are documented in available sources.

## Festivals & rituals

The Mano maintained the poro men's school and sande women's school as traditional institutions, and formerly worshipped Wala on Mount Nimba.

- **Poro** — A traditional school for men.
- **Sande** — A traditional school for women.
- **Wala worship** (*Wala*) — The creator god worshipped on Mount Nimba, later replaced by Methodist and Catholic Christianity.

## Foodways

The Mano are farmers, though specific crops, dishes, or foods are not documented.

## Oral tradition & literature

Mano origin narratives trace migration from Sudan through the Mali Empire to present-day Liberia in the thirteenth to seventeenth centuries.

- **Migration narrative** — Recorded by tribal leader John Gbatu and his grandson Stanley Delano Quaye, tracing movement from Sudan to Mali to Guinea to Liberia.
- **Soumano/Danhou Maghan clan** (*Soumano / Danhou Maghan*) — A clan of the Gbara remembered in Mali as helping defend the empire against North African invaders.

## Language & religion

Mano is a heavily tonal Mande language written in Latin orthography; worship of Wala has largely given way to Methodist and Catholic Christianity.

- **Mano language** (*Mano*) — A tonal Mande language also recorded as Mah, Mah-Yacouba, and Mawe, with nine register and contour tones.
- **Gospel of John** — Published in Mano Latin orthography with tone marks, opening with 'Kɛɛ pɛ séĩ é tĩã kɛɛ̀ ɓea, Wééa e ke ɓe'.

## Glossary

- *Mano* — The people and their language; also called Mano-Dan and Yacouba
- *Wala* — The creator god, also recorded as Ya'ala and Nyamba
- *Poro* — Traditional school for men
- *Sande* — Traditional school for women
- *Mah* — Alternative name for the Mano language
- *Mah-Yacouba* — Alternative name for the Mano language
- *Mawe* — Alternative name for the Mano language
- *Yacouba* — Alternative name for the Mano people
- *Mano-Dan* — Alternative name for the Mano people
- *Soumano / Danhou Maghan* — Clan of the Gbara in the Mali Empire
- *Gbara* — Empire group; the Mano and Dan were remembered as clans of the Gbara
- *Mande* — Language family of which Mano is a member
- *Nimba County* — Region in northeastern Liberia where the Mano live
- *Penannular* — Describes a bracelet or ring open at one end
- *Raphia* — Fiber used for weaving and adorning objects
- *Janus head* — Carved wooden head with two faces
