## Overview

The Zigula, also called Zigua and in some sources Zigula, are a Bantu ethnic and linguistic people of Tanzania. They inhabit the southwestern Tanga Region and northern Pwani Region, with major populations in Handeni District, northern Kilindi District, Pangani District, Tanga town, northern Morogoro, and western Bagamoyo. Their historic territory includes the Pangani valley, the Ruvu River islands, and more than 4,500 square kilometres of tribal region. The population was estimated at 355,000 in 1993 and is stated as 631,000 today. The Zigula speak the Zigula language, also called Chizigua, while Mushunguli or Mushungulu is spoken by a Bantu minority in southern Somalia. Zigula culture includes matrilineal settlement organization, farming, inland and coastal trade, basketry, mat-making, woodcarving, blacksmithing, ritual healing, initiation, and ancestor veneration.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Basketry and mats** — The Zigula are renowned for weaving baskets and mats.

**Bark-fabric clothing** — During boys’ initiation, participants wear bark fabric from *moza* or *mtondoro* trees.

### Clothing & dress

Zigula dress historically differed according to sex and age. Men usually wore loincloths made from fabric, animal skins, or shredded bark. Young women wore small beaded aprons around the genitalia. During boys’ initiation, the initiates smeared themselves with white mud and dressed in bark fabric from *moza* or *mtondoro* trees. Before returning to society, they received slashes called *chanje* on their hands. In the ritual of *kwaluka*, they were shaved, anointed with castor oil, dressed in new clothes, and honoured with a dance, feast, and beer. They state that, as a result of early coastal influences, Zigula people later adopted more contemporary clothing resembling that of the Swahili.

### Architecture

Historically, Zigula people lived in fortified towns with long, alley-like entrances. Settlements ranged from small communities of around thirty huts to larger ones containing up to two hundred huts. Most settlements were protected by palisades because of repeated raids. Settlements on islands in the Ruvu River lacked such defences and were connected to the mainland by small wooden or string bridges, some large enough for herds of sheep and goats.

Matri-clan kin groupings were traditionally the focus of settlement life. They do record that small ancestor houses were built at forest crossroads to honour maternal and paternal ancestors.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Zigula made swords, clubs, arrows, bows, drums, waterpipes, melodic bows, stools, and other implements. They were expert blacksmiths and worked metal with hand-bellows. They hunted with arrow poison. Wooden mortars and pestles and grinding stones were used to process grain. During initiation, clay figures were used in teaching sexual knowledge and adult behaviour.

The sources mention a clay statue of Seuta, described as the Zigua national hero, unveiled at the offices of the native administration in 1951.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Traditionally, the front teeth of both sexes were chipped into a triangular form. Women extended their earlobes to accommodate wooden or horn discs. Young women wore small beaded aprons.

During initiation, boys received *chanje*, slashes on their hands, before re-entering society. A woman might allow a topknot called *chungi* to grow until her child had been named, although the source describes this account as unclear.

## Music & performance

The sources name drums and melodic bows among the implements made by Zigula people. Initiation involved riddles and songs. Boys received instruction through songs, riddles, and clay figures during the three to four months following circumcision. Girls likewise learned about sexual behaviour through songs, riddles, and clay figures under the guidance of a *kungwe*.

Music also formed part of initiation ceremonies. After boys completed *kwaluka*, they were honoured with a dance, feast, and beer. Girls’ initiation ended with a ceremony involving the breaking of a winnowing tray, a dance called *kisazi*, and a feast. The sources also mention ceremonial events connected with first fruits, ancestor healing, marriage, and funerals.

No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription concerns the Zigula, and the sources provide no catalogue records for musical instruments.

## Dance & theatre

The sources identify dances in life-cycle ceremonies rather than a separate theatrical tradition. Boys’ initiation concluded with *kwaluka*, followed by shaving, anointing with castor oil, new clothing, a dance, a feast, and beer. Girls’ initiation included *kisazi*, a dance performed as part of the ceremony marking readiness for marriage.

The sources also describe singing and ritual performance in *Kisasa*, an outdoor healing ceremony involving fire, killed chickens, singing, and kudu horns.

## Festivals & rituals

The Zigula celebrate the first fruits each year in an event called *Mviko wa Mlungu*. Beer and grain are poured as libations around a bush *mkongo* tree. Ancestor worship, called *kutambika mzimu* or *mviko*, is central to Zigula religious life. Ancestral spirits are appeased because the dead are believed to live in *uzimu*, the land of the spirits, and to influence the living when neglected or offended.

The sources describe *Kisasa* as an outdoor healing ritual for people believed to be afflicted by ancestor spirits. It involves fire, killed chickens, singing, and kudu horns. Participants who undergo healing may become specialists. *Upungi*, also called *ukungwi*, diagnoses illnesses attributed to ghosts occupying the living through a medicine bundle made from parts of a deceased healer; libations are made in the bush to expel the ghost.

Birth rites included confinement of mother and child indoors for usually four to six days after delivery. The mother, infant, parents, and midwife were subject to anointing with oil, and the child received a name after being taken outdoors. Boys were generally circumcised at puberty through a combined circumcision and initiation ritual. Girls were confined for many months to a year after puberty and underwent *mnkinda* under the instruction of a *kungwe*.

Marriage involved negotiations over bridewealth, the killing and roasting of a cock, and rites involving an *msasa* tree. Historically, marriages were usually uxorilocal. Funeral practices included burial or leaving the body at *Kwaluhombwa* for wild animals. After mourning, *watani* performed *mpoza*, a purification using *ukongo* and the stomach contents of a slaughtered goat or cow.

## Foodways

The Zigula are primarily farmers who also keep small herds of sheep, goats, and cattle, as well as some donkeys. Their crops include sorghum, maize, beans, yams, manioc, pumpkins, cucumbers, sesame, groundnuts, tobacco, Indian hemp, castor, and coconuts. Bananas are uncommon. During droughts, manioc, beans, maize, and sorghum are the main crops.

Maize is harvested in late October or early November, while sorghum is harvested in July. Crops are usually sown in January before the major rainy season. Grain is processed with wooden mortars and pestles or grinding stones. Zigula people also hunt and fish, and raise hens at night in wicker cages.

The sources describe beer and grain as offerings during *Mviko wa Mlungu*. Oxen-based communal feasts were traditionally used to resolve conflicts. At marriage negotiations, a cock was killed, its head roasted, and consumed by the groom to increase his energy.

## Oral tradition & literature

Zigula traditions identify a northern origin and a hero named Seuta, who was known for his bow. The sources also mention “Breaking the grinding stone,” a notable fight whose exact importance is unknown and which is described as an origin mythology found among many matrilineal cultures. The Zigula, Ngulu, Bondei, and Shambala are connected in traditions concerning descent from Seuta.

Riddles, songs, and clay figures formed part of boys’ and girls’ initiation education. Oral instruction concerned sexual knowledge and appropriate adult behaviour. *Utani*, a light-hearted cooperation between neighbouring clans, maintained social relationships.

## Language & religion

Zigula or Zigua, also called Chizigua, is a Bantu language of Tanzania and Somalia. Mushunguli or Mushungulu is a dialect spoken by about 34,000 people from a Bantu ethnic minority in southern Somalia, including Jamaame, Kismayo, Mogadishu, and the Juba River valley. Mushunguli shares lexical and grammatical similarities with the language of the Zigua people of Tanzania. There is no official or traditional orthography for Mushunguli, although spelling practices from related Bantu languages can be adapted.

Zigula religious life combines ancestor veneration with belief in a supreme deity called *Chohile* or *Mlungu*. Forest spirits include *kinyamkira*, a malicious being with a single limb. The dead are called *wazimu* or *pepo* and inhabit *uzimu*. The Zigula also believe in *mgonzi*, magicians who practise sorcery, make remedies, and perform divination. Islam has surpassed Christianity, which is primarily Roman Catholic, and many Zigula are Muslims. Islamic influence appears in some magical and therapeutic practices and in the spread of Islam among the Zigula during the German period.
