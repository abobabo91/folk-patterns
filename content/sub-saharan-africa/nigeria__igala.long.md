## Overview

The Igala or Igara are a West African ethnolinguistic group native to the region immediately south of the confluence of the Niger and Benue Rivers in central Nigeria. Their homeland, Igalaland, lies in a triangular area formed by the two rivers, with Idah as its capital. Most Igala live in southern and eastern Kogi State, including Idah, Igalamela/Odolu, Ajaka, Ofu, Olamaboro, Dekina, Bassa, Ankpa, Omala, Uloshi, Ifeku island, Iyelen Lokoja, Ibaji, and Ajaokuta Local Government. Minorities live in Edo, Delta, Anambra, and Enugu states. The population of people of Igala descent is estimated at over 2 million. Igala belongs to the Benue–Congo branch of the Niger–Congo family and is spoken primarily in Kogi State. Igala folk culture is represented by farming, hunting, basketry, body decoration, masquerades, ritual performance, and objects associated with political and ceremonial life.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Igala basketry* forms an important part of material culture and reflects adaptation to the riverine and agrarian environment of the Niger–Benue confluence. Artisans weave storage containers, fishing traps, sieves, and carrying baskets from raffia palm, grasses, and flexible woody stems; the objects are used in farming, fishing, trade, and local markets.

*achi* is a woven material used in the Oloja burial ceremony. It consists of strips of raffia palm leaves, called *Iko*, and strips of cloth, and is used to adorn the body of the deceased.

The museum catalogue records a loom made of wood, cotton, and cane, with a partly completed cotton textile, a ball of warp threads, and a shuttle boat. The textile has blue, black, and orange warp, ikat-dyed mid blue warp threads, and blue weft threads.

No other textile traditions, named cloth types, embroidery systems, or documented pattern traditions are covered by the sources used.

**Motif vocabulary.** Incised geometric patterns; linear bands; spirals; geometric designs; emblematic figures of a dove and tortoises.

### Clothing & dress

The sources used do not describe everyday Igala clothing, named garments, footwear, belts, or ordinary head coverings. They do describe ceremonial materials and adornment associated with burial performance. In the Oloja masquerade, the body of the deceased is adorned with *achi*, woven from strips of raffia palm leaves, or *Iko*, and strips of cloth. The Oloja dancer wears a wooden headdress made to resemble a crocodile, with fierce teeth and a protruding jaw. Igala body decoration is called *Ina Ole*. It includes permanent scarification and non-permanent designs made with paint or dyes. In Ankpa, three deep horizontal cuts on each side of the face beside the mouth formerly served as a means of identification. Women decorate their wrists and arms as a statement of beauty, while markings on the face and chest of men can identify social or cultural meanings.

### Architecture

The sources used do not describe Igala house forms, settlement plans, roof construction, building materials, courtyards, workshops, or named architectural types. They state that Igala art appears in cultural architecture, but provide no architectural description beyond that statement.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum catalogue records a range of objects associated with the atlas display. These include a wooden throne with a tubular seat, feet pedestals, detachable lids, and a wooden plinth carved with emblematic figures of a dove and tortoises. A mirror-frame has a carved man’s head with an elaborate crest, while its rectangular lid has an iron hook handle and incised geometric decoration. A brass bell is made by lost-wax casting and has a cylindrical, slightly waisted form, a looped strap handle, linear bands, spiral decoration, and a wooden clapper attached with cotton cord. Other records include a wooden stool surmounted by a hornbill-type bird with a quadruped on its head, wooden figures, iron bracelets, copper wire, and hair-pins made of wood or copper.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The museum catalogue records iron bracelets in several forms: a pair consisting of four links, two partly wrapped with copper wire; a pair containing two pod-shaped links; and a bracelet made from a chain of iron links. It also records a wooden hair-pin shaped like a spear and a copper hair-pin whose head terminates in opposed spirals.

Igala body decoration, *Ina Ole*, includes piercing, scarification, tattooing, skin dyes, and painting for aesthetic or social identification. Scarification was performed by cutting the skin to leave raised markings. Paint and dyes created designs whose meanings depended on the person, occasion, or bodily location. The sources mention combs, rings, and the alphabet among the symbols used in decoration. No information is provided about tattoos, henna, ritual jewelry, or named amulets.

## Music & performance

Music is central to the Oloja masquerade, a five-day burial ceremony for older deceased men. The ceremony is led by elders and takes place once a year, honoring one or several deceased members of the community in the final part of the burial sequence, *Akwu eche*. The instrumentalists are elders. Their instruments include wooden gongs, metal gongs, rattles, a flute, and seven drums of various sizes.

The beginning of the ceremony is marked by the lead instrumentalist playing seven notes on the flute. Each note is louder than the preceding one, after which the other musicians join. The elders then take turns performing a dance. A further sequence of seven flute notes summons the Oloja dancer. The dancer’s movements imitate aspects of the deceased person’s occupation, personality, or habits.

The sources also state that music forms part of Igala ceremonial life, but do not identify other named musical genres, song forms, court ensembles, or instruments beyond those used in the Oloja ceremony.

## Dance & theatre

The Oloja masquerade is a named Igala performance tradition connected to the final stage of burial. The dancer wears a wooden crocodile-like headdress with fierce teeth and a projecting jaw. The performance combines music, dance, masquerade, and the destruction or removal of objects associated with the deceased.

The dancer goes to the deceased person’s house and throws part of the thatched roof into the bush or river. In the performance arena, the masquerader collects and destroys the tools of the deceased. The dancer then collects the *Okega*, described as the personal god of the deceased, takes snuff into the nostril three times, strikes the back of the head three times, and performs three final dance movements. At the conclusion, community members cry while the dancer is taken back to his abode.

Igala masks are also described as important works of art and ritual objects. They seek to balance the world of the living with the ancestral spiritual realm and can carry markings associated with political status and fashion. The sources do not describe a separate secular theatre tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe several Igala rituals connected with agriculture, ancestors, hunting, and death. The earth festival begins the farming year. It includes a communal hunt, the offering of animal remains to the earth shrine, the burning of grass, and the planting of the first yam. In another ritual, the king camps in the bush on the night before the festival and spears an animal for offering to the national earth shrine.

Before the yam harvest, the Igala honor their ancestors at the *Ibegwu* festival. The ceremony praises the ancestors for their protection during the previous year and seeks further blessings for the year ahead. The *Ibegwu* are ancestral beings connected with *Ọjọ*, the supreme creator, and are believed to protect human fertility, agriculture, and society.

Burial has three stages. *Egwu omi omi eji* is the stage when the body is placed in the grave. *Ubi eche* takes place after burial. *Akwu eche*, meaning the last shedding of tears, is the third stage and includes the Oloja masquerade. The sources give no calendar dates for these festivals.

## Foodways

The sources identify farming as a traditional Igala occupation and name the crops cultivated in the region. Rainfed crops include yam, cocoyam, maize, pumpkin, and cassava. Dry-soil crops include millet, Guinea corn, beniseed, and beans. The Igala economy also developed through trading, while hunting supplied goods and formed an important part of the cultural landscape.

The sources mention buffalo, antelope, and wild pig among the animals found in the landscape, but do not describe recipes, cooking techniques, named dishes, food taboos, beverages, or ceremonial meals. They state that the first yam is planted during the earth festival and that animal remains are offered to the earth shrine. No further foodways are covered by the sources used.

## Oral tradition & literature

Igala oral tradition includes accounts of origins, royal history, warfare, and ancestral practice. One legend says that Igalaland was discovered and founded by a hunter who found the area suitable for hunting. His camp became the original grounds of the Igala settlement, and his family continued the lineage of the area. The sources state that this is not a concrete historical account but represents the cultural importance of hunting. Some villages bear names connected with the game found there, including Oju-Ocha, meaning “the place where guinea-fowl come to wash.”

Igala lore also recounts that Oma Idoko offered his daughter, Inikpi, by burying her alive so that Igala could win a war of liberation from Jukun dominance. The sources do not describe named epics, proverbs, riddles, written literature, or a contemporary literary revival.

## Language & religion

Igala is a Yoruboid language spoken by the Igala ethnic group of Nigeria. It belongs to the Benue–Congo branch of the Niger–Congo family. Dialects named in the sources include Ankpa, Akpanya, Alọma/Ofabo, Central, Ibaji, Ogwugwu or Ogugu, Anyugba, Ebu, and Ife. The Central grouping includes Dekina, Idah, Igalamela-Odolu, and Ofu. Igala has seven oral vowels and five nasal vowels, an alphabet of 32 letters, and five tones: extra high, high, mid-high, mid, and low.

The Igala believe in a supreme creator called Ọjọ or Ojo. Their divine beings are organized into gods, ancestors, and diviners. Ancestors are called *Ibegwu* and are regarded as representatives and messengers of Ọjọ. They are honored through rituals and offerings and can provide protection or punishment. The sources do not describe Islam, Christianity, denominations, or other contemporary religious affiliations.
