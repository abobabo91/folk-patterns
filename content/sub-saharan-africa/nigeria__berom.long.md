## Overview

The Berom (also spelt Birom) are one of the largest autochthonous peoples of Plateau State in central Nigeria, concentrated on the high, undulating terrain of the Jos Plateau across four local government areas — Riyom, Jos North, Jos South and Gwol (Barkin Ladi) — with further communities in southern Kaduna State, among them Fadan Karshe, whose settlers trace their origin to the Berom district of Za'ang and who emigrated during the British colonial administration. Roughly a million people spoke Berom as of 2010, making it the most widely spoken Plateau language of the Benue–Congo branch of Niger–Congo; it is unrelated to Hausa or to the Chadic languages of the same state. In folk-culture terms the Berom matter for a festival cycle keyed tightly to farming and hunting, an unusually well-documented instrumentarium, and a naming system built out of the names of game animals.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources describe Berom cloth and fibre work only in terms of colour symbolism and of plaited or bunched plant material, not as a weaving or dyeing tradition with named techniques, so only the following can be stated.

**Green-and-maroon Berom cloth** — the cloth worn as Berom dress carries two colours that are read as emblems: green for the cactus (*ryep*) that grows in every Berom locality, and maroon for *tee*, a local powder rubbed on the forehead for protection and on the body to relieve pain.

**Shang** — a fibre buttock ornament listed among Berom dance equipment. British Museum holdings include several vegetable-fibre buttock garments and dance dresses, one of them recorded simply as a bunch of grass tied with string at the end, and photographs show young men and children from the rear wearing waist- and buttock-ornaments, one resembling a pom-pom with a bunch of sticks above it.

**Plaited grass and leaf sheaths** — ceremonial penis sheaths in the collection are made either of plaited grass or of leaves, placing plant-fibre plaiting rather than loom weaving at the centre of the documented body-covering repertoire.

**Basketry hat** — a basket-work hat collected in 1946, alongside the *bong* dance hat named in the instrument and dance-equipment lists.

The sources name no motif vocabulary for Berom cloth or basketry.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday and ceremonial dress in the sources divides along lines of sex and occasion. Women's dress is represented by the buttock garment, of vegetable fibre or of grass gathered and tied with string; photographs of Berom women show waist-ornaments with a bunch of leaves at the front. Men's and boys' dress includes penis sheaths of leaves or of plaited grass, the plaited examples specified as ceremonial. Cloth garments in the green-and-maroon Berom colours are worn with the powder *tee* rubbed on the forehead. A photograph of a group of male elders at a meeting shows them in cloths and hats with arm- and leg-ornaments, some carrying sticks; other portraits show an elderly man in a cap and a young man in a fur cap. Head coverings run from the basket-work hat to the *bong* dance hat; dance costume also includes dance skirts and the fly-whisk, *gadus* or *gawat*. The sources do not describe Berom footwear or belts.

### Architecture

The sources say almost nothing about Berom building. Only one detail appears: a photograph of elders meeting outdoors shows clay walls behind them. The Berom homeland is described as lying entirely on high ground on the Jos Plateau, undulating country whose most prominent plant is the cactus *ryep*, but no house form, roof type, compound plan or architectural ornament is documented in the sources used here.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Berom metalwork in the museum record is weaponry and it is composite in construction. **Swords** are forged iron with wooden bosses on hilts wrapped in snake skin, or with leather-wrapped hilts carrying two pairs of horn-like processes, the lower pair turning downward, and a single line of decoration along the blade. Their **sheaths** are rawhide, one ornamented on a single face with four brass discs. A **knife** has a leather-bound hilt and a triangular pommel and sits in a wooden, leather-bound sheath. **Spears** socket an iron head — plain or barbed — into a wooden or cane shaft; one shaft carries pokerwork ornament bound with a metal strip. A **shield** combines hide, wood and animal hair. The sources do not describe Berom pottery.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Brass is the one metal named for Berom ornament: the collection holds a brass ear-ring. The rest of the documented adornment comes from photographs. A Berom woman wears wrist-, neck-, ear- and nose-ornaments with her hair shaved into a circular style. A group of women and a child wear disc-shaped lip-plugs and ear-plugs and bowl-shaped hairstyles, and two of them are dressing the hair of the other two with small pointed tools — hairdressing as a recorded social act. A young man wears a nose-ring and neck-ornaments of chains and beads, with scarification at the corner of the mouth. Another young woman wears a neck-ornament of beads and metal discs. Male elders wear arm- and leg-ornaments. The powder *tee* is applied to the forehead for protection and to the body against pain.

## Music & performance

The Berom instrumentarium is the best-documented part of their material culture, catalogued by Roger Blench (2021) and partly present in museum collections. Idiophones include the xylophone, *kundung* or *yom bi tok*, made of cattle horns and cobwebs; a vessel rattle; ankle rattles made from the leaves of *Borassus aethiopum*; and the scraper or guiro, *gwák* or *gwàshák*, cut from dry *Euphorbia kamerunica* (*yěp*) and played by sliding a stick across the sawn body. Membranophones run from the barrel-drum *biŋ* and the gourd-drum *bing shi* to the hourglass drum *kalangu* and the conical drum *rwey biŋ* or *bing gwom*, which is reserved for royalty. Chordophones are the raft-zither *yom kwo*, the arched harp *yom waya*, and the trough-lute *yom shi*; other accounts name *yom nshi*, a two-string banjo with calabash and skin resonators, and *yom*, a straw string instrument. Aerophones include flutes, the single-note whistle ensemble *ju*, the fingerhole notch-flute *ju shelo*, the transverse horn *bwo nyama*, an end-blown horn and an end-blown trumpet. The British Museum holds a reed zither and a second zither built of broad and narrow canes laid in alternation and laced at the ends into a sounding board, braced at the back against five crossing strips, with plucked split canes over two bridges — thicker canes sounding the deeper notes, the outer canes the highest — and a wooden whistle, hollow at one end with a leather loop through an eyelet at the other. Christianity has added a body of Berom Christian song that circulates widely on the Plateau.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name no individual Berom dances, but they identify dance as the core display of the festival calendar and they inventory what dancers wear and carry. The week of **Nzem Berom** is when cultural displays from across Berom land are exhibited, especially in music, dance, arts and culture. Dance equipment consists of the fibre buttock ornament *shang*, the fly-whisk *gadus* or *gawat*, the dance hat *bong*, and dance skirts; the museum holds vegetable-fibre dance dresses described as buttock ornaments for ceremonial dances, and plaited grass penis sheaths likewise marked ceremonial. The conical drum *rwey biŋ* is restricted to royal use, which separates court performance from village celebration. No dramatic or masked theatre tradition is described in the sources used here.

## Festivals & rituals

Berom festivals are organised around agriculture and hunting, the two activities the sources place at the centre of Berom livelihood and cosmology, and the agricultural ones rotate between villages. The calendar runs: **Behwol** in February/March, a hunting festival for prosperity in which the Che, Boze, Anaguta and Izere peoples also take part; **Mandyɛng**, **Badù** and **Nshok** in March/April, harvest festivals; **Worong cun** in April/May, a planting festival held after good rains; **Tyǐ** in August, a red-ochre collection festival; **Búná** or **Vwana** in August, the fonio harvest festival; and **Mado** in October/November, a hunting festival for prosperity in which the Che also join.

**Mandyeng** ushers in the rainy season and was, with the closely similar **Nshok**, regarded as the festival that secured a good farming and hunting season. Not every Berom community holds them: those that perform Mandyeng claim roots at Riyom, among them Vwang, Kuru, Za'ang, Gyel, Rim, Bachit, Bangai, Lwa, Sop, Jol, Wereng Kwi, Gwo, Kakuruk, Kuzeng, Kurak, Kuchin, Rahos and Tahoss. Nshok differs in tying hunting to rainy-season farming and falls in April/May.

As Christianity and western education reshaped Berom life, the older ceremonies — Mandyeng, Nshok, Worom Chun, Vwana — were gathered into a single umbrella festival, **Nzem Berom**, first celebrated in 1980 or 1981 and held in the first week of April to coincide with the older Mandyeng, Nshok and Badu season. A Christian festival, **Wusal Berom**, was first celebrated in November 1992. The sources do not describe Berom birth, marriage or funeral rites.

## Foodways

The staple of the early Berom was *chun*, fonio, and it remains central to the diet; the year's fonio harvest has its own August festival, *Búná* or *Vwana*. Three preparations are named. *Tuk chun* is served with any soup of choice. *Katai chun* is a rich soup made mostly of wild vegetables. *Bwirik chun* is a gruel. Maize grits sometimes substitute for fonio in *katai*, and over time the Berom took up *pwana* and *ngai* (millet) for gruel. Hunting supplied meat and, in the pre-colonial period, was treated as much as a test of skill and bravery as an occupation — less economically important than farming, but prestigious enough that the naming system grew out of it. The sources describe no Berom tea or beer tradition, no ceremonial dish, and no dietary prohibition.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources document no Berom epic, folktale cycle or riddling tradition, but they do record one deeply embedded verbal practice and a body of modern literary work. Berom personal names are drawn from game animals, above all the duiker, which was held to be the most beautiful: Gyang, Pam, Dung, Davou, Chuwang and Badung are common boys' names, Kaneng, Lyop, Chundung, Nvou and Kangyang girls' names, each a different duiker species. Names such as Bot (frog), Tok (fish) and Tsok (toad) come from other undomesticated animals that were not game. The naming stock is direct evidence of how central hunting was to pre-colonial Berom society.

Written Berom is a translation project. The **Berom Language and Translation Board** (BLTB) produced the Berom Bible, *Bwok Basa Dagwi*, the earlier New Testament version being *Takada wuna sira pas*; Barnabas Dusu pioneered the work. Other Berom institutions with a cultural remit are the Berom Educational and Cultural Organisation (BECO), the Berom Youth Movement (BYM, motto *Gasi ha yi bwei*) and Berom Outreach Ministries. A collected volume, *The Berom Digest*, was edited by S. D. Nyam in 2004.

## Language & religion

Berom — *Cèn Bèrom* or *Lêm Bèrom* — belongs to the Plateau branch of Benue–Congo within Niger–Congo and is the most widely spoken Plateau language, used by speakers of all ages in rural areas while urban Berom shift to Hausa. Its dialect groups are Gyel–Kuru–Vwang, Du–Foron, Fan–Ropp–Rim–Riyom–Heikpang, Bachit, Gashish and Rahoss-Tahoss; the small Cen and Nincut dialects may be separate languages. Eastern Berom has twenty-four consonants and seven vowels, three level tones and rising and falling glides (*tút* 'to climb', high; *shɛl* 'small', mid; *bàsa* 'to teach, read', low; *nepâs* 'new', falling; *sǎn* 'empty', rising), written in a Latin orthography including ɛ, ɔ, gb, kp, ng, sh and ts. The Berom are linked to the Nok culture, dated between 200 BCE and 1000 CE. Most are Christian, over 95% belonging to COCIN or the Roman Catholic Church; the older Berom religion recognised a God, *Dagwi*. Politically the Berom have a paramount ruler, the Gbong Gwom Jos, a stool created by the British colonial administration in 1935 and held since 2009 by Da Jacob Gyang Buba.
