## Overview

The Tutsi — also written Watusi, Watutsi, or Abatutsi — are a Bantu-speaking people of Rwanda and Burundi, the second-largest of the three main groups in both countries alongside the Hutu and the Twa. Historically they were pastoralists who also filled the ranks of the warrior caste, and before 1962 they held the dominant positions in a sharply stratified Rwandan society of Tutsi aristocrats and Hutu commoners bound by a clientship structure. Sizeable Tutsi communities also live in the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, part of the wider Banyarwanda population of North and South Kivu formed by migrations before, during and after the colonial period. In folk-culture terms the Tutsi matter for a court-centred tradition built on cattle: their material world is one of milk vessels, coiled grass basketry, iron weapons and stringed instruments, and their historical leisure was spent on poetry, weaving and music.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here — the Wikipedia article and the museum catalogue records of the objects shown — do not name any Tutsi textile tradition by a vernacular term. What they do document is a coiled and plaited grass basketry tradition carrying black geometric pattern, which the catalogue describes object by object:

**Coiled grass basketry** — Round-bottomed baskets, shallow dishes, bowls, plates and drinking cups built by the coil technique from grass and other vegetable fibre, worked in natural colour against fibre dyed brown or black. Conical coiled lids and covers close the baskets; one cover carries a geometric design in black, another is a coil-sewn top made for a milk bottle, patterned black on natural.

**Coil-sewn mats and covers** — Grass mats sewn in coils, one with mauve rings on a natural ground and a zigzag pattern in the natural colour, another a leaf mat woven with dark zigzags. A third mat is built from a transverse warp of stems sewn together with black and white fibre threads that form alternating squares. A bottle cover is coil-sewn in the same manner, patterned in black on natural.

**Plaited fibre work** — Distinct from the coiled pieces: a plaited pot-stand in natural and black, a small plaited dish worked in two thicknesses of wickerwork with a diagonal stripe through the middle, a basketwork stand for a water jar with a black rim, a basketry ring, a spear ring of grass with cross-plaited decoration, and a tobacco pouch plaited from vegetable fibre with a fringe. A shield-like screen of grass is ornamented with black patterns.

**Motif vocabulary.** Zigzags; alternating squares; rings; a diagonal stripe; unnamed geometric designs in black on a natural ground.

### Clothing & dress

The catalogue records one garment: a girl's apron hung with wooden pendants and attached to a plaited hide girdle. Two images in the collection show dress rather than preserve it. A chalk and crayon drawing made at Nyanza, Rwanda, in 1958 portrays Prince François Karemera, described as a Tutsi royal dancer and a nephew of King Mutara, wearing costume, head-gear and jewellery. A gelatin silver photographic print shows a frontal head-and-shoulders portrait of an adult Tutsi woman, called a "princess" in the record, wearing a necklace, bands around the head, and a cloth patterned with flowers draped around the shoulders, photographed outdoors with hills behind her. Head-gear is represented by a bamboo head-ring fringed with bamboo tubes, each surmounted by an attached banana seed. Beyond these, the sources used do not describe Tutsi everyday or ceremonial dress, and they give no vernacular garment names.

### Architecture

The sources used do not describe Tutsi house form, building materials or architectural decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

No pottery appears among the objects shown, which is consistent with what Christopher Ehret notes of the Tutsi pastoral pattern: a people who rely on the milk, blood and meat of their cattle, shun the cultivation and consumption of grains, look down on pottery and hunting, and avoid fish. Dairy equipment is instead carved from wood — a necked wooden milk bottle with a flat base, a wooden butter pot with its lid, a wooden vase ornamented with a burnt ring round the neck, and a spherical wooden cup with a straight projecting handle cut from the solid. Other wooden objects include a comb, a container made for beer tubes, and a case for a tobacco pipe-stem. The ironwork is chiefly weaponry and tools: a socketed spear with a leaf-shaped ogee blade and a short iron butt on the wooden shaft, a knife with an iron blade and wooden handle, an arm-dagger with its wooden sheath, a composite sword with a decorated hilt, an adze with an iron blade and wooden haft, and a leaf-shaped iron hoe blade. A wooden bow strung with cane and a composite arrow — foliate iron point, bamboo shaft, feather flight bound with sinew — complete the armoury, together with a black wooden club tapering to a point.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Personal ornament in the collection is sparse but specific: a neck ornament made from a hippopotamus tooth, and the bamboo head-ring with its fringe of bamboo tubes and banana seeds. The photographic portrait records a woman wearing a necklace and bands around the head; the 1958 drawing of the royal dancer Prince François Karemera records jewellery worn with dance costume, without describing it further. Two further pieces belong to performance rather than daily wear: a circlet of iron bells connected by skin, and an iron bell attached to a strip of hide. The sources used say nothing about Tutsi tattooing, body painting or hairdressing.

## Music & performance

Wikipedia records that the leisure the Tutsi pastoral economy afforded them was spent cultivating poetry, weaving and music, and the objects shown bear this out with a group of stringed instruments. The central form is the **trough zither**: a composite instrument of wood, notched for eight strings, the string itself twisted sinew wound on a stick spool. A second example, collected in Burundi and labelled Watutsi, is an eight-stringed trough zither whose body is a rectangular, shallow wooden trough rounded at the short ends, the wood perforated with holes in the form of crosses and knife-stitches, strung with a vegetable fibre — probably sisal — that has been rubbed with tallow, and marked underneath with two large X-shaped ornaments burnt into the surface, the perforations themselves scorched and blackened. That record cites Jacques Maquet's *Ruanda* for a comparable "cithare à huit cordes." Alongside the zithers the collection holds a **harp** of wood and fibre string, and a further instrument catalogued as a zither or harp, made of wood with seven strings, some of them now missing. The iron bells — a circlet of them joined by skin, and a single bell on a hide strip — supply the percussion. The sources used name no Tutsi song genres and describe no performance occasions.

## Dance & theatre

One object speaks directly to dance. A chalk and crayon drawing made at Nyanza in 1958, one of a collection of sixty-one, portrays Prince François Karemera, identified as a Tutsi royal dancer and a nephew of King Mutara, in full costume with head-gear and jewellery. The circlet of iron bells connected by skin and the single iron bell mounted on a hide strip belong to the same world of accompanied movement. Beyond this, the sources used do not name Tutsi dances, distinguish ceremonial from entertainment repertoire, or record any theatrical tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

Rwanda has no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions, and the sources used here do not describe a Tutsi festival calendar or life-cycle rites. Two ritual observances are nevertheless recorded. Women, and especially menstruating women, are strictly forbidden to milk cows — a prohibition the Tutsi share with the neighbouring Hima to the north, and one that Ehret traces to Central Sudanic-speaking societies, where women are kept away from cattle, and whose practices survived their speakers' assimilation by Bantu populations. John Fage notes a second: the royal burial customs of the lacustrine kingdoms closely resemble those of the former Cushitic Sidama states of the southern Gibe region of Ethiopia, a resemblance he uses to argue that the Tutsi may descend from a Nilotic population that adopted monarchical traditions from adjacent Cushitic kingdoms before settling among Bantu inhabitants of the Great Lakes.

## Foodways

Tutsi diet as the sources describe it is pastoral and narrow by design. The Tutsi rely heavily on the milk, blood and meat of their cattle, and traditionally shun both the cultivation and the consumption of grains; the consumption of fish is avoided, a taboo Ehret traces back to the Tale Southern Cushitic pastoralists he regards as a principal ancestral population. Hunting is likewise avoided and looked down upon, as is pottery. Under the monarchy the Tutsi were the only proprietors of cattle and sustained themselves on their own products. The material evidence of that diet is the dairy equipment in the collection: the necked wooden milk bottle with its coil-sewn grass top, the wooden butter pot with its lid, the coiled basketry drinking cup, and the basketwork stand made to hold a water jar. A wooden container made for beer tubes points to drinking through tubes, though the sources do not describe the drink. The vocabulary of cattle keeping reaches into the language itself: *inka*, the Western Lakes Bantu word for cow, is a Sog Eastern Sahelian loanword that originally meant "cattle camp." Loanwords from Tale Southern Cushitic supply the words for "bull," "cow dung" and "lion."

## Oral tradition & literature

Poetry is named as one of the three pursuits — with weaving and music — that the Tutsi cultivated in the leisure their way of life afforded them, but the sources used here name no epic, no folktale cycle, no proverb or riddle tradition, and no storytelling context, and they record no contemporary literary revival.

## Language & religion

The Tutsi are a Bantu-speaking people. Rwandans speak Kinyarwanda and Burundians Kirundi, two similar languages; both the Tutsi and the Hutu of a given country speak the same language, one of the reasons many scholars have concluded that "Tutsi" expresses class or caste rather than ethnicity. English, French and Swahili serve as additional official languages for different historical reasons and are widely spoken as second languages in both countries. The Western Lakes Bantu languages carry loanwords from two extinct substrates: Sog Eastern Sahelian, a Kir-Abbaian language related to but distinct from Nilotic and Surmic, and Tale South Cushitic, the branch Ehret associates with the pastoral complex itself. The sources used do not describe Tutsi religion or spiritual practice.
