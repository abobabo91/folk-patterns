## Overview

The Swazi, or Swati, are a Bantu ethnic group native to Southern Africa. They live in Eswatini and South Africa’s Mpumalanga province, with Swati migrants also in South Africa and the United Kingdom. Their origins are traced through archaeology to East Africa, and their migration is described as proceeding through Mozambique before settlement in Eswatini in the 15th century. The Swazi language and culture unify the nation, whose identity extends to people owing allegiance to the twin monarchs, the *Ingwenyama* (“the Lion”) and *Indlovukati* (“the She-Elephant”). Approximately 1.2 million people live in Eswatini, while the Swati population in South Africa is slightly larger. Swazi culture is especially visible in its dance, singing, homestead structure, royal ceremonies, beadwork, pottery, carving and grasswork.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Beadwork** — Swazi people have historically made jewellery and clothing items from beads. The sources name *ligcebesha* as a colourful necklace and describe a girl’s colourful skirt called *indlamu*.

**Grass mats** — Mats called *emacansi* are made from special grasses. The sources also name *tihlantsi* among Swazi grass products.

**Basketry** — Swazis make baskets and other objects from special grasses. A museum catalogue records a beer-pot cover made of basketry, decorated with glass beads and a necklace of seeds and porcupine quills.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no specific textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

Swazi clothing and adornment include beadwork and fibre garments. The sources identify *ligcebesha* as a colourful necklace and *indlamu* as a colourful skirt for girls. A British Museum catalogue records a girl’s dress made of fibre with a band of red cotton fabric, as well as a belt made of beads and fibre. Another object is a love-letter made of cotton cloth, glass beads and mother-of-pearl buttons.

Ceremonial presentation of the bride is described in the *umtsimba*, the traditional Swazi marriage. On Sunday morning, the bride and her female relatives take part in a cattle-kraal ritual, after which the bride is smeared with red ochre. The bride also presents gifts to her husband and his relatives.

### Architecture

The principal Swazi social unit is the homestead, a traditional beehive hut thatched with dry grass. In a polygamous homestead, each wife has her own hut and yard surrounded by reed fences. The sources describe three structures used for sleeping, cooking and storage, including brewing beer. Larger homesteads may also contain bachelors’ quarters and guest accommodation.

At the centre is the cattle byre, or kraal, a circular enclosure made with large logs interspaced with branches. It has practical and ritual significance, serving as a store of wealth and a symbol of prestige. It contains sealed grain pits. Facing the cattle byre is the great hut occupied by the mother of the headman.

A group of homes forms a community, and the homes and land together form a chiefdom or *umphakatsi*. Several chiefdoms form an *inkhundla*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Historical Swazi pottery includes clay pots used for carrying water, cooking beer and decoration. These vessels are called *tინდziwo* in the source spelling. Wooden objects included the *umcwembe*, a utensil used for serving meat. Wooden sculptures were also popular.

Museum records document a head-rest cum stool carved in wood with four legs and decorated in alternating light and dark wood, and another wooden head-rest with four legs coloured in dark and light bands. A stone tobacco pipe carved from steatite is also recorded.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Swazi jewellery has historically been made from beads, seeds, porcupine quills, wood and horn. The sources name *ligcebesha*, a colourful necklace. Museum records include a necklace made of seeds and porcupine quills, a necklet made of wood beads with two horn pendants, and a hair-pin made of horn terminating in a snuff container.

A museum catalogue also records a belt made of beads and fibre and a beer-pot cover decorated with glass beads and a necklace of seeds and porcupine quills. In the *umtsimba* marriage ceremony, the bride is smeared with red ochre after the cattle-kraal ritual.

## Music & performance

Singing and dancing are prominent in Swazi culture, including praise-singing. The sources identify the *makhweyane* as a traditional Swazi instrument: a single-stringed, gourd-resonated musical bow.

A museum catalogue records a sansa instrument associated with Swaziland, South Africa and Swazi people. The object has nine metal lamellae, a bridge made of upright iron sheet and pyro-decoration. The catalogue states that it lacks a resonator and that the tone gains volume through the choice of wood in the corpus. It also records two iron rings attached to a hoop as rattles and states that the instrument was used on journeys rather than for dancing.

## Dance & theatre

Dancing is prominent in Swazi culture and appears in marriage ceremonies, royal events and communal gatherings. In the *umtsimba* marriage, the bridal party eats meat offered by the groom’s family and dances at the groom’s homestead. The bride later takes part in the cattle-kraal ceremony.

The annual *Umhlanga* Reed Dance is an eight-day ceremony. Girls cut reeds, present them to the Queen Mother and dance. Only childless, unmarried girls can take part. The royal family appoints a commoner maiden as *induna*, or captain, of the girls; she announces the dates and is described as an expert dancer knowledgeable about royal protocol. One of the King’s daughters serves as her counterpart.

## Festivals & rituals

The most important cultural event in Eswatini is the *Incwala* ceremony. It is held on the fourth day after the full moon nearest the longest day, 21 December. It is often translated as the “First Fruits” ceremony because the King tastes the new harvest, but the source also describes it as a Kingship Ceremony. When there is no king, there is no *Incwala*. Every Swazi may participate in its public parts. The climax is the fourth day of the Big *Incwala*, involving the King, Queen Mother, royal wives and children, royal governors, chiefs, regiments and the *bemanti*, or “water people”.

The annual *Umhlanga* Reed Dance takes place in late August or early September. It honours the Queen Mother and involves unmarried, childless girls cutting and presenting reeds before dancing. Its stated aims are to preserve girls’ chastity, provide tribute labour for the Queen Mother and encourage solidarity. The modern ceremony developed from the older *umchwasho* custom, an age-regiment system for young girls. The sources state that the chastity rite of *umchwasho* continued until 19 August 2005.

The traditional Swazi marriage is called *umtsimba* and is usually held during a weekend in the dry season, June to August. The bride and her relatives go to the groom’s homestead on Friday evening. Marriage rituals continue on Saturday and Sunday and include dancing, meat, a cattle-kraal ceremony, red ochre and gifts.

Birth, death and marriage are associated with family-level spiritual rituals. Funerals and religious events are usually held at family homesteads, where neighbours are invited.

## Foodways

The sources describe goat or cow meat as food served during the traditional marriage ceremony *umtsimba*. On Saturday morning, the bridal party sits by a nearby river and eats meat offered by the groom’s family. The sources also describe the cooking of beer in clay pots called *tინდziwo* and mention brewing beer as one of the activities carried out in a homestead.

The new harvest is central to *Incwala*, whose English translation is sometimes given as “First Fruits” ceremony. The King’s tasting of the new harvest is one part of the wider Kingship Ceremony. The traditional homestead contains sealed grain pits, indicating the storage of grain.

## Oral tradition & literature

Praise-singing is identified as a prominent part of Swazi culture.

## Language & religion

Swazi, or Swati, also known natively as *siSwati*, is a Bantu language of the Nguni group. It is spoken in Eswatini and South Africa, where it is an official language alongside English in Eswatini and one of the twelve official languages of South Africa. The number of speakers is estimated at approximately 4.7 million, including first- and second-language speakers.

The language has four dialects corresponding to Eswatini’s administrative regions: Hhohho, Lubombo, Manzini and Shiselweni. The sources distinguish a standard prestige variety and other varieties, including forms spoken in southern Eswatini and South Africa.

Traditional Swazi religion recognizes a supreme creator known as *Mvelincanti*, “he who was there from the beginning,” while ancestors are also recognized. Many Swazis combine these beliefs with Christianity introduced by missionaries. Traditional spiritual practices continue in family rituals associated with birth, death and marriage. A *Sangoma* is a traditional diviner chosen by a family’s ancestors. The training is called *kwetfwasa*, and divination involves *kubhula*, communication through trance with natural super-powers. An *Inyanga* is a medical and pharmaceutical specialist who uses bone throwing, *kushaya ematsambo*, to determine the cause of sickness.
