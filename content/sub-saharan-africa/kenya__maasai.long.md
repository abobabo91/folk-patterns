## Overview

The Maasai (*Iloikop*, self-designation *Ilmaasai*) are a Nilotic pastoralist people of the East African Rift, straddling the Kenya–Tanzania border across a homeland that stretches from the Laikipia Plateau and the Loita Hills in south-central Kenya through the Mara ecosystem and down into the Ngorongoro highlands, the Serengeti fringe, and the acacia savannas around Kilimanjaro and Arusha. Population estimates range from roughly 1.2 to 2 million, split between Kenya and Tanzania, with closely related Maa-speaking neighbours — the Samburu of the northern Kenyan drylands, the Il-Chamus (Njemps) of Lake Baringo, the Arusha and Parakuyo of northern Tanzania — sharing the same core linguistic and pastoral tradition. Their language, *Maa* (*ɔl Maa*), belongs to the Eastern Nilotic branch of the Nilo-Saharan family, related to Turkana, Karamojong and Teso. Maasai society is organised around a segmentary lineage system of some sixteen clans (*ilgilat*) grouped into two moieties, and cross-cut by a rigorously observed age-set (*olaji*) structure in which every male passes through boyhood, warriorhood (*ilmurran*), junior and senior elderhood in synchrony with his cohort. Cattle are the axis around which the entire cultural universe turns — as capital, bridewealth, ritual sacrifice, poetic subject, and the reason for the long transhumant swings between wet-season and dry-season pastures. In folk-culture terms the Maasai are recognisable worldwide for a small but potent inventory of signature contributions: the red-checked *shuka* cloth, the elaborate flat disc-collars and beaded jewellery of the women, the tall ochre-braided *ilmurran* silhouette, the buffalo-hide painted shield and long-bladed *empere* spear, and the throwing club *orinka* (*rungu*).

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Maasai are not, in the strict sense, a weaving people — historically dress was leather, and cloth entered the wardrobe only through nineteenth- and twentieth-century trade — but they have made a distinctive tradition out of imported cotton wrappers. The *shuka* is a rectangular length of woven cotton, most commonly in bright madder red (*nanyokie*, "the red one") with a black or dark-blue grid, though blue, purple, tartan and striped variants circulate freely; the cloth itself is generally milled in Tanzania, Kenya or increasingly in South Asia, but the choice of palette and the way it is draped are unambiguously Maasai. Red is understood to signal bravery, unity and the blood of cattle, and to deter lion; darker indigo and blue *shukas* are commoner among elders and among the Samburu to the north. Beyond the *shuka*, the true Maasai "pattern tradition" is beadwork (*enkiraru*, pl. *inkirarui*), a lexicon of stripes, radial fields and colour blocks worked in Czech and Bohemian glass seed beads on hide or on wire armatures. The colour code is codified: white (*naibor*) for milk, peace and purity; red (*nanyokie*) for bravery and the blood of the ox; blue (*narok*) for the sky and for God (*Enkai*); green (*nairurrua*) for the pastures after rain; orange and yellow for hospitality; black (*narok narok*) for the hardships that unite the people.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday male dress for warriors and younger men is two *shukas* — one wrapped around the waist as a kilt, one thrown over the shoulder — worn with beaded belts, wrist and ankle cuffs, and open leather sandals (*enamuka*) traditionally cut from raw cowhide and now very often from recycled tyre rubber, a substitution so complete that "tyre sandals" are read as a Maasai signature across East Africa. Warriors of the *ilmurran* grade grow the hair long, part it into many fine plaits, and coat both hair and body with a mixture of red ochre (*enturoto*) and animal fat, producing the polished russet finish that is the iconic warrior look; the plaits may be extended with sheep's wool and weighted with beads. On ceremonial occasions the *ilmurran* wear a lion-mane headdress (*olawaru*) — historically the reward of a warrior who had killed a lion single-handed, now heavily restricted — or an ostrich-feather corona (*enkuraru*) built on a leather band, a spectacular halo of black-and-white plumes reserved for the graduation ceremony *eunoto*. Women shave the head, wear one or two *shukas* or *kanga* wrappers knotted at the shoulder, and load the neck, ears and upper arms with beaded ornaments; married women add the *engonongoi*, the great flat disc-collar of concentric beaded rings that swings as they dance. Elderly men adopt a darker cloth, carry a long staff or the *orinka*, and wrap a plain cloak; retired warriors mark the transition by shaving off the ochred braids in the *eunoto* ceremony, an act witnessed by their mothers.

### Architecture

Maasai architecture is the architecture of a semi-nomadic cattle culture, and its signature is not the individual house but the compound: the *enkang* (or *boma*), a roughly circular kraal enclosed by a dense outer stockade of thorn-brush (*Acacia*, *Commiphora*) high enough to turn a lion, with the cattle penned in a central ring at night and the houses arranged around the inner perimeter. The house itself, the *enkaji*, is built by the women — house-building is unambiguously women's work — as a low loaf-shaped or rectangular structure on a frame of bent saplings lashed with sisal, plastered with a mixture of cow dung, mud, ash, human urine and grass that dries to a hard weatherproof shell. There are no windows; a single low doorway opens into a short curved passage that opens onto two sleeping alcoves (one for the wife and small children, one for older children and guests) and a hearth, with a small smoke-hole. Each wife in a polygynous household has her own *enkaji*, and the arrangement of doorways within the *enkang* encodes seniority. The *manyatta*, sometimes conflated with *enkang* in tourist literature, is properly a distinct settlement type: a larger ceremonial encampment built by the mothers of a warrior age-set to house the *ilmurran* collectively during their years of training, without the usual thornbush cattle enclosure. Because the pastoral cycle requires the compound to be abandoned every few years as pasture and firewood are exhausted, no monumental building tradition developed; the eloquence of Maasai architecture lies in its portability and its integration into the landscape.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Maasai do not have a strong indigenous pottery tradition — cooking vessels were historically obtained by trade with neighbouring Bantu peoples such as the Kikuyu and Chagga — and the household inventory is dominated instead by wood, leather, horn and gourd. The signature domestic object is the *calabash*, a bottle-gourd (*enkukuri*, pl. *inkukur*) hollowed, dried, smoked over a fire of *olive* (*oloirien*) heartwood to sterilise it and to impart a characteristic charred aroma, and used to store and ferment the mixture of fresh milk and cow's blood that is the classic Maasai beverage. Larger gourds serve as churns; smaller ones are the personal drinking vessel of the herder. Calabashes are commonly sheathed in beaded leather collars and closed with a beaded stopper, and given as wedding gifts. Leatherwork is the other major craft: hide bedding (*enkeene*), milking straps, shield covers, sword scabbards and sandals are all cut and sewn from cow, goat and (formerly) buffalo hide, tanned with plant extracts and often ochred. Metalwork was traditionally the province of a hereditary smith caste, the *il-kunono*, ritually marginal and endogamous, who forged the *empere* long-bladed leaf-shaped spear, the short sword (*ol-alem*) carried in a red leather scabbard on the belt, and the socketed spear-butts, using iron traded up from the coast or the Pare mountains. The throwing club — *orinka* in Maa, *rungu* in Swahili — is turned from a dense hardwood root-ball or from wild olive, with a heavy knobbed head and a tapered shaft; carried by elders as a badge of office, thrown by warriors and boys with lethal accuracy at small game and at attacking predators. The Maasai shield, *ol-onga'a*, is oval, cut from thick buffalo or ox hide stretched on a wooden strut, and painted in bold radial fields of red, white and black earth pigments in patterns (*sirata*) that identify the bearer's age-set, section and personal deeds. Wooden headrests, snuff containers of horn or gourd, and the elder's staff (*enkuma*) complete the household inventory.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Beadwork is the pre-eminent Maasai art form and is worked exclusively by women, who learn it from girlhood and who produce ornaments both for their own households and, increasingly, for a substantial cooperative export market. The *engonongoi* (also *isurutia*) is the great married-woman's collar: a broad flat disc of concentric beaded rings mounted on a wire armature, sometimes half a metre in diameter, that bounces on the wearer's chest during the *adumu* jumping-song and thereby "dances of itself". Younger unmarried girls wear narrower flat collars; brides receive a specially elaborate one from their mothers. Complementary pieces include the *nborro* wedding necklace with long pendant strands, beaded belts, and matching bracelets and anklets. Both sexes stretch and pierce the earlobes from childhood, the resulting long loops decorated with beaded pendants or with plugs of wood, ivory or, in modern practice, film-canister lids; the upper ear is also pierced for smaller ornaments. Facial and body scarification — small keloidal cheek and belly marks — was widespread historically and is now much less common. The removal of the two lower central incisors, once nearly universal, persists in some communities. Ochre body-paint on warriors, white chalk (*enturoto naibor*) circles and dots on initiates during the seclusion period, and the shaven, oiled and ochred head of the newly-married woman are all part of the same visual grammar in which the body itself is a bead-field.

## Music & performance

Maasai music is overwhelmingly vocal and overwhelmingly a-cappella: there are no drums, no lyres, and no melodic instruments in the traditional ensemble — a striking absence in an East African context — and the sole regularly-used instrument is the *kudu* horn (*olarinyani*), sounded to summon warriors and at the climax of the *eunoto*. The characteristic sound is polyphonic call-and-response: a song leader (*olaranyani*) improvises the melodic line and the narrative content, while the chorus of warriors answers with a rhythmic ostinato built on the guttural throat-pulse *"oh-oh-oh"* and on sharp inhaled grunts that drive the pulse. Overlapping entries, drone-like held notes, and rapid metrical shifts produce a dense, hocketed texture. Songs are composed for every occasion — praise of a particular ox by its colour-name, mockery of a rival age-set, courtship, lament, and the long narrative songs that recount a warrior's raids. Girls and women have their own repertoire, higher and more melodic, sung at weddings and at the drawing of water.

## Dance & theatre

The most internationally recognised Maasai performance is *adumu*, the "jumping dance" of the *ilmurran*, performed at *eunoto* and at other ceremonies of the warrior grade: a circle of warriors in full ochre and *shuka* takes turns stepping into the centre to leap vertically from a standing start, heels never touching the ground, higher and higher in competitive display, while the surrounding circle intones the driving vocal ostinato and the *engonongoi*-clad women dance on the periphery, jerking the shoulders so that the disc-collars bounce in counter-rhythm. The neck-and-shoulder "collar dance" of the women is its own distinct form. There is no formal theatre in the proscenium sense; dramatic performance is embedded in ritual — the mock cattle-raid, the warrior's boasting speech (*enkigúenà*) delivered before his peers, the elders' oratorical debate under the fig tree — all of which have strong performative and rhetorical conventions.

## Festivals & rituals

Maasai ritual life is structured by the age-set system and by a small number of cattle-centred ceremonies. Male initiation (*emuratare*) at around fifteen involves circumcision performed publicly and in silence — flinching is a lifelong disgrace — after which the initiate enters a seclusion period wearing black robes and white facial paint, hunts small birds whose stuffed skins decorate a headdress, and eventually graduates into the *ilmurran* grade. The *eunoto* ceremony, held roughly every fifteen years, marks the collective transition of an entire warrior age-set into junior elderhood: the mothers build a special *manyatta*, the warriors gather for days of feasting, singing, and *adumu*, the ostrich-feather *enkuraru* is worn, and at the climax each warrior's mother shaves off his ochred braids. *Olng'esherr* is the later ceremony that promotes junior elders to full senior elderhood. Marriage negotiations involve extended bridewealth in cattle and the ritual anointing of the bride with milk and fat. *Olpul* is the meat-feasting retreat: a group of warriors withdraws for days or weeks to a secluded bush camp to slaughter and roast an ox or several goats, drink the blood-broth, and gorge on meat in a ritualised bout of male commensality that reinforces age-set solidarity and, in some communities, is credited with medicinal recuperation. Rain-making and drought-breaking prayers are directed to *Enkai* through the *oloiboni*, the ritual expert-diviner of the Il-Aiser clan.

## Foodways

The traditional Maasai diet is unusually narrow and unusually animal-based: milk (*kule*), fresh or soured, is the staple, drunk from the smoked calabash; cattle blood (*saroi*), drawn from a small arrow-wound in the jugular vein of a living ox and then closed with mud so the animal is not killed, is mixed with milk to produce a nutrient-dense beverage particularly given to the sick, to women after childbirth, and to warriors after circumcision; meat (*enkiri*) — beef, mutton, goat — is eaten in quantity at ceremonial slaughters and at *olpul* retreats but is not everyday food. Vegetables, grains and fish were historically avoided as unfit for a cattle-people, though maize meal (*ugali*), rice, tea with heavy sugar and milk, and beans have entered the everyday diet through market contact. Wild plants are used medicinally rather than as staples: the bitter bark of *Acacia nilotica* and *Warburgia ugandensis* is boiled into meat soups (*motori*) drunk at *olpul* both as digestive and as tonic. Honey beer (*enaisho*) is brewed for elders' ceremonies.

## Oral tradition & literature

Maasai literature is oral, and the *oloiboni* — the hereditary diviner-prophets of the Il-Aiser clan, of whom the nineteenth-century Mbatiany and his sons Lenana and Sendeyo are the most famous — are its keepers of prophecy and history. The corpus includes praise-poems for oxen and for warriors, boast-songs (*enkigúenà*) declaimed by an individual warrior before his age-mates, formal genealogies (*enkiteng oo lmaasai*), proverbs (*inkinjejek*), riddles (*ilomon*), and a rich body of fables in which the hare, the hyena and the lion enact human folly. Historical narrative is transmitted through the naming of age-sets — each set has a name (*Ilterito*, *Ilnyangusi*, *Iseuri*, *Ilkitoip*, *Ilaimer*, and so on) that anchors a generation of events — so that "in the time of *Iseuri*" locates a story precisely. Written Maa literature is a twentieth-century development, catalysed by mission orthographies and more recently by Maa-language radio.

## Language & religion

*Maa* is spoken in a dialect continuum from the Samburu and Chamus varieties in the north through the Purko, Kisonko, Loita and Kaputiei of the central Maasai to the Parakuyo and Arusha in the south; mutual intelligibility is high across the continuum. The language is tonal, has a marked/unmarked gender system in which nouns take *ol-*/*ilo-* (masculine) or *en-*/*ena-* (feminine) prefixes, and is rich in cattle-colour vocabulary of famously fine discrimination. Traditional religion is monotheistic: *Enkai* (also *Ngai*) is the single high god, dual-aspected as *Enkai Narok* (the black god, benevolent, associated with rain and thunder) and *Enkai Nanyokie* (the red god, angry, associated with drought and lightning), dwelling on Ol Doinyo Lengai, "the mountain of God", the active volcano in northern Tanzania. Cattle are understood to have been given to the Maasai by *Enkai* at the beginning of time — a belief that has historically underwritten the cattle-raiding ethic. The *oloiboni* mediates between the community and *Enkai* through divination with pebbles cast from a cow-horn (*enkidong*). Christianity, chiefly Catholic and Anglican, has made significant inroads since the mid-twentieth century, and a substantial minority of Maasai in the northern ranges are Muslim through Swahili trade contact.
