## Overview

The Maasai are an Eastern Nilotic people of the Great Rift Valley, spread across the semi-arid rangelands of southern Kenya and northern Tanzania — Kajiado and Narok counties, the Loita and Ngong hills, the plains around Amboseli, the Mara, Loliondo and the Ngorongoro highlands. The 2019 Kenyan census recorded 1,189,522 Maasai in Kenya, up from 377,089 in 1989; a further population lives in Tanzania. Their language, *Maa*, belongs to the Nilotic branch of Nilo-Saharan, closely related to Dinka, Nuer and Kalenjin, and the community is subdivided into twenty-two territorial sections or *iloshon* — Purko, Kisonko, Matapato, Loitai, Keekonyokie, Siria, Laikipiak, Parakuyo and others — each with its own dialect, leadership and dress conventions. In folk-culture terms the Maasai matter because almost every object they make is legible as social information: a bead colour, a hair length, a cloak's hue states the wearer's age-grade, marital condition and section.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Shúkà** — the Maa word for the rectangular cloth wrapped and knotted at the shoulder, worn singly or in layers. Commercial cotton replaced calf hide and sheepskin in the 1960s; the dominant field is red, often checked or striped with black, blue, purple or orange. The *shúkà* is worn as a wrapped, pinned cloak rather than a tailored garment, which distinguishes it from the sewn robes of neighbouring agricultural peoples.

**Kanga** — the one-piece printed cotton garment, borrowed from the Swahili coast, worn especially by women in pairs and increasingly common inland.

**Kikoi** — a sarong-like woven cloth in many colours and textures, worn by Maasai living near the Kenyan coast, again a coastal loan rather than a Maa product.

**Maasai leatherwork** — before cloth, the wardrobe was hide: calf and sheep skins scraped, softened with fat and ochre, and cut into cloaks and skirts. Women's hide skirts and infant carriers were the main field for applied beadwork, and hide remains the substrate for ceremonial garments and for *makeshift cattle-hide bandages* used at circumcision.

**Maasai beadwork** — not woven but the community's principal pattern art: glass seed beads threaded on wire or sinew into flat discs, bands and panels. Beadwork is women's work, made in collective sessions, and the colour grammar is fixed enough that a finished collar can be read across sections.

**Maasai sandals** — flat sandals historically cut from rawhide, now almost universally from truck tyre rubber, with beaded or leather straps.

**Motif vocabulary.** Concentric rings on the flat collar (*enkarewa*), reading outward as bands; red for blood, cattle and bravery; black for the people and the hardships endured; white for milk and purity; blue for the sky and rain; green for the grazing land; orange and yellow for hospitality; alternating triangle bands; zigzag strap patterns; the beaded pendant fringe hung from a collar so that it shivers when a woman bounces her shoulders; ochre-red body and hide colouring; and, for boys in the months after circumcision, black cloth with white chalk facial patterning.

### Clothing & dress

Dress is a dated statement of age-grade. Boys awaiting circumcision wear black; for several months after the operation the new initiate wears black cloth and paints his face with white chalk. The *moran* — the warrior grade — wear red *shúkà*, long hair braided into thin strands and dressed with red ochre and sheep fat, and beaded belts, armlets and headbands; at *Eunoto*, the ceremony that promotes them to junior elder, the plaited hair is shaved off by the mother and elders wear their hair short thereafter. Married women wear the *enkarewa*, a large flat beaded disc collar, plus stacked coiled-wire neck rings, beaded headbands and brass earrings; an unmarried girl's collar and headwork differ in colour balance and fringe. Elders carry a cloak, a stool and a *rungu*. Footwear is the tyre-rubber sandal. Headdresses include the *enkuraru* and lion-mane and ostrich-feather headdresses worn by *moran* at ceremonies — the mane headdress being the trophy of a lion kill, now largely historical since lion hunting was banned.

### Architecture

The Maasai homestead is the *enkang* (also *boma*): a ring of houses enclosed by a dense thorn-bush fence of *Acacia* branches, with the cattle penned in the centre at night and gates assigned to each wife's household. Houses are built entirely by women. The *enkaji* is a low, loaf-shaped or rectangular-oval structure — a frame of pliable sticks lashed into a dome and plastered with a mixture of cow dung, mud, ash, grass and cattle urine, which dries to a hard waterproof shell. There are no windows beyond a small smoke hole; the doorway is offset with a short baffled entry passage that blocks wind, light and predators. Inside, the single space is divided by low stick partitions into a hearth, sleeping platforms of hide over sticks, and a pen for calves and sick lambs. Ceilings are about 1.5 m, so the interior is crouching height. The form is built for a semi-nomadic cycle: an *enkang* is occupied for a few years while grazing lasts and then abandoned, the fence left to rot, and there is essentially no applied architectural ornament — the decoration in Maasai life is carried on the body, not the building.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Maasai are not potters; their vessel tradition is the gourd. The *calabash* — a dried bottle gourd, often called *engonongoi* — is used for milk and for the blood-and-milk mixture, its interior smoked and disinfected with charred *olea* or acacia sticks, and its surface sheathed in hide, sewn leather straps and cowrie-and-bead banding, with a leather-and-bead stopper. Gourds are women's property, given at marriage and childbirth. Weapons are the other object class: the *orinka*, a throwing club cut from a single piece of hardwood root with a heavy knob, thrown accurately at up to seventy paces; the *rungu*, the knobbed club carried by elders as a badge of authority; the *ol alem* short sword in a red-dyed leather scabbard; and the long-bladed iron spear, forged by smiths of the *il-kunono* smithing lineage, which is socially set apart from herding families. The buffalo-hide *shield* (*ol kirket*) was painted in red, black and white with the heraldry of the bearer's section and age-set.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Beaded ornament is the dominant art form. Women make and wear the flat disc collar, stacked neck rings of coiled wire, beaded headbands, wire armlets and anklets, and layered ear ornaments. Both men and women pierce and progressively stretch the earlobes, using thorns to pierce and then twigs, bundled sticks, stones, ivory cross-sections or empty film canisters to enlarge them; metal hoops are worn in the stretched lobe, and women add smaller beaded studs in the upper ear. Bodies and hair are dressed with *ochre* mixed with fat — red ochre on *moran* hair and shoulders. Removal of the deciduous canine tooth buds in infancy is widespread: a 1991–92 Kenyan survey found 87% of children aged six months to two years had had one or more removed. Beadwork is sold to visitors today, and the trade is a real income stream for women's groups.

## Music & performance

Maasai music is overwhelmingly vocal. A song leader, the *olaranyani*, carries the melody while a chorus supplies rhythmic vocal harmonies beneath it — and unusually for the region, the Maasai make heavy use of drone polyphony, a sustained low vocal pedal under the lead line, sung with a throat-tightened, pulsed articulation. Drums are essentially absent from the core tradition; rhythm comes from voices, from stamped feet, from the grunted *"oooh-yah"* breath-pulse of the men's chorus, and from the clash of beaded collars and metal ornaments as dancers move. Women's repertoire is distinct: lullabies, humming songs, songs praising sons, and the *Namba*, a call-and-response harmony pattern built on repeated and sometimes nonsensical refrain phrases, monophonic melodies, and verse-ends that descend the scale. Women commonly sing and dance among themselves when they gather, without men present. Song contexts are age-graded and occasion-bound — herding songs, songs for the circumcision season, songs sung over cattle, warriors' praise songs recounting raids and lion hunts, and the long cycles sung at *Eunoto*, which can run ten days or more. The *olpul* — a meat-eating camp in the bush where *moran* slaughter and roast an ox away from the settlement — is a significant male performance setting, with singing, praise-chanting and storytelling.

## Dance & theatre

The Maasai have no masked or scripted theatre; performance is danced and sung ceremony. The best known form is the *Adumu* (also *aigus*), the competitive jumping dance of the *moran*: warriors form a circle, one or two step into the centre and leap vertically from a standing position without letting the heels touch down, while the chorus raises the pitch of its vocal drone in step with the height of the jump. Both *adumu* and *aigus* are Maa verbs meaning "to jump". The warriors of the Il-Oodokilani also perform a formal march-past at *Eunoto*. Women's dances are built on the shoulder — a forward-and-back thrust that sets the beaded disc collar rocking and flashing, danced in lines facing the men. Dances are graded: there are movements proper to uncircumcised girls, to *moran*, to newly promoted elders, and to mothers of warriors at *Eunoto*, and performing outside one's grade is a breach. Much of what tourists see staged at cultural *bomas* is an abbreviation of these ceremonial forms.

## Festivals & rituals

The Maasai calendar is age-set time, not seasonal festival time: the great events come once in a generation and are organised around the *olaji*, the age-set, which is opened and closed roughly every fifteen years. *Emorata*, circumcision, initiates boys into warriorhood; the boy's head is shaved two days before, the elders cut with a sharpened knife, and he must endure it without sound or movement — a cry brings dishonour and a flinch risks the surgeon's hand. *Eunoto* promotes *moran* to junior elder over ten or more days of singing and dancing, culminating in the shaving of the warriors' long plaited hair by their mothers. *Olng'esherr*, the meat-eating ceremony, closes the passage to full senior elderhood. *Enkipaata* opens a new age-set. Girls historically underwent *Emuatare*, a rite involving excision followed by early arranged marriage; the practice is illegal in Kenya and Tanzania and campaigners including Agnes Pareyio and Nice Nailantei Lengete — the first woman to address the Maasai elders' council at Kilimanjaro, who persuaded it to ban the practice — have promoted an alternative "cutting with words" ceremony of singing and dancing, though the older practice remains deeply entrenched. Rain and blessing ceremonies are led by the *laibon*, the diviner-healer whose authority rests on personal reputation rather than office. Burial is minimal: bodies are left for scavengers, sometimes smeared with the fat and blood of a slaughtered ox, and a corpse the hyenas refuse is taken as a social disgrace.

## Foodways

The classical diet is the products of the herd: milk, meat and blood, from Zebu cattle, supplemented with honey. Most milk is drunk fermented or as buttermilk left from churning, and consumption figures are extremely high by any comparative standard. Blood is tapped from the jugular of a living bullock with a blocked arrow, the wound then sealed, and drunk fresh or mixed with milk — served particularly to the sick, to women after childbirth, and to newly circumcised initiates. Meat is not everyday food: bulls, goats and lambs are slaughtered for ceremonies, and the *olpul* bush camp is where *moran* eat roast meat in quantity, boiled with bitter bark infusions believed to give strength and courage. Milk is stored and soured in the hide-cased gourd, disinfected with charred acacia, which gives it a distinct smoky taste. Fruits gathered in the bush are the snack food of children, herding women and *moran*. The diet has shifted decisively: a 2010 study found more than 58% of the modern Maasai diet is plant food, above all maize *ugali*, with sorghum, rice, potatoes and cabbage — known in Maa as "goat leaves" — now standard, a consequence of shrinking herds, park boundaries and the 1975–1992 cultivation ban in the Ngorongoro Conservation Area. Sweet spiced milky tea, *chai*, is the near-universal item of hospitality.

## Oral tradition & literature

Maa is an unwritten language in its traditional use, and the culture's memory is carried in genealogies of age-sets, in praise-poetry and in the prophetic utterances of the *laibon* lineage — the prophet **Mbatian**, after whom the highest peak of Mount Kenya is named, is the most celebrated. Warriors compose and perform *praise songs* naming their own deeds and their cattle, and the *olpul* camp and the evening *enkang* are the settings where stories, raid histories and animal tales are told. Proverbs and oratory are formalised: elders' debate at the *enkiguena*, the open assembly, is conducted with an etiquette of turn-taking and metaphor, and a young man's advancement depends partly on his speaking. Missionary and colonial-era collectors produced the first written record — A. C. Hollis's *The Masai: Their Language and Folklore* (1905) remains the largest single published corpus of Maa tales and proverbs. There is now an online Maa dictionary project and community-based literacy and education initiatives, several founded by Maasai themselves.

## Language & religion

*Maa* is an Eastern Nilotic language of the Nilo-Saharan family, closest to Samburu and Chamus (Il-Chamus) — both often treated as Maa-speaking sections rather than separate peoples — and related at greater distance to Dinka, Nuer and Kalenjin. Dialects track the *iloshon*; most speakers below elder age are trilingual in Maa, Swahili and English. Traditional religion is monotheistic: *Enkai* (Engai, Nkai) is a single deity of dual aspect — *Engai Narok*, the Black God, benevolent and associated with rain-bearing cloud, and *Engai Na-nyokie*, the Red God, vengeful and associated with drought and lightning. Society is founded on two totemic moieties, *Oodo Mongi* (the Red Cow) and *Orok Kiteng* (the Black Cow), subdivided into five clans; the lion is the tribal totem animal. *Ol Doinyo Lengai*, the active carbonatite volcano in northern Tanzania, is the Mountain of God. Christianity and, less commonly, Islam are now widely professed alongside continuing *laibon* practice.
