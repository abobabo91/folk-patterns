## Overview

The Mambwe are an ethnic group from Mbala and Senga Hill district of Northeastern Zambia and Kalambo District of Rukwa Region, Tanzania. In 1987, the Mambwe population in Tanzania was estimated to number 63,000; the number in Zambia had not been independently estimated, although the combined number of Mambwe and Lungu people in Zambia was estimated at 262,800 in 1993. The Mambwe speak Mambwe, also called *Cimambwe*, a Bantu language and a dialect of Mambwe-Lungu. Mambwe-Lungu is also spoken in Rukwa region and southern Sumbawanga town in Tanzania, as well as in Mankato, Mpulungu and Senga district of Zambia. The sources document Mambwe political history, clans, music, ceremony, religious practices and museum-held objects.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used do not document a Mambwe textile or pattern-bearing textile tradition.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name an incised geometrical ornament on a wooden belt and an incised leaf pattern on a gourd vessel, but do not identify these as Mambwe motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources used do not describe everyday or ceremonial Mambwe clothing, head coverings, footwear or dress distinctions. They mention bark cloth in which babies were carried during a conflict with the Bemba, but provide no broader account of clothing practice. A museum catalogue records a wooden belt with incised geometrical ornament, without identifying its use in dress.

### Architecture

The sources used do not describe Mambwe houses, villages, roofs or other forms of vernacular architecture. They state that a shrine or temple called *kavua* was erected in each village and dedicated to the ancestors of local chiefs.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Museum catalogue records include an iron axe with a wooden handle, a wooden European machete with iron, an iron hoe with a wooden handle, a wooden stool with three legs, a stool with four legs cut from solid wood, a stool made of wood, a wooden head-rest, a wooden spatulate artefact possibly used as a pottery beater, a cane basket, three baskets made of coiled grass, and a gourd vessel ornamented with an incised leaf pattern. The sources do not state which objects are specifically associated with Mambwe manufacture or use.

### Jewelry & body adornment

A museum catalogue records an armlet made of ivory and repaired in two places with local vegetal fibre, and a string of 28 barrel-shaped beads made of agate (?). The sources do not describe their meanings, occasions of use, or broader Mambwe body-adornment practices.

## Music & performance

The Mambwe have a traditional music piece consisting of a dance called *Nsimba* and musical instruments called *Vingwengwe*. The sources also name Kalambo hit parade, Solo Kalenge and Esther Namunga as artists who perform Mambwe Lungu traditional music. No further information is provided about the construction of the instruments, the musical forms, the repertory, or the contexts in which performances take place.

The sources used do not document additional Mambwe song genres, instrumental classifications, performance institutions or named musical ceremonies. They state that traditional dances and music form part of the annual harvest ceremony described under Festivals & rituals.

## Dance & theatre

*Nsimba* is identified as a traditional Mambwe dance. Traditional dances and music are also included in the ceremony held to celebrate a successful harvest and pray for rains for the next farming season. The sources do not describe the movements, performers, costumes or distinction between ceremonial and entertainment dancing. They do not document Mambwe theatre, masked performance, puppetry or dramatic traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

The Mambwe and the Lungu of Tafuna hold an annual traditional ceremony to celebrate a successful harvest and pray for rains for the next farming season. It takes place every last Saturday of June on the shores of lake Chila in Mbala. During the ceremony, the wives of the Chiefs present samples of food grown in their chiefdoms. The Chiefs bless the food and give thanks to the ancestral spirits for a successful harvest. The Chiefs then taste the food, after which the subjects are free to eat. The ceremony also includes traditional dances and music.

Traditional Mambwe religion recognizes a supreme god called *Leza*, who created the earth and everything on it but is described as remote from human affairs and not worshiped. Worship is directed to spirits of chiefly ancestors, called *imipasi yamwene yafwe*. Natural objects such as hills, caves, large rocks and permanent pools of water are divinized and referred to as *mayao* or *maleza*. These land shrines are associated with ancestral spirits called *imipasi* and are visited once a year in normal times and when crops have been harvested. Part of each crop is left at the shrine and the ancestral spirits are thanked. The sources also state that chiefs and their people appeal to the shrines during drought, crop failure, locust invasion, outbreaks of disease and invasion of their country.

The sources used do not document Mambwe birth, initiation, marriage or funeral rites.

## Foodways

The sources state that, during the annual harvest ceremony, the wives of the Chiefs present samples of food grown in their chiefdoms. The Chiefs taste the food before the subjects are free to eat, and part of each crop is left at a shrine during thanksgiving to the ancestral spirits. The sources do not name particular crops, dishes, grains, cooking methods or beverages.

In the account of conflict with the Bemba, the Mambwe are described as having been reduced to starvation and forced to live on rats, dogs and bark cloth in which babies were carried. This is presented as an episode of wartime deprivation, not as a description of ordinary foodways. The sources used do not document dietary rules, tea or coffee traditions, or named ceremonial dishes.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe accounts of Mambwe origins involving the common people and the Chiefs. The ruling line is said to have been founded by Changala, who came from Kola and was a Mulua. Changala is said to have settled in the south of Mambwe country under the authority of Chindo, head of the Simwinga clan. The account explains the relationship between the commoner title Chindo and the senior royal title Nsokolo, and traces the royal line through Mwimbe and Nsokolo wa Chisenga.

Mambwe clan names include Sichula, Sichilima, Simpemba, Simwinga and Simpasa, among many others. A quotation attributed to William Watson describes the Mambwe as suspicious of strangers, hardy, resilient and attached to their independence. The sources also list Mambwe Proverbs by A. Werner and A. N. Tucker among relevant literature. They do not document a named epic, riddle tradition, contemporary literary revival or preservation project.

## Language & religion

The Mambwe speak Mambwe, also called *Cimambwe*, a Bantu language and a dialect of Mambwe-Lungu. The Mambwe and Lungu peoples living at the southern end of Lake Tanganyika in Tanzania and Zambia speak a common language with minor dialectical differences. Mambwe-Lungu is also spoken by people of Rukwa region and southern Sumbawanga town in Tanzania, and in Mankato, Mpulungu and Senga district of Zambia. It has close affinities with languages spoken by Pimbwe, Rungwa and Namwanga. The sources do not describe a historical script.

Most Mambwe people today are Christian, including many Jehovah Witnesses or Catholics. Traditional Mambwe religion recognizes Leza as the creator, while worship is directed toward chiefly ancestral spirits. Shrines called kavua were erected in villages for local chiefly ancestors, and natural places called mayao or maleza were associated with imipasi.
