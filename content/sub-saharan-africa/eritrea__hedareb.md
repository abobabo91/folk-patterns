## Overview

The Hedareb, also called T'bdawe, are a Cushitic ethnic group native to northwestern Eritrea and extending to the borders with east Sudan. They are a subgroup of the Beja and are among the least-researched groups in Eritrea. Hedareb communities are nomadic or semi-nomadic pastoralists who migrate seasonally with herds of camels, goats and sheep. Their linguistic situation is diverse: one subgroup speaks the traditional Beja language, while another is more closely related to Sudanese Hadendoa; Hedareb people speak Beja or Tigre as a mother tongue, and some also speak Arabic. Hedareb society is organized hierarchically into clans and subclans.

## Oral tradition & literature

Beja has an extensive oral tradition that includes multiple poetic genres. A well-known epic is the story of the hero Mhamuud Oofaash, portions of which have appeared in publications by Klaus Wedekind. An edition appears in Mahmud Mohammed Ahmed’s *Oomraay*, published in Asmara. During the 1960s and ’70s, the Beja intellectual Muhammed Adarob Ohaj collected oral recordings of poetic and narrative material, now held in the University of Khartoum Institute of African and Asian Studies Sound Archives. Didier Morin and Mohamed-Tahir Hamid Ahmed used these recordings and their own collections in academic publications in French on Beja poetics. Red Sea University and the NGO Uhaashoon worked with oral story-tellers to produce 41 short readers and a longer collection of three short stories in Beja between 2010 and 2013.

## Language & religion

Beja, also called Bidhaawyeet or Tubdhaawi by native speakers, is an Afroasiatic language of the Cushitic branch spoken by the Beja people in parts of Egypt, Sudan and Eritrea. Most linguists classify it as the only member of the Northern Cushitic subgroup. The Hedareb speak Beja or Tigre as a mother tongue; most also speak at least one other language, typically Tigre, while a small group also speaks Arabic. Both Roman and Arabic scripts have been used to write Beja, but no writing system has gained wide support; the Latin script is the only system used in publications by more than one writer. Hedarebs are Muslim, and most are Sunni Muslims. As Muslim people, they follow Sharia law in most matters.
