## Overview

The Sihanaka are a Malagasy ethnic group concentrated around Lake Alaotra and Ambatondrazaka in central northeastern Madagascar. Their name means “people of the swamps,” referring to the marshlands around the lake. In 2013, they were estimated to number around 200,000. They speak a dialect of Malagasy, a Malayo-Polynesian language derived from the Barito languages of southern Borneo. Rice cultivation has long been central to Sihanaka life, while their location on the trade route between Antananarivo and Toamasina made them important traders in rice, zebu, slaves, and other goods. Their folk culture includes plant-fiber houses, raffia clothing, reed mats, ancestor rites, fady, funeral practices, pottery, fishing customs, and the continuing importance of traditional beliefs alongside Christianity.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Zozoro reed mats** — Women traditionally weave *zozoro* (Cyperus) reed mats. These mats served as walls, flooring, and roofing in Sihanaka houses.

**Raffia clothing** — Traditional dress was made from clothing woven of raffia fibers.

### Clothing & dress

Sihanaka traditional dress consisted of clothing woven from raffia fibers. Funeral clothing is described more specifically: before the body of a deceased person was removed, the widow wore a fine red *lamba* and all her jewelry. Afterward, villagers removed her *lamba* and jewelry, uncombed her hair, dressed her in an old *lamba*, required her to eat with a broken spoon and plate, and covered her with a rotting mat. She maintained this mourning state for at least eight months, during which she could not wash or speak to others during daylight.

### Architecture

Sihanaka houses were made from plant material because building homes in brick or clay was believed to displease the ancestors. Men constructed the carpentered frame, roof, windows, doors, and door and window frames. Women wove *zozoro* reed mats used for walls, flooring, and roofing. In the past, Sihanaka villages were often fortified by earthen walls called *tamboho*. European visitors in 1667 reported that the Sihanaka lived in fortified villages defended with bows and arrows, and that they had built a bridge.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Sihanaka women produced pottery, which was baked using wood collected by men in surrounding forests. Other documented household objects include *zozoro* reed mats, wooden or plant-material house components, and the spoons and plates used during mourning rites.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention jewelry chiefly in relation to funeral rites. A deceased man’s widow put on all her jewelry before watching the funeral procession, and villagers later removed it as part of her mourning treatment. Male children were traditionally circumcised by their families no later than the age of seven.

## Music & performance

The sources describe music and vocal performance primarily in the context of funerals. After a body was carried to a house where village women gathered, female relatives wept while other women played drums and recited prayers. At times, a selected man circled the house and sang a funeral chant, while the women inside responded in call-and-response. The men stayed in a separate *tranolahy*, or “men’s house,” where they prepared a feast of cooked meat and rum, periodically delivering food to the women mourners.

The traditional martial art of *moraingy*, described as particularly common among Sakalava communities, was historically also common among the Sihanaka.

## Festivals & rituals

Sihanaka social and religious life is organized around respect for the ancestors and *fady*, the ancestral interdictions or taboos established by them. The strongest reported prohibition was the ban on working in a paddy field on Tuesdays. Other groups within Sihanaka society observed taboos connected with particular days, including prohibitions on leaving the house or cleaning floor mats.

Ancestor-related practices included *joro*, collective prayers invoking God or the ancestors; burial in family tombs; the *famadihana* reburial ceremony; and the sanctification of stones called *tsangambato*, land called *tany masina*, and shrines called *doany*, *tony*, and *jiro*. The *ambalavelona* were understood as spirits of the ancestors themselves. Sihanaka also believed that ancestral spirits could possess the living, placing them in a trance called a *tromba*. *Ombiasy*, or wise men, provided spiritual guidance, communed with ancestors, and determined auspicious days for particular activities.

Funeral rites involved a separate door for carrying out the body, mourning, prayers, drums, a feast, purification with herbs and water, an eight-day period of fasting, and further purification called *afana*. The family also performed *faly ranom-bohangy*, a purification ritual involving lemon or lime leaves and two other herbs.

## Foodways

Rice has long been the principal Sihanaka food and crop, and the region around Lake Alaotra is among Madagascar’s most productive rice-growing areas. The rice-based diet was supplemented with game and other meats, including lemurs, wild boars, snakes, owls, rats, cats, and crocodile. Crocodile was once protected by a *fady*, although that prohibition was later abandoned.

Tobacco, marijuana, and pork were commonly forbidden in Sihanaka families. Many Sihanaka believed that transporting swine across Lake Alaotra would bring storms. Funeral feasts included cooked meat and rum. During the funeral purification process, lemon or lime leaves and two other herbs were soaked in water and sprinkled on the deceased person’s possessions.

Fishing in Lake Alaotra was traditionally divided by gender and age. Men were allowed to fish only for eels, women used nets to catch small fish, and children alone were permitted to fish with poles. Men and children left their catch on the shore for women to collect and carry to the village.

## Oral tradition & literature

Sihanaka oral history preserves several accounts of the group’s origins. One account holds that Betsimisaraka traders first reached the area by traveling inland along the Maningory River. Another connects ancient earthen defensive trenches with ancestors of the Merina who may have passed Lake Alaotra during migration. A further account describes a large group calling itself Sihanaka migrating from Imerina into the Alaotra area.

The sources also record folk beliefs concerning the *aye-aye*. A person who fell asleep in the forest was traditionally believed possibly to receive a pillow from an aye-aye: a pillow for the head signified wealth, while one for the feet indicated evil sorcery.

## Language & religion

The Sihanaka speak a dialect of Malagasy. Malagasy belongs to the Malayo-Polynesian branch of the language family and is derived from the Barito languages spoken in southern Borneo.

Traditional Sihanaka beliefs center on a creator god called *Zanahary*, the ancestors, and *fady*. Christianity influenced Sihanaka society after the conversion of the Merina royal court in 1869. Merina missionaries arrived in Sihanaka country, and later French Catholic and Protestant missionaries also worked in the area. Today, traditional beliefs remain strong, alongside orthodox Christianity and syncretic forms incorporating respect for ancestors and ancestral norms. The sources also describe *sampy* idols associated with the protective spiritual power of 11 deities and the use of *joro*, *tromba*, ancestral shrines, and family tombs.
