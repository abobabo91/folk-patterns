## Overview

The Boya are agro-pastoralists of the Boya Hills in South Sudan, growing sorghum and maize while herding cattle and small stock. They are known as one of the best-photographed Surmic groups, with a striking visual culture centred on beaded ornaments and body scarification. Their social life is structured by age-sets and lineage, and marked by cattle-raiding, hunting, and elaborate life-cycle rites. They speak Narim, closely related to the language of their Didinga neighbours, and today combine older rain-making and ancestor veneration with Catholic Christianity.

## Material culture

The Boya's material culture is a culture of the decorated body — hide, bead, brass, and scarred skin rather than woven textiles.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Boya worked pattern into hide, bead, brass and skin rather than woven cloth, making the body itself the principal ground.

- **Lokwoto beaded corset** (*lokwoto*) — A stiff, close-fitting torso piece of glass seed beads stitched to leather or fibre, worn by young unmarried women, with tightly banded colour blocks rather than open lattice work.
- **Leketio leather belt** (*leketio*) — A broad hide waist belt worked with cowrie or bead insets and metal studs, marking a woman's marital and reproductive standing.
- **Dolulu beaded skirt** (*dolulu*) — A short hide apron-skirt with dense bead embroidery on the front and a fringed lower edge, hung from the leketio, with colour blocks encoding age-grade.
- **Abalang goat-skin cloak** (*abalang*) — A supple, oiled and ochred goat- or calfskin cape worn over one shoulder, serving as sitting mat and child-carrier.
- **Ngaroto and emuya scarification** (*ngaroto, emuya*) — Ngaroto is raised keloid scarring in arcs and chevrons on the forehead and cheeks; emuya is larger cicatrice patterning on the belly, back and upper arms in dotted rows and lozenges, cut in stages at puberty, marriage and childbearing.

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress — beaded skirts, brass coils, and ochred hair — is the Boya's most distinctive visual signature.

- **Lopeta neck coils** (*lopeta*) — Stacked coiled brass rings worn around the neck in graduated tiers by women, producing sound when dancing.
- **Akiring cowrie headband** (*akiring*) — A narrow leather fillet with cowrie shells sewn in ranks, worn across the brow at dances and betrothals.
- **Emuron ochre hair pack** (*emuron*) — A paste of red ochre and animal fat worked into a married woman's hair and scalp, colouring and protecting the hair.
- **Lokoro spear** (*lokoro*) — An iron-bladed spear carried by adult men as dress and weapon.
- **Ekachil ivory arm ring** (*ekachil*) — An ivory or bone upper-arm bracelet marking a man who has killed large game or an enemy.

### Architecture

Boya settlements are hillside hamlets of round thatched huts with compounds fenced against stock and raiders.

- **Lomangoli round hut** (*lomangoli*) — A thatched tukul with a ring of timber uprights and mud-and-dung plastered walls, carrying a steep conical grass-thatch roof that sheds heavy rains.
- **Ebolo grass granary** (*ebolo*) — A raised basketry silo of woven grass and withies, capped with removable thatch, lifted on stone or timber footings to keep grain dry and safe from rodents.
- **Thorn-fenced kraal** (*kraal*) — A separate thorn-fenced cattle enclosure, often at the hamlet edge or on the plain below.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Household gear is dominated by gourd, wood, hide and basketry, with smithed iron for weapons and ornaments.

- **Achok gourd vessel** (*achok*) — A calabash cut, cured and sometimes bound with hide or fibre, used for milk, water, beer and grain and as the standard drinking vessel.
- **Ekicholong headrest stool** (*ekicholong*) — A single-piece carved billet with a curved saddle and one or two struts, worn slung from the wrist or belt and used as neck-rest at night to keep ochred hair off the ground.
- **Nyakolo winnowing tray** (*nyakolo*) — A shallow coiled or plaited grass fan used to toss threshed sorghum.
- **Lokoro spearhead** (*lokoro*) — A socketed iron spearhead made by smiths, along with knife blades, arrowheads and bracelet stock.
- **Ariau clay pipe** (*ariau*) — A short-bowled smoking pipe with a reed or wood stem, one of the few fired-clay forms in regular use.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment is cumulative and biographical, marking age, status and major life events through beads, coils, scarification and ochre.

- **Lopeta coiled brass ring** (*lopeta*) — Graduated tiers of brass coils worn around the neck by women, stacked to mark status and produce sound in dance.
- **Lokwalimoe ostrich-eggshell beads** (*lokwalimoe*) — Ostrich-eggshell discs ground and drilled flat and strung in ranks, the oldest bead form in the region, predating the glass trade beads now dominant.
- **Emong'o cattle-horn ornament** (*emong'o*) — Worked horn tips mounted as arm or head ornaments, referencing a man's favourite ox and worn as the material counterpart to ox-praise songs.
- **Ngaroto facial scarification** (*ngaroto*) — Raised keloid scars cut by specialist women in arcs and chevrons across the forehead, temples and cheeks at puberty.
- **Emuya belly and back cicatrice** (*emuya*) — A larger field of cicatrice patterning on the belly, back and upper arms in dotted rows and lozenges, marking marriage and childbearing.

## Music & performance

Performance is overwhelmingly vocal and percussive, centred on group song, handclapping and the sound of women's brass and bead ornaments.

- **Ox-praise song** (*ox song*) — Praise poems composed by a young man for his favourite ox, naming its colour, horn shape and lineage, the central lyric genre and vehicle for a man's poetic reputation.
- **Call-and-response group song** — Unaccompanied vocal performance led by a soloist and answered by a chorus of the same age-set, driven by handclapping and foot-stamping.
- **Women's lament singing** — The dominant funeral genre, sung by women over the dead.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is communal, age-graded and held in an open clearing, with women in full ornament stamping in lines while men leap vertically.

- **High jump-dance** — A standing vertical leap performed by men individually within a circle, read as a display of physical condition and marriageability, common to pastoral peoples of the Karamoja–Kapoeta belt.
- **Courtship dance** — The principal formal occasion on which unmarried men and women meet and assess each other, when women wear the lokwoto corset and akiring headband.
- **Harvest and rain-making dance** — Communal dances marking the sowing of sorghum and the harvest and threshing, with ox slaughter and sorghum beer.

## Festivals & rituals

The Boya calendar is agricultural and pastoral, centred on sowing and harvest, with life-cycle rites marking the visibility and status of the individual.

- **Rain-making rite** — The most important collective rite, conducted by hereditary rain specialists controlling rain stones and ancestral shrines, whose failure carries political cost.
- **Ngaroto scarification at puberty** (*ngaroto*) — The first cutting of facial scars at puberty, marking a person's passage into visible adulthood.
- **Marriage bridewealth** — Transfer of cattle between lineages, with the slaughter of animals for a feast as the public act; a young woman's beadwork and brass is bridewealth-financed and records the transaction.
- **Age-set initiation** — Binds men of a cohort for life and governs who dances, herds, raids and speaks together.
- **Funeral rite** — Burial within or beside the homestead, women's lament singing, slaughter of cattle for mourners, and a later gathering closing the mourning period.

## Foodways

Sorghum and maize are the staples, supplemented by milk, meat at occasions, hunted game, and sorghum beer as the medium of hospitality.

- **Sorghum porridge** — Sorghum ground on a saddle stone and cooked stiff, eaten by hand and dipped into relish of beans, wild greens or groundnut.
- **Sorghum beer** — Brewed by women from malted and fermented grain, drunk warm and thick from communal gourds, the medium of hospitality and collective labour.
- **Blood and milk** — Blood taken from a living beast and mixed with milk, a strengthening food eaten in cattle camps.
- **Nyakolo winnowing tray** (*nyakolo*) — Used to toss threshed sorghum before storage in the ebolo granary against the dry months.
- **Achok milk vessel** (*achok*) — Milk is drunk fresh or soured in achok gourds cured with smoke and ash, which sterilise the vessel and flavour the contents.

## Oral tradition & literature

Narim is an oral language with no historical literary tradition; the oral repertoire is origin narratives, genealogies, cattle-raiding accounts and ox-praise poetry.

- **Origin and migration narrative** — Narratives tracing the split of the Boya from the Didinga and the settlement of the Boya Hills.
- **Cattle-raiding narrative** — Stories naming specific raiding engagements with Toposa and Didinga neighbours.
- **Animal trickster tale** — Stories used for evening entertainment, part of the oral repertoire shared with other Surmic groups.
- **Ox-praise poem** (*ox song*) — Improvised lyric poetry composed by young men, the main literary form and vehicle for poetic reputation.
- **Proverb use** — Dense use of proverbs in dispute settlement and elders' speech.

## Language & religion

Narim is a Southwest Surmic language mutually intelligible with Didinga; the older religion centres on a high deity, ancestral spirits and specialists, with Christianity now widely professed alongside older practices.

- **Narim language** (*Narim, Larim, Boya*) — A Southwest Surmic language of the Eastern Sudanic branch, mutually intelligible to a substantial degree with Didinga, with no indigenous script.
- **High creator deity** — The central figure of the older religious system, addressed through lineage and homestead shrines.
- **Rain-maker specialist** — A hereditary religious specialist controlling rain stones and ancestral shrines, authority based on command of rain.
- **Diviner** — A specialist consulted over sickness, theft and the success of raids.
- **Catholic Christianity** — Widely professed through the Kimatong and Torit mission field, coexisting with rain rites, ancestral offerings and divination rather than displacing them.
