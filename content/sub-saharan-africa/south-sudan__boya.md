## Overview

The Boya are a small agro-pastoral people of the granite hills between Chukudum and Kapoeta who speak Narim, a language of the Surmic branch of Nilo-Saharan. They share close ties with the Didinga and are neighbours to Ateker-speaking peoples like the Toposa, with whom they have exchanged goods, words, and marriages for generations. Their livelihood combines hillside farming of sorghum, maize and groundnuts with cattle herding from seasonal camps on the plain. The hills are defensible, better watered than the surrounding area, and terraced for cultivation, making them the central fact of Boya culture.

## Material culture

Boya material culture is built from hide, bead, gourd, iron, horn and wood, with the decorated body as its organising principle rather than cloth or ceramic art.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Boya have no loom-weaving tradition; pattern instead lives on dressed hide, beadwork, cowries, and the skin itself through scarification.

- **Beaded corset** (*lokwoto*) — Close-fitting hide or fibre garment densely sewn with imported glass beads in horizontal colour bands, worn by girls and young women and renewed or elaborated at each change of status.
- **Beaded skirt** (*dolulu*) — Front-and-back leather skirt or apron with beadwork, cowries and metal concentrated on the lower register, cut and fringed so it swings and rattles in movement.
- **Coiled basketry and matting** (*nyakolo (winnowing tray)*) — Plaited and coiled grass trays, mats and granary liners with banding produced by alternating fibre colour; the winnowing tray is seen daily at every threshing floor.
- **Cowrie work** (*akiring (cowrie headband)*) — Cowrie shells from the Indian Ocean coast sewn in dense rows onto bands and skirt hems as both ornament and stored wealth.
- **Leather belt** (*leketio*) — Broad hide belt, often beaded and studded, worn at the waist to carry the skirt; the term is shared with neighbouring Ateker-speaking groups.

### Clothing & dress

Dress is graded sharply by sex and age, with married women in heavy beaded leather, girls in lighter beadwork and more ochre, and young men largely unclothed but heavily ornamented.

- **Women's leather skirt** — Two-piece hide skirt with the back panel longer than the front, scraped and softened with fat and ochre until supple, with beadwork and cowries concentrated along the hem.
- **Ochre hair pack** (*emuron*) — Mixture of red ochre, animal fat and sometimes ash worked into the hair and scalp to form a shaped, sun-hardened cap that is periodically rebuilt.
- **Goat-skin cloak** (*abalang*) — Dressed and oiled goat or sheep hide worn over one shoulder and knotted, serving as blanket at night and ground cover by day.
- **Ivory bracelet** (*ekachil*) — Arm or wrist ring cut from elephant ivory, historically a mark of a hunter or wealthy household; increasingly replaced by aluminium, plastic and horn.
- **Cattle-horn ornament** (*emong'o*) — Worked horn ornaments and pendants associated with cattle wealth and with individual named oxen.

### Architecture

Boya villages sit high on hill flanks terraced with dry-stone walls that hold both house platforms and sorghum plots, with no monumental architecture or specialised religious buildings.

- **Round thatched house** (*lomangoli (the thatch)*) — Mud-and-wattle cylinder with a steep grass-thatched conical roof tied to a timber frame; thatch is renewed communally.
- **Stone terracing** — Dry-stone retaining walls cut into the hillside to hold soil for sorghum and to level house platforms, the defining engineering of Boya settlement.
- **Grass granary** (*ebolo*) — Raised woven or daubed basket-granary set on stones or timber platform above ground, capped with a small thatch lid against rain and rodents.
- **Headrest stool** (*ekicholong*) — Carved from a single block of hardwood, this small stool serves as seat by day and pillow by night, protecting the ochred hair pack and closely identified with its owner.
- **Cattle enclosure** — Thorn-fenced kraal within or beside the compound, and seasonal camps on the plain where young men live with herds for months at a time.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The gourd rather than the pot is the signature container; pottery is made locally in modest amounts, and iron comes chiefly through smiths and trade.

- **Gourd vessel** (*achok*) — Calabash cut, dried and sometimes pyro-engraved or bound with beaded netting, used for milk, water, sorghum beer and butterfat, and given as marriage gift.
- **Iron spearhead** (*lokoro*) — Leaf-bladed socketed or tanged spearhead mounted on hardwood shaft, the principal weapon for herding, hunting and defence, and a standard item of bridewealth-adjacent exchange.
- **Hide shield** (*abwo*) — Oval or elongated shield of thick hide over a wooden spine, carried in dance and display as much as in fighting.
- **Clay pipe** (*ariau*) — Short-stemmed pipe of fired clay, often with a wooden or reed stem, used by men and older women for locally grown tobacco.
- **Grinding stone and winnowing tray** (*nyakolo*) — The paired tools of the sorghum economy: a saddle-shaped lower stone with hand-held rubber, and a coiled grass tray used to toss and separate grain from chaff.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Scarification is the central Boya art, with raised keloid rows cut and patterned across the torso to track age and status, complemented by beadwork, brass and ostrich-shell beads.

- **Patterned scarification** (*ngaroto*) — Registers of raised cicatrices worked in rows, arcs and chevrons across the torso, cut in stages across a woman's life rather than in a single sitting.
- **Body cicatrice** (*emuya*) — Individual raised keloid mark; the accumulated field of these is read locally as a record of endurance, status and beauty.
- **Coiled brass neck ring** (*lopeta*) — Heavy coiled brass or copper-alloy ring worn stacked at the neck, the metal acquired through trade and treated as portable wealth.
- **Ostrich eggshell disc beads** (*lokwalimoe*) — Small white discs ground and drilled from ostrich eggshell and strung in bands, one of the oldest bead technologies in East Africa and still valued against glass imports.
- **Lip and ear ornament** — Piercing of the lower lip and earlobe and helix rim for plugs, rings and short pins is reported in the region.

## Music & performance

Boya music is overwhelmingly vocal, communal and unaccompanied by melody instruments, with songs organised around cattle, work and collective contemporary events.

- **Ox praise song** — Solo or small-group song composed by a young man for his favourite ox, naming its colour pattern and horn shape; a principal genre of personal poetry.
- **Women's work song** — Call-and-response songs timed to grinding, threshing and winnowing, with the rhythm of the stone or tray as the pulse.
- **Cattle-camp song** — Herding and night-watch songs sung antiphonally by young men in the seasonal camps on the plain.
- **Drum** — Large single-headed hide drum, hand- or stick-beaten, that anchors dance gatherings; often the only constructed instrument present.
- **Horn trumpet** — End- or side-blown antelope or cattle horn used for signalling, for summoning to dance, and as a punctuating voice within it.

## Dance & theatre

Dance among the Boya is participatory rather than theatrical, happening in a ring of participants with no masking or professional performance, dominated by vertical leaping from flat feet.

- **Jumping dance** — Ring or facing-line dance in which participants leap vertically in turn, the height and control of the jump serving as display.
- **Courtship dance** — Evening gatherings, often at the cattle camps or after harvest, where young men and women assess one another with ochre, beadwork and fresh scarification prepared for the occasion.
- **Spear-and-shield display** — Men's dance with shield and spear, combining stamping advance and retreat with rhythmic striking of shield against shaft.
- **Wedding dance** — Multi-day dancing at the transfer of bridewealth and the bride's move to her husband's compound, involving both families' villages.
- **Mourning dance** — Slower, lower-bodied collective movement with wailing and drum at funerals, marking the passage of a senior person.

## Festivals & rituals

The Boya ritual year turns on rain and sorghum, with authority vested in rainmaking lineages and life-cycle ritual organised by age and cattle.

- **Rain ceremony** — Sacrifice and appeal conducted by a rainmaking lineage at the opening of the season, with the rainmaker credited for good years and pressured in bad ones.
- **First-fruits rite** — Ritual release of the new sorghum for general consumption, preceded by offering and by a prohibition on early eating.
- **Age-set promotion** — Graded initiation of young men through seclusion, instruction by elders and the slaughter of cattle, conferring the right to marry and speak in assembly.
- **Scarification rite** (*ngaroto*) — Staged cutting of a girl's or woman's torso at puberty, betrothal and after childbirth, performed by experienced female specialists.
- **Bridewealth transfer** — Negotiated handing over of cattle and small stock from groom's to bride's lineage, the central economic and political event linking families.

## Foodways

The Boya eat from two larders that rarely mix: sorghum, maize, groundnuts and other crops from the hills, and milk, butterfat and meat from the herds.

- **Sorghum porridge** — Sorghum ground on a saddle stone into flour and boiled to a stiff porridge eaten with relish of wild greens, pounded groundnut, sesame paste or goat.
- **Sorghum beer** — Brewed by women in clay pots and gourds, drunk collectively from a shared vessel through reeds or dipped bowls; a household's brewing capacity is a measure of its ability to mobilise labour and goodwill.
- **Gourd vessel** (*achok*) — Serves as churn, cup, store and measure in the kitchen and dairy.
- **Wild resources** — Honey from log and rock hives, shea and oil-bearing fruits, tamarind, wild greens gathered by women, and small game and fish taken opportunistically.
- **Clay pipe** (*ariau*) — Closes a meal; used by men and older women for locally grown tobacco.

## Oral tradition & literature

Narim was unwritten until the twentieth century, and Boya verbal art is oral, performed and functional, including origin narratives, migration histories, trickster tales, proverbs and personal ox-praise poems.

- **Ox-praise poem** — Personal lyric composed by a named individual about a named animal and sung by him alone; the closest thing to lyric authorship in the culture.
- **Clan origin narrative** — Story explaining the move into the hills and the relationship with the Didinga.
- **Animal trickster tale** — Evening story told to children in which the hare and hyena commonly occupy the clever and greedy roles familiar across the region.
- **Proverb** — Saying deployed in dispute settlement, where the elder who can produce the apt proverb often carries the argument.
- **Genealogy** — Recited to establish standing in bridewealth negotiations and land disputes.

## Language & religion

Narim belongs to the South-West Surmic group within Nilo-Saharan, closely related to Didinga with high mutual intelligibility; religion in practice is layered between older ancestral belief and Christian and traditional systems coexisting.

- **Narim language** (*Longarim, Boya*) — Tonal South-West Surmic language with complex verbal morphology, spoken by tens of thousands; Juba Arabic serves as the regional lingua franca.
- **Sky-and-rain divinity** — Older stratum of religious belief centred on a sky and rain divinity.
- **Ancestral shades** — Ancestors who remain implicated in the fortunes of their descendants.
- **Rainmaker** — Specialist from a rainmaking lineage who diagnoses misfortune and prescribes sacrifice, usually of a goat or ox at a kraal or named tree.
- **Christianity** — Spread substantially since the mid-twentieth century through Catholic and Protestant missionary work; coexists with ancestral practice and divination in most households.
