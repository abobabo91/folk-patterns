## Overview

The Tsonga are linked by language, trade, traditional leadership and shared clan histories. Their culture includes initiation, healing, dance, music, stories and farming. They make useful and decorative objects from wood, fibre, reeds, pottery, metal and gourds. Many Tsonga people now work in Mozambique and South Africa.

## Material culture

Tsonga material culture includes fibre skirts, reed mats, pottery, metal tools, carved wood, beadwork and decorated gourds.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources show fibre, bead and textile objects, but no named Tsonga textile tradition or pattern system.

- **Fibre-cord skirts** — These skirts are made from fibre cords, sometimes with blue and white beads below the girdle.
- **Waist-cloth** — A length of cotton textile was used as a male figure’s waist-cloth.
- **Reed mats** — A catalogue record identifies a mat made from reeds.
- **Beaded drinking vessel** — A gourd drinking vessel is decorated with burnt figures and white glass beads.
- **Incised gourd decoration** — Some gourds have white bead inlay and black geometric lines, animal figures or a pokerwork motif.

### Clothing & dress

The sources record a few skirts and a waist-cloth.

- **Girl’s fibre skirt** — A girl’s skirt is made of fibre cords.
- **Beaded fibre skirt** — One skirt has blue and white beads below the girdle and a leather thong at one end.
- **Grass or palm-leaf skirt** — Another skirt is made of grass or palm leaf strung on a fibre cord.
- **Cotton waist-cloth** — A male wooden figure is accompanied by cotton textile used as a waist-cloth.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Tsonga everyday objects include pottery, tools, carved wood, baskets and decorated gourds.

- **Burnished pottery vessel** — One vessel has an everted rim, a red burnished body and graphite burnished decoration.
- **Black-necked pottery vessel** — Another vessel has a red burnished body, a black burnished neck and a projecting lip.
- **Ceremonial battleaxe** — A ceremonial battleaxe has a wooden shaft.
- **Corrugated-blade knife** — An iron knife has a corrugated blade, wooden handle and sheath.
- **Reed and palm-leaf basket** — A food basket is made from reed and palm leaf.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment includes carved necklaces, metal bangles, bead bracelets, hair ornaments and snuff objects.

- **Carved wooden necklace** — A wooden necklace has carved bi-conical beads and six small antelope horns.
- **Metal bangles** — Metal bangles have incised linear designs on their inner and outer rims.
- **Fibre and bead bracelets** — Some bracelets are made of fibre and beads.
- **Maturity and marriage staff** (*isicoco*) — A Cleveland Museum staff has carved male heads and an ornament linked with maturity and marriage, while isicoco is the associated hairdo made with a fibre or sinew ring.
- **Bone divination tablet** — A bone tablet was used for divination.

## Music & performance

Tsonga music ranges from traditional dance music to disco, ndzhumbha and Shangaan electro.

- **Traditional dance music** — Traditional dance music was associated with General MD Shirinda, Fanny Mpfumo, Matshwa Bemuda and Thomas Chauke.
- **Tsonga disco** — Tsonga disco was popularized by Joe Shirimani, Penny Penny, Peta Teanet and Benny Mayengani.
- **Tsonga ndzhumbha** (*ndzhumbha*) — Tsonga ndzhumbha was also popularized by Joe Shirimani, Penny Penny, Peta Teanet and Benny Mayengani.
- **Shangaan electro** — This European style uses English words, sampled vocals and heavy synthesizers.
- **Trough zither** — A seven-string zither is made from a rectangular tray and vegetable fibre strings, with copper and brass wire decoration.

## Festivals & rituals

Tsonga ritual life includes male and female initiation schools and initiation into divination.

- **Men’s circumcision school** (*Ngoma; Hogo*) — Tsonga men attend Ngoma, also called Hogo, for circumcision and are then regarded as men.
- **Girls’ initiation school** (*Khomba; tikhomba*) — Older Vatsonga women lead Khomba for teenage girls called tikhomba, who learn about womanhood, conduct and marriage.
- **Diviner’s water initiation** (*Nzunzu*) — During diviner initiation, initiates are ceremoniously submerged in water in a re-enactment of the story of the water serpent Nzunzu.

## Foodways

Tsonga foodways center on mixed agriculture and pastoralism, with cassava as the staple.

- **Cassava farming** — Cassava is the main staple crop.
- **Mixed crop farming** — People also grow corn, millet, sorghum and other crops.
- **Pastoral care** — Men and teenage boys care for cows, sheep and goats.
- **Food basket** — A British Museum record describes a food basket made from reed and palm leaf.
- **Gourd drinking vessel** — A decorated gourd vessel is recorded, but its contents are not specified.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tsonga oral tradition includes folktales, songs, riddles and proverbs recorded in published works.

- **Lenge folktales** — E. Dora Earthy published Tsonga folktales from the Lenge dialect with English translations in 1937.
- **Tsonga song lyrics** — Henri-Alexandre Junod published Tsonga song lyrics with English translations in 1913.
- **Unborn-babe riddle** — One riddle asks what a mother loves but cannot run to meet when she returns home, with the answer being the unborn babe in the womb.
- **Water riddle** — Another riddle asks what can be beaten without leaving a scar, with the answer being water.
- **Crocodile proverb** — One proverb says that the crocodile’s strength is water.

## Language & religion

XiTsonga is a standardized Bantu language, and Tsonga religious practice centers on ancestors, spirits, divination and healing.

- **XiTsonga** — XiTsonga is spoken in Mozambique and South Africa, uses the Latin alphabet and is an official language of South Africa.
- **Tswa-Ronga** — Tsonga can be used as a cover term for Tswa, Ronga and related speech forms.
- **Tsonga dialects** (*xiRonga; xiHlanganu; xiBila; xiDjonga; xiN’walungu; xiHlengwe*) — Named dialects include xiRonga, xiHlanganu, xiBila, xiDjonga, xiN’walungu and xiHlengwe.
- **Traditional healer** (*n’anga*) — Traditional healers are called n’anga.
- **Ancestor belief** — Tsonga people believe ancestors can affect the lives of their descendants.
