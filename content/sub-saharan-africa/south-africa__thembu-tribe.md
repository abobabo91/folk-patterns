## Overview

The Thembu, or *AbaThembu*, are an isiXhosa-speaking nation historically associated with Thembuland. They emerged as a political entity during the reign of Nxeko, who settled in Dedesi in the present-day Eastern Cape region of South Africa. Thembu territory later extended through places including Msana, Mthatha, Qacha's Nek, and areas of KwaZulu-Natal. Before British conquest in the 19th century, the Thembu had an independent kingdom. Their history includes political integration with groups such as amaBomvana, amaVundle, amaMpondomise, and amaMfengu, as well as connections with the wider Xhosa-speaking frontier. Their folk-cultural significance in the supplied sources lies chiefly in oral history, praise traditions, clan identity, kingship, cattle symbolism, and historical migration.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The supplied sources name no textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources mention a “popular costume displaying royal authority” in connection with the exchange of the *inxaxheba*, the right hindquarter of a slaughtered cow. They also state that amaQwathi gave Mngxongo cattle that were all black, and that this association is preserved in *iziduko zamaNgxongo Ntsundu*, referring to the cattle’s colors. No further information is supplied about everyday clothing, ceremonial garments, head coverings, belts, footwear, or distinctions between men’s and women’s dress.

### Architecture

The great house of AbaThembu is called Eastern Thembu and is associated with Bumbane Great Palace in Mthata, Eastern Cape. The right-hand house is called Western Thembu and is associated with Qamata Great Palace in Cofimvaba, Eastern Cape. The sources describe these as political and royal houses, with the Eastern Thembus identified as the great house and the Western Thembus as the right-hand house.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention cattle, spears, small axes, sticks, clubs, and the inxaxheba.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They mention praise names and royal authority.

## Music & performance

They do, however, describe oral praise and expressions associated with Thembu identity. The Thembus are said to praise a phrase called *Sopitso* or *Yem-Yem*, glossed in the source as “ǀXam - ǀXam,” and connected with the Madiba, Dlomo, Nxongo, and Qwithi people. The sources also record the praise name *Aaǃ Zwelibanziǃ* for King Buyelekhaya Dalindyebo, while another passage gives his praise name as Zwelibanzi.

The history of Bhomoyi records the phrase *VelaBembhentsele*, said to have arisen after Bhomoyi received fifteen Basotho women following a dispute over land at Qacha's Nek. The sources further mention the war drum as a means by which warriors joined AbaThembu regiments during the conflict with the Basotho. No information is supplied about the construction of instruments, musical scales, performance settings, or musicians.

## Dance & theatre

They describe historical gatherings, royal praise, warfare, initiation-related references, and the presentation of cattle tribute, but do not characterize these as dance or theatre traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe several political, social, and ritual practices. AmaQwathi were named after a cow used at the graduation of Mtshutshumbe from initiation school. The sources also refer to the use of cattle tribute in exchange for land and to the allocation of sites to AmaMfene.

Cattle slaughter and the distribution of the *inxaxheba*, the right hindquarter, are presented as significant practices within royal kinship relations. Dlomo sent the inxaxheba to his junior Ndungwana, while Hlanga sent his *nxaxheba* to his junior Balisa. The sources describe the latter as a popular costume displaying royal authority. The account also records the arrival of amaTola, led by Mkhuma and his brother, who introduced the use of spears to abaThembu.

## Foodways

The sources mention cattle, the slaughtering of cows, and the distribution of the right hindquarter, called the *inxaxheba* or *nxaxheba*. They also describe cattle as tribute in exchanges involving land. During the conflict involving Ngubengcuka and the Basotho, cattle were raided and later recovered. The forest was renamed Nduku, meaning “stick,” in commemoration of the sticks used to drive the Basotho from the forest.

The sources mention fifteen Basotho women in the account of Bhomoyi.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral tradition is central to the supplied account of Thembu origins and history. According to Xhosa oral tradition, the Hala clan migrated along the east coast of southern Africa before settling in KwaZulu-Natal. The earliest known Thembu ancestor is identified as Chief Mbulali Ka-Nanzinzaba, whose grandson, uMthembu KaNtongakazi, led his people to Dedesi in the present-day Eastern Cape region.

The sources preserve genealogical narratives involving Mthembu, Ndilo, Mvelase, Ntongakazi, Bhomoyi, Mncotshe, Mdlane, Qoma, Jali, Mguti, Mngxongo, Hlanga, Dlomo, Balisa, Ndungwana, and other figures. They also recount the stories of Khazeka, described as one of two women remembered for fearlessness and victories in unifying and stabilizing the kingdom, and of Welangaye, a maiden from the Mpemvu clan.

Praise expressions, clan names, royal genealogies, and explanations of place names form a major part of the recorded oral tradition.

## Language & religion

The Thembu are described as an isiXhosa-speaking nation. In Xhosa, the name is *abaThembu*, with *aba-* described as a common prefix implying “people.” The sources also state that the Thembu were known as “ǀXam bu ǃe,” “ǀXam,” and “the Embo people,” and that *ǀXam bu ǃe* translates to “Thembu.” *AbaThembu* is further connected in the source with *ǀXam ka ǃue*, while *ǀXam* is glossed as “an oryx or an antelope.” The original name is given as *ǀXam ka ǃeten*, translated as “Antelope water people.”

The sources state that AbaThembu spoke ǀXam or ǀXam ke, described as an extinct language, and that the original Thembus spoke five to six dialects.
