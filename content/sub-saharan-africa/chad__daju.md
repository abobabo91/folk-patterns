## Overview

The Daju are a group of seven distinct ethnicities speaking related languages across isolated pockets of Sudan and Chad. Their traditional area is the Daju Hills in the southern Marrah Mountains of Darfur, while their historical and present settlements extend through Chad, the Nuba Mountains, Kordofan, and parts of South Sudan. The Daju languages belong to the Eastern Sudanic subfamily of Nilo-Saharan. The groups differ considerably: distance and separate languages have produced little general cultural affinity among them. In Chad, the Dar Daju Daju live in the Guéra Region, while the Dar Sila Daju live in southern Chad in the Ouaddai region. Daju culture documented in the sources includes grain farming, hunting, gathering, distinctive house forms, body tattooing, clan organization, and religious practices combining Islam with older customs.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do record body tattooing: women traditionally tattooed their eyelids, gums, and lips with acacia thorns, while fighters tattooed the peaks of their left hands with sacred black and red ink.

### Architecture

The typical Daju home is round with a cone roof. In towns, houses are often rectangular. Women are the primary house-builders, and community chores are shared.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do mention straw shrines dedicated to the high god Kalge among the Dar Daju Daju and Dar Sila Daju.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The documented forms of Daju body adornment are tattoo practices rather than jewelry. Women traditionally tattooed their eyelids, gums, and lips with acacia thorns. Fighters tattooed the peaks of their left hands with sacred black and red ink.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document religious customs among the Dar Daju Daju and Dar Sila Daju, who are predominantly Muslim while continuing traditional practices. These include the construction of straw shrines for their high god Kalge, whom they equate with Allah in Islam. The name *Par-Kalge* is derived from Kalge and refers to a sacred mountain near Napta. The Dar Fur Daju maintain their old and original religion. Islam became the religion of most Daju by the 15th century, although the sources state that it likely spread significantly before that date.

## Foodways

The Daju are primarily grain farmers, cultivating mainly millet, sorghum, and corn. They also hunt and gather, especially honey, berries, and wild fruits. Women perform much of the food-related daily labor: they plant and sow crops, grind grain, and cook meals.

## Oral tradition & literature

Historical accounts do preserve traditions concerning migration, rulers, and kingdoms. One account identifies the Daju prophet Saleh, who died and was buried beside Wadi Saleh in the southwestern corner of the Marrah Mountains. The sources also mention a list of kings and references to the Daju kingdom in Egyptian texts.

## Language & religion

The Daju languages are spoken in isolated areas of Sudan and Chad, including Kordofan, Darfur, and Wadai. They belong to the Eastern Sudanic subfamily of Nilo-Saharan. The languages are divided into Eastern and Western branches; the Eastern languages separated from the others perhaps as much as 2,000 years ago, while the Western languages spread more recently. Most Daju languages have subject–verb–object word order, with exceptions such as Sila, and use possessed–possessor order. The Dar Daju Daju and Dar Sila Daju are predominantly Muslim but retain traditional religious customs, including shrines to Kalge. The Dar Fur Daju maintain their older religion.
