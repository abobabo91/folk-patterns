## Overview

The Papel, also known as Moium, Oium, Papei, Pepel or Pelels, are an ethnic group primarily located in Guinea-Bissau, with communities also found in Casamance in Senegal and Guinea. Their population in Guinea-Bissau is about 183,000, with 9,000 living outside the country. They traditionally engaged in hunting and agriculture and are traditionally farmers. They live around the city of Bissau, in the Biombo Region, and are linguistically and culturally close to the Mankagnes and Manjack or Manjacas. The Papel language is a Bak language of Guinea-Bissau and belongs to the Bak language family. Papel is spoken in the central coastal regions, especially the Biombo Region, and uses a Latin-based script.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Six-strip cotton cloth** — The British Museum records several textiles made from six narrow strip-woven cotton lengths sewn together selvedge to selvedge. Their ground weaves include plain weave, with dyed black, white, blue, yellow, red and orange cotton warps and wefts. Some examples use supplementary weft, supplementary weft floats, unworked warps, and silver tinsel or plastic.

**Black-and-white checked textile** — One recorded textile has a white background crossed by warp and weft stripes, producing a regular grid resembling tartan. The central panel contains groups of three obliques in black supplementary weft, while the ends have a diamond-based pattern.

**Blue textile with coloured checks** — Another textile has a dark blue background, white bands, and white, yellow, red and green check bands at each end. A band of oblique dashes in the same colours appears at regular intervals.

**Diamond-patterned textile** — One garment has a central black-and-white square grid. Each grid segment contains five diamonds: a large central diamond and a small diamond in each corner, worked in dyed black cotton supplementary weft.

**Black and green textile with silver lurex** — A narrow strip of woven cotton cloth has a black warp and alternate black and green weft squares. Each square contains a complex geometric float-weave pattern in silver lurex.

**Coloured striped textiles** — The records include cloths with yellow and black warp stripes, red, blue and black warps and wefts, and alternating yellow, red and white warp stripes crossed by black weft stripes. Combinations of warp and weft colours create shades rather than solid blocks.

**Motif vocabulary.** diamonds, squares, triangles, oblique dashes, checks, grid, stripes.

### Clothing & dress

The British Museum records a cotton garment worn by men and women. It consists of six strips of woven cotton cloth sewn loosely together selvedge to selvedge. The textile uses white and dyed black warps and wefts and is divided into three decorative bands. Its central band has a black-and-white square grid with diamond decoration, while the bands at either end have additional patterned decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The British Museum records an iron edge-blade for a hoe and an iron adze-blade.

## Music & performance

The Papel article mentions war dances during the conflict with the Portuguese, but gives no instrument names, musical forms, or performance details.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention war dances. In 1891, after the killing of a Cape Verdean who had left a fortress, Papel forces were reported to have gathered at the top of Intim, celebrated the killing with war dances, and made preparations for war.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe historical kingship, conversion, succession, warfare, and burial.

According to oral history, Mecau, the son of a king from the Kingdom of Quinara, arrived at Bissau during a hunting trip, settled there because of its fertile soil, and established a kingdom. From Quinara he brought his pregnant sister Punguenhum and six wives: Intende, Djokom, Mala, Intsoma, Kliker and Intchipolo. Seven main Papel clans arose from Punguenhum and the six wives.

The Kingdom of Bissau and vassal states in Biombo used matrilineal succession. The son of the sovereign’s maternal sister was next in line for kingship, or succession passed to the nearest male relative in the king’s maternal grandparents’ line. Individuals inherited their mother’s clan name, although later the father’s name could also be taken.

The sources describe Catholic conversion in the historical period. King Bacompol Co converted to Catholicism in April 1694 and became the first Christian king of Bissau. In 1696, after King N’Kanande Ka was captured, he was sentenced to death, tortured and buried alive, while his pregnant wife was shot.

## Foodways

Agriculture and hunting are identified as traditional activities. The historical account mentions wax, ivory, slaves and Kola among goods traded with Europeans. In April 1699, an Afro-Portuguese trader named Maria Soares returned from Sierra Leone to Bissau with a cargo full of Kola.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Papel historical account includes an origin narrative transmitted through oral history. Mecau, the son of a king from Quinara, came to Bissau during a hunting trip, chose to remain there because of the fertile soil, and established a kingdom. His sister Punguenhum and his six wives became the ancestral lines of seven main Papel clans.

The sources also preserve clan associations. The N’nssassun clan is represented by the Leopard; the N’nsso by the Frog; the N’ndjukomo by the Hyena; the N’nsafinte by the hare; the N’niga by the Goat; the N’nssuzu by the Aardvark; and the N’nttat by the Monkey.

## Language & religion

Papel, also called Pepel, Papei, Oium or Moium, is a Bak language of Guinea-Bissau. It is spoken by the Papel people in the central coastal regions, especially the Biombo Region, where it is spoken by 136,000 Bissau-Guineans. Papel speakers are estimated to number around 140,000 globally, while 79,000 speakers live on Bissau Island, called *(b)uhlawʔ* or *(b)usawʔ* in Papel. The dialects include Biombo, *uyomʔ*, in the southwest and Safim, *safli*, in the northeast. Papel is linguistically similar to Mankanya and Mandjak and uses a Latin-based script.

The historical sources describe Catholic missionary activity and conversion alongside local religious practices. From 1683 to 1686, three Catholic missionaries oversaw a church in the kingdom. The Papel referred to the Portuguese as *brancos*, meaning “whites,” and other Europeans as *estrangeiros*, meaning “strangers.” The sources also refer to *Xina*, described as Animist images, and record disputes over attempts to remove them from Christian settlements.
