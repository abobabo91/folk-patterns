## Overview

The Kongo (*Bakongo*, singular *Mukongo*; in Kikongo *Bisi Kongo* or *EsiKongo*) are a Bantu people defined primarily as speakers of Kikongo, living along the Atlantic coast of Central Africa across three modern states: southwest of Pool Malebo and west of the Kwango River in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, south of Pointe-Noire in the Republic of the Congo, north of Luanda in Angola, and into southwest Gabon. A 1975 count gave 4,040,000; they are the largest ethnic group in the Republic of the Congo and a major one in the other two. Subgroups include the Yombe, Vili, Woyo, Bembe, Sundi, Bwende, Dondo and Lari. Their historical weight is exceptional: the Kingdom of Kongo, founded in the fourteenth century with its capital at Mbanza Kongo, received Portuguese ships in 1483, adopted Catholicism from 1491, and became the corridor through which more than 5.6 million captives were shipped to the Americas — so Kongo visual and ritual vocabulary is embedded in Cuban Palo Mayombe, Brazilian Candomblé Bantu, Haitian Vodou, Jamaican Kumina and African-American Hoodoo.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Raphia cloth (*mbadi*, *nteva*)** — Woven on a single-heddle vertical loom from the split leaflets of *Raphia vinifera* palm, producing square panels roughly the width of a man's outstretched hand. Kongo raphia was a currency as well as a garment: standardized cloth units circulated alongside the *nzimbu* shell money of the kingdom. Kongo weaving is distinguished from the neighbouring Kuba tradition by a preference for flat, tightly interlaced geometric fields and cut-pile borders rather than the all-over embroidered plush of Kuba *shoowa*.

**Kongo plush and cut-pile mats (*mbala*)** — Raphia pile cloth in which the weaver knots supplementary threads through the ground weave, then shears them flat, producing a velvet surface. Seventeenth-century examples reached European cabinets of curiosity as "Congo velvets" and are among the earliest sub-Saharan textiles in Western collections.

**Prestige cloth of the Loango coast** — The *Vili* and *Woyo* weavers of the coastal kingdoms of Loango and Kakongo produced fine interlaced panels traded north and south along the Atlantic littoral; their tight diamond lattices appear in the same Portuguese inventories that record Kongo ivories.

**Mpu cap (*mpu*)** — The signature Kongo regalia textile: a close-fitting knotted or looped cap made by a needle-looping technique from raphia or pineapple fibre, worked in relief so that lozenges, spirals, feline claws and interlace stand proud of the surface. The *mpu* was the insignia of a chief, *nganga* or titleholder; its openwork projections were read as claws and leopard reference. No neighbouring people made a comparable looped fibre crown.

**Woyo pot lids (*taampha*)** — Not a textile but the closest Kongo analogue to a pattern lexicon: carved wooden lids whose relief figures encode proverbs, presented by a husband or wife at a meal to make an argument without speech. The motif repertoire on these lids overlaps directly with that on textiles and caps.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Dikenga* / Kongo cosmogram — the quartered circle or cross marking the four moments of the sun and the boundary (*kalunga*) between living and dead; interlace and knot patterns signifying binding, obligation and the sealing of an oath; the lozenge or diamond, a compressed form of the cosmogram; feline claws and leopard rosettes for chiefly power; the coiled spiral for the ancestral return; concentric circles for the water-world of the *simbi*; chevron registers; the *nkisi* nail-field, a deliberately non-repeating pattern of driven iron.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress before the twentieth century centred on the raphia wrapper worn from the waist, knotted at the hip for men and passed under the arms or over one shoulder for women; length and fineness of weave, not cut, marked rank. Chiefly and titled dress added layered wrappers, a leopard skin or leopard-tooth necklace, a fly-whisk, a staff of office, and above all the *mpu* cap, which no untitled person could wear. Coastal Kongo nobility absorbed Portuguese court dress from the early sixteenth century — the manikongo and his court wore imported velvet, brocade, hats and shoes, and Kongo envoys appeared at Lisbon in European costume — so Kongo elite dress is one of the earliest documented African-European hybrid wardrobes. Insignia of the Christian kingdom were worn as body ornament: brass and bronze crucifixes (*nkangi kiditu*), medallions of Saint Anthony (*toni malau*), and rosaries were carried as chiefly and juridical emblems, held during oath-taking and investiture. Women's coiffure was elaborately built over a fibre armature and dressed with palm oil and red *nkula* camwood powder, the red pigment itself a ritual substance signalling transition.

### Architecture

Kongo villages were laid out as clusters of rectangular houses of light construction: a frame of poles, walls of raphia or palm matting lashed to the frame or of wattle daubed with clay, and a steeply pitched gabled or hipped roof thatched in palm and grass. Interiors were partitioned with mats; the whole building was a woven object, and Portuguese observers in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries remarked that walls, floors and ceilings were mat-lined like tapestry. The capital *Mbanza Kongo*, some 200 kilometres inland from the Atlantic, was a genuine city with a royal enclosure, ward divisions for nobility and foreigners, and — after conversion — masonry churches; after the third Kongo-Portuguese war the old capital, renamed São Salvador, was burnt and lay in ruins by 1678, and its remains are today the core of the Mbanza Kongo World Heritage site. The village cemetery is the other built statement: Kongo graves were raised mounds crowned with inverted vessels, imported ceramics, glass and later concrete or brick funerary monuments, and in the Boma and Mbanza-Ngungu area with *mintadi* stone figures — a mortuary architecture continuous from the seventeenth century to the present.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Kongo potters, women, built vessels by coil and paddle and fired them in open bonfires; grave-top pots were pierced or inverted to release the spirit. The great Kongo sculptural corpus is in wood and mixed media: the *nkisi* (plural *minkisi*), a container — figure, bundle, shell, gourd — charged by an *nganga* with earths, relics and medicines behind a resin-sealed mirror belly-pack. The best-known class is the *nkondi* ("hunter") and among them the monumental *mangaaka* figures of the Yombe and Vili, standing with fists on hips and jaw thrust forward, their torsos driven with iron nails and blades, each one the physical record of an oath sworn or a grievance sealed. The *nduda* is a smaller nail-figure for pursuing wrongdoers. Kongo ironworkers were noted smelters and smiths, forging sword blades for the kingdom's armies, and Kongo ivory carvers produced spiral-carved oliphants and, later, the tusks of the nineteenth-century Loango coast. *Mintadi* (singular *ntadi*), soapstone figures of chiefs seated in thinking postures, are carved in the Mbanza-Ngungu region and set on graves.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Chiefly ornament ran to leopard teeth and claws strung as necklaces, brass and copper armlets and anklets, iron bracelets forged by the smith as marks of office, and shell — the *nzimbu* cowrie-like shell that served as the kingdom's currency doubled as ornament and as ritual deposit. Christian metalwork entered the ornament repertoire permanently: cast brass *nkangi kiditu* crucifixes, often with small orant figures flanking the corpus, and *toni malau* Saint Anthony figurines, worn and carried as insignia by chiefs, *nganga* and judges. Filed teeth and body scarification in registers of raised keloid dots across the abdomen, back and temples marked initiation and beauty, and the scarification patterns on the bodies of *pfemba* maternity figures are the ethnographic record of that practice. Red camwood powder (*nkula*) and white kaolin (*mpemba*) — the colour of the dead and of the *simbi* water spirits — were applied to the body at initiation, mourning and divination.

## Music & performance

Kongo musical life is built on drum ensembles and the sounded voice of authority. The double iron bell (*ngongi*, *dibu*) and the slit wooden gong (*koko*) carry chiefly and announcement functions; single-headed and double-headed drums (*ngoma*, *ndungu*) supply the polyrhythmic base; friction and mirliton drums produce the growling voice associated with initiation societies. Melodic instruments include the raphia-and-gourd lamellophone (*sanza*/*likembe*, the latter spreading through the Congo basin in the late nineteenth century), the *nsambi* plucked bow-lute, the arched harp of the Kongo-Gabon border zone, and side-blown ivory and antelope-horn trumpets (*mpungi*) sounded for the chief. Song is antiphonal — a soloist against a responding chorus — and organized by function: divination songs for the *nganga*, hunting songs, work songs for paddling and palm-cutting, mourning songs (*makinu ma lufwa*) and *kinsamba* praise. The Kongo also composed explicitly historical songs: *Malele* ("Tragedy") is one of seventeen Kongo songs still sung by the Massembo family of Guadeloupe at the *Grap a Kongo*, originating as a warning of the arrival of the Portuguese — a four-century transatlantic survival. In the Americas the same repertoire became the *palo* and *yuka* drumming of Cuba, Jamaican Kumina and the *makuta* of Brazilian Candomblé Bantu.

## Dance & theatre

Kongo dance is largely ritual and corporate rather than theatrical. Initiation societies staged masked performance: the *ndunga* masqueraders of the Woyo, a policing and judicial society whose masked dancers with helmet masks in white kaolin and red pigment appeared at funerals of notables and at the enforcement of chiefly interdictions; and *kimpasi*, a Kongo initiation association in which novices "died" and were reborn, its dances marking the crossing of *kalunga*. Funeral dancing is the great public form — night-long drumming and circle dance at the wake of a notable, culminating in procession to the grave. Dance vocabulary emphasizes the pelvis and a low, driving hip articulation, and the *yuka* and *makuta* dances of Cuba and *bomba*-adjacent forms in the Caribbean derive directly from it. Twentieth-century Kikongo-language popular music, and the *ndombolo* and *soukous* dance idioms of Kinshasa and Brazzaville, grew in Kongo-speaking urban milieux from these roots. Dona Beatriz Kimpa Vita's Antonian movement (1704–1706) staged its own processional performance, marching thousands of followers into the ruins of the old capital.

## Festivals & rituals

The Kongo calendar is a four-day week (the days *Konzo*, *Nkenge*, *Nsona*, *Nkandu*) rather than an annual cycle, and market days, prohibitions and burial days are set by it; the same four-part division maps onto the *dikenga* cosmogram. Life-cycle rite dominates the ritual year. Birth is followed by seclusion of mother and child and by the naming that places a child in a matrilineage (*kanda*); twins (*nsimba* and *nzuzi*) receive particular honour and their own shrine observances. *Kimpasi* initiation, revived repeatedly in periods of crisis, took adolescents into a bush enclosure where they were ritually killed and resurrected, whitened with *mpemba* kaolin, taught a secret language, and returned with new names. Funerals of chiefs and notables are the largest gatherings: the body lies in state wrapped in accumulated layers of cloth — nineteenth-century Bwende and Kongo practice produced the enormous bound funerary bundle (*niombo* and *mumbi*), a textile-wrapped effigy of the deceased carried by dozens of men and interred with drumming and gunfire. Investiture of a chief (*lubuku*, the taking of the *mpu*) required seclusion, the conferring of the cap and iron bracelet, and an oath before a *nkisi*. Catholicism, present since 1491, folded All Souls' Day, Christmas and saints' feasts into this frame; Swedish Protestant missions arriving in the 1880s added their own calendar in the northeast.

## Foodways

The Kongo were, in the judgement of the European slave traders who valued them for it, expert agriculturalists — multiple crops, livestock, fishing, hunting, bushcraft and slash-and-burn cultivation. The starch base is cassava, adopted from the Americas in the sixteenth century and now absolute: fermented and pounded into *fufu* or *luku*, or soaked, wrapped in leaves and steamed into the dense cylindrical loaf called *kwanga* or *chikwangue*, the Kongo travelling food. Cassava leaves pounded with palm oil, groundnut and dried fish or smoked meat make *saka-saka* (*mpondu*), the everyday relish. Other staples are plantain, yam, taro, maize, groundnut and *bambara* groundnut. Red palm oil is the defining fat, and *maluvu* — palm wine tapped from the raphia or oil palm — is the defining drink, poured as libation to the ancestors before any is consumed and indispensable at bridewealth negotiation, investiture and funeral. Signature dishes include *moambe* (*poulet à la moambe*), chicken stewed in palm-nut pulp with chilli, and *liboke* / *maboke*, fish or meat seasoned and steamed in banana or *marantaceae* leaf packets over coals. Grubs, caterpillars and freshwater fish from the Congo and its tributaries are ordinary protein; goat is slaughtered for weddings and funerals. Salt and dried fish were long-distance trade goods moving inland from the Atlantic ports on the same routes as cloth.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kongo oral art is organized around the proverb (*kingana*, plural *bingana*) and the tale (*nsamu*, *nkingu*), performed in the evening with sung refrains the audience answers. The proverb is unusually materialized here: the Woyo *taampha* pot lid carves a proverb into relief figures so that a domestic grievance can be stated silently at the table, and proverb imagery likewise governs the postures of *mintadi* and the attributes of *minkisi*. Tales turn on the trickster, on the journey across the water to the land of the dead, and on the *simbi* spirits of pools and rapids; divination and judicial speech (*mambu*) are formal genres in their own right. The written record is precocious and largely Kongo-authored: the manikongo Afonso I wrote to the king of Portugal in the 1510s and 1520s protesting the seizure of his people, giving Kongo one of the earliest African epistolary archives. In the 1880s–1900s the Swedish missionary **Karl Laman** asked Kongo catechists to write their own history and customs in notebooks — roughly 400 of them survive and remain the single richest indigenous ethnographic source for any Central African people, underpinning Laman's four-volume *The Kongo* and his Kikongo dictionary.

## Language & religion

Kikongo (Guthrie Bantu zone H.10) is a Bantu macrolanguage comprising Beembe, Doondo, Koongo, Laari, Kongo-San-Salvador, Kunyi, Vili and Yombe; dialect distance is real, and speakers of coastal Kivili and central Kisansolo have difficulty understanding one another. Kituba (*Kikongo ya Leta*), a Kikongo-based creole, is a regional lingua franca alongside Lingala, French and Portuguese. Kikongo was written in Latin script by missionaries and Kongo literates from the sixteenth century. Religiously the Kongo are overwhelmingly Christian — Catholic since 1491, Protestant in the northeast after the Swedish missions of the 1880s — with the prophetic **Kimbanguist** church (founded 1921 by Simon Kimbangu, a Kongo) a major independent denomination. Beneath and through this runs *nkisi* practice, the *nganga* healer-diviner, the *simbi* water spirits and *bakulu* ancestors, and the *dikenga* cosmogram, whose transatlantic descendants are Palo Mayombe, Candomblé Bantu, Kumina, Haitian Vodou and Hoodoo.
