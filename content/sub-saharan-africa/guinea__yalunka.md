## Overview

The Yalunka, also called Dialonké, are a Mandé-speaking people and the original inhabitants of Futa Jallon, a mountainous region in Guinea, West Africa. They live primarily in Guinea, particularly in Faranah, with smaller communities in Kouroussa; other communities are found in northeastern Sierra Leone, southeastern Senegal, and southwestern Mali. Their historical origins are associated with mountainous Koulikoro along the Niger River valley, and their earliest recorded arrival in the hilly plateau region of Futa Jallon is placed sometime around the eleventh century. The Yalunka speak Yalunka, a language in the Mande branch of the Niger–Congo language family, closely related to Susu. In folk-cultural terms, the sources document farming, household organization, religious practices, marriage customs, secret-society activity, and a language with distinctive clause structure and aspect marking.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no Yalunka textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

They state only that husbands are responsible for feeding and clothing their wives within the account of Yalunka marriage and household relations.

### Architecture

Yalunka households are described as patriarchal units headed by a man, his wife or wives, and their unmarried children. Extended households form a compound, which may contain two or more married men from the same father and their families. Each family lives in a separate hut. Most Yalunka settlements are located in valleys between hills in a hilly region whose elevation is mostly 1,000 to 2,000 feet above sea level.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do state that families keep chickens, cattle, sheep, and goats, and that goats and cattle provide milk used directly and processed into cheese and other products.

## Music & performance

They do identify the Yalunka language and provide linguistic examples, including the verb */siga/*, meaning “go,” and the aspect markers */bata/*, */xa/*, and */nama/*.

The Yalunka language is described as more order-sensitive than many other languages. It has comparatively little morphology and only minor inflectional affixing. Rather than relying extensively on verb tenses, it uses aspectual distinctions such as whether an action is “accomplished” or “not accomplished,” whether it is immediate, and whether it is real or irrealis. The aspect marker */bata/* indicates an action accomplished in the immediate context; */xa/* indicates that an action is desired or permitted; and */nama/* forms a negative imperative.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe several religious and life-cycle-related practices. The Yalunka are predominantly Muslim and are considered devout, while also retaining pre-Islamic beliefs and practices in a syncretic way.

One traditional practice is *Barinkiina*, which involves making sacrifices in memory of ancestors in order to gain power. The Yalunka also make sacrifices for *Suxurena* and *Nyinanna*, described as nature spirits, to gain powers.

Marriage is traditionally arranged. Yalunka commonly practice polygyny and follow Islamic law allowing a man to have up to four living wives. The first wife has seniority and authority over wives married later. Before the marriage takes place, the boy’s family gives animals to the girl’s family. Goats and cattle function as bride-price payments and as markers of wealth. The Bondo secret society gradually establishes attitudes related to adulthood in girls and discusses fertility, morality, and proper sexual comportment; it also maintains an interest in members’ well-being throughout their lives.

## Foodways

The Yalunka are primarily subsistence farmers. Rice and millet are staple crops, while peanuts, sweet potatoes, maize, and beans are also grown. Chickens, cattle, sheep, and goats are kept. Goats and cattle provide milk, which is consumed directly and processed into cheese and other products.

Agriculture is divided by age and gender in the account provided by the sources. Herding is done by children. Women milk cattle and help men with some agricultural work. Livestock also has an economic role: goats and cattle are significant as markers of wealth and are transferred as bride-price payments before marriage.

## Oral tradition & literature

According to Susu oral tradition, the Yalunka are identified with the medieval Sosso Empire of Soumaoro Kanté. The sources also state that the Yalunka were agricultural animists and among the first settlers in Jallonkadu, the former name of the area that eventually became Futa Jallon.

The New Testament was translated into the Yalunka language by Greg Pruett, identified in the source as the current president of Pioneer Bible Translators, in 2013.

## Language & religion

Yalunka is also spelled Yalunke, Jalonke, Kjalonke, Dyalonké, Djallonké, or Dialonké. It is spoken in Guinea, Sierra Leone, Mali, and Senegal and belongs to the Mande language family. It is closely related to Susu, and the Yalunka and Susu languages are described as mutually intelligible. The language relies heavily on clause order to define the grammatical function of words. Its clause structure includes a subject noun phrase, optional adverbs and aspect markers, a possible direct object, a verb, obliques, the particle */nɛn/*, the particle */nun/*, and emphatic markers; all categories except the verb are optional.

The Yalunka first accepted Islam, later renounced it when Fula dominance and Islamic theocracies expanded in the region, and were defeated and returned to Islam in 1778. The sources describe contemporary Yalunka religious life as predominantly Muslim and devout, combined with retained pre-Islamic beliefs and practices. These include *Barinkiina* sacrifices for ancestors and sacrifices for *Suxurena* and *Nyinanna*, or nature spirits.
