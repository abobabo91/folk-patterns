## Overview

The Mamprusi are an ethnic group in northern Ghana and Togo. About 200,000 Mamprusi were estimated to live in the Northern Regions of Ghana in 2013. In Ghana, they live mainly in Nalerigu, Gambaga, Walewale, and surrounding towns and villages in the North East Region. Their language is Mampruli, a Gur language within the Niger–Congo language family. Mamprugu, the Mamprusi kingdom, was founded around the 13th century by Naa Gbewaa at Pusiga, a village 14 kilometres from Bawku. The Mamprusi revere Pusiga as their ancestral home. The kingdom extends across parts of Ghana, Northern Togo, and Burkina Faso.

## Festivals & rituals

Mamprusi succession to a skin is hereditary, and only male direct descendants of Naa Gbanwaah are eligible. The Mamprusi monarchy traces its origin to Tohazie, whose name means “the Red Hunter”; his grandson Naa Gbanwaah settled in Pusiga and established Mamprugu.

## Foodways

Traditional occupations include farming and raising livestock.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe historical and dynastic traditions concerning Tohazie, Naa Gbanwaah, Naa Gbewaa, Foɣu/Kufoɣu, Kachaɣu, and Zirile. The Mamprusi claim to eldership is associated with Cognatic Primogeniture, in which succession passes through the oldest male child; the account also states that Gbewaa designated the younger son Foɣu/Kufoɣu to rule over his eldest child Kachaɣu and his eldest son Zirile. A collection of Mampruli proverbs was published by R.P. Xavier Plissart.

## Language & religion

Mampruli is a Gur language spoken in northern Ghana, Northern Togo, Burkina Faso, Ivory Coast, and Mali. It belongs to the Western Oti–Volta subgroup and its southeastern cluster. Mampruli is partially mutually intelligible with Dagbani and is closely related to Dagbani, Nanun, Kamara, Hanga, Kusaal, Nabit, and Talni. The language is written in an orthography based on the Latin alphabet, with the additional letters ɛ, ŋ, and ɔ. Its usual accepted name is Mampruli; Mamprusi generally refers to the ethnic group rather than the language. The majority of the Mamprusi are adherents of Islam, and conversion began in the 17th century through the influence of Dyula merchants.
