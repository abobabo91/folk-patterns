## Overview

Suri, also called Surma by some authors, is a collective name for the Chai, Timaga, and Baale ethnic groups. They mainly live in Suri woreda in southwestern Ethiopia, in the West Omo Zone, near the South Sudan border; other members of the groups live partly in neighbouring South Sudan. Their homeland consists of relatively remote semi-arid plains, valleys, and foothills. The Suri population was estimated at 20,622 in 1998 and approximately 32,000 in 2016. The groups speak different South East Surmic languages within the Nilo-Saharan language family. Suri culture is associated with agro-pastoralism, cattle, agriculture, ceremonial stick fighting, body modification, scarification, rainmaking, oral tradition, and egalitarian village life.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

 During special occasions, Suri people wear brightly colored flowers on their heads and paint their faces and bodies. Because mirrors are absent, people paint one another. The paint is made by mixing leaves and flowers from various plants with crushed rock, which can be white or red, and water.

### Architecture

 They state only that Suri villages normally range from 40 to 1,000 people, with a few reaching 2,500 people.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Suri women make earthenware pots and sell them to neighbours, including the Dizi.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Body modification is an important part of Suri culture. At puberty, most young women have their lower teeth removed, pierce the lower lip, and stretch it so that lip plates of increasing size can be inserted. A lip plate is described as a sign of female beauty and appropriateness; a common view is that a larger plate indicates greater value in bride-price negotiations, although some people deny this interpretation. Some women have stretched their lips enough to accommodate plates up to sixteen inches in diameter.

Women also perform decorative scarification. They lift the skin with a hook or edged thorn, slice or remove it with a razor blade, and allow the remaining skin to scar. Men traditionally scarred their bodies after killing someone from an enemy group.

## Music & performance

The sources describe songs connected with cattle. Cows are economically, socially, and symbolically important, and Suri men are judged partly by the number of cattle they own. To praise their cattle or mourn their deaths, Suri people sing songs for them.

Ceremonial stick fighting, called *saginé*, is a major public performance and rite of passage for young men. More properly described as ceremonial duelling, it brings prestige and is especially important when seeking a bride. Fights usually take place between two villages during harvest time. Large audiences attend, and contests begin with 20 to 30 representatives from each side, each of whom gets an opportunity to duel against someone from the other village. Referees are present to ensure that the rules are followed.

The fights can end within the first couple of hits, while deaths, particularly from blows to the stomach, are not unheard of. Shooting incidents have also occasionally occurred between men involved in other disputes. Ethiopian law forbids stick fighting, but the tradition continues.

## Dance & theatre

 They describe ceremonial stick fighting as a public ritual and competitive performance.

## Festivals & rituals

 They mention harvest time and the dry season as important periods in Suri life. Stick fighting usually takes place between villages during harvest time. During the dry season, Suri people move cattle southward to find new grazing land, and clashes with neighbouring groups are most common at this time.

The Suri have several life-cycle and age-grade practices. Young men known as *Tegay* are unmarried and are mainly responsible for herding and defending cattle. Junior elders known as *Rora* form the dominant decision-making age-grade. Entry into this grade takes place through an initiation ritual held every 20 to 30 years. During initiation, young men being promoted are tested by elders and are sometimes starved and whipped until they bleed.

Marriage is closely connected with cattle. Men cannot marry until they possess enough cattle to begin paying bride-wealth to the bride’s family. Cows are given to the prospective wife’s family during and after the initial wedding ceremony. Young women’s lip piercing and lip-plate insertion are associated with puberty, beauty, marriage, and bride-price.

## Foodways

The Suri economy is based on agriculture and livestock herding. Cattle and goats are the main sources of wealth. Crops include sorghum, maize, cassava, cabbage, beans, yams, spice plants, and some tobacco. During the dry season, Suri people collect honey. Suri women also sell the produce of game hunting, although these activities have sharply declined in recent decades.

Cattle are generally not killed unless they are needed for ceremonial purposes. Their importance extends beyond food and wealth: Suri people sing songs to praise cattle or mourn their deaths.

Suri people also produce local beer called *gèso* for sale.

## Oral tradition & literature

According to Suri oral tradition, the people came from the Boma Plateau, the Omo valley, and the Gobi of Maji plateau to their present territory approximately 200 years ago. The tradition recounts movement first toward the Akobo, eastwards from the Blue Nile, followed by movement in four directions toward the Boma Mountain, Gobi maji Plateau, the Omo valley of the Omo river, and the mountain shologoy. These migrants also absorbed local groups. They state that Suri oral culture has been documented in *Suri Orature: An Introduction to the Society, Language and Oral Culture of the Suri People (Southwest Ethiopia)*. They also report that few Surma are familiar with Amharic and that literacy is relatively low, although schools have been built in recent decades and the number of literate Suri is growing.

## Language & religion

Suri is also known as Churi, Dhuri, Shuri, and Shuro. It is a Surmic language spoken in the West Omo Zone of the South West Ethiopia Peoples’ Region, near the South Sudan border. It has over 80% lexical similarity to Mursi. The language is spoken in two dialects by two nationalities: Tirma, also called Tirmaga, Cirma, Dirma, Terema, Terna, Tid, Tirima, or Tirmagi; and Chai, also called Caci or Cai. The three Suri groups—Chai, Timaga, and Baale—speak different languages within the South East Surmic group.

Approximately 97% of Suri people are animists. They believe in a sky god named *Tumu*, alternatively spelled *Tuma*, and also believe in spirits. Female diviners are consulted. Rainmaking is inherited and given to one man in specific clans. When his services are needed, men collect chips from a specific tree, chew them, mix the remaining juice with clay, and pour and smear the mixture over the rainmaker’s body. Rain is then expected. Evangelical Christianity has gained an estimated 200–300 followers, especially around Kibish town and among people who leave the area to study.
