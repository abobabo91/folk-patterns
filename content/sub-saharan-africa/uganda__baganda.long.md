## Overview

The Baganda are a Bantu ethnic group who share a common culture, history, language, and clan system. They are primarily native to Buganda, a subnational kingdom within Uganda. The Baganda comprised 15.3 percent of Uganda’s population at the time of the 2024 census, and the population in Uganda is estimated at 16.3 million. A single person is a Muganda, while several people are Baganda; the people are also called Abaganda, and Ganda appears in older English texts. They traditionally speak Luganda, and every Muganda belongs to a clan. The Kabaka, the king of Buganda, has a central place in social and political life. Organized Baganda communities also exist in Canada, South Africa, Sweden, the United Kingdom, and the United States.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Lubugo** — Barkcloth, also called olubugo or Lubugo, was the most common form of attire used throughout Buganda. It was made from the bark of at least 20 tree species, especially the Mutuba tree, and different trees produced colors and textures ranging from yellow and sandy brown to dark red-brown. The finest quality was rusty brown cloth called Kimote; a species producing white cloth was reserved for the Kabaka.

**Barkcloth manufacture** — Museum records describe barkcloth made from the bark of certain cultivated Ficus species. Barkcloth beaters were wooden clubs with cylindrical, grooved or serrated heads. One recorded beater has a head decorated with pointed patterns made by carving.

**Decorated barkcloth** — Museum examples include a red-brown barkcloth dress or cloak with bands of geometric patterns in black dye, barkcloth with geometric patterns stencilled in black, and large rectangular terracotta-coloured barkcloth with seams, repairs, and stains.

**Barkcloth tools** — The Museum of World Culture records a wooden barkcloth beater, 27 centimetres high, with a ribbed cylindrical head and carved pointed decoration. Other records describe barkcloth beaters with different coarse and fine grooves.

Barkcloth also served as bedding and for wrapping goods. Museum records include a strip on which beating had just begun and a finely corrugated rectangle with patchwork repairs.

**Motif vocabulary.** Geometric patterns, pointed patterns, bands, chequer plaiting.

### Clothing & dress

The traditional dress of Baganda women is the Gomesi, while the Kanzu is the traditional dress of Baganda men. Barkcloth, or Lubugo, is the traditional fabric and is made from the bark of the Mutuba tree. Historical descriptions state that Baganda covered the body from head to toe and regarded nudity as disgusting and shameful. Bark cloaks could resemble yellow corduroy cloth and were carefully made, crimped, and set as though stiffened with starch.

Museum records add garments made of goat skin, composed of several pieces sewn together and pierced with small holes around the edges, as well as a robe made from white gazelle skin. Recorded adornments include a headband of fibre and red, white, and blue glass beads, and a waist belt made of fibrous material covered with small blackened wooden rings.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Museum records include red pottery bottles blackened with plumbago, including milk-vessels with incised and coloured red-and-white bands, long slender necks, cup-shaped lips, and globular bodies. Other objects include a wooden pot with a plaited cap, a basketry beer strainer, a small coiled basket apparently made from bark strips, and a snuff-container made from two brass cartridge cases soldered together.

Recorded weapons and tools include wooden shields with bosses, cane wicker-work shields, shields with basket-work ornamentation, a knife with a large curved iron blade, and a metal bill-hook with a carved wooden handle. Museum photographs document Ganda men making cooking-pots by coiling clay and another Ganda man pounding grain with a wooden pestle and mortar.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Museum objects include necklaces made from elephant-tail hair and red glass beads, neck-rings made of iron or beads, an ivory child’s anklet, a silver armlet with hinged construction, and a dance anklet or rattle consisting of an iron bar with nine iron beads. Other recorded ornaments include a waist ornament composed of 102 strands of plaited fibre covered with white, red, and blue glass beads, a metal miniature-shield amulet, and an oval circlet covered with plaited dried leaf.

A snuff-bottle is made of blue glass and has a cover of red, white, and black glass beads ending in small cowrie shells. The sources state that Baganda men rarely kept facial hair and that women carefully shaved their armpit hair, except under a custom connected with a husband’s absence. They also state that the Baganda traditionally avoided scarification, tooth extraction, pierced ear lobes, and circumcision.

## Music & performance

Baganda music is a musical culture developed by the people of Uganda. Its instruments include the Amadinda and Akadinda xylophones, the Ennanga harp, the Etongoli lyre, drums, and the Kadongo lamellophone, whose plural is budongo. Amadinda, akadinda, ennanga, entongoli, and several types of drums were used in the courtly music of the Kabaka. The kadongo entered Baganda music more recently, dating to the early 20th century, and budongo music is not part of traditional court music.

Baganda music is based on an approximately equidistant pentatonic scale. The octave, mwànjo, is divided into five intervals of approximately 240 cents, or 2.4 semitones. The music is not harmony-based, chords are not used, and only octaves are consonant. Performances often use noisy or buzzing timbres: metal rings can be placed around kadongo lamellas, while scales of a kind of goana can be fixed to the ennanga so that the strings touch them.

The amadinda is a log xylophone with 12 wooden bars placed on two fresh banana stems. It is played by three musicians called omunazi, omwawuzi, and omukoonezi. The parts are called okunaga and okwawula, while the third musician performs okukoonera. The correct way of playing is called Okusengejja. Named advanced or mistaken techniques include Okudaliza, okusita ebiyondo oba ebisenge, Okubwatula, Okugugumula, Okuyiwa, and Okwokya.

## Dance & theatre

The sources state that dancing forms part of the twin-naming ceremony called okuzina abalongo.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe rites connected with naming, marriage, death, and the confirmation of twins.

In the former naming ceremony called okwalula abaana, a child was named and confirmed as a member of the clan. A saved piece of the child’s umbilical cord was used in confirming connection to the father. Twin-related names and titles include ssaalongo for the father of twins, nnaalongo for the mother, and particular surnames for older and younger twins. The naming and confirmation of twins is called okuzina abalongo and involves drinking, eating, obscene words, promiscuity, and dancing. Some participants may undergo a cleansing ceremony called okukansira.

Traditional marriage involves several rites. These include a visit to the bride-to-be’s paternal aunt, Ssenga; Okukyala, the visit to the bride-to-be’s parents; Okwanjula, the introduction ceremony; Akasiki, held the night before the wedding reception; Kaasuze katya, held on the morning of the reception; Embaga, the wedding reception; Okuzza omuzigo, a show of appreciation; and Okugenda mu kisenge, a honeymoon lasting roughly six days. In modern times, some ceremonies are skipped or merged.

Death rites include Okubika, the announcement of death; a communal wake; preparation of the body; Okuziraga, a ritual involving oil or water rubbed on the forehead or face; and Okwabya Olumbe, which marks the end of mourning and is usually held days or months after burial.

## Foodways

The sources mention eating and drinking as parts of the twin-naming ceremony and record a wooden pestle and mortar used by a Ganda man for pounding grain.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Baganda creation myth identifies Kintu as the first man on earth and in Buganda. Kintu married Nnambi, the daughter of the god Ggulu, and the Baganda are described as the descendants of Kintu and Nnambi. Nnambi’s jealous brother Walumbe is responsible in the myth for human disease, sickness, and death. Kayikuuzi tried to protect humans from Walumbe and continues trying to capture him from the underground where he hides. On the basis of this myth, the Baganda are called abaana ba Nnambi, or Nnambi’s children.

The sources also record a Ganda proverb: “He who makes you shed tears, you make him shed blood.” They describe clan traditions, mythological names, and songs whose titles refer to associated texts.

## Language & religion

Luganda is the traditional language of the Baganda and is described as a tonal language. Its tones significantly influence music: the progression of tones in a song text partly determines possible melodies, and musical patterns can evoke new text associations for Luganda speakers. The sources also state that the words Ganda, Abaganda, and Omuganda are connected with concepts of brotherhood, unity, and the clan or people.

According to the 2002 Census of Uganda, 42.7 percent of Baganda were Roman Catholic, 27.4 percent Anglican, 23 percent Muslim, and 4.3 percent Pentecostal. The sources state that most Baganda belong to Abrahamic religions, while some also secretly follow the traditional Kiganda religion. Traditional religious references include the Lubaales, Ggulu, Walumbe, and Kayikuuzi. Clan members are believed to share common ancestors, and each clan has a totem animal, plant, or thing that its members must not eat or harm.
