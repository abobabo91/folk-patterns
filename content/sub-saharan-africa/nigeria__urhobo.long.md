## Overview

Urhobo communities live in the western Niger Delta of southern Nigeria. The Smithsonian National Museum of African Art describes villages and village groups linked by common ancestry. This page concentrates on Urhobo-attributed material in museum collections and on a small set of captioned contemporary photographs. Each object has its own collecting history; no one object can stand for every Urhobo community or period.

## Carved head-dresses and masks

The British Museum's 1896 accession group contains several striking wooden head-dresses. One has a pair of faces below a dark crown and a wicker base for fitting it to the head. Another is topped by a carved ape, with its two faces finished in white and yellow ochre. A third shows a human figure above the wicker base. Other head-masks combine human faces with bird tails or long jaws and are painted in black, red, white, or ochre. These are varied sculptural forms, not interchangeable illustrations of one named ceremony.

The museum labels the selected group as made by Urhobo people. The wicker bases and carved forms show how the objects could be worn, but the records do not give a full performance account for each one. The gallery therefore describes what is visible and what the catalogue states, without assigning every head-dress a specific spirit, festival, or performer.

## Figures and shrines

Figurative sculpture is well represented. A British Museum group places a top-hatted man above a base supported by smaller figures. Other selected figures stand, sit, hold a fly whisk, wear a hat, or carry carved and attached ornaments. The top hat is a visible part of one historical carving; it is not a claim that all Urhobo dress took this form.

A larger British Museum sculpture has a gaping animal-like mouth, several tusks, and human and animal figures above it. The museum's curatorial text calls it an *ivwri* figure and records associations with protection, status, and controlled aggression. It uses careful language about how a group might have owned or used a large example. The Smithsonian, discussing a separate Urhobo female figure, distinguishes sculptures representing spirits from those representing actual or mythic ancestors, and says the distinction can be difficult without contextual evidence. Its documented *iphri* shrine figure shows another protective form: a human figure stands over a beast with bared teeth.

A British Museum shrine record was excluded despite a normalized Urhobo maker field because its older donation note says only ?probably Urhobo.? The catalogue's own uncertainty matters when filing an object under a particular people.

## Instruments, implements, and photographs

Selected instruments include a double-headed wooden drum, two tall drums with different tensioning systems, a cane-and-gourd rattle, and an iron gong with smaller attached bells. A carved ladle ends in a long-beaked bird; an iron knife carries brass rivets. These objects show a broader craft repertoire than the carved figures alone. A matched second rattle and weaker or repeated objects were omitted from the gallery.

A British Museum album photograph is captioned as an Urhobo woman carrying produce on a pole balanced on her head. The museum also notes her anklets and ivory bracelets. Its caption supplies the specific identification missing from many colonial photographs. The selected contemporary Commons images show a marriage gathering and captioned Urhobo dances. They document particular events and people, and should not be read as an unchanging reconstruction of older practices.
