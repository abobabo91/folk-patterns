## Overview

The Ashanti (*Asante*) are an Akan people of south-central Ghana, the largest and historically most powerful subgroup of the wider Akan cluster that also includes the Fante, Akyem, Akuapem, Bono, Kwahu, Nzema and Sefwi. Their heartland is the forested plateau country around Kumasi, capital of the historic Asante Empire and today of Ghana's Ashanti Region, with satellite populations across the Bono East, Ahafo and Eastern Regions and a substantial diaspora in Côte d'Ivoire, the United Kingdom and North America. Ashanti speakers number roughly 9–11 million within a total Akan-speaking population of around 20 million; their language, Twi (specifically the Asante Twi dialect of the Central Tano branch of Kwa), functions as one of the lingue franche of southern Ghana. Politically the Asante coalesced in the late seventeenth century under Osei Tutu and the priest Okomfo Anokye, whose conjuring of the *Sika Dwa Kofi* — the Golden Stool — from the sky supplied the spiritual charter of the confederacy; the Asantehene still reigns from the Manhyia Palace in Kumasi. In folk-culture terms the Ashanti matter as the great weavers, goldsmiths and stool-carvers of West Africa: the strip-woven *kente* silks of Bonwire, the stamped *adinkra* cloths of Ntonso, the lost-wax gold *mmrammuo* weights, the ancestral blackened stools, and the wooden fertility figure *akuaba* all sit within their tradition.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Kente** (*nwentoma*, "woven cloth") — the narrow-strip loom textile of the Asante and neighbouring Ewe, woven on a horizontal double-heddle loom in strips roughly ten centimetres wide and then edge-sewn into a large wrapper. Bonwire, a village northeast of Kumasi, is the historic Asante centre. Warp-faced ground stripes are broken by dense weft-float geometric blocks (*babadua*, *nkyimkyim*, *adweneasa*), each named cloth (e.g. *Oyokoman*, *Emaa da*, *Sika Futuro*, *Adweneasa* — "my skill is exhausted") carrying proverbial and dynastic meaning historically restricted to royal use.

**Adinkra** — cotton cloth block-printed with carbonised *badie* bark dye (*adinkra aduro*) using stamps carved from calabash. Ntonso, north of Kumasi, remains the centre. The stamps encode a corpus of over a hundred ideographic symbols — *Gye Nyame* (supremacy of God), *Sankofa* (return and fetch it), *Dwennimmen* (ram's horns, humility with strength), *Nyame Nti* (by God's grace), *Adinkrahene* (chief of adinkra symbols) — organised in grids divided by combed lines. Historically worn for funerals in russet, brown-black and indigo, adinkra now appears in white and coloured "Sunday" versions.

**Nsaa** — a heavy woollen blanket-cloth, not woven by the Asante themselves but imported from the northern savanna and Sahel and prized so highly in Kumasi that the adinkra symbol *Nsaa* stands for the recognition of quality ("he who does not know real *nsaa* buys the fakes").

**Kuntunkuni** and **brisi** — solid indigo-black and russet mourning cloths worn plain or lightly stamped for the darkest funeral occasions.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Adweneasa* (fullness of design), *nkyimkyim* (zigzag, adaptability), *babadua* (bamboo, strength), *mmaa dua* (women's staff, sisterhood), *epa* (handcuffs, law), *akoben* (war horn), *aya* (fern, endurance), *duafe* (wooden comb, feminine beauty), *mframadan* (wind-resistant house), *funtunfunefu* (the crocodile with a shared stomach, unity in diversity).

### Clothing & dress

Ashanti dress is toga-like: a large rectangular cloth (roughly 3.5 by 2 metres for men, smaller two- or three-piece sets for women) draped over the left shoulder leaving the right shoulder bare, worn over drawstring shorts (*danta*) for men and a wrapper-and-blouse ensemble (*kaba ne slit*) for women. The quality and pattern of the cloth signal rank and occasion: *kente* silks for weddings, enstoolments and durbars; adinkra for funerals; plain white calico for thanksgiving and childbirth. Chiefs and the Asantehene wear multiple *kente* wrappers layered with heavy gold ornaments, sandals (*ahenema*) whose soles must never be seen by ordinary eyes, and — on the greatest occasions — a headband of gold discs. Priestesses (*akomfo*) wear raffia skirts and white clay body paint (*hyire*) during possession. Widows in deep mourning wear russet *kuntunkuni*, hair cropped and unadorned; the return to coloured cloth marks the end of the mourning cycle.

### Architecture

The signature Asante building type is the courtyard *mframadan* ("wind-house") shrine complex, a rectangular arrangement of four open-fronted rooms around a central sunken court, walls built up in wattle-and-daub over a timber frame, plastered with red laterite mud and finished in raised low-relief arabesques of interlaced curvilinear motifs in red, black and white. The steeply pitched thatched roof projects deeply over the open fronts. Ten surviving shrine houses of this type in the villages around Kumasi (Asawase, Ejisu-Besease, Adarko Jachie, Bodwease and others) are inscribed as the **Asante Traditional Buildings** on the UNESCO World Heritage List, the last physical remnants of the pre-1874 courtly architecture largely destroyed in the Anglo-Asante wars. Palace architecture at Manhyia followed the same courtyard logic at a monumental scale. Domestic compounds (*fie*) are similarly organised around a swept earthen courtyard, matrilineally inherited, with cooking hearths under lean-to eaves and an ancestral stool room kept locked. Contemporary Kumasi is dominated by cement-block construction, but the courtyard plan and the reserved stool room persist.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Ashanti goldsmithing is the most celebrated metal tradition of West Africa, worked by the *sikadwumfo* guilds of Kumasi and its satellite villages in the lost-wax (*cire perdue*) method. **Akan goldweights** (*mmrammuo* or *abrammuo*) — small brass figurines and geometric forms cast to standard mithqal-derived units for weighing gold dust on brass scales — were made in tens of thousands between the fifteenth and late nineteenth centuries; the figurative weights depict proverbs, animals, warriors, chiefs, adinkra symbols and everyday objects and constitute one of the great miniature-sculpture corpora of Africa. Associated goldweighing kit includes the **kuduo** (a lidded cast-brass vessel with elaborate lid figures, used to hold gold dust, valuables and grave offerings), the **forowa** (a sheet-brass repoussé container for shea butter or cosmetics), the *abusua kuruwa* ("family pot", a lidded terracotta for the shaved hair of mourners), brass scale pans (*nsania*), spoons and shovels, and small brass boxes for gold dust.

Court regalia in cast and beaten gold — pectoral discs (*akrafokonmu*, "soul-washers' badges"), sandal ornaments, ring-swords and elephant-tail whisks — reach their apogee in the **Sika Dwa Kofi** itself, the Golden Stool said to hold the *sunsum* (soul) of the Asante nation, which is never sat upon and never touches the ground. Wooden **Asante stools** (*dwa*) are carved from a single block of *osese* wood in named forms (*Kotoko dwa*, *Mmaa dua*, *Adinkrahene dwa*) whose curved seat rests on a central column of proverbial figuration; upon a chief's death his personal stool is blackened with egg yolk and soot and installed in the ancestral stool room. **Linguist staves** (*okyeame poma*) — carved wooden staffs sheathed in gold foil topped with proverbial finials (a hand holding an egg, two men at a table) — are carried by the royal spokesman (*okyeame*) who mediates all speech to and from the chief.

Pottery is women's work, hand-built by coil without a wheel, low-fired and burnished; the *abusua kuruwa*, water jars (*ahina*), and cooking pots (*kukuo*) are the principal forms. Wood carving beyond stools includes the ubiquitous **akuaba** — a stylised wooden fertility doll with a disc-shaped head, high forehead, small barred features and ringed neck, carried tucked into the wrapper by women hoping to conceive and afterwards given to daughters as a toy.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Gold is the defining Ashanti ornament, worn in cast and hollow-formed pendants, rings (*pɛtea*), armlets (*abasabo*), anklets, and heavy multi-strand necklaces. Chiefs' gold pectorals (*akrafokonmu*) are worn by the soul-washers who tend the ruler's *kra*. Glass beads — Bodom, Bota, and imported Venetian trade beads — are strung as heirloom waist beads (*toma*) worn under clothing by women from infancy, and as ceremonial necklaces layered at puberty rites and weddings. The bride at a **puberty rite (*bragoro*)** appears anointed with shea butter and painted with white clay dots (*hyire*), waist heavy with beads, wrists and ankles ringed in gold. Scarification is minimal compared with savanna neighbours; kaolin body-marking of priests and initiates carries most of the visual weight. Hairstyles historically encoded status: shaved heads for the recently bereaved, elaborate cornrows and threaded coifs for adolescents, and the fillet-and-disc coif of the queen mother (*ohemaa*).

## Music & performance

Asante court music is built around drum ensembles, of which the most celebrated is *fontomfrom* — a battery of large barrel drums with the paired talking drums *atumpan* — played at the enstoolment and public appearances of chiefs. The *atumpan*, tuned a fourth or fifth apart, "speak" Twi through its tonal contours, reciting the *appellations* of the Asantehene and the founding history of the state. *Kete* is a slower, statelier court ensemble of drums, iron bells (*dawuro*) and flutes reserved for royal occasions, while *adowa*, more widely popular, accompanies funerals, thanksgivings and social gatherings. *Nnwonkoro* is a women's song genre of interlocking call-and-response, and *akom* is the possession-music of the traditional priesthood, driving the *okomfo* into trance. Instruments beyond the drum families include the *seperewa* (a seven- to fourteen-string harp-lute of clear Sahelian ancestry, historically the singer's instrument at court), the *mmɛnson* (an ensemble of seven ivory side-blown horns, each sounding a single pitch, hocketed into royal salutes), the gourd rattle *akasaa*, the double bell *dawuro*, and iron castanets. The linguist's staff-tapping and the herald's ivory trumpet punctuate speech at durbar. Modern Ghanaian highlife and its Kumasi-born offshoot *sikyi* highlife draw directly on adowa and *osode* rhythms.

## Dance & theatre

*Adowa*, the most widespread Asante dance, is performed by women in a circle to a signature bell-and-drum pattern, with hand gestures — cupped ear, pointed finger, sweep across the chest — that constitute a codified sign language of proverbs and emotional statement. *Kete* dance is slower and more restrained, danced by chiefs and their retinue in full regalia with the handkerchief-and-whisk vocabulary of royal display. *Fontomfrom* is a warrior's dance of stamping advances and retreats. The *akom* trance dance of the priesthood involves spinning, back-bending and the sudden collapse that signals possession by an *abosom* deity. There is no proscenium theatre tradition in the classical Asante repertoire; dramatic performance is embedded in the *durbar* — the ceremonial appearance of chiefs — in funeral rites, and in the *okyeame*'s theatrical rendition of proverbs, which functions as a courtly form of oratory-as-performance. The twentieth-century concert-party tradition of Kumasi and coastal Ghana, blending highlife music with comic morality plays in Twi, is the modern popular descendant.

## Festivals & rituals

The great calendrical cycle of the Asante is the *Adae* festival, occurring twice in each forty-two-day *adaduanan* cycle: *Akwasidae* on the Sunday and *Awukudae* on the Wednesday. On these days the Asantehene and subordinate chiefs enter the stool room to feed the ancestral blackened stools with mashed yam and sheep's blood, receive homage, and hear petitions. The culminating *Adae Kese* ("great Adae"), held at intervals of several years, is the largest public festival of the Asante state. The **Odwira** festival, celebrated in September in Akuapem and formerly at Kumasi, marks purification of the state, the sanctioning of the new yam, and remembrance of ancestors. Life-cycle rites include the eight-day naming ceremony (*abadinto*), the female puberty rite (*bragoro*) in which a girl is seated in state, bathed, decked in beads and presented to the community, marriage (*awaregyeɛ*) organised through matrilineal *abusua* negotiation and *tiri nsa* bridewealth, and the elaborate multi-stage funeral (*ayie*) which remains the central social investment of Asante life, with russet and black cloth, adinkra hangings, adowa performance, and the collection of the deceased's hair and nails in an *abusua kuruwa*.

## Foodways

Ashanti cuisine is dominated by the pounded starch **fufu**, made from boiled cassava and plantain (or yam) beaten in a wooden mortar (*wɔduro*) with a tall pestle until smooth and elastic, served in a communal bowl and eaten by hand with a soup ladled over it. The classic pairings are *nkate nkwan* (groundnut soup), *abenkwan* (palm-nut soup) and *nkrakra* (light tomato-and-pepper soup), each simmered with smoked fish, snails, bushmeat or goat. **Ampesi** is boiled yam, cocoyam or plantain served with *kontomire abom* (steamed cocoyam-leaf stew) or garden-egg stew. **Kenkey** and *banku* (fermented maize dumplings) are more coastal but eaten throughout Kumasi with fried fish and pepper sauce (*shitɔ*). Snacks include *kelewele* (spiced fried plantain), roasted plantain with groundnuts (*kɔfi brokeman*), and *bofrot* (fried dough balls). Palm wine (*nsafufuo*) and millet beer (*pito*) are the traditional drinks, with *akpeteshie* the distilled palm spirit; libation of schnapps or palm wine to the ancestors opens every serious gathering.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Asante possess one of West Africa's richest proverb (*ɛbɛ*) traditions, deployed in ordinary speech, at court through the *okyeame*, and materially in the carved finials of linguist staves, in adinkra symbols, and in the figurative goldweights — each *mmrammuo* casting often illustrating a single proverb ("two crocodiles with one stomach still fight over food"). The great narrative cycle is **Anansesem**, the tales of *Kwaku Ananse* the spider trickster, told at night after the day's work and forbidden by convention during daylight; Ananse's cunning and greed are the vehicle for etiological, moral and simply comic stories, and the cycle survives into the Caribbean as Anancy. Court historians (*abakɔmfo* and *nseneefo*) preserved dynastic history in drum texts, horn appellations and recited praise poetry commemorating the founders of the confederacy and their wars. The written Twi literary tradition, beginning with Basel Mission orthography in the nineteenth century, includes the historical writings of J. B. Danquah and the twentieth-century novels of Asare Konadu and Efua Sutherland.

## Language & religion

Asante Twi belongs to the Central Tano branch of the Kwa family within Niger-Congo, a two-tone language written in a Latin orthography that includes *ɛ*, *ɔ* and the digraph *ny*. It is mutually intelligible with Akuapem Twi and Fante and functions as a lingua franca across southern Ghana. The traditional religion recognises a distant supreme creator, **Nyame** (also *Onyankopɔn*, "the great one"), whose omnipresence is invoked daily and whose *sankofa*-shaped altar (*Nyame dua*, a forked branch holding a pot of water and offerings) once stood in every compound. Below Nyame stand the *abosom* — deities of rivers, lakes, forests and specific localities, chief among them the Tano and the Bosomtwe — served by the possession-priesthood of *akomfo*. Ancestors (*nsamanfo*), embodied in the blackened stools, are fed at each Adae and are the immediate court of appeal in family matters. Islam has a longstanding but minority presence carried by northern *Zongo* traders in Kumasi, and Christianity — Methodist, Presbyterian, Catholic and, more recently, Pentecostal and charismatic — is now the majority affiliation, though most Christian Asante continue to observe stool rituals, funeral rites and libation as social rather than confessional obligations.
