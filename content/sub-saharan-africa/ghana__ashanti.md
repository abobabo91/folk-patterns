## Overview

The Ashanti are the largest and historically most powerful Akan subgroup, with a heartland around Kumasi, ancient capital of the Asante Empire. They coalesced in the late seventeenth century under Osei Tutu and priest Okomfo Anokye, whose spiritual charter was the Golden Stool. They are renowned across West Africa for weaving, goldwork, and carving — their strip-woven kente silks, stamped adinkra cloths, lost-wax gold figurines, and carved stools represent some of Africa's finest material traditions.

## Material culture

Ashanti material culture is characterized by masterful work in textiles, gold, and wood, each tradition carrying layers of proverb, status and spiritual meaning.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Ashanti are West Africa's preeminent weavers, with two globally recognized cloth traditions — kente silks and adinkra block-prints — each encoding proverb and rank.

- **Kente cloth** (*nwentoma*) — Narrow-strip silk woven on a horizontal loom in ten-centimetre strips, then edge-sewn into large wrappers with named patterns carrying proverbial meaning and historically restricted to royalty.
- **Adinkra cloth** (*adinkra*) — Cotton block-printed with carbonized bark dye using carved calabash stamps, featuring over a hundred ideographic symbols organized in grids, historically worn for funerals.
- **Nsaa** — Heavy woollen blanket-cloth imported from the northern savanna and so prized in Kumasi that its adinkra symbol stands for recognition of quality.
- **Mourning cloths** (*kuntunkuni, brisi*) — Solid indigo-black and russet plain or lightly-stamped cloths worn on the darkest funeral occasions.

### Clothing & dress

Ashanti dress is a toga-like wrapping of large rectangular cloth over one shoulder, with quality and pattern signaling rank, occasion and life stage.

- **Men's wrapper** (*nwentoma*) — A large rectangular cloth roughly 3.5 by 2 metres draped over the left shoulder leaving the right bare, worn over drawstring shorts.
- **Women's ensemble** (*kaba ne slit*) — A wrapper-and-blouse combination in smaller two- or three-piece sets, with cloth quality marking rank and occasion.
- **Chief's regalia** — Multiple layered kente wrappers with heavy gold ornaments, special sandals whose soles must never be seen, and on great occasions a gold-disc headband.
- **Priestess attire** (*akomfo*) — Raffia skirts and white clay body paint worn during possession rituals.
- **Widow's mourning dress** (*kuntunkuni*) — Deep russet cloth with cropped unadorned hair, with return to coloured cloth marking the end of mourning.

### Architecture

The signature Ashanti structure is the courtyard shrine complex with wattle-and-daub walls, red laterite plaster and ornamental low-relief arabesques.

- **Shrine house** (*mframadan*) — A rectangular arrangement of four open-fronted rooms around a central sunken court with wattle-and-daub walls plastered in red laterite and finished with interlaced curvilinear motifs in red, black and white.
- **Asante Traditional Buildings** (*mframadan*) — Ten surviving shrine houses around Kumasi inscribed as UNESCO World Heritage, the last remnants of pre-1874 courtly architecture largely destroyed in the Anglo-Asante wars.
- **Domestic compound** (*fie*) — A matrilineally inherited arrangement of rooms around a swept earthen courtyard with cooking hearths under lean-to eaves and a locked ancestral stool room.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Ashanti metalwork in gold and brass is celebrated across West Africa, while pottery and wood carving produce essential vessels and ritual objects.

- **Akan goldweights** (*mmrammuo, abrammuo*) — Small brass figurines and geometric forms cast by lost-wax method to standard mithqal units for weighing gold dust, depicting proverbs, animals, warriors and everyday objects.
- **Kuduo** — A lidded cast-brass vessel with elaborate figures used to hold gold dust, valuables and grave offerings.
- **Asante stool** (*dwa*) — Carved from a single block of osese wood with a curved seat resting on a central proverbial column; upon a chief's death it is blackened with egg yolk and soot for the ancestral stool room.
- **Linguist staff** (*okyeame poma*) — A carved wooden staff sheathed in gold foil topped with proverbial finials, carried by the royal spokesman to mediate speech with the chief.
- **Akuaba** — A stylised wooden fertility doll with disc-shaped head, high forehead and ringed neck, carried by women hoping to conceive and given to daughters as a toy.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Gold ornaments and beaded waist-cloths are the primary adorned forms, with specific pieces marking status, age and ritual participation.

- **Gold ornaments** (*pɛtea, abasabo*) — Cast and hollow-formed gold pendants, rings, armlets, anklets and multi-strand necklaces worn by chiefs and wealthy individuals.
- **Soul-washer pectorals** (*akrafokonmu*) — Gold pectorals worn by the soul-washers who tend the ruler's spiritual essence.
- **Heirloom waist beads** (*toma*) — Strands of glass beads worn under clothing by women from infancy, marking status and lineage.
- **Puberty rite beads** (*toma*) — Beads layered at a girl's bragoro ceremony along with shea butter anointing and white clay body dots.

## Music & performance

Ashanti court music centers on drum ensembles that speak Twi through tonal contours, accompanied by horns, harp-lutes and bells.

- **Fontomfrom** — A battery of large barrel drums with paired talking drums that 'speak' Twi through tonal contours, reciting the appellations of the Asantehene and founding history.
- **Talking drums** (*atumpan*) — Paired tuned drums tuned a fourth or fifth apart that communicate royal appellations and state history through Twi tonal language.
- **Kete ensemble** (*kete*) — A slower, statelier court ensemble of drums, iron bells and flutes reserved for royal occasions.
- **Adowa** — A women's dance accompanied by drums and bells, more widely popular than court music, at funerals, thanksgivings and social gatherings.
- **Harp-lute** (*seperewa*) — A seven- to fourteen-string harp-lute of Sahelian ancestry, historically the singer's instrument at court.

## Dance & theatre

Dance in Ashanti tradition ranges from women's circle dances with codified hand-gesture language to warriors' stamping and possession trance.

- **Adowa dance** (*adowa*) — Women perform in a circle to a signature bell-and-drum pattern with hand gestures constituting a codified sign language of proverbs and emotion.
- **Kete dance** (*kete*) — A slower, more restrained dance performed by chiefs and retinue in full regalia using handkerchief-and-whisk vocabulary of royal display.
- **Fontomfrom dance** (*fontomfrom*) — A warrior's dance of stamping advances and retreats performed to the fontomfrom drum ensemble.
- **Akom trance dance** (*akom*) — Priesthood possession dance involving spinning, back-bending and sudden collapse that signals possession by an abosom deity.

## Festivals & rituals

The great calendrical cycle is the Adae festival occurring twice in each forty-two-day period, with the Odwira marking the new yam and ancestor remembrance.

- **Adae festival** (*Adae*) — Occurring twice in each forty-two-day cycle on Sunday (Akwasidae) and Wednesday (Awukudae), when chiefs enter the stool room to feed blackened ancestral stools and receive homage.
- **Great Adae** (*Adae Kese*) — The culminating multi-year festival held at intervals, the largest public gathering of the Asante state.
- **Odwira festival** (*Odwira*) — Celebrated in September marking purification of the state, sanctioning of the new yam and remembrance of ancestors.
- **Naming ceremony** (*abadinto*) — An eight-day ritual marking a child's entry into the community and assignment of names.
- **Puberty rite** (*bragoro*) — A female rite in which a girl is seated in state, bathed, decked in beads and wrist-gold, and presented to the community.

## Foodways

Ashanti cuisine centers on pounded starch fufu served with soups simmered with fish, snails or bushmeat, and palm wine libation opening every serious gathering.

- **Fufu** — Boiled cassava and plantain or yam beaten in a wooden mortar until smooth and elastic, served in a communal bowl with soup ladled over it.
- **Groundnut soup** (*nkate nkwan*) — A classic fufu pairing simmered with smoked fish, snails, bushmeat or goat.
- **Palm-nut soup** (*abenkwan*) — A rich fufu accompaniment made from palm nuts and simmered with proteins.
- **Ampesi** — Boiled yam, cocoyam or plantain served with steamed cocoyam-leaf stew or garden-egg stew.
- **Palm wine** (*nsafufuo*) — Traditional fermented drink used for libation to ancestors at the opening of every serious gathering.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Ashanti possess one of West Africa's richest proverb traditions, deployed in speech, court oratory and materially in carved goldweights and adinkra symbols.

- **Proverbs** (*ɛbɛ*) — A rich corpus deployed in ordinary speech, through royal orators, and materially in carved finials, adinkra symbols and figurative goldweights.
- **Anansesem spider tales** (*Anansesem*) — Narrative cycle of Kwaku Ananse the spider trickster told at night after work, forbidden by convention during daylight, serving as vehicle for etiological and moral stories.
- **Drum texts** (*fontomfrom*) — Preserved by court historians as repositories of dynastic history, royal appellations and praise poetry commemorating founders and wars.
- **Twi literature** — Beginning with Basel Mission orthography in the nineteenth century, including historical writings, twentieth-century novels by Asare Konadu and Efua Sutherland.

## Language & religion

Asante Twi is a Niger-Congo language functioning as a lingua franca across southern Ghana, while traditional religion centres on the distant creator Nyame served by possession-priesthoods of the abosom and ancestor veneration.

- **Asante Twi** (*Twi*) — A two-tone Central Tano language written in Latin orthography including ɛ, ɔ and ny digraph, mutually intelligible with Akuapem Twi and Fante.
- **Supreme creator** (*Nyame, Onyankopɔn*) — A distant omnipresent creator whose presence is invoked daily and whose altar stands in compounds.
- **Deities** (*abosom*) — River, lake, forest and local deities chief among them the Tano and Bosomtwe, served by the possession-priesthood of akomfo.
- **Ancestors** (*nsamanfo*) — Embodied in blackened stools, fed at each Adae festival and serving as the immediate court of appeal in family matters.
- **Creator's altar** (*Nyame dua*) — A forked branch holding a pot of water and offerings, once stood in every compound as a daily reminder of divine omnipresence.
