## Overview

The Asante built a powerful mercantile empire from about 1701, dominating the gold and kola trade of the Volta basin until colonial rule in 1901. Their culture is exceptionally rich in regalia, court ceremony, and objects encoded with proverbs and historical meaning. Today, nine to eleven million people speak Asante Twi and practice traditions that blend indigenous religion, Christian faith, and a complex ritual calendar tied to the chief's stool and the ancestors.

## Material culture

Asante material culture is densely layered with meaning: woven patterns carry named identities, cast objects encode proverbs, and the objects themselves—stools, cloths, weights, vessels—serve as shrines, witnesses, and containers of ancestral and spiritual power.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Narrow-strip weaving and block-printed cloth are the foundation of Asante textile art, with patterns that mark rank, occasion, and spiritual purpose.

- **Kente cloth** (*nwentoma*) — Narrow strips about four inches wide are woven by men on a treadle loom, then sewn together; warp stripe sequences carry named identities and supplementary weft-float figures called adwini.
- **Adwinasa** — The most prestigious kente, its ground entirely filled with adwini motifs so no plain warp shows; historically reserved for royals and their attendants.
- **Asasia** — A rare twill-weave silk kente produced only for the Asantehene on a loom with a third heddle; the diagonal structure is forbidden to commoners.
- **Adinkra cloth** — Cotton block-printed with calabash stamps using black dye boiled from badie bark; the oldest use is funerary, with different colours marking the type of death and kinship.
- **Nsaa blankets** — Thick wool blankets with lozenge and chevron bands, imported from Sahelian weavers and used as prestige gifts and litter coverings.

### Clothing & dress

Men wear a large cloth wrapped toga-fashion over one shoulder; women wear layered pieces—wrapper, overpiece, and shoulder cloth—with distinct styles marking rank, office, and life stage.

- **Ntoma** — A large uncut cloth wrapped with the right shoulder and arm bare; made of kente for state occasions, or printed cotton and adinkra otherwise.
- **Ahenema sandals** — Leather sandals with brightly wrapped straps and gilt medallions worn by chiefs; an enstooled chief's bare foot must not touch the ground, making the sandals taboo-equipment as much as footwear.
- **Dansinkran** — A soot-and-shea hair preparation worn by queen mothers and senior royal women, who also crop the hair close and bind the chest in mourning cloth.
- **Akɔmfoɔ priest dress** (*akɔmfoɔ*) — Priests are distinguished by raffia or cotton skirts, white clay marks, and charm-hung bandoliers.

### Architecture

The classic Asante building is a courtyard compound with four rectangular rooms around an open court, decorated with low-relief plaster in spirals and symbolic figures.

- **Courtyard compound** — Four rectangular rooms set around an open rectangular court with one side left as an open-fronted dais; walls are wattle-and-daub over split-palm frames on laterite plinths.
- **Abosomfie shrine houses** (*abosomfie*) — Shrine houses of deities; thirteen examples near Kumasi are inscribed as the UNESCO World Heritage site Asante Traditional Buildings.
- **Plaster decoration** — The lower half of open rooms carries bold low-relief bas-relief in curvilinear arabesques—spirals, concentric circles, crocodile and cockerel figures—picked out in red ochre, white kaolin, and black.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Brass lost-wax casting and blackened pottery vessels serve both daily and ceremonial purposes, with figurative weights and lids that recount proverbs and ancestral narratives.

- **Akan goldweights** (*mrammuo*) — Brass weights cast by lost wax, the earlier ones geometric and the later figurative—beetles, sandals, drums, guns, birds with snakes, whole proverb tableaux—used with scales to measure gold dust.
- **Kuduo** — Lidded cast-brass vessels for gold dust, beads, and personal charms, used at soul-washing rites and buried with their owners; the grandest carry figural lids showing a chief surrounded by attendants.
- **Asante stools** (*dwa*) — Adzed from a single block of osese wood, with a curved seat over a central column and four corner supports; a deceased elder's stool may be blackened and kept as an ancestral shrine.
- **Sika Dwa Kofi** — The Golden Stool, which holds the sunsum of the nation and is never sat upon.
- **Abusua kuruwa** — The family pot, a blackened coil-built terracotta vessel with a lidded, often figurally ringed neck, set after a funeral in the asensie grove of pots.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Gold is cast and hammered into miniature proverb scenes and worn on the body to mark rank, office, and ritual status.

- **Akrafokonmu** — The soul-washer's badge: a chased gold disc with a rosette or concentric-ring face worn at the throat by the boys who purify the king's kra.
- **Cast gold rings** (*mpetea*) — Carry miniature birds, snails, cannon, and human jaws; chiefs' toes, knees, wrists, and hat brims are hung with cast ornaments.
- **Bodom beads** — Large disc-shaped glass beads, with imported aggrey stone beads strung for priests and royals.
- **Waist beads** — Women wear waist beads from girlhood, with puberty and marriage rites marked by the addition of new strings.

## Music & performance

Asante music is organized around drum ensembles, each with its own repertory, dance, and protocol; drum language recites the chief's appellations in reproduced speech tone.

- **Atumpan** — Paired peg-tuned talking drums that recite ayan appellations and historical praise for the chief in reproduced Twi speech tone.
- **Fontomfrom** — Huge barrel drums played in pairs, accompanying the chief's own dance of state.
- **Adowa ensemble** — The ubiquitous ensemble at funerals and public gatherings, built on petia and apentemma drums, the hourglass pressure drum donno, the iron dawuro bell, and ntorowa gourd rattles.
- **Seperewa** — A six- to fourteen-string Akan harp-lute whose repertory fed directly into palm-wine guitar and Kumasi highlife.
- **Mmɛnson trumpet ensemble** (*mmɛnson*) — Seven-horn ensembles of side-blown ivory and animal-horn trumpets that sound the chief's appellations.

## Dance & theatre

Dance forms range from the small-stepping adowa performed at funerals to the chief's martial fontomfrom, with gestures that form a legible vocabulary of meaning.

- **Adowa** — The most widely danced form: small stepping figures with the weight low, the arms and hands doing the real work—gestures point upward to invoke God or cross the wrists to signal captivity.
- **Fontomfrom** — The chief's heavy, martial dance, performed with sword or gun.
- **Akom possession dance** (*akom*) — The possession dance of the ɔkɔmfoɔ, performed to akom drumming until the deity mounts the priest.
- **Concert party** — Twentieth-century Ghanaian travelling comic theatre that rebuilt Ananse as a stage trickster alongside vaudeville routines and highlife bands.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual year runs on adaduanan, a forty-two-day cycle; its twin pivots are Akwasidae and Awukudae, when the chief feeds the ancestral stools and sits in state.

- **Akwasidae** — Falls on a Sunday; the chief enters the stool room, feeds blackened ancestral stools with mashed yam and sheep's blood, then sits in state at a durbar under tiered state umbrellas.
- **Odwira** — The annual purification and new-yam festival that cleanses the stools and state, forbids eating new yam until the ancestors are served, and ends in a night of licensed mourning followed by feasting.
- **Bragoro** — A girl's first menstruation rite, in which she sits in public under an umbrella, is bathed, fed ɛtɔ, and adorned with beads and gold.
- **Ayie funeral** (*ayie*) — The largest ceremony of Asante life: days of drumming, adowa dancing, adinkra mourning cloth, contributions of drink, and afterwards the abusua kuruwa set at the asensie.
- **Tiri nsa** — The head drink presented to the bride's abusua, sealing a marriage.

## Foodways

Forest staples of cassava, plantain, cocoyam, and yam are pounded into fufu or boiled and served with palm-nut and groundnut soups, with meat and fish adding protein.

- **Fufu** — Cassava and plantain pounded together in a wooden mortar into a smooth elastic ball, swallowed in pinches dipped in soup.
- **Abenkwan** — Palm-nut soup thickened with boiled oil-palm fruit pulp, served with fufu.
- **Ɛtɔ** — Mashed yam or plantain worked with palm oil and served with boiled eggs; the ritual dish fed to a girl at bragoro and eaten on one's soul-birthday.
- **Momone** — Smoked and fermented fish, a primary source of protein along with land snails and bushmeat.
- **Nsafufuo and akpeteshie** — Tapped palm wine and its distilled form; schnapps has largely replaced palm wine for mpaeɛ, the poured libation opening any serious gathering.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Ananse spider trickster cycle is the foundation of Asante storytelling, with performance interrupted by mmoguo interlude songs that listeners may start.

- **Anansesɛm** — Tales of Kwaku Ananse the spider, a small, greedy, verbally brilliant trickster who outwits the sky-god and larger animals; the term covers the whole spider-story genre.
- **Ɛbɛ proverbs** — The medium of the ɔkyeame's art, encoded visually in goldweights, adinkra stamps, and staff finials; R. S. Rattray published 830 in 1916.
- **Apae praise-poetry** (*apae*) — Fast praise-poetry shouted for the king by the afenasoafo.
- **Kwadwom dirge** (*kwadwom*) — Archaic royal dirge chanted by royal minstrels.
- **Atumpan drum histories** — Drummed histories that carry the court's curated oral record.

## Language & religion

Asante Twi is a tonal Akan dialect; the indigenous religion recognizes a remote creator Nyame, river and forest deities, the ancestors fed at blackened stools, and manufactured charms.

- **Asante Twi** — One of three literary Akan dialects, tonal and mutually intelligible with Akuapem and Fante; the tonality makes drum speech on the atumpan possible.
- **Onyankopɔn Nyame** — The remote creator, invoked in every libation as the highest power.
- **Abosom deities** (*abosom*) — Deities of rivers and forests, chief among them Tano and the crater lake Bosomtwe.
- **Nsamanfo ancestors** (*nsamanfo*) — The ancestors, who are fed at the blackened stools and honored in funeral rites.
- **Asuman charms** (*asuman*) — Manufactured charms encoding protective medicine and spiritual purpose.

## Glossary

- *nwentoma* — Kente cloth, narrow-strip woven cloth
- *adwini* — Supplementary weft-float figures in kente
- *Asantehene* — The Asante chief, the ruler
- *mrammuo* — Akan goldweights, brass weights for measuring gold dust
- *suman* — Personal protective charm
- *dwa* — Asante stool
- *sunsum* — Spirit, patrilineal soul
- *kra* — Soul, carries a day-name
- *ɔkyeame* — Linguist, the chief's spokesman
- *adaduanan* — Forty-two-day ritual cycle of six seven-day weeks
- *durbar* — Court gathering where the chief sits in state
- *abusua* — Family lineage, matrilineal clan
- *asensie* — Grove of pots, ancestral shrine
- *atumpan* — Paired talking drums
- *ayan* — Appellations, titles and praise names
- *mpaeɛ* — Poured libation offering
- *Nyame* — God, the creator
- *abosom* — Deities of rivers and forests
- *nsamanfo* — Ancestors
- *asuman* — Manufactured protective charms
- *ɔkɔmfoɔ* — Priest or priestess
