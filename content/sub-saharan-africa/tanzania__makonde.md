## Overview

The Makonde culture developed on Mozambique’s Mueda Plateau and is now spread across Tanzania and Mozambique. The Ruvuma River roughly separates Tanzanian Makonde from Mozambican Maconde. Makonde culture is strongly linked to wood carving, masks, initiation rites, sculpture, and performance. Life-cycle ceremonies teach community values and mark the move into adulthood.

## Material culture

Wood is the main material in Makonde art, ritual objects, household objects, masks, figures, and sculptures.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources do not document a Makonde textile tradition, but they describe important carved styles and patterns.

- **Tree of Life carvings** (*Ujamaa*) — Connected human forms climb over one another to express family, unity, and mutual support.
- **Spirit carvings** (*Shetani*) — Abstract or partly abstract figures show spirits with distorted human, animal, or otherworldly features.
- **Naturalistic human carvings** (*Binadamu*) — This style shows people, daily life, work, family, and social roles.
- **Strength of power carvings** (*Dimoongo*) — This style shows connected wrestlers supporting a winner, or people united with family and community.
- **Mask scarification patterns** (*Mapiko*) — Some masks have beeswax patterns that represent scarification.

### Clothing & dress

Dress changes during initiation and marks the move into a new social status.

- **Men’s initiation clothing** — After months of initiation, boys return to their families as men with new names, clothing, and celebrations.
- **Girls’ initiation garments** — After a ceremonial bath, girls are oiled and dressed in new garments made for the occasion.
- **Embroidered skull cap** (*Kofia*) — A museum catalogue records a kofia on a painted wooden lipiko mask showing a Swahili trader.

### Architecture

The sources do not describe Makonde house forms or settlements, but they state that wood was traditionally used to build houses.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Makonde carvers used local wood and simple tools to make ritual, household, and everyday objects.

- **Soft plateau wood** (*Njala*) — Carvers used njala, a soft wood found on the Mueda Plateau.
- **Wooden club** — Museum catalogues record a wooden club.
- **Wooden snuff-box** — A wooden snuff-box with decorative engraving is recorded in a museum catalogue.
- **Wooden shield** — A museum catalogue records a wooden shield covered with black and white cowhide.
- **Carved staffs and figures** — Museum catalogues record wooden staffs and figures.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Tattoos and mask adornments mark identity, initiation, and imagined human features.

- **Initiation tattoos** — Face and body tattoos traditionally marked young men and women as Makonde adults.
- **Wooden lip plugs** — Museum catalogues record wooden lip plugs, also shown on female masks.
- **Ear spools** — Female masks may include ear spools.
- **Mask earrings** — Masks may have aluminium earrings.
- **Beaded eyes** — A female wooden figure has glass beads inset as eyes.

## Music & performance

Music and performance are central to initiation, teaching community values through song, dance, stories, and art.

- **Girls’ initiation singing and drumming** (*Ciputu*) — During ciputu, girls take a ceremonial bath while the community sings and drums outside.
- **Initiation teaching** — Children learn behavior and community relationships through routines, stories, music, and art.
- **Ritual figures** — Clay and wooden figures represent proverbs and morals during initiation instruction.

## Dance & theatre

Mapiko is the main named dance tradition and uses masks to show spirits, ancestors, dead people, and living characters.

- **Masked rite dance** (*Mapiko*) — Masked dancers perform during Makonde rites of passage.
- **Mask** (*Lipiko*) — A lipiko is one mask, while mapiko means several masks.
- **Evil spirit character** (*Mashapilo*) — In male initiation, a dancer represents Mashapilo, an evil spirit linked with malice and disturbed health.
- **Returned dead man** — One masked dancer represents a dead man who has returned to haunt the village.
- **Girls’ ceremonial dance** (*Mdimu*) — A ceremonial mdimu dance is performed by masked mapiko dancers at the end of girls’ initiation.

## Festivals & rituals

Makonde rituals mark the move from childhood to adulthood and maintain balance between the living and the dead.

- **Boys’ initiation** — Boys undergo seclusion, instruction, circumcision, and a months-long process before returning as men.
- **Girls’ puberty rite** (*Ciputu*) — Ciputu prepares girls for womanhood through seclusion, teaching, bathing, oiling, new clothes, song, drumming, and dance.
- **Ceremonial bath** — Girls take a ceremonial bath at the end of their seclusion and instruction.
- **Fire symbolism** — Fire represents new life and the continuity of the cultural group.
- **Life-force balance** — Makonde spirituality connects the living and the dead, and ritual protects this balance.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources do not name Makonde literary works, but initiation uses stories, proverbs, and teaching figures.

- **Initiation stories** — Children receive instruction through stories and routines during initiation.
- **Moral figures** — Wooden and clay figures represent proverbs and morals.

## Language & religion

Makonde is a Bantu language linked to ancestor worship, animism, and continuing Muslim or Catholic affiliations.

- **Makonde language** (*ChiMakonde*) — Makonde, also called ChiMakonde, is a central Bantu language closely related to Yao.
- **Swahili name** (*Kimakonde*) — Kimakonde is the Swahili name for the language.
- **Portuguese name** (*Maconde*) — Maconde is the Portuguese name for the language and for Makonde people in Mozambique.
- **Ancestor worship** — Traditional Makonde religion is an animistic form of ancestor worship.
- **Sorcery and healing** — Sorcery and witchcraft remain active systems, and sorcerers may be healers, protectors, leaders, and spiritual authorities.

## Glossary

- *Mapiko* — plural masks or masked dance
- *Lipiko* — one Makonde mask
- *Ujamaa* — Tree of Life carving style
- *Shetani* — spirit figure carving style
- *Binadamu* — naturalistic human carving style
- *Dimoongo* — strength of power carving style
- *Njala* — soft wood from the Mueda Plateau
- *Kofia* — embroidered Islamic skull cap
- *Ciputu* — girls’ puberty rite
- *Mdimu* — ceremonial dance
- *Mashapilo* — evil spirit in male initiation
- *ChiMakonde* — another name for Makonde
- *Kimakonde* — Makonde name in Swahili
- *Maconde* — Makonde name in Portuguese
