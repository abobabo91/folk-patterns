## Overview

The Bara are a Malagasy ethnic group living principally in the southern part of the central plateaus of Madagascar, especially in the Toliara Province and the Ihosy-Betroka area. Their history begins along the Ihosy River in the Arindrano region of southwest Madagascar, near Toliara, and Ihosy was their historic capital. An estimated 520,000 Bara lived in Madagascar in 2000, constituting roughly three percent of the population. The Bara are the island’s largest zebu-herding people and have historically followed a semi-nomadic lifestyle, although agriculture has become increasingly important. They speak Bara Malagasy, a dialect of Malagasy, which belongs to the Malayo-Polynesian language group and derives from the Barito languages of southern Borneo. Their folk culture is closely associated with zebu herding, cattle raiding, oral storytelling, dance, weaving, ancestor rites and distinctive musical traditions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Cotton weaving** — Bara women traditionally weave local cotton for clothing. Cotton is hand picked, ginned and woven using a high-whorl drop spindle. Raw yarn is dipped in a softening solution before weaving; before softening it is called *fole velo* (“living yarn”) and is believed to possess magical powers.

**Silk weaving** — Silk, called *landy*, was produced from the local silkworm that fed on indigenous tapia trees growing in the Isalo area. Bara silk was coarser and more uneven than silk produced by the Merina or Betsileo, but was prized across the island for its durability.

**Bark cloth** — In the far eastern part of Bara territory, clothing was made from beaten bark cloth. Men used the material for loincloths, while jackets and tunics were formed from stitched woven mats.

**Harefo mats** — Mats made from *harefo* (*Eleocharis plantagines*) were used in clothing. Women wore tube dresses made from two or three mats stitched together and tied at the shoulder or belted at the waist.

**Mahampy weaving** — Woven *mahampy* reeds were used to make bandeau-style tops worn with women’s tube dresses.

**Woven mats** — Stitched woven mats formed men’s jackets and tunics. They were also used in household life and in the preparation of cotton yarn.

Motif vocabulary. The sources name no Bara textile motifs or patterned design vocabulary.

### Clothing & dress

Bara women traditionally made clothing from locally woven cotton. In the far eastern part of Bara territory, women wore tube dresses made from two or three stitched *harefo* mats, tied at the shoulder or belted at the waist, often with a bandeau-style top of woven *mahampy* reeds. Men wore beaten bark-cloth loincloths with jackets or tunics made from stitched woven mats; older men’s clothing included sleeves. Silk clothing was made from *landy*, while cotton yarn was prepared with a softening solution before weaving. The sources describe clothing materials and garment forms.

### Architecture

The Bara live in rectangular earthen houses. The soil’s high iron content gives the houses a red colour. During winter, the space beneath the eaves is used for hanging and sun-drying maize intended for storage, sale or planting in the following year.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do describe the *hazomanga*, a wooden pillar that served as the most sacred public altar for group prayer and offerings in each village, and a *kiady*, a special barrier erected during certain healing rituals.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 Wearing charms is common among the Bara. Charms called *tsimokotra* were historically made from the bones of lemurs’ feet to relieve fatigue. Raw cotton yarn called *fole velo* could be tied around the wrist for protection, or wrapped around participants in circumcision ceremonies. During mourning, bereaved family members cut their hair to express sorrow.

## Music & performance

The most typical Bara musical instrument is the *lokanga*, a three-string fiddle made with goat gut or vegetable fibre. The contemporary dance and musical tradition *kilalaky* originated among Bara cattle rustlers and has become popular across Madagascar. Its musical accompaniment is performed on djembe, kabosy, electric guitar, bass guitar, drum kit and keyboard. Bagzana and Tsiliva are identified as popular performers of *kilalaky*. The sources associate this tradition with cattle rustling and with the public visibility of Bara dance and music.

Bara dance is also connected to zebu herding. The *karitaky* dance imitates the movement of a zebu kicking.

## Dance & theatre

The most popular contemporary Bara dance is *kilalaky*, performed in a line and originating among Bara cattle rustlers. Its associated musical style is also called *kilalaky*. The *karitaky* dance is inspired by a zebu kicking. These sources describe dance and musical performance.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do describe life-cycle, healing, ancestor and mourning practices.

At birth, a village *ombiasy*, or seer, was traditionally consulted to determine whether a child was destined for a fortunate or unhappy fate. If the fate was considered unhappy, an exorcism could be performed in which the infant was slid down a chute into a body of water and retrieved by the mother. If the evil destiny was judged too strong, the infant was left on an anthill to die. During childbirth, the mother was secluded for four days; men watched over the birthing process and conducted associated rites.

Cattle raiding was traditionally a rite of passage for young men. A young man was not expected to marry respectably without having successfully stolen at least one or two cattle in a raid. Circumcision ceremonies could involve *fole velo* yarn being wrapped around participants. Agreements were formalized through a blood pact called *fatidra*.

The Bara entomb the dead in natural mountain caves, particularly in Isalo National Park, which they consider sacred. Where caves are unavailable, they build stone-covered tombs away from the village. Mourners visit the bereaved in the “house of many tears,” where women perform ritual wailing. The spirits of the dead are believed to remain as ghosts, and villages historically relocated after a death. During *bilo* or *salamanga* healing rituals, an *ombiasy* may erect a *kiady* barrier to prevent people associated with a recent death, foreigners or strangers from entering.

## Foodways

Raising and selling zebu herds is the principal Bara economic activity, and agriculture has increasingly included rice, cassava, millet and maize. The rice-planting season was traditionally timed around the arrival of a local migrating quail, *kibodolo* (*Coturnix communis*). In winter, maize is hung beneath the eaves of rectangular earthen houses to dry before it is stored, sold or planted the following year. They do describe food-related household work: men traditionally prepared the soil of rice fields and purchased or hunted food, while women grew crops for sale or family consumption, weeded rice fields, harvested and processed rice, gathered water and prepared daily meals. A fady forbade serving food or drink to someone with utensils, plates or cups previously used to serve another person.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Bara have a rich oral storytelling tradition. Their myths and stories are distinguished by a stark and terse use of language. A comparatively simple structure and symbolism in Bara storytelling has been used by linguists and anthropologists as a starting point for analyzing the evolution and variation of oral storytelling traditions on Madagascar. Bara history and social memory are connected to the traditions of kingship, cattle raiding, ancestors and the *fady*, the ancestral taboos that regulate social life.

## Language & religion

The Bara speak Bara Malagasy, a dialect of the Malagasy language. Malagasy belongs to the Malayo-Polynesian language group and is derived from the Barito languages spoken in southern Borneo.

Some Bara are Christians, but most retain traditional religious beliefs. Tamarind trees and the indigenous bontona tree were traditionally sacred. Offerings left near tamarind trees include woven mats, locks of hair, shells and other items. Charms were tied to bontona branches during prayer. Each village had a *hazomanga*, a wooden pillar considered its most sacred place of worship and public altar; it was typically 20 to 30 feet high, with a sachet containing sacred herbs, sand and wood chips hung from the top.

Bara men traditionally conducted rites honouring ancestors, while women conducted rites associated with fertility and the forces of nature. Social life is strongly guided by *fady*, ancestral taboos that vary by class, village and family.
