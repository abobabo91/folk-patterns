## Overview

The Bété are an ethnic group within Côte d'Ivoire, representing approximately 18% of the population as of 2010. They are concentrated in the southwestern portion of the country, around the city of Gagnoa and the Gagnoa Department. The Bété are a tribe of the Kru ethnic group, with 93 distinct subgroups. Their languages form a dialect continuum within the Kru language group. Bété cultural and artistic links with the Dan, the We (Gwere), the Guéré and the Guro are reflected particularly in mask dances. Their history includes resistance to French colonial rule, conversion to Christianity, cash-crop farming, and complex relationships between village, clan and imposed colonial ethnic categories.

## Material culture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They state that Bété statues are less common and less complex than masks in traditional culture. These statues are used in ceremonies commemorating female ancestors and are described as simple, sober, and abstracted.

## Music & performance

 They do describe masked dancing as a central part of traditional Bété socioreligious practice. Creativity is valued both in mask design and in the dances in which masks are used. Masked events have traditionally served several social and ritual purposes, including cleansing and protecting the village, performing funeral rites, greeting visitors and dignitaries, expressing social criticism, overseeing judicial proceedings, and leading men to war. In modern times, masks are primarily used for entertainment, at funerals, or to mark the end of a mourning period.

## Dance & theatre

Bété are especially associated with complex masks and mask dances. The culture of making and wearing masks came from the We (Guéré) people and spread to neighboring cultures, including the Bété. Bété masks are divided into three categories. A Great Mask is traditionally limited to one per village and serves as the repository of the village’s spiritual forces, protecting the community. “Middle” masks are well-constructed and consecrated masks used by dancers at festivals and other community events; a village can possess several, and they can be used simultaneously in ceremonies. “Training” masks are used by boys while practicing mask dances.

## Festivals & rituals

 They describe mask dances at festivals and other community events, and record several ritual and civic functions for masked performances: cleansing and protecting the village, funeral rites, greeting visitors and dignitaries, social criticism, judicial proceedings, and leading men to war. In contemporary contexts, masks are used primarily for entertainment, funerals, and marking the end of a mourning period.

Bété society is patrilineal. Traditionally, inheritance is adelphic: a man’s heirs are first his brothers and cousins of his generation, and only after all of them have died can his children inherit his possessions. Polygyny has traditionally been practiced among the Bété and other ethnic groups of Côte d’Ivoire. In a 1985 survey in Gagnoa, 45% of Bété women were polygamously married, despite a 1964 government prohibition.

## Foodways

Under colonial rule, the Bété began farming cash crops, specifically cocoa and coffee.

## Oral tradition & literature

Bété oral history states that the people lived in the Sahel or West Sudanian savanna until warfare in the 17th century led them to emigrate to what is now the southwestern portion of Côte d'Ivoire, around Gagnoa and the Gagnoa Department.

The sources describe the first writing system for the Bété language, the Bété syllabary, developed by Bété artist Frédéric Bruly Bouabré in the 1950s.

## Language & religion

The Bété languages are a cluster of Kru languages spoken in central-western Ivory Coast. They include many dialects grouped in the sources as Western Bété of Gagnoa and Kouya, and Eastern Bété of Guiberoua, Bété of Daloa, and Godié. The first writing system was the Bété syllabary, developed by Frédéric Bruly Bouabré in the 1950s.

Much of the population is Christian, specifically Roman Catholic. In the late 1970s, a wave of conversion to Islam occurred among the Bété, influenced by French-language reformist preaching. Traditional mask practices are connected with village spiritual forces, protection, cleansing, funerals, judicial proceedings, and war.
