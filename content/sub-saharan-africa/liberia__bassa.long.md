## Overview

The Bassa are a West African ethnic group primarily native to Liberia and a subgroup of the larger Kru peoples of Liberia and Ivory Coast. They form a majority or significant minority in Grand Bassa, Rivercess, Margibi, Montserrado and Bong counties. Small communities are also found in Sierra Leone and Ivory Coast. Their overall population is described as about 1.1 million, making them the second-largest ethnic group in Liberia at 18 percent. The Bassa language is a Kru language within the Niger-Congo family. Bassa culture includes lineage-linked villages, farming, lagoon fishing, craft occupations, initiation societies, masks, brasswork, oral traditions and an indigenous writing system. Their material culture is represented in the cited museum records by masks, a brass-related regional context, a skin quiver with arrows, a woven fish trap and a small wooden mask from Grand Bassa.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Geometric ornament on a skin quiver** — A museum record describes a skin quiver from Grand Bassa with an outer leather covering ornamented at the top in geometric patterns. The record does not identify a named Bassa textile tradition or compare the decoration with neighboring cultures.

**Woven basket construction** — The sources state that some Bassa masks are fastened to the forehead area of a woven basket worn by the performer. The museum records do not identify a vernacular name for this basket or describe its weaving technique.

**Braided bonnet** — Bassa mask performers are described as wearing a braided bonnet fastened at an angle to the forehead. No further information about its fibers, pattern vocabulary or production is given.

### Clothing & dress

The sources provide little information about everyday clothing. They describe special clothing associated with masks: a performer may wear a braided bonnet fastened at an angle to the forehead, or a woven basket to which a mask is attached. A British Museum record describes a wooden helmet mask whose edges are pierced for attachment to a costume.

### Architecture

Inland Forest Bassa villages are intentionally arranged in clusters of huts that appear disorganized but are placed so that a person looking in any direction encounters huts and has difficulty establishing bearings. The layout lacks a main road and includes empty lots, making movement through the village difficult for outsiders. The selection of a village location depends on access to fresh water, good farmland and certain reeds used as roofing material.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Bassa are described as having occupational categories including farmers, barterers, traders, lagoon fishermen, blacksmiths, carvers, weavers, potters and other craftsmen. Their brasswork includes *ma go*, meaning “small head,” miniature brass-cast masks made for the Dan, with protruding open lips, flared nostrils, pierced slit eyes and a tattoo pattern extending from forehead to chin. Another object is the Brass Finial, an iron rod cast over with brass. Its top features a woman’s head with five rows of hair running from front to back and an encircling herringbone pattern. The museum records also describe a skin quiver with five arrows, iron barbed points, an inner wooden quiver and an outer leather covering, and a woven fish trap from Grand Bassa.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources discuss brass jewelry primarily in relation to the neighboring Dan. Dan women wore substantial brass jewelry, including anklets and bracelets, and the quantity of brass was associated with the husband’s wealth. The jewelry was banned in the late 1930s by Liberia’s minister of health, Rudolf Fuszek, after which surplus brass was melted down and recast. The sources connect Bassa production of *ma go* miniature masks with this surplus brass.

## Music & performance

The sources describe performance in relation to masks. Some masks are intended to project reverence and fear through monstrous faces with protruding eyes, swollen lips and contorted human and animal features. Other masks are portrait masks made by skilled carvers according to sculptural laws passed down by masters of the craft. Ancestor masks embody concepts of beauty and are intended to give leniency, peace and promises of peace to heirs; they are deliberately not made to resemble specific individuals. “Devil” masks are worn by soothsayers and performers such as acrobats and jugglers, who are described as agents of social control. One mask type was made to resemble a disfigured person so that a performer could dance before crowds and tell jokes, giving the person’s features a positive association and helping prevent social exclusion.

## Dance & theatre

Bassa mask performance includes dancing, crowd interaction and joking. A mask made in the likeness of a disfigured person was used by a performer who danced with crowds and told jokes as part of a deliberate social act. “Devil” masks were worn by soothsayers and performers including acrobats and jugglers. Other masks represented spirits from the darkest parts of the forest or ancestor figures.

## Festivals & rituals

The Bassa are among the peoples who practice the women’s initiation society called *Sande*, also referred to as *Bondo*. The practice includes rituals involving special helmet masks. The mask is worn by a society official during special ceremonies and represents the idealized beauty of a female primordial ancestor spirit believed to reside in bodies of water. The sources state that the primordial ancestor spirit originates from a body of water during a sacred journey by a society member or official. The mask itself is not given a separate name; *Sowei* or *sowo* refers to the title of the association official who wears the helmet mask.

The sources also describe secret rites of passage for men and women connected with the Sande society and other initiation traditions.

## Foodways

The Bassa are traditionally settled farmers who grow yam, cassava, eddoes and plantain. Before colonial occupation, their occupational categories also included lagoon fishermen, farmers and barterers or traders.

## Oral tradition & literature

Bassa oral history recounts movements from Ivory Coast through Eastern Guinea, Grand Gedeh and the coast. The sources associate the original name *Gbooh* or *Gbɔ̌ɔ̀* with the meaning “People of the East.” They also relate the name Bassa to *Bassa Sooh Nyombe*, translated as “Father Stone’s people,” and describe early European traders shortening the phrase. One account names Ɓǎ Gìà-xwí Sàwá, Ɓǎ vɛ̀nɛ̀, and Ɓǎ Sɔ́ɔ́ as leaders associated with the journey and with relations between Bassa traders and Europeans. The sources also describe oral history concerning the Nyanya kingdom or state, the Dii artifact and conflicts among Kru groups.

A Bassa legend says that the Brass Finial was the walking stick of a mythical ancestor named Sma Vlen, who migrated toward the coast during the 16th century.

## Language & religion

Bassa is a Kru language within the Niger-Congo family. The Bassa language is described as a dialect continuum with Western, Central, Rivercess and Eastern Bassa clusters. The sources name an indigenous writing system called Ehni Ka Se Fa and also describe the Vah alphabet, associated with Thomas Flo Lewis. Vah is described as alphabetic, with 30 consonants, seven vowels and five tones indicated by dots and lines inside vowels. The IPA has largely replaced Vah in publications, although Vah remains respected and is used by some older men for record keeping. The first Bible was translated into Bassa in 1922, and the entire Bible was published in Bassa in 2005.

Traditional Bassa religion has a moral and ethical foundation that reveres ancestors and supernatural spirits. Christianity arrived during the colonial era, and Christian ideas were fused with the traditional idea of a Supreme Being and powerful first ancestor. Contemporary Bassa people predominantly practice Christianity while retaining elements of traditional religion.
