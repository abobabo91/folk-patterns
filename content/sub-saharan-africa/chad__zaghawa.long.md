## Overview

The Zaghawa, also called Beri or Zakhawa, are an ethnic group living in Libya, northeastern Chad, and western Sudan, including Darfur. Their language is spoken especially in east-central Chad, northwestern Sudan, and, by a smaller number of people, Libya. The Zaghawa language is an eastern Saharan language and is also described as Nilo-Saharan; its speakers call it Beria, from Beri and *a*, “Zaghawa” for “mouth.” The Zaghawa have been estimated at 384,150 people, while the language has about 447,400 native speakers. Their folk-culture profile in the supplied sources centres on pastoralism, nomadic clans, camel and livestock keeping, pottery, basketry, caste-based artisan work, Islamic practice, and the material culture recorded around Kebkebiya and Kireyka in Darfur.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery is an inherited occupation of the artisan castes called the *Hadaheed* or *Hadahid*. The supplied museum records show women working with clay vessels in Kebkebiya, North Darfur, Sudan. One woman is described scraping the inside of a clay vessel with a piece of gourd; another is working with a lump of clay; a third is holding a stone beside a round clay vessel. The photographs also show a sieve, containers, and a vegetable-fibre basket called “reika.” The records do not identify particular ceramic forms, decoration, metal objects, or woodworking traditions. Ironwork is listed among the inherited occupations of the artisan castes, whose work was traditionally regarded within Zaghawa society as dirty and of inferior status.

## Festivals & rituals

The Zaghawa accepted the Maliki school of Sunni Islam while retaining some pre-Islamic rites. One named practice is *karama*, described as a ritual sacrifice of animals to ward off evil spirits. The sources state that the century of conversion to Islam is debated, with estimates ranging from the 13th to the early 17th century. They also state that Zaghawa leadership converted to Islam as a result of Tijani Muslim missionaries from West Africa travelling through the area to make the Hajj, and that in the 1940s the Zaghawa began turning to Islam from their traditional religion en masse. The supplied sources do not describe an annual festival calendar, seasonal ceremonies, birth rites, coming-of-age rites, weddings, or funerals.

## Foodways

The sources describe pastoral livelihoods based on cattle, camels, and sheep, together with the harvesting of wild grains. In contemporary times, the Zaghawa are described as leading a sedentary lifestyle and growing millet, sorghum, sesame, melons, pumpkins, peanuts, and okra. The sources do not name dishes, cooking methods, beverages, ceremonial foods, or dietary rules. They state that the Zaghawa accepted the Maliki school of Sunni Islam, but do not provide further food-related religious practice.

## Oral tradition & literature

The supplied sources do not describe a Zaghawa epic, named folktale cycle, proverb tradition, riddle tradition, or oral-poetry genre. They do identify two published collections, *Contes Zaghawa du Tchad*, containing thirty-seven tales and two legends. A Zaghawa tribesman named Daoud Hari wrote *The Translator*, a memoir about Darfur, and a Zaghawa woman named Halima Bashir co-authored *Tears of the Desert* with Damien Lewis; the sources state that both works spread knowledge about atrocities in Darfur. The language article also lists *Contes Zaghawa du Tchad* among works concerning the Zaghawa language and culture.

## Language & religion

Zaghawa is spoken by the Zaghawa people of east-central Chad and northwestern Sudan, especially Darfur, and by a smaller number of people in Libya. The sources describe it as both an eastern Saharan and a Nilo-Saharan language. Speakers call it Beria, from Beri and *a*, “Zaghawa” for “mouth.” Named clan or dialect groupings include Beria, Tuba, Kobe, and Wegi; the listed Tuba forms include Biria and Brogat, while the Kobe forms include Dirong, Guruf, Kobe, and Kapka. A Zaghawa Latin alphabet was created by Adam Tajir in the 1950s from clan identification marks, sometimes called the camel alphabet. In 2000, Siddick Adam Issa prepared Beria Giray Erfe, and in 2007 the system was turned into a computer font by Seonil Yun in cooperation with SIL International and the Mission Protestante Franco-Suisse au Tchad. An Arabic-script alphabet is also under development. Religiously, the Zaghawa accepted Maliki Sunni Islam while retaining practices such as *karama*.
