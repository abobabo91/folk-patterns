## Overview

The Tsonga people, also called *Vatsonga*, are a Bantu ethnic group primarily native to Southern Mozambique, especially Inhambane, Gaza and Maputo, and to South Africa, particularly Limpopo and Mpumalanga. Smaller numbers are found in Zimbabwe and Northern Eswatini. They speak Xitsonga, a Southern Bantu language. In 2011, there were 7.3 million Shangaan speakers, including 4 million speakers in Mozambique and 3.3 million in South Africa; smaller populations were recorded in Eswatini and Zimbabwe. Tsonga communities have been connected through language, cultural integration, trade, traditional leadership and related clan histories. Their folk culture includes initiation practices, traditional healing, dances, music, oral literature, agriculture, pastoralism and objects made from wood, fibre, reeds, pottery, metal and gourds.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources do not document named Tsonga textile traditions or distinctive pattern systems beyond the catalogue descriptions of fibre, bead and textile objects.

**Fibre-cord skirts** — Museum records describe a girl’s skirt made of fibre cords, a skirt made of fibre cords with blue and white bead ornaments below the girdle, and a skirt made of grass or palm leaf strung on a fibre cord.

**Waist-cloth** — A museum record describes a male wooden figure together with a length of cotton textile used as a waist-cloth.

**Reed mats** — A catalogue record identifies a mat made of reeds.

**Beaded drinking vessel** — A gourd drinking vessel is decorated with burnt figures and white glass beads.

**Gourd decoration** — Two vessel records describe gourds inlaid with white beads and decorated with incised geometric motifs in black; one also depicts various animals and has a suspension loop of vegetable fibre tied around the neck.

**Motif vocabulary.** Incised geometric line patterns; burnt figures; depictions of various animals; pokerwork motif.

### Architecture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Museum records document pottery vessels, including a vessel with an everted rim, a red burnished body and additional graphite burnished decoration, and another with a red burnished body, black burnished neck and projecting lip. Metal and wooden tools include daggers, knives and a ceremonial battleaxe with a wooden shaft. One iron knife has a corrugated blade, wooden handle and sheath; another lacks a sheath. Other objects include wooden head-rests, snuff-boxes, a wooden bowl with handles and a carved wooden chain, a wooden receptacle with an incised pattern, a reed and palm-leaf food basket, and gourds decorated with beads, burnt figures and incised motifs.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The catalogue records include a wooden necklace with alternately orientated carved, bi-conical beads resembling vertebrae and six small antelope horns imitating lions’ claws. Metal bangles are decorated on their outer and inner rims with incised linear motifs. Bracelets are made of fibre and beads. A Cleveland Museum of Art staff from the 1800s–1900s bears carved male heads with an ornament signifying maturity and marriage; the associated hairdo is called *isicoco* and uses a fibre or sinew ring into which the wearer’s hair was woven. Other records include snuff-boxes, snuff-bottles and a bone divination-tablet. The sources do not describe tattooing, henna or further adornment practices.

## Music & performance

The sources describe several forms of Tsonga music. More traditional dance music was associated with General MD Shirinda, Fanny Mpfumo, Matshwa Bemuda and Thomas Chauke. Tsonga disco and Tsonga ndzhumbha were popularized by Joe Shirimani, Penny Penny, Peta Teanet and Benny Mayengani. A more westernized sound using English words, sampled vocals and heavy synthesizers is called Shangaan electro in Europe and was pioneered by Nozinja, the Tshetsha Boys and DJ Khwaya.

The museum catalogue includes a marimba from Inhambane, Mozambique, described as a musical instrument with two wooden sticks. It also records a stringed trough zither made from a rectangular tray with seven strings of vegetable fibre, decorated with copper and brass wire ornament and a string loop beneath. The sources do not describe the performance contexts, tuning, repertoire or social roles of these instruments. They also do not document named song genres, wedding music, funeral music or court performance.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name the **Makhwaya**, **Xighubu**, **Mchongolo** and **Xibelani** dances as traditional dances of the Tsonga people. They also describe Tsonga disco and Tsonga ndzhumbha as experimental or popularized musical genres connected with Tsonga communities. The sources do not explain the choreography, costumes, occasions, ceremonial meanings or distinctions between entertainment and ritual performance. They do not document a Tsonga theatrical or dramatic tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do not identify an annual Tsonga festival calendar or provide names and dates for seasonal festivals. They do describe life-cycle initiation practices. Tsonga men traditionally attend an initiation school for circumcision called *Ngoma*, also known as *Hogo*, after which they are regarded as men. Young teenage girls attend an initiation school led by older Vatsonga women called *Khomba*; participants are called *tikhomba*. The sources state that only virgins may attend and that the instruction concerns womanhood, conduct as *tikhomba* in the community and preparation for marriage.

Traditional healing also involves ritual initiation. A legend names Nkomo We Lwandle and Dunga Manzi as early Tsonga diviners. A powerful water serpent, Nzunzu, allegedly captured them and submerged them in deep waters. The legend is re-enacted during a diviner’s initiation by ceremoniously submerging initiates in water, from which they emerge as diviners. The sources do not cover weddings, funerals, births or annual religious celebrations.

## Foodways

The Tsonga traditional economy is based on mixed agriculture and pastoralism. Cassava is the staple, while corn, millet, sorghum and other crops are also grown. Women perform much of the agricultural work, while men and teenage boys care for domestic animals, including cows, sheep and goats. Some men grow cash crops. Most Tsongas now have jobs in South Africa and Mozambique.

A British Museum catalogue records a food basket made of reed and palm leaf, but the sources do not identify named dishes, cooking methods, drinks, ceremonial foods, tea or coffee traditions, dietary rules or the preparation of food and beverages. A gourd drinking vessel is recorded, decorated with burnt figures and white glass beads, but its contents are not specified.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources document Tsonga folktales, songs, riddles and proverbs. E. Dora Earthy published Tsonga folktales from the Lenge dialect with facing-text English translations in the 1937 volume of *Folklore*. Henri-Alexandre Junod included Tsonga song lyrics with English translations in his 1913 study *The Life of a South African Tribe*, and also recorded Tsonga riddles and proverbs.

One riddle asks what a mother dearly loves but cannot run to meet on her return home; the answer is the unborn babe in the womb. Another asks what can be beaten without leaving a scar; the answer is water. Recorded proverbs include one about swallowing a large stone and having confidence in the size of one’s throat, one about tattooing marks on the back being known by the tattooer, and one stating that the strength of the crocodile is water. Junod later published *Quelques Proverbes Thonga* in 1931. The sources do not describe an epic tradition or contemporary literary revival beyond the recorded publications.

## Language & religion

Tsonga, or Xitsonga, is a Bantu language spoken by Tsonga people in South Africa and Mozambique. It is mutually intelligible with Tswa and Ronga, and the name Tsonga is sometimes used as a cover term for all three, also called Tswa-Ronga. Xitsonga is an official language of the Republic of South Africa and was officially recognized in the Constitution of South Africa in 1996. It is standardized as a written language and uses the Latin alphabet. The sources name dialects including xiRonga, xiHlanganu, xiBila, xiDjonga, xiN’walungu and xiHlengwe.

Tsonga people acknowledge ancestors, who are believed to affect the lives of their descendants. Traditional healers are called *n’anga*. Spirits identified in the sources include the Ngoni, Ndau and Malopo. The sources do not provide a general account of Tsonga religious affiliation beyond ancestral practice, divination and healing.
