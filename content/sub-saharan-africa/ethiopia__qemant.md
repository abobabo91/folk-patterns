## Overview

The Qemant, also known as western Agaws, are a small Cushitic ethnic group in northwestern Ethiopia, specifically in Gondar and the Amhara Region. They live in an area traditionally called Qwara, along an axis from Ayikel in Chilga woreda to Kirakir and north to Lake Tana in the woredas of Lay Armachiho. Their population was reported as 172,000 in the 1994 national census, while only 1,625 people still speak Qimant. Most remaining speakers live near Ayikel, about 40 miles (64 km) west of Gondar. The Qemant are mainly farmers. Their importance for folk-culture research lies in their endangered language, distinctive social organization, Hebraic religious practices, sacred groves, priesthood, and oral traditions.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Qemant worship was traditionally conducted outdoors, usually near a sacred tree called a *k'ole*, which was wrapped in variously colored strips of cloth. Because of dislike of being observed by increasingly pervasive Christians, the Qemant eventually constructed a prayer-hall at Chelga.

## Music & performance

They do record religious and judicial roles associated with the Qemant *womber*, the highest political and religious leader. The *womber* chose delegates called *afa liq*, an Amharic title meaning “mouth of the master,” to represent him in judicial matters. These men traveled through the countryside, settled disputes, saw that laws were obeyed, and punished wrongdoing, usually with a fine.

## Festivals & rituals

Qemant religious observance includes a literal reading of the 11th chapter of Leviticus. Their practices include animal sacrifices and the tending of sacred groves called *degegna*. Worship is conducted outdoors, usually at a site near a sacred tree called a *k'ole*, wrapped in strips of cloth of various colors. Shabbat is observed on Saturday, when lighting a fire is forbidden. The sources state that the Qemant also practiced levirate marriage, similar to the Jewish practice of *yibbum*.

There were formerly two superior *wombers*, at Karkar and Chelga, with the first senior, and a varying number of subordinate *wombers* in other parts of Qemantland. The last *womber* of Karkar died in 1955. The only *womber* since then has been Mulunah Marsha, *womber* of Chelga, born 1935. Each *womber* chose two stewards, one from each moiety, who served different elements of sacred meals.

Two orders of priesthood conducted sacrifices and other religious ceremonies: the *kamazana*, the higher order of the Kïbbïr moiety, and the *abayegariya*, the lower order of the Yetanti moiety. During a sacrifice, the *abayegaria* holds the victim’s legs while the *kamazana* wields the knife.

## Foodways

Qemant religious observance includes dietary rules associated with a literal reading of the 11th chapter of Leviticus. As with Rabbinic Judaism, permitted animals can only be consumed if they are properly slaughtered. The sources also state that each *womber* chose two stewards from the two moieties who served different elements of sacred meals. No staple grains, named dishes, cooking methods, beverages, or coffee and tea traditions are documented.

## Oral tradition & literature

According to Qemant tradition and history, everything began when Noah’s ark settled at the top of Mount Ararat, also believed to be Mount Guna in the northern Amhara region. The meaning of “guna” in the Qemant language is “starting point.” Qemants are said to descend from Yaner, also called Ayaner, described as “the grandson of Canaan, the fourth son of Ham, son of Noah.” Some legends state that Canaan’s son Arwadi came to Ethiopia from the land of Canaan to found the Qemant group; other legends identify Arwadi’s son Yaner as its founder.

The sources also state that Noah was buried in present-day Fasil Ghebbi and that his wife Aykel was buried in Aykel city, which was named after her. Because of a lack of written evidence, the origin of the Qemant is obscure for some scholars.

## Language & religion

Qimant is a Cushitic language. Only 1,625 people still speak it, and it is considered endangered because most children speak Amharic. The Qemant traditionally practiced a religion often described as “Hebraic,” or Judaism, and are historically related to, but distinct from, the Beta Israel. A recent sociolinguistic survey described the religion as being in a precarious situation because of rapid assimilation. About 1% of Qemant people follow the Qemant religion, while about 99% are baptized and converted to Ethiopian Orthodox Christianity. Converts often consider themselves Amharas.

The Qemant call God Yïdära or Mïzgänä. He is described as omnipresent, omnipotent, omniscient, and anthropomorphic, and can be approached directly or through figures called *k'edus*, “holy ones,” who are angels or culture heroes. The Qemant are divided into the patrilineal moieties Kɨbbɨr and Yetanti; Kɨbbɨr is higher in rank, and marriage must take place between members of different moieties.
