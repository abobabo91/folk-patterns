## Overview

The Hehe are a Bantu ethnolinguistic group based in the Iringa Region of south-central Tanzania. They live primarily in Uhehe, between the Great Ruaha and Kilombero rivers, in the Usungwa mountains and the plateaux of the Southern Highlands. Their territory includes rainforest, rolling grasslands, Brachystegia woodland and dry plains beside the Great Ruaha River and its tributaries. In 2006, their population was estimated at 805,000; an additional 4,023 lived in Uganda in 2014. The Hehe speak the Bantu language Hehe, also known as *Kihehe*. Their historical importance is associated with agricultural society, highly organized warfare, the spear, and resistance to German colonial expansion. They defeated a German expedition at Lugalo on 17 August 1891 and continued resistance under Mkwawa.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The museum catalogue records include cotton cloths. One head-cloth is described as yellow cotton with a block-printed “lattice” design. Another cloth is a square piece of cotton with four “tails” on one side and one on the opposite side, ornamented with squares of coloured glass beads. A waist cloth is made of yellow-dyed cotton with printed geometric patterns in black and white.

**Motif vocabulary.** The catalogue names a “lattice” design, squares, and geometric patterns; it does not provide vernacular motif names.

### Clothing & dress

The sources do not distinguish everyday and ceremonial clothing or provide Hehe vernacular names for garments. The museum catalogue records several forms of dress and bodily covering: an apron made of leopard skin with knobs of black goat skin; an apron with a clipped feather “rosette” sewn onto a skin base; a head-dress made of feathers on a skin foundation; an annular head-dress made of leopard skin with pendent strips of otter skin; and a head-cloth made of yellow cotton with a block-printed design. Another cloth is ornamented with coloured glass beads.

### Architecture

Hehe homesteads were normally surrounded by large maize fields. During the period of warfare, villages became more consolidated and houses were built closer together; after the wars, homesteads were again built farther apart, ideally surrounded by their own fields. Larger houses for many wives could be surrounded by an open courtyard. The sources state that housing was well insulated against violent extremes of heat and cold.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum catalogue records pottery bowls and water pots, including a bowl for washing hands, a bowl with an everted lip, and a bowl for cooking porridge. It also lists a pottery bowl attached to a water pipe made from a gourd with reed stems. Wooden household objects include a milking pot carved from a single piece of wood, a wooden ladle with black triangles ornamenting its lip, a carved wooden staff, and a three-legged wooden stool. Basketry objects include baskets, flasks, and a grass receptacle with two lungs. Iron objects include a bill-hook, bell, axe, knives, and weapon fittings. The Hehe sources identify the spear as the chief weapon and describe the use of wooden shafts, iron heads, hide, and wood.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The museum catalogue records necklaces, necklets, anklets, armlets, and other bead and metal ornaments. These include a necklet made of beads with a strung fringe; an unfinished necklace of coloured beads; anklets with iron bells or iron rattle bells; an armlet made of colobus monkey skin; and a necklace made of vegetable-fibre braid with two horn pendants and coloured glass beads. Another necklace consists of strings of white, black, and red beads. The catalogue also records an apron ornamented with a clipped feather “rosette,” a leopard-skin head-dress with otter-skin strips, and a feathered head-dress.

## Music & performance

The Hehe language article describes *Kihehe* as a tone language with high and low tones, and states that tones can distinguish one word from another or distinguish different forms of the same verb. It records the prefix *tu-* meaning “we” and gives an example meaning “we will sing,”.

The museum catalogue records a drum carved from wood in an hour-glass shape, with a hide membrane stretched with pegs. It also records an iron bell with a hide sling and an anklet with iron rattle bells.

## Dance & theatre

The Hehe sources discuss military organization, warriors, spears, and armed attacks.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe several social and legal customs. A child received a family name, described as the praise name, and inherited forbidden foods from the father. A Wahehe could not marry someone with the same praise name and the same forbidden food, or someone related through the female line; there was a preference for marrying cross cousins. Two cows and a bull were important parts of bride-wealth for a wife. In the judicial system, cases were presented orally and openly, and different numbers of male and female witnesses were considered sufficient for different cases.

## Foodways

The Hehe were primarily an agricultural people. By 1800, scattered homesteads were normally surrounded by large maize fields. The sources also mention sweet potatoes and state that the camp of Mkwawa contained no bag of corn but did contain sweet potatoes. Some people kept a limited number of cattle and goats, and two cows and a bull formed an important part of bride-wealth.

The sources state that certain foods were forbidden according to a person’s family inheritance: a child received the types of forbidden food from the father. The museum catalogue records a pottery bowl for cooking porridge.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that a child received a family name, called the praise name.

The sources preserve historical descriptions of Mkwawa and the Hehe wars, including accounts of the ambush at Lugalo, the fighting near Kilosa, the attack on Kalenga, and Mkwawa’s death on 19 July 1898. They also record that Mkwawa became a national hero in Tanzania. These materials describe historical memory and colonial documentation.

## Language & religion

Hehe is a Bantu language spoken by the Hehe people of the Iringa region of Tanzania, south of the Great Ruaha River. It is also known by its native name *Kihehe*. Four main dialects are identified: Kalenga, Koisamba, Sungwa, and Mufindi. Hehe has 15 noun classes marked with prefixes and a complex tense-aspect-mood system. It is a tone language with high and low tones; long vowels can carry rising or falling tones. Hehe may be mutually intelligible with Bena, and there has been some Bible translation by the British and Foreign Bible Society.
