## Overview

The Lozi, also known as Balozi, are a Bantu-speaking ethnic group native to Zambia and Zimbabwe. Their population is estimated at 1,562,000 in all countries combined. They refer to their land as *Bulozi* or *Barotseland*, centred on the floodplains of the Zambezi River. Most Lozi live on and around the Barotse Floodplain, and the annual movement between floodplain and higher ground strongly shapes their culture. Lozi society is organised around a monarchy whose reigning head, the Paramount King, is called the *Litunga*. The people include several tribes, among them the Bamakoma, Kwanda, Lukolwe, Bafwe, Batotela, Bayeyi, Mbowe, Bambukushu, Mishulundu, Muenyi, Mwanga, Ndundulu and Basubia. Their material culture includes pottery, basketry, woodcarving, musical instruments, masks and ceremonial dress.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name geometric decoration, zigzag decoration, geometric incisions, bands of raised geometric carving, rectangles, cross lines, cross rows, dots, rings, checkered-patterned squares, vertical bands and stripes.

### Clothing & dress

Lozi traditional dress distinguishes men’s and women’s clothing. The *siziba* is worn by men and consists of a shirt and a knee-length skirt. It is sometimes paired with a sleeveless jacket and *mashushu*, described as a red beret. Women wear the *musisi*, a garment possibly influenced by crinolines worn by wives of nineteenth-century missionaries. Museum records associated with Lozi *Makishi* describe round hats made of bark, willow and grass. One hat is recorded as 25.5cm high and another as 25cm high; both have traces of white and red on the outside and were used at *Makishi* dances.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum records document a broad range of Lozi-associated household objects and tools. Pottery includes a water pot with a handle and painted decoration, a pottery vessel, a pottery mug with a loop handle and zigzag decoration, and a yellowish-brown pottery jar with grooves on the neck and shoulder, red ornament and ochre decoration. The jar was used for water or beer. Wooden household objects include bowls, pots, lids, a milk pot, a spoon, a dipper or ladle, a head-rest, a stool, a canoe model and a carved food-vessel lid. Basketry appears in baskets, bags, a receptacle with a cover, a mug and a rectangular receptacle. Metalworking is represented by iron axes, a dagger with a triangular blade, a razor, an adze with a steel blade and a double gong.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources document a composite neck ornament made from six lengths of coiled wire with a calabash pendant attached by string. An armlet consists of glass, ostrich eggshell and wooden beads on a vegetal-fibre cord. The museum records also describe charms made from materials including bone, wood, gum, *abrus* seeds, fibre, hair, antelope or duiker horn, feathers, calabash and glass.

## Music & performance

The museum records identify numerous Lozi-associated musical instruments. These include a wooden xylophone called *sirimba*, with twelve keys, carved geometric decoration, fibre strings, iron screws and twelve calabash resonators. A pair of wooden beaters accompanies it. Other instruments include composite drums with wooden bodies, hide membranes and cords; a drum with a reptile-skin drumhead fixed with metal tacks; an antelope-horn hunting horn or trumpet; an ivory horn war-trumpet; an antelope-horn trumpet; a wooden-board *sansa* with metal keys and a gourd resonator; and an iron double gong. The catalogue also records a possible musical-instrument striker formed from a carved wooden rod with geometric incised decoration and brass-wire binding.

The sources state that several Lozi dances were accompanied by singing and often by drums. *Liwale* and *Ngomalume* were ceremonial dances performed at royal events, while *Liimba*, *Lishemba* and *Sipelu* were performed in the evening. *Siyemboka* was performed at beer parties, festivals, ceremonies and girls’ initiations.

## Dance & theatre

The Luyana or Lozi had two main ceremonial dances. *Ngomalume* was a war dance performed by men, involving belly movement, while *Liwale* was performed by women. Neither was accompanied by singing, and both were performed at royal events. The account of *Ngomalume* includes the saying that “no hungry man can dance” it effectively.

Other dances included *Liimba*, *Lishemba* and *Sipelu*. These were accompanied by singing and often drums and were performed in the evening. In *Sipelu*, men and women stood in opposite lines, with only one or two pairs dancing at a time. *Siyemboka* was introduced by the Mbunda and was performed at beer parties, festivals, ceremonies and girls’ initiations.

The museum records document *Makishi* masks and hats associated with dance. A *Nalandile* mask was made of bark fabric stretched over a wicker frame, with a wooden face painted dark brown and small holes at the eyes, nose and forehead. Another mask had brown and white cross lines and six cross rows of brown and white dots. A further mask, 37cm in dimension, had lines and rings around the eyes and checkered-patterned squares on the cheeks.

## Festivals & rituals

Lozi culture is strongly influenced by the flood cycle of the Zambezi River. At the beginning of the wet season, annual migrations take place from the floodplain to higher ground. The most important of these events and festivals is *Kuomboka*, during which the *Litunga* moves from Lealui in the flood plain to Limulunga on higher ground. The event usually takes place in February or March. The annual floods displace hundreds of people every year.

The sources also connect performance traditions with royal events, festivals, ceremonies, beer parties and girls’ initiations. *Ngomalume* and *Liwale* were performed at royal events, while *Siyemboka* was performed at beer parties, festivals, ceremonies and girls’ initiations. The museum records identify *Makishi* masks and hats as objects used in *Makishi* dances and state that the hats were for initiands or initiators.

## Foodways

The supplied sources mention food and drink only in limited contexts. A museum catalogue records a pottery jar used for water or beer, while the Lozi mythology describes Kamunu killing animals and cooking their meat for food. Museum objects include a wooden milk pot and a wooden dipper or ladle. A carved wooden lid is identified as a lid for a food vessel.

## Oral tradition & literature

Lozi mythology describes Nyambe as a creator god whom the Lozi call Nyambe. Nyambe’s wife was Nasilele and his mother was Ngula. One founding account presents Kamunu as the first human being. Nyambe instructed Kamunu to name the other creations and told him that the animals were his siblings. Kamunu learned skills including mastery over fire, forging metal, moulding pottery, carving spoons, plates and canoes, sowing crops and domesticating animals. After Kamunu repeatedly killed animals, Nyambe withdrew from him and eventually moved from Earth into heaven, called *Litooma* in Lozi mythology.

Other versions describe Nyambe and Nasilele’s daughter Mwambwa as the first Luyi sovereign and founder of the nation. Her eldest daughter Mbuyu later took over the sovereignty. A further version presents Mwambwa as the first female chief of the Luyi people and gives her the title *Njemakati*, meaning “a woman from whom the kingdom originates.” The sources state that these myths exist in several versions and have changed through oral transmission.

## Language & religion

Lozi, also known as *Silozi* and *Rozi*, is a Bantu language of the Niger–Congo language family within the Sotho–Tswana branch of Zone S, identified as S.30. It is spoken primarily in southwestern Zambia and in Namibia. The language is closely related to Northern Sotho, Tswana, Kgalagari and Sotho. *Silozi* is the endonym used by native speakers, and Lozi is sometimes written as Rotse.

The origins of Silozi are traced to a mixture of Luyana and Kololo. The Luyana settled on the upper Zambezi floodplains, where they established a kingdom called Barotseland or Bulozi. In the 1830s, the Kololo conquered the Luyana and imposed their rule and language. By 1864, the indigenous population had overthrown the Kololo, but the original Luyana language had largely been replaced by the hybrid language Silozi. The sources describe Nyambe as creator god in Lozi mythology and record several accounts of creation, death and the origin of Lozi sovereignty.
