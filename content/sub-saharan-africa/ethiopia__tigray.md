## Overview

The Tigray are the direct heirs of the Aksumite civilization (1st–8th century CE), whose adoption of Christianity in the 4th century shaped highland Ethiopian culture. They inhabit the northern Ethiopian highlands and closely related communities live in Eritrea. The Tigray are custodians of a rich material and liturgical tradition spanning textiles, ecclesiastical metalwork, rock-hewn churches, and a vast manuscript heritage in Ge'ez and Tigrinya.

## Material culture

Tigrayan material culture fuses Aksumite lithic and Byzantine traditions with highland craft practices, from handwoven textiles to lost-wax metalwork and hand-built pottery.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The netela shawl, woven from hand-spun cotton with decorative tibeb borders, anchors Tigrayan textile identity.

- **Netela** (*ነጠላ*) — A sheer white handwoven cotton shawl worn draped over the shoulders and drawn up over the head in church.
- **Tibeb** (*ጥበብ*) — The decorative border woven or embroidered at one or both ends of the netela, in geometric bands with crosses, diamonds, and church-window lattices.
- **Kuta / gabi** — The kuta is a thicker wrap for men; the gabi is a heavy multi-layer cotton blanket-shawl worn against highland cold.
- **Cross motif** (*mesqel*) — Stepped, lobed, and interlaced cross forms that are the core of the Tigrayan pattern vocabulary.
- **Eight-pointed star motif** (*kokäb*) — A recurrent geometric element in tibeb borders, derived from late-antique Mediterranean prototypes.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress centers on the white cotton qemis tunic, wrapped with kuta or gabi; feast dress is the habesha kemis gown with dense colored silk borders.

- **Qemis** — A knee-length white cotton tunic for men, worn over jodhpur-cut trousers; women's versions are longer and wider with embroidered tibeb panels.
- **Habesha kemis** — A full-length white cotton gown with dense tibeb borders in colored silk and metallic thread, worn on high feasts and weddings.
- **Albaso hair style** — Many fine braids close to the scalp gathered into a plaited crown, dressed with butter, worn by married women in rural districts.
- **Lion's-mane headdress** (*lämd*) — Ceremonial warrior dress worn at weddings and festivals, retaining ancient martial tradition.
- **Rhinoceros-hide shield** (*gasha*) — A warrior shield retained ceremonially and worn with the lämd headdress.

### Architecture

Tigrayan architecture spans monumental Aksumite granite stelae, vernacular stone hidmo farmhouses, and rock-hewn churches.

- **Aksumite stelae** (*hawelti*) — Monumental granite obelisks carved to imitate multi-storey tower-houses, with the Great Stele originally about 33 m tall, inscribed on UNESCO World Heritage List (1980).
- **Hidmo farmhouse** (*hidmo*) — The flat-roofed stone-walled farmhouse of central and eastern Tigray, built of undressed basalt or sandstone with juniper joists and a beaten-earth roof.
- **Debre Damo monastery** — A 6th-century monastery reached only by leather rope up a sheer cliff, one of the oldest standing churches in sub-Saharan Africa.
- **Rock-hewn churches of Gheralta and Tembien** — Including Abreha we-Atsbeha, Wukro Cherqos, and the cliff-face sanctuaries of Maryam Korkor and Daniel Korkor.
- **Sanctuary** (*maqdas*) — The central sanctuary of modern circular churches, housing the tabot (altar table).

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Village women make hand-built pottery; male smiths cast processional and neck crosses in brass and silver, with Aksum and Adwa as historic centers.

- **Jebena coffee pot** (*jebena*) — A tall-necked pottery vessel with a bulbous body and spouted lip, used in the coffee ceremony.
- **Mogogo griddle** (*mogogo*) — A flat clay griddle on which injera flatbread is baked.
- **Aksum cross** (*mäsqäl*) — A hand-cast processional cross in brass or silver, characterized by dense interlace and a lobed outline, made by lost-wax technique.
- **Neck cross** (*maţäb*) — A small cross worn from baptism on a blue-black silk cord, the universal marker of Orthodox Christian identity.
- **Mesob basketry table-hood** (*mesob*) — A round woven hood of dyed grass in concentric colored bands, used as a table cover.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver filigree and the cross dominate personal ornament, with amulets protecting against illness and the evil eye.

- **Pectoral cross** (*mäsqäl*) — A heavier silver cross worn by women on high feasts over the neck cross.
- **Silver filigree earrings** (*gwara*) — Earrings worked in silver filigree and granulation, produced in Aksum and Adwa.
- **Amulet cylinder** (*kitab*) — A leather or silver cylinder containing folded parchment prayers written by a debtera, worn against illness and the evil eye.
- **Forehead and neck tattoos** — Small crosses on the forehead, temples, chin, and gums, and lattice bands on the neck and hands, applied in childhood with soot and plant dye.
- **Henna decoration** — Henna applied to the palms and soles of brides during weddings, a practice shared with lowland Muslim neighbors.

## Music & performance

Secular music centers on the five-string krar lyre and ten-string begena; liturgical music is the three-mode zema chant of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church.

- **Krar bowl lyre** (*krar*) — A five-string bowl lyre that anchors Tigrayan secular music.
- **Begena harp** (*begena*) — A larger ten-string harp known as 'the harp of David', used devotionally.
- **Wax-and-gold poetry** (*säm-enna-wärq*) — A sung poetry tradition of double meaning, shared with Amharic, favoring oblique praise and hidden meanings.
- **Zema chant** (*zema*) — The liturgical chant of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church, codified in three modes (ge'ez, ezel, araray) and notated with mələkət neume-marks.
- **Female ululation** (*elelta*) — A distinctive vocal expression that punctuates every ceremonial climax.

## Dance & theatre

The shoulder dance awris, isolating rhythmic shoulder pulses while the head remains still, is the signature Tigrayan dance form.

- **Awris shoulder dance** (*awris*) — A shoulder dance in which men and women pulse the shoulders rhythmically to kebero and clapping while the head and hips remain still.
- **Sword and shield dance** (*shilela*) — A martial display dance at weddings and festivals, with men leaping and boasting their lineage and past deeds.
- **Aquaquam sacred dance** (*aquaquam*) — A slow, hieratic swaying dance performed in ranks before the sanctuary on major feasts, combining sistrum and staff movements.

## Festivals & rituals

The Tigrayan year turns on Ethiopian Orthodox festivals: Meskel (Finding of the True Cross) with its great bonfires, Timkat (Baptism of Christ) with processional water rites, and the girls' Ashenda festival.

- **Meskel** — Celebrated on 27 September, commemorating the Finding of the True Cross with a great conical bonfire of daisy-covered poles in every village; inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List (2013).
- **Timkat** (*Ṭəmqät*) — Celebrated on 19 January, commemorating the Baptism of Christ; the tabot is carried in procession to a body of water for dawn baptismal renewal; inscribed by UNESCO (2019).
- **Hidar Ṣəyon pilgrimage** (*Hidar Ṣəyon*) — Celebrated on 30 November, a great pilgrimage feast of Aksum drawing tens of thousands to the Church of Maryam Ṣəyon.
- **Ashenda girls' festival** (*Ashenda*) — Held mid-August at the end of the Filseta fast, unmarried girls dress in new habesha kemis and beaded ashenda grass skirts and move house to house singing and drumming.
- **Fasting periods** (*ṣom*) — No animal products on Wednesdays and Fridays, and through the long Lent (Hudade), Advent (Ṣom Nabiyat), and Filseta fast.

## Foodways

The staple is injera, a spongy sourdough flatbread of fermented teff, served as both plate and utensil with stews; coffee ceremony is central to hospitality.

- **Injera flatbread** (*injera*) — A large, spongy sourdough flatbread of fermented teff batter, poured onto the hot clay mogogo and baked with a lid, served as plate and utensil.
- **Tihlo dumpling** (*tihlo*) — A distinctively Tigrayan stiff dumpling of roasted barley flour eaten with a fork-like wooden pick dipped into communal wat.
- **Berbere spice paste** (*berbere*) — A paste of chilli, garlic, ginger, ajwain, korarima, long pepper, and fenugreek that is a foundation of the sauce repertoire.
- **Doro wat chicken stew** (*doro wat*) — Long-simmered chicken and boiled egg in berbere chilli sauce, the feast-day dish.
- **Coffee ceremony** (*jebena buna*) — Coffee is roasted on the spot over charcoal, ground in a mortar, brewed in the jebena pot, and served in three successive rounds (abol, tona, baraka) over an incense burner.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tigray is the origin point of written Horn of Africa literature, with Ge'ez manuscripts including the Bible, Kebra Nagast, and Saint Yared's hymnaries copied in monastic scriptoria.

- **Kebra Nagast epic** (*Kebra Nagast*) — The national epic 'Glory of the Kings' (14th century) recounting the descent of the Solomonic dynasty from Solomon and the Queen of Sheba.
- **Ge'ez manuscripts** (*Ge'ez*) — The classical Ethiopic liturgical language written in the Fidäl syllabary, preserved in vast manuscript literature including the Bible and lives of saints.
- **Parchment preparation** (*bra'na*) — Manuscripts are copied on parchment prepared from goat or sheepskin, written with reed pen and lamp-black or vegetable ink.
- **Praise poem** (*massé*) — A vernacular Tigrinya oral genre celebrating lineage and deeds.
- **Portrait poem** (*melke'e*) — A vernacular Tigrinya genre that portrays individuals through metaphor and wax-and-gold double meaning.

## Language & religion

Tigrinya is spoken by 9–10 million people and functions as the working language of Tigray Region; the overwhelmingly majority are Ethiopian Orthodox Täwaḥədo Christians.

- **Tigrinya language** (*Tigrinya*) — A Semitic language of the Ethiopic branch, spoken by roughly 9–10 million people across Tigray and highland Eritrea, written in the Fidäl syllabary.
- **Ethiopian Orthodox Church** (*Täwaḥədo*) — An Oriental Orthodox (non-Chalcedonian) communion that has been the state church since Ezana's conversion in the 4th century.
- **Debtera clerical order** (*debtera*) — Lay-clerical chanters, scribes, and healers who serve the parish and monastic churches and prepare protective amulet scrolls.
- **Tabot altar table** (*tabot*) — A consecrated replica of the Ark of the Covenant housed in the sanctuary of every Orthodox parish church.
- **Jeberti Muslim community** (*Jeberti*) — A minority of Tigrayans who are Sunni Muslims, historically merchants and long integrated into highland Tigrayan society.
