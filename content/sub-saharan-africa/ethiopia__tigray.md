## Overview

Tigrayans are indigenous to the northern Ethiopian highlands and speak Tigrinya, a North Ethio-Semitic language. Their folk culture is rooted in place: Tigray holds Yeha, Aksum and Debre Damo, the monumental core of the Kingdom of D'mt and the Aksumite Empire. Their craft—carved and cast crosses, parchment manuscripts, painted icons, hand-woven cotton mantles—descends in visible continuity from that Christian civilization of late antiquity. The overwhelming majority belong to the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church.

## Material culture

Tigrayan material culture centers on hand-woven cotton textiles, carved and cast metalwork, and stone architecture, all rooted in Aksumite Christian tradition.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Tigrayan weavers produce fine handspun, hand-woven cotton mantles with ornament concentrated in supplementary-weft and warp-float border bands.

- **Shamma mantle** (*shämma*) — Broad white mantle of handspun, hand-woven cotton worn draped over the shoulder, woven on a pit treadle loom, with ornament concentrated in a single woven band.
- **Netela shawl** (*nätäla*) — Light women's shawl, a single width of gauzy cotton with a coloured border worn over the head in church with the decorated edge folded to the front.
- **Gabi mantle** (*gäbi*) — Thick mantle of four to six layers of cotton pressed and stitched together, worn for warmth in cold highland nights and as a marker of adult male dignity.
- **Tibeb border weaving** (*tibeb*) — Supplementary-weft and warp-float bands worked into the selvedge of shamma, netela and kemis dresses, favouring narrow, densely repeated geometry and cross-derived figures.
- **Geʽez manuscript decoration** (*harag*) — Parchment codices from Tigrayan monastic scriptoria carry harag interlace headpieces, canon-table arcades and full-page ornamental crosses; the same vocabulary migrates onto textile and metalwork.

### Clothing & dress

Women wear white kemis dresses with tibeb borders under netela shawls; men wear white narrow-cut trousers, long shirts, and shammas or gabis.

- **Kemis dress** (*kemis*) — Ankle-length white cotton dress with a tibeb border at hem, cuff and neck opening, worn by women under the netela shawl.
- **Albaso braided coiffure** (*albaso / shuruba*) — Elaborate women's hairstyle of rows of fine braids tight to the scalp opening into a loose mass behind, dressed with butter.
- **Jano mantle** (*jano*) — Mantle with a broad red stripe worn by men on ceremonial occasions, replacing the plain cloth.
- **Kaba cope** (*kaba*) — Coloured velvet or brocade cope worn by priests and debtera with a turban and carried prayer staff.
- **Maqwamiya prayer staff** (*maqwamiya*) — Staff carried by priests and debtera.

### Architecture

Highland Tigray builds in stone, from the characteristic rectangular hidmo dwelling with flat roof to monumental rock-cut churches and Aksumite stelae.

- **Hidmo dwelling** (*hidmo*) — Rectangular drystone or mud-mortared house with massive walls, few openings, flat roof of timber joists weighted and rolled to shed rains, with internal stone piers and a raised platform separating living space from the byre.
- **Abreha we-Atsbeha church** — Rock-cut church carved into sandstone with cubical capitals, cruciform piers and coffered ceilings.
- **Wukro Cherkos church** — Rock-cut church in Tigray's distinctive monuments carved into sandstone.
- **Debre Damo monastery** — Built basilica on a sheer-sided amba, reached by rope.
- **Aksumite stelae** — Giant granite stelae including the Obelisk of Axum and the Ezana Stone, rendering multi-storey timber-and-stone palace architecture in monolithic rock.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Tigrayan metalwork and pottery center on prestige cast bronze crosses and hand-thrown black or red jebena coffee pots.

- **Jebena coffee pot** (*jebena*) — Round-bellied black or red burnished clay coffee pot with a long neck and straw-lid stopper, thrown or coil-built by women potters.
- **Processional cross** (*meskel*) — Bronze and brass cross cast by lost-wax with openwork interlace arms and lateral ears.
- **Hand cross** (*mäshaf mäsqäl*) — Filed cross for priests to offer for kissing.
- **Tigrayan neck cross** — Silver cross worked in dozens of regional patterns — the Axum, Adwa, Lalibela and Gondar types are named and collected.
- **Mesob basket** (*mesob*) — Tall coiled hourglass table-basket of dyed grass worked in red, black and natural spirals.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Tigrayan dress is completed with silver-studded leather belts, amulet cords at the neck, open leather sandals, and telsem talismanic scrolls.

- **Magic and healing scroll** (*kitab, telsem*) — Strip of goatskin parchment cut to the client's height, painted by a debtera with talismanic guardian faces, eight-pointed telsem seals and Geʽez prayer text, then rolled into a leather cylinder and worn.
- **Amulet cord** — Cord worn at the neck as part of traditional dress.

## Music & performance

Church music follows zema, the Ethiopian Orthodox chant system, while secular music centers on the krar lyre and masinqo fiddle.

- **Zema chant** (*zema*) — Ethiopian Orthodox chant system attributed to the sixth-century Aksumite deacon Saint Yared, notated with melekket neumes and sung in three modes — geez, ezel and araray.
- **Krar lyre** (*krar*) — Five- or six-string bowl lyre played in secular music.
- **Masinqo spike fiddle** (*masinqo*) — Single-string spike box fiddle with a diamond-shaped soundbox bowed by the travelling azmari minstrel.
- **Guayla dance song** (*guayla*) — Drum-driven dance song performed at weddings and tezkar memorial feasts with massed clapping and shoulder dancing.
- **Qene couplet** (*qene*) — Compressed couplet form built on deliberate double meaning in azmari improvisation.

## Dance & theatre

Guayla is the signature Tigrayan dance, with participants forming a ring moving counter-clockwise with shuffling steps while shoulders work in rapid isolated pulses.

- **Guayla ring dance** (*guayla*) — Participants form a ring or arc, moving counter-clockwise with a shuffling step while the shoulders and upper chest work in rapid isolated pulses, led by a krar or kebero and driven by handclapping.
- **Eskista shoulder tremor** (*eskista*) — Shoulder tremor dance accompanying both wedding and secular music, regionally inflected with Tigrayan style more vertical and bouncing.
- **Aquaquam liturgical dance** (*aquaquam*) — Priests and debtera in two facing rows advance and retire in slow synchronized steps with sistrum and staff.
- **Shilela boasting chant** (*shilela*) — Competitive chant at weddings and sung in memory of Adwa.

## Festivals & rituals

The Ethiopian Orthodox calendar governs the year, marked by Ledet, Timkat, Meskel, Fasika and the distinctively Tigrayan Ashenda girls' festival.

- **Timkat Epiphany** (*Timkat*) — Held on 19 January, when the tabot is carried under canopy from church to a water source for the renewal of baptism; the Aksum Timkat at Mai Shum reservoir is among the largest in Ethiopia.
- **Meskel finding of the Cross** (*Meskel*) — Held 27 September, marking the finding of the True Cross with the burning of the damera bonfire of poles and meskel daisies.
- **Ashenda girls' festival** (*Ashenda*) — Held at the close of the two-week Filseta fast in mid-August: girls dress in new kemis with elaborate braids and a bunch of tall ashenda grass tied at the waist, and go from house to house singing and drumming for gifts.
- **Tezkar memorial feast** (*tezkar*) — Memorial feast held 40 days and one year after burial.
- **Qal kidan marriage** (*qal kidan*) — Church-crowned marriage rite, or the more common sämanya civil form.

## Foodways

Tigray is the heartland of teff cultivation; the staple is injera, the large spongy sourdough pancake fermented three days and baked on a clay mogogo griddle.

- **Injera pancake** (*injera*) — Large spongy sourdough pancake fermented three days and baked on a clay mogogo griddle, served as both plate and bread.
- **Tihlo barley dough** (*tihlo*) — Stiff balls of roasted barley dough dipped by a two-pronged wooden fork into a meat sauce, a Tigray–Enderta speciality.
- **Himbasha holiday bread** (*himbasha*) — Sweet decorated bread scored into wedges for holidays.
- **Buna coffee ceremony** (*buna*) — Green beans roasted in a pan and passed for the smoke to be inhaled, pounded in a mukecha mortar, brewed in the jebena and poured in three successive rounds — awel, kalaii, bereka — with popcorn or roasted barley.
- **Sewa barley beer** (*sewa / suwa*) — Home-brewed barley beer drunk in Tigray.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tigrinya oral tradition rests on mesalat proverbs and qene couplets, while narrative genres include trickster tales, saints' legends and historical place-names.

- **Mesalat proverbs** (*mesalat*) — Proverbs forming the foundation of Tigrinya oral tradition.
- **Qene couplet poetry** (*qene*) — Compressed couplet form built on deliberate double meaning — a wax and gold surface sense concealing a barbed inner one.
- **Gädl hagiographies** (*Gädl*) — Saints' legends from monastic tradition, including the Gädlä Ewostatewos recording the saint's travels in Tigray.
- **Kebra Nagast dynastic legend** (*Kebra Nagast*) — Aksumite dynastic legend recording Solomonic descent.
- **Adwa war memory** — Memory of Adwa, the 1896 defeat of Italy on Tigrayan ground, sung in shilela war-boast and fukkera verse.

## Language & religion

Tigrinya is a North Ethio-Semitic language written in the Geʽez fidäl syllabary; the great majority of Tigrayans are Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Christians.

- **Tigrinya language** (*Tigrinya*) — North Ethio-Semitic language of the Afroasiatic family, the closest living relative of Geʽez, written in the Geʽez fidäl syllabary.
- **Geʽez liturgical language** (*Geʽez*) — Liturgical language surviving in Ethiopian Orthodox practice.
- **Debtera healer** (*debtera*) — Church-trained figure working outside the priesthood, writing healing scrolls and casting talismans against the buda and zar spirits.
- **Buda evil eye** (*buda*) — Spirit or curse against which talismans are cast.
- **Ts'ebel holy-water cure** (*ts'ebel*) — Holy-water cure persisting in folk religion alongside Orthodox practice.
