## Overview

The Chaga, or Chagga (Swahili *Wachagga*), are a Bantu-speaking people of the Kilimanjaro Region of northern Tanzania, living on the slopes of Mount Kilimanjaro and in the lands running down to its lower western and eastern flanks. They are the third-largest ethnic group in Tanzania; the German colonial government estimated around 28,000 households on the mountain at the beginning of the twentieth century, and by 1988 the population was put at over 800,000. They founded the Chagga states that governed the eastern Kilimanjaro region before colonial rule, and are recognised as one of the economically successful groups in the country — a standing attributed to the fertile volcanic soil, to intensive farming with terracing and irrigation practised since the Bantu expansion, and to trade. In folk-culture terms they matter for a highly engineered agricultural landscape, a dense ritual cosmology, and the *kihamba* homestead that organises both.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here — the Wikipedia articles on the Chaga people, the Chaga language and Chaga cuisine — describe no textile or weaving tradition, and there are no museum catalogue records among the sources, so nothing can be said about pattern-bearing cloth for this people.

### Architecture

Chagga settlement is made of small clusters rather than large agglomerations, each family in its own home. The household unit is the *kihamba*: a fenced farmhouse enclosure, ringed by the *masale* plant (*Dracaena fragrans*), which is a revered symbol of peace and forgiveness. Inside the fence is a banana grove whose long overhanging fronds shade tomatoes, onions and varieties of yam, and in the middle of the grove stands a round, beehive-shaped house of earth roofed with grass or banana leaves. A fire burns in the centre of the single room on three supporting stones, with bananas drying in a small loft above it; the sleeping place — a leather hide or a bed of dry banana leaves — lies near the door, where the husband's hoe and tools are also kept. A homestead may hold several structures for the head of the family, wives, children and slaves, plus stalling for livestock, and a smaller well-kept store raised on poles for millet, bananas, beans, honey and banana beer. The garden is enclosed by a living hedge of dracaena or thorny capers, with flowering creepers arching over the entrance and an irrigation channel running nearby. Neighbouring homesteads usually belong to one clan, linked by interior paths and separated from the next clan's ground by a larger hedge or an earth bank; a *mtaa* is a chiefdom made up of several clans. House designs differ east and west of the Weru Weru River. When Rebmann reached Kilema in 1848 he remarked at once on the order the *mangi*'s firm authority had produced.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Blacksmiths work in many homesteads, bent over hot embers with an anvil and goatskin bellows; women making and firing earthen pots are a less common sight. Beehives are made from hollowed tree trunks fitted with stoppers and hung from nearby trees, and hides are stretched on pegs to dry at the door of the house. The hoe is the tool kept in the sleeping quarters with the husband's other equipment. The most elaborate everyday engineering is not an object but the water system: water is gathered from distant sources beyond the virgin forest, led along hillsides and cliffs, dropped down less conspicuous slopes into reservoirs and distributed through a network of small channels giving all households equitable access — the practice the French missionary Alexandre Le Roy singled out when he visited in 1890.

## Music & performance

Tanzania has no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions, so none bear on Chaga music.

## Festivals & rituals

Ritual life turns on the ancestors and on *Ruwa*, the supreme deity and sun god, called *Iruva* in the western states of Machame and Masama and *Ruwa* from Usseri to Kibosho; both names are also the Chagga word for the sun. Ruwa is not held to be the creator of humankind but a liberator and provider of sustenance, merciful and tolerant when sought, and he resides on the summit of Kilimanjaro, which makes the mountain sacred. Old shrines survive in parts of the high forest, planted with *masale*. In this cosmology natural and supernatural realms interlock and every human act carries spiritual weight: misfortune follows improper conduct, malice, or the ill-will of spirits, and good fortune must be coaxed from a universe of unpredictable ancestral spirits. The ancestors of kings must be propitiated on behalf of the whole kingdom. Animal sacrifice is the principal channel of communication with the dead, the stomach and intestines offered as the link between living and ancestral worlds.

The life cycle must run in proper sequence. Once a child reaches the age of circumcision and is ready for marriage, the parents are expected to stop bearing children, since further procreation risks deaths in the family. A death out of sequence, particularly of the young, is read as a sign of foul play. Childlessness severs the ancestral chain: the bodies of childless people and infertile women are disposed of in the wilderness instead of the family graves, and a man without male heirs faces extinction because no one remains to perform the rites that carry him to the afterlife. Initiation rites in the central zone of the mountain involve tribal lore and secret symbols. Female circumcision and the age sets *rika* were adopted under Ongamo influence and later abandoned. Ritual imagery is drawn from ordinary life — spittle, semen, blood and urine; bananas, meat and beer — and carries double meanings, fire standing for both hearth-warmth and destruction, water for both calm and danger.

The seasons rather than fixed festivals mark the year. The Chagga used a solar calendar of twelve thirty-day months plus a five-day intercalary period, thirteen units in all, alongside a lunar reckoning that gave each of the 28 lunar days its own character; years were not counted. In the Kilema, Marangu and Mwika kingdoms the major seasons are *Kori* or *Sesu*, the hot dry season of roughly January to February; *Kisiye*, in two parts — heavy rains from about March to May and cold weather from June to September; and *Fuli*, the light rains of October to December. The cold interval between Kisiye and Fuli is *Mbeo* in Marangu and *Ndohu* in Old Moshi. Kilema divides Kisiye further into *Asuma*, the first rains; *Kisiye Tjalungana*, the height of the rains; *Kirangeta*, the deep cold; and *Mbaluwaru*, its close, announced by the call of the *mberu*, a black-and-white carnivorous bird. Onset and end shift from year to year and with altitude.

## Foodways

Bananas are the staple crop, grown in the grove that surrounds every homestead, with yams, beans and maize alongside; coffee became a significant cash crop in the late nineteenth century under German occupation. Annual crops — beans, maize and *mbege* (eleusine) — are grown on *shamba* land, held for a year at a time. Banana beer is stored in the raised granary with millet, bananas, beans and honey, and beer is also the customary form of *masiro*, the annual payment that acknowledges a loan of *kihamba* land. Food carries magical properties in Chagga belief: it sustains both the living and the spirits, and is bound up with sexuality as a means of holding off death and promoting life. Special foods are given to mothers after childbirth, and sharing food communally expresses bonds of kinship and friendship. Meat comes mainly from lineage feasts, where male cattle, goats and sheep are slaughtered; households keep only two or three head of cattle because the animals are stall-fed inside the huts on fodder women carry up from the plains, and old cows can be reclaimed from a borrower for slaughter after a proper apology.

## Oral tradition & literature

Clan histories are the main narrative form the sources record: they tell whether a clan arose locally or came up from the plains, and describe ancestors arriving with the tools of hunters, blacksmiths, livestock keepers or cultivators. European travellers to Kilimanjaro in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries collected such accounts from the kings — Karl Peters recorded Mangi Marealle of Marangu saying that the Wamarangu descended from Ukamba, the Wamoshi from the Usambara Mountains, and that the Wakibosho had always lived on the mountain. Origin legends trace some ancestry to the Usambara Kilindi dynasty of the Nguu Mountains, and tell of the *Wakoningo*, pygmies who crossed Kilimanjaro from east to west towards the Congo Basin; a story from Uru describes visitors from the west seeking lumber for a mythical king. Few modern *mangis* now assert the outside origins, since conceding them would weaken the Chagga claim to the land. Initiation lore in the central chiefdoms is transmitted with secret symbols, and distinct clan customs — cursing stones, fire worship in the ancient chiefdoms of Samake, Nguni and Kyuu in Siha Kingdom — carry their own traditions.

## Language & religion

Chaga, also *Kichaga* or *Kichagga*, is a Bantu dialect continuum spoken south of Mount Kilimanjaro, with dialects somewhat related to Kamba of southeast Kenya; *Mangi*, 'king', is a word common to all of them. Nine dialects are named: Kivunjo, Kimarangu, Kirombo, Kimachame, Kisiha, Kikibosho, Kiuru, Kioldimoshi and Kingassa. The language group comprises West Kilimanjaro (including Meru and Machame), Central Kilimanjaro (including Mochi or Old Moshi, and Wunjo), Rombo, Rusha (Arusha-Chini), Kahe and Gweno. Kichagga is changing fast, older forms striking present-day speakers as almost classical; the Ngasseni dialect is largely unintelligible to other speakers, while Machame and Marangu forms exert a standardising pull. Christianity and Islam took root on Kilimanjaro during the nineteenth century, over an earlier syncretic, earth-based and ancestor-venerating faith centred on Ruwa. Veneration of ancestors is strongly maintained to this day.
