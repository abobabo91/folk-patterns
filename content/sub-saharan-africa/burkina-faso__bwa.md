## Overview

The Bwa are farmers living in the savanna of Burkina Faso and Mali, known for their striking carved and painted wooden masks and the scarification that marked their identity. Their material culture — masks, textiles, metalwork and architecture — all share the same geometric vocabulary of checkerboards, zigzags and concentric circles. The masks and the rituals surrounding them are the spine of Bwa religious and social life, purchased from the neighbouring Nuna after the Volta-Bani War and now central to initiations, funerals and the agricultural year.

## Material culture

Bwa surface pattern runs across carved and painted wood, woven cotton and earth-rendered walls, all speaking the same graphic language.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The same geometric grammar of checkerboards, zigzags and circles appears on masks, cloth, walls and carved objects.

- **Nwantantay mask** (*nwantantay*) — The tall vertical plank mask of the southern Bwa, carved from light wood and painted in black, white and red, its surface divided into stacked geometric fields.
- **Leaf mask** — The northern Bwa mask built of bundled leaves and fibre, worn for Dwo ceremonies and destroyed after use, belonging to an older stratum of Bwa religion.
- **Faso dan fani** — Narrow-strip handwoven cotton of Burkina Faso, woven on the men's double-heddle loom and worn as wrappers, tunics and mask costumes.
- **Bogolan** — Mud-resist cotton of the Bani–Mouhoun corridor, with fermented iron-rich mud painted on an alkaline-dyed ground to leave pale geometry on black-brown.
- **Indigo resist** — Tie- and stitch-resist indigo dyeing on strip cloth, producing concentric rings and grid-stamped fields for women's wrappers.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress is built from strip cloth; ceremonial dress merges with masking, where the dancer disappears into a full-body costume of dyed fibre.

- **Men's everyday tunic** — A wide-sleeved tunic of faso dan fani strip cloth worn over drawstring trousers.
- **Women's wrapper and shoulder cloth** (*pagne*) — A wrapped skirt with matching shoulder cloth and head-tie, in indigo or bogolan for market days and ceremonies.
- **Nwantantay dancer costume** — A full-body costume of dyed and shredded hibiscus or bast fibre, cuffed at wrist and ankle, so no human skin is visible.
- **Age-grade regalia** — Initiated age grades wear plain fibre skirts and carry whisks and rattles to accompany the masks.
- **Facial and torso scarification** (*Nieniegue*) — Dense parallel and chevron registers cut across cheeks, forehead and torso at initiation, marking ethnic identity and adult status, now largely abandoned.

### Architecture

Villages are built of sun-dried earth, rendered in plaster and decorated with the same painted geometric registers as the masks.

- **Soukala compound** (*soukala*) — A walled family compound of flat-roofed rectangular rooms opening onto an interior court, with roof terrace for drying grain and sleeping.
- **Adobe granary** — Round, corbelled granaries raised on stones against termites, closed with a removable conical thatch cap, storing grain against lean years.
- **Banco construction** (*banco*) — Sun-dried earth, puddled and coursed, then rendered with mud-and-vegetable plaster renewed after each rainy season.
- **Decorated exterior walls** — Incised and painted registers of checkerboard, zigzag and chevron in white kaolin, black and red earth, applied by women.
- **Earth altar shrine** (*bayiri*) — Low mounded cones of accumulated libation at the village centre, tended by the earth priest.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Blacksmiths cast bronze and brass by the lost-wax method and forge iron, making masks, ritual objects and household wares; ceramics are hand-built and open-fired.

- **Cast bronze and brass figures** — Animal iconography cast by lost-wax method — hyena for stupidity, bush cow for strength, double chameleon and hornbill as the commonest forms.
- **Forged iron staffs and hoes** — Iron staffs and hoes forged for shrine and field use.
- **Hand-built ceramics** — Open-fired water jars, cooking pots and wide-mouthed beer jars for household use.
- **Decorated calabash vessels** — Calabashes cut into bowls, ladles and drinking vessels and decorated by pyrography with burnt-in zigzag and grid patterns.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Cast brass and bronze ornament comes from the blacksmith caste; the most consequential adornment was permanent scarification that marked ethnic and adult identity.

- **Cast brass and bronze ornament** — Rings, bracelets, anklets and pendants made by the blacksmith caste.
- **Double chameleon pendant** (*double chameleon*) — A brass pendant worn by women seeking to conceive, the chameleon standing for transformation and gestation.
- **Cowrie shell adornment** — Cowrie shells sewn in rows onto mask costumes, belts, dance aprons and strung as necklaces.
- **Facial scarification** (*Nieniegue*) — Dense parallel and chevron registers cut at initiation across cheeks, forehead and torso, the same graphic sets as carved onto mask faces.

## Music & performance

Music is the structural spine of Bwa ceremony, played by musicians of the occupational caste; the signature ensemble is a set of interlocking end-blown flutes that speak in Bwa tones.

- **Interlocking flute ensemble** — Five to nine end-blown wooden flutes, each player sounding a short repeating figure that interlocks into a composite melody, literally speaking Bwa and naming individuals.
- **Drums** — Drums drive the mask dances and accompany ceremonial performance.
- **Rattles, iron bells and struck calabashes** — Percussion instruments that mark the dancers' entries and accompany mask ceremonies.
- **Age-grade praise songs** — Songs sung by initiated cohorts that honour the masks as they appear, acquired together with the masks when purchased from the Nuna.

## Dance & theatre

Bwa masquerade is theatre in which the spirit, not the man, is the performer; dancers move to make the painted registers read as motion.

- **Nwantantay dance** (*nwantantay*) — Dancers move with the plank held vertical, then sweep it in great figure-eights and arcs so the painted registers read as motion.
- **Animal mask dances** — Serpent masks are carried low and undulating, hawk masks snapped and darting, each danced in character.
- **Leaf mask procession** — Northern village masks move in processional, less virtuosic fashion, appropriate to Dwo's gravity.
- **Women's mask entertainment** — Some wooden mask performances are staged specifically as entertainment for women, and women take full part in mask ceremonies.

## Festivals & rituals

The Bwa year turns on the agricultural cycle; masks come out in the dry season to close the year, purify the village and release the rains.

- **Masquerade season** — The great mask season runs February to May, in the dry months between harvest and first planting.
- **Initiation rite** — Children are taken from the village, instructed in mask motif meanings and the crooked path of right conduct, and returned as adult members of a named age grade.
- **Earth shrine and Do cult** (*bayiri, Do, labie*) — The labie, the village's eldest man and earth priest, makes sacrifices at the bayiri on behalf of the community.
- **Elder funerals** — Funerals bring out the full mask complement, as the masks escort the dead to the ancestors and the spirit Lanle.
- **Fertility rites** — Fertility rites involving chameleon imagery fill the ritual year between the mask seasons.

## Foodways

Bwa cooking rests on savanna grains pounded into stiff porridge, dipped into sauces of baobab leaf, okra, groundnut or dried fish, with beer a ritual substance.

- **Millet and sorghum porridge** — Millet and sorghum pounded and cooked into stiff porridge, eaten at every meal.
- **Soumbala condiment** (*soumbala*) — Fermented locust-bean condiment of the West African savanna, pressed into dark balls and sold in market.
- **Sorghum and millet beer** (*dolo*) — Malted sorghum and millet brewed into thick, mildly alcoholic beer, drunk from calabash bowls and poured in libation at the earth shrine.
- **Shea butter** (*Vitellaria*) — Shea butter from wild Vitellaria trees used as cooking fat and as a skin and medicinal preparation.
- **Granary system** — Grain storage technology existed to carry a household through a failed season, physically embodied in corbelled adobe granaries.

## Oral tradition & literature

Bwa oral tradition is anchored in cosmogony concerning Wuro, the creator, and his sons Dwo, Soxo and Kwere; these accounts are transmitted inside initiation and encoded in mask motifs.

- **Wuro creation narrative** (*Wuro*) — The creator who designed the earth in balance, was wounded by human action, and sent his eldest son Dwo as the channel between himself and humanity.
- **Dwo, the mediating son** (*Dwo*) — Wuro's eldest son sent to serve as the channel between god and humans, the focus of the Do cult and the leaf-mask complex.
- **Soxo, spirit of the wilderness** (*Soxo*) — One of Wuro's sons, the spirit of the wilderness and the bush.
- **Kwere, the lightning** (*Kwere*) — One of Wuro's sons, associated with lightning.
- **Mask motifs as mnemonic notation** — A carver and an initiate can read a plank mask as a sequence of moral propositions about knowledge, ignorance and right conduct.

## Language & religion

The Bwa speak Bwa, a closely related set of Gur languages; the indigenous religion is animist, organized around Wuro and the earth shrine, with Islam and Christianity layered over it.

- **Bwa language** (*Bwamu*) — Bwa (Bwamu), a closely related cluster of Gur languages in the Niger–Congo family, with northern and southern dialects.
- **Jula trade language** (*Jula*) — Jula serves as the trade language across West Africa.
- **French administration** — French is used in schools and administration.
- **Indigenous animist religion** (*Wuro, Do*) — Organised around Wuro and his sons, the earth shrine and the cult of Do, with the mask corpus as its material expression.
- **Purchased mask complex** — The wooden-mask complex, songs and dances were purchased from the Nuna after the 1915–16 Volta-Bani War.

## Glossary

- *nwantantay* — The tall vertical plank mask of southern Bwa, painted in geometric registers.
- *faso dan fani* — Narrow-strip handwoven cotton of Burkina Faso, woven on men's double-heddle looms.
- *bogolan* — Mud-resist cotton dyed with fermented iron-rich mud, creating pale geometry on black-brown.
- *soukala* — Walled family compound with flat-roofed rectangular rooms opening onto an interior court.
- *banco* — Sun-dried earth, puddled and coursed, used to build walls and rendered with mud-and-vegetable plaster.
- *bayiri* — Earth altar shrine at the village centre, a low mounded cone of accumulated libation, tended by the earth priest.
- *labie* — The village's eldest man and earth priest, leader of the Do cult and the council of elders.
- *double chameleon* — A paired cast-brass motif associated with fertility and transformation, worn as pendants by women seeking conception.
- *hornbill* — The commonest cast-bronze figure, a bird form carrying symbolic meaning.
- *Nieniegue* — Exonym for the southern Bwa, meaning 'scarred Bwa', derived from their facial and body scarification.
- *dolo* — Thick, mildly alcoholic beer brewed from malted sorghum and millet, a ritual substance.
- *soumbala* — Fermented locust-bean condiment of the West African savanna, pressed into dark balls.
- *Vitellaria* — Wild shea tree, source of shea butter used as cooking fat and medicinal preparation.
- *pagne* — Wrapped skirt worn by women, often paired with matching shoulder cloth and head-tie.
- *Wuro* — The creator deity in Bwa cosmogony, who designed the earth in balance and sent his sons to mediate with humanity.
- *Soxo* — One of Wuro's sons, the spirit of the wilderness and bush.
- *Kwere* — One of Wuro's sons, associated with lightning.
- *Bwamu* — Bwa language, a closely related cluster of Gur languages in the Niger–Congo family.
- *Jula* — Trade language used across West Africa.
