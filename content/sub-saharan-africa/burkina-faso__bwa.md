## Overview

The Bwa are an acephalous farming people of the Black Volta savanna in western Burkina Faso, organized by lineage and earth shrines rather than by chiefs. Their economy rests on millet, sorghum, maize, yams and groundnuts, grown from densely built earthen compounds. They are known internationally for two masking traditions: the vertical plank masks called nwantantay painted in high-contrast geometry, and the leaf masks of the Do cult that many Bwa regard as older and more authentically theirs. Their material culture—earth, wood, fibre and cotton—shares a unified graphic language of high-contrast geometric patterns that appears on masks, mud walls, woven strips, calabashes and scarified skin.

## Material culture

Bwa material culture is overwhelmingly earth, wood, fibre and cotton, unified by a graphic language of high-contrast geometric field patterns that migrates freely between mask surface, mud wall, woven strip, calabash and skin.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Bwa weaving belongs to the Volta-basin narrow-strip tradition in which men weave bands on double-heddle looms and women sew them into cloth, then dye, resist or paint it.

- **Narrow-strip cotton cloth** (*faso dan fani*) — Hand-spun cotton woven in bands and sewn into wrappers and blankets; promoted as a patriotic Burkinabè textile since the 1980s.
- **Mud-resist cloth** (*bogolan*) — Cotton painted with fermented river mud over a tannin ground so pale motifs stand out against a dark field.
- **Indigo resist** — Locally grown indigo used with tied, stitched or folded resists to produce white-on-blue geometry on women's wrappers and ceremonial cloths.
- **Fibre masquerade costume** — Dense fibre cape of dyed and undyed hibiscus or Daniellia fibre with a woven fringe that conceals the dancer; as much part of the mask's identity as the carved plank.
- **Geometric field motifs** — Checkerboard, zigzag and concentric circle patterns read as moral and cosmological shorthand, with meanings taught progressively through initiation grades.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday Bwa dress is modest cotton savanna clothing, with visual extravagance reserved for masquerade and the transitional costume of initiates.

- **Men's wrapper and tunic** — Hand-woven cotton trousers and loose tunic, historically with a strip-woven blanket carried over the shoulder in the cold harmattan months.
- **Women's wrapper** (*pagne*) — Two or three strip-woven or indigo-dyed lengths wrapped at the waist, with a second cloth for carrying an infant on the back.
- **Initiate's fibre dress** — Young people undergoing initiation are set apart by fibre skirts, ash or ochre body coating and restricted ornament, marking a period outside ordinary social life.
- **Masquerade fibre cape and hood** — The dancer's full-body covering of knotted and dyed plant fibre, worn with anklets of iron or seed rattles that make the footwork audible.

### Architecture

The Bwa build in puddled adobe, and their compounds are among the most photographed vernacular architecture of Burkina Faso—flat-roofed rectilinear rooms resurfaced each year after the rains.

- **Family compound** (*soukala*) — A cluster of adjoining flat-roofed adobe rooms enclosed by a continuous wall that reads as a single fortified block, entered through one or two low doorways.
- **Adobe granary** — Moulded earthen storage bin, usually a tapering cylinder with a small top opening and a fitted conical thatch cap, often the most carefully finished structure in the compound.
- **Bas-relief wall painting** — Exterior walls dressed in low relief and painted in black, white and earth-red geometry by women, who replaster and repaint after the rainy season.
- **Banco earth construction** (*banco*) — Sun-dried earth mixed with chaff, dung or shea residue, used for adobe walls, granaries and mosques in the Sudano-Sahelian tradition.
- **Flat terrace roof** — Beams of Borassus palm or hardwood carrying a packed earth terrace used for drying grain and for sleeping in the hot season.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Craft specialisation among the Bwa followed the regional pattern: smiths formed an endogamous group with ritual as well as technical authority, and their wives were commonly the potters.

- **Blacksmith's forge** — The smith carved masks, forged hoes and ritual iron, and in many villages held responsibility for Do cult objects, combining technical skill with ritual danger.
- **Lost-wax brass casting** — Small figures, rings, bells and pendants cast by the wax-thread method characteristic of the Volta basin; shares forms and probably workshops with neighbouring Lobi-Dagara brasswork.
- **Iron staff and ritual iron** — Forged staffs, bells and altar irons set in shrines and carried in ritual, the material understood as powerful because of its transformation by fire.
- **Calabash pyrography** — Gourds scraped, cut and burned with a heated blade into fine geometric registers, used as bowls, dippers and beer vessels, carrying the same checkerboard and concentric-circle vocabulary as the masks.
- **Earthenware** — Coil-built and open-fired water jars, cooking pots and beer jars made by women, undecorated or lightly roulette-impressed.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Bwa ornament is modest in material—brass, iron, seeds and cowries rather than gold or silver—and emphatic in meaning, with permanent body marking as the primary statement of identity.

- **Scarification pattern** — Facial and body scarification formerly marked lineage and initiation grade; the same raised linear patterns are cut into mask surfaces, linking the mask's face to a human one.
- **Cowrie beadwork** — Cowrie shells sewn to fibre bands, mask crests and belts, valued as former currency and as a sign of wealth and fertility.
- **Brass rings and bracelets** — Cast brass and forged iron bracelets, anklets and finger rings worn as everyday ornament and as protective objects.
- **Seed and cord anklets** — Rattling anklets of hard seeds or small iron bells worn by dancers, so that the mask's rhythm is heard as well as seen.

## Music & performance

Bwa music is percussion-led and made for masquerade, work and funerals, with its central sound the paired drum and iron idiophone driving a masked dancer.

- **Drum ensemble** — Sets of single-headed and hourglass drums of differing pitch played in interlocking patterns; specific rhythms belong to specific masks and signal which spirit is entering the plaza.
- **Iron bell and rattle** — Struck iron bells and gourd rattles supply the metallic pulse that anchors the drum polyrhythm.
- **Transverse flute** — Simple side-blown flutes of reed or millet stalk played by herdboys and in some masquerade ensembles.
- **Xylophone** (*balafon*) — Gourd-resonated wooden xylophone widespread across western Burkina Faso and northern Côte d'Ivoire; the Senufo–Bwa–Tagbana balafon practice has been recognized by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage.
- **Funeral song** — Sung lament and praise performed by women at funerals, the occasion at which masks most often appear.

## Dance & theatre

Masquerade is Bwa theatre: there is no separate stage tradition, and the performance combines choreographed dance, characterisation and drum dialogue.

- **Plank mask dance** (*nwantantay*) — Danced with a tall vertical board above the face, the dancer whipping the plank in wide arcs and figure-eights so the painted geometry reads as moving pattern.
- **Butterfly mask** — A very wide horizontal disc-and-wing mask spun on the axis of the dancer's head in rapid rotation, commonly associated with the swarming of butterflies after the first rains.
- **Hawk and serpent masks** — Bird masks with long lateral wings and tall serpent masks several metres high, each with its own rhythm, gait and lineage association.
- **Leaf mask** (*Do*) — A costume of fresh green leaves and fibre with no carved face, worn in Do ceremonies; its dance is quieter and more processional than the wood masks and is bound to the agricultural calendar.
- **Masked initiation cycle** (*lanle*) — Young men's initiation into the mask society, in which initiates learn the rhythms and the carved vocabulary; knowledge is graded and full explanation is withheld from the uninitiated.

## Festivals & rituals

Bwa ritual life turns on the agricultural year and on the earth shrine rather than on a royal calendar; the two great poles are the mask festivals of the dry season and the propitiation of the land before planting.

- **Earth shrine rites** (*bayiri*) — Offerings at the shrine of the land, made through the earth-priest of the founding lineage, to secure rain, fertility and the moral health of the village.
- **Dry-season mask festival** — After the harvest and before the rains, masks emerge over several days for initiation, for closing mourning and for public celebration.
- **Funeral masquerade** — Masks appear at the second funeral of an elder to escort the dead out of the village and to restore order among the living.
- **Do ceremonies** — Leaf-mask observances marking planting and the renewal of the bush, in which the fresh, perishable costume is understood to embody wild vegetal life itself.
- **Initiation** — Seclusion, instruction and return of age-sets, historically accompanied by scarification and by the granting of rights to dance particular masks.

## Foodways

Bwa cooking is the millet-and-sauce cuisine of the savanna: a stiff grain porridge eaten with a vegetable sauce enriched with shea butter, dried fish or meat, and millet beer as the social and ritual drink.

- **Millet or sorghum porridge** (*tô or saghbo*) — Pearl millet or sorghum pounded and cooked into a thick paste, served with sauces of baobab leaf, sorrel, okra or fermented néré seeds.
- **Fermented néré cakes** (*soumbala*) — Pungent cakes made from fermented seeds of the néré tree, the standard savoury seasoning across Burkina Faso.
- **Shea butter** (*karité*) — Nuts gathered by women, boiled, roasted and pounded into butter used for cooking, lighting and cosmetics.
- **Millet beer** (*dolo*) — Brewed by women in large earthenware jars and sold in courtyard drinking houses; indispensable to Bwa sociability and ritual, and the brewing calendar structures much of village life.
- **Staple crops** — Groundnuts, cowpeas, yams, maize and sesame fill the rest of the field calendar; guinea fowl, chicken and goat are eaten sparingly and usually in connection with sacrifice or guests.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Bwa had no professional caste of praise-singers, and their verbal art is domestic and initiatory rather than courtly, resting with elders and the officers of the mask society.

- **Origin accounts** — Stories tracing village founding and the descent of lineages from the arrival of the first ancestor at the earth shrine.
- **Etiological stories** — Tales explaining the gift of masks by the spirits of the wild and the relation of Do to the creator.
- **Trickster cycles** — Stories in which the hare and the hyena work out, comically, the boundaries of proper conduct.
- **Proverb** — The prestige verbal form, deployed in dispute settlement by lineage elders; painted mask motifs are described as proverbs made visible.
- **Volta-Bani war narratives** — Substantial body of local historical narrative remembering the villages' resistance to French conscription and taxation with specific names and places.

## Language & religion

Bwamu is a Gur language of the Niger–Congo family with dialect clusters; the indigenous religion centres on Do, the force of renewal, alongside the earth and lineage ancestors, with Islam and Christianity well established.

- **Bwamu language** (*Buamu*) — A Gur language with dialect clusters divided into northern, southern and western groups; French is the language of school and administration, and Jula serves as market lingua franca.
- **Creator and son** (*Difini and Do*) — The indigenous religion recognises a remote creator, commonly named Difini, and directs practical worship toward his son Do, the force of renewal manifest in the bush.
- **Earth shrine and earth-priest** — The earth is approached through the shrine and the earth-priest, who makes offerings to secure the moral health of the village.
- **Lineage ancestors** — Ancestors are honoured as part of the indigenous religion, with genealogy named in funeral oratory and women's lament.
- **Blacksmith ritual position** — Blacksmiths hold a distinct ritual position, and in many villages the Do cult is theirs to administer.

## Glossary

- *Do* — Son of the creator god and the force of renewal in the bush, embodied in leaf-mask ceremonies
- *nwantantay* — Tall vertical plank mask painted in black, white and red geometry, danced with whipping motions
- *faso dan fani* — Hand-spun cotton cloth woven in narrow strips and sewn together, promoted as the national textile of Burkina Faso
- *bogolan* — Cotton cloth painted with fermented river mud over a tannin-treated ground to create dark cloth with pale motifs
- *pagne* — Woman's wrapper, typically two or three lengths of strip-woven or indigo-dyed cloth wrapped at the waist
- *soukala* — Family compound: a cluster of flat-roofed adobe rooms enclosed by a continuous outer wall, housing an extended patrilineal household
- *banco* — Sun-dried earth mixed with chaff, dung or shea residue, used for adobe construction in the Sudano-Sahelian region
- *balafon* — Gourd-resonated wooden xylophone widespread across western Burkina Faso and northern Côte d'Ivoire
- *lanle* — Masked initiation cycle for young men into the mask society, in which knowledge is taught in grades
- *bayiri* — Earth shrine rites and offerings made through the earth-priest to secure rain, fertility and village moral health
- *tô or saghbo* — Thick paste made from pounded pearl millet or sorghum, served with vegetable sauces
- *soumbala* — Pungent cakes made from fermented néré tree seeds, the standard savoury seasoning across Burkina Faso
- *karité* — Shea nut, gathered by women and pounded into butter used for cooking, lighting and cosmetics
- *dolo* — Millet beer brewed by women and sold in courtyard drinking houses, essential to ritual and sociability
- *Difini* — The remote creator god recognised in Bwa indigenous religion
- *Daniellia fibre* — Plant fibre used to make the dense cape of masquerade costumes, dyed or left undyed
