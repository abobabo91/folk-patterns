## Overview

The Camus, also known as Ilchamus, Iltiamus, and Njemps, are a Maa-speaking people living south and southeast of Lake Baringo in Kenya. They numbered approximately 32,949 people in 2019 and are one of the smallest ethnic groups in Kenya. They are closely related to the Samburu, who live farther north-east in the Rift Valley Province. In oral traditions, the Ilchamus economy developed from foraging and fishing to a sophisticated system of irrigation, later combined with pastoralism under the influence of Samburu immigrants and neighbouring Maasai. Their history and folk-culture profile therefore include fishing, irrigation, pastoralism, oral tradition, language, and changing social organization.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources mention *manyatta* in connection with Ilchamus settlement history. They state that Ilchamus spread into their respective manyattas of melwat and wasu Nanyokie Ilchamus le keper after settling around mparingo, identified in the sources with Lake Baringo.

## Music & performance

They identify Camus or Chamus as a language and describe Ilchamus oral traditions, but provide no specific musical form, instrument, or performance practice.

## Festivals & rituals

The available account instead describes historical and economic change: Ilchamus traditions recall a movement from foraging and fishing to irrigation, followed by a combination of irrigation and pastoralism under the influence of Samburu immigrants and neighbouring Maasai.

The sources also describe an origin and settlement tradition. Ilchamus are said to have arrived earlier than other residents of Baringo South around the shores of mparingo. The sources state that the area was later registered as “Lake Baringo” by scholars from Germany because of a pronunciation error, and identify Laikipia as a cradle land and dispersal area after the mass movement of the Maasai under Lenana, called the “Loibon” in the source.

## Foodways

They do state that fishing was an early Ilchamus economic activity and that the economy later incorporated irrigation and pastoralism.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe Ilchamus oral traditions as accounts of economic and social development. In these traditions, the economy underwent successive elaborations from foraging and fishing to irrigation, and then to a combination of irrigation and pastoralism under the influence of Samburu immigrants and neighbouring Maasai. The sources connect these changes with developments in culture and social organization.

The settlement account also forms part of the available oral and historical material. It places the Ilchamus around mparingo, identified as Lake Baringo, and names Laikipia as their cradle land and dispersal area. It further describes the movement of Ilchamus groups into manyattas and the dispersal of some Ilkeroi families among named hosts. No folktales, epic poems, proverbs, riddles, written literature, or contemporary preservation projects are documented.

## Language & religion

Camus or Chamus, whose autonym is *il-Chamus*, is classified within the Maa languages of the Eastern Nilotic language branch. It is closely related to the Samburu language, with between 89% and 94% lexical similarity, and is considered a Samburu dialect by some. Together, Samburu and il-Chamus form the northern division of the Maa languages.

The sources describe Ilchamus as one of the sub-dialectical groups among Maa-speaking communities of Kenya and Tanzania in East Africa. They name Ilpurko, Lkieek onyokie, Lkaputei, Lmatapalo, Lkisonko, Larusa of Tanzania, Sambur of maralal, Lmomonyot, and Ldikirri of Laikipia as other dialectical groups.
