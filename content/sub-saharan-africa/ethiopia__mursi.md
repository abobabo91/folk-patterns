## Overview

The Mursi, or *Mun* as they refer to themselves, are a Surmic ethnic group in Ethiopia. They mostly reside in the Debub Omo Zone of the South Ethiopia Regional State, near the border with South Sudan, in an isolated region between mountains, the Omo River and its tributary the Mago. According to the 2007 national census, there are 11,500 Mursi, including 848 people living in urban areas. The Mursi speak Mursi as a mother tongue, and the language belongs to the Southeast Surmic languages and the wider Eastern Sudanic family of the Nilo-Saharan languages. Their folk-cultural profile includes agro-pastoralism, age-grade rites, body painting, lip plates, public religious rituals and distinctive language traditions.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do identify lip plates as a well-known aspect of Mursi and Surma life. These are large pottery, wooden discs or “plates” worn in the lower lips of women. Girls’ lips are pierced at the age of 15 or 16, or when they begin puberty, and the piercing gradually becomes larger. Unmarried women occasionally wear lip plates to a dance, and they are increasingly worn to attract tourists and earn extra money. The sources also mention a documentary about Mursi women wearing lip plates and the effects of tourism.

### Architecture

They state that the Mursi homeland lies between mountains, the Omo River and the Mago, and that it is one of the most isolated regions of the country.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention pottery and wooden discs as materials used for lip plates.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Body painting has cultural significance within Mursi society and is described as a blessing and protection from negative entities or outcomes. The paint can be made with ash or stone mixed with water. Women’s lower-lip plates are made from pottery or wood and are known as *dhebi a tugoin*.

## Music & performance

They mention dances in connection with unmarried women who occasionally wear lip plates to a dance. The sources also describe public rituals performed by the *Kômoru*, the inherited priestly or shamanic office. These rituals seek to bring rain, protect people, cattle and crops from disease, and ward off threatened attacks from other tribes.

## Dance & theatre

They state that unmarried women occasionally wear lip plates to a dance and that the documentary *Framing the Other* presents the culture of the Mursi people, particularly women’s lip plates and the popularity of tourism.

## Festivals & rituals

They document several life-cycle and social rites. Girls’ lips are pierced at the age of 15 or 16, or when they begin puberty, after which the piercing gradually becomes larger. Mursi men pass through age grades. During the first years of life until their mid-teens, they are assigned to the *changalay* and *dhongai* age grades; they then move into *teru* when transitioning into adulthood. Stick fighting is one tradition associated with the *teru* age grade.

The principal religious and ritual office is that of the *Kômoru*, the Priest or Shaman. This is an inherited office, distinct from the more informal political role of the *Jalaba*. The *Kômoru* represents the well-being of the group and communicates between the community and *Tumwi*, especially during drought, crop pests and disease.

## Foodways

They state that the Mursi are agro-pastoralists and that the *Kômoru* performs rituals intended to protect cattle and crops from disease.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do identify Mursi-English-Amharic lexicographical work and linguistic descriptions, including a basic lexicon and a dictionary. The sources also mention *Framing the Other*, a documentary directed by Ilja Kok and Willem Timmers that presents Mursi culture, tourism and interactions between a Western tourist from Holland and a Mursi woman from Ethiopia.

## Language & religion

Mursi, also called *Dama*, *Merdu*, *Meritu*, *Murzi* and *Murzu*, is a Southeast Surmic language spoken by the Mursi people in the South Omo Zone on the eastern side of the lower Omo valley in southwest Ethiopia. It is similar to Suri and is classified within the Southeast Surmic languages, alongside Suri, Me'en and Kwegu. It is also part of the Eastern Sudanic family of the Nilo-Saharan languages. The sources state that two orthographies exist: an Amharic-based orthography and a Latin-based alphabet developed by David Turton and Moges Yigezu of Addis Ababa University.

Mursi religious belief includes *Tumwi*, a force greater than the people themselves, usually located in the Sky. Sometimes *Tumwi* manifests as a thing of the sky, or *ahi a tumwin*, such as a rainbow or a bird. Mursi religion is classified as animism, although some Mursi have adopted Christianity. A Serving in Mission Station in the northeastern corner of Mursiland provides education, basic medical care and instruction in Christianity.
