## Overview

The Mossi are a large Gur people centered in Burkina Faso. Their society is organized around family and political rank, with the Mogho Naaba as the highest political position. Oral traditions tell of Yennenga and Rialé, whose son Ouedraogo is seen as the father of the Mossi people. Masks, royal objects, textiles, and ceremonies are major parts of the profile.

## Material culture

The profile highlights indigo cotton textiles, masks, dolls, royal figures, carved wood, and metal objects.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Mossi textile traditions in the profile center on handwoven indigo cotton.

- **Cotton shawl** — This man’s prestige garment is made from thirteen narrow cotton strips sewn together, with indigo dye, extra weft stripes, and twisted fringes.
- **Indigo cotton textile** — A textile from Ouagadougou shows plain weaving and indigo dyeing and was knotted and dyed by a Mossi woman.

### Clothing & dress

The main documented garment is a man’s indigo cotton shawl.

- **Cotton shawl** — The shawl uses thirteen narrow strips, hand-spun indigo cotton, border stripes, and fringes at both ends.
- **Incised geometric designs** — Incised geometric designs are recorded on a wooden cap mask.

### Architecture

The profile does not describe Mossi buildings but records a possible chief’s house-post.

- **Carved house-post** — A weathered wooden house-post has a carved human head with iron nails around the neck and may have belonged to a chief’s house.
- **Tethering post** — A wooden tethering post is possibly associated with a chief’s house.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Recorded objects include tools, pouches, figures, and posts, but no Mossi ceramics are described.

- **Hammer-club** — This object has an iron head and a wooden shaft.
- **Leather pouch** — The pull-out pouch has red fringes and stamped decoration.
- **Bronze dancing figure** — A bronze figure shows a dancer holding a fan.
- **Antelope figure** — An antelope figure is made of metal.
- **Female figure** — A wooden female figure has incised lines and a small iron band around the wrist.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The profile gives only a few examples of body decoration and doll adornment.

- **Scar tattoos** — A Mossi mask shows a female figure with scar tattoos on the body and face.
- **Doll decorations** — A Mossi doll may be decorated with beads, pearls, shells, fabric, metal earrings, bone, or animal hide.

## Music & performance

The profile does not name Mossi instruments or song types but records ritual mask performance and royal horse ceremony.

- **Wan-balinga mask performance** (*wan-balinga*) — This mask was worn and danced with at an elder’s funeral and during annual memorial services.
- **Friday court ceremony** — The Friday Mogho Naaba ceremony recalls the flight of the Moro Naba’s sister and the power of the royal court.
- **Royal horse display** — The Moro Naba mounted a white horse three times and returned to the palace each time.

## Dance & theatre

Mossi mask dancing is tied to funerals, memorials, rituals, religious observances, and public festivals.

- **Wan-balinga mask** (*wan-balinga*) — This mythical mask figure is described as the mother of the first Mossi ruler.
- **Funeral mask performance** — Masks accompany the dead, watch funeral procedures, honor the deceased, and test whether the spirit may join the ancestors.
- **Animal-form masks** — Recorded masks include elongated projections, horns, geometric designs, and animal forms.

## Festivals & rituals

Mossi ceremonies connect royal power, ancestor duties, public festivals, and funerals.

- **Royal festivals** (*na possum*) — At the annual na possum festivals, carved wooden royal ancestors are displayed and household heads renew loyalty to the chief.
- **Friday Mogho Naaba ceremony** — This ceremony recalls the Moro Naba’s sister fleeing to Yatenga with the nam, or amulets of power.
- **Public art festivals** — Masks from different Mossi regions appear at the International Art & Craft Fair, Week of the Culture, and Atypical Nights of Koudougou.
- **Funeral and memorial rites** — Funerals and later memorial services help guide the spirit of a dead elder, while an improper funeral is believed to bring trouble to descendants.
- **Doll wedding rite** — After a girl’s excision ceremony, her doll is placed in a hut and given to her younger sister on the sister’s wedding night.

## Oral tradition & literature

Mossi history is preserved mainly through oral tradition and tells of the founding family of the Mossi.

- **Yennenga and Rialé** — The warrior princess Yennenga married the solitary Mandé hunter Rialé after he rescued her, and their son Ouedraogo became the father of the Mossi people.
- **Ouedraogo** — Ouedraogo is recognized in oral tradition as the father of the Mossi people.
- **Contes du Larhallé** — Contes du Larhallé is listed with a collection of Mossi proverbs and sayings as further reading.
- **Ajami records** (*Ajami*) — Mossi records were primarily written using the Ajami script.

## Language & religion

Mooré is the main Mossi language, while spiritual life centers on ancestors, land, masks, and ritual obligation.

- **Mooré** — Mooré, also called Mossi, is a Gur language of the Oti–Volta branch and one of Burkina Faso’s four official languages.
- **Mossi language** (*Mossi*) — Mossi is another name for Mooré, which is the main language in Ouagadougou.
- **Regional dialects** — Regional forms are described for Yatenga, the northern region, Koupela, and Tenkodogo, and they are mutually understandable.
- **Ancestor beliefs** — Ancestors are believed to influence earthly life and to help or punish their descendants.
- **Sacred masks** — Masks are sacred links to the spirits of ancestors and nature.
