## Overview

The Suku people, also called Basuku, are an ethnic group of Bantu origin who traditionally inhabit the south-western Democratic Republic of the Congo and north-western Angola. As of 2017, about 200,000 people were believed to identify as Suku, many living in the Congo’s Kwango Province. Suku society is matrilineal, and the Suku language is a Bantu language of the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Suku culture includes hunting, cultivation, initiation institutions, masks, divination, leadership insignia, slit drums, swords, and carved figures. The sources describe cultural similarities between the Suku and Yaka people.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used do not document a distinctive Suku textile or pattern tradition.

**Raffia fringe** — Raffia appears on several Suku masks. The kakungu is covered with full-length raffia fringe strips, while the hemba has raffia fringe around the face; the m-bawa is covered with raffia cloth.

**Mats** — During initiation, boys learn useful occupations including weaving mats. The sources do not provide a named mat pattern or describe differences from neighboring cultures.

**M-bawa** — The *m-bawa* mask is constructed from vines bound into a spherical structure and covered with raffia cloth. It has a diameter of 100 centimeters around the head and two horns at the top, made of wood or actual animal horns, with white spots painted on the horns.

**Hemba** — The *hemba* mask is made from a cylindrical piece of wood carved into a helmet or bell shape, with a human face, coiffure, and raffia fringe around the face. Its coiffure has triangular decoration bands.

**Kakungu** — The *kakungu* is made from soft wood called *mengela* or *m'tsenga* and covered with skins of larger animals and raffia fringe strips. Its basic colors are red and white.

**Motif vocabulary.** Triangular decoration bands, white spots, inflated cheeks, prominent chin, animal superstructure, human face, coiffure.

### Clothing & dress

The sources used do not describe everyday Suku clothing, footwear, belts, or named garments. During initiation, boys are left naked for a couple of days and sleep close together on mats on the ground. Initiates later wear the hemba mask during the end of the initiation period. The catalogue records describe wooden masks with raffia fringe, helmet forms, painted white and black surfaces, and one mask surmounted by a quadrupedic animal. The sources do not identify these masks as daily dress or provide a broader account of ceremonial clothing.

### Architecture

The sources used do not describe Suku houses, village plans, roofs, domestic architecture, or permanent built environments. They do describe the temporary initiation site called *mukhanda*. It includes special structures, masks, and sculpted posts covered with charms and medicines that barricade the initiation site. After the boys leave, the camp is burned.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources do not document Suku ceramics or household pottery. They describe several wooden, metal, and mixed-material objects. The *N’koku ngoombu* is a wooden slit drum with a carved human head on its handle; diviners sit on it and use it in preparing medicines. The *mbeedya phoko* is a broad double-bladed sword with a pointed metal hilt and an unusually shaped sheath. It represents leadership and the powers, visible and invisible, of life and death. The sources also describe carved wooden statuettes and initiation objects made from wood, vines, animal skins, raffia, and charms.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used do not document Suku jewelry, tattoos, henna, hairpins, or named ornaments. They mention variations in coiffure and headgear on leadership statuettes. These include *Bweni* hats with a rising crest and protruding knobs or a projecting brim, *Mpu* skull caps of regional chiefs in Suku, and the *kambaandzia*, a miter-like form resembling a pointed cone. The sources do not describe these forms as worn jewelry.

## Music & performance

The sources used do not identify named Suku musical genres or provide a general account of instrumental music. They describe singing, drums, and performance within initiation. During the preparation for *mukhanda*, young candidates visit relatives and give dances for traditional gifts. The initiates sing songs of mourning while recalling separation from their families and the possible chance of death. On the day of circumcision, the ceremony begins around 5 am with drums, while the kakungu and m-bawa masks appear.

The *N’koku ngoombu* is a wooden slit drum with a carved human head on its handle. It is a distinct symbol for diviners, who sit on it, and it is also used in preparing medicines. The sources do not provide a name for a broader drum tradition or specify musical scales, song structures, performers, or occasions outside initiation and divination.

The *mbeedya phoko* is used ceremonially in Lunda war-dance, which recalls legendary battles and ends by pointing toward the land of origin. The sources do not explain the dance’s music or identify its performers beyond its association with chiefs and leadership.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is central to the Suku initiation process described in the sources. Young candidates dance while visiting relatives before entering the initiation camp. The kakungu, m-bawa, and hemba masks appear during different stages of the ceremony. The hemba mask appears near the end of the camp session, and its dance is taught to the initiates. When initiates return from the camp, the hemba masks dance as the camp is burned, followed by dancing and celebration.

The kakungu and m-bawa are associated with circumcision, departure from the camp, and other initiation events. The hemba is worn by the newly initiated at the end of the process. The sources do not describe theatre, puppetry, dramatic dialogue, or entertainment performances separate from ritual.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not describe an annual Suku festival calendar or named seasonal festivals. They provide a detailed account of *mukhanda*, the initiation process through which Suku boys reach manhood. When enough uncircumcised boys between the ages of 10–15 are available, elders announce the initiation. One month before the boys are sent away, the candidates visit relatives and perform dances for traditional gifts.

The formal opening takes place the day before circumcision. Participants gather by a *mukaamba* tree, and, if the charm specialist’s arrow remains in the tree, it is cut down and carried to the village in male solidarity. The kakungu, m-bawa, and hemba masks appear at various stages. After circumcision, the boys observe food restrictions, silence, bodily requirements, and other rules. They clean their incisions on the third day and receive strengthening charms the next day. When the incisions heal, after about three weeks, they may remain at the camp for another one to three years, learning occupations, storytelling, sex education, advice, and mannerism. On returning, they seek an elder to receive kaolin as a symbol of peace and welcome.

## Foodways

The sources used do not describe named Suku dishes, cooking methods, beverages, or a general food system. They state that women cultivate yams, beans, peas, pineapple, and peanuts, and collect and forage fruits, berries, and roots. During initiation, camp leaders impose many food restrictions. The boys are punished if they complain, break silence, make a mistake, or fail to follow orders. The sources do not specify the restricted foods, preparation methods, ceremonial dishes, or dietary rules outside the initiation camp.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used do not document Suku folktales, epic poetry, proverbs, riddles, written literature, or contemporary literary preservation efforts. Storytelling is named as one of the subjects taught to boys during the period after circumcision at the *mukhanda* camp. The *mbeedya phoko* is used in Lunda war-dance that recalls legendary battles, but the sources do not identify the legends or provide their narratives.

## Language & religion

Suku is a Bantu language of the Democratic Republic of the Congo. There is debate about its classification: Nurse and Philippson accept its traditional classification in the Yaka branch of Bantu. Suku society is matrilineal.

The Suku have a creator god called *ndzambyaphuungu*, who inhabits the sky and travels the breeze. This being is described as the originator of life and death and as the explanation for occurrences and unanswerable questions. The sources state that there is no cult following and no depiction or representation of this creator god.

The Suku and Yaka have a diviner called a *ngoombu*. The diviner works with clients who have experienced misfortune, strange events, sickness, or death. To determine the source of these problems, the diviner requires *nziimbu* shell money, a coin, or a piece of cloth belonging to the victim.
