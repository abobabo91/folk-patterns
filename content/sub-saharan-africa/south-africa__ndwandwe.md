## Overview

The Ndwandwe are a Bantu Nguni-speaking people who populate sections of southern Africa. They are also known as the Nxumalo, a name connected with one of their early chiefs. Today, Ndwandwe communities can be found in South Africa, Mozambique, Eswatini, and Zimbabwe. Historically, Ndwandwe chieftaincies formed part of the Northern Nguni migration and settled between the Pongola River and the Black Umfolozi River during the eighteenth century. Their historical centers included Magudu Mountain and the area south of present-day Pongola. The Ndwandwe became a significant power in present-day Zululand and later established a lasting presence across Southern Africa through migration, political fragmentation, and incorporation into other communities.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources identify royal settlements and a main capital on Magudu Mountain south of present-day Pongola.

## Music & performance

The sources state only that various Ndwandwe groups speak Nguni dialects.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe sacred burial places associated with the AmaNdwandwe. According to one account, after his death Zwide was buried on the southern slopes of Magudu Mountain in a forest considered sacred by the AmaNdwandwe, where other Ndwandwe leaders are also buried.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources record differing accounts concerning the fate of King Zwide. Oral traditions of Ndwandwe descendants from the Pongola Valley state that Zwide was killed not long after the Battle of Mhlatuze River by Shaka’s soldiers on the mountain that now bears his name, Zwide, south of present-day Pongola. Another account states that he fled northwards with some followers to the upper reaches of the Komati River, where he died in 1825.

## Language & religion

The various Ndwandwe groups speak Nguni dialects. The source describes the Ndwandwe as Bantu Nguni-speaking and places them within the Northern Nguni migration. It also identifies a forest on the southern slopes of Magudu Mountain as sacred to the AmaNdwandwe.
