## Overview

The Ik are an ethnic group or tribe native to northeastern Uganda, near the Kenyan border. Most live in small clan villages known as *odoks* or *asaks*, in the area surrounding Mount Morungole in Kaabong district. Their population is estimated between 10,000 and 15,000. Ik-speaking communities have traditionally resided in Kamion Subcounty, whose five parishes are Kapalu, Timu, Kamion, Lokwakaramoe, and Morungole. Their traditional homeland is a narrow strip of territory along the Kenya-Uganda border, stretching from Mount Morungole and Kidepo National Park to Mount Lopokok and Timu Forest. The Ik language belongs to the Kuliak subgroup of the proposed Nilo-Saharan language family. Their folk culture includes clan organization, agricultural festivals, rites of passage, beer traditions, ritual dance, and a language regarded by its community as vital to ethnic identity.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do record that, during *tasapet*, a man’s hair is shaved after his older brothers have completed the rite. This is the only documented bodily or appearance-related practice connected with a life-cycle ceremony in the supplied sources.

### Architecture

Ik clans live in small, walled villages known as *odoks* or *asaks*. Villages may be visited by tourists.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Agricultural tools appear in the *Dzíber-ika mεs* holiday: families bring beer and their agricultural tools to the *di*, the meeting place of the elders, and beer is sprinkled over the tools to bless them.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They record hair-shaving as part of *tasapet*, when a man who has fulfilled the stated conditions is taken to live in the bush for a month and slaughters a bull.

## Music & performance

The article on Ik culture does, however, describe a ritual dance connected with defense against raids. The Ik are described as habitually peaceful and as frequently raided by neighboring tribes. In this dance, men practice responding to an attack by defending the village, while women help lead children to hidden positions and care for wounded people.

The sources also connect performance with the documentary *Ikland* (2011), which presents the Ik in a positive light by showing how they survive and live as families, their music and dancing, and their ability to step into acting roles. The documentary concludes with members of the tribe mimicking a staged performance of *A Christmas Carol* by Charles Dickens as a Western metaphor for “redemption.”

## Dance & theatre

The sources name no distinct Ik dramatic genre or theatrical tradition. They describe a ritual dance in which men rehearse defending a village during an attack, while women lead children to hidden positions and care for wounded people. Its stated context is communal preparation for raids rather than entertainment.

The sources also mention the documentary *Ikland* (2011), produced in the mid-2000s by Spectacle Films and directed by Cevin Soling and David Hilbert. It depicts members of the tribe taking on acting roles and concludes with a staged performance of *A Christmas Carol* by Charles Dickens. No further information is supplied about Ik theatre, masks, puppetry, dramatic genres, or performance institutions.

## Festivals & rituals

The most important annual holiday described in the sources is *itówé-és*, or “blessing of the seeds.” It is celebrated over three days, generally in January, and marks the beginning of the agricultural season. On the first day, a sacred tree is planted, and people bring seeds to be blessed under it while dancing around the tree. Beer is brewed, and the following morning elder members taste it before it is drunk. No individual may drink until all older members of the tribe have done so.

The second major holiday is *Dzíber-ika mεs*, or “beer of the axes.” Beer is brewed by individual families and brought to the *di*, the meeting place of the elders, together with agricultural tools. The beer is drunk and then sprinkled over the tools to bless them. This holiday is usually celebrated in November or December. *Inúmúm-έs*, or “opening the harvest,” is celebrated around August. Harvested grains are cooked communally and eaten by men at the *di*.

Two named rites of passage are *ipéyé-és* and *tasapet*. Both are practiced only by men. *Ipéyé-és* marks the beginning of manhood: a young man must slaughter a male goat instantaneously with a spear that does not penetrate the other side of the goat’s body. *Tasapet* initiates elderhood and cannot be completed until all of a man’s older brothers have undergone it. The man’s hair is then shaved, he lives in the bush for a month, and he slaughters a bull. Men who have completed *tasapet* are considered the highest members of the Ik, and no decisions can be made without their consent.

Marriage is generally arranged between families. The first ceremony is called *tsan-es*, in which the engaged couple are rubbed with oil. The groom throws a spear at a tree to test his abilities as a hunter. In a later ceremony, the groom’s family brings livestock and grain to the bride’s family; beer is served and remaining problems between the families are discussed.

## Foodways

The Ik are primarily subsistence farmers. Their staple crops include sorghum, millet, corn, and tobacco. The Ik language article also names sorghum as *ŋám*, finger millet as *rébᵃ*, maize as *ɲaɓʊraˊ*, pumpkins as *kaiɗeˊ*, beans as *moriɗᵃ*, and vegetable greens as *waicíkᵃ*.

Their diets are supplemented by hunting and gathering. Foods named in the sources include honey, various fruits, and white ants known as *danj*. The Ik may trade with neighboring groups for products such as cattle. Agriculture shapes the ritual calendar: *itówé-és* begins the agricultural season, while *Inúmúm-έs* marks the opening of the harvest.

Beer is brewed by the Ik and plays an important role in traditions. During *itówé-és*, beer is brewed, tasted first by elder members, and then drunk. During *Dzíber-ika mεs*, families bring beer and agricultural tools to the *di*, where the beer is used to bless the tools. In marriage ceremonies, the groom’s family welcomes the bride’s family with beer, and in later ceremonies the groom provides food or beer to other members of the clan.

## Oral tradition & literature

The principal literary work connected with the Ik in the supplied sources is *The Mountain People*, an ethnography published by anthropologist Colin Turnbull in 1972. It is based on fieldwork during a drought and famine in 1965–66. Turnbull’s account described the Ik as forced into radical individualism and portrayed the abandonment of familial responsibility under severe hardship. The book relied on surviving elderly Ik as sources for earlier practices, including hunter-gatherer activities, marriage, childbirth, death rituals and taboos, and religion.

Turnbull’s research is controversial. In 1983, Bernd Heine argued that Turnbull’s methods and conclusions were flawed, citing limited knowledge of the Ik language and tradition and questioning several of his claims. Heine’s research contradicted Turnbull’s claim of frequent non-monogamy, and he concluded that Turnbull’s account was at variance with most observations.

## Language & religion

Ik is also known as Icetot, Icietot, Ngulak, or, in derogatory usage, Teuso and Teuth. The Ik refer to their own language as *Icé-tód*. It is one of the Kuliak languages of northeastern Uganda, and the Kuliak languages form their own branch of the proposed Nilo-Saharan language family. The other two Kuliak languages are described as moribund, while Ik is highly vital. Young children still learn it, remain monolingual until they go to school, and speak it in all domains of life. Many speakers also know local Teso–Turkana languages, Swahili, and, to a lesser extent, English.

The Ik are predominantly Christian. They also believe in nature spirits called *kíʝáwika*, which reward generosity. The language contains terms for prohibitions and taboos, including *cuᵉ*, the prohibition against failing to give water to elders first; *dɛ*, the prohibition against failing to give leg-meat to elders; and *ifófóés*, the prohibition against eating the first harvest secretly.
