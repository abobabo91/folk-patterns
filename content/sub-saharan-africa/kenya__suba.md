## Overview

The Suba, also known as the Abasuba, are an ethnic group native to eastern Lake Victoria. They predominantly live in Homa Bay County and Migori County in southwestern Kenya, as well as across parts of Mara Region in northern Tanzania. In Kenya, their settlements include the offshore islands of Rusinga and Mfangano and mainland shores including Mbita, Suna, and Gwassi. The 2019 Kenya Population and Housing Census enumerated 157,787 Suba people. Linguistically and historically, the Suba are of Bantu origin and belong to the Great Lakes Bantu branch. They migrated between the mid-17th and 18th centuries from the Kingdom of Buganda and Busoga in present-day Uganda, fleeing succession conflicts and internal warfare. Their folk-cultural significance lies in the continued presence of distinct cultural institutions, including male circumcision ceremonies, clan totems, sacred rainmaking shrines, and archaeological rock art traditions.

The annual Rusinga Cultural Festival is identified as a major element of the Suba cultural and linguistic revival.

The sources identify male circumcision ceremonies as a distinct Suba cultural institution. Sacred rainmaking shrines, called *emisambwa*, are also identified as part of Suba cultural institutions.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They do, however, describe a substantial linguistic and literary revival. The Suba Language Project was founded by linguist Duncan Okoth Okombo, and the community has supported the publication of an Olusuba Bible and educational curricula.

The sources give two accounts of biblical translation. One states that efforts to translate the Bible into Suba began as early as 1988 and that the translation was completed in 2011. The Suba language article states that the New Testament was translated into Suba in 2010. The sources also mention *Tujifunze Lugha yetu*, a book written in Abasuba and translated into Kiswahili by Otieno Apiyo Caspar-Nursing.

The sources describe this literary work as part of a wider effort to address language decline. The education system teaches English and Luo to newer generations of Suba children, while fluent Suba speakers are described as middle-aged. The sources state that many elders are blamed for not ensuring that children learn the language from an early onset.

## Language & religion

Kisuba, also known as Olusuba, is a Bantu language spoken by the Suba people of Kenya. It belongs to the Great Lakes Bantu branch, and Suba clans are located on the eastern shore and islands of Lake Victoria in Kenya and Tanzania. The language has an extensive noun-classification system using prefixes to mark gender and number. The sources describe ten noun classes, a usual subject–verb–object word order, and a writing system based on the Latin script.

Most Suba adopted Dholuo as their primary spoken language after centuries of contact, intermarriage, and social assimilation with incoming Western Nilotic Luo groups. A majority of Suba people are bilingual in Dholuo, and interaction, education, religion, and intermarriage contributed to the formation of the Luo-Suba alliance. This process reduced the demand for Suba speakers and contributed to the endangerment of Olusuba.

The sources describe an ancient polytheistic history involving ancestral spirits who protect family and land. They also state that Christianity is presently the major religion practiced by the Suban people. Suba cultural institutions include sacred rainmaking shrines called *emisambwa*.
