## Overview

The Antankarana, also called Antakarana, are an ethnic group of Madagascar living in the northernmost part of the island, from Antsiranana down the west coast to Tetezambato and including Nosy Mitsio. Their territory is bounded to the east by the Bemarivo River. Their name refers to the *tsingy*, the limestone rock formations that distinguish their traditional territory, especially the Ankarana massif and Ankarana Reserve. There were 50,000 Antakarana in Madagascar as of 2013. They are an offshoot of the Sakalava and claim Malagasy and Arab ancestry. Antakarana is a northern dialect of Malagasy, an Austronesian language belonging to the Malayo-Polynesian language group. Their folk culture is marked by royal traditions, ancestral rituals, *fady* taboos, spirit possession, fishing, zebu keeping, raffia clothing and rice-based foodways.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Raffia panels* were historically made by combing raffia fibers into strands, knotting them into cords and weaving the cords into panels. The panels were stitched together to make clothing and prayer rugs.

### Clothing & dress

Antankarana clothing was historically made from woven raffia. The fibers were combed into strands, knotted together to form cords and woven into panels. These panels were stitched together to create clothing and prayer rugs. Women and men historically wore long raffia smocks.

### Architecture

Antankarana homes are typically raised on stilts above ground level. Young men establishing families traditionally leave their father’s house and build their own dwelling from locally gathered wood and thatch. A *zomba* is a house reserved for royalty and was traditionally placed near the central clearing of a village, where the town hall was often situated. The Ankarana landscape also contains natural stone shelters and caves that served as places of refuge during conflicts and became sacred in Antankarana tradition.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do describe two-man canoes made from a single hollowed-out log, fishing nets and locally gathered wood and thatch used in house construction.

## Music & performance

 They do record *tromba*, ancestral spirit possession practiced as a means of communicating with ancestors. The Antankarana believe that spirits of the dead often inhabit crocodiles, and royal ancestral spirits possessing *tromba* mediums are almost always of Sakalava ancestry.

Royal ceremonies include the recitation of *joro*, ancestral invocations through which the ancestors’ blessings are requested. The same ceremonies may include the traditional dance called *rabiky*. Large dance parties called *baly* are popular among Antankarana youth.

## Dance & theatre

The traditional dance *rabiky* is performed at royal ceremonies. Large dance parties called *baly* are also popular among Antankarana youth.

## Festivals & rituals

Antankarana royal authority is reaffirmed every five years at Ambatoharaña in a ritual mast-raising ceremony called the *tsangantsainy*. The ceremony includes a pilgrimage to Nosy Mitsio commemorating the flight of Antankarana refugees to the island during the 1830s, when they escaped the advancing armies of the Kingdom of Imerina. Participants also visit the tombs of ancestors who died there. Until recently, the ceremony sometimes included raising French and Antankarana banners in honor of the treaty signed with the French in 1841.

The king, or *Ampanjaka*, is selected by a council of elder members of the royal family. He recites *joro* at ceremonies requesting ancestral blessings. The Antankarana also select a single noble woman as *Ndriambavibe*, a position with authority and importance comparable to that of the king but constituting a separate leadership role.

The Antankarana maintain a custom called *tsangatsaine*, in which two trees growing before the house of a noble family are tied together. The action symbolizes the unity of the community, the merger of past with present and the dead with the living.

Antankarana funerals are often celebratory. Among villagers living near the sea, the remains of a loved one may be placed in a coffin that the family carries while running into the sea. Other nobles are mainly entombed in the Islamic cemetery near Ambatoharaña, while Tsimiaro was buried at his request in the Ankarana cave where he had taken refuge from the Merina.

## Foodways

Rice is the foundation of every Antankarana meal. It is often eaten with fish broth, greens, beans or squash. Manioc and green bananas are staples commonly eaten when other preferred foods are too expensive or out of season.

The Antankarana were historically fishermen and pastoralist zebu herders and are now generally agriculturalists, while cattle remain important for milk and as a form of wealth. Sea fishing is carried out in two-man canoes made from a single hollowed-out log. Fishing also provides eels, fish, crayfish and other food sources from rivers. The Antankarana have hunted whales, turtles and fish, and shrimp fishing is described as a highly lucrative contemporary activity.

Zebu sacrifice is a typical element of major rituals and celebrations, including Muslim holidays and life events such as marriage, death and birth. The number of cattle sacrificed to the ancestors measures loyalty, while the number given away indicates generosity.

## Oral tradition & literature

Antankarana oral history preserves accounts of royal origins, migrations, warfare and ritual instruction. It recounts the resistance of Andriamanpangy, a descendant of Kozobe, and the founding of the Antankarana kingdom by his son Andriantsirotso. It also tells of Tsimatahodrafy, a mysterious eight-year-old girl who revealed herself as a sorceress and instructed Andriantsirotso in rituals intended to secure his safe return and the establishment of a strong kingdom.

The sources also describe the oral history surrounding King Tsimiaro I’s escape from the Merina. After praying for God’s help, he swore that the Antankarana would convert to Islam if they survived. The account connects the crossing to Ambavan'ankarana, which retains a sacred character and is a site of pilgrimage and ritual commemoration.

## Language & religion

Antakarana is a northern dialect of Malagasy spoken by the Antakarana people in the province of Antsiranana. It is classified as a northern Malagasy dialect within the Austronesian language family and shares close linguistic affinities with Northern Sakalava, Bushi, Northern Betsimisaraka and Tsimihety. It is predominantly spoken in the Diana Region and is also used to a lesser extent in parts of the Sava and Sofia Regions. French colonial presence influenced the dialect through the incorporation of French loanwords.

Most Antankarana identify to varying degrees as Muslim. Their Islam is highly syncretic, combining ancestor worship and local customs and beliefs with major holidays and cultural elements borrowed from Arab Muslim culture. The Shādhiliyya Sufi order spread among the Antankarana beginning in the late 19th century. Antankarana also practice *tromba* spirit possession, believe in nature spirits called *tsiny* and observe numerous *fady*, or ancestral taboos, protecting wildlife, wilderness areas and social relations.
