## Overview

The Igala homeland lies between the Niger and Benue Rivers, with Idah as its capital. Most Igala live in southern and eastern Kogi State, while smaller communities live in nearby states. Farming, hunting, trade, basketry, body decoration, masquerades, and ritual life are important parts of Igala culture. Ancestors and the supreme creator Ọjọ are central to religious practice.

## Material culture

Igala material culture combines riverine and farming adaptations with objects used in burial, political life, body decoration, and ritual performance.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Igala weaving uses raffia, grasses, woody stems, cotton, and cane for baskets, fishing tools, burial cloth, and textiles.

- **Basketry** (*Igala basketry*) — Artisans weave storage containers, fishing traps, sieves, and carrying baskets for farming, fishing, trade, and markets.
- **Burial cloth** (*achi*) — Achi is made from raffia strips called Iko and strips of cloth, and adorns the dead during the Oloja burial ceremony.
- **Woven textile** — A museum textile has blue, black, and orange warp, ikat-dyed blue threads, and blue weft threads.
- **Geometric marks** — Igala objects may show incised geometric patterns, linear bands, spirals, geometric designs, and figures of doves and tortoises.

### Clothing & dress

The profile gives little information about everyday clothing but describes ceremonial dress and body markings.

- **Burial adornment** (*achi*) — In the Oloja masquerade, the dead person is adorned with achi made from Iko raffia strips and cloth.
- **Crocodile headdress** — The Oloja dancer wears a wooden headdress shaped like a crocodile, with fierce teeth and a projecting jaw.
- **Body decoration** (*Ina Ole*) — Ina Ole includes permanent scarification and temporary designs made with paint or dyes.
- **Ankpa facial marks** — In Ankpa, three deep horizontal cuts on each side of the face once identified a person.

### Architecture

The sources mention Igala cultural architecture but do not describe houses, settlements, or building types.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Igala objects include carved wood, cast brass, iron, copper, and tools linked to ceremonial and everyday life.

- **Wooden throne** — A wooden throne has a tubular seat, pedestal feet, detachable lids, and a plinth carved with doves and tortoises.
- **Mirror-frame** — A mirror-frame has a carved man's head with an elaborate crest, an iron-hook handle, and geometric decoration.
- **Brass bell** — A lost-wax cast brass bell has a waisted body, a looped handle, bands, spirals, and a wooden clapper.
- **Hornbill stool** — A wooden stool is topped by a hornbill-like bird carrying a four-legged animal on its head.
- **Wooden figures and tools** — The catalogue also records wooden figures, iron bracelets, copper wire, and wooden or copper hair-pins.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Igala adornment uses iron, copper, wood, paint, dyes, piercing, and scarification for beauty and social meaning.

- **Iron bracelets** — Bracelets include linked forms wrapped with copper wire, pod-shaped links, and chains of iron links.
- **Spear-shaped hair-pin** — One wooden hair-pin is shaped like a spear.
- **Spiral-headed hair-pin** — A copper hair-pin has a head ending in two opposite spirals.
- **Body decoration** (*Ina Ole*) — Ina Ole includes piercing, scarification, tattooing, skin dyes, and painting for beauty or social identification.
- **Body symbols** — Combs, rings, and the alphabet are among the symbols used in body decoration.

## Music & performance

Music leads the Oloja burial ceremony through elders, flutes, gongs, rattles, and seven drums.

- **Oloja ceremony music** (*Oloja*) — Elder instrumentalists play wooden gongs, metal gongs, rattles, a flute, and seven drums of different sizes.
- **Seven-flute opening** — The lead musician begins with seven flute notes, each louder than the one before it.
- **Summoning notes** — A second set of seven flute notes calls the Oloja dancer into the performance.
- **Occupation dance** — The dancer's movements imitate the deceased person's work, character, or habits.

## Dance & theatre

The Oloja masquerade joins music, dance, a crocodile headdress, and the destruction of the dead person's objects.

- **Oloja masquerade** (*Oloja*) — The Oloja masquerade is a burial performance held during the final stage of the ceremony.
- **Roof-throwing act** — The dancer throws part of the dead person's thatched roof into the bush or river.
- **Tool destruction** — The masquerader gathers and destroys the deceased person's tools.
- **Personal god ritual** (*Okega*) — The dancer takes the Okega, snuffs three times, strikes the back of the head three times, and makes three final dance movements.
- **Igala masks** — Igala masks are ritual art objects that connect the living world with the ancestral spiritual realm and may show political status or fashion.

## Festivals & rituals

Igala ritual life honors the earth, ancestors, agriculture, hunting, and the dead.

- **Earth festival** — The earth festival begins the farming year with a communal hunt, offerings of animal remains, grass burning, and the planting of the first yam.
- **Royal animal offering** — Before one earth festival, the king camps in the bush and spears an animal for the national earth shrine.
- **Ancestor festival** (*Ibegwu*) — The Ibegwu festival honors ancestors before the yam harvest and asks for protection and blessings.
- **Burial stages** (*Egwu omi omi eji*) — Egwu omi omi eji is the stage when the body is placed in the grave.
- **Final mourning stage** (*Akwu eche*) — Akwu eche means the last shedding of tears and includes the Oloja masquerade.

## Foodways

Igala foodways center on farming, hunting, and trade, but the sources do not describe dishes or cooking methods.

- **Yam farming** — Yam is a major rainfed crop, and the first yam is planted during the earth festival.
- **Rainfed crops** — Other rainfed crops include cocoyam, maize, pumpkin, and cassava.
- **Dry-soil crops** — Dry-soil crops include millet, Guinea corn, beniseed, and beans.
- **Hunting landscape** — Buffalo, antelope, and wild pig are among the animals found in the region.
- **Shrine offerings** — Animal remains are offered to the earth shrine.

## Oral tradition & literature

Igala oral tradition preserves stories about origins, hunting, war, royal history, and ancestors.

- **Hunter-founder story** — One legend says a hunter founded Igalaland after finding it suitable for hunting, and his family continued the local lineage.
- **Oju-Ocha place-name** (*Oju-Ocha*) — Oju-Ocha means “the place where guinea-fowl come to wash.”
- **Inikpi story** (*Inikpi*) — Oma Idoko is said to have buried his daughter Inikpi alive so Igala could win a war against Jukun dominance.
- **Hunting lore** — The founding story shows the cultural importance of hunting, and some village names refer to local game.

## Language & religion

Igala is a tonal Yoruboid language, and Igala religion centers on Ọjọ, ancestors, gods, and diviners.

- **Igala language** (*Igala*) — Igala is spoken mainly in Kogi State and belongs to the Benue–Congo branch of Niger–Congo.
- **Igala dialects** — Named dialects include Ankpa, Akpanya, Alọma/Ofabo, Central, Ibaji, Ogwugwu or Ogugu, Anyugba, Ebu, and Ife.
- **Creator** (*Ọjọ*) — Ọjọ or Ojo is the supreme creator.
- **Ancestors** (*Ibegwu*) — Ibegwu are ancestral beings regarded as representatives and messengers of Ọjọ.
- **Personal god** (*Okega*) — Okega is described as the personal god of the deceased in the Oloja performance.
