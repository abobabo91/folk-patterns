## Overview

The Luvale live mainly in northwestern Zambia and southeastern Angola. In Zambia they are centered on Zambezi, formerly known as Balovale. Their culture includes matrilineal social organization, initiation ceremonies, mask traditions, basketry, music, ritual practices, and agricultural life. They are closely related to the Lunda and Ndembu and share cultural similarities with the Kaonde, Chokwe, and Luchazi.

## Material culture

Luvale material culture includes pottery, metalwork, basketry, mat weaving, woodwork, barkcloth costumes, and carved objects.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Luvale craft production includes mats, baskets, barkcloth costumes, and woven vegetable-string clothing.

- **Woven mat** (*visalo*) — Luvale are known for weaving mats called visalo.
- **Coloured vegetable-string costume** — A museum catalogue records a tight-fitting net costume made from coloured vegetable string.
- **Barkcloth makishi costume** (*makishi*) — During mukanda, makishi wear dyed woven barkcloth costumes with elaborate masks.
- **Winnowing basket** — A reed and string plaited flat basket with a dark and light twilled pattern was used as a winnowing tray.
- **Plaited basket** — A reed-plaited basket has a neck, a black and brown twilled pattern, a round top, and a square bottom.

### Clothing & dress

The profile describes ceremonial clothing linked to wali and mukanda rather than everyday dress.

- **Initiation girdle** (*zeva*) — During wali, an initiate makes and wears a girdle called a zeva throughout the ceremony.
- **Traditional cotton cloth** (*chitenge*) — When a wali initiate returns to her village, she is covered with a traditional cotton cloth called chitenge.
- **Bark-string head-dress** — A museum catalogue records a hemispherical head-dress made of bark string, red earth, and grease.
- **Barkcloth costume** — During mukanda, male initiates and makishi are associated with dyed woven costumes made from barkcloth.

### Architecture

The profile names a grass hut for wali and an isolated bush camp for mukanda.

- **Initiation grass hut** (*litungu*) — A grass hut called a litungu is built for a girl undergoing wali initiation.
- **Fig tree** (*muulya*) — A girl entering wali seclusion is taken near a fig tree called muulya.
- **Boys’ bush camp** — During mukanda, boys live for one to three months in an isolated bush camp.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Luvale are described as skilled in pottery, metalwork, basketry, mat weaving, and woodwork.

- **Hide-seat stool** (*likupu*) — Some Luvale stools have a hide seat on a wooden frame called a likupu.
- **Carved double dish** — A carved wooden double dish has two lidded dishes, with a fish on the upper lid.
- **Iron-bladed axe** — Museum records include axes with iron or steel blades and carved or coloured wooden handles.
- **Cassava ladle** — Museum records include two wooden cassava ladles.
- **Human-face comb** — A wooden comb has ten teeth and a handle carved with a human face and tall hair-do.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The profile records shell, copper, beads, oil, and red ochre as forms of adornment.

- **Shell ornament** — A white helix-shell ornament has yellow-brown spots, a hole at the top, and a strip of hide.
- **Copper ornaments** — A pair of small U-shaped copper ornaments has bosses, repousse dots, hollow interiors, and jingling metal pieces.
- **Beaded axe handle** — Beads are used as decoration on the wooden handle of an axe.
- **Oil and red ochre body covering** — During wali, the initiate is covered in oil and red ochre after performing dances.

## Music & performance

Luvale music uses xylophones, thumb pianos, drums, rattles, and other instruments in ceremonies and dancing.

- **Xylophone** (*jinjimba*) — The jinjimba is a Luvale xylophone.
- **Small hand piano** (*likembe*) — The likembe is a small hand piano.
- **Royal drum** — A royal drum has a wooden body, lizard-skin membranes, four handles, and a gourd tube with a spider-web diaphragm.
- **Thumb piano** (*sansa*) — A sansa may have a resonator, eight iron keys, and a carved wooden sounding board.
- **Composite double rattle** — A composite double rattle made of twigs, grass basketry, and seed rattles was used for dancing.

## Dance & theatre

Luvale mask performances represent ancestors and are closely linked to initiation ceremonies.

- **Masked male dancers** (*makishi*) — During Likumbi Lya Mize, masked male dancers called makishi perform with girls who have undergone wali.
- **Pwebo mask figure** (*Pwebo*) — Pwebo, also called Mwanapwebo, is one of the makishi figures.
- **Chisaluke mask figure** (*Chisaluke*) — Chisaluke is one of the makishi figures in the mukanda masquerade.
- **Mupala mask figure** (*Mupala*) — Mupala, also called Kayipu, is one of the makishi figures.
- **Katotola mask figure** (*Katotola*) — Katotola is one of the makishi figures in the mukanda masquerade.

## Festivals & rituals

Luvale ceremonies mark initiation, coming of age, marriage, and the traditional year.

- **Mize ceremony** (*Likumbi Lya Mize*) — Likumbi Lya Mize takes place during the last weekend of August and generally lasts a week.
- **Female initiation** (*wali*) — Wali begins when a girl experiences her first menstruation cycle and enters seclusion for instruction and preparation for marriage.
- **Male coming-of-age ceremony** (*mukanda*) — Mukanda, also called kumukanda, involves boys between the ages of 8 and 12 and includes preparation, circumcision, seclusion, and graduation.
- **Marriage bride price** (*matemo*) — Bride price is called matemo or vikumba.
- **Welcome celebration** (*vitilekela*) — A marriage welcome celebration is called vitilekela.

## Foodways

Luvale foodways center on farming, hunting, fishing, and processing crops.

- **Staple crops** — Staple crops include manioc, cassava, yams, and peanuts.
- **Beer maize** — Maize is grown for beer.
- **Snuff crops** — Tobacco and hemp are grown for snuff.
- **Big-game hunting society** (*yanga*) — A society of hunters called yanga is responsible for catching bigger game.
- **Dried catfish** — Luvale fishermen export dried catfish to the mining centers of the Copperbelt.

## Oral tradition & literature

Luvale oral tradition preserves migration stories, dynastic history, clan identity, sand designs, and names.

- **Clan recitation** (*kulisasula jikumbu*) — The clan recitation called kulisasula jikumbu identifies a clan member.
- **Sand design and calculus** (*tusona*) — Tusona are ideographic tracings made in sand and are included in the mukanda curriculum.
- **First migrant leader** (*Kenga Naweji*) — Luvale oral tradition says Kenga Naweji was the first leader among migrants moving south from north of Lake Tanganyika.
- **Founder of the Luvale chieftainship** (*Chinyama cha Mukwamayi*) — Chinyama cha Mukwamayi is described as the founder of the Luvale chieftainship.
- **Returned person name** (*Kahilu*) — Kahilu means “he who has returned/come back,” and the female version is Omba.

## Language & religion

Luvale is a regional Bantu language, and Luvale religion includes a sky god, spirits, divination, and healing.

- **Sky god** (*Kalunga*) — Kalunga is the Luvale sky god of creation and supreme power.
- **Nature and ancestral spirits** (*mahamba*) — Mahamba are nature and ancestral spirits belonging to individuals, families, or communities.
- **Sorcerer** (*orwanga*) — Sorcerers called orwanga may activate evil spirits to cause illness.
- **Diviner** (*nganga*) — A diviner called nganga attempts to identify the source of a patient’s problem.
- **Traditional healer** (*chimbanda*) — A traditional healer called chimbanda may select a new name for a seriously ill child.
