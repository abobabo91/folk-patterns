## Overview

The Boma are a Bantu ethnic group in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. They live mainly along the banks of the lower Kasai and Lukenié rivers. The sources give an estimated 38,000 native speakers, while noting that estimates vary. The Boma speak Boma, a Bantu language. The Boma formed the Boma Kingdom in the 17th century, which continued until Belgian colonial conquest. The population includes Boma Nku in the south, mainly in the Kasai region and the middle stretch of the Kasai River, and Boma Ngeli in the north, in the region of Mushie. Museum catalogue records document bows, a basket, a sword, a bronze ring, and photographs of Boma adults and children.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Ngondo** — A bronze ring recorded by the Museum of World Culture. The catalogue describes it as a coin and status symbol, and states that it formed part of the bride-price.

### Clothing & dress

The British Museum photographic records show Boma male and female adults and children, most wearing cloths around the lower bodies. The photographs also record personal ornaments. The sources do not identify the garments by vernacular name, distinguish everyday from ceremonial dress, or document head coverings, belts, footwear, or other forms of dress.

### Architecture

A British Museum photographic record shows rectangular, single-storey, thatched-roof buildings associated with a small group of Boma adults and children. Another photograph shows trees and single-storey European buildings in the background. The sources used do not provide further information about Boma architecture, named building types, interior arrangements, decoration, or urban traditions.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Museum records document a bow made of wood and string, a basket made of cane, and a sword with a skin-covered sheath. The sword record also identifies the object as a knife with a sheath and associates it with Boma. The Museum of World Culture catalogue records a bronze ring called “Ngondo,” described as a coin and status symbol. It states that rings of this kind were collected on bast cords or similar materials and could be melted down for practical use. No Boma ceramic tradition is described.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The Museum of World Culture catalogue describes the bronze ring “Ngondo” as a status symbol that formed part of the bride-price. It gives the diameter as approximately 6 cm and states that such rings were collected on bast cords or similar materials. The catalogue also records the term “Nsimbi,” explaining it through “simba,” meaning to hold, hold fast, or hold in. British Museum photographs show personal ornaments, but the sources do not identify their forms or materials. Tattoos, hair practices, and other body adornment are not documented.

## Language & religion

Boma is a Bantu language of the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Maho (2009) considers Boma, B.821 Mpe (Kempee), and B.822 Nunu to be closely related languages. Mpe and Nunu do not have ISO codes, and the source distinguishes this Nunu from the Nunu dialect of Ngiri. The Boma Nku inhabit the south, mainly in the Kasai region, while the Boma Ngeli live in the north in the region of Mushie; both are described as coming from lands surrounding the upper reaches of the Lukenié River. The sources used do not document Boma religious beliefs, institutions, spiritual practices, or historical writing systems.
