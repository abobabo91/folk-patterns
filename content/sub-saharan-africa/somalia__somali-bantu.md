## Overview

The Somali Bantu are a Bantu ethnic minority group in Somalia, also known as Jareer, Jareerweyne, or WaGosha. They primarily live in southern Somalia, especially in agricultural communities near the Jubba and Shabelle rivers, with concentrations in the Juba region, the Lower Shabelle, Jubaland, and Bay regions. Their ancestors came from various cultural groups in present-day Tanzania and Mozambique through the nineteenth-century Somali slave trade, although some alternative origin theories are disputed. Population estimates vary: a BBC report estimated around 900,000 in Somalia in 2003, while Ken Menkhaus estimated approximately 350,000, or about 5% of Somalia’s population, for the same year. The sources emphasize their riverine farming, Bantu linguistic and cultural traditions, ancestral East African identities, and the survival of practices connected with music, dance, basket weaving, and agriculture.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources state that an important cultural aspect of Somali Bantu art uses bright colors and fabrics.

### Architecture

Somali Bantu communities historically established agricultural settlements along the Jubba and Shabelle rivers. Nineteenth-century travelers described Gosha communities as fortified agricultural settlements populated by former slaves from various Bantu groups. These communities cleared forests, cultivated crops, traded with neighboring Somalis, and grew through the arrival of additional runaway slaves.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do mention farming, irrigation canals, rafts used on the river, and guns acquired from the sultan of Zanzibar.

## Music & performance

Music is identified as one of the ancestral practices retained by Somali Bantu communities, with similarities to traditions practiced in Tanzania. The sources also state that Bantu communities maintain smaller social units associated with ceremonial dance groupings.

The sources describe possession dances and the use of magic as ancestral animist traditions retained by many Bantu, whether Muslim or not. Traditional Bantu rituals and dances continue in some contexts alongside Islamic practices.

The sources mention that similarities with Tanzanian traditions extend to hunting, harvesting, and music.

## Dance & theatre

The sources document possession dances among ancestral animist traditions retained by many Somali Bantu. Smaller social units are often divided according to matrilineal kinship groups, and these groups are often interchangeable with ceremonial dance groupings. Traditional Bantu dances continue in some contexts alongside Islamic practices.

No named dance, dramatic tradition, theatrical form, mask performance, or entertainment genre is given in the sources.

## Festivals & rituals

The majority of Somali Bantus have converted to Islam, and Islam is described as their predominant religion. They celebrate Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha. Traditional Bantu rituals and dances continue in some contexts alongside Islamic practices, and many people have retained ancestral animist traditions, including possession dances and the use of magic.

They state that formal intermarriage between Somali Bantus and Somalis is extremely rare and typically results in ostracism when it occurs.

Bantu oral traditions recount a rebellion led by a woman named Wanakucha. The rebels sought to return to homelands in Tanzania and Mozambique but settled in the Gosha region along the Jubba River, where they cleared forests and established farming communities. Another event preserved in Gosha oral traditions is the defeat of a major Ogaden attack around 1890.

## Foodways

Somali Bantu communities have traditionally been sedentary agriculturalists engaged in farming along riverine areas. Enslaved Bantu were made to work on plantations along the Shebelle and Jubba rivers, cultivating cash crops such as grain and cotton. Later Gosha communities cultivated crops and established agricultural settlements along the Jubba River.

The sources describe irrigation canals and plantations, as well as the historical use of coerced agricultural labor under Italian colonial rule. The sources mention that most Somali Bantus have converted to Islam.

## Oral tradition & literature

Bantu oral traditions preserve accounts of migration, enslavement, rebellion, and settlement. One tradition describes a rebellion led by Wanakucha, after which the rebels settled in the Gosha region along the Jubba River. Oral traditions also preserve the defeat of a major Ogaden attack around 1890. The victory is largely attributed to Nasib Bundo, a Yao former slave who became a leading Gosha figure and was known as the “Sultan of the Gosha.”

The sources record the names of several historical and traditional figures, including Nasib Bundo and Wanakucha.

## Language & religion

Somali Bantus adopted a local dialect of Somali while retaining Bantu linguistic and cultural traditions. By the early twentieth century, many Gosha communities had become increasingly integrated into Somali society and had adopted the Somali language. Bantu populations maintain ancestral tribal identities connected with southeastern African origins, including identities associated with groups such as the Zigua. The sources distinguish Somali Bantu communities from Swahili-speaking societies such as the Bajuni and Bravanese.

The majority of Somali Bantus have converted to Islam. The sources state that some first embraced Islam in order to free themselves from slavery, since Muslims could not be slaves of other Muslims. Alongside Islam, many have retained ancestral animist traditions, including possession dances and the use of magic. Somali Bantu religious saints and Sufi-influenced practices have been regarded by Somali Muslims as unorthodox because of their liberal interpretation of Islam. A small number of Somali Bantu in the Ifo refugee camp converted to Christianity by 1996, and an International Organization for Migration report from 2002 noted a Bantu-constructed Christian church in that camp.
