## Overview

The Dagbamba, also called the Dagomba or Dagbamba, are an ethnic group of Ghana and Togo whose homeland is Dagbon, in northern Ghana. Their population is over 3.1 million. They speak Dagbani, a widely spoken language in northern Ghana belonging to the Mabia, or Mole-Dagbani, subgroup of the Gur languages. Dagbon covers about 20,000 km2 and includes the city of Yendi, the administrative capital of the Dagbon Traditional Kingdom, and Tamale, the largest Dagomba settlement. Dagomba society is marked by hierarchical chieftaincy, sophisticated oral tradition, professional drummers, and the preservation of royal titles and land ownership for female chiefs. Their history combines centralized kingship under the Ya-Na with the authority of indigenous Tindaamba, who preserve ancient traditions and lead traditional religious acts.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Dagomba houses are arranged in a specific order, with the chief or elderly man’s hut built in the centre. The court and administrative capital of Dagbon is at Yendi, while Tamale is the largest Dagomba settlement and the capital of the Northern Region.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do state that the seat of the Yaa Naa, or king of Dagbon, is a collection of lion and cow skins; for this reason, the political system is often called the Yendi Skin rather than a throne, crown, or stool.

## Music & performance

Dagbon history is transmitted through oral tradition, with drums and other musical instruments forming part of the Dagomba oral system. Drummers act as professional griots known as *Lunsi*. They preserve and communicate Dagbon history, including the political history associated in oral tradition with Tohazie, translated in the source as “red hunter.”

Chieftaincy is central to Dagomba social organization. The Yaa Naa, or paramount King, stands at the head of a hierarchical system of rulers, while chiefs oversee specific towns, regions, artifacts, resources, or trees. Chiefs traditionally sit on a stack of skins.

## Dance & theatre

They do record an oral tradition incorporating drums and other musical instruments, with *Lunsi* serving as professional griots.

## Festivals & rituals

The Dagomba celebrate several festivals. These include the Fire Festival, the Damba Festival, the Guinea Fowl Festival, the Yam Festival, *Konyuri Chuɣu*, identified in the source as Eid al-Fitr, and *Chimsi Chuɣu*, identified as Eid al-Adha. A localized Market Festival, *Daa Chuɣu*, is celebrated by the people of Tolon.

The Bugum Festival, also known as the Fire Festival, existed before the formation of the Kingdom of Dagbon. Families gather for communal celebrations and rituals to honor their ancestors, ask for blessings, and ward off evil spirits. Muslims and non-Muslims attend. Muslims celebrate *Bugum* to commemorate the arrival of Prophet Noah’s, or Nuhu’s, Ark after the flood. During the night, bundles of grass are used as torches. Because the celebration brings in a new year, some non-Muslims use the occasion to offer sacrifices to God and their ancestors.

Dagomba religious life also includes traditional shrines and soothsaying. Each city has a shrine, or *buɣli*. The source names Jaagbo as the shrine of the People of Tolon and Wonoyili as the shrine of Nyankpala. Soothsayers, or *baɣa*, are consulted for prophecies and solutions to problems, and many soothsaying roles are inherited through a maternal brother.

## Foodways

The sources connect Islam in Dagbon with trade, economic growth, and the later development of Islamic education.

## Oral tradition & literature

Dagomba history has been passed down through oral tradition. Drummers known as *Lunsi* act as professional griots, preserving historical knowledge through drums and other musical instruments. The political history of Dagbon is said in oral tradition to originate in the life story of Tohazie, translated as “red hunter.”

The sources also describe traditions concerning Naa Gbewaa, regarded as the founder of the Dagbon Kingdom. According to the source, Gbewaa unified the Dagomba people into a kingdom, while his descendants centralized it. Oral tradition also connects the Dagomba and Mossi through a common ancestor, Naa Gbewa, and identifies Yennenga as the founder of the Mossi kingdoms.

## Language & religion

The Dagomba speak Dagbani, which belongs to the Mabia, or Mole-Dagbani, subgroup of the Gur languages. The Dagomba have a writing system called the Dagbani script, using a modified Arabic alphabet with Dagbani syntax. Archival manuscripts have been lost, although many were transferred to Denmark. After joining the Gold Coast as a protectorate, the Dagomba adopted the Greek and Latin alphabets.

The Dagomba practice both Islam and the Dagbon Traditional Religion. Islam was brought to the region by Soninke traders, known as Wangara by Ghanaians, between the 12th and 15th centuries. Since the time of Naa Zanjina, Islam has been the state religion and has grown steadily. Christianity arrived in association with European colonization. In the Dagbon Traditional Religion, there are several gods, *wuni* in the singular and *wuna* in the plural. Naawuni is the chief of these gods. Indigenous Tindaamba preserve ancient Dagbon traditions, lead traditional religious acts, and address local issues; they are chosen by an oracle rather than appointed by the Yaa Naa.
