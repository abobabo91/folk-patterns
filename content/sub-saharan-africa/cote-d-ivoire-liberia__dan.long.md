## Overview

The Dan, also called Mano-Dan, are a Mande people of northwestern Ivory Coast and neighbouring Liberia, numbering roughly 700,000. Their largest settlement is Man, in Ivory Coast, and much of their territory lies in the high forest south of the Nimba range, into which they moved from the northern savanna. Officially they are known as Yacouba (or Yakouba); in Liberia they are also called Gio, a Bassa word for "slave" and a derogatory exonym. Their neighbours include the Krahn, Kpelle and Mano. They speak Dan, a Southern Mande language. In folk-culture terms they are known above all for wood sculpture — a very large variety of masks, each with its own form and purpose, and carved ceremonial ladles — together with brass casting by the lost-wax method and the masquerades of their men's societies.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here do not describe Dan weaving, dyeing or embroidery traditions. What they record of Dan pattern-bearing material is carved and cast rather than woven: incised linear bands on wooden spoon handles, geometric carving on stool tops, and textile used as attached trim rather than as a figured cloth — one basketry head-dress in the collection is finished with black feathers and a blue cotton fringe along one side of the rim, and one mask carries a black-and-white cotton beard at the chin. Others use plant-fibre rather than cloth: a composite mask is fringed all round with bast, and one carved head on a pedestal has strips of plaited fibre pinned to it to represent hair.

**Motif vocabulary.** The catalogue records name repeated incised lines on spoon handles and on the backs of spoons, geometric and other patterns on the top of a four-legged stool, scarification rendered as two horizontal lines under each eye on a female head, and a crescent of triangular peaks forming the brow of one mask.

### Clothing & dress

The sources used do not describe Dan everyday or ceremonial clothing directly. What they do document is the masked figure as a dressed persona: the men's societies call upon and control tutelary spirits from the bush, who appear in public as masked figures, and the masks are perceived to embody the most powerful of spirit forces, called *ge* or *gle*. Several of the masks in the collection are made to be completed with attachments — holes bored along the chin for a fringe, a leather strap at the chin representing a beard, a cotton beard tied to the chin, a bast fringe all round the face, and an iron ring set into the top of the back — so the carved face was one element of a fuller costume whose other parts the catalogue does not record. One basketry head-dress survives, decorated with black feathers and a blue cotton fringe.

### Architecture

Traditional Dan dwellings were small single-room huts of mud and thatch. Each of a man's wives had her own hut, in which her children lived until they were old enough to move out — a form that follows directly from a household made up of a husband, one or more wives and their children. Today houses are large and rectangular with several rooms; older building practice persists mainly in the poorest districts. Above the level of the individual house, the settlement itself is the significant built unit: a Dan village is divided into quarters, called quarters in Liberian English, each housing an extended family or lineage and each headed by its own quarter chief, who is chosen either as the oldest male of the family or for having the most forceful personality. Towns grouped together under a central government are called clans.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Wood sculpture is the principal Dan medium: masks above all, and carved wooden spoons. The large ceremonial ladle, the *wunkirmian*, is carved to honour a woman distinguished by her generosity and hospitality, and belongs to the *wunkirle*, the most hospitable woman of her village quarter, who traditionally names her own successor. Its commonest form has the handle carved as a human head with the narrow eyes and oval face of the *buangle* mask, portraying a particular woman; other examples in the collection turn the handle into a female torso with two legs, or into a pair of legs alone. Carvers also set metal into wood: several spoon heads have six metal teeth inserted in the mouth. Household woodwork includes four-legged stools with carved tops. Brass casting was blacksmiths' work, done at night to keep the object from onlookers, by the *cire-perdue* or lost-wax method.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Cast brass ornament is well represented: brass bracelets, brass finger rings, brass anklets, and one brass anklet hung with nine crotals. Brass casting among the Dan was under way at least by the early 19th century and possibly much earlier, and the We people to the east may have introduced it. A Liberian government decree of the late 1930s outlawing all brass jewelry freed up a supply of metal, which casters turned to figurative sculpture — figures about eight inches tall, holding objects of cultural importance, made chiefly as prestige items. One such cast figure in the collection is a boy with his right hand raised, a long-beaked bird in his left hand, a ring and small bundle on his head and a wide belt at the waist. Carved heads record body adornment too: pierced ears, three rings around the neck, and scarification lines below the eyes.

## Music & performance

The sources used do not name Dan instruments or song genres. They do record singing as part of the women's Feast of Merit: highly respected women prepared food for a large number of local and foreign guests, and during the feast they danced brandishing their rice-filled ladles while singing in strident voices. There are no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions listed for the country in the material consulted. One object suggests sound in performance — a pair of ankle bells of metal, dated to the early 19th century — as does the brass anklet strung with nine crotals.

## Dance & theatre

Dan performance centres on masquerade. The men's fraternal societies demonstrate their power through masquerades in which spirits from the bush appear as masked figures, and through those mask-spirits the societies settle disputes, enforce rules and correct behaviour. Certain masked performers carry Janus-faced heads on a staff, the two faces looking in opposite directions to signify the *gle*'s ability to see in all directions. The masks themselves are built for performance: one has a movable jaw and four boss-like eyes, another steel teeth and horns across the eyebrows. Women's dancing is documented at feasts, where the honoured women dance holding their ladles, and where the standing of the woman being honoured may be shown by her being carried through the village in a hammock by the women of her quarter.

## Festivals & rituals

The Feast of Merit is the occasion the sources describe most fully. Women recognised for their hospitality prepare food for many guests; the honoured woman marches at the head of the line of women from her quarter, each carrying a pot of cooked rice or soup, and she either distributes the food herself or uses her ladle to direct its distribution. At some feasts the honoured women of a village compete in generosity by giving out peanuts, candy, coins and other foods, with the other women of the quarter contributing gifts in her name. The other major ritual complex is initiation. All Dan males attend *bon*, or bush school, as adolescents, where they are prepared to meet the mysteries of the spirit world and taught the rules of adult Dan men; women have a comparable society. Above the village, communities are allied through the Leopard society, *Gor* — Dan for "leopard" — a peacemaking body whose spirit is responsible for reconciling communities that have been in conflict. Livestock is kept partly for ritual: white animals are eaten only on special ritual occasions involving much feasting, or sacrificed for the forgiveness of sin.

## Foodways

The Dan are primarily farmers who clear forest land each year. Staple and cash crops are rice, cassava, sweet potatoes and several kinds of maize, with cocoa, coffee and rubber grown today. Women are given small plots for their own vegetables, for the household or for market sale, and greens are gathered from both garden and wild forest plants. Palm oil is extracted from the many wild oil palms and used for cooking and as fuel. Men do most of the agricultural work and all of the hunting and most of the fishing; women help with weeding and harvesting and prepare the meals; children drive cattle, wild animals and birds off the crops. Livestock includes cattle, sheep, goats and fowl. Cooked rice and soup are the foods carried in pots at feasts, and rice fills the ceremonial ladles the women brandish as they dance. The sources used do not describe named Dan dishes or drink traditions.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used do not record Dan folktales, epics or proverbs. What they do preserve is a body of remembered history of named warrior chiefs: Grougbay Zobaneeay, who drove other groups from what is now Nimba County as far as Loguatuo in Ivory Coast; Kipko Toh'ah-Gbeu, who pushed the Kru from the site of Tapitah and whose last major engagement was at Sanniquellie, from which he retired wounded, and who gave his daughter Lhe'kpahseu in marriage to Grougbay Zobaneeay; Bho'Yaah of present-day Garplay, among the last chiefs to resist the Americo-Liberian advance into Nimba before making a truce; and Gonsahn Ghe'Gbeu of Miampleu Yeezleu. A different channel of transmission is dreaming, which is the means by which people communicate with Zu.

## Language & religion

Dan is a Southern Mande language, spoken by around 800,000 people in Ivory Coast, 150,000–200,000 in Liberia and about 800 in Guinea. It is tonal, with nine to eleven contour and register tones depending on dialect, and four to five level tones. Dialects are Gio (Liberian Dan), Gweetaawu (Eastern Dan), Blowo (Western Dan), and Kla, which is evidently a separate language. Alternative names include Yacouba, Yakubasa, Gio, Gyo, Gio-Dan and Da. The Liberian orthography marks seven tone levels with diacritics and writes nasal vowels by adding *n*. Dan religion centres on Zlan, the Supreme God who created the universe and whom no one can reach or see, and on Zu, an independent power regarded as Zlan's Holy Spirit, present throughout the universe and appealed to for many kinds of help. Most Dan believe in reincarnation, by which Zu can pass a person into another person or an animal after death. Zu is harnessed through masquerade or divination, by making an object for it to inhabit.
