## Overview

The Tandroy — also written Antandroy, and called Ampatres in the 17th century — are a traditionally nomadic people of the arid far south of Madagascar, in the region their name describes: *roy* means thorn, *an* "place of", *t* "from", so Tandroy are "people of the thorns", after the spiny endemic thickets of the southwest. Their homeland is the modern Androy Region, roughly between Amboasary and Beloha and between the ocean and Bekily, with the population densest around Ambovombe. There were about 600,000 Tandroy in 2013, making them the fifth largest ethnic group on the island. They speak a dialect of Malagasy, a Malayo-Polynesian language derived from the Barito languages of southern Borneo. In folk-culture terms they stand apart from most Malagasy through plank-built houses, spear dances, cotton weaving charged with spiritual value, and painted tombs crowned with zebu skulls.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Hand-spun cotton weaving** — Cotton is hand-carded and spun by women, who roll a wooden spindle against the thigh to twist the thread, then weave it into cloth. Weaving is exclusively women's work.

***Fole velo*** — Before the spun thread is dipped in its stiffening solution it is called *fole velo*, "living yarn", and is held to be imbued with spiritual power. It is used in rituals — the circumcision ceremony among them — and an *ambiasa* (healer) ties it around a person's wrist as protection. No other Malagasy textile in these sources carries an equivalent pre-finishing sacred state.

***Lamba*** **and loincloth** — Woven cotton is made up into *lamba* wrappers and loincloths. A British Museum loin-cloth in this atlas shows the type: off-white cotton with a blue and red geometric design woven in silk across each short end, and a fringe at both ends — so the decorated band is confined to the ends rather than spread across the field.

***Landy*** — Raw silk is also used for clothing, and silk appears as the patterning yarn on the cotton loincloth above.

### Clothing & dress

The southern region is hot for most of the year, and Tandroy dress is correspondingly minimal; the sources note that outsiders turned this into a stereotype of "half-clothed savages" during the colonial period. The staple garments are the woven cotton *lamba* wrapper and the loincloth, sometimes in raw silk (*landy*). The museum objects shown here fill out the rest of the outfit: a hat woven of basketry, a hat-and-wig made of goat hair, and a sandal of cattle hide, one of a pair. Metal bracelets, silver necklaces and metal hair-pins (below) are worn with these. The sources do not describe belts, nor a separate ceremonial costume distinct from daily wear, beyond the ritual use of *fole velo* thread tied at the wrist and worn in the circumcision rite.

### Architecture

Most Malagasy dwellings are built from pliable plant material; the Tandroy are one of the few groups to build in wood plank. The house is square rather than the rectangle usual elsewhere on the island, raised on low stilts, roofed with a peak, and walled with vertically hung planks fixed to a wooden frame. It has no windows and three wooden doors, each assigned: the front door is the women's entrance, the rear door is for children, and the third is used by the men. Yards are fenced with prickly-pear cactus (*raketa*) or with lengths of indigenous succulents cut from the surrounding spiny forest. The other major structure is the tomb, called *fanesy*, "your eternal place", as among the neighbouring Mahafaly: large, rectangular, sized to the wealth of the man buried in it, and covered with colourful painting. Tall stones representing male and female were traditionally set on either side; towers are now often built instead of the stones. A model Tandroy house can be visited on the grounds of the Museum of the Tandroy, in the Berenty Reserve about two hours north of Taolagnaro.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources say nothing about Tandroy pottery. Iron is the historical speciality: the Tandroy were known as great ironsmiths, and their skill at repairing firearms was particularly reputed in the imperial period before French colonisation in 1897. The objects in this atlas are weapons, containers and utensils — wood-and-metal spears, an axe with a wooden handle and an iron blade, and a sling of cow skin. Containers are made from what the dry south provides: a gourd milk container, a lidded raffia money basket for jewellery and medicinal plants, and a raffia amulet basket holding medicinal plants. A wooden spoon is carved with a figure. Cattle underlie the whole inventory — the herding of zebu is the principal economic activity, and zebu milk and curd are dietary staples.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Silver is the prestige metal: two of the necklaces here are silver. Bracelets are metal, worn in numbers — five are in this selection alone — and hair is dressed with metal hair-pins. Adornment extends to hair itself in the form of a hat-and-wig made of goat hair. The one documented ritual ornament is textile rather than metal: the *ambiasa* ties *fole velo* thread around a person's wrist to protect them. Baskets made for jewellery and medicinal plants, woven of raffia and fitted with lids, indicate that ornaments were kept alongside amulet material. The sources do not mention tattooing or cosmetic body painting.

## Music & performance

Stringed instruments dominate. The Tandroy build *marovany* box zithers from pine planks and string them with unwound bicycle cables. The *mandolina* and *gitara* are the Tandroy names for a popular southern chordophone close to the *kabosy* but strung with nylon fishing line and fitted with five or seven movable frets, so the player can shift the instrument's tuning. The *lokanga* is a bowed instrument with a gourd resonator, related to the *jejy voatavo* played further north, but with the resonator carved to make a three-stringed fiddle. Vocal music is built on rich polyharmonic melodies. The music that accompanies the spear dances is punctuated by shrill whistles and fipple flutes. Performance is bound into ritual: the *bilo* trance is the state in which the Tandroy commune with, and are possessed by, the ancestors, and ritual wailing belongs to the funeral sequence, alongside the communal feast at which zebu are sacrificed.

## Dance & theatre

The Tandroy are known across Madagascar for their distinctive dances, which are performed with spears and accompanied by music marked by shrill whistles and fipple flutes. Funerals carry their own performance register: the mood at the coffin ceremony can be celebratory, and in some Tandroy communities the men lift the coffin and run into the sea with it. The sources describe no masked, puppet or dramatic theatre tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources give no annual or seasonal festival calendar; Tandroy ritual life as documented here is life-cycle and ancestral. Circumcision is the central rite for males, and in some communities the foreskin is fired from the barrel of a gun in celebration; *fole velo* thread is used in the ceremony. Funerals are the largest undertaking. The family holds a communal feast at which zebu are sacrificed and the meat distributed to the community; after the body has lain in state for several days it is coffined and more zebu are killed. The coffin then waits — sometimes months — while the *fanesy* tomb is completed, and a further two-day mourning ceremony takes place during construction. Zebu sacrifice and ritual wailing may be repeated when the coffin is set in the tomb, stones are heaped over it to fill the chamber, and the skulls of the sacrificed zebu are placed on top to declare the dead man's wealth. The deceased's house is then burned to close the rites, and the family does not return to visit the tomb. Prohibitions (*fady*) set by elders and ancestors run through daily life: women may not milk zebu, killing the radiated tortoise is forbidden generally — one king was forbidden even to look at one — and naming a dead king is *fady*.

## Foodways

Extreme aridity in Toliara province means the Tandroy eat less rice than the average Malagasy and depend instead on crops that need less water. The traditional diet is maize, sweet potato, manioc and zebu milk and curd, with yams, taro root and millet as further staples, generally boiled in water and sometimes served in whole milk or flavoured with crushed peanuts. Subsistence farming covers cassava, millet, rice and maize, and honey is a major Tandroy product. Milk is handled in gourd containers of the kind shown here, and it is *fady* for women to milk zebu at all. Ceremonial eating is beef: zebu are sacrificed at the funeral feast and the meat given to the community, and further animals are killed when the body is coffined, their skulls ending up on the roof of the tomb.

## Oral tradition & literature

The region's oral history splits along clan lines. The Karimbola of western Tandroy tell no migration story — their tradition implies the inhabitants were always there — while in the east the original Mahandrovato population was gradually outnumbered by Antanosy and Bara refugees. Narratives of descent also define clans: members of the Zafindravoay hold that they descend from the union of a Tandroy woman and a crocodile, and crocodiles are widely feared. The *kokolampo* spirit is understood to act for good or ill in a person's life. One rule governs speech directly: it is *fady* to utter the name of a deceased king. The sources name no epic cycle or written literary tradition.

## Language & religion

The Tandroy speak a dialect of Malagasy, a Malayo-Polynesian language of the Barito group whose relatives are spoken in southern Borneo. Religion combines the common Malagasy belief in a creator god with the cult of the ancestors, who live on, intervene among the living and must be respected; the living commune with them, and are possessed by them, in the *bilo* trance. *Mpisoro* soothsayers are a standing feature of village society and hold recognised authority in traditional communities, and the *ambiasa* healer works with consecrated *fole velo* thread. Christianity reached southern Madagascar from the late 19th century, brought mainly by Lutheran missionaries, and Lutheranism remains the dominant Christian form among the Tandroy — though most continue to hold to the older beliefs alongside it.
