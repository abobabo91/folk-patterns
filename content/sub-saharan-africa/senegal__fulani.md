## Overview

The Fulani are the most widely dispersed ethnic group in West Africa, split between semi-nomadic herders of the Sahel and sedentary river-valley farmers. Their material culture is built for mobility—everything of value is portable, hung, wrapped, or worn. They are overwhelmingly Muslim, governed by the ethic of pulaaku: reserve, patience, modesty and self-control. Fulani communities today face significant hostility in several West African states over land conflicts and accusations of Islamist ties.

## Material culture

Fulani art and objects are designed for a mobile life: everything woven, carved, or forged is portable or wearable, with decorated gourds and textiles serving as wealth, lineage record, and household inventory.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Fulani weavers and dyers create indigo-dyed cloth and tapestry-woven blankets with dense geometric patterns that echo cattle, water, and tether designs.

- **Great marriage blanket** (*arkilla kerka*) — Woven in six to eight strips by male weaver-caste artisans, then sewn into a two-by-four-metre panel in indigo, madder-red, white and black with interlocking lozenges, hung as a curtain across the bridal hut.
- **All-wool herder's blanket** (*khasa*) — Thick blanket woven from long-haired sheep fleece in cream and dark brown or indigo bands, worn over the shoulder against cold and traded across the Sahel.
- **Lighter marriage hanging** (*arkilla jenngo*) — Narrower relative of the kerka with fewer strips and more open pattern, used as bed hanging and dowry piece.
- **Indigo cloth with burnished surface** (*gara*) — Cotton strip-cloth layered in fermented indigo vats until near-black and metallic, then beaten with wooden mallets to raise a burnished surface.
- **Embroidered robes** (*Fulani embroidery*) — White or ochre cotton thread in chain and satin stitch over chest, neck and sleeves of men's robes and yoke of women's wrappers, in registers of triangles and stepped diamonds.

### Clothing & dress

Men wear the wide boubou robe embroidered at the chest with the iconic conical Fulani hat; women layer wrapped cloth, head-ties and dozens of ornaments; at courtship gatherings, young men are the ornamented sex.

- **Conical hat** (*Fulani hat*) — Woven straw or reed cone with wide brim, bound with dyed leather strips and often topped with a leather knob and pendant thongs, worn by herders against sun and rain.
- **Wide robe** (*boubou (mbubbu)*) — Sleeveless or wide-sleeved robe over drawstring trousers in white, indigo or burnished gara cloth, with embroidery massed at the chest pocket.
- **Herder's staff** (*sawru*) — Long staff carried by herders, often carved in wood and used for herding and daily life.
- **Painted faces for courtship dance** (*gerewol paint*) — At the gerewol gathering, young men paint faces yellow-ochre and red with white line down nose, lips and eyelids blackened, ostrich plume in turban.

### Architecture

Pastoral Fulani build demountable grass houses that are struck and loaded onto pack animals at each move; sedentary Toucouleur villages of the river build mud-brick compounds arranged around swept courtyards.

- **Demountable grass house** (*suudu hudo*) — Hemisphere or loaf-shaped dwelling of bent millet-stalk or acacia arches lashed at crown and thatched with woven grass mats, erected and struck by women.
- **Decorated grass mat screens** (*rolled reed screens*) — Woven grass mats bound with dyed leather thong in banded patterns, rolled for transport, the principal decorated surface of the nomadic home and dowry property.
- **Village mosque** (*mosque*) — Massive tapering buttressed mud-and-timber walls with projecting toron beams, low minaret over the mihrab, following sahelian manner.
- **Mud-brick compound house** (*banco house*) — Rectangular mud-brick dwelling with flat or low-pitched thatch roof, arranged around swept sandy courtyard with fenced millet-stalk screen enclosure.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The milk gourd is the defining Fulani object, with a woman's nested stack displayed, carried and exhibited as her wealth, lineage record and household inventory.

- **Decorated milk gourd** (*kaggu*) — Halved Lagenaria gourds scraped, smoked and incised with fine registers of hatched triangles, zigzags and concentric bands, rubbed with soot or ochre so pattern reads dark.
- **Wooden churn** — Turned and incised by woodworking caste, used for churning butter.
- **Carved herder's staff** (*sawru*) — Turned and incised wooden staff carved by the laoɓe woodworking caste.
- **Forged knife** (*knife*) — Forged by blacksmiths and sheathed in tooled leather, worn at the upper arm by herders.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Fulani women wear massive twisted gold crescent earrings supported by cord over the crown, silver ornaments, amber and glass beads, and hair dressed in high crested coiffures hung with coins and discs.

- **Twisted gold crescent earring** (*kwottenai kanye*) — Hammered gold hoop worked in four lobes, so heavy it is supported by a cord of red wool or cotton over the crown of the head.
- **Amulet case** (*tumar*) — Flat rectangular or cylindrical container of silver or tooled leather holding a folded Qur'anic text, hung at neck or strapped across chest.
- **Hair ornaments** (*coins and silver discs*) — Strung in high crested coiffures over a shaped pad and parted in fine rows, with amber, carnelian, coral and glass trade beads.
- **Lip tattoo** — Fine line of dark blue-black tattoo along lower lip and gum margin, the mark of adult Fulani womanhood.

## Music & performance

Fulani music is caste music, performed by hereditary bards who praise and recite lineages; the three- to five-string plucked lute called hoddu is the signature instrument.

- **Praise singer and genealogist** (*gawlo (awlube plural)*) — Hereditary bard attached to noble lineages, paid to recite genealogy and praise.
- **Plucked lute** (*hoddu*) — Three- to five-string plucked lute with hide-covered wooden body, ancestor of the banjo and close cousin of the Wolof xalam.
- **Smaller lute** (*molo*) — One- or two-string relative of the hoddu.
- **End-blown reed flute** (*fulannu (serdu)*) — Slender flute whose breathy overblown line is the archetypal cattle-camp sound, often sung simultaneously by the player to produce two-voice drone-and-melody.
- **Water drum** (*calabash water-drum*) — Calabash floated upside-down in water and struck with rings, played by women to keep rhythm.

## Dance & theatre

The gerewol is a male beauty dance and courtship rite where young men in painted faces dance for hours on tiptoe, rolling eyes and baring teeth while women judges select partners.

- **Male beauty dance and courtship gathering** (*gerewol*) — Young men in painted faces and ostrich plumes line up and dance for hours on tiptoe, rolling eyes and baring white teeth while unmarried women judges select; a choice can constitute a marriage.
- **Slow grimacing charm dance** (*yaake*) — Related to the gerewol, a slow dance of charm and courtship display.
- **Welcome dance** (*ruume*) — Dance that opens the gerewol gathering.

## Festivals & rituals

The Muslim year and cattle calendar govern festivals: Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha are the great celebrations, the gerewol gatherings reunite dispersed clans for marriages and dispute-settling, and life-cycle rites mark naming, circumcision, marriage and death.

- **Eid al-Fitr** (*Korité*) — Great village festival ending Ramadan.
- **Eid al-Adha** (*Tabaski*) — Great festival requiring the sacrifice of a ram per household and new clothes for every member.
- **Prophet's birthday** (*Gamou (Mawlid)*) — Draws Toucouleur Sufi adherents to Tijani centres.
- **Naming ceremony** (*inde*) — Held on the seventh day after birth, when head is shaved, ram sacrificed and name pronounced by imam.
- **Herds crossing rivers** (*river crossing*) — Pastoral calendar ceremony at end of dry season when herds cross the Senegal and Niger rivers; gerewol gatherings follow in rains when clans reunite, marriages made and disputes settled.

## Foodways

The diet is built on milk for herders and grain for farmers; soured milk and millet couscous is the signature dish, served to guests across the Sahel.

- **Steamed millet couscous with soured milk** (*lacciri e kossam*) — Steamed millet couscous under soured milk, sometimes sweetened, the dish most identified with Fulani households.
- **Sweetened dessert with milk** (*thiakry (dégué)*) — Fine millet granules in sweetened curdled milk, served at naming ceremonies and Ramadan breaks.
- **Soured milk** (*kossam*) — Herder's staple food alongside fresh milk and butter.
- **Green tea ritual** (*ataya*) — Green gunpowder tea boiled with sugar and mint and poured from height into small glasses, served in three rounds of descending strength and ascending sweetness, each round a required social unit.

## Oral tradition & literature

Fulani oral literature is dominated by genealogy and pastoral epic; the Silâmaka and Poullôri cycle is the best-known Fulani narrative poem, while women tell taali folktales and animal trickster cycles at night.

- **Genealogy recitation** (*dilluɓe*) — Praise-recitation of lineage performed by hereditary bards, reaching back generations.
- **Fulani pastoral epic** (*Silâmaka and Poullôri*) — Cycle of the Macina hero and his companion, their defiance of the Bambara king of Ségou and their deaths, the best-known narrative poem of the Fulani-speaking world.
- **Folktale tradition** (*taali*) — Folktales told at night by women to children.
- **Arabic-script Pulaar** (*ajami*) — Arabic script adapted to Pulaar, used for devotional verse and Sufi poetry in the Fouta Toro since at least the eighteenth century.

## Language & religion

Pulaar is an Atlantic language with over twenty noun classes and consonant mutation; the Fulani are overwhelmingly Sunni Muslim of the Maliki school, most belonging to the Tijaniyya Sufi order.

- **Atlantic language** (*Pulaar (Fulfulde)*) — Niger-Congo language characterised by unusually large noun-class system and consonant mutation, a national language of Senegal written in Latin and ajami script.
- **Islamic school** (*Maliki*) — Sunni Muslim school to which the Fulani overwhelmingly adhere.
- **Sufi order** (*Tijaniyya*) — Sufi order to which most Senegalese Fulani belong.
- **Clerical movement** (*torodbe*) — Eighteenth-century Fouta Toro movement of Islamic clerics.
- **Nineteenth-century religious war** (*jihad of El Hadj Umar Tall*) — Launched by a Toucouleur of Halwar, issuing from the thoroughly Islamised Fouta Toro region.
