## Overview

The Fulani are the pastoralist and semi-pastoralist people who span the widest ethnic belt in Africa, from Senegal to Sudan. In Senegal, they divide between settled, Islamic scholars called Toucouleur in the Senegal River valley and transhumant herders called Peul of the Ferlo scrublands, though both speak Pulaar and share Fulani identity. They are celebrated across West Africa for their material culture: intricately woven textiles, carved calabashes, and gold jewelry. Their society has been Muslim for centuries, with deep roots in the eleventh-century kingdom of Takrur and the nineteenth-century state of El Hadj Umar Tall.

## Material culture

Fulani material culture is founded on portable, functional beauty—textiles commissioned from caste weavers, carved calabashes that serve as dowry and dairy, and gold jewelry that marks status and occasions.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Fulani do not weave themselves but commission complex narrow-strip weavings from endogamous weaver castes, producing some of the Sahel's finest textiles.

- **Khasa** — A thick wool blanket woven from local sheep fleece on narrow ground-looms, dyed indigo and cream in bold bands and lozenges, made from ten to twelve sewn strips and used as a winter wrap and wedding gift.
- **Arkilla kerka** — A great wedding curtain up to six metres long, woven in strips and hung around the bridal bed, its geometric register of hooks, chevrons, and lozenges read by initiated women as a narrative of marriage and fertility.
- **Peul indigo cloth** (*gara*) — Narrow-strip cotton dyed in fermented indigo vats, sometimes resist-stitched before dyeing, produced by Soninke and Tukulor dyers and worn as head wraps and pagnes.
- **Fulani embroidery** (*mbubb*) — Dense chain-stitched and couched embroidery in silk or cotton on the yoke, chest, and cuffs of men's boubous and on cap crowns, using arabesques, eight-pointed stars, and Islamic protective motifs.
- **Leather goatskin work** (*talkuru*) — Cured and dyed goat and sheep hides worked by the Garanke leather caste into cushions, saddle covers, amulet cases, sandals, and fringed tooled sheaths for milk calabashes.

### Clothing & dress

Toucouleur men wear indigo or white boubous with embroidered chest yokes and caps; pastoral Peul herders wear shorter tunics with leather belts and the distinctive wide conical straw hat; women layer indigo wraps, heavy necklaces, and ankle rings.

- **Mbubb** — The wide-sleeved boubou worn over matching trousers and a long undertunic, with an embroidered chest yoke, the signature garment of settled Toucouleur men.
- **Fulani hat** (*tengaade*) — A wide conical straw hat with leather strapping, sometimes fringed with talismans, whose four cardinal peaks reference the herder's orientation across the bush.
- **Bridal dress ensemble** — In Fuuta Tooro, indigo, white, and gold-embroidered boubous worn with layered gold and amber jewelry from the bride's trousseau.
- **Tchoodi** — Tattooed indigo gum-line used as a beauty mark by Senegalese Fulani women.

### Architecture

Pastoral Fulani live in portable dome huts arranged around a thorn-branch corral; settled Fulani in Fuuta Tooro live in rectangular mud-brick houses around swept courtyards, much like their Wolof and Soninke neighbours.

- **Suudu hudo** — A demountable dome hut built by women from a frame of bent millet stalks or acacia withies lashed into a hemisphere and thatched with grass mats.
- **Wuro** — The pastoral Peul camp, consisting of a cluster of dome huts arranged around a thorn-branch corral into which the herd is driven at night.
- **Hoggo** — The thorn-branch corral at the centre of the pastoral camp where the herd is secured at night.
- **Sardo** — The wooden rack on which the calabash stack occupies the place of honour opposite the entrance in pastoral dwellings.
- **Mbaar** — A reception platform under a millet-stalk sunshade in settled houses, used for receiving guests in the swept sand courtyard.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The defining object of the Fulani interior is the milk calabash, incised with geometric motifs and stacked by married women as dowry, working dairy, and status marker; other objects are carved wood and forged metal from specialist castes.

- **Milk calabash** (*horde*) — A hemispherical bowl cut from gourd, cured, and incised or pyrographed with geometric registers of triangles, herringbone, and dot-in-circle motifs; a married woman's stack is her dowry and index of social standing.
- **Leeso** — A carved wooden bed with turned posts, part of the pastoral household's inventory.
- **Wooden churns and ladles** — Long-handled ladles, milking bowls, and churns complete the pastoral dairy inventory.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Sahelian Fulani jewelry is among Africa's most recognizable, centred on the twisted gold hoop earring transferred by red cord to the crown of the head, paired with layered necklaces of amber, carnelian, and coral.

- **Kwottenai kanye** — The twisted, hollow, hoop-shaped gold earring so heavy that its weight is transferred from the earlobe to the crown of the head by a red woollen cord; smaller silver and gilded-copper versions are made for daily wear.
- **Hirsi** — Cast silver amulet boxes holding folded Qur'anic verses, hung on the chest.
- **Braided coiffures** — Elaborate crested and braided hairstyles with silver and amber attachments, coins on either temple, and long thin braids at the sides, marking the classic Halpulaar bridal look.
- **Ankle bracelets** — Cast silver and bronze bracelets that mark married status.
- **Henna** (*fudden*) — Applied to the palms and feet of brides as facial marking and adornment.

## Music & performance

Fulani music divides between pastoral vocal and flute-based repertoire and the caste-professional courtly music of griots; the herding flute is inseparable from the image of the young cowherd.

- **Fulere or serdu** (*fulere*) — A simple end-blown wooden or reed pipe played by young herders, its plaintive descending phrases held to communicate with the herd.
- **Hoddu** — A four- or five-string plucked lute with a hemispherical calabash body covered in cowhide, used by women at pounding and weddings and by professional griots.
- **Nyanyeru or riti** (*riti*) — A one-string bowed spike-fiddle used to accompany praise-song.
- **Gawlo** — The professional bardic griot who recites genealogies, praise-poems for cattle and warrior ancestors, and long narrative fantang pieces at weddings and naming ceremonies.
- **Pekaan** — The sung poetry of the subalɓe fisherman caste of the Senegal River, alternating solo verse and choral refrain and dedicated to river spirits and exploits.

## Dance & theatre

Formal Fulani dance is comparatively restrained, particularly among settled Toucouleur; pastoral Peul contribute line dances and the famous geerewol beauty contest of the eastern Wodaabe branch.

- **Yaake** — A pastoral dance tradition of the Fulani.
- **Geerewol** — The famous male beauty contest of the Wodaabe Fulɓe of Niger and Chad, in which young men in ochre makeup and ostrich-plume headdresses line up before female judges at the end of the rainy season.
- **Pastoral men's line dances** — Swaying in unison with linked arms, raised staffs, and high-pitched ululation, seen at ngenndi seasonal gatherings and weddings among the Ferlo Peul.

## Festivals & rituals

The Islamic calendar structures the public year with Korité, Tabaski, Gamou, and ziyara pilgrimages; life-cycle rituals centre on naming ceremonies, marriage cycles, and seasonal ngenndi gatherings.

- **Korité** — Eid al-Fitr, celebrated at the end of Ramadan.
- **Tabaski** — Eid al-Adha, celebrated with mass sacrifice of rams.
- **Denabo** — The naming ceremony held on the seventh day after birth, when the child's head is shaved, a ram is slaughtered, and the griot recites ancestral genealogies.
- **Ngenndi** — The seasonal reunion at the end of the rains, when scattered lineages converge for weddings, cattle exchanges, and courtship dances.
- **Pulaaku** — The code of virtues comprising semteende (reserve, modesty), munyal (patience), hakkille (foresight), and sagata (courage), enacted at all gatherings and taught to children as the ethical armature of Fulani identity.

## Foodways

Fulani cuisine is founded on milk—fresh, soured, cultured, and clarified—which accompanies nearly every meal and is traded daily with settled cultivators for grain.

- **Latchiri or nyiiri** (*latchiri*) — A stiff porridge of pounded millet or sorghum, the staple grain dish eaten with a leaf sauce of baobab, sorrel, or okra.
- **Thiakry** — Steamed millet couscous granules folded into sweetened soured milk, the celebratory dish served at namings, weddings, and Korité.
- **Kosam** — Fresh cow's milk, a staple of the pastoral diet.
- **Nebbam nay** — Clarified butter that accompanies nearly every meal.
- **Ataaya** — Green tea brewed strong and sweet in three successive rounds, in the Sahelian ceremony shared with Moors and Tuareg.

## Oral tradition & literature

Fulani oral literature is unusually rich, sustained by the Toucouleur clerical tradition of Ajami manuscripts and by the gawlo bardic caste's enormous corpus of genealogy, praise-poems, and epic.

- **Silâmaka and Poullôri** — The celebrated epic of the Massina Fulɓe, a hero-tale of resistance to Bambara overlordship.
- **Jammoore** — Long alliterative praise-poems for individual named cattle, learned by young herders as a mark of accomplishment.
- **Mergi** — Riddles and proverbs recited on winter evenings.
- **Jamu** — Genealogies recited by griots at naming ceremonies and gatherings.
- **Ajami manuscript tradition** (*Ajami*) — Pulaar written in Arabic script, still practised in Qur'anic schools and producing devotional poetry, historical chronicles, and pedagogical texts.

## Language & religion

Pulaar is an Atlantic branch Niger-Congo language mutually intelligible across West Africa, now written in Latin, Arabic script (Ajami), and the indigenous Adlam alphabet; virtually all Senegalese Fulani are Sunni Muslims of the Maliki school, mostly Tijaniyya Sufi order.

- **Pulaar** — The Fulani language of Senegal, notable for an elaborate noun-class system with over twenty classes.
- **Adlam** — An indigenous alphabet devised in Guinea in the 1980s for writing Pulaar, now included in Unicode.
- **Tijaniyya** — A Sufi order to which the great majority of Senegalese Fulani belong, historically propagated in the region by El Hadj Umar Tall.
- **Duɗal** — Qur'anic schools that practise the Ajami manuscript tradition.

## Glossary

- *Fulɓe* — Plural of Pullo, the Fulani people
- *Toucouleur* — Settled, Islamically learned Fulani of Fuuta Tooro
- *Fuuta Tooro* — The middle Senegal River valley, heartland of settled Fulani
- *Pulaar* — The Fulani language of Senegal
- *Khasa* — Indigo and cream wool blanket, thick and heavy
- *Arkilla kerka* — Great wedding curtain hung around the bridal bed
- *Horde* — Milk calabash, hemispherical and incised
- *Kwottenai kanye* — Twisted gold hoop earring of the bride
- *Pulaaku* — Code of virtues: reserve, patience, foresight, courage
- *Gawlo* — Professional bardic griot
- *Hoddu* — Four- or five-string plucked lute with calabash body
- *Ngenndi* — Seasonal gathering of scattered lineages at rainy-season end
- *Mbubb* — Wide-sleeved boubou, main garment of Toucouleur men
- *Tengaade* — Wound turban or wide conical straw hat
- *Wuro* — Pastoral Peul camp
- *Tijaniyya* — Sufi order followed by most Senegalese Fulani
- *Takrur* — Eleventh-century kingdom of Fuuta Tooro
