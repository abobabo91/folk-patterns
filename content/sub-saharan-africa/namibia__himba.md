## Overview

The Himba are semi-nomadic cattle herders living in northwestern Namibia and southern Angola. They speak OtjiHimba and are culturally distinct from the neighboring Herero despite linguistic kinship. Almost all their material culture—dress, jewelry, houses, and cosmetics—is still made and maintained within the homestead. Their appearance, dress, and body adornment serve as a precise notation of age, marriage, and family ties.

## Material culture

Himba material culture is built from skin, ochre, iron, copper, shell, and beads rather than woven cloth, with red ochre treatment (otjize) as the signature element.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Himba pattern work is arithmetic and positional, applied to hide, beads, iron and the body itself rather than woven into cloth.

- **Himba leather (skin dress)** — Women's skirts and men's loin garments cut from calfskin and sheepskin, softened and dressed with fat, then rubbed with red ochre so the hide matches the wearer's skin tone.
- **Red ochre treatment** (*otjize*) — Butterfat creamed with ground red ochre, often scented with omuzumba resin, applied to skin, hair and leather to cleanse, screen sun, deter insects, and express the Himba aesthetic ideal of earth and life.
- **Married woman's headpiece** (*erembe*) — A sheepskin sculpture worn from about a year into marriage or after a first child's birth, with braided, ochre-loaded hair streams falling from it, marking a change in status.
- **Older hide headdress** (*ekori*) — A three-lobed leather headdress, often iron-studded, of the older Herero-Himba tradition still in use among Himba and Zemba.
- **Shell pendant** (*ohumba*) — A conus shell disc traded inland from the Atlantic coast, worn as a pectoral on hide or beaded strap as a wealth object showing trade ties and status.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress is short and skin-based, suited to hot semi-arid climate, with status and age marked through hair arrangement and beaded ornament rather than occasion-specific costumes.

- **Women's daily dress** — A pleated hide skirt, small beaded back-piece (if mother), heavy iron and bead anklets, arm rings, and the erembe once married.
- **Men's daily dress** — A hide wrap skirt and increasingly modern textiles bought in town; sandals cut from old car tyres.
- **Hair as status marker** — Boys keep a single rear plait from puberty; married men cover unbraided hair with a cap; widowers expose it uncovered; girls wear many ochre-textured plaits, some arranged to veil the face; married women's plaits hang from the erembe.
- **Girls' beaded apron** — Small front aprons and hide backpacks for women who have given birth, built from iron and copper beads, glass trade beads, ostrich eggshell discs and leather thong in stacked bands.
- **Teaching doll** (*Himba doll*) — Small hide-and-bead figures dressed in miniature skirts, erembe and beadwork, made for girls to learn the dress code before wearing it themselves.

### Architecture

The homestead is a cosmologically organized circular hamlet centered on the ancestral fire and sacred cattle kraal, where huts are conical frames of mopane wood plastered with clay and dung.

- **Homestead** (*onganda*) — A circular hamlet of one extended family with huts and work shelters enclosing the ancestral fire and the sacred cattle kraal.
- **Sacred ancestral fire** (*okuruwo*) — The fire kept by a designated fire-keeper, positioned between the headman's hut and the kraal gate, the ritual center addressing the dead.
- **Hut structure** — Conical or beehive frames of mopane wood plastered by women and girls with red clay soil and cattle dung as binder, renewed as it weathers.
- **Seasonal stock posts** — Lighter secondary enclosures built to follow pasture use, abandoned seasonally as families move with rainy-season and rested dry-season grazing.
- **Structural decoration** — Ornament comes from the stockade of upright mopane, the swept plaza, and the kraal fence itself rather than applied surface decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The defining vessel is the calabash gourd tended on vines to supply soured milk, the household's central utensil; metalwork is cold-worked and recycled from imported wire.

- **Soured-milk gourd** (*calabash*) — Gourds grown on vines tended by women to guarantee supply for souring milk, the central food and the defining household vessel.
- **Wooden vessels and tools** — Milk pails, stirring sticks, headrests, stools and mortars carved from mopane and other hardwoods.
- **Cold-worked metalwork** — Iron and copper wire drawn into arm, leg and neck rings, beaten sheet and nail-heads for hide ornament, with imported wire long replacing smelted stock.
- **Leatherworking tools** — Awls and hide scrapers are household equipment maintained by women, the artisans for all handicraft, clothing and jewelry.
- **Fire-keeper's ritual paraphernalia** — Fire-making equipment and objects at the okuruwo are ritual rather than domestic items, maintaining the connection between living and ancestors.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment is dense, heavy and worn permanently rather than occasionally, with coiled iron and copper rings, beaded anklets, and plaits colored with red ochre as the defining elements.

- **Coiled arm and leg rings** — Stacked coiled iron and copper rings worn permanently, often in multiple strands ascending the limb in graded diameters.
- **Beaded neck ornaments** — Multi-strand necklaces of glass beads, ostrich eggshell discs and metal, with the ohumba conus shell hanging at the chest as prestige pendant.
- **Ochre-colored plaits** — Hair weighted and colored with otjize, mounted from the erembe, the entire surface treated daily with red ochre as permanent adornment.
- **Broad beaded anklets** — Heavy iron and bead anklets stacked on the leg in bands, marking status and worn throughout life.
- **Circumcision as body modification** — Boys are circumcised before puberty as a rite of passage; body modification here is ritual rather than decorative.

## Music & performance

Himba musical life centers on the homestead with group singing, hand-clapping, stamped rhythm, solo herding songs to individual cattle, and night dancing at the plaza.

- **Homestead group singing** — Unaccompanied or lightly accompanied group singing with hand-clapping and stamped rhythm performed in the homestead.
- **Praise and herding songs** — Solo songs addressed to individual cattle, sung while herding or at homestead gatherings.
- **Night dancing** — Dancing at the swept center of the onganda with clapped rhythm and individual dancers stepping forward in turn from the circle.

## Dance & theatre

Dancing takes place in the homestead plaza in a clapped circle and is tied to homestead sociality and life-cycle occasions; there is no masking, puppet or staged theatre tradition.

- **Clapped circle dance** — Dancers step forward singly from a clapped circle in the swept center of the homestead, tied to social gatherings and life-cycle occasions.
- **Status performance through appearance** — A strongly developed performance of social position through dress, jewelry and hair arrangement rather than through dramatic action.
- **Modern tourist staging** — Himba dance has been staged for visitors and foreign television as a contested modern development, publicly condemned by Epupa chief Kapika in 2013 for mocking Himba culture.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual year is governed by rainfall and pasture rather than a calendar of dated festivals; recurring rites include fire-keeper's calls to ancestors every seven to eight days, and life-cycle rites carry the most weight.

- **Fire-keeper's weekly address** — Every seven to eight days the fire-keeper approaches the okuruwo to address Mukuru and the ancestors on behalf of the family.
- **Circumcision** — Boys are circumcised before puberty as a rite of passage marking entry into manhood.
- **Marriage arrangement and bridewealth** — Girls are betrothed by their fathers from puberty onset; bridewealth is negotiated in cattle between families, with an ox the standard offer; marriage is polygynous.
- **Childbirth as status marker** — A girl becomes a fully-fledged woman only when she bears her first child, marked by wearing the small beaded back-piece and the erembe.
- **Death and inheritance** — The community evaluates care owed to the bereaved; inheritance of cattle and water-rights passes through the mother's matriclan, not the father's patriclan.

## Foodways

The Himba count wealth in cattle and eat mostly milk; the staple pairing is soured milk with maize porridge, with meat tied to ritual or occasional slaughter.

- **Soured milk and porridge** (*oruhere ruomaere*) — The staple pairing of soured milk with maize porridge, and in seasons of shortage, plain stiff porridge alone.
- **Milk production** — Cows and goats are milked by women and girls, with the soured-milk calabash kept in continuous production as the central food.
- **Livestock herds** — Fat-tailed sheep and goats bred alongside cattle supply both milk and meat; a sacred herd kept in the kraal beside the ancestral fire is not eaten casually.
- **Grain cultivation** — Rain-fed maize and millet grown at the base homestead and ground for porridge; cornmeal bought in town now supplements it.
- **Occasional meat eating** — Meat eating is tied to slaughter, men's work largely ritual or occasional; livestock are sold for cash only opportunistically.

## Oral tradition & literature

OtjiHimba is unwritten; its verbal art includes genealogical recitation, cattle praise names, and narrative about misfortune, drought and war, transmitted in the homestead.

- **Genealogical recitation** — Tracing of both the patriclans and the matraclans from memory, the practical instrument of bilateral descent and essential for inheritance and marriage.
- **Cattle praise naming** — Individual cattle are named and praised in recitation, a central verbal art form.
- **Narrative memory** — Stories about omiti (malicious medicine) and droughts and wars structure recent memory, including the 1980s drought that killed 90% of Himba livestock.
- **Crandall ethnographic documentation** — David P. Crandall's year-long residence among cattle-herders produced the best documentation of Himba life and speech.
- **Sherman photographic and film archive** (*The Ovahimba Years*) — Rina Sherman's seven-year record of film, photography and sound, the closest thing to an archive of Himba speech and performance.

## Language & religion

OtjiHimba is an unwritten Bantu dialect; the OvaHimba are monotheists who worship Mukuru and clan ancestors, with the ancestral fire as the ritual center.

- **OtjiHimba language** (*OtjiHimba*) — An unwritten dialect of Herero within Bantu-Niger–Congo, spoken across the Kunene on both sides of the Namibia–Angola border.
- **Supreme being** (*Mukuru*) — The distant monotheistic god who only blesses; ancestors commonly act as his representatives in addressing the living.
- **Clan ancestors** — Ancestors who both bless and curse, mediated through the family's sacred ancestral fire.
- **Malicious medicine or witchcraft** (*omiti*) — Belief in omiti accounts for death and illness turning fatal; diviner-healers are consulted to identify its source.
- **Color terms** (*zuzu, vapa, buru, dambu*) — Four Himba color terms partition the visible spectrum along lines cutting across English categories; a linguistic curiosity studied repeatedly.
