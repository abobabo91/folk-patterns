## Overview

The Kuba (*Bakuba*, "people of the throwing knife") are a cluster of some eighteen related Bantu-speaking groups of the southern savanna-forest ecotone of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, occupying the country between the Kasai, Sankuru and Lulua rivers in what is administratively Kasai Province. The confederation is dominated politically and demographically by the Bushoong (*Bushong*), whose paramount ruler — the *nyim* — has since the seventeenth century presided over a loose federation that also includes the Ngeende, Ngongo, Kete, Kel, Pyaang, Bulaang, Bieeng, Shoowa (Shoowa), Bokila and others. Total Kuba population is commonly estimated at around 250,000 to 400,000, though census data for the interior Kasai remains patchy. Their languages belong to the Bantu C-zone (Bushoong being the prestige dialect), and their oral chronicles trace a migration southward from the confluence of the Sankuru and the Kasai under the culture-hero king *Shyaam a-Mbul a Ngoong*, credited around 1625 with importing maize, cassava, tobacco and — crucially — the raphia weaving and embroidery techniques for which the confederation is now globally famous. In folk-culture terms the Kuba matter as one of the great courtly art traditions of Central Africa: the embroidered *ntshak* raphia panels ("Kasai velvet") that furnish museum collections from Tervuren to New York, the royal *ndop* portrait statues of successive *nyim*, the triad of court masks (*mwaash a mbooy*, *ngady a mwaash*, *bwoom*) that dramatise the founding myth, and a decorated material world — cups, boxes, headrests, pipes — saturated with the interlace patterns that Bushoong artists name and reason about as a formal system.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Raphia cloth** (*mbal*, *mbala*) — the base fabric of Kuba textile art, woven by men on a single-heddle inclined loom from the split fibres of the young leaves of the raphia palm (*Raphia vinifera*). The undyed panel is roughly a metre square, with a slightly stiff, linen-like hand; it is then pounded in a mortar to soften it before further work.

**Ntshak** — the long overskirt wrapper, sometimes exceeding eight metres, made of raphia panels sewn edge to edge and bordered with appliqué, embroidery and tufted work. Worn wrapped around the waist by both sexes for dance and funerals.

**Cut-pile embroidery / "Kasai velvet"** (*bushoong* type, most closely associated with the Shoowa) — the technique for which the Kuba are internationally known. Women pull dyed raphia threads through the woven ground with a needle, cut the loop with a small knife flush to the surface, and repeat until a dense plush pile builds up in geometric fields. The resulting surface has the softness and depth of velvet, hence the nineteenth-century European trade name.

**Appliqué and openwork** — used especially on funerary ntshak and on the men's dance skirts, with cut-out silhouettes of dyed raphia laid over a contrasting ground and secured with running stitch, and with deliberate irregular tears patched with contrasting appliqué in the *mapel* manner.

**Shoowa panels** — smaller squares (roughly 50–60 cm) produced by Shoowa women as prestige gifts, tribute and grave goods, worked entirely in cut-pile and stem stitch; these are the pieces most often framed as art abroad.

**Motif vocabulary.** Kuba pattern is a named, discursive system. Bushoong weavers and embroiderers distinguish and name dozens of interlace and lozenge motifs, including *imbol* (the interlace knot), *woot* (after the founding ancestor Woot), *mikope ngoma* (drum of chief Mikope), *bikwaam* (crocodile back), *nnaam* (lightning zigzag), and various *muyingu* diamond-lattice grids. Motifs are combined, deliberately broken, and shifted in mid-panel — the celebrated Kuba "syncopation" that so interested twentieth-century designers and mathematicians.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress in the Kasai villages is now largely industrial cotton wax-print, but the ceremonial wardrobe remains raphia. Men appear at court and at funerals in a long *ntshak* wrapper cinched with a beaded belt, a bare chest crossed by shoulder cords of cowrie and glass beads, and, for titled elders, a conical fibre or beaded cap (*laket*) bristling with feathers. The paramount *nyim* wears the *bwaantshy*, a ceremonial regalia of astonishing density — an embroidered raphia skirt, a beaded and cowrie-encrusted tunic, a beaded belt with a leopard-tooth apron, and the *mukyeem* helmet-mask reserved to the king, sheathed in cowries and beads and crowned with an elephant trunk and tusks in beadwork. Women wear a shorter *ntshak* overskirt above a plain raphia underskirt, with a broad woven or beaded belt and, in some communities, a small beaded *cache-sexe* apron for initiation dances. Both sexes historically wore an embroidered raphia sash across the torso for age-grade and title ceremonies. Body painting in red *tukula* (camwood powder mixed with palm oil) and white kaolin marks initiation and funerary states.

### Architecture

Kuba villages are laid out on a rigorous rectilinear grid — an unusual layout in the equatorial forest belt, and one Kuba oral history attributes to king Shyaam. Houses are rectangular, with walls of woven raphia mats or split-palm laths lashed to a timber frame, and a steep pitched roof thatched in palm leaves. The palace complex of the *nyim* at Nsheng (Mushenge) is a walled compound of such houses organised around ceremonial courtyards, with the walls of the principal reception houses covered in woven geometric matwork whose patterns echo those of the textiles — the same *imbol* and *muyingu* motifs rendered in dyed and natural palm strip rather than in embroidered raphia. Doorways of chiefly houses are framed by carved posts, and the raphia wall panels are periodically renewed as tribute obligation by particular villages. There is no monumental stone tradition; the Kuba built for a wet climate and rotated their settlements, so the visual monumentality of the culture is invested in the palace matwork and in portable art rather than in permanent structures.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Kuba visual life is unusually dense with decorated small objects, most of them associated with palm-wine drinking, tobacco, and cosmetics. Carved wooden **palm-wine cups** (*mbwoongntey*) take the form of human heads, cupped hands, or double-bodied janus figures, their surfaces incised with the same interlace motifs as the cloth. **Cosmetic boxes** (*ngedi mu ntey*) — often crescent- or box-shaped with sliding lids — hold red *tukula* paste. **Headrests** (*mutshaam*) with caryatid supports, carved **pipes** with long stems, divination instruments including the friction oracle *itoom* (also called *itombwa*, a small carved animal rubbed with a disc to give yes/no answers), and drums with figurated bases are worked in the same idiom. Ironwork was historically the province of specialist smiths who forged the *shongo* — the multi-bladed throwing knife whose silhouette gave the confederation its exonym Bakuba — along with ceremonial swords and hoes. Ceramics exist but are workaday: undecorated earthenware cooking pots and water jars, produced by women, without the elaboration seen in textiles or wood. Basketry in dyed raphia and palm produces lidded storage baskets and the fine trays used to sort camwood and kaolin.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Kuba body adornment is dominated by beadwork and cowries rather than by metal. The king's *bwaantshy* regalia is estimated to include tens of thousands of cowrie shells and imported glass beads sewn onto raphia and leather grounds; titled dignitaries wear scaled-down versions with beaded collars, cross-body baldrics, wristlets and anklets. Copper and brass wristlets and anklets are worn by titled women, and iron neck-rings by some age grades. Scarification (*ngingi*) in raised keloid patterns on the temples, chest and back, echoing textile motifs, was common into the mid-twentieth century and persists in attenuated form. Teeth were traditionally filed to points as a mark of adulthood. Hair is dressed into elaborate coiffures held with carved combs and, for women of rank, mounted over a fibre armature to produce the high crest visible in early twentieth-century photographs. The *laket* and *mukyeem* caps of titled men, and the beaded diadems of chiefly women, complete a hierarchy in which the density of beadwork visibly indexes rank.

## Music & performance

Kuba music is a court and village tradition centred on drums, slit-gongs and voice. The royal drum ensemble at Nsheng includes the great *pelambish* signal drum, the double-headed *ngoma*, and the wooden slit-gong *lokombe* (also *nkumvi*), whose tonal language carries formal announcements between villages. Xylophones (*madimba*) with gourd resonators are played by professional entertainers, and the *lokombe* accompanies praise-singing of the *nyim* and the recitation of the king-list. Vocal music includes antiphonal work songs at the raphia loom and at the pounding mortar, funerary laments led by senior women, and the praise songs of the court griots who chant the eighty-odd names in the royal genealogy from Woot down to the reigning *nyim*. Whistles, small hand drums and rattles accompany the masked dances. There is no Kuba entry on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity as of this writing; the closest regional inscription is the Congolese *rumba*, which belongs to a very different urban milieu.

## Dance & theatre

The great theatrical form of the Kuba is the masked drama enacting the origin myth of the royal house, staged at investitures, at the *nyim*'s funeral, and at boys' initiation (*nkaan*). Three masks dominate this cycle. *Mwaash a mbooy* (also *mwaash amboy*, *moshambwooy*) represents Woot, the founding ancestor and first king, sheathed in leopard skin, cowries and beads with a raphia beard and a trunk-like projection. *Ngady a mwaash* (or *ngaady a mwaash*) is Woot's sister-consort, painted with alternating triangles of black, white and red across the face and bordered with beads and cowries, her diagonal cheek lines commonly read as the tears of the incest at the origin of the royal line. *Bwoom* — the third mask — is a broad, bulging-browed helmet mask variously identified as a commoner, a pygmy, or a rival prince, whose interventions in the dance stage the political tension between king and people. The dances are slow, weighted, and heavily costumed, with the masker's body entirely hidden under raphia skirts, leopard skins and beaded panels; small bells at the ankle mark the step. Boys' initiation (*nkaan*) involves a period of seclusion in a bush camp, tuition in patterns and proverbs, and emergence with new adult names and scarification.

## Festivals & rituals

The Kuba ritual calendar is oriented less to a solar cycle than to the biographies of the *nyim* and the age-grade progressions of the villages. The installation of a new king at Nsheng — a rare event, since reigns are long — is the great pan-Kuba occasion, drawing tribute delegations from all subordinate chiefdoms and requiring the commissioning of a fresh set of regalia, mats and masks. Royal funerals are equally elaborate and involve the production of large numbers of ntshak panels as grave furniture; the king is buried with a substantial part of the palace textile store, and villages are levied for replacements. The commissioning of an *ndop* — the seated portrait statue of a reigning *nyim*, carved during his lifetime with an identifying emblem (*ibol*) at the base — belongs to the same complex; some two dozen such statues survive, mostly in museum collections, and constitute one of the few sub-Saharan African portrait sculpture traditions. Boys' initiation (*nkaan*) recurs at village level every few years. Divination, healing, and the propitiation of nature spirits (*ngesh*) are handled by specialist practitioners outside the court structure.

## Foodways

Kuba foodways are those of the southern savanna-forest edge. The staples are cassava (*mangbwadi*) prepared as a stiff *fufu*-type paste (*bidia*), maize, plantain and cocoyam, with palm oil as the principal fat and palm wine (*malafu*) as the ritual and social drink par excellence — hence the extraordinary elaboration of the carved drinking cup. Bushmeat from the forest edge, river fish, snails and caterpillars supply protein; goats and chickens are kept and eaten at ceremonies rather than daily. Groundnuts, beans, okra, bitter-leaf greens and forest mushrooms round out the sauces that accompany the starch. Salt was historically produced by the leaching of plant ash. Tobacco, imported by Shyaam according to tradition, is smoked in long-stemmed carved pipes as a social and ritual act rather than a solitary habit. Food is not itself a heavily decorated domain — the aesthetic energy of Kuba culture is invested overwhelmingly in the container and the setting rather than in the dish.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kuba oral literature is dominated by the royal chronicle — the recited genealogy of the *nyim* from Woot through Shyaam a-Mbul a Ngoong to the present incumbent — memorised by court historians and performed at investitures and funerals. Around this spine cluster origin myths of the confederation, tales of the founding of each subordinate chiefdom, and a rich body of proverbs (*miningo*), riddles, and moral tales featuring the trickster and the animals of the forest. Pattern itself functions as a mnemonic and rhetorical system: the named interlace motifs on cloth, mat and cup are read as visual proverbs, and Kuba informants have famously been able to name and discuss patterns as a discursive vocabulary. The Kuba do not appear to have developed a mnemonic device precisely analogous to the Luba *lukasa* memory-board (which belongs to the neighbouring Luba further east), but the palace matwork, the *ndop* statues with their identifying *ibol* emblems, and the recited king-list together perform a comparable historiographical function. There is no pre-colonial script; missionary and colonial-era transcriptions from the late nineteenth century onward, notably the work of Emil Torday for the British Museum in 1907–09 and later of Jan Vansina, constitute the first written corpus.

## Language & religion

The Kuba speak closely related Bantu languages of Guthrie's C-zone, with Bushoong serving as the prestige court language and lingua franca of the confederation; Ngeende, Kete, Shoowa and the others are mutually intelligible to varying degrees. French is the language of the state and of schooling, Lingala the vehicular language of the wider Congo, and Tshiluba the regional lingua franca of the Kasai. Traditional religion centres on a distant creator god (*Nyeeem* or *Mboom*), the ancestors — above all Woot and the line of *nyim* — and a population of nature spirits (*ngesh*) associated with rivers, forest groves and particular villages, propitiated by specialist mediums. Christianity, introduced by Belgian Catholic missions from the late nineteenth century and by Protestant missions shortly after, is now the majority affiliation, with Catholic and Kimbanguist congregations dominant; ancestral and royal ritual continues in parallel with Christian practice rather than being displaced by it.
