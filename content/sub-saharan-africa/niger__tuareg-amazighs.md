## Overview

The Tuareg people are an Amazigh ethnic group and traditionally nomadic pastoralists who principally inhabit the Sahara, extending from southwestern Libya through southern Algeria, Niger, Mali, Burkina Faso, and northern Nigeria. Smaller communities in Chad and Sudan are known as the Kinnin. Their population is estimated at 4 to 5 million worldwide, while their combined population in the listed territories exceeds 2.5 million. The Tuareg speak languages belonging to the Berber branch of the Afroasiatic family, including Tamasheq, Tamahaq, Tamajeq, and Tawellemmet. They are semi-nomadic, mostly practice Islam, and have controlled trans-Saharan trade routes. Their folk-cultural significance is particularly visible in their social organization, oral traditions, clothing, writing system, pastoral life, and artisanal castes.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Tagelmust** — The *tagelmust* is a garment traditionally worn by Tuareg men. Its indigo color sometimes stains the skin underneath, producing the blueish tint associated with the English exonym “Blue People.”

**Tifinagh** — *Tifinagh* is the Tuareg’s traditional Libyco-Berber writing script. Vestiges of an inscription in Tifinagh have been found on the wall of the ancient Tin Hinan sepulchre.

### Clothing & dress

Tuareg men traditionally wear the *tagelmust*, a veil whose indigo color can stain the skin and produce a blueish tint. The term *Kel Tagelmust* means “veiled people” in reference to this garment. The sources also state that the Tuareg term for a woman is *Tamajaq*, with the variants Tamasheq, Tamahaq, and Timajaghen, but these terms refer to people rather than garments.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Tuareg artisans belong to endogamous castes known as the *Inhăḍăn*, also called Inadan. These artisans have included blacksmiths, jewelers, wood workers, and leather artisans. They produced and repaired saddles, tools, household items, and other objects for the Tuareg community. In Niger and Mali, artisan castes were attached as clients to noble or vassal families and carried messages for their patrons.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources identify Tuareg jewelers among the artisan castes. They state that artisan castes included blacksmiths, jewelers, wood workers, and leather artisans, and that these groups were endogamous.

## Music & performance

Tuareg artisanal castes have included singers, musicians, and storytellers who preserved oral traditions. They are called *Agguta* by the Tuareg and have been called upon to sing during ceremonies such as weddings and funerals. They state that songs and poetry also reflected recognition gained through successful *rezzous*, or raids, in the pre-colonial period. The sources further record that Tuareg religious scholars propagated Islam in North Africa and the Sahel between the 7th and 17th centuries.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that Tuareg religious ceremonies contain allusions to matrilineal spirits, fertility, menstruation, the earth, and ancestresses. They also describe a widespread “cult of the dead,” understood as a form of ancestor veneration, and note that elements of pre-Islamic cosmology and rituals continue within Tuareg culture and tradition.

Islamic festivals are mentioned in connection with the artisan castes: in Niger and Mali, artisan clients traditionally sacrificed animals during Islamic festivals.

## Foodways

They state only that Tuareg caste groups differ in food and eating behaviors. One explanation attributed to a smith in Niger compares nobles to rice, smiths to millet, and slaves to corn.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tuareg oral tradition includes songs, poetry, singing, music, and storytelling. The *Agguta* are singers, musicians, and storytellers who have maintained oral traditions and may be called upon during weddings and funerals. Songs and poetry also recorded the prestige associated with successful *rezzous*, especially when a raid achieved a large haul or relied on cunning. They state that oral tradition connects the Tuareg clans to a common mother and preserves accounts of their social origins. The sources also mention the Tuareg’s traditional Libyco-Berber writing script, Tifinagh.

## Language & religion

Tuareg languages include Tamasheq, Tamahaq, Tamajeq, and Tawellemmet, which belong to the Berber branch of the Afroasiatic family. The languages are also known as Tamasheq, Tamajeq, or Tamahaq according to dialect. The Tuareg’s traditional Libyco-Berber script is Tifinagh. The term *Kel Tamasheq* means “speakers of Tamasheq,” while *Kel Tagelmust* means “veiled people.”

The Tuareg mostly practice Islam and primarily follow the Maliki school of Sunni Islam. They adopted Islam during the medieval period after its arrival with the Umayyad Caliphate in the 7th century and embraced the Maliki school in the 16th century under the tutelage of El Maghili. Earlier Berber mythology, pre-Islamic cosmology, matrilineal spirits, fertility rites, ancestor veneration, and the “cult of the dead” remain described as elements within Tuareg culture and religious practice.
