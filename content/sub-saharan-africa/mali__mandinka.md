## Overview

The Mandinka, also known as Malinke, are a West African ethnic group primarily found in southern Mali, The Gambia, southern Senegal, and eastern Guinea. They number about 15 million and are the largest subgroup of the Mandé peoples. Mandinka belongs to the Manding branch of the Mande language family, whose languages function as a lingua franca in much of West Africa. Mandinka communities are concentrated in Mali in Kita, Kayes, and Koulikoro, while their largest urban center is Bamako. Most Mandinka live in family-related compounds in traditional rural villages and work as subsistence farmers. Their folk-culture significance is documented through their oral transmission of history and knowledge, griot musical traditions, caste organization, kora performance, rites of passage, and continuing connections with the history of the Mali Empire.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state that marabouts write Qur'anic verses on slips of paper and sew them into leather pouches, which are worn as protective amulets. The sources also mention leatherworkers as part of the enslaved strata in the traditional social structure.

### Architecture

Mandinka villages are traditionally composed of small mud houses with conical thatch or tin roofs. Villages are organized on the basis of clan groups, and most Mandinka live in family-related compounds. The basic social unit is the *lu*, or extended family, led by a family head called a *fa*. A *dugu*, or village, is formed by a collection of *lu* and is led by the *fa* of the most important *lu*, assisted by the *dugu-tigi*, the village head or head of the first family to settle there. A group of *dugu-tigi* forms a *kafu*, or confederation, headed by a *kafu-tigi*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources mention pottery makers, metal smiths, gold artisans, woodworkers, and leatherworkers within Mandinka economic and social life. During the expansion of the Mali Empire, Mandinka gold artisans and metalworking smiths settled along the coast and in the hilly Fouta Djallon and plateau areas of West Africa. They state that women traditionally tend rice plants by hand, while men cultivate peanuts, millet, maize, and other crops and engage in small-scale husbandry.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Mandinka religious practice includes protective amulets made from leather pouches containing slips of paper on which marabouts write Qur'anic verses. They state that female genital mutilation has traditionally been practiced among Mandinka women and is locally called *Niaka*, *Kuyungo*, *Musolula Karoola*, or *Bondo*. These terms refer to a practice involving partial or total removal of the clitoris, or partial or total removal of the labia minora with the clitoris.

## Music & performance

Mandinka music is closely connected with oral history. Stories, songs, and proverbs are transmitted through sung performances by griots, who are known locally as *jalolu*, the plural of *jali*. The sources also use the term *jalibaa* for praise singers. Griots preserve music and literary traditions, and their performances transmit historical knowledge from one generation to the next.

The best-documented Mandinka instrument in the sources is the *kora*, a twenty-one-stringed West African harp. It is made from a halved, dried, hollowed-out gourd covered with cow or goat skin. The strings were traditionally made from a cow’s tendons and are now described as fishing line. Another description states that the kora is made from half a calabash, with cowhide fastened by decorative tacks. Sound holes in its side are used to store coins offered to praise singers in appreciation of their performance. The kora is played to accompany a griot’s singing or by itself and is described as the hallmark of traditional Mandinka musicians.

Mandinka musical traditions also include drumming. The sources identify the *donso*, or hunters, as a guild or brotherhood associated with Mandinka society.

## Dance & theatre

They describe music, sung oral history, griot performance, and spiritual ritual.

## Festivals & rituals

Mandinka life-cycle practice includes the rite of passage called *kuyangwoo*. It marks the beginning of adulthood for children between four and fourteen years of age. Young people are placed in separate groups according to sex and undergo genital mutilation. In earlier years, children spent up to a year in the bush, but the period was later reduced to coincide with physical healing, lasting between three and four weeks. During this time they learn adult social responsibilities and rules of behaviour. Preparation is made in the village or compound for their return, and a celebration marks their return to their families.

Marriage is traditionally arranged by family members. The suitor’s family formally sends kola nuts to the male elders of the prospective bride’s family. If the nuts are accepted, courtship may begin. Polygamy has been practiced since pre-Islamic times, and a Mandinka man may have up to four wives if he can care for each equally. The sources state that wives are expected to live together in harmony and share compound responsibilities such as cooking and laundry.

Mandinka religious practice includes an annual rain ceremony and the “sacrifice of the black bull” to pre-Islamic deities. Mandinka children receive their names on the eighth day after birth, and names are almost always taken from an important person in the family.

## Foodways

Mandinka subsistence farming relies on peanuts, rice, millet, maize, and small-scale husbandry. During the wet season, men plant peanuts as their main cash crop and also grow millet. Women traditionally grow rice, described in the sources as African rice, and tend the plants by hand. This work is characterized as extremely labour-intensive and physically demanding.

Only about 50% of rice consumption needs are met by local planting; the remainder is imported from Asia and the United States. They state that most Mandinka people practice Islam and that many contemporary Mandinka are predominantly Sunni Muslim.

## Oral tradition & literature

Mandinka society is described as oral, with mythologies, history, and knowledge transmitted verbally from one generation to the next. The griots, known locally as *jalolu* or *jali*, preserve music and literary traditions through stories, songs, proverbs, and sung versions of oral history. The passing down of oral history through music is described as one of the most distinctive traits of Mandinka culture.

The history of Mandinka is connected in oral tradition with the Mali Empire and the rule of Sundiata Keita. The sources describe conflicting legends concerning Tiramakhan Traore, including one in which he led a migration because people in Mali had converted to Islam and another in which he had converted and married Muhammad’s granddaughter. These contrasting accounts are presented as evidence of a complex interaction between Islam and Mandinka oral history.

Malian author Massa Makan Diabaté wrote novels referring to Mandinka legends, including *Janjon*, which won the 1971 Grand prix littéraire d’Afrique noire. His novels *The Lieutenant of Kouta*, *The Barber of Kouta*, and *The Butcher of Kouta* address Mandinka proverbs and customs. Alex Haley’s 1976 novel *Roots: The Saga of an American Family* follows family connections to an eighteenth-century ancestor described as a Mandinka man known as Kunta Kinte.

## Language & religion

Mandinka is a Mande language of the Manding branch. It is spoken by the Mandinka people of northern Guinea-Bissau, the Casamance region of Senegal, and The Gambia, where it is one of the principal languages. The language has five vowels and two tones, high and low. The Latin and Arabic alphabets are widely used. The Latin alphabet is official, while the Arabic alphabet is older and more widely used. Mandinka is also written with the N’Ko script, invented in 1949, and the Garay alphabet has seen limited use.

Most Mandinka people practice Islam, and contemporary Mandinka in West Africa are predominantly Sunni Muslim. Some Mandinka syncretise Islam with traditional African religions. In these communities, spirits are believed to be controllable through the power of a marabout, who knows protective formulas. Important decisions may be made only after consulting a marabout. Islamic practice has developed over many centuries, while some pre-Islamic practices, including the annual rain ceremony and sacrifice of the black bull, have continued.
