## Overview

The Zulu (*amaZulu*) are the largest ethnic group in South Africa, numbering roughly eleven to twelve million and concentrated in KwaZulu-Natal — the humid coastal belt and thornveld between the Indian Ocean and the Drakensberg escarpment, drained by the Thukela, Mfolozi and Mkhuze rivers, with the royal centres at Nongoma and Ulundi and a large urban population in Durban and Pietermaritzburg. Their language, *isiZulu*, belongs to the Nguni subgroup of Bantu and is the most widely spoken language in South Africa, with over 13.7 million first-language speakers. The Zulu began as a minor clan founded c. 1574 by Zulu kaMalandela and became a kingdom under Shaka from 1816. In folk-culture terms they matter for one of Africa's most legible visual codes — colour-coded glass beadwork that carries messages about age, wealth and marital status — alongside a burnished blackware pottery tradition, coiled *ilala* palm basketry, and virtuoso hardwood carving in headrests, meat platters and spoons.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Zulu material identity rests less on woven cloth than on beaded surface, coiled fibre and dressed hide — the "textile" traditions here are additive and structural rather than loom-based.

**Zulu beadwork** — glass seed beads sewn onto cloth or hide, or netted and threaded into freestanding panels, collars, aprons and belts. It descends from *iziqu*, the medallions of war worn criss-crossed over the shoulders as a sign of bravery; before glass reached the coast through Portuguese trade, the beads were wood, seeds and berries. What distinguishes Zulu work from Ndebele or Xhosa beadwork is the geometric triangle grammar and the regional colour schemes below, which make the work readable rather than merely decorative.

**Zulu love letter** (*ucu*) — a small beaded panel or necklace given as a courtship token. The message sits in the colour sequence and the triangle orientation; a young man wearing many of them advertises the number of girls who have favoured him, and his prestige rises with the count.

**Zulu basket** — coiled basketry in *ilala* palm (*Hyphaene coriacea*), split and dyed with bark, root and leaf infusions to browns, ochres and blacks, then coiled over a grass core. The signature forms are the *ukhamba*-shaped lidded beer basket and the *imbenge*, a shallow flared bowl-cover originally used to cap a beer pot and now also worked in telephone wire in the Durban workshops.

**Isigcebhezana** — the short beaded leather or hide skirt, cut short and worn in ceremonial contexts; it is essential to the *umemulo* and other rites and reads as cultural attire, not undress.

**Isibamba** — a thick belt coiled from dried grass and sheathed in glass or plastic beadwork, worn by pregnant women to carry the weight of the abdomen. It is one of the few Zulu garments made for a single physiological stage of life.

**Isicholo** — the flared, disc-shaped married woman's headdress, historically built onto the hair itself with ochre and fat over a grass armature, later made as a detachable red-ochred cap and often beaded at the rim.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Isishunka* (white, light blue, dark green, pale yellow, pink, red, black — held to carry no fixed meaning); *isithembu* (light blue, grass green, bright yellow, red, black — a clan-area scheme); *umzansi* (white, dark blue, grass green, red — also clan-based); *isinyolovane* (any combination outside the named schemes, connoting perfection and charm); the equilateral triangle (unmarried girl when pointed one way, married woman reversed); paired triangles forming a diamond (married woman) or an hourglass (married man); the horizontal band register; the criss-cross shoulder line inherited from *iziqu*. Colour meaning is regional, not national — green reads as jealousy in one district and as grass in the next, so the message cannot be read without knowing where the piece was made.

### Clothing & dress

Dress encodes life stage precisely. An unmarried girl (*tshitshi*) wears a short beaded skirt and nothing above; as she moves through betrothal to marriage she covers progressively more, engaged women marking status with hairnets and married women with the *isicholo* headdress and a long pleated cowhide skirt (*isidwaba*) falling past the knee, over which a beaded cloth apron is worn. Mature women wear the most elaborate headdresses and the longest hide skirts. Men are conservative in beadwork: the core garment is a leather belt carrying a front apron (*isinene*) of twisted hide strips and a rear flap (*ibheshu*) of soft calfskin, with fur or hide armbands and anklets (*amashoba*) of white cow-tail switches worn for dancing, and a headring or *isicoco* historically marking the married man. Ceremonial dress adds leopard or genet skin for royalty and *induna* rank, feather headpieces, and the full complement of beaded collars. Western clothing is everyday wear; the above is ceremonial and celebratory.

### Architecture

The homestead is the *umuzi*: a ring of dwellings enclosing a central cattle byre (*isibaya*), fenced with a palisade, entered at the downhill side, with the headman's mother's house at the apex opposite the gate and wives' houses ranked by seniority around the arc. The classic dwelling is the *iQukwane* or beehive house — a hemispherical dome of saplings bent and lashed into a lattice, thatched in overlapping courses of grass sewn to the frame with plaited fibre rope, with a low arched doorway that forces a stoop and a hearth sunk in the floor. The finest examples have an interior wickerwork of decorative binding patterns and a floor of polished, cow-dung-burnished earth (*ubulongwe*), dark and mirror-smooth. Military barracks (*ikhanda*, pl. *amakhanda*) built under Shaka reproduced the *umuzi* plan at vastly larger scale, and their maintenance was the duty of the resident regiment. Twentieth-century homesteads shifted to cylindrical wattle-and-daub or cement-block houses with conical or corrugated-iron roofs, but the byre-centred plan persists.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**Ukhamba** is the round-shouldered beer pot, hand-built by coiling, burnished with a pebble and fired in a pit, then blackened and sealed by smudging with green leaves or aloe — producing the matte-to-glossy carbon-black surface Zulu pottery is known for. Larger *uphiso* pots carry beer between homesteads; small *imbiza* forms are for brewing and cooking. Decoration is restrained: raised bosses (*amasumpa*, "warts"), applied in relief in linear and lozenge fields, the signature Zulu treatment. In wood: the **Zulu headrest** (*isigqiki*), carved from a single block with lug feet and *amasumpa*-derived relief, doubling as a personal seat and a channel to the ancestors in dream; the **meat platter** (*ugqoko*), a long low oval dish; spoons and ladles (*ukhezo*) with figured handles; and milk pails. **Snuff containers** — in gourd, horn, wood and beaded calabash — are near-universal. Weapons remain identity objects: the **assegai** (*iklwa*, the short stabbing spear of Shaka's reform), the throwing spear, the **knobkerrie** (*iwisa*), the fighting sticks (*izinduku*) and the oval cowhide **shield** (*isihlangu*, later the shorter *umbumbuluzo*), whose hide colour once identified regiment.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Beaded collars (*ithemba*), neckbands, wristbands and anklets dominate. The most distinctive Zulu ornament is the **earplug** (*iziqhaza*, sing. *isiqhaza*) — a large disc set into a stretched earlobe, historically carved from wood with inlaid pith or bone, and from the mid-twentieth century made as plastic mosaic discs in bright interlocking geometry, a self-consciously modern continuation of the same form. Married men wore the *isicoco*, a ring of fibre and gum sewn into the hair and polished with beeswax. Brass and copper wire bangles, cow-tail switches, and porcupine-quill hairpins complete the register. Quantity signals wealth directly: the more beads worn, the wealthier the wearer is read to be.

## Music & performance

Zulu music is overwhelmingly vocal and built on call-and-response between a leader and a tightly tuned chorus, with overlapping entries rather than unison cadences. The core instruments are the *ugubhu* gourd-resonated bow, plucked while the player's mouth modulates overtones over the gourd's opening; the *umakhweyana* braced bow, adopted from Tsonga neighbours; the *umtshingo* end-blown notched flute; the *isitolotolo* jaw harp; and drums (*isigubhu*) for ceremonial and church use. **Maskandi** (*maskanda*) is the itinerant guitarist's genre — a picked, fast-strummed style opening with a *izihlabo* flourish, carrying spoken self-praise (*izibongo*) mid-song, and associated with migrant labourers; Phuzekhemisi and Shiyani Ngcobo are its best-known exponents. **Isicathamiya** — from *cathama*, "to tread softly" — is the male a cappella tradition born in Durban and Johannesburg hostels, sung in bass-heavy close harmony with choreographed tiptoe steps, competitively performed in all-night hostel contests and internationally carried by Ladysmith Black Mambazo. **Mbube**, its louder predecessor, gave the world Solomon Linda's "Mbube" (1939). Nazareth Baptist Church congregations founded by Isaiah Shembe developed their own hymnody and processional dance, deliberately set to Zulu rather than European musical idiom. Praise poetry (*izibongo*) delivered by the royal *imbongi* is performed rather than read — declaimed at speed, with breath-group phrasing and no fixed melody.

## Dance & theatre

Zulu dance is grounded in *ingoma*, the unaccompanied or drum-driven dance of high kicks and ground-stamping performed by lines of men (*isishameni*, *isizingili* and other regional styles) and by young women in *umzansi* style. **Indlamu** is its best-known form: paired or massed male dancers in *ibheshu* and cow-tail *amashoba*, raising one leg above the head and bringing the foot down flat and hard in unison, shield in one hand, stick in the other — a martial display descended from regimental drill. The **Umhlanga** (Reed Dance), held annually near Nongoma since 1984, puts thousands of young women in beaded *isigcebhezana* before the king. **Stick fighting** (*ukungcweka*, with *izinduku*) sits between sport and duel: boys begin as young as five, opponents must be age-matched, a disarmed man may not be struck, and only sticks are permitted. The **gumboot dance** (*isicathulo*) emerged among Zulu mine and dock workers, who slapped and stamped their Wellington boots in percussive interlock. There is no indigenous masked or scripted theatre; the dramatic tradition is praise declamation and competitive dance.

## Festivals & rituals

**Ukweshwama**, the first-fruits ceremony, is the central annual rite — a day of prayer thanking the creator and the ancestors at the new harvest, at which a regiment of young warriors confronts a bull bare-handed to inherit its strength, the power then passing to the king. **Umhlanga** falls in September, its stated purpose the promotion of pride in virginity and restraint in sexual relationships; the king uses the gathering to address the young men and women of the nation on political matters. Life-cycle rites: *imbeleko*, the goat sacrifice introducing a newborn to the ancestors; *umemulo*, the coming-of-age ceremony for a woman turning twenty-one, marking her readiness to accept a suitor and marry, with the ox slaughtered and the *isigcebhezana* worn; *umgonqo*, the seclusion preceding it. Marriage runs on *ilobolo*, bride-wealth reckoned in cattle drawn from the groom's father's herd. It was open-ended and negotiable until 1869, when Theophilus Shepstone as Natal Secretary for Native Affairs fixed it at ten head for commoners plus the *ingquthu* cow for the bride's mother, fifteen for a chief's siblings and twenty-plus for a chief's daughters — a codification ritualists regard as untraditional. The wedding proper, *umabo*, involves the bride's gift-giving to the groom's family. Death brings *ukubuyisa*, the rite bringing the deceased home as an ancestral spirit (*idlozi*) a year after burial.

## Oral tradition & literature

Zulu narrative divides between *izinganekwane* — folktales told in the evening by women and older girls, opened with a formula and interspersed with sung refrains the audience joins — and *izibongo*, the praise poems of kings and lineages, which are historical record as much as poetry. The tales run heavily on tricksters: *Chakijana*, the small clever weasel-mongoose who outwits the powerful, and *uHlakanyana*, the precocious dwarf-child born speaking, both studied at length by Noverino Canonici. The cannibal ogres (*amazimu*) and the *impundulu* lightning-bird populate the darker tales. Clan praise-names (*izithakazelo*) are recited to greet and place a stranger by lineage. Henry Callaway's 1868 *Nursery Tales, Traditions, and Histories of the Zulus* and his 1870 collection of *izinyanga zokubula* divination texts remain the earliest substantial corpora, recorded in isiZulu with facing translation. The written tradition begins with Magolwane's oral compositions for Shaka and continues in the twentieth century through B. W. Vilakazi's poetry and R. R. R. Dhlomo's historical novels on the Zulu kings.

## Language & religion

*IsiZulu* is a Nguni Bantu language with three click consonant series (c, q, x) absorbed from Khoisan contact, a fifteen-noun-class system, and mutual intelligibility with Xhosa, Swati and Northern Ndebele — the latter created when Mzilikazi's regiment broke from Shaka and moved north into what is now Zimbabwe. It is written in Latin orthography standardised in the mission era and is one of South Africa's twelve official languages. Most Zulu identify as Christian, heavily concentrated in African Initiated Churches — the Nazareth Baptist Church founded by Isaiah Shembe, the Zion Christian Church, the United African Apostolic Church — alongside Anglican, Catholic and Dutch Reformed membership. Ancestor veneration runs in parallel rather than in competition: the *isithunzi* (prestige, shadow) survives death as an *idlozi* if the person lived with *ubuntu*. The *sangoma* diviner consults the ancestors; the *inyanga* herbalist compounds *muthi*, sharply divided between white *muthi* that heals and black *muthi* that harms — whose users are shunned as witches.
