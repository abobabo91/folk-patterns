## Overview

The Ovimbundu, also known as the Southern Mbundu, are a Bantu ethnic group living on the Bié Plateau of central Angola and in the coastal strip west of these highlands. They make up 38 percent of Angola’s population and are the country’s largest ethnic group. Ovimbundu communities are also found in cities outside their main habitat, so a significant part of the people is scattered throughout Angola. Their history includes the formation of regional groups and political units, caravan trade, agriculture, colonial rule, paid plantation labour, political conflict, displacement, and post-2002 reconstruction. Christianity is widespread, especially through the Igreja Evangélica Congregacional de Angola and the Catholic Church, while some people retain beliefs and practices from African traditional religions.

## Material culture

### Architecture

 They do record that the construction of the Benguela Railway in 1904 contributed to the decline of caravan trading, and that many villages and much infrastructure were destroyed during the Civil War.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Regional specialization in metalwork developed through trade among independent chiefdoms. Ovimbundu societies participated in caravan trade involving wax, rubber, honey, and ivory.

## Music & performance

 The available material records caravan trade and its professional leaders and diviners.

## Festivals & rituals

 The sources state that some Ovimbundu retain beliefs and practices from African traditional religions.

## Foodways

The Ovimbundu developed sophisticated agriculture and bred small animals, including chicken, goats, and swine. They also obtained a small number of cows from farmer-herders to the south, including the Nyaneka-Nkhumbi and Ovambo. Cornmeal production became a regional specialization, and during the twentieth century the Ovimbundu shifted substantially toward cash-crop production of corn, which was sold through an expanding network of Portuguese traders.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They do state that each trading caravan had a professional leader and diviner, but provide no account of the narratives, performances, or knowledge associated with these figures.

## Language & religion

The sources identify the Ovimbundu as a Bantu ethnic group and also call them the Southern Mbundu. The Ovimbundu overwhelmingly follow Christianity, mainly through the Igreja Evangélica Congregacional de Angola, founded by American missionaries, and the Catholic Church. Some people retain beliefs and practices from African traditional religions. The sources also describe the Ovimbundu as the main social basis of UNITA, which fought against the Portuguese from 1966 to 1974, opposed the MPLA during the Angolan Civil War from 1975 to 2002, and is now an opposition political party.
