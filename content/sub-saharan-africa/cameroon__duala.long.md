## Overview

The Duala are a Bantu ethnic group of Cameroon, concentrated mainly in the Littoral Province, especially in the Moungo, Nkam, and Wouri divisions. Their settlements lie along the coast or just inland, with the Wouri estuary—where the Wouri, Mungo, and Dibamba Rivers meet—as the centre of Duala country. Douala is their traditional capital, and many Duala live in and around the city. The group is closely related to other Sawa or coastal peoples, including the Ewodi, Bodiman, Pongo, Bakole, Bakweri, Bamboko, Isubu, Limba, Mungo, Wovea, and Oroko. Duala belongs to the Bantu language family and the Niger–Congo language family. The Duala became prominent coastal traders by the 16th century and have remained influential through trade, education, Christianity, urban employment, and ownership of property.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The museum records do, however, document several forms of carved wooden material culture:

**Carved wooden stools** — British Museum records describe rectangular stools with crescent-shaped seats, open-work bases, small or half-height backrests, and carved supports. One stool has female figures with raised arms supporting the seat; another has leopards carved at the front and back, circles of burnt decoration, and dark-brown staining.

**Serpent-supported stool** — One British Museum stool has an open-work base with two S-figured serpents and a curved seat.

**Inscribed stool** — A stool carved from a single block has a crescent-shaped seat, a bird and two leopards on one side of its support structure, and the inscription “KAMEFUN” on the other.

**Prestige seat** — The Metropolitan Museum of Art catalogue records a wooden prestige seat dating from the mid–late 19th century.

**Pigmented stool** — Another British Museum object is a pale-coloured, carved and pigmented wooden stool with letters on its seat.

**Motif vocabulary.** female figures with raised arms, leopards, bird figure, S-figured serpents, circles of burnt decoration.

### Architecture

The sources state that many Duala live in and around Douala, that many still own parts of the city, and that urban property ownership allows some to live from rents and development.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Duala material culture in the supplied records is represented chiefly by carved wood. The British Museum catalogue lists stools made from wood, including rectangular stools with open-work bases, curved or crescent-shaped seats, backrests, carved animals, human figures, serpents, letters, inscriptions, staining, pigmentation, and burnt decoration. The Metropolitan Museum of Art records a wooden prestige seat from the mid–late 19th century.

A Museum of Ethnography record describes an instrument made from a wooden plate with iron keys and a small ornamented gourd attached by a string. The plate is recorded as 12.2 centimetres long and 8.8 centimetres wide, and the object is identified with Cameroon and Duala.

## Music & performance

The Duala language article identifies Duala as a tonal language with subject–verb–object word order and records its historical use in religious and informal contexts. The Duala people article states that, at least until the German period, Duala men used a kind of “drum language”: coded messages were tapped out to communicate news over long distances.

A Museum of Ethnography catalogue record describes a small instrument associated with Duala. It consists of a wooden plate with iron keys, identified in the record as being of a “sansatyp” type, with a small ornamented gourd forming the resonating bottom. The gourd was attached to the instrument by a string. The catalogue gives the plate’s length as 12.2 centimetres and its width as 8.8 centimetres.

Pirogue racing has traditionally been the most important Duala sport and has a performance dimension involving music, display, competition, and divination. During the German colonial period, races were held annually on 27 January, identified in the source as the Kaiser’s birthday. Under the French, races became semiannual and were held on 14 July and 11 November. A typical racing pirogue is 20–28 metres long, has no keel, and has a bow carved with intricate designs. Each vessel is manned by 40–50 canoeists, mostly men who work as fishermen. In the past, diviners interpreted race results to predict the future; the source states that a Christian priest presides instead today.

## Dance & theatre

The Duala people article does record traditional annual festivals as visible expressions of pre-Christian beliefs in modern life. It also describes the Ngondo as an assembly of important chiefs and mentions secret societies including the Ekongolo, Jengu, Losango, and Munji.

## Festivals & rituals

The Duala have been mostly Christianized since the 1930s, with Evangelical denominations, particularly the Baptist church, dominant. Remnants of pre-Christian ancestor worship persist, and the sea has an important place in Duala belief. The sources state that Duala belief holds that ancestors live in the sea. Demi-human water spirits called *Miengu*, singular *Jengu*, live in the waters and mediate between worshippers and God.

Traditional festivals held each year are described as the most visible modern expression of these beliefs. The Ngondo is identified as an assembly of important chiefs.

Pirogue races were held annually on 27 January during the German colonial period. Under the French administration, they occurred twice yearly, on 14 July and 11 November. Until the late 1930s, a family on Jebale Island was said to be able to summon the Miengu water spirits to assist favoured participants in the races.

The sources state that Duala inheritance is patrilineal and that property was traditionally divided among male heirs after the father’s death. The Duala traditionally practiced polygamy, although the custom became rarer after the introduction of Christianity.

## Foodways

The sources state that rural Duala work mainly as fishermen and farmers, usually at subsistence level, and that fishing is the trade of choice. The museum records include a photograph titled “Infödingar från Duala efter lyckat fiske,” described as people from Duala after successful fishing.

## Oral tradition & literature

Early Duala history is preserved in oral traditions. The Duala trace their ancestry to a man named Mbedi, who lived in an area called Bakota in what is today Gabon or the Republic of the Congo. His sons Ewale and Dibombo migrated north to Pitti on the Dibamba River, where they separated after a quarrel. Ewale’s followers moved toward the Wouri River estuary, while Dibongo and his companions travelled toward the Sanaga River and later divided into different groups. Ewale’s people became the Duala, and Dibongo’s became the Limba.

According to Duala traditions, the Bakoko and Bassa occupied the Wouri estuary when the Duala arrived. The source places their displacement inland in the late 17th or early 18th century.

The sources record the early missionaries’ development of a written form of the Duala language, partly to translate the Bible.

## Language & religion

Duala is a Bantu language and belongs to a subgroup called Sawabantu. It is a dialect cluster spoken by the Duala and Mungo peoples. The cluster includes Duala proper, Bodiman, Oli, Ewodi, Wuri, Pongo, and Mongo; Maho also notes a Duala-based pidgin called Jo. Duala is tonal and has subject–verb–object word order. It is part of the Niger–Congo language family.

The language became a trade language through its spread by early missionaries, especially among neighbouring Wovea and Isubu communities. Alfred Saker completed the first translation of the Bible into Duala in 1870. The Basel Mission promoted Duala as a lingua franca in southern Cameroon after German colonization, although German was used increasingly in schools. In 1910, German educational policy restricted the use of Duala in schools and official communications. After World War I, French was ordered in schools in the French mandate, while English-medium schooling became standard in the British mandate.

Most Duala have been Christianized since the 1930s. Evangelical denominations, particularly the Baptist church, dominate, while ancestor worship and belief in Miengu or Jengu water spirits remain part of the documented religious landscape.
