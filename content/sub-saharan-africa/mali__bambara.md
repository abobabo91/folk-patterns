## Overview

The Bambara are a Mandé people whose history is linked to farming communities, Ségou and the Bamana Empire. Their culture includes caste and age-group societies, ritual sculpture, masks, textiles, music objects and wooden figures. Much Bambara art was made for religious use, ceremonies and offerings. Oral traditions are carried mainly by griots, called Jeliw.

## Material culture

Bambara material culture includes cotton textiles, ritual masks, wooden figures, locks, musical instruments, headdresses and iron objects, often made for ceremony or spiritual use.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Bambara textiles are often made from narrow woven cotton strips joined together and decorated with mud or discharge dyeing.

- **Mud cloth** (*Bogolanfini*) — Bogolanfini is hand-spun cotton cloth decorated with mud resist patterns such as dashes, crosses, circles, zig-zags, stripes, diamonds, triangles and squares.
- **Bambara mud cloth** (*Bokolan fini*) — This cotton textile is made from narrow strips sewn together before dyeing and is usually white and dark brown-black.
- **Discharge-dyed wrapper** — These cotton wrappers use brown discharge-dyed patterns, including waves, repeating ovals and dense geometric designs.
- **Indigo-dyed blanket** (*munnyuure*) — A munnyuure blanket is woven from ten wide strips with indigo and white bands and extra white-weft patterns.
- **Carrying cloth** — This cloth is made from four narrow strips and has mud-resist sections forming a central diamond-chain pattern.

### Clothing & dress

Documented Bambara dress includes cotton wrappers, skirts, blankets, carrying cloths and a masquerade costume.

- **Woman’s wrapper** — Women’s wrappers may be single-width or made from seven or eight woven strips, with fringes, brown or yellow dyeing, and mud or discharge patterns.
- **Married woman’s skirt** — This hand-woven cotton skirt has a five-by-eight grid of crosses, chevrons, zig-zags and triangles.
- **Masquerade costume** — This cotton costume is made from 22 narrow strips and has a drawstring neckline, sleeves and two lower sleeves for the legs.
- **Cotton garment from Ségou** — This garment measures 185 by 90 centimetres and has dark-brown discharge-dyed patterns.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Bamana everyday and artistic objects include locks, loom tools, musical instruments, calabashes and poisoned arrows.

- **Human-shaped lock** — Some wooden and metal locks are carved as stylised human beings.
- **Crocodile lock** — One lock is carved in the shape of a crocodile.
- **Woman’s-head heddle pulley** — A wooden and metal loom pulley is carved as a woman’s head.
- **Slit-gong** — This wooden instrument has a carved human head at one end and metal studs for eyes.
- **Poisoned arrows** — These arrows have cane shafts, cane binding and triangular iron arrowheads.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention neck ornaments, earrings, nose ornaments, metal teeth, shells, seeds and scarification on objects and figures.

- **Bead neck ornament** — Bead neck ornaments are shown or mentioned in the sources.
- **Metal earrings** — Metal earrings are among the recorded forms of adornment.
- **Nose ornament** — Nose ornaments are mentioned in the sources.
- **Scarification pattern** — Bambara statues are described with marked or powerful scarification patterns.
- **Cowrie and abrus decoration** — Cowrie shells and abrus seeds appear on objects and headdresses.

## Music & performance

Bambara music uses whistles, guitars, rattles, slit-gongs and trumpets, and it is closely linked with oral performance.

- **Wooden whistle or flute** — A wooden whistle or flute is among the recorded Bambara sound-making objects.
- **Six-string guitar** — This wooden guitar has six strings and a reptile-skin resonator.
- **Calabash rattle** — A calabash rattle or sistrum is mounted on a bent stick and topped with a human head.
- **Slit-gong** — A scraped wooden slit-gong is used as a musical object.
- **Dance trumpet** — A wooden trumpet was used to summon people to dance.

## Dance & theatre

Bamana dance is tied to farming ceremonies, initiation and masquerade, especially through animal-form headdresses and masks.

- **Antelope harvest headdress** (*Tyi Warra*) — The Tyi Warra, or ciwara, is worn at harvest time by selected young men from the farmers association.
- **N’Tomo mask** (*n’tomo*) — The n’tomo mask was used by dancers during male initiation ceremonies.
- **Komo head-dress mask** (*Komo*) — This mask has a clay-covered carved-wood base, an animal head, antelope horns, tusks and porcupine quills.
- **N’Tomo society mask** (*N’Tomo*) — A wooden mask is recorded as having been used by the N’Tomo society.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe initiation, farming and masquerade rituals but give no annual festival calendar.

- **Male initiation** (*n’tomo*) — Male initiation ceremonies used n’tomo masks and included statues made for young men.
- **Sonkalaniw initiation figures** (*sonkalaniw*) — Sonkalaniw were made for the initiation of young men by a Jo group known as boso.
- **Nyeleniw initiation figures** (*nyeleniw*) — Nyeleniw were used in blacksmiths’ Jo initiation.
- **Age-group societies** (*Tons*) — Tons were age-based, sex-based or vocational associations that helped socialize people and pass on traditions.
- **Soli ton** — The Soli ton was made up of young men who lived apart, worked on farms and later took wives.

## Foodways

- **Pounding and sifting meal** — One photograph shows women pounding an unidentified meal, sifting it into a calabash and carrying a child.

## Oral tradition & literature

Bambara oral literature is mainly carried by griots, who preserve stories, praise songs, history and old songs.

- **Griot** (*Jeliw*) — Jeliw are storytellers, praise singers and human history books who study singing and recitation for many years.
- **Tales of kings and heroes** — Bambara oral literature includes many tales about kings and heroes.
- **Songs of the old Mali empire** — Many griot songs are said to date back to the old empire of Mali.

## Language & religion

Bambara is Mali’s main Manding lingua franca, and most Bamana combine Islam with some traditional ancestor-honouring practices.

- **Bambara language** (*Bamanankan*) — Bambara, or Bamanankan, is a Manding language spoken throughout Mali as a lingua franca.
- **N’Ko script** (*N’Ko*) — N’Ko is one of the scripts used to write Bambara.
- **Masaba script** (*Masaba*) — Masaba was developed beginning in 1930 for writing Bambara.
- **Ancestor honouring** — Many Bamana Muslims continue traditional rituals, especially those honouring ancestors.
- **Syncretic Islam** — Most Bamana follow Islam while also keeping some traditional rituals.
