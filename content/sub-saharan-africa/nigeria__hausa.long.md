## Overview

Hausa communities live across the border between northern Nigeria and southern Niger and farther afield in West Africa. Kano, Katsina and Zaria are among the historic urban centers represented in the sources for this collection. The selected objects come mostly from British Museum records attributed to Hausa makers or users. They show a wider material world than formal dress alone: household trays, Qur'an boards, musical instruments, horse equipment, pottery, leather cases and amulets. A cultural attribution on a museum record applies to that object; it does not make every form universal among Hausa communities.

Textile, leather and metal trades intersect in the surviving pieces. A robe may combine handwoven strips, stitched seams and embroidery; horse equipment may bring together leather, brass, cloth and iron. The Smithsonian documents Hausa people working at Kano's indigo dye pits in 1959 and a mason and apprentice building a mud-brick wall at Katsina the same year. These photographs identify craftspeople and places rather than treating the designs as timeless motifs.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Smithsonian's Hausa robe is assembled from narrow strips of handwoven cotton and bears extensive embroidery around the neck and across the body. Its museum account explains that the embroidery could take months, with a calligrapher laying out the pattern. The motifs include triangular forms and a small shape interpreted as a Qur'an board. Smithsonian records also show how an embroidered hat can carry colored geometric and curving designs. The museum notes that hats of this form spread beyond Hausa communities; an embroidered cap seen elsewhere is therefore not proof of Hausa authorship.

The selected British Museum trousers are made from 75 narrow, handwoven strips sewn together and embroidered in geometric patterns. Other selected cloths have indigo-dyed bands or strips. One garment combines machine-woven damask with hand sewing and embroidery, so its textile history cannot be reduced to a single handmade technique. The British Museum also preserves two drawn plans for embroidery on a man's gown; these are design documents, not surviving gowns. A recorded horizontal loom (*turkudi*) makes the weaving equipment visible alongside the finished textiles.

### Clothing & dress

The selected garments include a broad men's gown, decorated trousers, a child's woven bolero, riding boots, leather sandals and embroidered slippers. A British Museum gown described as *boubou*, *tobe* or *riga* is built from dozens of handwoven cotton strips. The Smithsonian robe offers a second example of the labor invested in a formal man's garment. Both museum records are more precise than a generic claim that every Hausa gown has the same shape or motif.

Accessories vary. A plaited straw hat (*malfa*) with leather decoration differs from the densely embroidered cotton cap described by the Smithsonian. Yellow-dyed leather riding boots and patterned slippers show how leatherworkers joined practical footwear with display. The British Museum portrait of a Hausa woman shows a wrapped cloth and head covering, but the photograph alone does not establish a standard outfit for all Hausa women.

### Architecture

Smithsonian archival photographs identify mud-brick building at Katsina, Hausa houses with raised surface patterns at Kano in 1974, and urban compounds in both cities. The visual record supports earthen walls and applied geometric relief as concrete parts of the building tradition. A selected British Museum door screen adds an architectural fitting, while Commons photographs document decorated entrances and palace gates. The images represent particular buildings and dates, not a single fixed house plan for every Hausa settlement.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The selected household material includes coiled basketry trays and dishes, a patterned mat, a carved wooden stool, a pottery hearth, a jug used for ritual ablutions and a metal water container (*shantali*) also used for ablutions. A brass lid was probably made for a food or kola-nut container. These object descriptions show uses that broad labels such as “Hausa pottery” would miss.

Leather runs through the collection: a saddlebag, purse, Qur'an cover, a charm case (*rufin laya*), sheaths and horse gear. The British Museum records a group of decorated leatherware made in Zaria in the 1970s by the Hausa leatherworker Alhaji Balarabe. Its acquisition history also says some pieces were made for Fulani users. The maker and user can thus belong to different communities. Iron and brass stirrups, a dagger, sword, knife, axe and a powder horn document metal and leather work associated with riding and everyday use.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The selected adornments include bracelets, beads, a leather-tab necklace and decorated horse forehead gear. Several small leather cases enclose paper and are catalogued as amulets or charms. The catalogue does not give one shared meaning for every protective object. One Europeana record describes an amulet intended to protect against thieves; its own source supplies that particular function. Horse trappings belong to equestrian display rather than to jewelry worn by a person, even though the source search filed one under jewelry.

## Music & performance

British Museum records identify an *algaita* shawm, a gourd drum, stringed instruments, a rattle, a reed instrument and a carved whistle. The visible materials include wood, gourd, cane, skin and leather. The records support the existence and construction of these instruments, but generally do not name a performer or a particular repertoire.

UNESCO's description of the Durbar in Kano places drumming alongside horse preparation and riding among skills taught in the community. The procession also displays clothing and equipment made by leatherworkers, weavers, dyers, embroiderers and blacksmiths. It is a living civic and religious event in which these crafts meet, and UNESCO explicitly describes participation across ethnicities.

## Dance & theatre

The sources consulted here document the Kano Durbar as a procession with mounted riders and people on foot, organized around the emir and his court. UNESCO describes four processions, each with its own timing and costume. It is a public performance and ceremony involving people from several communities. The selected objects do not support a fuller account of named Hausa dance forms or theatre genres.

## Festivals & rituals

The Durbar in Kano takes place around Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha. UNESCO inscribed it on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2024. Its account describes gun salutes, movement through city quarters, horse processions and the transmission of craft and riding skills. The event brings people of different ethnicities, genders and ages together; this profile includes it because it is rooted in Kano's civic setting and strongly represented in Hausa material culture.

Qur'an boards, the leather Qur'an cover and ablution vessels in the object selection document religious learning and practice at the level of particular things. The British Museum records small charm cases and amulets as well. Their precise contents, users and occasions cannot be inferred from the images alone.

## Foodways

The selected collection contains a brass cover probably used over food or kola nuts, a cup, baskets, a cooking hearth and a market photograph. These give evidence of serving, cooking and exchange.

## Oral tradition & literature

The selected Qur'an boards carry written passages and the leather cover protects loose sheets. The drawn embroidery plans show another use of graphic design on paper. These records document writing, study and design.

## Language & religion

Hausa is a Chadic language in the Afro-Asiatic family. The museum objects in this selection strongly document Islamic settings: Qur'an boards, ablution vessels, a Qur'an cover and dress used for formal occasions. They should not be taken as an inventory of every Hausa religious practice. Museum catalogues use words such as “charm” and “amulet” for several leather objects but rarely explain their local interpretation.
