## Overview

The Somali (*Soomaali*) are a Cushitic-speaking people of the Horn of Africa, occupying a broadly contiguous territory that stretches from the Gulf of Aden coast southward to the Tana River in northern Kenya, and inland from the Indian Ocean across the Ogaden plateau to the Ethiopian highlands. Their homeland encompasses the Republic of Somalia, Somaliland, the Somali Region of Ethiopia, Djibouti and Kenya's North Eastern Province, with a global population commonly estimated at 25–30 million including a substantial diaspora in the Arabian Peninsula, the United Kingdom, Scandinavia, North America and the Netherlands. The Somali language belongs to the Lowland East Cushitic branch of the Afroasiatic family and was standardised in Latin script only in 1972; before that a Perso-Arabic adaptation (*wadaad's writing*) and the indigenous *Osmanya* script coexisted with a vast oral literature. Somali society is organised around a segmentary lineage system of patrilineal clan-families (Darood, Hawiye, Isaaq, Dir, Rahanweyn and the riverine and coastal minority groups), and its economy has historically balanced camel-and-goat pastoralism on the interior *guban* and *ogo* plateaux against the maritime and trading cultures of the Benadir coast — Mogadishu, Merca and Brava — which for a millennium fed into the Indian Ocean world. In folk-culture terms the Somali matter as the pre-eminent camel pastoralists of the eastern Horn, as the makers of an exceptionally refined coiled basketry tradition (*xeedho*, *dhiil*), as silversmiths and weavers of the Benadiri coast, and as custodians of one of Africa's densest oral-poetic canons.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Benadiri weaving** (*dhar Banaadiri*) — the coastal narrow-strip loom tradition of Mogadishu, Merca and Brava, historically worked by hereditary weavers of the Reer Benadir. Strips of handspun cotton, roughly 25–30 cm wide, are woven on a pit treadle loom, dyed with indigo and local reds, and sewn edge-to-edge into large body cloths. The signature product is the **alindi** (*alandi*), a red-and-indigo checked or striped women's wrap that functions as the classic Benadiri bridal cloth and is still commissioned for weddings across the southern Somali diaspora.

**Shuqqad / *shuqa*** — the plain white cotton body cloth worn as a shoulder wrap by men, longer and lighter than the alindi, sometimes finished with a coloured stripe at the selvedge.

**Guntiino** — an untailored length of cloth, often plain red-orange or of alindi weave, wrapped over one shoulder and knotted to form the classic Somali woman's dress. Modern *guntiino* is frequently cut from imported printed cotton but keeps the diagonal drape of the older form.

**Dirac** — a long, sheer voile or polyester over-dress worn over a full-length petticoat (*gorgorad*) and shoulder shawl (*garbasaar*), the standard formal dress of Somali women in the twentieth century and today; usually solidly dyed and shot with metallic thread.

**Motif vocabulary.** Somali textile and object ornament is predominantly geometric and non-figurative, drawing on triangles (*tumor*-like amulet shapes), lozenges, stepped chevrons, indigo-and-red stripes, and — on basketry and leather — banded colour fields rather than curvilinear vine work. Where figurative or scrolling motifs appear on the coast they generally reflect Arab, Swahili and Gujarati importation.

### Clothing & dress

Men's everyday dress in the interior is the *macawiis* (a sarong-like waist wrap, most often in South-East Asian checked cotton imported via Aden), a Western or long *khamiis* shirt above it, a *koofiyad* embroidered cap, and for older men a folded *shuqqad* over the shoulder. On horseback and on formal occasions the *khamiis* is paired with a length of white cloth wound as a turban (*duub*). Sandals (*kabo*) of tanned hide or, historically, of woven palm-fibre are worn. Women's dress in the pastoral interior is built around the *guntiino* wrap in red-orange or alindi cloth, secured with a shoulder brooch, over a plain slip; married women cover the hair with a *shash* headscarf, while the *garbasaar* shawl and full *dirac* ensemble mark urban and festive contexts. Bridal dress in the Benadir tradition is a full alindi *guntiino* with heavy silver jewellery and elaborate henna (*xenna*) worked on hands and feet during the *xeedho* week. Amuletic leather cases (*xirsi*) containing Qur'anic verse, sewn to a cord or belt, are worn by children and travellers.

### Architecture

The signature Somali dwelling of the pastoral interior is the *aqal* (also *aqal Soomaali* or, among the northern Isaaq, *aqal geed*), a portable dome-hut framed with bent acacia or *galool* saplings lashed into a hemispherical armature and covered with woven fibre mats (*kebed*) and, in wet season or for wealthier households, cured hides. The frame is dismantled and packed onto the pack-camel (*rati*) at each move, and its assembly is women's specialist knowledge, passed matrilineally alongside the basketry repertoire. The interior is divided by hanging mats into a sleeping side and a hearth side, with the *xeedho* and milk vessels displayed on the women's side. A related but sturdier form, the *mundul*, is a conical thatched round-house of the sedentary agro-pastoral Rahanweyn and riverine Bantu-Somali communities of the inter-riverine south, built with a central pole and mud-and-wattle walls. On the Benadir coast the vernacular is entirely different: a dense fabric of flat-roofed coral-rag townhouses in Mogadishu's Hamar Weyne and Shangani quarters, Merca and Brava, built of coral limestone (*jiingad*) set in lime mortar, faced with carved plaster and organised around small interior courtyards, with elaborately carved teak doors of the wider Swahili-Omani tradition. Historic monumental architecture includes the Fakhr ad-Din mosque in Mogadishu (13th century) and the medieval trading towns of Zeila and Berbera on the Gulf of Aden.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Somali material culture is unusually basketry-centred, with wood, leather and silver in support, and comparatively little indigenous fired ceramic tradition — clay vessels have long been imported or, in the riverine south, made by neighbouring Bantu potters.

**Somali basketry** — coiled grass-and-fibre vessels are the defining Somali craft. The **xeedho** is a tightly coiled, lidded milk-and-food container with a narrow neck, decorated in bands of red, black and natural fibre; a ceremonial *xeedho* filled with sweetened meat and dates is presented by the bride's family and ritually opened by the groom's kin on the seventh night of the wedding. The **dhiil** is a taller coiled milk vessel used for camel milk and its fermented form *caano geel*. Flat coiled mats and food-covers (*gembi*, *dhasho*) in concentric-ring patterns of madder red and indigo black on straw ground are made across the pastoral north.

**Somali silver** — silversmiths, historically concentrated in the coastal towns and among specialised occupational lineages, produce heavy anklets, spiral bracelets (*jimbaar*), chest amulets, ear ornaments and the filigreed clasps of the Benadiri bridal set. Techniques include granulation, filigree and repoussé, with clear formal continuities to Yemeni and Omani silverwork across the Gulf of Aden.

**Somali woodcarving** — turned and carved *galool* and juniper wood is worked into the **Somali headrest** (*barkin*), a small stool-like neck-rest used by pastoralists to preserve elaborate hairstyles during sleep, into camel saddles (*haan*), milk-vessel stands, spoons, Qur'an boards (*loox*) for children learning the *kitab*, and into the carved teak doors of the Benadir coast.

**Somali leatherwork** — tanned goat, sheep and camel hide is worked into sandals, saddle covers, water-skins (*sibraar*), the amulet cases (*xirsi*) worn on the body, and the covers of manuscript codices.

**Somali dagger — *billaawe*** — the classic Somali men's dagger, a short leaf-shaped double-edged blade with a horn or wood grip and a leather-and-silver sheath worn at the waist, historically an item of male adult dress across the north; larger curved forms (*bilaawad*) also occur.

**Somali incense burner — *dabqaad*** — a squat, rectangular soapstone censer carved from the greenish stone quarried around El Buur in the Galguduud region. Its centre is hollowed to hold embers on which *foox* (frankincense) or *cuud* (aloewood) is smouldered; the *dabqaad* is passed round for guests to perfume their clothes and hair, and is a required item in any respectable household.

**Haaqad** — a wooden or basketry cover placed over milk vessels and food bowls to keep out dust and flies, often coiled in the same technique as the *xeedho*.

**Somali camel bell** — carved from a single block of hardwood or forged in iron, hung at the neck of lead camels in a caravan; each herder's bell has a distinctive tone by which straying animals can be located at night.

**Somali wooden comb** (*shanlo*) — a long-toothed carved comb, often with a geometrically incised handle, used to dress and part the elaborate braided hairstyles of women and, historically, the *afro*-style hair of young pastoral men stiffened with butter and ochre.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Somali jewellery is dominated by silver rather than gold and by amuletic rather than purely decorative forms. Heavy anklets and spiral cuff bracelets, chest pectorals hung with coin pendants, filigree earrings and hair-pins, and the triangular or cylindrical *xirsi* amulet case worn on a cord across the chest are common across the pastoral north; the Benadiri coast adds heavier gold sets — necklaces, wrist and ankle bangles, and forehead ornaments — of clear Indian Ocean derivation. **Somali prayer beads** (*tusbax*), typically 99 or 33 beads of wood, amber, coral or plastic strung on a cord with a distinguishing head bead, are carried by adult men and are as much an item of dress as of devotion. Henna (*xenna*) worked on the hands and feet in dense geometric fields is central to weddings and to the Eids. Body scarification is not a Somali practice, but distinctive women's hairstyles — many small braids gathered into a crown, sometimes coloured with henna — and the men's *koofiyad* embroidered cap serve as the primary markers of adult identity. Facial tattooing in small indigo dots at the chin, temple or between the brows was formerly common among older pastoral women and is now largely gone.

## Music & performance

Somali music is predominantly vocal, poetry-driven and, in its classical pastoral forms, unaccompanied or accompanied only by hand-clapping and the frame drum. The pastoral repertoire is organised by genre and by the type of work or ritual it belongs to: *hees-ho'* sung to camels at watering, *hees-mool* sung while churning milk in the *dhiil*, *hees-geeraar* on horseback, and lullabies (*hobey*). Sung poetry is the prestige form: the long alliterative *gabay*, the shorter *geeraar*, the lyric *jiifto* and, in the twentieth century, the enormously popular *heello* / *hees* modern song form that grew out of the *balwo* love-lyric of Borama in the 1940s. Instruments, once few, include the lyre *shareero* (cognate with the Ethiopian *krar*), the single-string bowed lute *kaban*, the tambourine *daf* and the tall wooden drum *durbaan*, joined on the Benadir coast by the oud (*cuud*) and Arab-style ensembles. The twentieth-century state theatre in Mogadishu — the Waaberi troupe and singers such as Magool, Hasan Aden Samatar and Maryam Mursal — carried this idiom into orchestrated modern song. Somali oral poetry was inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2022 under the entry *Hees Diiwaan* / Somali oral poetry.

## Dance & theatre

Somali social dance is organised in facing lines of men and women or in mixed circles, with rhythmic clapping and stamping carrying much of the accompaniment. The northern *dhaanto* — a courting dance historically associated with the Ogaden and now emblematic of Somali dance generally — pairs sung couplets with a stepping, hip-swinging line movement; *saylici* and *jaandheer* are related northern forms. In the south the *shirib* and *saar* of the inter-riverine and coastal populations show clearer Bantu and Arab influences, including possession-cult drumming linked to the *saar* healing tradition shared with the Ethiopian *zar*. Wedding dances (*aroos*) are the principal public dance context, with the bride's henna evening and the seventh-day *xeedho* opening as choreographed set-pieces. Formal theatre in the Western sense emerged only in the mid-twentieth century at the National Theatre in Mogadishu, drawing on the *heello* song form to create the sung-play (*riwaayad*) that dominated Somali stage culture from the 1950s to the collapse of the state in 1991.

## Festivals & rituals

The Somali ritual year is Islamic in its major markers — *Ciid al-Fitr* at the end of Ramadan and *Ciid al-Adha* at the pilgrimage — celebrated with new clothes, henna, communal prayer and the slaughter of a sheep or goat. Weddings are the great social spectacle, structured over several days from the *nikaax* contract through the henna evening (*xafladda xenna*) to the seventh-night *xeedho* ceremony, in which the groom's kin must successfully untie the elaborately bound ceremonial basket to claim its sweet contents. *Dabshid* (also *Neyruus*), a pre-Islamic fire festival marking the solar new year and historically celebrated with bonfires, mock stick-fights (*istun*) and the burning of a symbolic stick, survives in attenuated form in parts of the south. Life-cycle rites include the naming ceremony on the seventh day after birth (*siyaaro* / *aqiiqa*), boys' circumcision, and mourning gatherings (*duco*) in which Qur'an is recited over three, seven and forty days. Pastoral rituals around rain-making, camel-branding (*sumad*) and the seasonal moves between *jilaal* dry season and *gu* wet season are marked by specific poetic genres rather than by fixed calendar festivals.

## Foodways

Somali cuisine sits at a crossroads of pastoral, Arab, Persian, Indian and Italian influence. The dietary base of the interior is camel and goat milk — fresh, and fermented as *caano geel* — supplemented by meat (mutton, goat, camel), rice and sorghum. Signature dishes include *bariis iskukaris*, a spiced pilau of basmati rice with cardamom, cumin and cloves cooked with lamb or goat; *suqaar*, small-cubed sautéed meat; *muqmad* / *oodkac*, dried and clarified-butter-preserved meat carried by travellers; *canjeero* / *laxoox*, a sourdough pancake close to Ethiopian *injera* but smaller and finer, eaten at breakfast with ghee and sugar or with stew; and *sabaayad*, a layered flatbread of clear Indian derivation. The southern riverine cuisine adds maize and sesame; the Benadir coast contributes fish stews, *malawah* sweet pancakes and the Italian legacy of *baasto* (pasta) with a spiced meat sauce, a genuinely nationalised dish. Tea (*shaah*) is boiled with milk, cardamom, cloves and cinnamon in the Indian Ocean manner; coffee (*bun* or *qaxwa*) is drunk with the beans lightly roasted in ghee and salt in the older pastoral form, or as Arab-style spiced coffee. The chewing of *khat* (*qaad*, *jaad*) leaves in afternoon male sociability sessions (*mafrish*) is a defining, and contested, Somali social institution.

## Oral tradition & literature

Somali culture is, by long consensus of its own poets and of outside observers from Richard Burton to B. W. Andrzejewski, one of the most poetry-saturated societies on earth — Burton's 1854 label "a nation of poets" has stuck. The core genres form a hierarchy: the *gabay*, a long-lined alliterative poem of praise, invective or political argument, is the prestige adult form and demands strict alliteration on a single consonant throughout; the *geeraar* is a shorter martial or horseback poem; the *jiifto* and *masafo* are shorter lyric or devotional forms; the *buraanbur* is the women's alliterative form, performed at weddings and social gatherings. Composed and memorised without writing until the 1972 script reform, poems circulate by named reciters (*hafidayaal*) who preserve attribution to a specific author across generations — Sayyid Maxamed Cabdulle Xasan, Cilmi Boodhari, Xaaji Aadan Axmed Xasan and Hadraawi are among the canonical figures. Prose narrative includes clan genealogies (*abtirsiinyo*), historical narratives, folktales built around the trickster figures Dhegdheer and A Igaal Shidaad, and a large body of proverbs (*maahmaah*). The manuscript tradition of *Somali kitab* — Arabic-script religious books, Qur'ans, and Sufi devotional works, often with Somali marginalia in *wadaad's writing* — was carried by the Qadiriyya, Ahmadiyya and Salihiyya brotherhoods and preserved in the coastal towns and the interior *xer* religious settlements.

## Language & religion

Somali (*af Soomaali*) is a Lowland East Cushitic language with three main dialect clusters: Northern (or Common) Somali, which forms the basis of the standard written language; Benadiri, spoken on the southern coast; and Maay, spoken by the Rahanweyn of the inter-riverine south and sufficiently distinct to be treated by some linguists as a separate language. The language was written in Arabic script (*wadaad's writing*) for religious purposes from at least the 13th century, in the indigenous *Osmanya* alphabet invented by Cismaan Yuusuf Keenadiid around 1920, and, since 1972, in a Latin orthography devised by the linguist Shire Jaamac Axmed. Somalis are almost entirely Sunni Muslims of the Shafi'i school, with a long-established Sufi tradition dominated by the Qadiriyya and, from the 19th century, the Ahmadiyya and Salihiyya brotherhoods; the veneration of local saints (*awliyo*) at shrine-tombs (*qubbo*) remains widespread in the countryside despite reformist pressure. Pre-Islamic Cushitic religious substrata survive in the sky-god concept *Waaq* — preserved in place-names such as Caabudwaaq ("worshipper of Waaq") — and in the *saar* possession cult of the south.
