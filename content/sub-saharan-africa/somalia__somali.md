## Overview

The Somali are pastoral and urban people spread across the Horn of Africa, speaking a Cushitic language and practicing Sunni Islam shaped by Sufi tradition. Their culture balances nomadic camel herding — with portable houses, coiled baskets, and milk-based foodways — against a thousand years of Indian Ocean trade that created stone towns, weaving guilds, and urban song. Poetry held the place of written law until 1972, memorised and chained in verse debate across the peninsula. Islamic calendar, Sufi shrines, and older solar festivals mark the year alongside the pastoral migration cycle.

## Material culture

Somali material culture splits between portable pastoral gear engineered for camel transport and settled coastal crafts shaped by Indian Ocean trade.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Benadiri coastal weaving produces the cotton wraps and striped cloths that fed both household use and Indian Ocean trade; interior pastoralists work geometric basketry as the pattern gallery of the nomadic camp.

- **Benadiri weaving** (*Benadiri*) — A loom tradition of the coastal towns (Mogadishu, Merca, Barawa) worked by hereditary weaver communities, producing cotton cloth for domestic and export use across the Indian Ocean.
- **Alindi cloth** (*alindi*) — The best-known Benadiri product: a cotton wrapper patterned in warp-direction stripes of narrow and broad bands, produced in Merca and Mogadishu and worn as a woman's body-wrap.
- **Guntiino garment** (*guntiino*) — The older Somali woman's garment: a long rectangle of cloth wound around the torso and knotted over one shoulder, leaving the other bare.
- **Shuqqad wrap** (*shuqqad*) — The men's wrapped cloth of the peninsula, a length of cotton wound at the waist and worn plain white or narrow-striped in pastoral and coastal settings alike.
- **Somali basketry** (*basketry*) — Coiled and twined vessels and mats built from split palm leaf and grass over a grass bundle core, dyed in bands of red, black, ochre and natural straw with strictly geometric patterns in horizontal registers.

### Clothing & dress

Women wear the older wrapped guntiino or the imported dirac shift; men wear the shuqqad wrap or long khamiis with an embroidered skullcap or turban.

- **Dirac shift** (*dirac*) — A long, light voile or polyester shift worn over a full slip (gorgorad), now the standard festive garment for women.
- **Shash headscarf** (*shash*) — A headscarf tied at the nape, worn by married women to cover the hair.
- **Garbasaar shawl** (*garbasaar*) — A shawl worn over the shoulders by married women.
- **Koofiyad skullcap** (*koofiyad*) — An embroidered skullcap worn by men.
- **Billaawe dagger** (*billaawe*) — A leaf-bladed dagger sheathed at the waist on a leather belt, historically both tool and sidearm, with a hilt of horn, wood or silver.

### Architecture

The nomadic interior uses the aqal dome house built and owned by women; the agricultural riverine south uses wattle-and-daub mundul houses; coastal stone towns follow the Swahili pattern.

- **Aqal dome house** (*aqal / aqal soomaali*) — A portable, domed frame dwelling built by women: bent saplings lashed into a hemispherical armature and clad with woven mats and hides, disassembling into camel loads.
- **Reer household camp** (*reer*) — A household settlement ringed by a thorn-branch enclosure (odaa) that folds the livestock in at night.
- **Mundul house** (*mundul*) — A wattle-and-daub house with a conical thatched roof, built in the agricultural riverine south.
- **Arish house** (*arish*) — A rectangular flat-roofed house found in the riverine south.
- **Masjid al-Qiblatayn mosque** (*Masjid al-Qiblatayn*) — A two-mihrab mosque in Zeila dating to the 7th century, among the oldest mosques in Africa.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The signature object is the meerschaum incense burner carved with geometric bands; woodcarving, leatherwork and metalsmithing complete the repertoire of portable and domestic goods.

- **Dabqaad incense burner** (*dabqaad / haaqad / girgire*) — A square-footed cup cut from soft white or grey meerschaum-like stone quarried near Mogadishu, carved with incised geometric bands and used to burn frankincense and uunsi.
- **Barkin headrest** (*barkin*) — A hardwood headrest with an incised arched foot that lets a pastoralist sleep without disturbing a dressed coiffure.
- **Koor camel bell** (*koor*) — A wooden camel bell whose clapper and tone distinguish one herd from another.
- **Kitab case** (*kitab*) — A tooled leather or silver container for a Qur'anic text, the most ornamented small object in the Somali repertoire.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Somali silver is worked into filigree necklaces, bangles and amulet cases; bridal preparation centres on dense henna patterning of hands and feet applied over several evenings.

- **Xirsi or tumaar amulet** (*xirsi / tumaar*) — A cylindrical or box-shaped amulet case containing a written Qur'anic verse and worn on a cord at the neck or upper arm.
- **Cadaan henna** (*cadaan / xinne*) — Dense black or red henna patterning of hands and feet, applied over several evenings in a women's gathering before the wedding.
- **Silver filigree and granulated work** — Necklaces, heavy hollow bangles, anklets and ear ornaments worked in filigree and granulation, strung with coral, amber, carnelian and trade beads.

## Music & performance

Somali music is built on voice and rhythm; the core instrument is the kaban oud, and the defining genre is sung poetry in alliterative metres.

- **Gabay poem** (*gabay*) — The longest and most serious alliterative poetic form, used for political argument and lament.
- **Buraanbur poem** (*buraanbur*) — The women's alliterative metre, performed at weddings and namings with drum and clapping.
- **Heello song** (*heello / hees*) — A short lyric form sung to oud, drum and later full band, becoming the vehicle of nationalism and independence from the 1940s onward.
- **Kaban oud** (*kaban*) — An oud lute adopted from Arabia, central to twentieth-century urban song and the core instrument of Somali music.
- **Waaberi ensemble** (*Waaberi*) — A state radio ensemble of Mogadishu that carried Somali song to a mass audience from the nationalist period onward.

## Dance & theatre

Dhaanto is the best-known communal dance, performed in paired lines with a bouncing step imitating a camel's gait; staged musical theatre developed in twentieth-century coastal cities.

- **Dhaanto dance** (*dhaanto*) — An Ogaden-associated dance in which paired lines advance and retreat with a bouncing step imitating a camel's gait, sung antiphonally between men's and women's lines.
- **Niiko dance** (*niiko*) — A women's dance built on rapid hip and shoulder movement, performed at weddings in women's-only gatherings.
- **Ciyaar soomaali dances** (*ciyaar soomaali*) — The broader repertoire of clan and regional line dances, including the jaandheer of the northwest and shirib.
- **Riwaayad musical play** (*riwaayad*) — A twentieth-century urban theatrical form combining spoken drama with sung heello, developed in Mogadishu and Hargeisa and used for political satire and social commentary.

## Festivals & rituals

The calendar is Islamic, marked by Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha; an older solar new year festival (Dabshid) survives alongside life-cycle rites and shrine visitation.

- **Eid al-fitr** (*ciid*) — The feast closing Ramadan, marked by new clothes, henna and xalwo sweets.
- **Eid al-adha** (*ciid-adha*) — The feast bringing the sacrifice of a sheep, goat or camel and the distribution of meat, marked by new clothes and celebration.
- **Dabshid solar new year** (*dabshid / neeroosh*) — The Somali solar new year marked by bonfires, flame-leaping, and in coastal towns a mock stick-fight, following the pre-Islamic Cushitic solar calendar.
- **Magac-bixin naming ceremony** (*magac-bixin*) — A naming ceremony held on the seventh day after birth with animal sacrifice (caqiiqo) and incense burning.
- **Xeedho wedding knot-riddle** (*xeedho*) — A Darod-associated riddle-object: a tightly bound, hide-wrapped vessel of dates and ghee that the groom's party must unwrap by solving its knot, accompanied by poetic contest.

## Foodways

The pastoral base is milk and meat; the coastal and riverine regions add grains, rice, and breads fermented or layered in Indian-influenced styles.

- **Caano geel camel milk** (*caano geel / garoor*) — Fresh and soured camel milk, a pastoral staple; garoor is soured camel milk.
- **Muqmad dried meat** (*muqmad / oodkac*) — Meat cut into cubes, dried and preserved in ghee for travel — the classic pastoral provision.
- **Canjeero pancake** (*canjeero / lahoh*) — A fermented sorghum or flour pancake close to Ethiopian injera but smaller and thinner, eaten at breakfast with ghee and sugar or with stew.
- **Bariis iskukaris rice** (*bariis iskukaris*) — Rice cooked with meat stock, onion and the spice blend xawaash (cumin, coriander, cardamom, cinnamon, clove, turmeric), garnished with fried banana on the side.
- **Xalwo sweet** (*xalwo*) — A dense ghee-and-sugar confection flavoured with cardamom and studded with nuts, the obligatory celebration food.

## Oral tradition & literature

Poetry carried law, genealogy and diplomacy until 1972; alliterative metres (gabay, geeraar, jiifto, buraanbur) were memorised verbatim and answered in verse chains called silsilad.

- **Gabay alliterative poem** (*gabay*) — The longest and most serious alliterative poetic form used for political argument and lament, with lines that sustain a single consonant for hundreds of lines.
- **Silsilad verse chain** (*silsilad*) — Sustained public debates in verse in which poems were answered in kind across the peninsula; the Guba chain of the mid-twentieth century is the most famous.
- **Sheeko xariiro folktale** (*sheeko xariiro*) — Folktales told to children, with a repertoire of trickster hyena and fox tales.
- **Abtirsiinyo genealogy** (*abtirsiinyo*) — Genealogical recitation in which a person names their ancestors back twenty or more generations.
- **Sayyid Mohammed Abdullah Hassan** — A leader of the twenty-one-year Dervish resistance against the British, remembered as much for his poetry as for his campaigns.

## Language & religion

Somali is a Lowland East Cushitic language adopted in Latin script in 1972; the people are Sunni Muslim of the Shafi'i school, with Sufi orders shaping popular religion and customary law (xeer) operating in parallel with sharia.

- **Somali language** (*af-soomaali*) — A Lowland East Cushitic language of the Afroasiatic family, closest to Afar and Saho, with dialects including Northern/Common Somali, Benadiri and Maay.
- **Osmanya script** (*osmanya*) — An indigenous alphabet devised by Osman Yusuf Kenadid around 1920 for writing Somali before the 1972 Latin orthography reform.
- **Wadaad religious specialist** (*wadaad*) — A religious specialist who writes amulets, teaches Qur'an and adjudicates alongside the secular elder (oday).
- **Xeer customary law** (*xeer*) — Customary law governing compensation (diya), grazing rights and water access, operating in parallel with sharia.
- **Sufi orders** — The Qadiriyya, Ahmadiyya and Salihiyya orders shape popular religion through dhikr assemblies and shrine visitation (siyaaro) to saints' tombs.
