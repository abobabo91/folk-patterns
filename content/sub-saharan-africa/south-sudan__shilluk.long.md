## Overview

The Shilluk are a Luo Nilotic people of the northeastern Upper Nile state of South Sudan, settled along the western bank of the White Nile. They call their own language *Dhøg Cøllø* — *dhøg* being the Shilluk word for both "language" and "mouth" — a Luo language of the Western Nilotic subfamily, closely related to the *dha anywaa* of the neighbouring Anuak, with whom the Shilluk share much vocabulary and descent. They are the third-largest ethnic group in South Sudan after the Dinka and the Nuer. Most are sedentary agriculturists; cattle-raising matters to the economy as it does among Nilotic peoples generally, but agriculture and fishing weigh more heavily here than usual, and both men and women work the fields. In folk-culture terms the Shilluk are known for the kingdom they founded in 1454, for the divine royal lineage descended from the culture hero Nyikang, and for one of the earliest and fullest published bodies of African oral literature.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here — the Wikipedia articles on the Shilluk people and their language, and the British Museum catalogue records for the objects shown — describe no woven or dyed textile tradition. Pattern in the Shilluk objects held by the museum is carried instead by pottery surfaces and by shell-covered headwear, and is described below under ceramics and under jewelry and body adornment.

**Motif vocabulary.** The catalogue records name impressed string pattern, cross-hatched incised decoration, and a dot pattern filled with white; the sources give no vernacular names for these.

### Clothing & dress

What the sources document of Shilluk dress is headwear, and specifically headwear that marks rank and role. A **chief's head-dress** in the British Museum is made of giraffe mane hair strung with cowrie and conus shells. A **warrior's head-dress** is built differently: a circlet of skin carrying two rows of white cowrie shells, finished with a fringe of black human hair. A third head-dress is constructed over a base structure of rush, stitched all over with rows of cowries, and has two leather thongs attached for fastening; its body is human hair. A **head-ring or crest** of waterbuck hair is also held, its exact use uncertain in the record itself. Beyond the head, the collection holds an arm-dagger whose sheath — leather and snake skin — has a loop so the weapon can be worn against the arm, which makes it dress as much as armament. The sources describe no everyday garment, footwear or belt, and say nothing about women's dress.

### Architecture

Two photographic prints in the British Museum are the only architectural evidence in these sources. One, taken at a Shilluk village near Fashoda, shows round shelters with conical thatched roofs, and fences. The second shows a Shilluk settlement of thatched shelters together with byres — cattle housing standing among the dwellings — enclosed by a fence of wood or basketry. Round plan, conical thatch, and a fenced compound containing both people and cattle are therefore attested; the sources give no vernacular name for the house type, no account of how the walls or roof were built, and no description of architectural decoration. Nor do they describe any royal compound or shrine building, despite the political importance of the kingship discussed below.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Shilluk potters worked both vessels and pipes, and the decoration is incised rather than painted. Catalogued vessels include one with an impressed string pattern and a slip-glazed lip, and one burnished on the inner and outer mouth and outer neck with cross-hatched incising below the neck. Pipe-bowls were made in animal form: several take the shape of a hyena, and a separate pottery **figure** of a hyena is glazed dark brown with a white-filled dot pattern. Complete smoking pipes combine materials — a pottery bowl, a stem of bamboo or carved wood, and a mouthpiece of gourd, with hide stitched on by fibre to secure bowl and mouthpiece to the stem; one such bowl carries incised lines and cross-hatching picked out in white. Ironwork appears in dagger blades, one straight and one crooked, set into wooden handles. Wooden objects include oval stools carved from a solid block on four feet, ball-headed and knobbed clubs, and a club carved to a point at both ends with leather wrapped a third of the way down. Two oval shields are held, one of giraffe hide perforated for a wooden handle, one of buffalo or hippopotamus hide with a wooden staff handle. A spade made from a giraffe scapula was used for digging wells.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ivory was worked into armlets, including armlets for the upper arm. Beadwork is represented by a bracelet of string, glass beads and metal, kept as a manufacture sample, and by a necklace of forty-two thick stone disc beads — possibly quartz — threaded on vegetable-fibre cord, closed by an elaborate fibre hook and loop and finished at each end with three amber-coloured ring beads. Cowrie and conus shells are the dominant ornament in the head-dresses described above, sewn in rows across hair or skin, and human hair itself is used as a material rather than only dressed on the head. The sources do not document tattooing, scarification, body painting or hairdressing practices.

## Music & performance

The sources name no Shilluk instrument and describe no musical genre or performance occasion. Two things at the edge of music are recorded. The British Museum holds a **dance staff**, which implies dance performed with hand-held objects but is described no further. And Wilhelm Hofmayr's *Die Schilluk* prints songs in the Shilluk language, some with an accompanying musical transcription — so nineteenth- and early twentieth-century Shilluk song was written down in notation, though the sources used here do not say what those songs were about or when they were sung. Diedrich Westermann's 1912 collection likewise includes songs and prayers alongside its tales.

## Dance & theatre

Only the dance staff in the British Museum attests dance directly; the sources describe no named dance, no masking and no theatrical form. One choreographed event is documented, though as ritual rather than performance: the symbolic mock battle fought between the two families during a Shilluk wedding, described under Festivals & rituals below.

## Festivals & rituals

Marriage is the life-cycle rite the sources treat in detail, and it is organised around the transfer of bride-wealth. The ideal payment ran to roughly ten head of cattle together with several sheep — *diek nom* — and symbolic items including spears, *jam nom*; the total was negotiable against what the groom could raise. The sequence of ceremonies is fixed: the cattle are handed over, a wedding feast is held at the bride's home, the two families fight a symbolic mock battle, and the couple then moves to the groom's family compound. The bride commonly returns to her natal home to give birth to her first child, which affirms her lineage ties. In cities, above all Juba, the institution has survived in reduced form. One chief described families accepting three goats or a modest sum of cash instead of the full herd, which puts marriage within reach of young men; celebrations move into communal public space and the community contributes to the cost, so the collective character of the rite is preserved even as the payment shrinks.

The kingship carried its own ritual logic. The *reth* ruled from the divine lineage of the culture hero Nyikang, and his own health was understood to affect the health of the nation. The sources record no annual or seasonal festival calendar, and nothing of birth, initiation or funeral rites beyond the first-birth custom noted above.

## Foodways

The sources describe the Shilluk economy — sedentary agriculture, cattle-raising, and fishing more prominent than among most Nilotic groups — but name no crop, dish, cooking method, ceremonial food or drink tradition. The only meal recorded is the wedding feast held at the bride's home, whose contents are not described.

## Oral tradition & literature

Shilluk oral literature was documented unusually early and unusually fully. In 1912 Diedrich Westermann published *The Shilluk People, their Language and Folklore*, containing sixty-one tales in Shilluk with English translations, alongside prayers, songs and riddles, and a grammar of the language. Wilhelm Hofmayr's *Die Schilluk. Geschichte, Religion und Leben eines Niloten-stammes* carries a selection of more than two hundred Shilluk proverbs and riddles with German translations, plus songs, several with musical transcription.

The riddles work by describing a familiar thing as a scene and withholding its name. *"Nemei ki rei gen fa gute"* — "Brothers who never hurt each other" — is answered by the two horns of a cow. *"Nejok gwoti fen"*, "the black-white cow is making white the earth", is the moon. *"Nemei doge lun fen"*, "two brothers, their mouth is turned down", is the nose. And *"Agar agar, yat win"* — "a long row of trees full of white birds" — is the teeth, an image drawn from riverbank trees so thickly covered with snow-white birds that they look snowed upon. Nyikang, the culture hero from whose lineage the *reth* descended, stands behind the royal tradition, though the sources used here do not narrate his story.

## Language & religion

*Dhøg Cøllø* belongs to the Luo branch of the Western Nilotic subfamily of the Nilotic languages; the name "Shilluk" is itself an Arabic-derived pronunciation. The language has a rich tone system — three level tonemes (Low, Mid, High) and four contours (Rise, Fall, High Fall, Late Fall) — and distinguishes vowels by advanced versus retracted tongue root, as most Nilotic languages do. Noun number marking is famously irregular: over 140 distinct patterns have been recorded and no predictable system identified. Christian missionaries developed a Latin alphabet of 29 characters, 10 vowels and 19 consonants, in the early twentieth century. Most Shilluk are Christian; some keep the traditional religion or combine the two, and a few have become Muslim. Colonial and missionary policy split the people denominationally: Catholic mission stations at Lul, Detwoc, Tonga and Yoynyang served the Nile's western bank, while the American Inland Mission worked from Doleib Hill on the Sobat.
