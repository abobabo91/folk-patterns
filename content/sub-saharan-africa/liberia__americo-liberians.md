## Overview

Americo-Liberians are a Liberian ethnic group of African American, Afro-Caribbean, and liberated African origin. They trace their ancestry to free people of color and emancipated African Americans and West Indians who emigrated to Liberia in the nineteenth century and became founders of the state. The first black American settlers arrived in 1822, while the broader designation “Congo” also encompassed descendants of free-born and formerly enslaved African American, West Indian, recaptive, and Sierra Leone Creole settlers. Americo-Liberians settled in Monrovia, Careysburg, Clay-Ashland, Buchanan, Maryland, Mississippi-in-Africa, Cape Mount, Greenville, Crozierville, New Georgia, and towns along the St. Paul River. In 2009, their population was estimated at 150,000 within Liberia’s total population of 3.5 million.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Present-day Americo-Liberians wear both African-style and Western-style dress. The group popularized Western clothing including the top hat, tailcoat, lounge suit, and frock coat. Americo-Liberian men wore top hats, frock coats, lounge suits, and spats. Women in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries wore elaborate Victorian and Edwardian-style American dresses associated with African-American and white American communities in the southern United States.

For special occasions including weddings, parades, and presidential inaugurations, Americo-Liberians continued to wear elaborate dress while adapting it to newer Western fashions and African-style dresses. Americo-Liberian weddings follow African-American or Afro-Caribbean styles: the bridegroom wears a lounge suit and the bride wears a white wedding dress.

### Architecture

Americo-Liberian architecture developed as a fusion of antebellum architecture from the United States and the African environment of Liberia. Houses were built from weather-board or stone frame and included verandahs. Wealthier Americo-Liberians used Georgian, Neoclassical, and Greek Revival styles associated with antebellum southern plantation great houses. Churches, buildings, and homes in Americo-Liberian settlements featured American-style forms, while roads and bridges were also developed according to American models.

The homes of elites often resembled American Southern plantation homes. In Monrovia, the Grand Masonic Temple became an important building associated with Americo-Liberian political and elite networks. A marble Masonic Lodge was built in 1867 and was considered one of the city’s most impressive buildings.

## Foodways

Americo-Liberian cuisine combines African-American, Afro-Caribbean, and local indigenous Liberian rice and foofoo dishes. Americo-Liberians introduced African-American baking techniques to Liberia, and the country’s baking traditions are described as deriving from African-American immigrants.

Named foods in the sources include cornbread, fried chicken, collard greens, palm butter soup, rice, and foofoo. The cuisine combines African-American soul food with local African dishes, including palm butter soup and rice. The sources also state that Americo-Liberians preferred African-American Southern food and introduced American baking techniques.

## Language & religion

Americo-Liberians speak Liberian English and varieties including Merico and Liberian Settler English. These varieties were influenced by African-American Vernacular English, Gullah, and Barbadian Creole. The settlers introduced a form of African-American Vernacular English that influenced the pidgin English or patois already present in the region. Standard Liberian English, also called Liberian Settler English, continues to be spoken by descendants of the original settlers.

English has been the dominant and official language since the arrival of the Americo-Liberian settlers and has played a central role in education, governance, and communication. Americo-Liberians are predominantly Protestant Christians, especially Baptists and Methodists; others are Episcopalians, while a smaller minority adhere to Catholicism. Early African-American settlers practiced Christianity, sometimes combined with traditional African religious beliefs.
