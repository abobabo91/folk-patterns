## Overview

The Ibibio, also called the Ibom, are a coastal people of southern Nigeria, concentrated in Akwa Ibom State and extending into Cross River State and the eastern part of Abia State (Arochukwu and Ukwa East). Their language, *Ùsèm Ìbìbìò*, belongs to the Ibibio-Efik dialect cluster of the Cross River languages — also called Ibibioid — within the Niger-Congo family. The Annang, Ekid, Oron and Ibeno share personal names, culture and traditions with them and speak closely related, more or less mutually intelligible varieties. The Ibibio are reputed to be among the earliest ethnic groups inhabiting southern Nigeria, with an estimated arrival in their present home around 1000 BCE. In folk-culture terms they matter for two things above all: the masking societies, chiefly *Ekpo*, whose masks and costumes constitute the greatest works of art in Ibibio society, and body art, which plays a major role in their visual tradition.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources document Ibibio pattern-bearing textiles mainly through the museum objects themselves rather than through named weaving traditions.

**Raffia mats** — Mats woven as a single length of plain hand-woven raffia carrying a band of supplementary-weft geometric decoration dyed dark brown, finished with a fringe of unworked warps. A plaited variant in black, orange and natural colour is also held.

**Plaited-cord and crocheted masquerade costumes** — Full-body garments of crocheted vegetable-fibre string, striped horizontally in black, in red and black, or in bands of black alternating with the natural fibre colour, with raffia fringes at wrists and ankles and a rear slit to admit the wearer. One is recorded as used by a male secret society.

**Bast cloth (*basttyg*)** — Lengths of multicoloured bast textile from the Old Calabar–Bonny area, one indigo-dyed and recorded as a hip cloth, 113 and 131 cm long.

**Shrine cloth** — A composite textile of dyed red, black and bleached white machine-woven cotton together with printed machine-woven cotton, sewn into a top panel bearing two appliqué human figures — one holding a closed parasol, one a sword — above rows of triangles in dyed and printed cloth, left unhemmed.

**Motif vocabulary.** Supplementary-weft geometric bands; appliqué human figures with parasol and sword; rows of triangles; rouletted and circular pottery decoration; inverted S motifs in relief; trefoil and floral pokerwork on calabash; the dot, described by Okon Umetuk as the only perfect mark to indicate and summarize beauty in Ibibio culture; the X applied to forehead, wrists and ankles as a mark of mediation.

### Clothing & dress

Photographs in the sources show an adult male in a patterned waisted wrapper with an item of personal ornamentation at the neck and a sheathed blade on a leather belt, and a chief in a robe and head-gear flanked by rows of men and boys in head-gear, shirts and cloths wrapped around the lower body; a woman making pottery wears a cloth around the lower body. Ceremonial dress is sharply distinct. *Ekpo* performers wear a face mask with waist-length raffia attached, secured to the head with a rope, over a knee-length raffia skirt, with lower legs, arms and hands painted with charcoal. Men's costumes incorporate materials from the wilderness — raffia and seed-pod rattles — while women's costumes use light-coloured cloth to represent the order of village living. Collections hold a plaited string jacket with sleeves and a fibre fringe along the lower edge and sleeve ends, and a composite dance cap combining a plaited raffia cap, a leather face-piece with the eyes ringed in white buttons, and a shock of raffia hair and fringe. Head-rings are worn: leather-bound and flattened in section, one bound with snake or lizard skin; hide circlets with rows of projecting knobs; and *Idiong* head-rings of hide and silk, or of wood covered with skin.

### Architecture

The sources say little about Ibibio house form, materials or roofing. What they describe is the sacred landscape rather than the dwelling: scattered through each village were *akai*, sacred forest lands that no one was permitted to clear for cultivation. Burial grounds, the shrines of village deities and the spots reserved for the secret societies — *Ekpo Onyoho*, *Ekoong*, *Idiong*, *Ekong* — were all sacred, as was everything in them. Non-members could not enter a society's *akai* even to gather firewood, sticks, fruits such as *mkpook*, vegetables such as *afang* and *odusa*, or snails, or to hunt; the ban existed so that outsiders would not discover the society's secrets or desecrate ancestral graves. One photograph records the interior of an Ibibio building, possibly a shrine, containing a chair made of *offolow*, figures, calabashes and a bundle of animal skulls hanging from a pole. Masks are stored in the off-season in a family or village shrine.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Ibibio are known for woodcarving, raffia-weaving and pottery. Pottery in the collections includes spherical and rouletted bowls, a child's soup bowl with rouletted and circular decoration, bottle vases with grooved necks, ridges and raised knobs, loop-handled bowls with punctate or longitudinal striations on the handles, and a small dark-brown vessel with two contiguous cylindrical spouts, conical projections above the shoulder and inverted S motifs in relief. Photographs show a woman squatting on the ground shaping a pot by hand. Calabash work includes a hinged-lid bowl with a cut-and-burnt trefoil pattern hinged with scarlet worsted, an elongated vessel with floral pokerwork, a ladle, and a gourd gunpowder container with a carved stopper and a leather pouch. Wooden objects include carved spoons, a quadrangular stool on a circular pierced pedestal with incised flower and leaf motifs, and a nailed five-piece box with fretwork cresting and engraved decoration. Ikot Ekpene, a town known for its marketplace, sells crafts to tourists and middle-class Nigerians, and many carvers of *Ekpo* masks live there. The Ibibio are not known for metalworking, though a significant number of craftspeople make it; most metal objects are practical rather than decorative. Ibibio coffins, by contrast, are highly decorative, with ornamental painted metal motifs, coloured plastic sheets and glass side panels. Ibibio women of Calabar decorated imported Birmingham brass trays in repoussé by hand with nails — *nlonga*.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Both temporary and permanent body modification are used, with rhythm and nature as governing motifs; hairdressing, body painting and body modification are the main focus, and the intentional fattening of young women is culturally important. *Odung* is the body painting used for marriage, childbirth, coming-of-age and death, and can also mark a man's status in the *Ekpo* society; it is most common among women, often applied after childbirth, takes five to eight hours and is normally done by professionals, leaving stains that last up to three months. *Iduot* symbolizes fertility: crushed camwood is added to water to make a red pigment applied to palms, feet, legs and faces, used partly for its bleaching effect, which with continued use produces a smooth, light complexion. Eye shadow, lipstick and eyeliner now serve the same expressive purpose. Collections hold a leather-bound bracelet stitched around the perimeter, bound at three points in brown leather, with a protuberance perhaps containing medicine.

## Music & performance

Ibibio instruments in the collections are percussion, idiophones and horns. A composite drum has a skin head lashed with vegetable fibre to a cylindrical carved wood body whose lower part takes the form of a human head supported on a four-legged stool; another drum stands on three feet with striations over the lower half of the body. A carved wood double rattle joins two open-ended boxes in a dumb-bell form, one containing four slivers of wood suspended on a wire and the other five, acting as clappers. There is a wooden double bell with pokerwork decoration and a peg through the handle; clapperless iron gongs of welded construction with three projecting prongs or tripartite upturned corners; a clapperless leaded-bronze bell, lost-wax cast in an inverted tulip form with a looped strap handle, its front decorated with low-relief segmented vertical and X-shaped lines separated across the waist by a line of three tightly coiled spirals; a musical horn made from a twisted horn expanding near the tip; and a musical bow of wood with cane string wound at both ends and ornamental feather tufts bound at intervals with vegetable stalks. A wooden rattle-staff, black and alternately expanding and contracting with a knobbed top, carries a loose bundle of wooden rattles and animal skulls bound with cane two-thirds of the way down. Documentation projects at ELAR cover dirge songs and drum language in Ibibio traditional ceremonies.

## Dance & theatre

Masking is the Ibibio performance tradition the sources document. *Ekpo* masks were used to elicit fear and execute social control; to put on one is to surrender earthly identity and assume an ancestral one, and masks may be those owned by deceased ancestors, made to resemble ancestors, or made to resemble village heroes. Many are carved from a lightweight wood called *ukot*, the palm wine tree, which makes them easier to wear and move in; a horizontal wooden bar may be inserted for the wearer to bite. New raffia is added each season. Male and female masks are contrasted by colour: feminine masks use light colours such as white with delicate features, masculine ones dark colours representing the mystic forces of the forest, with large features deliberately made ugly through bulging eyes or misplaced mouths, and deformities drawn from human disease — gangosa, a form of yaws, is often depicted, along with baldness and walking sticks as symbols of karma and old age. An articulated lower jaw appears frequently. The collections also hold an *Ogbom* dance crest fragment and a wooden dance headdress.

## Festivals & rituals

There is no annual festival calendar in the sources, but the *Ekpo* season structures the year: from June through December the society plays a large role in community life, and on days when the masks are out farming, shopping and the fetching of food and water are prohibited, while crimes carry heavier consequences. Punishments are lighter today; in the 1940s a person caught stealing during the *Ekpo* season would be killed by members. The society is called secret although its purpose and activities are widely known — the true secrets are a series of code words and dance steps taught to initiates, which allow members to travel freely during the season; travelling without them results in arrest. Membership is open to all Ibibio males, but wealth is needed to reach the politically influential grades. Masked *Ekpo* or *Obon* members enforce the decisions of the *Obong Ikpaisong*, acting as messengers of the spirits and as the community's military and police. Religious practice centres on libation, sacrifice, worship, consultation and invocation of *Abasi Enyong* (God of Heaven), *Abasi Isong* (God of the Earth) and *Abasi Ibom* (the Supreme Being), and on the *mme Ndem*, spiritual entities of the Ibibio divisions — *Atakpo Ndem Uruan Inyang*, *Etefia Ikono*, *Awa Itam* — served by temple chief priests and priestesses. The *Mbia Idiong* diviners diagnose what a person did in a previous incarnation and prescribe the sacrifices that can reverse a destiny (*essien emana* or *uwa*). Before the gradual British abolition of slavery from the mid-1880s, slaves were used in regular ritual human sacrifice and, through the mid-19th century, were killed in hundreds at the death of important chiefs.

## Foodways

The sources name forest foods gathered from the *akai* — fruits such as *mkpook*, vegetables such as *afang* and *odusa*, and snails — and record a child's soup bowl among the pottery.

## Oral tradition & literature

Proverbs are the documented verbal genre. *The Sayings of the Wise: Ibibio Proverbs and Idioms* by Anietie Akpabio, published in 1899, records among others "Eto keet isikabake akai" — one tree does not make a forest; "Ekpo ufɔk ɔkɔbɔ owo" — trouble often begins at home; "Ikpat eka unen isiwotdo nditɔ" — a hen's feet cannot kill the chickens, meaning a mother's actions are never meant to harm her children; "Idop, idop ewa, enye ata ɔkpɔ unam" — it is a quiet dog that eats the fattest bone; and "Ofum ese ekpep eto unek" — the wind teaches the tree how to dance. Naming is itself a literary practice: names are given for the circumstances of birth, family history, and the qualities parents hope to impart, from birth-order terms (*Akpan*, first son; *Adiaha*, first daughter) to *Iniobong*, "God's time", and *Emem*, "peace". Preservation work includes the ELAR documentation of dirge songs and of drum language in Ibibio ceremonies, and the Ibibio kasahorow dictionary, books and proverb resources.

## Language & religion

Ibibio is a tonal language with five phonemic tones — high, mid, rising, falling and low — SVO word order, rich pronoun markers and complex tense/aspect conjugation, and a base-20 numeral system for numbers up to 100. In pre-colonial times the Ibibio used *Nsibidi* ideograms, as did the Igbo, Efik, Anaang and Ejagham; a carved wooden board in the collections is incised on both sides with what is recorded as Nsibidi script or symbols. The language has influenced Afro-American diasporic speech: AAVE *buckra* and Afro-Cuban *abakua* derive from Ibibio *mbakara*. Christianity arrived through nineteenth-century missionaries — Samuel Bill began at Ibeno and founded the Qua Iboe Church — and the Ibibio are today predominantly Christian; the first Ibibio Bible, *Nwed Abasi ke Iko Ibibio Ayo Mfin*, was presented at Ibom Hall in Uyo on 27 August 2020.
