## Overview

The Luhya — also written Luyia or Abaluhya — are a Bantu-speaking people of the fertile Lake Victoria basin in western Kenya, with related populations across the border in eastern Uganda and in northern Tanzania. They are Kenya's second-largest ethnic group, counted at 6,823,482 in the 2019 census, roughly 14% of the national population. Rather than one tribe, they form what the sources call an "ethnolinguistic constellation" of more than twenty related subgroups — Bukusu, Maragoli, Idakho, Isukha, Wanga, Samia, Tiriki, Kabras, Tachoni and others — each with its own dialect and clan system inside a shared identity. Their settlement area runs along the northern and western slopes of Mount Elgon, the middle Nzoia River and the lowlands of present-day Kakamega, Vihiga and Busia. For folk culture the interest lies in iron, hide, pottery and basketry work documented in museum collections, and in the funeral music that colonial-era photographs record.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here do not describe any woven or dyed cloth tradition for the Luhya. What they do document is worked hide and plant fibre, which carry the same load that cloth does elsewhere.

**Segmented antelope-skin cloak** — A man's cloak in the British Museum is made of six separate pieces of antelope-skin leather sewn together, so the garment's surface is articulated by its seams rather than by pattern applied to a single hide. Photographs from the same collection show Maragoli men wearing animal hides in the fields.

**Banana-fibre and banana-bark work** — Banana plants supply both string and sheet material. A woman's girdle strings iron beads onto banana fibre; a ring-shaped head-pad of banana bark serves both to cushion a pot carried on the head and to stand the pot on when it is set down.

**Split palm-stem basketry** — A basket with a handle is made from split palm stem. Grass is worked separately into fish traps, two of which are in the collection.

**Cow-dung basket lining** — A photograph records a Maragoli woman smearing the inside of a basket with cow dung, which she holds in a pot or calabash, with four large baskets and a pot set out in front of her — a sealing treatment that turns an open weave into a container for loose or wet contents.

**Fibre mask** — A mask made of fibre is finished with a fringe of long twisted strings.

### Clothing & dress

The catalogue records dress mainly at the two extremes of hide and imported cloth. Men wore leather: the six-panel antelope-skin cloak above, and in photographs animal hides worn while drying sorghum. Women in the photographs wear dresses, one of them with a shaved head. Body ornament did much of the work of dress — a woman's waist girdle of iron beads threaded on a string, a second girdle of iron beads on banana fibre, and a woman's necklace built on a leather thong hung with a knife, rings, china beads and glass beads, so that tool and ornament sit on the same cord.

Muslim dress appears at the Wanga court. A full-length portrait identifies Bakairi, a Wanga elder, in a turban, robes and shoes, photographed outside a thatched building. By the time of other photographs in the same series, Kitosh men building a house are described as wearing American clothing, and a man identified as a Luyia "magician" wears cloth rather than hide while smoking a pipe.

### Architecture

The one direct record is a photograph of two Kitosh men constructing the frame of a dwelling out of branches and sticks — a framed, not walled-up-first, building sequence, photographed in a rural setting. A thatched roof appears in the background of the portrait of the Wanga elder Bakairi. Beyond the homestead, one built form is agricultural rather than residential: a Maragoli **quail pole**, photographed as a tall pole standing above the bush with baskets hanging from it. Grain is processed in the open air — sorghum heads are spread in a circle on the ground to dry, and girls winnow millet by pouring it from their hands into a basket. The sources do not otherwise describe house plan, wall construction or ornament, and say nothing about how homesteads were laid out.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery in the collection is functional and specialised by contents: a bowl for cooking vegetables and meat, a beer vessel, a pipe-bowl, and a plate used as a turntable — that is, as a potter's own working surface. Wood supplies a man's four-legged stool and a spoon for stirring porridge. A snuff-box is made from a blackened horn with a leather strap.

Iron working, which the sources describe as well developed before colonial rule, dominates the rest. Weapons include spears with socketed iron heads on wooden shafts, a sword with an iron blade and wooden handle, and reed-shafted arrows with tanged iron heads — one barbed — bound with vegetable fibre. A harvesting knife has a short wooden handle sheathed in goat skin. Defence is hide: a shield of thick buffalo hide with a carved wooden stem down the centre, the hide lashed to it with thongs so that a gap is left for the hand, and a small leather loop at the top for hanging. One iron hoe blade is catalogued as having circulated as currency, worth one dollar.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Luhya ornament as collected is overwhelmingly iron. The British Museum holds an iron neck ring, an armlet of coiled iron wire, an iron wristlet, a finger ring of coiled flat iron wire, and an unfinished bangle — a curved iron bar ornamented along its surface, caught mid-manufacture. Iron beads are strung for women's waist girdles, on plain string in one case and on banana fibre in another. Imported material enters alongside it: the leather-thong necklace carries china and glass beads with a small knife and rings.

Two pieces alter the body itself or are worn on the head. A lip-plug is cut from a cylinder of quartz, rounded at one end and tapering to a slight point at the other. A composite head ornament, catalogued as possibly a razor, mounts a crescent-shaped iron blade on a thong of leather and hair. Cattle are adorned too — an iron ornament hangs from a cow's neck, and a cattle bell of iron hangs on a leather strap. A photograph shows a woman with a shaved head.

## Music & performance

Two stringed instruments and one bell are all the sources give. Both strings are zithers. The first is described as a reed "harp" of seventeen thin reeds. The second is a composite instrument of hide and canes bound together with vegetable-fibre string into a rectangular board, its strings formed of strips of cane "bark" lifted free of the board itself — so the sounding strings and the body are one material, separated rather than attached — with three iron rings fixed at one end. The iron cattle bell with its leather strap belongs to herding rather than to performance, but it is the collection's third sounding object.

The performance context the sources name is a funeral. A photograph records a band of Luyia men playing at a funeral dance, with a crowd of men, women and children standing behind the musicians, outdoors among trees and buildings. This places instrumental ensemble music at mortuary rites rather than at a court or a festival stage, and shows it as a whole-community gathering rather than a specialists' performance. The sources name no song genres, no other performance occasions, and no individual musicians.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name one dance occasion — the funeral dance photographed with its band of musicians — and give no name for the dance, no description of its steps or formation, and no other dramatic tradition. The fibre mask with its fringe of long twisted strings is in the collection but is not linked in the record to any performance.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources give no annual festival calendar for the Luhya. What they document instead is religious change and ritual specialism. Precolonial belief centred on Nyasaye/Were, on ancestral spirits, and on ritual specialists, who together with councils of elders and lineage leaders held authority in the small clan-based polities that made up most of Luhya society. A photograph in the British Museum shows a man identified as a Luyia "magician" standing in a field, wearing cloth, smoking a pipe, and holding a pot and what appears to be a bunch of ears of grain.

Christian missions arrived from the first decade of the twentieth century — the Friends Africa Mission, the Church of God, the Anglicans and the Roman Catholic Church, with centres at Kaimosi, Bunyore and Mumias — and conversion intersected with those existing beliefs rather than simply replacing them. The Quaker missionary Arthur Chilson, who began the Friends Church at Kaimosi in Tiriki, took the local name Shikanga as he worked in Kabras. The most explicit fusion was the **Dini ya Msambwa** movement, led among the Bukusu by Elijah Masinde in the 1940s and 1950s, which combined Christian and older elements with anti-colonial protest and was repressed by the colonial state. Clans regulated marriage, land rights and ritual obligations; among the Banyala the rule was that no one married within their own sub-clan. Funerals are the one life-cycle rite the sources describe as an occasion for music and mass attendance.

## Foodways

Cultivation combined shifting agriculture with herding and hunting. The crops named are sorghum, finger millet, cowpeas and bananas; after independence, smallholder sugarcane and maize farming became associated with the region. Photographs show the processing stages directly: sorghum heads spread in a circle on the ground to dry, watched over by two Maragoli men, and two girls sitting on the ground with baskets, one winnowing millet by pouring it from her hands into the basket.

The cooking vessels name the dishes obliquely but precisely. A pottery bowl is catalogued specifically for cooking vegetables and meat, and a wooden spoon for stirring porridge — the grain staple in its cooked form. A separate pottery vessel is made for beer, giving fermentation its own dedicated ware. The quail pole photographed in Maragoli country, with baskets hung from it, belongs to catching birds. Tobacco has two forms of equipment: a pottery pipe-bowl and a snuff-box made from a black horn with a leather strap for carrying. The sources name no individual named dish, no ceremonial food, and no dietary rule, though they do record Muslim dress at the Wanga court and the presence of Arab and Swahili traders in Wanga and Samia country from the mid-nineteenth century.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe no epic, folktale cycle or riddling tradition, but they do show genealogy working as history. Clans trace their origins to legendary ancestors, migration leaders, or occupational specialisations, and those names are remembered in sequence: the Marachi take their name from Marachi, son of Musebe, son of Sirikwa, and the sub-clans owed allegiance to Ng'ono Mwami, of the Ababere lineage. Isukha leadership is recalled through Nkotsi Liyami Makaka Shitsuxane of the Mahalia clan, son of Nkotsi Lijoodi Makaka, son of Nkotsi Liyami Makaka Muchina, with *Nkotsi* (also Ngochi, Nkochi, Nkozi) the title of a sub-tribal king. The Kabras name is glossed from *Avalasi*, "warriors" or "mighty hunters", and the ethnonym *Abaluhya* itself is explained as "people of the north" or "those of the same hearth". Naming also absorbed events: Luhya children born around the Second World War were called Keyah, after KAR, the King's African Rifles.

## Language & religion

The Luhya speak a cluster of Bantu languages known collectively as Luhya or *OluLuyia*, treated by linguists as related but distinct tongues — Lubukusu, Lulogooli, Lunyore, Lusamia, Lwisukha, Lwidakho, Lunyala, Lukabarasi, Lutachoni, Lumarama, Olutsotso, Oluwanga and others. Shared features are noun classes, extensive verbal morphology and the prefixes *aba-*/*ava-* for people and *olu-* for languages, so that an individual is *OmuLuyia*, the people *AbaLuyia*, and the homeland *EbuLuyia*. Bukusu is ethnically Luhya but its dialect is a variety of Masaba. Most Luhya are multilingual in one or more Luhya varieties plus Swahili and English. Religion today is largely Christian, layered over belief in Nyasaye/Were and ancestral spirits, with Islam present historically at the Wanga court.
