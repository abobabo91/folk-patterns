## Overview

The Ewe live along the coast and south of the Volta River in West Africa. Their culture is known for narrow-strip weaving, complex drum music, and dances linked to war, hunting, funerals, and social life. Ewe is a Gbe language with many regional varieties. Traditional religion is Vodun, alongside widespread Christianity.

## Material culture

Ewe material culture is especially known for narrow woven strips joined into colourful wrappers, cloths, blankets, rugs, and ceremonial decorations.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Ewe weaving uses narrow strips with varied colours, stripes, blocks, bands, and figurative patterns.

- **Strip weaving** — Small looms make long, narrow cloth strips that can become robes, blankets, rugs, and ceremonial decorations.
- **Ewe kente** — Men’s wrappers are made from narrow hand-sewn strips with stripes, geometric patterns, and figurative designs.
- **Keta cloth** — Keta cloth uses hand-woven cotton strips sewn together with alternating warp patterns, blocks, and weft-float designs.
- **Anlo Ewe wrapper** (*Anlo Ewe*) — This woman’s wrapper uses twelve narrow cotton strips with blue, red, orange, turquoise, and patterned sections.
- **Ewe cloth** — One man’s wrap-around garment uses twenty-one narrow strips in many colours, including tweed-like stripes made by twisting yellow and blue threads.

### Clothing & dress

Ewe men and women wear woven wrappers and wrap-around cloths, sometimes sewn into simple sleeveless tunics.

- **Woman’s wrapper** — Women’s wrappers may use narrow or wide strips, indigo cotton, bright yarns, supplementary weft motifs, braiding, and fringes.
- **Man’s wrapper** — Men’s wrappers use strip-woven cloth, hand-sewn hems, supplementary patterns, and fringes.
- **Sleeveless tunic** — One man’s cloth was folded and sewn at the sides, leaving openings for the head and arms.
- **Wrist ornament** — A photograph shows an adult man wearing a wrist ornament with a cloth, shirt, and staff.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources give little information about Ewe household objects, but they mention wooden and metal parts of musical instruments.

- **Drum frame** — Some drums are constructed with wooden staves and metal rings.
- **Bell** — Bells are played with sticks and can provide rhythmic patterns.

## Music & performance

Ewe music is mainly percussion-based, rhythmically complex, and closely connected with dance, work, play, and religion.

- **Atoke** — The atoke is a high-pitched gong played with an iron rod.
- **Double bell** (*gankogui*) — The gankogui is a clapperless double bell played with a stick.
- **Beaded gourd rattle** (*axatse*) — The axatse is a beaded gourd rattle struck against the knee and with the free hand.
- **Drum orchestra** — The drum ensemble includes the atsimevu, sogo, kidi, and kagan, with the atsimevu leading.
- **War music** (*Agbekor*) — Agbekor is associated with songs and music about war.

## Dance & theatre

Ewe dances include war, hunting, women’s, social, and entertainment dances with strong links between movement and drumming.

- **Hunting dance** (*Adevu*) — Adevu is a professional hunting dance meant to help hunters catch animals and give an animal a ritual funeral.
- **Peace dance** (*Agbadza*) — Agbadza was once a war dance but is now used socially and recreationally to celebrate peace.
- **War dance** (*Atsiagbekor*) — Atsiagbekor is a modern form of Atamga, performed in platoon formation for social gatherings and cultural presentations.
- **Women’s display dance** (*Atsia*) — Atsia is mostly performed by women, with movements directed by the lead drummer.
- **Social dance** (*Bobobo*) — Bobobo developed from Wusuta and Highlife music and is performed at funerals and other social occasions.

## Festivals & rituals

Ewe ritual life includes shrine music, ancestor communication, funerals, naming ceremonies, and festivals linked to war dances.

- **Asafotu festival** (*Asafotu*) — The Tongu people celebrate Asafotu every year around December, and Gbedzimido is performed there.
- **Ancestor drum music** (*Tro-u*) — Tro-u invites ancestors to sacred occasions at a shrine, sometimes with a priest or priestess present.
- **Spiritual marriage** (*Si*) — Si describes a spiritual marriage between a deity and a faithful person.
- **Naming ceremony** — A final name is given at a naming ceremony seven days after birth.
- **Yewe cult** (*Yewe*) — The cult of Yewe is linked with sacred music, and Yewe is described as the God of Thunder and lightning.

## Foodways

The sources mention farming and trade in rice, cotton, palm oil, and copra.

- **Rice farming** — Rice farming was historically important to the Ewe.
- **Palm oil** — Palm oil was a documented Ewe economic product.
- **Copra** — Copra was produced and exported by the Ewe.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ewe oral tradition preserves migration stories, historical figures, proverbs, and written works in the Ewe language.

- **Migration tradition** — Ewe oral tradition recalls migration from Ketu or Amedzofe.
- **Story of Agɔ Akɔli** (*Agɔ Akɔli*) — The story of the brutal king Agɔ Akɔli of Notsie is linked to the escape and dispersal of the Ewe.
- **Baobab proverb** — A traditional proverb compares knowledge to a baobab tree that no one can fully encircle with their hands.
- **The Tragedy of Agbezuge** (*Amegbetɔa alo Agbezuge ƒe Ŋutinya*) — Sam J. Obianim’s work is identified as the first novel written in Ewe.
- **The Fifth landing stage** — Ferdinand Kwasi Fiawoo’s play depicts part of Ewe history.

## Language & religion

Ewe is a tonal Gbe language, while Vodun remains the traditional religion alongside widespread Christianity.

- **Ewe language** (*Eʋe*) — Ewe, also written Evhe or Eʋe, is spoken mainly in Ghana and Togo and uses the African Reference Alphabet in writing.
- **Coastal dialects** (*Aŋlɔ*) — Coastal or southern varieties include Aŋlɔ, Tɔŋu, Avenor, Dzodze, and Watsyi.
- **Inland varieties** (*Ewedomegbe*) — Inland varieties called Ewedomegbe include Ho, Kpando, Peki, Danyi, and Kpele.
- **Creator God** (*Mawu*) — Mawu is the creator God in Vodun.
- **Lesser deities** (*trɔwo*) — The trɔwo are lesser deities created by Mawu.
