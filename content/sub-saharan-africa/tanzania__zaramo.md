## Overview

The Zaramo, also called Dzalamo or Saramo, are a Bantu ethnic group of Tanzania’s central eastern coast, especially the Dar es Salaam and Pwani regions. They are described as the largest ethnic group in and around Dar es Salaam. The supplied sources give different population figures: one estimates about 0.7 million people, while the article on the Zaramo language gives an ethnic population of about 200,000 and states that only a few elderly speakers remain. Zaramo belongs to the Niger-Congo language family and is critically endangered. Most Zaramo people speak Swahili as their first language. Zaramo culture combines coastal Islamic influences with older practices involving initiation, fertility, ancestors, farming, pottery, basketry, music, and dance. Singeli is identified as a musical genre originating from their community in Dar es Salaam.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that Zaramo people have borrowed forms of dress from general Swahili and Arab culture, including the wearing of a skull cap. They also describe Islamic festivals and Muslim observances as part of Zaramo life. The sources therefore do not permit a fuller account of everyday and ritual dress.

### Architecture

Zaramo settlements include *pangone*, described as shanty clusters of villages. During the period of population growth in Dar es Salaam, many Zaramo settlements, particularly Buguruni, were absorbed into expanding shanty cities established by immigrants. The sources also describe stockade-fortified villages developed in response to slave raids and slave trading. Villagers could leave the coast, return during the daytime to farm and fish, and use fortified settlements for protection. No detailed house plan, roofing material, courtyard form, or decorative architectural system is documented.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Zaramo pottery consists mainly of ceramic water jars, cooking pots, and dishes. General cooking pots are called *chungu*. Dishes used for frying are called *kaango* or *kikaango*, depending on size, while smaller bowls are *bakuli*. Shallow dishes placed on shrine pedestals for incense offerings are called *chetezo* or *kitezo*. Large water vessels are *mtungi*, used for bathing and drinking. *Funiko* are lids.

Potters generally use pinch-pot techniques for smaller vessels and coiling for larger ones. A pottery wheel is not used. Vessels dry for two to seven days and are fired on a wood fire for two to three hours. Bodies are burnished with seeds, metal, stones, or shells. Red and black pigments are applied after firing. Incised decorations are called *marembo* or *mapambo*, and *biiki* are comb-like tools used to make parallel lines.

Mwana hiti figures are usually carved from wood, although some Zaramo traditions prescribe gourds as symbols of fertility. They are often cylindrical, with a head and torso, and commonly lack arms, legs, and genitalia. Wooden staffs also serve as walking supports, ritual aids, symbols of authority, and representations of power. *Kifimbo* staffs are small symbolic staffs associated mainly with military authority, while *kome* staffs are tall blackwood staffs associated with chiefly power.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Mwana hiti figures can be decorated with metal when hair is represented, or with white beads used as jewelry. They do describe the care of the mwana hiti during female initiation: the initiate bathes it, oils it, dresses its hair in the same style she wears, and feeds it. These actions form part of the figure’s ritual and educational function rather than a documented jewelry tradition.

## Music & performance

The sources identify singeli as a musical genre originating in the Zaramo community in Dar es Salaam. The sources do, however, record several dances connected with initiation.

After male initiates have completed their period in the initiation hut, their mothers hold a village dance called *mbiga*. The initiates later return to the village and celebrate their emergence with *mlao*, a dance marking their recognition as men of society. Female initiation concludes with an *mbwelo* dance after the initiate has been returned to her family.

Mwana hiti objects can occur in forms other than figures, including walking sticks, staffs, stools, musical instruments, and grave posts.

## Dance & theatre

Zaramo initiation includes named dances with distinct ritual functions. *Mbiga* is held by the mothers of male initiates after the initiates have completed their seclusion and are allowed to bathe again. *Mlao* is a dance of emergence that follows the return of the male initiates to the village. Female initiation concludes with *mbwelo*, a dance celebrating the initiate’s return to her family after seclusion and instruction. They mention *mtoto wa bandia*, wooden puppet-like grave markers used as mnemonic honorary devices.

## Festivals & rituals

They describe Islamic festivals and Muslim observances as influences borrowed from Swahili and Arab culture.

The best-documented ritual cycle is initiation. Male initiation is called *nhulu*, meaning “growth.” It takes place during the dry season and occurs about once every three years. Each novice, *mwali*, has an instructor called *mhunga*, who guides the youth through circumcision and teaches Zaramo sex lore and practice. After circumcision, the initiates stay in a *kumbi*, or initiation hut, where they are taught and are not permitted to bathe for two weeks. After the later village celebrations, the instructors burn the kumbi and objects associated with initiation.

Female initiation begins with a girl’s first menses. The girl, also called *mwali*, is secluded in her mother’s house for anywhere from two weeks to one year. Her paternal aunt generally serves as *shangazi*, taking responsibility for teaching and ceremonies. During seclusion, the initiate learns housekeeping, childcare, sexual and moral behavior, and mature interaction in society. She is then carried to a *mkole* tree, where she is circumcised by an operator called *mnhunga*, and is later celebrated with mbwelo.

Funeral rites are called *tambiko*. The family cleans the grave and offers food and drink to one another and to the deceased. A temporary hut may be built around the grave as a shrine. The sources state that Zaramo people regard the dead with respect and reverence.

## Foodways

Zaramo are settled farmers who also keep livestock. Their staple foods include rice, millet, maize, sorghum, and cassava. Cash crops include coconuts, legumes, cashews, pineapples, oranges, and bananas. Cassava is important because it can grow with very little rain. Coastal Zaramo also fish for personal consumption and trade.

The supplied sources document pottery used in food preparation and serving. *Chungu* are cooking pots. *Kaango* and *kikaango* are dishes used for frying, with the distinction depending on size, and *bakuli* are smaller bowls. *Mtungi* are large vessels used to hold water for bathing and drinking; a household generally has two, one for each use. Everyday pottery can also be used in ceremonies, although many ceremonies require a new vessel.

The sources mention ceremonial mwali bathing, healing rituals, grave offerings, and incense offerings in relation to pottery. They do state that food and drink are offered during tambiko funeral rites.

## Oral tradition & literature

Zaramo origins are explained in an oral tradition concerning the Shomvi people and the warrior-hero *Pazi*. In the early 19th century, an offshoot group of Kamba people from Kenya attacked the Shomvi, who sought Pazi’s help. After defeating the Kamba, Pazi demanded salt, cloth, and other luxuries. When the Shomvi could not provide them, they offered Pazi and his family residence on the coast in exchange for an annual tribute. The war and its aftermath were said to have founded the Zaramo.

The sources also state that the Zaramo language has very few translations. A list of Zaramo words and phrases was provided by a native speaker for *Short Specimens of the Vocabularies of Three Unpublished African Languages: Gindo, Zaramo, and Angazidja*. The publication of the New Testament from 1975 is also mentioned.

## Language & religion

Zaramo, also called Zalamo, Kizaramo, Dzalamo, Zaramu, Saramo, and Myagatwa, is a Bantu language in the Niger-Congo family. It is classified within the Atlantic-Congo, Volta-Congo, Benue-Congo, Bantoid, Southern, Narrow Bantu, Central, G, and Zigula-Zaramo groupings. The language is critically endangered, with only a few elderly speakers remaining. Most Zaramo people speak Swahili as their first language, and contemporary Zaramo people have adopted Swahili-Arabic names. Zaramo uses Latin script. The supplied sources state that there are no known dialects.

More than 98% of Zaramo people are described as Muslims, specifically of the Shafi‘i school of Sunni Islam. Conversion among coastal Zaramo began in the 19th century, and by 1913 Muslims made up half of the Zaramo population. Islamic practice exists alongside older traditions, including matrilineal kinship, female rituals, the Kolelo fertility cult, worship of Mulungu, the Kubandwa Cult, the Uwuxala Society, and the work of the *mganga*, a medicine man, healer, or diviner. Ancestors are called *mizimu*, and their perceived influence is connected with illness, death, infertility, and poor agriculture.
