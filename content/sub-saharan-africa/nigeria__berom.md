## Overview

The Berom are a large people of the Jos Plateau in central Nigeria. Their culture centers on farming and hunting, reflected in their festival calendar and in personal names drawn from game animals. Most are Christian today, though they were historically linked to the Nok culture. Their material culture is especially well-documented in music and dance equipment.

## Material culture

Berom material culture is best known through their musical instruments, dance costumes, and weapons, with plant-fibre work central to body covering and ornament.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Berom cloth and fibre work centers on colour symbolism and plaited or bunched plant material rather than weaving or dyeing techniques.

- **Green-and-maroon Berom cloth** — The cloth worn as Berom dress carries two colours read as emblems: green for the cactus that grows in every Berom locality, and maroon for a local powder rubbed on the forehead for protection and on the body to relieve pain.
- **Shang** — A fibre buttock ornament used as dance equipment.
- **Plaited grass and leaf sheaths** — Ceremonial penis sheaths made either of plaited grass or of leaves, placing plant-fibre plaiting at the centre of the documented body-covering repertoire.
- **Basketry hat** — A basket-work hat collected in 1946, alongside the dance hat.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday and ceremonial dress divides along lines of sex and occasion, with vegetable-fibre and grass garments, cloth in green-and-maroon colours, and various head coverings.

- **Buttock garment** — Women's dress of vegetable fibre or grass gathered and tied with string, sometimes with a bunch of leaves at the front.
- **Penis sheath** — Men's and boys' dress made of leaves or plaited grass, with plaited examples specified as ceremonial.
- **Dance hat** (*bong*) — A head covering worn in dance costume.
- **Fly-whisk** (*gadus or gawat*) — Part of dance costume.

### Architecture

Almost nothing is documented about Berom building, though photographs show clay walls.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Berom metalwork in the museum record is composite weaponry of forged iron with wooden, leather, horn and snake-skin components.

- **Sword** — Forged iron with wooden bosses on hilts wrapped in snake skin, or with leather-wrapped hilts carrying two pairs of horn-like processes and a single line of decoration along the blade.
- **Sheath** — Rawhide covering for weapons, one ornamented on a single face with four brass discs.
- **Spear** — An iron head — plain or barbed — socketed into a wooden or cane shaft, one shaft carrying pokerwork ornament bound with a metal strip.
- **Shield** — Combines hide, wood and animal hair.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Berom adornment includes brass ornaments, disc-shaped lip-plugs and ear-plugs, various neck, wrist and arm ornaments, and the protective powder tee.

- **Brass ear-ring** — The one metal named for Berom ornament in the collection.
- **Lip-plugs and ear-plugs** — Disc-shaped ornaments worn by women and children.
- **Tee** — A local powder rubbed on the forehead for protection and on the body to relieve pain.
- **Nose-ring** — An ornament worn by young men, sometimes with neck-ornaments of chains and beads.
- **Hairstyling practice** — Women dress the hair of others with small pointed tools, documented as a recorded social act.

## Music & performance

The Berom instrumentarium is the best-documented part of their material culture, with a wide range of idiophones, membranophones, chordophones and aerophones.

- **Xylophone** (*kundung or yom bi tok*) — Made of cattle horns and cobwebs.
- **Ankle rattles** — Made from the leaves of Borassus aethiopum.
- **Scraper or guiro** (*gwák or gwàshák*) — Cut from dry Euphorbia kamerunica and played by sliding a stick across the sawn body.
- **Barrel-drum** (*biŋ*) — A membranophone in the Berom drum ensemble.
- **Hourglass drum** (*kalangu*) — A membranophone in the Berom drum ensemble.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is the core display of the Berom festival calendar, with performers wearing fibre ornaments, fly-whisks, dance hats and skirts, and the royal drum reserved for court performance.

- **Nzem Berom** — A week when cultural displays from across Berom land are exhibited, especially in music, dance, arts and culture.
- **Dance equipment** — Consists of the fibre buttock ornament shang, the fly-whisk, the dance hat bong, and dance skirts.

## Festivals & rituals

Berom festivals are organised around agriculture and hunting and rotate between villages, keyed to the farming and hunting calendar.

- **Behwol** — A hunting festival in February/March for prosperity in which the Che, Boze, Anaguta and Izere peoples also take part.
- **Mandyeng** (*Mandyɛng*) — A harvest festival in March/April that ushers in the rainy season and was regarded as the festival that secured a good farming and hunting season.
- **Badù** — A harvest festival in March/April.
- **Worong cun** — A planting festival in April/May held after good rains.
- **Búná or Vwana** — The fonio harvest festival in August.

## Foodways

The staple of the early Berom was fonio, which remains central to the diet and has its own August festival.

- **Chun** — Fonio, the staple of the Berom diet.
- **Tuk chun** — Served with any soup of choice.
- **Katai chun** — A rich soup made mostly of wild vegetables.
- **Bwirik chun** — A gruel.

## Oral tradition & literature

Berom personal names are drawn from game animals, above all the duiker, reflecting how central hunting was to pre-colonial Berom society.

- **Duiker names** (*Gyang, Pam, Dung, Davou, Chuwang, Badung, Kaneng, Lyop, Chundung, Nvou, Kangyang*) — Common boys' and girls' names, each a different duiker species, reflecting that the duiker was held to be the most beautiful.
- **Other animal names** (*Bot, Tok, Tsok*) — Names such as Bot (frog), Tok (fish) and Tsok (toad) come from other undomesticated animals that were not game.
- **Berom Bible** (*Bwok Basa Dagwi*) — Produced by the Berom Language and Translation Board, with an earlier New Testament version called Takada wuna sira pas.
- **The Berom Digest** — A collected volume edited by S. D. Nyam in 2004.

## Language & religion

Berom belongs to the Plateau branch of Benue–Congo within Niger–Congo and is most widely spoken in rural areas; most Berom are Christian.

- **Berom language** (*Cèn Bèrom or Lêm Bèrom*) — Belongs to the Plateau branch of Benue–Congo within Niger–Congo and is the most widely spoken Plateau language.
- **Dialect groups** — Gyel–Kuru–Vwang, Du–Foron, Fan–Ropp–Rim–Riyom–Heikpang, Bachit, Gashish and Rahoss-Tahoss.
- **Nok culture** — The Berom are linked to the Nok culture, dated between 200 BCE and 1000 CE.
- **Gbong Gwom Jos** — The paramount ruler stool created by the British colonial administration in 1935 and held since 2009 by Da Jacob Gyang Buba.
