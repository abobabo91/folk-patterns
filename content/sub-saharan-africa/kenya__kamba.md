## At a glance
| | |
|---|---|
| Who | The Kamba, or Akamba, are a Bantu-speaking people. |
| Where | Southeastern Kenya, especially Ukambani; also elsewhere in Kenya, Tanzania and Uganda. |
| How many | Kenya’s 2019 census recorded 4,663,910 Akamba. |
| Language | Kikamba, a Bantu language in the Niger–Congo family. |
| Religion | Traditional belief is monotheistic, centered on Ngai or Mulungu and the spirits of departed family members. |
| Known for | Wood carving · Basketry and sisal weaving · Beadwork · Music and dance · Kilumi healing and rain-making ceremonies |

## Overview

The Kamba live mainly in the dry lowlands of southeastern Kenya. Their culture includes wood carving, basketry, pottery, beadwork, music, dance, and medicinal knowledge. Historically, Kamba traders connected inland communities with Swahili and Arab coastal markets. Family life, naming, elder councils, and rituals for rain, healing, and the departed remain important cultural themes.

## Material culture

Kamba material culture combines household crafts, trade goods, farming tools, leatherwork, beadwork, pottery, and highly visible wood carving.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Kamba craft traditions use sisal, grasses, fibres, leather, beads, gourds, wood, iron, and clay.

- **Basketry and sisal weaving** — Kamba baskets are made from grasses, fibres, and sisal for carrying, storing, and selling goods.
- **Beadwork** — Beads are used in necklaces, headbands, bracelets, anklets, and dance regalia, with colours sometimes marking dance groups.
- **Leatherwork** — Leather was used for kilts, skirts, sandals, and practical household goods.
- **Gourd vessels** (*calabash*) — Cleaned and decorated calabashes serve as domestic containers.
- **Wood carving** — Kamba carvers make stools, beehives, walking sticks, staffs, animal figures, bows, arrows, clubs, and decorative sculpture.

### Clothing & dress

Modern Kamba dress is mainly contemporary Kenyan and European clothing, while older dress used leather, bark, beads, and sandals.

- **Men’s short leather kilt** — Historically, men wore short kilts made from animal skins or tree bark.
- **Women’s leather or bark skirt** — Women traditionally wore knee-length skirts decorated with beads.
- **Ornamented headband** — Women wore heavily decorated headbands with beadwork.
- **Leather sandals** — Leather sandals were worn when travelling to markets or visiting, while people often went barefoot at home or in the fields.
- **Dance regalia** — Dance groups used matching bead colours and patterns to distinguish themselves.

### Architecture

The sources give little detail about a distinctive Kamba house form, but the family household is the main social unit.

- **Family household** (*musyi*) — The musyi is the central Kamba family household.
- **Extended family or clan** (*mbai*) — The mbai refers to the extended family or clan.
- **Rural household** — Rural homes supported farming, food preparation, animal keeping, craft work, and life across generations.
- **Coastal urban communities** — Kamba urban communities developed in places including Mariakani, Kinango, Kwale, Changamwe, Chaani, and Kisauni.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Kamba everyday objects serve farming, hunting, household work, trade, and ceremony.

- **Three-legged stool** — Three-legged wooden stools are among Kamba everyday objects.
- **Beehive** — Kamba woodworkers make beehives for practical use.
- **Bows and arrows** — Bows and arrows belong to Kamba hunting and everyday material culture.
- **Iron tools and weapons** — Ironworking produced tools and weapons used in farming, hunting, pastoralism, and trade.
- **Pottery** — Pottery remains a Kamba craft, although the sources do not name a distinct vessel style.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Traditional Kamba adornment used copper, brass, and beads to decorate the body and show group identity.

- **Neck chains** — Men wore copper or brass neck chains.
- **Bracelets** — Men and women wore bracelets made from metal or beads.
- **Anklets** — Anklets were part of traditional Kamba adornment.
- **Beaded necklaces** — Women wore bead necklaces linked to coastal trade and personal adornment.
- **Beaded headbands** — Women wore headbands decorated with beads.

## Music & performance

Kamba music links work, life events, social criticism, religious practice, and lively pentatonic singing.

- **Work song** (*Ngulu Mwelela*) — Ngulu Mwelela is sung during work such as digging.
- **Social-commentary dance songs** (*Mwali*) — Mwali songs often criticize antisocial behaviour.
- **Ritual dance music** (*Kilumi*) — Kilumi uses songs and dance in healing and rain-making ceremonies.
- **Religious dance music** (*Ngoma*) — Ngoma is performed in healing and rain-making settings.
- **Love and satirical songs** (*Mbalya*) — During Mbalya, the dance leader composes love songs and satirical verses to tease and entertain dancers.

## Dance & theatre

Kamba dance combines singing, agility, athletic movement, acrobatics, and religious action.

- **Healing and rain-making dance** (*Kilumi*) — Kilumi uses movement, blessing, offerings, and prayer to restore environmental balance.
- **Religious dance** (*Ngoma*) — Ngoma is associated with healing and rain-making.
- **Circumcision dance** (*Mwilu*) — Mwilu is performed for circumcision.
- **Youth dance** (*Mbalya; Ngutha*) — Mbalya, also called Ngutha, is a youth dance performed after the day’s work.
- **Post-wedding disco gathering** (*Kamandiko*) — Kamandiko is a modern disco-style gathering often held after a wedding party.

## Festivals & rituals

Kamba ritual life centers on rain, healing, marriage, coming-of-age, naming, and remembrance of the departed.

- **Rain-making and healing ceremony** (*Kilumi*) — Kilumi brings together dance, offerings, prayers, and appeals for spiritual help during inadequate rain.
- **Healing and rain-making rite** (*Ngoma*) — Ngoma is another ritual tradition connected with healing and rain-making.
- **Circumcision rite** (*Mwilu*) — Mwilu is associated with circumcision and coming-of-age.
- **Naming ritual** — Names may remember grandparents, birth circumstances, migration, visitors, or hoped-for qualities.
- **Council of elders** (*king’ole*) — The king’ole historically exercised authority in a society without a firmly established monarchy.

## Foodways

Kamba foodways combine farming, pastoralism, hunting, gathering, trade, brewing, and medicinal plant knowledge.

- **Fermented brew** (*kaluvu*) — Kaluvu is a locally fermented brew.
- **Medicinal preparations** (*miti*) — Miti are medicinal preparations made from parts of medicinal plants.
- **Maize and millet farming** — Women cultivated maize and millet along with sweet potatoes, pumpkins, beans, pigeon peas, greens, arrowroot, and yams.
- **Sugar-cane wine** — Sugar-cane wine was among the goods traded by Kamba communities.
- **Cattle trade** — Cattle were part of Kamba subsistence and long-distance trade.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kamba oral tradition carries origin stories, work songs, love songs, satire, names, and ritual speech.

- **Creation account** — A creation account tells of Mulungu creating a couple from heaven and placing them at Nzaui.
- **Anthill origin story** — After a great rainfall, another couple emerges from anthills, and the lineages become linked through marriage, famine, prosperity, and dispersal.
- **Social-commentary songs** (*Mwali*) — Mwali songs criticize antisocial behaviour.
- **Improvised teasing verses** (*Mbalya*) — Mbalya leaders improvise teasing, romantic, and satirical verses.
- **Birth-circumstance names** — Personal names preserve memories of birth circumstances, family relationships, migration, and hoped-for qualities.

## Language & religion

Kikamba is a Bantu language, and traditional Kamba religion centers on one creator and the spirits of departed family members.

- **Kikamba language** (*Kikamba*) — Kikamba belongs to the Bantu branch of the Niger–Congo family and is related to Kikuyu, Embu, Mbeere, and Meru.
- **Creator** (*Ngai; Mulungu*) — Ngai or Mulungu is an invisible creator associated with the sky.
- **Other creator names** (*Asa; Ngai Mumbi; Mwatuangi*) — Asa, Ngai Mumbi, and Mwatuangi are other names for the creator.
- **Spirits of the departed** (*Aimu; Maimu*) — The Aimu or Maimu mediate between families and God and receive offerings and libations.
- **Alphabet** — The Kikamba alphabet lacks the letters c, f, j, r, x, q, and p.
