## Overview

The Karamojong, also called Karimojong, are a Nilotic ethnic group living mainly in north-east Uganda, in the southern part of the region known as Karamoja. Their population is estimated at 475,000 people. Their language, Ngakarimojong, belongs to the Nilotic language family and the Nilo-Saharan language family. The Karamojong are agro-pastoral herders whose principal livelihood is livestock herding, while crop cultivation is secondary and possible only in suitable areas. Their culture is organized in part through a generation-based age system, pastoral transhumance, wrestling connected with marriage, traditional dance, and the social importance of cattle. Museum records document objects associated with Karamoja, Moroto, Kangole, Amudat, Nakipiripirit, and Nabilutuk.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources do not describe named Karamojong textile or pattern traditions in sufficient detail for this section.

**Beaded women’s aprons** — A British Museum catalogue records an apron made of glass beads arranged in patterns on a hide backing with hair. The beads are pale, mid, and dark blue and red, with some iron beads.

**Women’s hide dress** — A Museum of World Culture record identifies a front piece of a married woman’s dress from Nakipiripirit, made of hide. The record states that unmarried women’s front pieces were decorated with cowrie shells and beads and were smaller.

**Cowrie-shell girdles** — Museum records document a woman’s girdle from Nakipiripirit with cowrie shells, as well as British Museum records of a leather waist-band decorated with cowrie shells and a girdle made of pale hide with ostrich eggshell discs and glass beads.

**Married woman’s tail-piece** — A British Museum record describes a tail worn behind by a married woman, made of vegetable fibre and decorated with pink and white glass beads and larger blue and white glass beads.

**Hide arm ornaments** — A British Museum catalogue records an arm ornament made from five plaited hide strips, with a tassel of loose strips, some decorated with coils of aluminium wire.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name linear motifs on one calabash stopper, but do not provide a broader Karamojong motif vocabulary.

### Clothing & dress

The sources document clothing and adornment for both women and men, but do not provide a complete account of everyday dress. Women’s garments include hide front pieces and aprons made from glass beads arranged on hide. The Museum of World Culture record for Nakipiripirit distinguishes the front piece of a married woman’s dress from the smaller front piece worn by unmarried women, which could be decorated with cowrie shells and beads. British Museum records describe a married woman’s fibre tail-piece, a leather waist-band decorated with cowrie shells, a girdle of hide and ostrich eggshell discs, and open sandals made of buffalo hide.

Men’s and women’s headgear are represented by catalogue records. A composite headdress consists of black ostrich feathers mounted on cow hide with a fibre chin strap. Another head-dress, called “Chignon” in the record, is made of human hair and clay and painted brown, blue, and grey. A Museum of World Culture record describes a man’s helmet made of mixed materials. The sources do not describe named vernacular garment terms beyond *rungu* for a club.

### Architecture

The sources do not describe Karamojong houses, settlements, fences, roofing, or other forms of vernacular architecture.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Museum records document several household and working objects associated with Karamoja. These include iron knives, a finger-knife with a leather guard, spears, arrows with leaf-shaped iron heads, and clubs called *rungu*. The collections also include clay pots without ears from Kangole, calabash bowls, a milk vessel, a carved wooden spoon, a fly-whisk made from a cow’s tail, wooden vessels, and water vessels made from gourds with leather straps.

Several vessels are identified as drums used for storing food. Records from Nakipiripirit describe cylindrical vessels made from wood or other materials, with dimensions including 15 cm in diameter and 24,5 cm in height, and 20 cm in diameter and 31 cm in height. A calabash bottle from Nakipiripirit has a woven carrying covering made from strips.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The museum records document body ornaments made from hide, cowrie shells, glass beads, metal, hair, clay, and other materials. These include necklaces, bracelets, waist ornaments, neck ornaments, arm ornaments, earrings, and a man’s lip ornament made from white stone. A child’s bracelet consists of aluminium and copper spirals on a metal core. A woman’s neck ornament is made of iron with rolled ends, while another waist ornament consists of five loops of predominantly black glass beads.

Hair and head adornment are also represented. The British Museum records a frontal head ornament made of felted human hair and clay, painted, and a “Chignon” made of human hair and clay. The sources do not document tattooing, henna, or a broader named system of body decoration.

## Music & performance

The sources do not describe named Karamojong musical genres, instruments, song forms, or performance contexts in detail. A Museum of World Culture collection category called “Horisonter - Musik” includes several objects identified as drums, but the catalogue descriptions state that these were vessels for storing food rather than providing a musical function. One Museum of World Culture record identifies a dance bell worn below the knee by a girl. The Wikipedia article states that Karamojong traditional dance is remarkable and has stood the test of time, but does not name a dance form or describe its musical accompaniment.

## Dance & theatre

Karamojong traditional dance is described in the Wikipedia source as remarkable and enduring. A museum catalogue records a dance bell worn below the knee by a girl. The sources do not name particular dances, theatrical forms, dramatic genres, or performance occasions.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do not identify an annual Karamojong festival calendar or named seasonal festivals. They do describe several social and ritual practices. Livestock herding involves pastoral transhumance: for 3–4 months in a year, people move their animals to neighboring districts in search of water and pasture. The availability of food and water affects interaction with other ethnic groups.

Wrestling is a rite of passage into manhood and a requirement connected with engagement. A young Karamojong man must wrestle the woman he wishes to marry. If he wins, he is considered a man and may marry her; dowry negotiations can then begin. A non-Karamojong man who wishes to marry a Karamojong woman is also required to undergo the ceremony. The sources do not describe funeral rites, birth ceremonies, named initiation ceremonies, or festival dates.

A Museum of World Culture record describes a goat-hide strap worn within a family during an offering against illness and accidents. This is the only explicitly described ritual object in the museum records.

## Foodways

The sources state that the Karamojong are cattle-keeping people and that livestock herding has social and cultural importance. Crop cultivation is a secondary activity undertaken only where practicable. Water and food availability are persistent concerns. Museum records identify milk vessels, calabash bowls, pots, and several drums or vessels used for storing food. A Museum of World Culture record describes a milk vessel from Nabilutuk, while several objects from Nakipiripirit are identified as food-storage vessels.

The Karamojong language article records *ŋakipi* for water and *ŋakile* for milk. It also states that traditional liquids such as water and milk are feminine plural forms in the language, while bottled beer is a more recently introduced masculine singular term. The sources do not identify staple grains, named dishes, brewing traditions, cooking techniques, or dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe Karamojong and Teso Cluster migration traditions. According to the Wikipedia accounts, the group is said to have migrated from Abyssinia between 1600 and 1700 AD as a single group before fragmenting into several peoples. Another account places the wider migration from present-day Ethiopia around 1600 A.D. The name Karamojong is derived from the phrase “ekar ngimojong,” explained as “the old men can walk no farther.” The language article gives a related explanation, approximating the name as “the old men sat down,” referring to a migration more than 300 years ago.

The language source mentions selected Ngakarimojong proverbs but does not reproduce them. It also records the greeting *Ejoka?*, the response *Ejok-nooi*, and other phrases. The sources do not describe a named epic, folktale cycle, riddle tradition, contemporary literary movement, or formal preservation project.

## Language & religion

Ngakarimojong is a Nilotic language of the Nilo-Saharan language family spoken by the Karamojong in north-east Uganda. It is also written as ŋaKarimojoŋ or ŋaKaramojoŋ in Karamojong, and as Ngakarimojong or N'Karamojong in English. Closely related languages and dialects are associated with the Jie, Dodoth, Teso, Turkana, Tesyo, Jiye, Toposa, and Nyangatom. These peoples are described as part of the Karamojong cluster, also called the Teso cluster.

Written Ngakarimojong uses the Roman alphabet. Spelling rules were established by missionaries in the 1960s, and the orthography was created mainly by British Anglicans and Italian Roman Catholics. The language has three basic tones and three contour tones. The vocabulary source records *Akuj* for God, while noting that its root also means “north” and “up.” It states that traditional religious beliefs are reflected in the language. The sources do not describe the current religious landscape in detail.
