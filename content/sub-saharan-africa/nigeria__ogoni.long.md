## Overview

The Ogoni are an ethnic group in Rivers South-East senatorial district of Rivers State, in the Niger Delta region of southern Nigeria. They number just over 2 million and live in a 1,050-square-kilometre homeland also called Ogoniland. Their territory lies near the coast of the Gulf of Guinea, east of Port Harcourt, and is bounded by the Imo River on the north and east. Ogoniland extends across the Local Government Areas of Khana, Gokana, Tai and Eleme, and is divided into the kingdoms of Babbe, Gokana, Ken-Khana, Nyo-Khana, Tai and Eleme. Ogoni culture is represented in the sources especially through wooden sculpture, masks, masquerades, pottery, language diversity and oral tradition. The Ogoni also became internationally known through the Movement for the Survival of the Ogoni People and protests concerning oil production and environmental damage.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used do not document named Ogoni textile or pattern traditions.

**Raffia clothing** — Raffia clothing forms part of the performance of Ogoni masks and headdresses. The sources do not describe its construction, named patterns or differences from neighboring cultures.

**Plaited fibre loops** — Museum records describe Ogoni pottery vessels with fibre loops attached to handles or threaded through holes in their rims. The records do not identify these loops by a vernacular name or describe them as a textile tradition.

**Raffia cloth hoods** — Several carved wooden masks in the museum records have raffia cloth hoods, sometimes accompanied by long black hair or a raffia fringe. The sources do not provide a named pattern vocabulary.

### Clothing & dress

The sources do not describe everyday Ogoni clothing, gender-specific dress, footwear, belts, wrapper cloth, coral bead regalia or chieftaincy attire. In performance contexts, masks and headdresses are accompanied by raffia clothing. Museum records also describe a carved wooden figure from Ogoni whose lower part has a braided “helmet” for the wearer’s head, and other figures mounted on bases that were probably intended to be worn on the head. The available sources do not establish whether these objects represent everyday, ceremonial or theatrical dress beyond their connection with performance and head-worn forms.

### Architecture

The sources used do not describe Ogoni houses, settlement forms, roofing, building materials, decoration or named architectural traditions.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum records document a substantial group of Ogoni pottery vessels. These include wide-mouthed bowls with three handles and crescent-shaped decorations, shallow red dishes on short pedestals, globular jars with short necks and incised sinuous diagonal decoration, and bowls mounted on stepped or ordinary pedestals. Some pedestal bowls have six ornamental excrescences around the rim; others are pale pink, brick red, unglazed or unburnished. One bowl has a plaited fibre loop threaded through two holes in the rim. The records also include an iron sword with a broad flat blade, a slit at the end opposite the handle, and a carved wooden hilt with a knob.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used do not document Ogoni jewelry, named adornment types, tattoos, henna, hair practices or ritual body decoration. Museum records mention grass fringes, raffia fringes, long black hair attached to a mask, and painted mask surfaces, but do not identify these as jewelry or body adornment.

## Music & performance

The sources do not name Ogoni musical instruments, song genres or musical performance structures. They do describe dances and masquerades as performance contexts for masks and headdresses. These performances may include fully articulated limbs and raffia clothing, and the Amanikpo and Karikpo societies sponsored dances and masquerades. Full-figure puppets were also used with the intention of promoting fertility and dispelling evil spirits. The sources do not state whether these performances involve drums, other instruments, particular songs, seasonal events or life-cycle ceremonies.

The Ogoni art tradition is closely connected with wooden sculpture. Masks may depict ancestral spirits, animals, restless spirits or real people of high status, while some masks had regulatory roles. The sources describe human-like masks with small heads, hinged jaws, white or brown pigment especially around the eyes, and mounts that did not cover the face entirely. These objects could be graceful and elegant or unrefined and grotesque. The museum records add masks with articulated jaws, stick grids representing teeth, grass fringes, horns, elongated noses, tusks, skulls, cooking pots and other carved projections.

## Dance & theatre

Ogoni dances and masquerades use masks, headdresses, puppets and raffia clothing. The performances add complexity and emotion to the carved forms through articulated limbs and movement. The Amanikpo and Karikpo societies sponsored these events, including performances with full-figure puppets intended to promote fertility and dispel evil spirits. The sources also state that the dances are influenced by the environment and interpret it through their routines.

The museum catalogue records describe masks representing human faces, pigs, skulls and other forms. Several have articulated lower jaws, while others have horns, tusks, proboscises or stylized facial features. The sources do not provide names for individual mask types, dramatic plots, performance calendars or distinctions between ceremonial and entertainment theatre.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not document an annual Ogoni festival calendar, named festivals, dates, wedding rites, coming-of-age ceremonies or funeral practices. They state that Ogoni performances with masks, headdresses and full-figure puppets were connected with fertility and the dispelling of evil spirits. According to the cultural account, the supreme female god Bari, also called Wa-Bari and Kara-Bari, was consulted through shrines concerning earthly issues. Death was understood as a transition to the spirit world, while also being regarded as unnatural because it was attributed to evil spirits called Taa, with the alternate names Poro-Edon and Yo.

## Foodways

The sources do not name Ogoni dishes, staple recipes, cooking methods, dietary rules, beverages or ceremonial foods. They state that the climate and agriculture support palms, yams, assorted fruits and vegetables. Palm oil was the main production described in the source. One museum record identifies a three-legged cooking pot carved on top of a wooden mask, but the catalogue does not explain its culinary or ritual use.

## Oral tradition & literature

According to oral tradition, the Ogoni migrated from ancient Ghana to the Atlantic coast before eventually reaching the eastern Niger Delta, where they were absorbed into an existing Ibibio, Annang, Igbo and Ijaw population. The sources do not provide named folktales, epics, proverbs or riddles. They state that the name “Ogoni” originated from the Ibani/Ijaw word “Igoni,” meaning strangers.

Oral tradition also preserves the account of Bari, the supreme female god who created the world and was consulted through shrines. The sources mention Ken Saro-Wiwa as a writer, environmental activist and television producer, and describe his account of the rapid social changes caused by modernity, colonialism, the money economy, indigenous colonialism and the Nigerian Civil War. The Ogoni Bill of Rights was presented by the Movement for the Survival of the Ogoni People in October 1990.

## Language & religion

The Ogoni languages, also called Kegboid languages, are spoken by the Ogoni people of Rivers State, Nigeria. They are divided into East and West clusters, with limited mutual intelligibility between members of each cluster. The East cluster includes Khana, Tẹẹ and Gokana; the West cluster includes Eleme and Baan. The sources also describe Khana as mutually intelligible with dialects of Gokana, Tai, Eleme and Baen Ogoi. The language article gives approximately 1,800,000 speakers for Khana and Tẹẹ together, about 250,000 for Gokana, about 90,000 for Eleme and around 50,500 for Baan.

The religious material describes Bari, Wa-Bari or Kara-Bari as a supreme female god who lived in the skies. Before colonization, every aspect of life was said to revolve around this god and shrines were consulted for earthly matters. Evil spirits associated with death were called Taa, Poro-Edon or Yo. The sources do not describe the current religious landscape, denominations, historical scripts or religious conversion.
