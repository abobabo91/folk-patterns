## Overview

The Sherbro are an indigenous people of Sierra Leone, numbering 134,606 — about 1.9% of the national population. Their homeland is Bonthe District on the southern coast, where they make up 40% of the population, and they also live in coastal Moyamba District and in the Western Area, above all in Freetown; Sherbro Island lies off this coast. They speak Sherbro, a Mel language of the Niger–Congo family, and work chiefly as fishermen and traders. In folk-culture terms they are notable for a divided cultural inheritance: the southeastern Sherbro are close allies of the Mende, speak Mende and have absorbed much Mende tradition, while the Sherbro of the Western Area are allied to the Krio and have taken in a large amount of Krio tradition. Before the colonial era they were one of the dominant peoples of the territory; in the twenty-first century they are a small minority.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Njaye kpoikpoi** — a display cloth of eleven strips of hand-spun cotton in white and indigo, hand-woven and then hand-sewn together along the selvedges. Its distinguishing principle is that each strip carries a *different* design, and the strips are planned so that the separate designs resolve into one single composition once the cloth is assembled: two wide bands of supplementary weft floats in indigo run across each end, and these flank rectangles — two to a strip — aligned across the seams to form a large cross that dominates an otherwise white field, its corners meeting at the centre.

**Beadwork panels** — rectangular panels of strung glass beads, finished with braided fibre rope, made as women's aprons and belts. The technique is bead-on-cord rather than embroidery onto a ground cloth, and the panel is a self-contained rectangle carrying the whole design.

**Motif vocabulary.** Triangles in yellow, red, white and blue (apron); triangles in black, yellow, red and blue outlined in white (belt); banded supplementary weft floats in indigo; a cross built from aligned rectangles across joined strips; small rectangular panels of incised geometric pattern on carved figures.

### Clothing & dress

The catalogue records show women's dress items specifically: a beaded apron and a beaded belt, both rectangles of strung glass beads on braided fibre rope, the apron patterned with yellow, red, white and blue triangles and the belt with black, yellow, red and blue triangles outlined in white. The same conventions appear on carved female figures, which are shown wearing an apron of red glass beads hung around the waist and a necklace of black shell beads or red glass beads, and in one case a raised head-dress running from the front to the back of the head. What the sources do record is a broader dress history: the Sherbro have a more westernised culture than other indigenous groups in Sierra Leone, following intermarriage with Portuguese and English traders from the fifteenth to the eighteenth century and with the Krio from the 1790s, and westernised Sherbro carry Western names.

### Architecture

The sources used for this profile do not describe Sherbro house forms, building materials, roofing or architectural decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The one household implement documented here is a **snuff pestle with its mortar**, carved of wood: a stick rounded at one end and cut into a flat spoon at the other, tied to the mortar with twined string so that pestle and vessel stay together as a single object. Wood-carving is the material the collection represents most consistently — besides the snuff set there are standing female figures cut in the round, one on a square base, with details rendered in low relief: a flower at the neck, five rings carved around the neck, and a rectangular panel of geometric pattern set on the stomach.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment in the documented objects is almost entirely bead-based. Necklaces of black shell beads and of red glass beads are shown on the carved figures, and the beaded apron and belt described above are worn at the waist. On one figure, five rings are carved around the neck, and a raised head-dress is carved running from the front to the back of the head. Imported glass beads and braided plant fibre are the recurring materials, worked into flat patterned rectangles rather than strung ropes.

## Music & performance

The sources used here record almost nothing of Sherbro musical practice. The single point they establish is biographical rather than ethnographic: John Akar, an entertainer of Sherbro origin, composed the music of Sierra Leone's national anthem. No instruments, song genres or performance occasions are described.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used for this profile do not describe Sherbro dance or dramatic traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used here do not set out a Sherbro festival calendar or life-cycle rites. Sierra Leone has no elements inscribed on the UNESCO Lists of Intangible Cultural Heritage, so that register adds nothing either. What the sources do document is the institutional frame within which such observances sat: chieftaincy. Some of the region's most prominent chieftaincy families were founded by Sherbro intermarriage with English agents trading in the Sherbro Country from the 1620s, producing Afro-European clans including the Sherbro Tuckers and the Sherbro Caulkers — the latter descended from Thomas Corker (died 1700) and Seniora Doll, and continuing as the Shenge and Bonthe Caulkers. Thomas Caulker was King of Bumpey; Kpana Lewis (1830–1912) was a Sherbro chief who opposed colonial rule. As late as the 1920s the Sherbro were still governed by their own chiefs. The recorded clan names are Sherbro Charley, Sherbro Bullom, Sherbro Caulkers, Sherbro Clevelands, Sherbro Hubbards, Sherbro Macfoy, Sherbro Rogers, Sherbro Rutile and Sherbro Tuckers.

## Foodways

The sources used for this profile do not describe Sherbro staples, dishes or cooking. The only consumption practice they attest indirectly is snuff-taking, through the carved wooden pestle and mortar held in the collection.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources document the beginning of Sherbro as a written language: the first recorded publication in Sherbro is a selection of seven parables from Matthew and Luke in the New Testament, translated by James Schön of the Church Missionary Society and published in 1839. Sherbro writers and clergy appear in the historical record — Barnabas Root, born Fahma Yahny and died 1877, was a clergyman and missionary to his own people.

## Language & religion

Sherbro belongs to the Mel branch of the Niger–Congo family and is also known as Southern Bullom, Shiba, Amampa, Mampa and Mampwa. Although it has more speakers than the other Bullom languages, it is classed as endangered: its use is declining among the Sherbro themselves in favour of Krio and English, and it appears in the UNESCO Red Book of Endangered Languages. Language use also splits the group geographically — most southeastern Sherbro speak Mende as well, while most Sherbro of the Western Area do not. On religion the sources are brief: many Sherbro assimilate as Krio, with whom they share the Christian faith and Western names, and Joseph Christian Humper, a bishop in the United Methodist Church, is listed among notable Sherbro. No indigenous religious practice is described in these sources.
