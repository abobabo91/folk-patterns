## Overview

The Nkole are a Bantu people of south-western Uganda, closely related to the Nyoro, Kiga, Toro and Hema. They are known above all as cattle-keepers, and their material culture — milk vessels, baskets, weapons and tools — follows directly from that practice. Their objects show fine craftsmanship in wood, grass, iron and beadwork, organized around pale grounds with dark geometric patterns.

## Material culture

Nkole material culture centers on basketry, woodworking, and ironworking, with a consistent aesthetic of pale grounds decorated with dark geometric figures.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Nkole have no woven cloth tradition; their surface pattern lives in coiled grass basketry.

- **Coiled grass basketry** — Baskets, bowls, plates, trays and covers are coiled from grass or leaf with black patterns on pale grounds, some shaped like beehives.
- **Conical coiled covers** — Covers and plates in grass or leaf are coiled conically and patterned in black against pale, including fine lids on wooden milk vessels.
- **Coloured banded coil-work** — Bottles and lids are coiled from dyed grass in red, black and yellow horizontal bands.
- **Plaited grass matting** — Composite mats made of grass strands joined by transverse bands of black grass, sometimes with cane embroidered decoration.

### Clothing & dress

The sources say almost nothing about Nkole dress.

- **Head-dress mat** — A composite grass mat catalogued as both mat and head-dress, red and yellow with bands of cane embroidered decoration in dark grey and three fibre cords for attachment.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Ironworking and woodworking dominate Nkole material culture, centered on weapons, tools and milk vessels.

- **Milk vessel** — A conical wooden vessel with a broad circular base, tapering neck and narrow aperture, topped with a finely coiled grass lid.
- **Composite milkpot** — A wooden milkpot with a groove below the neck and iron and copper symbols stapled onto it.
- **Bamboo quiver** — Quivers of bamboo, wood or hide carry arrows and are closed with hide or removable wooden caps, with wooden ones carved and painted in black.
- **Iron spear** — A socketed iron spear blade on a hard wooden shaft with a short iron butt.
- **Smoking pipe** — A pottery bowl paired with a wooden stem.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Glass beads and cowrie shells are the main adornment materials, appearing on objects rather than worn on the body.

- **Beaded fly-whisk** — A fibre and hair fly-whisk with a handle covered in bands of turquoise-blue, red, pink, white and black glass beads, cowries at the top, and cowries, a metal bell and wooden charms at the base.
- **Beaded gourd bottle** — A gourd bottle entirely covered in a geometric design of dark blue, pink and white beads.
- **Horn amulet** — An amulet made of horn, possibly for ritual use.

## Music & performance

The sources name only one musical instrument and no song genres, ensembles or performance occasions.

- **Musical horn** — A pale green spiral hollow horn with an oval hole near the tip.
- **Ebyevugo** — A Runyankore poetry form, mentioned in sources but with no description of how it is performed.

## Dance & theatre

The sources document no Nkole dances or dramatic traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe no annual festivals or birth, initiation, marriage or funeral rites.

- **Ritual fly-whisk** — A fly-whisk whose handle carries cowries, a metal bell and two wooden charms, associated with ritual use.

## Foodways

Cattle-keeping is the foundation of Nkole foodways, and milk vessels reflect this central practice.

- **Milk** — Milk is the primary food product, served in specialized wooden vessels.
- **Matooke** (*ebitookye*) — Bananas or matooke, an everyday staple food.
- **Beans** (*ebihimba*) — Beans, an everyday staple food.
- **Millet bread** (*oburo*) — Millet bread, an everyday staple food.
- **Maize** (*ebicoori*) — Maize or corn, eaten as maize meal or corn bread.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources give greeting formulas and one poetry form, but no folktales, epics, proverbs or riddles.

- **Ebyevugo** — A Runyankore poetry form mentioned in the sources but with no description or named works.
- **Greeting formula** (*Agandi*) — A greeting meaning literally 'other news!' and used for 'How are you?', answered with Ni marungi, 'good news!'.

## Language & religion

Nkore is a Bantu language with high mutual intelligibility with Kiga; the Banyankole are predominantly Christian.

- **Nkore language** (*Nkore / Runyankore*) — A Bantu language written variously as Nkole, Nyankore, Orunyankore, Runyankore and Runyankole, with 84–94% lexical similarity to Kiga.
- **Address term for woman** (*Nyabo*) — The Runyankore word for addressing a woman.
- **Address term for man** (*Sebo*) — The Runyankore word for addressing a man.

## Glossary

- *Nkore* — The Bantu language spoken by the Banyankole
- *Runyankore* — Alternate spelling of Nkore language
- *Banyankole* — The Nkole people
- *Ankole* — The historical kingdom and region in south-western Uganda where the Nkole are native
- *Ebyevugo* — A Runyankore poetry form
- *Agandi* — Greeting meaning 'other news!' or 'How are you?'
- *Ni marungi* — Response greeting meaning 'good news!'
- *Nyabo* — Runyankore address term for a woman
- *Sebo* — Runyankore address term for a man
- *ebitookye* — Bananas or matooke in Runyankore
- *ebihimba* — Beans in Runyankore
- *oburo* — Millet bread in Runyankore
- *ebicoori* — Maize in Runyankore
