## Overview

The Baggāra have mixed Arab and Arabized indigenous African ancestry. Most live in Chad and Sudan, with smaller populations in nearby countries. Cattle herding and seasonal movement are central to their traditional life, though many now live in settled communities. Their material culture includes decorated clothing, leather goods, gourds, drums, spears, and woven materials.

## Material culture

Baggara material culture uses cotton, wool, leather, wood, gourds, reed, iron, and woven fibre for clothing, storage, shelters, tools, and adornment.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Baggara textiles include decorated cotton clothing, patterned women’s cloth, woven mats, and leather-bound wooden beds.

- **Men’s cotton tunic** (*Jibba*) — The jibba is a cotton tunic with dyed cotton and wool applique panels and embroidery at the neck, cuffs, and pockets.
- **Women’s patterned cloth** (*Tob*) — The tob is a patterned or light-coloured floral cloth worn by women.
- **Roll-up bed** (*Darangal*) — The darangal is made from thin bashom wood sticks bound with cowhide strips called seyur.
- **Woven mats** — Woven mats appear in photographs behind women near thatched buildings.

### Clothing & dress

Baggara dress includes decorated tunics, women’s tobs, turbans, and jellabiyas.

- **Men’s tunic** (*Jibba*) — Men wear cotton jibbas decorated with applique and embroidery.
- **Women’s cloth** (*Tob*) — Women wear patterned tobs, including light-coloured floral cloth.
- **Long robe** (*Jellabiya*) — A photograph shows a bearded man wearing a jellabiya and turban.
- **Turban** — Photographs show men wearing turbans, including an older man carrying a cane.

### Architecture

Photographs show thatched buildings supported by wood and used with woven mats.

- **Thatched-roof building** — Museum photographs show thatched-roof buildings in South Darfur.
- **Wooden supports** — Wooden supports are visible behind a woman in one photograph.
- **Woven mats** — Woven mats appear around the buildings.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Everyday objects include iron-tipped spears, drums, trays, leather bags, gourds, and wooden items.

- **Long spear** — One spear has a broad leaf-shaped iron blade, a reed or bamboo shaft, and an iron spiral at the butt.
- **Second long spear** — A second spear has a broad leaf-shaped iron point, a reed shaft, and a spiral-like metal fitting.
- **Drum** — The museum records two skin-covered drums suspended from wooden stakes.
- **Leather storage bag** (*Dabia*) — The dabia is a bottle-shaped leather bag used for millet, sorghum grain, or flour.
- **Gourd storage vessel** (*Boursa*) — The boursa is a round gourd with a separate lid made with vegetable fibre and leather thongs.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Women wear neck, ear, and nose ornaments, while cattle may have decorated leather neck-pieces.

- **Amulet neck ornament** — Women’s neck ornaments are described as carrying amulets.
- **Ear ornament** — Photographs show women wearing ear ornaments.
- **Nose ornament** — Photographs show women wearing nose ornaments.
- **Bull neck-piece** (*Gilada*) — The gilada is a red-leather neck-piece for a riding bull, decorated with cowrie shells, plaited leather edges, and long tassels.

## Music & performance

Photographs show men and boys gathered around skin-covered drums and a woman speaking or singing.

- **Skin-covered drum** — Two drums are suspended from wooden stakes and beaten with wooden sticks.
- **Drum gathering** — Groups of men and boys gather around the drums.
- **Vocal performance** — A woman is shown speaking or singing with her eyes closed while wearing a patterned tob.

## Festivals & rituals

The profile records seasonal migration and historical movements, but no ritual festivals.

- **Seasonal migration** — Nomadic groups move between wet-season grazing lands and river areas during the dry season.
- **Mahdist-period migration** — From 1883 to 1898, tens of thousands of Baggara moved to Omdurman and central Sudan and supplied troops for Mahdist armies.

## Foodways

Baggara foodways center on cattle herding and storing millet and sorghum.

- **Cattle herding** — Nomadic cattle herding is a traditional livelihood, and cattle ownership is central to the term Baggāra in Sudan.
- **Grain storage bag** (*Dabia*) — The dabia stores millet, sorghum grain, or flour made from these grains.
- **Seasonal herding movement** — Baggara groups move between wet-season grazing lands and river areas during the dry season.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources give historical accounts but no named Baggara storytelling or literary traditions.

- **Juhaynah ancestry claim** — The sources record claims that the Baggara descend from the Juhaynah Arab tribe.
- **1391 letter** — A documented letter from 1391 concerns Arab settlements and raids in the region.
- **Baggara cultural formation** — Braukämper dates the formation of Baggara culture to the 17th century in Wadai, where camel-herding Arabs met eastward-moving cattle-rearing Fula people.

## Language & religion

The Baggara mostly speak Chadian Arabic, while the profile gives no information about their religion.

- **Chadian Arabic** — Most Baggara speak a distinct dialect known as Chadian Arabic.
- **Shuwa Arabic** — Shuwa Arabic is another name for the language, though the term has fallen out of use among specialist linguists.
- **Shuwa** — Shuwa is described as a geographically and socially narrow term of Kanuri origin.
- **Sudanese Arabic influence** — Baggara dialects in Southern Kordofan show Sudanese Arabic influence from contact with settled people and Sudanese Arab camel herders.
