## Overview

The Antemoro — the name means "people of the shore" or "people of the coast" in Malagasy — live on the southeastern coast of Madagascar, mostly between Manakara and Farafangana, clustered around Vohipeno and the mouth of the Matitanana river. Their name derives from Imoro, another name for the province of Matitana. The 2013 population estimate was around 500,000. They speak a dialect of Malagasy, a Malayo-Polynesian language derived from the Barito languages of southern Borneo. Their ancestry traces to East African Bantu and Indonesian Austronesian speakers like most Malagasy, while the Anteony, Antalaotra and Anakara clans claim descent from settlers arriving from Arabia and Persia around 1500. They matter in folk-culture terms above all for *sorabe*, the Arabic-script writing of Malagasy that no other Malagasy group developed, and for the *ombiasy* astrologer-sages who carried it across the island.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources describe Antemoro textiles by material and construction rather than by named weaving traditions or pattern systems, so only the following can be stated.

**Tafitsihy** — mats woven from *harefo* reeds, sewn together to make jackets and tunics. Two British Museum jackets in this collection are plaited of grass: two lengths of plaiting sewn together halfway along the long edge to form the back, then folded and sewn partway up the outer edge for the underarm seam, with a folded grass section applied around collar and armholes.

**Fanto** — beaten barkcloth, made into the loincloth worn beneath the reed ensemble.

**Raffia-and-cotton weaving** — a British Museum skirt is woven of raffia and cotton in beige with red, off-white and brown stripes: two rectangles sewn along the long edges and then folded and closed into a tube.

**Antemoro paper** — not a textile but the community's best-known handmade sheet material, decorated with pressed fresh flowers and leaves, traditionally used to record secret knowledge in *sorabe* and now a major product sold to tourists and exported.

The sources name no motif vocabulary for Antemoro textiles.

### Clothing & dress

East African Bantu, Arab and Islamic influences mark Antemoro dress. Men traditionally wore a turban or fez-style hat with long loose robes of the kind worn across the Muslim world; *harefo* reeds woven into *tafitsihy* mats were sewn into jackets and tunics, the sleeves long for older men, over a *fanto* barkcloth loincloth. Women wore sleeveless sheath dresses made of two or three reed mats sewn together, belted at the waist or drawn over one shoulder, often with a mat bandeau or halter top. It was forbidden for women to wear *lamba* of cotton or any material other than woven raffia mats: the belief was that finer cloth would make them attractive to men outside their clan, whom they could not marry. Rulers were distinguished by *fady* forbidding them shirts or hats. Later cotton dress is represented in the collection by a wife's red cotton shirt with white lace at cuffs, collar and neck and six clear plastic buttons, and a wife's head-band of red cotton backed in white with a white tassel at each end for tying.

### Architecture

The sources describe Antemoro settlement rather than house construction. Villages are clustered in the southeastern coastal area near Vohipeno and Manakara. The one architectural feature the sources name is defensive and social: the Anakara clan, guardians of the sacred idols and texts, lived in villages sealed off by spiked wooden palisades that no one could enter without permission, a way of maintaining their separation from the rest of Antemoro society. Nothing else about house form, roofing, materials or ornament appears in the sources used here.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum records show a household repertoire in wood, cane, coconut shell and plant fibre rather than pottery or metal. A cylindrical wooden rice container with its own circular wooden lid holds the staple. A fruit basket is made of cane; a grain filter and a seat are made of vegetable fibre; a spoon combines wood with coconut shell. Two mats are distinguished by use rather than by decoration — a sleeping mat and an eating mat, both of vegetable fibre — which matches the wider Antemoro reliance on plaited plant material for clothing as well as furnishing. The single object combining wood with metal is a fish harpoon, consistent with a coastal economy. One further catalogued object is a paper document carrying religious writings.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources name no jewelry types, but they do describe one worn object in detail: amulets inscribed with verses of the Qur'an in the *sorabe* script, commonly made and worn by the Antemoro. Charm-making is a livelihood as well as a devotional practice — the manufacture of charms is listed among the community's occupations, and the Anakara clan knew how to make charms, cast spells and practise geomancy using local gemstones. Tattooing, henna and hair practices are not covered by the sources used here.

## Festivals & rituals

Antemoro ritual life turns on ancestral observance, clan privilege and *fady* (taboo). The most consequential rite is *sombili* (also given as *sombily*), the ritual sacrifice of animals for the ancestors — a prerogative the Zafiraminia held until the Zafikasimambo clan absorbed it on taking power around 1550, and which they then used to shape political and economic activity in the region. Butchering particular animals for human consumption was assigned to particular clans, and members of a clan could traditionally eat only with others of the same clan. Divination is the other pillar: *ombiasy* predicted the future from lunar phases and offered charms, spells and divinations across the island.

Life-cycle rites are governed by clan endogamy and by mourning rules. Marriage outside the clan was deeply taboo; transgressors were mourned as though dead and cut off entirely. At death, every member of the community is mourned in the same way regardless of social class: the whole community observes a week of mourning during which no one washes or changes clothes, and on the eighth day the mourning breaks, everyone bathes and dresses afresh. A widow continues mourning until the parents of the deceased declare it over. A widower stays shut in his house for one or two weeks while the parents of the deceased send a female relative to care for him and keep him company. The *fady* established by a king were observed scrupulously for centuries after his death.

## Foodways

Rice is the staple and the export: the Antemoro were major rice exporters by the early 18th century, and they still grow rice and coffee in the southeastern homeland, where coffee generates income for many families. The Ampanira clan was historically a principal producer and trader of sea salt. Dietary rules are drawn from two layers. The prohibition on eating pork came with Islam and survives as a *fady* even where Islamic practice lapsed. The taboo against dogs as unclean is shared with Islam but predates its arrival on the island. Commensality was itself regulated: members of a given clan could traditionally only eat with others of the same clan, and the butchering of certain animals for human consumption was the assigned responsibility of particular clans. The objects collected here are the apparatus of a rice-and-fish household — a lidded cylindrical wooden rice container, a vegetable-fibre grain filter, a cane fruit basket, a wood-and-coconut-shell spoon, a dedicated eating mat, and a metal-tipped fish harpoon. The sources name no prepared dishes.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Antemoro are unique among Malagasy peoples for an extensive scribal tradition: the Arabico-Malagasy manuscripts known as *sorabe*, written in Arabic script. One surviving manuscript may predate the seventeenth century; most extant ones were produced in the nineteenth, though some are copies of considerably earlier texts. *Sorabe* writings range from instructional texts for teaching astrology to historical records and other documentation, and materials written in the script were considered sacred. Those who master the writing are called *katibo* and are held to possess unusual power. Alongside the manuscripts run oral and written historical traditions — genealogies, and accounts of the Antemoro–Zafiraminia conflict that other Malagasy groups tell as a battle between two giants. The Antemoro also preserve traditions of ancestral origin referring to people from "Diba and Jawa," and to an ancient population remembered as *Vahoaka Gola*. The Anakara and other religious specialists preserve a secret language, *Kalamo* — Arabic vocabulary carried on Malagasy morphology and syntax, locally called *kalamon' Antesitesy*.

## Language & religion

The Antemoro speak a dialect of Malagasy, a Malayo-Polynesian language of the Barito group of southern Borneo, traditionally written in the *sorabe* script; an older Antemoro vocabulary contains a far higher proportion of Arabic words than the present-day language. Religion is layered. Before conversion to Islam in the seventh or eighth century the ancestral Malagasy clans were monotheists with a creator god and intermediary spirits; by the time of conversion the creator had been largely forgotten and the spirits raised to deities. Islam arrived in force with the settlers, then thinned into ancestor-honouring and *fady*, leaving Qur'anic amulets, the pork prohibition and the script. The Anakara were believed to communicate with *ziny* (genies). Since the late twentieth century Muslim missionaries have prompted a revival, and many Antemoro have reconverted or begun practising more devoutly.
