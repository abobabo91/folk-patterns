## Overview

The Songye are a Bantu-speaking people of the south-central Democratic Republic of the Congo, occupying the wooded savanna and gallery-forest country between the Lomami and Lualaba rivers, with the town of Kabinda functioning as their historical and administrative heart. Their language, Kisonge, belongs to the same Bantu subgroup as Luba-Kasai and Luba-Katanga, and Songye oral tradition and Luba dynastic tradition are mutually entangled: many accounts hold that the two peoples share a common origin in the Shaba (Katanga) region, with the Songye moving north and west while the Luba consolidated to the south-east, and Luba royal charters frequently name Songye ancestors among their founding figures. Population estimates commonly fall between one and one and a half million, distributed across dozens of chiefdoms rather than a single unified polity; the Songye were historically organised as a loose federation of autonomous chiefdoms, each headed by a chief (*yakitenge* or similar titles vary regionally) whose authority was checked by powerful men's associations, above all the masking society *bwadi bwa kifwebe*. That balance — an installed hereditary chief on one side, a secret regulatory society on the other — is the political fact that most directly shaped Songye art, because the objects that survive in collections are overwhelmingly instruments of one or the other. The Songye sat astride the long-distance copper and salt routes running north from the Katangan mines, and were known in the nineteenth century both as formidable smiths and as aggressive traders and raiders, a reputation that European accounts amplified. Their signature material contributions are the *nkisi* (plural *mankishi*) power figure — the heavily accumulated, metal-sheathed, horn-charged male statue that is among the most immediately recognisable sculptural forms in all African art — and the *kifwebe* mask, whose parallel incised striations became one of the twentieth century's most-copied African design idioms. To these should be added a superb corpus of forged iron and copper: ceremonial axes and adzes, prestige blades, and the cruciform copper ingots that circulated as currency and store of wealth across the wider Katangan world.

## Material culture

Songye material culture is dominated by objects of power and office rather than by domestic decoration: figures charged with medicine, masks owned by an initiated society, forged blades that were both weapon and credential, and copper that was simultaneously money, metal and ornament. Surface pattern, where it appears, is usually incised or raised rather than woven or painted, and the same triangular, chevron and parallel-line vocabulary migrates across mask, blade, gourd and skin.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Like their Kuba and Luba neighbours, the Songye worked raffia rather than cotton before the colonial period, though they are not a weaving centre in the way the Kuba are, and much of the finest patterned cloth circulating in Songye chiefdoms was acquired by trade from the west.

- **Raffia cloth** (*mbuki*) — Palm-fibre cloth woven by men on a single-heddle loom, pounded soft in a mortar, and used for wrappers, burial shrouds and tribute payment; plain grounds are more common than the cut-pile embroidery of the Kuba.
- **Geometric surface vocabulary** — A shared repertoire of chevrons, lozenges, triangles and parallel bands recurs across raffia panels, incised gourds, forged blades and scarified skin, so that a single design idea appears in several media at once.
- **Beaded and cowrie-worked panels** (*lukasa*-style beadwork) — Bead and cowrie work applied to caps, belts, sashes and figure ornaments; the technique is shared with the Luba, whose *lukasa* memory boards give the idiom its familiar name, and among the Songye it more commonly serves regalia than mnemonic recitation.
- **Trade cloth as prestige** — Imported cottons, and later factory prints, were absorbed into chiefly dress from the late nineteenth century and quickly outranked local raffia in ceremonial display.
- **Bark cloth** — Beaten bark was used in some forested districts for utilitarian wrappers and as a wrapping for medicines and charges.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday Songye dress was a wrapped skirt or loincloth with the upper body bare, so that ornament, coiffure and scarification carried the burden of signification; ceremonial dress, by contrast, was accumulative, piling skins, feathers, beads, copper and iron onto the body of a chief or a dancer.

- **Chief's regalia** (*bwalelo*) — The assembled insignia of office — cap, leopard or civet skin, beaded sash, copper ornaments, staff, ceremonial adze and stool — worn and displayed together at installation and at public judgement.
- **Wrapper** — Raffia or, later, trade cotton wound at the waist, the basic garment for both sexes.
- **Animal-skin mantle** — Leopard, genet or civet pelts reserved to chiefs and senior titleholders, the leopard in particular indexing legitimate lethal authority.
- **Feather headdress** — Feather crests and plumes worn by dancers and titleholders, often combined with fibre ruffs at the neck.
- **Masking costume** — Full-body raffia suits with long fibre neck ruffs and sleeves worn with *kifwebe* masks, designed to conceal the dancer's body entirely so that the appearing being is not read as a man in a mask.

### Architecture

The Songye have no monumental architecture and no single signature dwelling form comparable to the Central Asian yurt; theirs is a village architecture of rectangular earth-walled, thatch-roofed houses arranged along a street or around a plaza, and its ethnographic interest lies less in the buildings than in how space is socially zoned.

- **Village house** — Rectangular pole-frame house with wattle-and-daub walls and a pitched grass or palm-thatch roof, usually single-roomed with a shaded veranda and an adjacent cooking shelter.
- **Village plan** — Compounds commonly ranged along a central open street or plaza that serves as the setting for masquerade, judgement and dance.
- **Men's shelter** — Open-sided shelter on the plaza where men gather, hear cases, work and receive visitors; the practical centre of village public life.
- **Chief's enclosure** — A larger compound, sometimes fenced or screened, containing the chief's house, his stool and regalia and the space where communal *nkisi* were kept and fed.
- **Initiation camp** (*mukanda*) — Temporary bush encampment built for boys' initiation and burned or abandoned at its conclusion, so that the most ritually charged Songye building is by design impermanent.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Metal is where Songye craft reaches its height. Smiths held near-ritual status, and iron and copper objects functioned simultaneously as tools, weapons, currency, insignia and sculpture. Pottery, by contrast, is a women's domestic craft, coil-built and largely undecorated beyond incised or roulette-impressed bands.

- **Power figure** (*nkisi*, pl. *mankishi*) — Carved male figure, from small personal examples to communal statues well over a metre high, activated by a ritual specialist (*nganga*) and typically sheathed in copper or brass strip, studded with nails, hung with horns, skins, feathers and beads, with a hollowed head or abdomen packed with medicine; communal figures belonged to a village and were fed and consulted on its behalf.
- **Horn charge** (*nkishi* horn) — Antelope or goat horn set into the crown or belly of a figure and packed with *bishimba* — the composite medicine of mineral, vegetable and animal matter that makes the object efficacious rather than merely representational.
- **Ceremonial axe and adze** (*nsapo* axe, Songye adze) — Forged blades socketed into carved wooden hafts, the adze often surmounted by a human head; carried over the shoulder as an emblem of office rather than used for work.
- **Prestige blade** (*mpungu*) — Forged iron knives, short swords and throwing blades with openwork, pierced or chevron-cut bodies, made to be seen and inherited as much as wielded.
- **Copper cross ingot** (*handa*, and the smaller *kwilu* croisette) — X-shaped copper ingots cast in open sand moulds in the Katangan copper belt and circulating widely among the Songye as currency, bridewealth and buried store of value; also reworked into ornament.
- **Chief's stool** (*kabila*) — Carved wooden stool, sometimes caryatid-supported in the Luba manner, on which a chief was seated at installation and which stood as a proxy for his person and continuity of office.
- **Household pottery** — Coil-built cooking pots, water jars and palm-wine vessels made by women, open-fired, with restrained incised or comb-impressed decoration at the shoulder.
- **Incised gourds and cups** — Calabashes and wooden cups for palm wine, worked with the same triangle-and-chevron vocabulary that appears on masks and blades.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Songye adornment is worked on and into the body as much as hung from it: scarification and coiffure were the primary signs of identity, status and beauty, and the same forms are the first thing a Songye sculptor renders on a figure's head and torso.

- **Scarification** (*kilonda*) — Raised keloid patterns cut on forehead, temples, torso and abdomen in rows of dots, chevrons and lozenges, marking lineage, initiation grade and adult beauty; they are transcribed faithfully onto *mankishi*, which is why the figures function as a record of styles no longer practised.
- **Coiffure** (*shankadi* coiffure) — Elaborate cantilevered and cruciform hairstyles built over a fibre or cane armature and oiled, shared with the Luba-Shankadi and rendered in sculpture as a high stepped or crested crown.
- **Copper ornament** — Arm rings, anklets, neck rings and coiled wire worked from Katangan copper, worn in quantity as both adornment and portable wealth.
- **Beaded collar and belt** — Glass-bead collars, belts and bandoliers, imported beads entering the region in volume through nineteenth-century trade.
- **Amulets** — Small charged bundles, horns, teeth and miniature figures worn at the neck or waist for protection, fertility and success in hunting or litigation.

## Music & performance

Songye music is percussion-led and functional: it convenes the court, escorts the dead, announces the masks and drives the initiation camp. Slit drums and membrane drums carry signalling as well as rhythmic duties, and singing is typically antiphonal, a soloist answered by a chorus in the responsorial pattern general to the Congo basin.

- **Membrane drums** — Single-headed drums of graded size played in sets with hands and sticks, the largest belonging to the chief and sounded to convene assembly or to mark a death.
- **Slit drum** — Hollowed hardwood log with a longitudinal slit, struck to carry messages between villages and to accompany masquerade, its two pitches able to encode speech tone.
- **Lamellophone** — Plucked-lamella instrument on a board or box resonator, played solo or in small ensembles for entertainment and praise song.
- **Whistles, horns and flutes** — Side-blown antelope horns and small whistles used for signalling in hunt and masquerade, and by mask-society members to produce non-human vocal effects.
- **Responsorial song** — Solo praise and narrative lines answered by chorus and handclapping, the vehicle for lineage praise, work song and mourning.

## Dance & theatre

Songye performance is masquerade rather than staged theatre, and its central institution is *bwadi bwa kifwebe*, the society that owns the striated masks. The society policed behaviour, collected on the chief's behalf, appeared at investitures and funerals, and in some communities acted as an executive counterweight to the chief himself — so the masked appearance is best read as an act of governance performed as spectacle.

- **Mask society** (*bwadi bwa kifwebe*) — Initiated men's association whose masked appearances enforced norms, levied contributions, and framed the installation and death of chiefs.
- **Striated mask** (*kifwebe*, pl. *bifwebe*) — Mask with a domed forehead, projecting box mouth and a surface covered in fine parallel incised striations, worn with a full raffia suit; the striation itself (*bifwebe* striation) is the signature Songye design idiom.
- **Male mask** (*kilume*) — Aggressive type, commonly distinguished by a high sagittal crest and bolder striation, associated with disruptive force and public discipline; the larger the crest, the greater the power commonly attributed.
- **Female mask** (*kikashi*) — Flatter, white-dominated, low- or no-crested type, associated with benevolence, order and reproductive continuity, danced with more restrained movement.
- **Danced possession and grotesque play** — The masks move between menace and comedy, with lurching, stamping and sudden charges at spectators, a controlled release of violence that the society alone is licensed to perform.
- **Initiation performance** (*mukanda*) — Songs, dances and instruction staged inside the boys' bush camp and at the public re-entry of the initiates, the point at which the village sees its sons returned as men.

## Festivals & rituals

Songye ritual life is keyed to office, initiation and death rather than to an agricultural calendar of named festivals, and its most important events are convened rather than annual: an investiture, a funeral, the closing of an initiation, the consultation of a communal figure in crisis.

- **Chiefly investiture** — Multi-day installation at which the chief is seated on the *kabila* stool, invested with *bwalelo* regalia, and presented to the masks and the assembled lineages.
- **Boys' initiation** (*mukanda*) — Seclusion in a bush camp with circumcision, instruction, ordeal and the learning of society knowledge, closed by a public return that confers adult status; the institution is shared across a wide Central African belt.
- **Funerary rite** — Extended mourning with drumming, dancing and shaving of heads, followed by burial with grave goods; the death of a chief in particular required masked appearance and the transfer of regalia.
- **Feeding the communal figure** — Periodic offerings, libations and sacrifices made to a village *nkisi* by its keeper, with consultation in epidemic, infertility, drought or war.
- **Divination and affliction cure** (*nganga* consultation) — Diagnosis of misfortune by a specialist and the making, charging or reactivation of a charm to answer it, the ordinary ritual work around which the spectacular objects cluster.

## Foodways

Songye subsistence combined shifting cultivation with hunting, fishing in the Lomami and Lualaba systems, and small livestock, and the modern diet is built on cassava introduced through the Atlantic trade. The staple is a stiff porridge of cassava flour, often blended with maize, eaten by hand with a sauce.

Cassava dominates twice over, as a flour for the porridge commonly called *bidia* or *fufu* and as a leaf, pounded and long-simmered into the dark green relish known widely in the Congo as *pondu* or *sombe*. Sauces are built on palm oil, groundnut paste, dried and smoked river fish, and bushmeat; maize, plantain, beans, yams, sweet potato, pumpkin leaves, okra, mushrooms and forest caterpillars all enter the pot seasonally. Goats and chickens are kept less for daily eating than for sacrifice, bridewealth and hospitality, so meat is frequently a ritual food. Palm wine tapped from oil and raffia palms, and maize or cassava beer, are the drinks of assembly and rite, poured in libation to ancestors before being drunk by the living; the same gourd cups carry the incised geometry found on masks and blades. Cooking is women's work over a three-stone hearth in an open kitchen shelter, and the pots, mortars and stirring paddles are locally made. Food etiquette encodes rank plainly: choice portions go to the chief, to elders and to guests, and an invitation refused is a visible insult.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Songye had no writing before the colonial period, and their historical and juridical memory was carried in praise, genealogy and narrative performed aloud. Chiefly genealogies recited at investiture serve as political charters, establishing who may hold office and on what grounds; because Songye and Luba traditions cite one another, these recitations are also the main evidence for the shared Shaba origin that both peoples claim. Alongside genealogy stands a large corpus of *nkindi*-type proverbs and aphorisms — condensed, figurative, and used with real weight in dispute settlement, where a well-placed proverb can function as precedent. Folk narrative includes trickster and animal tales, etiological stories explaining the origin of death, fire, chieftaincy and the smith's craft, and hunter's tales; riddling contests and tongue-twisters are a children's and young people's genre that doubles as training in the figurative language adults will later use in court. Initiation transmits a further stratum of restricted verbal knowledge — mask songs, society formulae and the names and properties of medicines — that is deliberately not public, which means the published record of Songye oral literature is thinner than the living one. Objects participate in this verbal economy: a *nkisi* has a name and a history of what it has done, and beaded and cowrie-worked regalia of the *lukasa* type point toward the wider Luba practice of using worked surfaces as prompts for recitation.

## Language & religion

Kisonge (Songe) is a Bantu language of the Luba group within Guthrie's Zone L, closely related to Kiluba and Tshiluba and mutually intelligible with neighbouring varieties in places; most Songye today also use Swahili or Tshiluba as a regional lingua franca and French as the language of school and state, and dialect variation across the many chiefdoms is considerable. Religiously, the great majority of Songye are Christian — Catholic and various Protestant churches, with independent and Pentecostal congregations growing — but Christianity was received onto an existing framework rather than replacing it. That framework recognises a distant creator god (commonly *Efile Mukulu*) who is not approached directly, an active and near population of ancestral spirits and nature spirits who must be kept in good relation, and misfortune understood as caused rather than random: illness, infertility, crop failure and death are traced to witchcraft, to a broken obligation, or to an offended spirit, and are answered by diagnosis and remedy rather than by resignation. The *nganga* is the specialist who performs that work, and the *nkisi* figure is its instrument — which is why Songye figures are aggressive in aspect and accumulative in construction, since each addition of horn, nail, skin or medicine is an increment of capacity. Mission pressure and, later, state hostility drove much of this practice out of public view during the twentieth century and displaced very large numbers of figures and masks into European and American collections, so that the most complete Songye ritual assemblages now sit in museums while the practices they served continue, altered and often discreet, at home.
