## Overview

The Songye (also Songe, Basongye) are a Bantu people of the central Democratic Republic of the Congo, occupying the savanna and gallery-forest belt between the Sankuru/Lulibash river in the west and the Lualaba in the east, across present-day East Kasai, parts of Katanga and Kivu. They number on the order of a million and speak Songe, a Bantu language of the Niger-Congo family. Politically they are not one polity but thirty-four conglomerate societies, each under a chief advised by a council of elders and nobles (*bilolo*); groups east of the Lomami call themselves Songye, while western polities identify as Kalebwe, Eki, Ilande, Bala, Chofwe, Sanga and Tempa. They matter in folk-culture terms chiefly for two sculptural complexes that rank among the most recognisable in Central Africa: the striated *kifwebe* mask and the charged *nkishi* power figure, both produced within a framework of blacksmithing, chiefly investiture and mystical specialism.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Songye surface pattern lives primarily on wood, skin and metal rather than on loom-woven cloth; the region's weaving is raffia-based and closely related to that of the Kuba and Luba neighbours.

**Mbuki raffia cloth** — panels woven on a single-heddle loom from the fibre of the raffia palm leaflet, pounded soft and used for wrappers, masquerade skirting and funerary payment. Songye raffia work is plainer than Kuba cut-pile: its patterning comes from warp/weft contrast, stitched appliqué and dye rather than from embroidered pile.

**Bifwebe striation** — the defining Songye pattern system: dense parallel incised lines (*bifwebe*) covering the entire face of a *kifwebe* mask, cut with an adze and knife, then filled alternately with white kaolin, red camwood and black soot. The striation is not decoration but a colour argument — white for order and the benevolent, red and black for the dangerous forces the mask carries.

**Kilonda scarification** — raised keloid patterning cut into the skin of the abdomen, temples and back, and transferred onto the bodies of *nkishi* figures, where the sculptor reproduces the owner's or lineage's marks. On female figures the scarification field above and around the navel is the principal decorated surface.

**Kalengula** — copper sheet and strip applied over carved wood, most often over the face, brow and temples of a large communal *nkishi*. Beaten copper is both protective skin and statement of wealth, tying the figure to the copper economy of the Katanga belt.

**Lukasa-style beadwork** — glass-bead and cowrie work on regalia, belts and figure attachments, shared with the neighbouring Luba, whose *lukasa* memory boards use beads and pegs as mnemonic pattern. Songye application is on dress and on the accumulated surface of power figures rather than on freestanding boards.

**Nkishi horn charge** — not cloth, but the most consequential "applied surface" in Songye practice: an antelope horn, packed with *bijimba*, driven into the crown of a power figure, then surrounded by shells, animal skin, feathers, nails and studs. The accreted exterior is deliberately additive and changes over the object's working life.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Bifwebe* — parallel striations reading as furrows or animal pelage; *kalengula* — copper-sheet facing; the square protruding mouth; globular pierced eyes; the linear ridge nose; the sagittal crest (large on elder male masks, low or absent on female); triangular and lozenge navel scarification; cowrie clusters signalling fertility and wealth; the black/white/red triad of moral opposition.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress in the pre-colonial and early colonial village was a raffia wrapper for both sexes, knotted at the waist for men and secured above the breast or at the hip for women, with imported cotton cloth displacing raffia through the twentieth century. Ceremonial dress is entirely different in register. A *kifwebe* masquerader is covered head to foot: the wooden mask, a full body-suit and sleeves of knotted raffia netting, long flowing black raffia fibres taken from tree bark and roots, and a goat skin fastened around the waist — no skin is visible, since the dancer is not a person during performance. The volume and finish of the costume rise with the dancer's rank in the *bwadi bwa kifwebe*. Chiefly regalia — *bwalelo* — assembles leopard skin, copper ornament, beaded belts, a fly-whisk, ceremonial adze and staff, and is put on at investiture, when the new supreme chief performs the *kutomboka musanga* dance marking him as warrior and protector.

### Architecture

Songye villages are agricultural settlements of rectangular houses with walls of wattle-and-daub over a pole frame and steeply pitched thatch of grass or palm, arranged along a central open space that serves for masquerade, judgment and assembly. The chief's compound is distinguished less by scale than by furniture and enclosure: a fenced court, a shrine structure housing the large communal *nkishi*, and the *kabila* stool, the carved seat that materialises the office. Family-level power figures are kept in a lineage shrine within the compound; smaller personal figures travel with their owners. The blacksmith's forge is the other charged building of the village — smithing is tied to the founding cultural heroes, and Songye axes and blades circulated widely enough to be excavated in the Khami ruins in present-day Zimbabwe and adopted by the Luba.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Metalwork is the craft on which Songye prestige rests. Smiths produced the *nsapo* axe and the *mpungu* iron blade — parade and status weapons with openwork or inlaid copper hafts — and the **Songye ceremonial adze**, whose blade issues from a carved human head in the manner of Luba and Shaba court adzes. Copper reached the Songye as currency ingots: the *kwilu* croisette and the larger *handa* cross ingot, X-shaped castings that functioned as wealth, bridewealth and raw material for *kalengula* sheathing. In wood, the *kabila* chief's stool, headrests, cups and the *kiteki* figure type round out the corpus. Ceramics are domestic and undecorated relative to the sculpture: coiled, open-fired cooking and water jars made by women.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment concentrates on copper, iron, cowrie and glass bead — bangles, anklets, neck rings and belt strings, worn in quantity as accumulated wealth rather than as single showpieces. Body marking is the more culturally weighted practice: *kilonda* keloid scarification on the abdomen, temples and back, cut at initiation and after childbirth, which the sculptors of *nkishi* figures reproduce faithfully enough that a figure's marks identify its lineage. Hair is dressed in high built coiffures of the **shankadi** type associated with the wider Luba–Songye zone — cascading stepped or terraced arrangements built over a frame, held with oil and clay, and echoed in the carved coiffures of figures and masks. Charms worn on the body — small horns, packets and studded pendants — are made by the *nganga* on the same principles as the large figures.

## Music & performance

Songye music is exceptionally well documented for a Central African people because Alan P. Merriam spent long field periods in the Bala village of Lupupa Ngye and wrote both the village ethnography *An African World* and the classic essay "The Bala Musician". Instruments include slit drums and sets of tuned membrane drums, which accompany masquerade and announce assemblies; rattles and iron bells struck to mark the beat; and the lamellophone and xylophone types of the wider Luba–Songye region. Song is overwhelmingly responsorial — a soloist lines out a phrase, the assembled group answers — and repertoire is organised by occasion rather than by concert genre: initiation and circumcision songs of *mukanda*, chiefly praise, hunting songs, funeral lament, work songs and drinking songs. Merriam's central finding concerns the musician's social position: the Bala village recognised music-making as a specialism and paid for it, while regarding professional musicians with ambivalence — a status simultaneously valued and mistrusted, which he compared explicitly to the deviant-but-tolerated artist elsewhere. The masquerade band is inseparable from the mask: the *kifwebe* dancer performs to drum patterns that cue the shifts between aggressive and controlled movement.

## Dance & theatre

The core performance tradition is the *kifwebe* masquerade of the **bwadi bwa kifwebe** society. Its theatre is moral instruction: male masks — crested, harshly striated, polychrome — carry aggressive, unstable, unpredictable movement, appearing at initiation, circumcision, the enthronement of a supreme chief and at chiefly funerals, where their disruption asserts the society's power to police antisocial behaviour and sorcery. Female masks, whitened with kaolin, rounder in feature and longer in face, move gently and under control, and appear at public ceremonies and rites concerned with reproduction and continuity. All dancers are men. The choreographic contrast is the message: the masquerade demonstrates to spectators how one is and is not to behave. Chiefly investiture has its own dance, the *kutomboka musanga*, which enacts the shedding of blood and the chief's double nature as warrior and protector.

## Festivals & rituals

Songye ceremonial life is keyed to the life cycle and to political succession rather than to a fixed annual calendar. Boys' initiation and circumcision (*mukanda*) is the central rite of passage, held for an age cohort, during which the *kifwebe* masks appear and initiates are taught conduct and obligation. Entry into the *bwadi bwa kifwebe* itself demands a further initiation: learning an esoteric secret language, and undergoing a deliberately violent and frightening ordeal intended to break the initiate's fear of the unknown. The investiture of a supreme chief is the largest public event, with elaborate ritual and the *kutomboka musanga* dance. Funerals of chiefs and dignitaries call out the male masks. Hunts organised by the chief are ritual as much as subsistence, staged to display the power conferred on him by the founding cultural hero. Consultation of the *nganga* — the community-sanctioned specialist in mystical power, opposed to the malevolent *basha masende* sorcerers — punctuates ordinary life: he commissions each *nkishi*, prescribing its dimensions, sex, morphology and species of wood, compounds the *bijimba* charge for its abdominal and cranial cavities, and later adds the shells, horns, skins and studs that raise its potency.

## Foodways

The Songye are primarily farmers, and the staple is cassava, pounded and cooked into a stiff porridge eaten with a relish of leaves, beans, fish or meat; maize, plantain, groundnut, palm oil and cassava leaf fill out the everyday diet. Hunting, organised at village or chiefly level, supplies game, and river fishing supplements it; trade with neighbouring communities brings in what the village does not produce. Palm wine is the drink of assembly, poured at judgments, funerals and masquerade, and libation to the ancestors precedes consumption. Specific named dishes are not reliably documented in the ethnographic literature on the Songye distinct from the general Kasai–Katanga repertoire, so they are omitted here rather than guessed.

## Oral tradition & literature

Songye oral tradition is organised around dynastic origin narrative and proverb. The founding account traces the people to Tshimbale and Kongolo, figures who established the Luba kingdom and from whose empire the Songye ancestors withdrew after political dissension — a charter narrative that legitimises chiefly authority as inherited from the founding cultural hero, and that the Kalebwe chiefdoms link specifically to the Luba region of Shaba. The proverb is a formal genre in its own right: Muepu Mibanga's *Songye: livre des proverbes* (1988) collects 277 pages of them. A parallel, restricted oral corpus exists inside the *bwadi bwa kifwebe*, whose initiates must master a secret esoteric language unintelligible to non-members. Twentieth-century documentation includes Leo Frobenius's 1905–06 field notes on the Bassonge and Merriam's village monograph; Congolese authors such as Viktor Kabamba Nkamany and Jean-Marie Lusuna Kazadi have written from within the community since the 1980s.

## Language & religion

Songe is a Bantu language of the Niger-Congo family, spoken across the Sankuru–Lualaba interfluve with dialect variation tracking the political divisions between eastern Songye proper and the western Kalebwe, Eki, Ilande, Bala, Chofwe, Sanga and Tempa. There is no indigenous script; written Songe uses Latin orthography, alongside Swahili, Tshiluba and French. Christianity is now widespread, but the older cosmology remains the frame for ritual practice: a single supreme creator, *Efile Mukulu*, who brought into being sun, moon, star, water, earth, fire, wind and rainbow. Water, a female element, delivers humans into physical life; each person receives a *kikudi*, an individualised mental force originating from a star and distributed by the wind; earth is the final matrix. Benevolent ancestral spirits guard the living, while *mikishi* wander malevolently, and rivers, waterfalls and particular trees are the points at which the living reach the ancestors.
