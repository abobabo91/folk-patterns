## Overview

The Makuwa people, also known as Makuwa or Wamakuwa, are a Bantu ethnic group found in northern Mozambique and in southern border provinces of Tanzania, including the Mtwara Region. They are the largest ethnic group in Mozambique and are concentrated mainly north of the Zambezi River. Sociologists distinguish four geographical and linguistic subdivisions: lower or Lolo Makua, upper or Lomwe Makua, Maua, and Niassa Makua or Medo. The total Makua population is estimated at about 3.5 million. They speak variants of the Makua language, also called Emakua, a Bantu-group language. Their documented cultural history includes agriculture, hunting, metal ore processing, tool manufacture, trade, oral tradition, traditional religion, and material objects made from iron, wood, bamboo, pottery, fibre, gourds, and seed pods.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used do not document Makua textile traditions, named textile patterns, or distinctive woven techniques.

**Motif vocabulary.** The museum records describe incised lines, circular bands, punctures, zig-zag compartments, straight vertical lines, horizontal bands, and carved ornament on objects, but do not identify these as named Makua motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources used do not describe everyday or ceremonial Makua dress, garments, head coverings, belts, footwear, or named clothing traditions. The sources mention that, before the 18th century, Makua people exchanged food, ivory tusks, and metal products for textiles, salt, and other products. They also state that the Makua language is spoken alongside Afrikaans and Zulu in South Africa, Portuguese in Mozambique, Swahili by some elders and by many people on the Tanzania-Mozambican border, and English in South Africa and Tanzania.

### Architecture

The sources used do not describe Makua houses, settlements, roofs, building materials, workshops, or named architectural forms. They state that the Makua people were concentrated in a large region north of the Zambezi River and that their legendary original home was Namuli.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Makua people have a documented history of processing metal ore and manufacturing tools. Iron was extracted from ore in a wood-burning hearth as a community activity and was worked into axes, knives, spears, rings, and other items. Museum records include an axe with a wooden handle and an iron blade, handmade pottery pipe-bowls, a black pottery bowl shaped like an hour glass, bamboo objects, wooden boxes for gun caps, a wooden head-rest, a day reckoner made from short lengths of bamboo threaded on fibre, and a carved ebony figure of a kneeling woman grinding corn.

The museum records also describe an earthenware lime box, a bamboo snuff tube, a double-gourd smoking pipe, a rattle made from thirteen seed pods, and a bamboo flute with three side holes and simple incised decoration.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used do not describe named Makua jewelry traditions, tattooing, henna, hair practices, or ritual body adornment. Museum records identify seven small circular wooden ear ornaments with incised linear patterns. They also record charms made from teeth, including two *ngiri* (?) teeth suspended on a cord, four leopard's teeth threaded on soft brown cord, and two *ngiri* (?) teeth suspended on two shreds of cotton cloth.

The Wikipedia source states that Makua metalworkers made rings. It does not explain their form, use, gender association, or ritual meaning.

## Music & performance

The museum catalogue records a bamboo flute, described as having one end open, one end closed, three holes at the side, and simple incised decoration. It also records a rattle made from thirteen seed pods threaded onto fibre string and attached to a wooden stick; the seed pods contain dried seeds and have small slits or decorative cuts. These records identify objects but do not describe their musical scales, performance settings, repertories, or names in Makua.

The sources used do not document Makua song genres, named musical traditions, professional performers, wedding or funeral music, court performance, or other musical contexts. The Wikipedia sources describe Makua language varieties and dialects but do not connect them to specific musical forms.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used do not document named Makua dances, theatre traditions, masquerades, dramatic forms, or ceremonial and entertainment distinctions. The museum records include a rattle and a flute, but provide no information about performances in which they were used.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not provide an annual Makua festival calendar or identify named seasonal festivals. They also do not describe Makua birth, initiation, wedding, funeral, or coming-of-age ceremonies.

The traditional religion of the Makua people includes the principal deity Muluku and an evil spirit named Minepa. A legend states that Muluku created the first man and woman from two holes in the ground and gave them the art of using tools. After the humans disobeyed, Muluku summoned two monkeys and gave them the same tools and instruction. The legend explains that Muluku cut off the monkeys' tails and fastened them to the man and woman, telling the monkeys to be human and the humans to be monkeys.

## Foodways

The sources used do not identify Makua staple grains, named dishes, cooking methods, ceremonial foods, beverages, or dietary rules. They state that the Makua people have traditionally been dedicated to agriculture and hunting. Before the 18th century, they exchanged food, ivory tusks, and metal products for textiles, salt, and other products.

A museum record depicts an ebony carved figure of a kneeling woman grinding corn, with a basin in front of her. The record does not identify the corn, the grinding process, the food produced, or the social context of the activity.

## Oral tradition & literature

A mythical legend in Makua oral tradition says that the Makua ancestor was the first man and woman born of Namuli, their original home, while other living creatures came from nearby mountains. Scholars are described as uncertain whether Makua origins were in the mountains, west of Lake Malawi, or northern lands such as Tanzania or the south. They agree that the Makua were likely an established ethnic group in northern Mozambique by the 1st millennium CE.

Another Makua religious legend concerns Muluku, Minepa, the creation of the first man and woman, and the transformation of humans and monkeys. The sources do not document named epics, proverbs, riddles, literary works, or contemporary literary preservation projects. An ethnography of the Makua people was published in 1847 by Eugene de Froberville after he interviewed and learned Makua traditions and culture from over three hundred Makua people in Mauritius plantations.

## Language & religion

Makhuwa, also called Emakhuwa, Makua, Macua, or Mozambican, is the primary Bantu language of northern Mozambique. It is spoken by roughly 5.8 million Makua people, especially north of the Zambezi River and in Nampula Province. The Makua language is the most widely spoken indigenous language of Mozambique.

The sources distinguish dialects and varieties including Central Makhuwa, Meetto, Chirima, Marrevone, eNahara, eSaka, and Ruvuma Makhuwa. Mutual intelligibility among these varieties is limited, and Central Makhuwa, or Makhuwa-Makhuwana, is the basis of the standard language. In Makhuwa, tone is distinctive; the eNahara dialect has low and high tones, with high tone indicated by an acute accent.

Traditional Makua religion reveres ancestors and nature spirits. It is held by 66 to 70 percent of the Makua people according to the Wikipedia source. Coastal Makua traders converted to the Shafi'i school of Sunni Islam under the influence of Swahili-Arab customers. The coastal Muslim people are called the *Maka* in Makua; the term may derive from Mecca or from Makua words meaning “salt” or “coast.”
