## Overview

The Kanuri are an African ethnic group living largely in the lands of the former Kanem and Bornu Empires in Niger, Nigeria, Chad, and Cameroon, with a diaspora community in Sudan. The largest population resides in northeastern Nigeria, while Kanuri communities also live in southeastern Niger, northern Cameroon, and parts of Chad. Some 3 million Kanuri speakers live in Nigeria, not including approximately 200,000 speakers of the Manga dialect; the Kanuri population in Niger is given as 400,000. Kanuri varieties belong to the Nilo-Saharan language family and are associated historically with the Kanem and Bornu empires. The people have traditionally been sedentary, engaging in farming, fishing in the Chad Basin, trade, and salt processing.

## Oral tradition & literature

Proverbs are important in Kanuri religious and social life. They are created by wise old men and teach social attitudes, beliefs, experiences, and lessons drawn from daily life. Proverbs may warn people against foolish or dangerous acts or encourage them. Kanuri oral literature has also been recorded in written collections. In 1854, Sigismund Koelle published *African Native Literature, or Proverbs, Tales, Fables, and Historical Fragments in the Kanuri or Bornu Language*, containing Kanuri texts with English translations, including proverbs, stories, fables, and historical fragments. The sources state that Koelle misidentified the trickster ground-squirrel, *kə̀nyérì*, as a weasel. Richard Francis Burton later included a selection of the proverbs in *Wit and Wisdom from West Africa*.

Kanuri tradition says that Sef, son of Dhu Ifazan of Yemen, arrived in Kanem in the ninth century and united the population into the Sayfawa dynasty. The same source describes this tradition as reflecting later Islamic influence.

## Language & religion

Kanuri is a Saharan dialect continuum of the Nilo-Saharan language family, spoken by Kanuri and Kanembu peoples in Nigeria, Niger, Chad, and Cameroon, as well as by a diaspora community in Sudan. Its main dialects include Manga Kanuri and Yerwa Kanuri; other varieties listed are Central Kanuri, Tumari Kanuri, Bilma Kanuri, and Kanembu. Kanuri has subject–object–verb word order, three tones, and both postpositions and post-nominal modifiers. It has been written in the Ajami Arabic script for at least four hundred years, especially in religious and court contexts, and is also written in a modified Latin script. A standardized romanized orthography was officially approved in Maiduguri, Nigeria, in 1975.

The Kanuri are predominantly Sunni Muslim. Kanem became a centre of Muslim learning, and the Kanuri controlled the area surrounding Lake Chad through the Kanem Empire, which reached its height in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.
