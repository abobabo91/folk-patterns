## Overview

The Tikar, also historically called Tikari, Tigar, Tigari, and Tigre, are a Central African ethnic group in Cameroon. They inhabit the Adamawa Region and certain regions of Bamenda Province, including areas associated with the Fungum, Bum, Kom, Mbem, Mbaw, Wiya, War, Tang, Banso, Ndop, and Bafut. Six adjoining Tikar kingdoms are identified: Bankim, Ngambé-Tikar, Kong, Nditam, Ngoumé, and Gâ. The Tikar speak Tikar, a Northern Bantoid, semi-Bantu language in the Niger-Congo family. They are described as artists, artisans, storytellers, ironworkers, and makers of masks, textiles, brass pipes, horns, trumpets, and elaborate palaces.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Toghu** — The design of Toghu cloth print became a cultural marker of the Tikar and Grassfields peoples. Its intricate designs are used for clothing, architecture, art, and the demarcation of royal ritual spaces.

**Ndop** — Ndop cloth print is identified alongside Toghu as a cultural marker of the Tikar and Grassfields peoples.

**Toghu and Ndop cloth print** — Together, these textile designs created a style that distinguished the Tikar and other Grassfields peoples from groups outside the region.

### Clothing & dress

The sources identify Toghu and Ndop cloth print as textile designs used for clothing. The designs are also associated with royal ritual spaces, architecture, and art, indicating a ceremonial and cultural use beyond ordinary dress.

### Architecture

Tikar and Grassfields palaces are described as elaborate structures. They feature hand-carved pillars supporting the roof overhangs, together with an ensemble of door posts, lintels, and sills framing the entrance. Interior doorways face open courtyards.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Tikar are associated with iron-working, which contributed to their importance in trade and politics in Cameroon. They are also known for highly detailed masks and beautifully decorated brass pipes. Tikar horns and trumpets are purposefully sculpted for particular spiritual and cultural events.

## Music & performance

The sources identify Tikar horns and trumpets as objects that play a significant role in spiritual and cultural ceremonies. Each design is purposefully sculpted for a specific event. The sources also describe the Tikar as storytellers and record an extensive spiritual system of ancestral reverence. No information is provided about musical occasions such as weddings, funerals, courts, or festivals.

## Dance & theatre

*Menang* is identified as a traditional dance of the Tikar.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe planting rituals through which the Tikar bless seeds and work implements. The sources also record beliefs concerning pregnancy and birth: blood that would normally be released during menstruation was believed to form the fetus’s skin, blood, flesh, and organs, while the father’s sperm was believed to form the bones, brain, heart, and teeth; in the case of a son, masculinity was also attributed to this.

Ancestral reverence is central to Tikar spirituality. Ancestral spirits were traditionally believed to be embodied in the skulls of deceased ancestors. The eldest living male in each lineage possesses the skulls, and when a family relocates, a dwelling purified by a diviner is built to house them. When a skull is not preserved, a family member undergoes a ceremony involving the pouring of libations into the ground, after which earth from the offering site represents the skull.

## Foodways

They mention planting rituals connected with seeds and work implements, but provide no further account of food production or consumption.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Tikar are described as storytellers, and oral tradition plays a major role in accounts of their origins and history. Some oral traditions trace their origin to the Nile River Valley in present-day Sudan, while other accounts connect their ancestors with the Kingdom of Kush. Tikar oral tradition also recounts migration into Cameroon, the establishment of kingdoms, and the flight from the spread of Islam.

Mbum oral tradition states that the ancestors of the Tikar were ruled by Nya Sana, whose royal lineage eventually produced Took Gokor. Another Mbum Fon and Yesum or Yelaa are credited with founding the Kingdom of Nganha. Their daughter, Princess Wou-Ten, also called Betaka or Belaka, is said to have established the Kingdom of Tinkala and ruled as Fon from 1201 to 1246.

The sources also preserve traditions concerning Tinki, Guié, Ncharé, N'Gouen, Doundje, Kpo, G'Batteu, Mbli, Indie, and Ouhin, whose migrations were associated with the founding of Tikar kingdoms, villages, and dynasties. A separate account says that Tinki ruled until his death in 1387, after which Mveing ruled until 1413.

## Language & religion

Tikar, also called Tigé, Tigré, or Tikari, is a Northern Bantoid, semi-Bantu language spoken in Cameroon by the Tikar people and by the Bedzan Pygmies, who speak their own dialect. It is hypothesized to be a divergent language in the Niger-Congo family. The language has four dialects, including Tikari, Tigé, and Túmú; Túmú is spoken in Bankim and Nditam. The available evidence places Tikar closest to the Mambiloid and Dakoid languages.

The Tikar predominantly practice Christianity today, while a small number practice traditional religions and Islam. They refer to God the Creator as Nyuy and maintain a tradition of reverence for lineage ancestors. The sources describe Islam-related migrations from northern Cameroon toward Adamawa and later toward Foumban, in response to forced conversion by Muslim Fulani invaders. They also state that the Fulani conquest did not result in Islamization, although Islam was accepted by a later Bamum ruler, Sultan Ibrahim Mbouombouo Njoya, in the early 20th century.
