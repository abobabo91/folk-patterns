## Overview

The Nuer are a Nilotic people concentrated in the Greater Upper Nile region of South Sudan, with a large population across the border in the Ethiopian region of Gambella. They are the second-largest ethnic group in South Sudan and the largest in Gambella. They call themselves *Naath*, and their language, *Thok Naath*, belongs to the Western Nilotic branch of the Nilotic family. The Nuer are pastoralists whose herds are not only an economy but the organising framework of their life: as E. E. Evans-Pritchard put it, cattle are "the thread that runs through Nuer institutions, language, rites of passage, politics, economy, and allegiances." For folk culture that matters concretely — raw materials, personal names, marriage payments and sacrificial practice all derive from the herd, and the objects museums hold from them are overwhelmingly made of hide, horn and the by-products of cattle-keeping.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here — the Wikipedia articles and the museum catalogue records of the objects shown — do not describe a loom-woven or dyed textile tradition among the Nuer. What they document instead is worked hide and leather, and one body-marking tradition that carries a named, patterned design:

**Gaar** — the facial markings cut as part of initiation into adulthood. The most common male initiation pattern is six parallel horizontal lines cut across the forehead with a razor, often with a dip in the lines above the nose. Dotted patterns are also common, especially among the Bul Nuer and among women. The pattern varies between Nuer subgroups, so the marking identifies not just an initiated adult but which section of the people they belong to.

**Worked hide** — the museum objects shown here are cut and fitted skin rather than cloth: a woman's apron of kid leather whose lower edge is cut into two V-shapes with long leather fringes attached, and a dancing girl's girdle of hippopotamus leather. Evans-Pritchard records that the Nuer raw-material repertoire — drums, rugs, clothing, spears, shields, containers and leather goods — comes from cattle.

### Clothing & dress

The catalogue records of the objects in this atlas give the clearest picture the sources offer. A woman's apron is made of kid leather, the lower edge cut into two V-shapes from which long leather fringes hang. A dancing girl's girdle is made of hippopotamus leather, with ten pendant iron cones attached at the front together with an iron ring — an object made for movement, where the metal answers the dancer. A British Museum photographic print of a young man blowing into a milk cow shows him wearing what the cataloguer records as waist-beads; another print shows a woman wearing neck-, arm- and wrist-ornaments and a child wearing neck-, wrist- and ankle-ornaments, which indicates that ornament rather than garment carried much of the visible dress. The sources do not describe Nuer head coverings, footwear, or a separate ceremonial costume distinct from everyday dress.

### Architecture

The sources say little directly about Nuer building. What they establish is the constraint: the Nuer are pastoralists who move seasonally — travelling when heavy rains come, to protect cattle from hoof disease, and when grazing and water for the herd run short, moving up to higher ground. Two British Museum photographs taken among the Nuer show thatched shelters, in one case three of them standing behind the photographed scene, and in the other a thatched shelter with a fenced compound, inside which stands a decorated clay object the cataloguer records as possibly a shrine. Beyond thatch, fenced compounds and clay built into the compound as something other than a dwelling, the sources used do not describe Nuer house form, construction or ornament.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The objects here are domestic, pastoral and martial. Two **smoking pipes** pair a pottery bowl with a long wooden stem and a gourd mouthpiece; one has a skin mouthpiece and a leather loop decorated with cowrie shells, the other a round bowl finished with a knob. Two **neck-rests** are carved in wood with three legs. A **shield** is made of hippopotamus hide with a central wooden staff, and a **spear** has a wooden shaft and head ornamented with hide and animal hair. Cast metal is represented by a **triple bell** of brass alloy: a large outer bell carrying two smaller bells suspended inside on iron hooks, with a square suspension loop on top, bands of grooves around the body, two bands of rectangles in high relief near the top, and a brass alloy clapper in the innermost bell. Herding equipment is here too, in an **iron cow-bell** mounted on a ring with a plaited hide collar. A pair of boys' **whistles** is made of animal hoof.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Nuer ornament in this collection is metal and hide worked into simple, strong forms: armlets of iron, of brass wire and of hide; a pair of ear pendants of coiled brass wire, and a single ear pendant of iron wire. One iron armlet has been nicked along its edge to give it a row of points. The photographic prints record women and children wearing ornaments at the neck, upper arm, wrist and ankle, and a young man wearing beads at the waist. The major body-marking practice is *gaar*, the facial scarification cut at initiation (above). Circumcision is not historically a Nuer practice; it appears where Nuer have been assimilated or partly assimilated into other groups, and is sometimes imposed on people who have committed incest.

## Music & performance

The sources name no Nuer instrument repertoire in detail, but they do place sound at the centre of ritual. Drums are among the goods Evans-Pritchard lists as made from cattle. The objects shown here are themselves sounding things: a brass alloy triple bell, whose inner bell carries a clapper; an iron cow-bell on a plaited hide collar, which turns the herd itself into an audible presence; and a pair of boys' whistles made of animal hoof. Slaughter is the documented performance context the sources describe most clearly — the Nuer do not kill cattle simply to eat meat, and when an animal is killed the community gathers to perform rituals, dances or songs before and after. One British Museum object, a girdle of hippopotamus leather hung with ten iron cones and an iron ring, is catalogued as a dancing girl's, which makes the dancer's own body a sound source. Beyond this the sources used do not describe Nuer song genres, named instruments, or professional performers.

## Dance & theatre

The sources record that dances and songs accompany the slaughter of cattle, performed collectively before and after the animal is killed, and that a girdle of hippopotamus leather hung with ten iron cones was made for a dancing girl — but they name no individual Nuer dance and describe no dramatic or masking tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The Nuer ritual year in the sources is governed not by a calendar of festivals but by the herd and the rains, and the decisive rites are those of the life cycle and of sacrifice. Initiation into adulthood is marked by cutting *gaar* across the forehead with a razor. Marriage turns on cattle: bridewealth passes from the husband's lineage to the wife's, and that transfer is what makes the children members of the husband's lineage. Two institutions follow from this logic. In **ghost marriage**, a man can "father" children after his death, because descent is defined by the exchange of cattle rather than by biology. An infertile woman may take a wife of her own; that wife's children, biologically fathered by men from other unions, become members of her patrilineage, and she is legally and culturally their father. Girls usually marry at seventeen or eighteen; a girl engaged very young has the wedding and consummation delayed. Sacrifice is the other great rite: an ox killed without ritual intent risks its spirit cursing the killer, while an animal that dies naturally may simply be eaten. At death, flesh, life and soul separate — the flesh goes to the earth, the breath returns to God, and the soul remains as a shadow or reflection and departs with the sacrificed ox to the place of the ghosts. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage element is inscribed for South Sudan.

## Foodways

The Nuer staple crop is millet, eaten as porridge and brewed as beer, and turned to especially in the rainy season when the herds are moved to higher ground. Fish is the other mainstay; the diet consists primarily of fish and millet. Milk is central, and surplus milk is made into cheese where a household has enough. Meat is eaten within limits: cattle are not killed for the pleasure of eating, and the animals slaughtered are typically old or sick, with rituals, dances or songs performed around the killing. Any animal that dies of natural causes is eaten, including animals the Nuer have scavenged. Typical foods include beef, goat, cow's milk and mangoes, and sorghum in three named preparations: *ko̱p*, finely ground, handled until balled and boiled; *walwal*, ground, lightly balled and boiled to a solid porridge; and *injera* or *yɔtyɔt*, a large pancake-like yeast-risen flatbread. The herd yields non-food goods as well: dung is chopped and left to harden and then used for containers and as toothpaste, and dung and urine also make mouthwash; burning dung produces smoke that keeps insects off the cattle.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources name no Nuer epic or folktale cycle, but they document one durable oral form: genealogy. A Nuer person's second name is always a Nuer given name, followed by the father's given name, then the grandfather's, and so on, and many Nuer can recount ten generations of paternal lineage because they carry those names themselves. Names are also a record of events — *Dɔmaac*, "bullet", or *Mac*, "fire or gun", is given to a child born in wartime. *Nya* is the standard prefix on women's names, and *Gat*, "baby of", is a prefix for both, as in *gatnyal*, baby girl. *Nhial*, "sky", is a common name for either. Most Nuer are also nicknamed after cattle: boys take the name of a favourite ox, chosen from its form and colour, girls take the name of the cows they milk, and cow names can be passed down. Since the civil war and the arrival of cash, cattle themselves are named by how they were acquired — "the cattle of money" and "the cattle of girls/daughters".

## Language & religion

*Thok Naath*, "people's language", is a Western Nilotic language, close to Dinka and Atuot, written since 1928 in a Latin-based alphabet of 39 letters. Vowels may be long or short, modal or breathy; breathy voice is written with an underscore, as in *ko̱p*. Dialects include Eastern and Western Jikany, Abigar, Cien, Lou, Nyuong, Thiang, Bul Chol, Gawaar, Laak, Jagei, Leek, Dok and Haak, all sharing one written standard. Nominal inflection is famously irregular — two suffixes across sixteen patterns and some sixty stem alternations, which Baerman called "paradigmatic chaos". In religion, God is *Kwoth*, the spirit of the sky and creator, who comes through rain, lightning and thunder; the rainbow is God's necklace, and the sun and moon are signs of him. Below him, the spirits of the air are the most powerful lesser spirits, and spirits attach to clan-spear names, such as *WiW*, a spirit of war associated with thunder. Many Nuer carry a Christian first name from missionary contact.
