## Overview

The Herero, or *Ovaherero*, are a Bantu ethnic group living primarily in Namibia, with significant populations in Botswana and Angola and a small number in South Africa. In Botswana, communities are found in Maun and villages including Sepopa, Toromuja, Karee, Etsha, Mahalapye, Pilane, Tsabong, Omawaneni, Draaihoek and Makopong. The 2023 census identified 178,987 Namibians as Ovaherero. They speak Otjiherero, a Bantu language of the Niger–Congo family. The Herero are traditionally pastoralists whose economy and social life have been closely connected with cattle. Their material culture includes distinctive women’s dress, horn-shaped headdresses, cattle-related symbolism, metal and leather ornaments, wooden tools and weapons, and objects associated with pastoral life. Their history also includes German colonial rule, the 1904–1907 rebellion and the genocide committed by the German Empire between 1904 and 1908.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Ohorokova** — *Ohorokova* are floor-length dresses worn by married and older Herero women every day, while younger and unmarried women wear them mainly for special occasions. They are high-necked, have voluminous skirts gathered from a high waist or below the bust, use multiple petticoats and may incorporate up to ten metres of fabric.

**Patchworked dresses** — Everyday dresses may be ingeniously patchworked from smaller pieces of fabric, including material salvaged from older garments. Dresses made from a single material are reserved for special occasions.

**Ohorokova vivid fabrics** — The dress style derives from clothing worn by German missionaries in the late nineteenth century but is now made in vivid colours and prints. In urban Windhoek, designers and models are updating the dress with glamorous sheer and embellished fabrics.

**Otjikaiva** — The *otjikaiva* is a horizontal horned headdress worn with women’s dress. It is made to match or coordinate with the dress and can be formed from rolled-up newspaper covered in fabric.

**Ekori** — The traditional headdress *Ekori* symbolized the horns of cows, which were the main source of wealth for the people. Missionaries regarded its shape as a symbol of the devil and rejected it.

**Military-style ceremonial clothing** — On ceremonial occasions, Herero men wear military-style garments including peaked caps, berets, epaulettes, aiguillettes and gaiters. The style is connected with uniforms taken from German soldiers during the 1904–1907 war and is worn to honour fallen ancestors and preserve their memory.

**Leather and bead ornaments** — Museum records document leather, fibre, metal-bead, iron-bead, ostrich-eggshell and copper-bead ornaments associated with Herero, Namibia and German South West Africa.

**Motif vocabulary.** Horns of cows, vertical zig-zags, serrated edges.

### Clothing & dress

Herero women’s dress is characterized by the floor-length *ohorokova*, a high neck, a voluminous gathered skirt, multiple petticoats and long sleeves with sculptural volume. Sleeves may be puffed from the shoulders or frilled at the wrists, and coordinating neckerchiefs are knotted around the neck. Married and older women wear the dresses daily, while younger and unmarried women mainly use them for special occasions. Everyday garments may be assembled from smaller pieces of fabric, whereas single-material dresses are reserved for special occasions.

The most distinctive head covering is the horizontal horned *otjikaiva*, which pays homage to cows that historically sustained the Herero. Decorative brooches and pins may be attached to its centre front. Men’s ceremonial clothing includes peaked caps, berets, epaulettes, aiguillettes and gaiters in a military style. The sources describe women’s neckerchiefs and men’s ceremonial garments.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Museum catalogue records document a range of Herero-associated objects from Namibia and German South West Africa. These include knives with iron blades and wooden handles, daggers with wooden sheaths, swords with straight iron blades and hard brown wooden hilts and sheaths, a wooden crossbow, an arrow and a wooden bottle. A wooden snuff-box is carved on the outside with vertical zig-zags and closed by a wooden disc at the end of a thong. A smoking pipe has a horn mouthpiece, a steatite bowl and a white-metal cover connected to the stem by a chain.

A museum catalogue also records a small meat barrel or plate with a flat bottom and short handles. Its recorded measurements are 17.8 centimetres in length, 8.8 centimetres in width and 5.7 centimetres in height. The inscription identifies it as being from Damara, South-Africa, while the catalogue associates the record with the Herero People and Damaraland.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Museum records document necklets, necklaces, bangles, armlets, leglets, girdles, neck ornaments, head ornaments and ankle rings associated with Herero, Namibia and German South West Africa. Materials include iron beads, copper beads, steel rings, leather, fibre, metal, ostrich eggshell and thongs.

One necklet consists of four strings of cylindrical iron beads with an oval leather plate at the front, ornamented with smaller iron beads. Another necklace is made of cylindrical copper beads with similar iron beads at intervals, threaded on a thong. A bangle or armlet is made of iron with serrated edges, while another is formed of steel rings. A woman’s leglet consists of seven leather loops threaded with iron rings and connected by a thong.

Museum records also describe girdles made of ostrich eggshell or leather, a neck ornament made of small ostrich-eggshell discs divided vertically by leather, a leather and fibre head ornament with metal beads, and a pair of copper ankle rings recorded as women’s marriage rings.

## Festivals & rituals

The Herero religious tradition includes the holy fire *Okuruuo*, also called *OtjikaTjamuaha*. It is located at Okahandja and is understood as a link to ancestors through which they speak to Ndjambi on behalf of the people. During immigration, the fire was doused and quickly relit. From 1923 to 2011 it was situated at the Red Flag Commando.

On Herero Day in 2011, a group associated with Paramount Chief Kuaima Riruako claimed that the fire had faced eastwards for the previous 88 years, although it should face towards the sunset. They removed it and placed it at an undisclosed location, causing controversy within the Ovaherero community.

Cattle are involved in social and religious practices. In celebrations such as marriages, cattle are normally eaten, while religious or ancestral-veneration ceremonies involve the sacrifice of cows or other animals.

## Foodways

Cattle are the most valued domestic animals in Herero culture, and cattle herding and trading are described as the most significant activities. Men conduct cattle herding and cattle trading, while women milk cows, perform household work, harvest small field crops and care for young children. Women also carry out much of the trading through bartering for other goods.

The sour milk *Omaere* was prepared by women in pre-colonial times. The sources state that the worth of an *OmuHerero* woman was measured by milk. Cattle are normally eaten at marriages, while cows or other animals may be sacrificed in religious or ancestral-veneration ceremonies.

Goats and sheep are kept for meat. Goatskin is made into child carriers and household ornaments, and goat dung is considered medicinal and is normally used to treat chickenpox. Horses and donkeys are common means of transport, and some Herero people are believed to consume donkey meat. Dogs are used by men for hunting and herding. Chickens are kept for meat and eggs.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources record the importance of historical memory in ceremonial clothing: Herero men’s military-style dress is worn to honour fallen ancestors and keep memories alive.

The Herero genocide appears in several works of fiction listed by the source, including Uwe Timm’s *Morenga*, Thomas Pynchon’s *Gravity’s Rainbow* and *V.*, Mari Serebrov’s *Mama Namibia*, Guy Saville’s *The Afrika Reich*, Jackie Sibblies Drury’s 2012 play *We Are Proud to Present a Presentation About the Herero of Namibia, Formerly Known as Southwest Africa, From the German Sudwestafrika, Between the Years 1884–1915*, and Anthony Scholefield’s *The Eagles of Malice*, published in 1968. These are literary representations rather than descriptions of Herero oral literature.

## Language & religion

Otjiherero is a Bantu language in the Niger–Congo family and is spoken by the Herero and Mbanderu peoples in Namibia and Botswana, as well as by small communities in southwestern Angola. Between 2015 and 2018, there were 250,000 speakers in these countries. Its distribution includes Hereroland, the Omaheke region, the Otjozondjupa and Kunene regions, and communities in Windhoek.

Otjiherero is taught in Namibian schools as both a native tongue and a secondary language. It is used as principal material at the University of Namibia and is one of six minority languages used by Namibian State Radio. The language was transcribed in an alphabet based on Latin script after missionary Gottlieb Viehe translated the Bible into Herero. Father Peter Heinrich Brincker translated theological works and songs.

Herero religion includes belief in *Okuruuo*, the holy fire linking people with ancestors and Ndjambi. Modern-day Herero are mostly Christians, primarily Catholic, Lutheran and Born-again Christian.
