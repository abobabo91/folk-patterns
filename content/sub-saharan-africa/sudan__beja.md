## Overview

The Beja are a Cushitic-speaking people inhabiting the Eastern Desert from southeastern Egypt through eastern Sudan into northwestern Eritrea, with roots going back to 4000 BC or earlier. They are nomadic pastoralists whose material culture centers on portable equipment like camel pack-saddles, shields, and decorated leather goods. The Beja language is unique as the sole member of the Northern Cushitic branch, and their culture places high value on poetry and formal speech. They became Muslim by the 15th century following Arab migration and intermarriage.

## Material culture

Beja material culture reflects nomadic pastoralist life through portable, practical objects decorated with geometric patterns in leather, plaited plant fiber, and cowrie shells.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Plaited palm-leaf and coiled grass work, along with stained and incised leatherwork, carry the main ornament in Beja objects.

- **Plaited palm-leaf and coiled grass work** — Conical and trumpet-shaped ornamental headpieces are built from coiled plaited palm-stem leaf or coiled grass, covered with leather and sometimes cowrie shells; the same plaiting appears in camel pack-saddles as woven palm-leaf matting and plaited vegetable-fibre rope.
- **Stained and incised leatherwork** — A rectangular leather cushion-cover is worked with geometric and linear motifs, the incised areas filled with green, red or yellow stain, with end flaps cut in openwork and set with strips of green plastic fixed by metal studs.

### Clothing & dress

Beja dress is documented mainly through photographs showing gowns, turbans, robes and head-gear, with changes reflecting Arab influence over time.

- **Gown** — British Museum photographs show Beja men and children wearing gowns, some with turbans and walking-sticks.
- **Robe and head-gear** — Camp photographs from Ethiopia show adults in robes and head-gear alongside children wearing cloth with neck-ornaments and arm-ornaments.

### Architecture

Beja architecture is the portable and semi-portable building of nomads in the Eastern Desert rather than fixed village forms.

- **Tent with textile covering** — A light framework of branches is skinned with textiles stretched over it, pitched directly on desert ground with no enclosure or foundation.
- **Shelter with poles, mats and platform** — Shelters made of poles and mats with a wooden platform, possibly a bed, standing in the open in front of them, built from materials the household already carries.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Beja everyday objects are weapons, riding gear and household furnishings made from carved wood, iron, hide and leather.

- **Dagger** — A curved iron blade with a wooden hilt, flaring quillons and pommel, both embellished with incised linear motifs.
- **Shield** — Two types survive: one circular with a conical central boss formed in a mould; the other of hide with a conical boss and a single handle, bound with wire and fitted with a leather carrying strap.
- **Camel pack-saddle** — A carved wooden frame with high pommels lashed together with leather thongs, its padded seat built from hessian sacking and woven palm-leaf matting, tied to the frame with plaited vegetable-fibre rope.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Beja adornment consists mainly of tall ornamental headpieces made from plaited and coiled plant materials, covered with leather and sometimes cowrie shells.

- **Trumpet-shaped hat** — Made of plaited coiled palm-stem leaf covered with leather.
- **Conical ornament with cowrie shells** — Built of coiled grass, covered first with leather and then with cowrie shells so that the shell surface is the finished face.
- **Neck-ornament and arm-ornaments** — Worn by children in camp photographs, documented but not further described.

## Music & performance

Verbal art rather than instrumental music holds cultural prestige among the Beja, with poetry highly praised and the standing of the language resting in part on it.

## Dance & theatre

No sources cover Beja dance or dramatic traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe only the religious frame for rituals: the Beja were Christian in the tenth century and became Muslim by the 15th century following Arab migration and intermarriage.

- **Jebel Uweinat** — A sacred place revered by the Qamhat Bisharin, the group now largely resident in the Kharga Oasis.

## Foodways

The sources do not cover Beja foodways, cooking methods, staple foods or ceremonial food practices.

## Oral tradition & literature

Beja has an extensive oral tradition with multiple poetic genres, where poetry and formal speech are load-bearing in claims over land.

- **Mhamuud Oofaash** — The best-known epic hero, portions of which have appeared in publications by Klaus Wedekind, with an edition in Mahmud Mohammed Ahmed's *Oomraay*, published in Asmara.

## Language & religion

Beja is the sole member of the Northern Cushitic branch of Afroasiatic, with around 2,550,000 speakers in Sudan as of 2022, and the Beja are Muslim.

- **Bidhaawyeet and Tubdhaawi** (*Bidhaawyeet / Tubdhaawi*) — The indefinite and definite forms of the Beja language name.

## Glossary

- *Bidhaawyeet* — The Beja language in indefinite form
- *Tubdhaawi* — The Beja language in definite form
- *Medjay* — Ancient name for the Beja people
- *Blemmyes* — Roman-era name for the Beja people
- *Cushitic* — Language family to which Beja belongs
- *Northern Cushitic* — The branch of Cushitic that Beja uniquely represents
- *Eastern Desert* — Arid belt between the Nile and Red Sea where Beja live
- *Camel pack-saddle* — Carved wooden frame for carrying loads on camels
- *Qamhat Bisharin* — Beja group now largely resident in the Kharga Oasis
- *Jebel Uweinat* — Sacred place revered by the Qamhat Bisharin
- *Afroasiatic* — Language family containing Beja and Arabic
- *Plaited palm-stem leaf* — Material used to build ornamental headpieces
- *Incised linear motifs* — Carved line patterns used in decoration
