## Overview

The Bezanozano, also called Antankay, occupy the inland belt of Madagascar between the Betsimisaraka lowland rainforest to the east and the Merina highlands of Imerina to the west — a fertile, forested country they call *Ankay*, whose principal towns are Ambatondrazaka and Moramanga. They numbered around 100,000 in 2013 and speak a dialect of Malagasy, a Malayo-Polynesian language descended from the Barito languages of southern Borneo. Their name means "those of many small plaits", after their traditional hairstyle. They are held to be among the earliest Malagasy groups to settle the island and are associated with the *vazimba*, Madagascar's first inhabitants, whose tombs are scattered through Bezanozano land. For folk culture the group matters as a threshold people: eighteenth-century middlemen between highland kingdoms and coastal traders, later conscripted porters, and keepers of ancestor cult, honey-gathering and a dance of their own.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used give no named Bezanozano weaving or cloth tradition. What they do record is that weaving belongs to women in the traditional division of household labour, and that the objects museums hold from this people are plaited and woven in vegetable fibre rather than in cloth.

**Plaited basketry** — Baskets are built from natural fibre with some strands dyed, so the pattern is carried by the contrast of coloured and undyed elements in the plaiting itself; one museum piece was collected unfinished, with the weave still open. The same technique yields a sleeping mat and a ring worn on the head for carrying pots.

**Fibre containers** — A soft plaited container made specifically for keeping spoons is among the collected objects, indicating fibre work shaped to particular household uses rather than to generic storage.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe no garments. The one element of appearance they do record is the hairstyle from which the people take their name — many small plaits, the trait distinctive enough that neighbours named them for it. Dress convention appears in the sources only through etiquette rather than costume: women were traditionally expected to walk behind a man, and men working as porters held that if a woman passed in front of a man at work he would be injured at his next load. Nothing in the sources used covers head coverings, belts, footwear or a ceremonial wardrobe separate from daily wear.

### Architecture

Before the Merina conquest the Bezanozano lived in fortified villages under local chiefs, in small communities governed collectively by their elders. The nineteenth century brought a second building repertoire with it: as Merina culture was assimilated under Merina rule, stone tombs and *trano gasy* houses began to be built in Ankay alongside the earlier forms. Stone tomb building is now the ordinary burial architecture of the group. The other structures the sources name are not dwellings but landscape features treated as built: ancient tombs and earthen tomb-shaped mounds, all of them ascribed to the *vazimba* and maintained as places of pilgrimage and sacrifice. Industrial building arrived from outside as well — Queen Ranavalona I planted a munitions factory at Mantasoa in the region, and the two royal transport roads driven from Antananarivo to the port of Toamasina fixed the southern limit of Bezanozano territory between them. French colonisation after 1896 introduced terraced irrigated paddy fields and ox carts.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery in the collections is unornamented and functional: plain pots, and a mortar made in pottery rather than wood. Woodwork is the broader category — a reaping knife with an iron blade in a wooden handle, a blow-pipe, a holster for darts, a honey pot bound with cord, and a single object that is at once a seat and a game-board. Horn supplies two further forms, a drinking horn of cattle horn and a set of fire-lighting equipment. Iron entered late: ore was abundant in Ankay precisely because the local population had never learned to smith it, so systematic exploitation of timber and iron came with Merina rule, and the fabrication of metal tools only with the French colonial period.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used record no Bezanozano jewellery, tattooing or cosmetic practice. The single documented body practice is the hairstyle of many small plaits that gave the group its name.

## Music & performance

Three instruments survive in the collected objects: a drum with a wooden body and a skin head, and two wooden flutes. The sources name none of them in Malagasy, and give no song genres, no professional musicians and no repertoire. What they do supply is the performance occasion. Funerals are celebrated with large quantities of rum drunk, and the *famadihana* reburial ceremony — shared with the Merina — is the calendar's principal gathering. Ritual at the *vazimba* tombs involves sacrifice and petition addressed to the spirits for favourable intervention, and ancestors are invoked collectively rather than by name. Older unified religious practice centred on eleven protective *sampy*, idols said to have been brought from Sakalava territory, and these were the group's original source of common identity. Verbal style is itself a performance register worth noting: indirect speech, discretion and tact govern expression and social interaction, close to highland Merina manners and markedly unlike the Betsimisaraka of the east coast. Justice was historically conducted in the same idiom, as public deliberation continued until consensus was reached.

## Dance & theatre

One dance is named in the sources and it is unique to this people: the **Betatoato**. The sources used give no description of its steps, its accompaniment or its occasion, and no theatrical or masked tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual year turns on the dead. The Bezanozano bury in stone tombs and, like the Merina, practise *famadihana*, the reburial ceremony in which the dead are taken up again; funerals are marked by heavy drinking of rum. Most Bezanozano venerate ancestors, the *razana*, who in ritual are sometimes addressed as *zanahary* — gods, both male and female, understood as the most ancient and most powerful of the ancestors and invoked anonymously and collectively so that no ancestor is excluded and thereby offended. Distinct from the *razana* are the *tompontany*, the masters of the land, who are the *vazimba*: their descendants cannot be traced, so every ancient tomb or tomb-shaped earthen mound is treated as theirs and becomes a site of pilgrimage, sacrifice and ritual. Sorcery is believed in and witchcraft feared. Prohibitions, *fady*, structure ordinary conduct: pork has historically been forbidden at Ambatondrazaka and in certain other communities, and the indri lemur was probably taboo in many villages, where it may not be killed or eaten, must be released if trapped, and must be buried with the same rites as a person. Refusal of ritual could itself be political — the Bezanozano declined to observe mourning customs at the death of Andrianampoinimerina.

## Foodways

Rice is the staple and is grown in terraced, irrigated paddies, with corn, beans and other staples beside it. The technique particular to this region is *vary verina*: rice on the upper terrace, beans and corn on the terrace below. Peanuts and beans came in with Merina cultivation during the nineteenth century. Cattle were a store of wealth alongside slaves in the eighteenth century, and cattle horn served the household as drinking vessel and fire-lighting kit. Honey is the region's signature product, gathered from the forest and sold at market or at roadside stands; the Bezanozano were considered the best honey collectors, and honey had an important role in Merina royal ritual, which is one reason the trade mattered beyond subsistence. A wooden honey pot bound with cord is among the objects shown here. Pork is avoided at Ambatondrazaka and in some other communities under *fady*, and indri flesh is forbidden where that taboo holds. Rum is the drink of funerals. The sources used describe no tea or coffee custom and no named dishes.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral history carries the group's account of itself: that they lived in small communities governed collectively by village elders, and that matters of justice were settled by public deliberation until consensus emerged. Popular belief holds that the Bezanozano descend from the *vazimba*, the island's earliest inhabitants, while simultaneously insisting that the living descendants of those ancestors cannot be identified or known — a paradox maintained rather than resolved. The line of memory is also a wound: the name Bezanozano became synonymous with *andevo*, the historic slave class, because the group was so heavily conscripted into *fanampoana*, the unpaid labour and porterage owed to the Merina crown. After independence in 1960 a strong social movement worked to rebuild an ethnic identity with those class constraints discarded, and local officials of the PSD sought equal participation of the descendants of *andriana*, the nobles, and of slaves. The sources name no epic, tale cycle or proverb collection.

## Language & religion

The Bezanozano speak a dialect of Malagasy, a Malayo-Polynesian language of the Barito group whose relatives are spoken in southern Borneo; the sources record no script tradition. Religion is overwhelmingly ancestral: veneration of the *razana*, collective invocation of the *zanahary*, propitiation of the *vazimba* as owners of the soil, and belief in sorcery. Protestant missionaries and churches established themselves in Ankay from the later nineteenth century, but Merina settlers make up the majority of the region's Christians and very few Bezanozano have converted. Belief shapes land use directly: virgin forest is left standing because it holds *vazimba* tombs, shelters spirits and is home to the *fady*-protected indri, whereas the neighbouring Betsimisaraka clear new forest for pasture and planting and treat uncultivated land as unclaimed — a difference that produces friction along the border.
