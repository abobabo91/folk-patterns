## Overview

The Shona make up at least 70% of Zimbabwe's population and speak chiShona, grouped by dialect clusters across the country. They arrived on the plateau from the Congo River basin at the end of the first millennium. In folk-culture they matter for Great Zimbabwe's drystone architecture, mbira music, decorated pots, divining tablets, and a twentieth-century stone-sculpture movement.

## Material culture

Shona material culture emphasizes geometric patterns like zigzags, lozenges and diamonds, worked in clay, metal wire, beads and textiles.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Shona textiles are made from bark fiber, vegetable string, grass and glass beads, decorated with geometric patterns.

- **Bark-fibre cloth** — A rectangular strip woven in a pattern of saltires with a long fringe, and a cap made of barkcloth fibre.
- **Woven vegetable-string cloth** — Black cloth woven from vegetable string with an unwoven pattern of zigzags and lozenges.
- **Grass plaiting** — A cap of woven grass carries a zigzag pattern; leg ornaments are built from small rattles made of woven leaf strips.
- **Beadwork** — Girdles are strung from glass beads in alternating colors or multicoloured zigzag patterns, and bags are worked in glass beads.
- **Spinning** — A spindle with a shaft of yellow jointed cane and a pottery whorl, wound with cotton thread.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional dress was animal skin, later replaced by traded and locally made cloth with coordinating adornments.

- **Mhapa and Shashiko** (*Mhapa, Shashiko*) — Traditional animal skin garments covering front and back.
- **Leopard-skin apron** — A kaross made from tanned leopard skin.
- **Glass bead girdles** — Multiple strands of beads worn functionally and ornamentally, some carrying skin scrapers and medicine phials.
- **Brass and copper wire armlets** — Spirally twisted wire forming armlets and bangles worn on the arms.
- **Woven-leaf leg ornaments** — Small rattles strung between parallel fibre cords, worn as both dress and dance equipment.

### Architecture

Shona architecture ranges from round homesteads to monumental drystone walling that extends back to their ancestors.

- **Musha** — A traditional homestead of round huts arranged around a cleared yard called the Ruvanze.
- **Great Zimbabwe** — A stone-walled town built of local granite laid without mortar, dated to about 1250–1450 AD, with an eastern enclosure that yielded six soapstone birds and a soapstone bowl.
- **Drystone walling** — Free-standing walls, drystone-walled stairs on hilltops and platform-terrace walls built without mortar.
- **Ziwa-type walling** (*ziwa*) — Drystone walling in Zimbabwe's eastern province with cattle kraals and underground homes with drystone-walled verandas.
- **Chisvingo** — An example of Shona drystone walling architecture.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Shona pottery and metalwork feature geometric designs in plumbago, red, incised line and relief.

- **Hari** — Shona earthenware pots; storage and serving pots are most decorative with spherical bodies, incurved necks and geometric patterns.
- **Soapstone bowl** — A soapstone vessel found in Great Zimbabwe's eastern enclosure excavation.
- **Iron daggers and knives** — Blades with incised line or zigzag designs on wooden handles, including long swords with central ridges and notched hilts.
- **Wooden and horn objects** — Headrests with concentric-circle designs, combs, carved bowls with pointed handles or incised diamond borders, and carved spoons.
- **Snuff containers** — Flasks and bottles made of horn, ivory, wood and gourd, several bound or covered with brass wire.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Shona adornment emphasizes drawn wire and glass beads, with brass and copper wire forming most jewelry.

- **Brass wire armlets** — Spirally twisted brass and copper wire formed into armlets and bangles.
- **Brass wire ear ornaments** — Ornaments formed from brass wire worn in the ears.
- **Cymbal-shaped brass pendant** — A disc pendant of brass hanging from a leather thong.
- **Beaded girdles** — Several strands of glass beads in pink, red, white, black arranged in quatrefoils or small alternating patterns.
- **Ivory snuff-flask** — An ivory container bound with brass wire for carrying snuff.

## Music & performance

The mbira and ngoma drum are central to Shona music, played in interlocking parts with improvised vocals.

- **Mbira dzavadzimu** — A 22–24-key mbira used to summon spirits in ancient Shona music.
- **Mbira Nyunga Nyunga** — A 15-key mbira taught from primary school to university.
- **Ngoma** — A drum that varies in size and shape, played with sticks or an open palm depending on type.
- **Hosho** — A hollowed maranka gourd rattle containing hota seeds, played by a singer who answers and embellishes the lead vocal.
- **Kushaura and Kutsinira** (*kushaura, kutsinira*) — Interlocking parts where the kushaura lead chooses a known melody under a text phrase, and the kutsinira second part interlocks to produce repeated notes.

## Dance & theatre

Drums accompany dance with audience participation, and leg rattles serve as dance equipment.

- **Amabhiza** — A dance whose small drum is struck with hand and stick.
- **Mhande** — A drum-pair tradition using Shauro and Tsinhiro drums for lead and response.
- **Dinhe** — A drum-pair tradition using Mhito and Mitumba miviri drums for lead and response.
- **Chokoto** — A drum-pair tradition using the two-headed Chimudumbana and Chigubha drums.
- **Leg-rattle ornaments** — Small woven-leaf rattles strung between parallel fibre cords, worn as both dance and dress equipment.

## Festivals & rituals

The bira ceremony summons ancestral spirits for guidance, and sacred places anchor ritual in the landscape.

- **Bira** — A ceremony often lasting all night that summons spirits for guidance and intercession, played on multiple mbira dzavadzimu with hosho and ngoma.
- **Umbuyiso and Ukuthethela** (*Umbuyiso, Ukuthethela*) — Rain-making ceremonies in Zimbabwean indigenous religion.
- **Dzavadzimu** — Ancestral spirits, the only ones able to communicate with both the living and God, turned to as protectors and guides to moral behaviour.
- **Mitupo** — A clan-emblem system that regulates marriage, helps avoid incest and ensures people marry someone they know.
- **Divining tablets** — Wooden or ivory tablets with incised geometric designs, tied together for divination, or nutshells split into halves and strung on leather.

## Foodways

Sadza, a thickened porridge made from sorghum or maize, is the staple grain dish, with beef and preserved milk.

- **Sadza** — A thickened porridge made from sorghum and maize as the staple dish.
- **Hwahwa** — Traditional beer made from sorghum and maize.
- **Beef and milk** — Beef eaten dried and stratified by wealth; preserved milk eaten with sadza made from sorghum, now usually with kale and greens.
- **Decorated serving pots** (*Hari*) — Storage and serving pots are the decorated ones in Shona pottery hierarchy, in contrast to cooking pots.
- **Serving utensils** — Carved wooden bowls, spoons with hollowed handles, combined knife-spoons in brass and iron, decorated gourds and grease holders bound with wire.

## Oral tradition & literature

Shona indigenous religion is transmitted orally through proverbs and historical spirit mediums, with a written literature emerging in the twentieth century.

- **Rooranai Vematongo** — A proverb meaning marry or have a relationship with someone that you know, expressing the Mitupo marriage rule.
- **Mbuya Nehanda** — A historical spirit medium remembered by name in Shona oral tradition.
- **Sekuru Kaguvi** — A historical spirit medium remembered by name in Shona oral tradition.
- **Feso** — The first novel in Shona, written by Solomon Mutswairo and appearing in 1957.
- **Mbira song** — Improvisational verbal art where the kushaura player selects a known melody under a phrase or lines of text, then commented upon during performance.

## Language & religion

ChiShona is a Bantu language with a simple five-vowel system and unmarked tones, while traditional religion centers on Mwari reached through prayer, holy family members or ancestral spirits.

- **ChiShona** — A Bantu language of Guthrie's zone S.10 with over 14 million speakers, based on Zezuru as standard.
- **Mwari** — The creator deity, also called Musikavanhu (creator of people) or Nyadenga (one who lives high up), reached directly in prayer or through intermediaries.
- **Unhu** — The guiding principle of life in traditional Shona religion.
- **Dzavadzimu** — Ancestral spirits reached as intermediaries between the living and Mwari.
- **Clement Doke** — South African linguist who codified chiShona as a written standard in the early twentieth century using special characters later replaced by Latin digraphs.
