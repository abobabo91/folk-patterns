## Overview

The Beembe, also known as Bembe, Babembe, or Babeembe, are a Bantu people living in southern Congo-Brazzaville, especially in Bouenza and in the cities of Brazzaville, Dolisie, and Pointe-Noire. They are a Kongo subgroup whose customs resemble those of other Kongo peoples, while their sculpture distinguishes them within the region. Oral tradition places their separation from the Kongo around the eight century. They migrated Northeast from the Kongo and settled some five hundred kilometers from the capital of the Kingdom of the Kongo, in which they formed part during its height of power. Beembe society is economically based on agriculture, while hunting was also a main activity. Their material and intangible culture is particularly documented through ancestral worship, funerary customs, scarification, musical instruments, and carved ancestral figures known as *Muzidi*.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Local raffia cloth** — Local raffia cloth was originally used to wrap *Muzidi* ancestral figures. It was dyed with red *tukula* dye before imported cottons came into use.

**Imported cottons** — Over time, imported cottons replaced local raffia cloth in the textiles used to wrap ancestral figures.

**Textile reliquary** — Beembe ancestral devotion was directed toward the textile reliquary as well as wooden ancestral sculptures. During funerary observances, the body could be shrouded and displayed within a textile shrine.

**Textile wrapping of Muzidi** — After an ancestor was placed inside a carved figure, the figure was wrapped in textiles and stuffed with banana leaves and other fibrous materials.

**Scarification designs** — Geometric designs were carved into many Beembe figures, representing the scarification markings associated with beauty and belonging within the community.

**Motif vocabulary.** V with a ^ on top, v or ^ extending into a broad curve, diamond shape stretching from the sternum to the hips.

### Clothing & dress

They do describe textiles used in funerary and ancestral contexts: bodies could be shrouded and displayed within a textile shrine, and *Muzidi* figures were wrapped in local raffia cloth, later in imported cottons. The figures could also be stuffed with banana leaves and other fibrous materials.

### Architecture

They state that the family unit generally included three generations and that members of a matrimonial clan could live in several villages. After a funerary celebration, an ancestral figure could be placed inside the family home or within a special family enclosure.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do describe wooden ancestral sculptures, small wooden drums, rattles, staffs, cartridge cases, cloth, banana leaves, fibrous materials, and the implements carried by male ancestral figures to represent the ancestor’s profession in life. A newer rattle made from tin cans was called *ntengele*, while a staff-rattle with cartridge case rattles attached to it served as a symbol of peace between Europeans and the Beembe.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Beembe body decoration included scarification, which signified local beauty and belonging within the community. The custom was popular until around the 1950s but has almost disappeared today. Three common forms are described: a V with a ^ above it; a v or ^ whose lines continue outward into a broad curve; and a diamond extending from the sternum to the hips. These markings also appear on some Beembe figurines.

## Music & performance

Beembe musical instruments are described as generally similar to those of the Kongo, with some exceptions. The *mukonzi* is a small wooden drum approximately 72 cm in length and fitted with a handle. It is played with two drumsticks made of *luwunga*. Some communities use two *mukonzi* at the same time. In this pairing, the larger drum is called *nigiri*, meaning “mother,” and the smaller one *ngonglo mwana*, meaning “little one”; together they represent a mother-child relationship. A slightly larger but similar drum is the *nkonko*, found among the Basundi.

Beembe rattles include the double-rattle *kitsarakara*, used by younger people. It consists of two fruit shells bound together with a band of cloth, with rocks or seeds inside. The hand-held calabash rattle *mukwanga* is used in comforting children. Since colonization, the Beembe learned to make *ntengele*, a rattle made from tin cans. A staff-rattle, made from a staff about the height of an average man with cartridge case rattles attached, functioned as a symbol of peace between Europeans and the Beembe.

## Dance & theatre

They state that the funerary celebration known as *Kitu* included song, dance, and feasting in honor of the newly deceased.

## Festivals & rituals

Beembe social and religious life centered on relations between living people, ancestors, and spiritual specialists. The village chief, the *nga-bula*, mediated with the ancestors. Before leaving on a hunt, the leader invoked ancestral spirits, using as intermediaries statuettes kneeling in the position of a hunter waiting for prey.

A person began an ancestral journey with a funerary celebration. The deceased person’s spirit could be placed into the *muzidi*, while the body was shrouded and displayed within a textile shrine. A celebration known as *Kitu* included song, dance, and feasting. Funeral customs also included high cryings and people throwing themselves onto the ground. At the end of the celebration, the figure was placed either in the family home or in a special family enclosure. The funerary customs are described as similar in style and form to those of the Bwende, also called Babwende.

Ancestors continued to be treated as influential members of the family after death. Prayers directed toward an ancestor were communicated with the help of ancestral figures and concerned family prosperity, disease, livestock, or troubles at home. A diviner could identify resentments within the village that were understood to cause a problem, after which a figure might be made to help resolve it.

## Foodways

They state that Beembe society is economically based on agriculture and that funerary celebration included feasting. Hunting was also a main activity.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do state that oral tradition suggests the Beembe separated from the Kongo around the eight century. The sources also describe the transmission of ancestral knowledge through funerary practice, prayers, divination, and the relationship between families and carved figures. No contemporary literary revival or preservation project is identified.

## Language & religion

The Beembe are identified as a Bantu people and as a Kongo subgroup. Beembe religious practice included belief in a creator god called *Nzambi*, who was not depicted figuratively and was understood as master of life and death. Death caused by a sorcerer, *ndoki*, was treated differently: the *ndoki* could magically “eat” the life force of clan members.

Ancestors remained closely connected with the living and received offerings through a “priest” who appealed to statuettes called *kitebi* or *bimbi*, consecrated by the diviner. Ancestor worship was described as older than the use of magical statues, *nkisi*, by sorcerers. The Beembe also created *Muzidi*, carved ancestral figures that represented a person in both form and spirit. These figures were treated and cared for like family and passed down through generations.
