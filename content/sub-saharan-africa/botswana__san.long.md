## Overview

The San (also *Basarwa* in Setswana, *Bushmen* in older Anglophone literature, and *Sān* in the Khoekhoe usage from which the exonym derives) are the collective name for the indigenous hunter-gatherer peoples of southern Africa, whose homelands stretch across the Kalahari Basin of Botswana, Namibia, Angola, Zambia, Zimbabwe and South Africa. In Botswana, where they number roughly 55,000–65,000 out of a southern African total of perhaps 100,000–130,000, they are concentrated in the Central Kalahari, the Ghanzi district, the Okavango panhandle, and the Kgalagadi and Ngamiland fringes. They speak languages of three unrelated or only distantly related families — Kx'a (including *Juǀ'hoan*), Tuu (including *ǀXam* and *Nǁng*), and Khoe-Kwadi (including *Naro*, *Gǀui* and *Gǁana*) — all sharing the click consonants (ǀ dental, ǃ alveolar, ǂ palatal, ǁ lateral) that are the acoustic signature of the region. Genetic and archaeological evidence places their ancestors among the deepest-rooted human populations on earth, with continuous occupation of the subcontinent for at least 20,000 years and probably far longer; the rock-painting traditions of the Drakensberg, the Tsodilo Hills and the Brandberg are their direct cultural inheritance. In folk-culture terms the San matter as the world's most sophisticated foragers of an arid environment: the *ǃGoin* trance dance, the poisoned reed arrow, the ostrich-eggshell water canteen, the beaded apron, and the painted rock shelter are all their signature contributions, and Tsodilo — often called the "Louvre of the Desert" — is a UNESCO World Heritage Site precisely because of the density of their pictorial record.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The San have historically had no woven-cloth tradition in the Central Asian or West African sense — the Kalahari yields neither cotton nor flax, and the nomadic band economy did not sustain a loom. What functions as "textile" in San material culture is instead worked hide and beaded net-work. Cured antelope skins (springbok, duiker, gemsbok, kudu) are scraped with a hafted stone or iron *ǀa* blade, softened by rubbing with brain matter and animal fat, and smoked over a low fire to a warm russet or dark brown. Decorative surface is achieved by incising, by singeing hair away in geometric fields, and above all by attaching networks of ostrich-eggshell beads (*ǁxaro* beads) either directly to the hide or as separate collars and aprons stitched with sinew. Among Ju|'hoan and !Kung groups the *ǁxaro* bead itself is the medium of a formal gift-exchange partnership network of the same name, linking households across hundreds of kilometres. Contact-period San in the Ghanzi and Kgalagadi frontier zones adopted glass trade beads from the nineteenth century onward, laying them out in horizontal banded aprons and headbands whose colour vocabulary — white, blue, red, black — has become the visible marker of San dress at cultural festivals today.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional dress is minimal, functional and hide-based. Men wear a triangular front loincloth or breechclout (*ǃgoara* in Juǀ'hoan) of soft springbok skin, sometimes with a rear flap, secured by a thong belt from which hang a hunting knife, a firelighting kit and a small skin bag. Women wear a front apron (*ǃgabi*) and a larger back apron or hide cloak (*kaross* in the Afrikaans-derived regional term, *ǀnusi* in some Khoe varieties) that doubles as an infant carrier and, tied at the shoulder, as a night blanket. The *kaross* is the single most important piece of San dress: cut from a whole small-antelope hide, worn hair-side in during cold, fur-side out for display, and often edged with rows of ostrich-eggshell beads or, in beaded regions, glass trade beads. Both sexes go barefoot or wear soft rawhide sandals (*ǀgoro*) laced with thong. Hair is worn short or shaved, sometimes dressed with fat and red *ǁhara* ochre; leather headbands beaded across the brow are common at dances. Since the twentieth century, and especially in the resettlement villages of the Central Kalahari Game Reserve buffer, everyday dress is cotton Western clothing, with the beaded apron and *kaross* reserved for the trance dance, tourist encounters, and festivals such as the Kuru Dance Festival at D'Kar.

### Architecture

Nomadic San have no monumental architecture and, in the classical band-society pattern, no permanent dwellings. The signature San shelter is the *tshu* or *ǂoèh* — a light dome about 1.5–2 metres high built in a few hours by the women of a household from a ring of flexible acacia or grewia branches pushed into the sand, bent inward and lashed at the crown, then thatched with grass bundles tied in overlapping courses from the base up. A single low opening faces the shared central hearth of the encampment (*nǃore* is the broader term for a band's home range, not the camp itself). Camps (Juǀ'hoan *ǀgoashi*) typically comprise five to fifteen such shelters arranged in a rough oval around a cleared dance ground, each household's hearth just outside its door. The camp is moved when local food and firewood are exhausted, commonly every few weeks in the wet season and more frequently in the dry. Rock shelters and overhangs — the painted sites of Tsodilo, the Matobo and the Drakensberg — served as seasonal residences, ritual sites and painting galleries but were never built structures. Since the 1960s, and accelerated by the 1997 and 2002 removals from the Central Kalahari Game Reserve, most Botswanan San live in government-planned villages (New Xade, Kaudwane) in rectangular mud-and-thatch or concrete-block houses, with the *tshu* now largely a demonstration form.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Ceramics are marginal in the classical San toolkit: pottery is heavy, fragile and incompatible with a walking economy, and where it occurs (some Khoe-speaking groups on the Okavango fringe) it is generally a low-fired open-mouthed cooking pot obtained by trade from Bantu-speaking neighbours. Metalwork is likewise adopted rather than indigenous — iron arrowheads and knife blades reached the Kalahari through Tswana and Herero smiths, and San hunters rework them to their own designs. The signature San objects are, instead, of hide, bone, wood, sinew, plant fibre and ostrich eggshell:

**The San arrow** — a light composite reed shaft, typically 60–70 cm, with a hardwood foreshaft socketed into the reed and a small iron or bone point bound with sinew. The point is smeared with poison rendered from the pupae of the *Diamphidia* leaf beetle, dug from beneath its host *Commiphora*; a single hit brings down a giraffe over one to three days. The bow (*ǃau* in Juǀ'hoan) is short (about 1 metre), of grewia wood, strung with twisted sinew.

**The ostrich-eggshell canteen** — a whole ostrich egg with a small hole drilled at the pole, filled with rain or seep water and plugged with a grass stopper, sometimes engraved with fine hatched geometric patterns. Filled canteens are cached in the sand at known points across the range, a critical dry-season technology.

**The digging stick** (*ǁkai*) — a fire-hardened hardwood shaft weighted with a bored stone (the classic *bored stone* of southern African archaeology), used by women to prise up tubers, bulbs and truffles.

**The San leather bag** — a soft antelope-hide shoulder bag (*ǂoà*) with a drawstring closure, often fringed and beaded, used to carry gathered plant food, ostrich-eggshell beads in the making, and personal kit.

**Ostrich-eggshell beads** — the making of the *ǁxaro* bead is itself a defining San craft: a fragment of shell is chipped into a rough disc, pierced with a hand drill, threaded, and then ground as a strung batch against a stone to a uniform small diameter. A single apron may hold ten thousand beads and represent months of women's work.

Musical instruments (see below), fire drills (*ǃai*), springhare probes, hoop-and-net snares and reed straws for sipping seep water complete the everyday inventory.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Body adornment is dominated by ostrich-eggshell beadwork: strung necklaces in single or multiple loops, banded browbands, wrist and ankle cuffs, and the beaded fringes of aprons and *kaross* cloaks. Glass trade beads in white, sky blue, red and black, worked in the same banded geometry, have supplemented eggshell since the nineteenth century. Small pendants of tortoise shell or duiker horn, filled with sweet-smelling *sâ* powder (a mixture of dried aromatic plants), are worn at the neck by women. Scarification is light and mostly functional-medicinal: short parallel cuts (*ǃgwa*) rubbed with plant ash or the ash of a burnt animal part are made to convey the qualities of that animal — eland fat cut into a young hunter's brow to give him steadiness, for example. Red ochre (*ǁhara*) mixed with fat is rubbed into the hair and skin at dances and at girls' initiation. Piercing is generally limited to the earlobes and, in some groups, the nasal septum for a small quill or bead.

## Music & performance

The core of San musical life is the *ǃGoin* healing or trance dance (also called the Giraffe Dance, Eland Dance or, more generally in the literature, the great dance), performed at night around a fire with the women seated in a tight inner ring clapping polyrhythms and singing wordless or lightly-worded medicine songs of great melodic subtlety, while the men circle in a stamping outer ring wearing dance rattles (*ǂkhoisi*) of dried moth-cocoons filled with ostrich-eggshell fragments lashed around the calves. As the night deepens the healers (*nǀom kxaosi*, "owners of *nǀom*") enter *!kia*, the trance state, in which the *nǀom* energy is said to boil up the spine; they lay hands on each person present, drawing out sickness with characteristic shuddering cries. This complex of polyphonic song, interlocking clap, cocoon rattle, ecstatic dance and hands-on healing is the single most studied performance tradition of the region and is central to San self-representation today. Instruments include the mouth-bow (*ǃgwashi* or *dongo*) — a hunting bow braced against the mouth as a resonator and struck with a light stick — the multi-string pluriarc *ǃgwashi* proper of the Juǀ'hoan (a canoe-shaped body with four or five separately curved necks), the musical bow *ǀgabus* struck with a reed, and the thumb piano (*mbira*-family), the latter clearly adopted from Bantu neighbours. Songs are typically pentatonic, with descending yodelled contours and hocketed interlocking parts that anticipate, in miniature, the choral polyphony of the wider region.

## Dance & theatre

Beyond the healing dance, the San repertoire includes distinct animal dances performed at girls' initiation and at commemorative gatherings — the Eland Dance (*tshoma*) for a girl's first menstruation, in which senior women mime the courtship of the eland bull while the initiate sits secluded; the Giraffe, Gemsbok and Honey dances associated with specific medicine songs; and hunting mimes (*ǂoma*) in which men re-enact a recent hunt for the entertainment and instruction of the camp, one dancer taking the part of the animal with a horned headdress or skin draped over the shoulders. There is no formal theatre with a fixed script; performance is embedded in ritual, in storytelling around the night fire, and in the hunt-mime tradition, which shades directly into the pictorial record of the painted shelters, where therianthropic (part-human, part-antelope) figures appear to depict trancers in transformation. Contemporary San performance for outside audiences is centred on the annual **Kuru Dance Festival** at D'Kar in Ghanzi (running since 1997), which brings together bands from across Botswana, Namibia and beyond.

## Festivals & rituals

Ritual life is structured around the life cycle and the hunt rather than a calendrical festival cycle — the classical San year has no harvest, no sowing and no fixed feast days. The most important individual rite is the girl's puberty seclusion (*tshoma* or *ǃGoah kǁa*), in which the initiate is kept in a small hut for the duration of her first menses, instructed by the older women, and honoured on emergence by the Eland Dance. Boys' initiation centres on the first-kill ceremony: on bringing down his first large antelope a young hunter is cut on the brow, chest and arms with the animal's fat and blood rubbed into the wounds so that he takes on its qualities. Marriage is generally informal, established by residence and gift-exchange rather than by ceremony, though the *ǁxaro* partnership between the two families is formally begun. Death is marked by rapid burial in a flexed position, the camp abandoned, and the name of the deceased avoided for a period. The healing dance itself, held whenever illness or social tension calls for it and often simply on a bright night, functions as the recurring communal ritual in the absence of a fixed calendar. Rain-making, the calling of game, and the reading of divining discs (*ǁgom*) — small marked pieces of leather, bone or horn cast on the sand — are practised by specialists.

## Foodways

The classical San diet is the textbook case of a balanced foraging economy: roughly two-thirds gathered plant food by weight, one-third hunted meat. Women's gathering supplies the caloric base — the mongongo nut (*ǁxa* / *Schinziophyton rautanenii*) of the northern Kalahari, whose kernels are roasted and cracked and provide a remarkable proportion of daily calories in Juǀ'hoan country; the tsamma melon (*Citrullus lanatus*) and the gemsbok cucumber, both critical dry-season water sources; morama beans, marula fruit and nuts, baobab pulp, sour plum, and dozens of tubers and truffles located by subtle above-ground signs. Men's hunting supplies the protein and the fat: eland, gemsbok, kudu, springbok, hartebeest and warthog taken with the poisoned arrow, and smaller game — springhare, porcupine, tortoise, guinea fowl — with snares, throwing sticks and the springhare probe. Meat is grilled on the coals or dried into strips (*biltong* in the Afrikaans regional term); marrow is a delicacy. Water is drunk directly from seeps, cached in ostrich-eggshell canteens, and in the driest months sipped through a hollow reed pushed into damp sand. Honey, when found, is the greatest luxury and the object of specific songs. There is no dairying, no cultivation and no fermented beverage in the classical tradition; sorghum beer and maize meal have entered the diet only through Bantu contact and, more recently, resettlement.

## Oral tradition & literature

San oral literature is one of the great unwritten canons of Africa, recorded most fully in the *ǀXam* texts collected by Wilhelm Bleek and Lucy Lloyd in the Cape in the 1870s from prisoners of the Breakwater Convict Station — some 12,000 pages of dictation now held at the University of Cape Town and inscribed on the UNESCO Memory of the World Register. The corpus centres on the trickster-demiurge *ǀKaggen* (the Mantis), his wife the Dassie, and their adopted family, whose adventures explain the origin of the moon, the eland, death, and the stars; the great Ju|'hoan cycles centre on the parallel figure *ǂGaoǃna* or *Kaoxa*. Storytelling (*kukummi* in *ǀXam*) is a night-fire genre, often collective, with listeners responding rhythmically; songs are embedded in the stories. Praise for the eland, laments for the dead, and hunters' first-person accounts (*n!oahnsi*) form parallel genres. Since the Bleek-Lloyd corpus, the ethnographic record has been expanded by Megan Biesele's Ju|'hoan work in the 1970s and by the Kuru Family of Organisations' community publishing in Botswana since the 1990s.

## Language & religion

The San languages fall, as noted above, into three families — Kx'a, Tuu and Khoe-Kwadi — that share the click phoneme inventory but are otherwise not demonstrably related; each has extraordinary consonant systems, with Juǀ'hoan cataloguing more than eighty distinct consonants and *ǃXóõ* (Tuu, spoken across the Botswana–Namibia border) generally credited with the largest consonant inventory of any documented language, over 130 including click plus non-click contrasts. Most are severely endangered, with *ǀXam* extinct since the early twentieth century and several others reduced to a handful of elderly speakers; Naro and Juǀ'hoan retain the largest living communities. Religion in the classical sense is a monotheism-with-trickster: a distant creator-god (*ǂGaoǃna*, *Huwe*, *Thora* — the name varies by group) associated with the east and the rising sun, a lesser or shadow deity associated with sickness and the west, and a rich stratum of ancestor and animal spirits. The eland is the pre-eminent spirit animal, its fat carrying *nǀom*; the mantis, the moon and the stars are all persons in the mythic order. Ritual life is mediated by the trance healer rather than by a hereditary priesthood, and the painted rock shelters — most densely at **Tsodilo Hills**, inscribed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site in 2001 — are commonly interpreted as the visible record of trance experience, executed by shaman-artists over at least two millennia.
