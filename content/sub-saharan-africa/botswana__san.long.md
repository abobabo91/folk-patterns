## Overview

The San are the hunter-gatherer peoples of southern Africa's Kalahari basin and its margins, spread across Botswana, Namibia, Angola, Zambia, Zimbabwe, Lesotho and South Africa. Botswana holds the largest population — roughly 63,500 in 2017, about 2.8% of the country — concentrated in the Ghanzi district, the Central Kalahari Game Reserve, and the Okavango and Tsodilo Hills region in the northwest; Namibia counted 71,201 in 2023. They speak languages of three unrelated families — Khoe, Tuu and Kxʼa — all using click consonants, and identify by nation rather than by the collective exonym: *ǃKung* (including the *Juǀʼhoansi*), *ǀXam*, *Nǁnǂe* (of the ǂKhomani), *Kxoe*, *Haiǁom*, *Gǀui* and *Gǁana*, *Tshuwau*. Genetic work places their divergence from other human populations around 200,000 years ago, with full isolation by 100,000 years ago and re-integration later; the ǂKhomani are among the most genetically diverse populations ever sequenced. In folk-culture terms they matter for a hunter-gatherer material repertoire of extraordinary economy — hide, sinew, bone, ostrich eggshell, reed, poison — and for the longest continuous painting tradition on earth.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The San did not weave. Their pattern tradition is carried on hide, shell and rock rather than cloth, which makes it unusual among the cultures in this atlas and is the reason the seed entries below are objects rather than fabrics.

**San rock painting** — Ochre, haematite, charcoal and white clay applied to granite and sandstone shelter walls across southern Africa, with the Tsodilo Hills in northwestern Botswana the densest Botswana concentration. Subjects are eland and other large antelope, hunters with bows, trance figures with animal heads or streaming nasal blood, and processions of human figures. Unlike neighbouring Bantu-language rock art, San painting is figurative and shamanic rather than schematic, and the eland — the animal richest in *nǀom*, potency — dominates.

**Ostrich eggshell bead** — Discs cut from broken ostrich eggshell, drilled, strung on sinew and then ground flush against stone so a whole string wears as one smooth cylinder. Beads appear in headbands, aprons, necklaces and net-like beaded panels. Colour variation is achieved by burying or heating shell to warm it toward ochre and brown; the resulting light/dark counting of beads produces the banded geometry typical of *Juǀʼhoansi* work.

**Kaross** — *Kaross*, the hide cloak of gemsbok, duiker or springbok, worn fur-in, tied at the shoulder and belted so the lower fold becomes a pouch. It is simultaneously garment, carrying sling, ground sheet and infant carrier. The hide is worked with fat and brain matter and scraped with a stone or iron blade rather than tanned with bark, which leaves the San kaross softer and paler than Tswana equivalents.

**San leather bag** — Small collecting and quiver bags of scraped antelope hide, sewn with sinew, sometimes with the animal's leg skin used whole so the bag keeps the taper of the limb. Surfaces carry incised or pyro-engraved line ornament and applied eggshell beadwork; the largest examples are *kaross*-like gathering bags, the smallest hold tinder, ochre, arrow poison and roots.

**San arrow** — The signature technology: a reed shaft, a bone or iron link-shaft, and a small poisoned point, carried with a bow in a quiver made from the root bark of the *Acacia* or a hide tube. Poison is *diamphotoxin* from the larvae of *Diamphidia* beetles, slow-acting so the animal must be tracked for hours or days. Arrows are individually marked and lent between men; because the owner of the arrow that kills an animal distributes the meat, arrow-marking is a social instrument for spreading meat through a band as much as a maker's signature.

**Ostrich eggshell water flask** — A whole egg with a drilled mouth, plugged with grass, engraved with hatched bands and stored in cached groups, used with the *sip well* (a hole scraped to damp sand, a hollow grass stem inserted, water drawn up by mouth and passed down a second straw into the egg).

**Motif vocabulary.** *Eland* — the potency animal of trance and girls' initiation; *therianthrope* — half-human, half-antelope trance figure; *nasal bleed line* — the streaming line marking a healer in deep trance; *entoptic zigzag and grid* — geometric forms of the trance threshold; *hunting file* — a line of bow-carrying figures in profile; *fat-tailed sheep and cattle* — later contact-period subjects; *handprint*; *hatched band* — engraved parallel-line register on eggshell and gourd; *bead register* — alternating pale/ochre eggshell bead courses; *giraffe*, *ostrich* and *rhino* as named game species.

### Clothing & dress

Daily dress is minimal and made entirely of hide. Men wear a front apron or loincloth of soft skin with a hide belt from which hang a knife, fire-sticks, a tinder pouch and a small bag of medicines; the quiver and bow are slung across the back. Women wear a rear apron and a front apron, the front one beaded with ostrich eggshell in horizontal registers, and over both the *kaross*, which in a woman's hands is the single most important object she owns: it carries firewood, melons, tubers, and an infant against her back. A smaller *kaross* serves as a dedicated baby carrier. Sandals of thick hide are cut for tracking over hot sand and thorn. Headbands of eggshell bead, sometimes with a tortoise-shell scent container or a small horn of powdered aromatic root hung at the forehead, are worn by both sexes. Ceremonial dress distinguishes itself by quantity of bead rather than by a separate garment: a girl emerging from the *eland bull* initiation wears massed eggshell strands, and healers dance with rattles of dry seed pods bound to the calves.

### Architecture

San building is a seasonal grammar rather than a house type. The *scherm* (in Afrikaans usage; the Juǀʼhoansi term is a hut of the same construction) is a dome or hemispherical windbreak of flexible branches pushed into the sand, bent and lashed, then thatched with grass. In the hot spring, when bands move constantly following budding greens, a night's shelter may be no more than a curved screen of branches against the wind with a fire in front of it. In the dry season, when people gather around permanent waterholes, the same construction is built more thickly, thatched properly against sun and rain, and set in a formalised ring with doorways facing the cleared central space where dancing, cooking fires and meat distribution happen. The ring is the architecture: dwelling entrances address each other, hearths sit just outside each doorway, and there is no fenced enclosure, no livestock kraal and no headman's house at the centre — a spatial expression of the egalitarian order. Since the 1950s–1990s government modernization and settlement programmes in Botswana and Namibia, most San live in fixed villages of mud-and-pole or concrete-block housing, and the grass dome survives at Ghanzi and D'Kar as heritage construction and for tourism.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The San were not potters and not smiths; iron reached them as trade goods from Bantu-language neighbours and was reworked cold or by simple hot-forging into arrow link-shafts, knife blades and digging-stick tips. The characteristic objects are organic: the *digging stick*, a fire-hardened point used for tubers, bush onions and sip wells, and a woman's principal tool alongside the *kaross*; the fire-drill pair of hardwood spindle and softer hearth board; ostrich eggshell flasks; tortoise-shell bowls and cosmetic containers; springhare-hook probes for pulling game from burrows; snares of twisted plant fibre and sinew; mortars for pounding *mongongo* nuts; and net bags of plant-fibre cordage. Bone, horn and sinew substitute for nails, glue and thread throughout. Each item is portable by design — a band moving between waterholes carries its entire material estate.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ostrich eggshell bead is the medium of San adornment and, through gift exchange, of San social relations: strings, headbands, necklaces, bandoliers, waistbands and beaded apron panels circulate as named gifts within the *hxaro* partnership network, which links individuals across bands and hundreds of kilometres, and in which ornaments are given rather than traded. Glass trade beads, brass rings and wire were absorbed from the nineteenth century onward and used in the same registers alongside eggshell. Other adornment includes seed and nut pendants, tortoise-shell containers of powdered aromatic root worn at the neck, sinew wrist and ankle bands, and dance rattles of dry cocoons or seed pods. Bodies are scarified with small cuts on the face, chest and arms — a hunter receives cuts rubbed with the burnt medicine of an animal he has killed, transferring its qualities — and skin is dressed with fat and red ochre. Hair is worn short, sometimes dusted with ochre.

## Music & performance

San music is built on voice, clap and rattle, with a small family of stringed and struck instruments. The *ǀgwashi* (also *dongo*) is a plucked five-string bowl lute or pluriarc of the Juǀʼhoansi and ǃKung, played solo and to accompany song; the *gora* or *!gora*, a mouth-resonated bow with a quill lashed between string and stave that the player sets vibrating by inhaling and exhaling, is one of the most distinctive instruments in Africa; hunting bows are played as mouth-bows and as ground-bows braced over a resonating hollow. Thumb pianos and single-string fiddles entered later from Bantu-language neighbours.

The defining genre is the **healing dance** or **trance dance** — the *ǀgi* or *num tchai* of the Juǀʼhoansi, the **giraffe dance**, **eland dance**, **honey dance** and **gemsbok dance** among named song-sets. Women sit in a ring around the fire, clapping in interlocking patterns and singing in *yodelled, hocketed polyphony* — short overlapping vocal cells, each singer contributing pitches that combine into a shifting whole, one of the most technically remarkable vocal practices documented anywhere. Men dance in a circle outside them, stamping, wearing leg rattles, until *nǀom* boils up the spine and a healer enters *kia*, laying hands on the sick and drawing out sickness with a shuddering cry. Music has no court and no professional class: songs are owned by individuals, named, given away, and dreamt. Children's game-songs, mocking songs and hunting songs fill the long hours that San of all ages spend in conversation, joking and play — leisure being, as ethnographers of the Juǀʼhoansi repeatedly note, structurally central to this economy rather than residual to it.

## Dance & theatre

There is no theatre in the sense of a scripted stage tradition; performance is danced, and the boundary between ritual and entertainment is deliberately thin. The **healing/trance dance** is the central ceremonial form, held whenever sickness, tension or simple appetite for it arises, sometimes through the whole night into the following day. Animal-named dance sets — **giraffe**, **eland**, **gemsbok**, **honey** — are distinguished by their song melodies and by which healer owns them. The **eland bull dance** marks a girl's first menstruation: senior women dance with horns held at the forehead in imitation of the bull's mating display while the initiate is secluded, and the eland's fat and potency are transferred to her. Boys' first-kill rites are similarly danced. Mimetic **hunting dances**, in which men act out the stalk, the shot and the death of the tracked animal, shade into pure entertainment, performed for the pleasure of the imitation and the laughter it gets. Children's dances mimic the adult forms without ritual weight.

## Festivals & rituals

There is no calendar of dated festivals — a consequence of a foraging year structured by rainfall rather than by planting, and of a society with no priesthood or centralised authority. The year's real markers are ecological: the hard early spring, a hot dry period after the cool dry winter when plants are dormant and autumn nut stores are exhausted; the dry months when meat matters most because game cannot range far from receding water; and the first rains, when bands disperse from the dry-season waterhole rings and shelters become nightly rain screens again. Gatherings intensify at the dry-season waterholes, and the great dances happen when numbers allow them.

Life-cycle rites carry the ritual weight instead. A child is named after a grandparent or other relative, never a parent, from a pool of roughly 35 names per sex — the name rule and the age rule together generate the whole kinship terminology, an Inuit-type system in which the older of any two people decides what to call the younger. A girl's first menstruation brings seclusion and the **eland bull dance**. A boy's first large kill brings scarification with the burnt medicine of the animal, marking his entry into the meat-distribution economy. Marriage is overwhelmingly monogamous (about 95% of relationships as of 1994) and established by bride service and gift rather than by bridewealth cattle. The dead are buried, the camp moves, and the place is avoided. Above all, exchange itself is ritualised: the *hxaro* gift partnership, in which named ornaments and tools travel between partners for decades, is the mechanism by which this gift economy — giving rather than trading — reproduces itself.

## Foodways

There is no staple grain. The diet is wild plants gathered by women and meat hunted by men, with plants supplying the bulk of calories in most seasons. The *mongongo* nut (mangetti) is the single most important food of the Juǀʼhoansi of the Dobe area — high in oil and protein, storable, and gathered in enormous quantity; tsama melons and other Kalahari cucurbits supply both food and water; and the gathered repertoire includes tubers, bulbs, bush onions, berries, fruits, resins and gums. Meat is roasted directly on coals or boiled in a pot when one is available; marrow is cracked and eaten; hide is worked and nothing of a large animal is discarded. Ostrich eggs are eaten and the shells kept as flasks. Insects supply roughly 10% of animal protein, most heavily in the dry season, and between 18 and 104 species are consumed depending on location — grasshoppers, beetles, caterpillars, moths, butterflies and termites. Honey is prized and has its own dance. *Hoodia gordonii* is chewed on the hunt to suppress hunger and thirst; the CSIR patented its active glycoside p57 in 1998 and a benefit-sharing agreement returning royalties to the San was signed in 2003, one of the first agreements of its kind for indigenous knowledge, though no revenue has yet reached them. Meat distribution is the central food rule: the owner of the killing arrow distributes the carcass, spreading it through the band regardless of who shot it.

## Oral tradition & literature

The San have no single epic. Their literature is a body of myth, animal tale and cosmological narrative recorded most fully in the *ǀXam* material collected in Cape Town in the 1870s by Wilhelm Bleek and Lucy Lloyd from ǀXam prisoners and informants — above all *ǁKabbo*, *Dia!kwain* and *ǀHanǂkassǀo* — filling more than 12,000 notebook pages of parallel ǀXam text and English translation, now the **Bleek and Lloyd Collection** (a UNESCO Memory of the World inscription) and the basis of the reconstructed ǀXam literature. Its recurring figure is *ǀKaggen*, the Mantis, trickster-creator who makes the eland, dies and revives, and whose stories explain the origin of the moon, of fire and of death. Other cycles concern the **Early Race**, the people of the first order who became animals; the girl who threw ashes into the sky to make the Milky Way; and the origin of the sun as a man's armpit. Storytelling belongs to the fire and the long conversational evening, told in the first person with heavy use of quoted speech, song insertions and onomatopoeia — the notebooks preserve the songs mid-narrative. Twentieth-century collecting among the Juǀʼhoansi, Naro and Nharo continued the work; the Kuru Development Trust and Kuru Art Project at D'Kar in Ghanzi district, founded 1990, turned the same narrative and visual repertoire into a contemporary printmaking and painting movement.

## Language & religion

San languages fall into three unrelated families — **Khoe** (Kxoe/Khwe, ǁAni, Naro, Gǀui, Gǁana, Tshuwau), **Tuu** (ǀXam, Nǁnǂe of the ǂKhomani, ǂʼAmkoe relatives) and **Kxʼa** (ǃKung/ǃXuun including Juǀʼhoansi) — all characterised by large click inventories, among the most phonemically complex sound systems in the world. There was no indigenous script; ǀXam survives only in Bleek and Lloyd's orthography, and Naro and Juǀʼhoansi now have Latin-based orthographies developed for literacy and Bible translation. ǀXam is extinct; most other varieties are endangered, with Naro and Juǀʼhoansi the healthiest. Religion centres on a great god and a lesser, capricious trickster god (*ǀKaggen*, *Gǃara*, *Gaoxa*, names varying by nation), on *nǀom* as an impersonal potency concentrated in the eland, in healers and in rain, and on the trance dance as its working technology. San rainmakers were sought out by neighbouring states — at Mapungubwe, shamans entered trance to capture the rain-animal — and that reputation persisted through the Zimbabwe-plateau polities. Christianity, largely Lutheran and evangelical, is now widespread in settled Botswana and Namibian communities, coexisting with trance healing.
