## Overview

The Betsileo are a highland ethnic group of Madagascar and the country’s third-largest ethnic group by population. They occupy the south of the Madagascar plateau, from the north of the Mania River to the foot of the Andringitra Massif, between the Bongolava chain, the Eastern Forest and the neighboring territories of other Malagasy groups. Much of the region lies within the Malagasy province of Fianarantsoa, whose capital has the same name. Their territory is traditionally divided into Northern, Central and Southern Betsileo areas. The Betsileo speak a dialect of Malagasy, a Malayo-Polynesian language derived from the Barito languages of southern Borneo. Their folk culture is associated with rice terraces, zebu raising, woodcarving, woven textiles, ceremonial practices and the continued combination of Christian and ancestral traditions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Woven cotton textiles** — The museum records include cotton cloths with stripes, geometric patterns, fringes and embroidery. One waist-band is woven from off-white cotton with brown, green and pink stripes, with braided bands sewn to its ends.

**Burial cloths** — The catalogue records burial cloths woven from cotton. One consists of dark brown, light brown, orange and white wide stripes, thin green stripes and a geometric pattern woven along the ends; another uses off-white cotton with red stripes and red geometric patterns.

**Striped Betsileo tissue** — A Museum of Ethnography record describes a Betsileo tissue sewn at the middle, striped in different colours, with silk embroidery and fringed warp threads.

**Brown embroidered cloth** — A British Museum record describes a rectangular brown cotton cloth with fringe on all sides and an embroidered silver geometric pattern with the initials “EB” at the centre.

**Basketry** — The catalogue records hats, covers, mats and baskets made of basketry, as well as a hat made of vegetable fibre.

The sources used do not name additional Betsileo textile traditions or vernacular pattern names.

### Clothing & dress

The sources used record a waist-band woven from cotton, several basketry hats, a hat made of vegetable fibre and a cap made of plaited grass. They also record cotton burial cloths and other woven cloths with stripes, fringes, geometric patterns and embroidery. ### Architecture

Betsileo traditionally lived in huts made of vegetable fibre, while wooden huts were reserved for nobles. Both types could be decorated with motifs or the horns of zebu, following Malagasy architectural norms. Mud and brick houses are more common today. The Betsileo region around Fianarantsoa is characterized by hills and valleys whose sides contain rice paddies formed into elaborate terraces. The sources used do not provide a vernacular name for a house type, or details of roofs, interior plans or urban architecture.

## Music & performance

The British Museum catalogue records a musical instrument with the native name *Valiha*, described as a tube zither or guitar made from a section of bamboo. It has numerous strings formed from the bark or peel of the bamboo and raised on small bridges at both ends. The catalogue also records reed, bark and bamboo flutes, as well as an unfinished reed or bamboo musical instrument described as a snuff-box. These records establish the presence of string and wind instruments associated with the museum’s Betsileo material, but the sources do not describe musical genres, repertories, performance settings, song forms or musicians.

The sources describe the role of diviners, astrologists and witch doctors in Betsileo society. Astrologists and diviners are consulted to set dates for ceremonies or read people’s futures, while witch doctors are believed to manipulate magic and converse with ancestors. The sources do not connect these practices to named musical performances.

## Festivals & rituals

One of the most significant Betsileo ceremonies described in the source material is *famadihana*, translated there as “turning of the bones.” During the ceremony, the remains of Betsileo ancestors are taken from their tombs, wrapped in new linens and celebrated. The sources do not provide a calendar date or season for this ceremony.

Betsileo religious life combines Christian practices with ancestral traditions and religious tenets. Much of the Malagasy population converted to Christianity after the arrival of European missionaries in the nineteenth century, and the source states that about 94% of Betsileo are Christians. Most Betsileo are described as either Protestant or Catholic, while ancestral traditions continue to be observed alongside or in combination with Christian practices.

Pre-Christian beliefs recognized ancestral spirits, ghosts and nature spirits, as well as a creator deity named Zanahary, who is still invoked for ceremonial purposes. The sources used do not describe birth rites, coming-of-age ceremonies, weddings, funerals apart from *famadihana*, annual festivals or a complete ritual calendar.

## Foodways

Rice cultivation is the Betsileo economic mainstay, and rice is described as a staple food in Madagascar. The Betsileo diet almost invariably consists of rice accompanied by livestock such as beef, chicken or duck, and secondary crops such as cassava, beans and other farm vegetables. Cattle raising is also important, although the source describes it as less significant than among neighboring groups such as the Bara.

The Museum of World Culture catalogue includes a photograph identified as a rice field in Betsileo, Madagascar, with the printed description “Rice Fields in terraces in the Betsileo.” The sources used do not name Betsileo dishes, cooking methods, ceremonial foods, drinks, dietary rules or food-related festivals.

## Oral tradition & literature

The different Betsileo kingdoms, including Fandriana, Fisakana, Manandriana and Isandra, had oral traditions dating back to the 17th century. The sources used do not preserve the contents of specific tales, epics, proverbs or riddles, and they do not identify a Betsileo literary revival or preservation project.

## Language & religion

The Betsileo speak a dialect of Malagasy. Malagasy is described as a branch of the Malayo-Polynesian language group derived from the Barito languages spoken in southern Borneo. The sources do not provide a Betsileo writing system, historical script or named literary language.

About 94% of Betsileo are Christians, most being either Protestant or Catholic. Christian practice exists alongside ancestral traditions and religious tenets. Earlier Betsileo beliefs recognized ancestral spirits, ghosts, nature spirits and the creator deity Zanahary. Diviners, astrologists and witch doctors remain significant in society: they are consulted about ceremonies, health issues, poisoning and the future, and witch doctors are believed to communicate with ancestors.
