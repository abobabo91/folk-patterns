## Overview

The Southern Ndebele (*amaNdebele*) are a Bantu-speaking people of roughly one million, concentrated in the old Transvaal highveld — today's Mpumalanga and Gauteng provinces, in a belt running from the Steelpoort and Olifants river valleys south toward Bronkhorstspruit, KwaMhlanga and Pretoria's eastern periphery. They are distinct from the Northern Ndebele of Zimbabwe, who are Nguni migrants of Mzilikazi's nineteenth-century Mfecane exodus; the Southern Ndebele descend from an earlier Nguni movement inland, and split historically into the **Ndzundza** and **Manala** polities, with the Sumayela (Northern Transvaal) Ndebele forming a third, heavily Sotho-influenced branch. In folk-culture terms they are among the most internationally legible African art traditions: the geometric mural painting of Ndzundza homesteads and the beaded regalia worn at girls' initiation were reproduced on aircraft fuselages, BMW bodies and gallery walls from the 1980s onward, making a domestic women's art into a global visual signature.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Beadwork (*ubuhlalu*)** — The core Ndebele medium. Glass seed beads, obtained through trade from the mid-nineteenth century, are sewn in rows onto tanned goatskin or (later) canvas and cotton backing, worked in a hard-edged geometric register with white grounds. Unlike Zulu *isishunka* colour-coded "bead language," Ndebele composition is architectural — blocks, stepped bands and outlined rectangles rather than triangles read as messages.

**Jocolo** — The five-panelled beaded apron worn by married women, its lower edge divided into flaps said to reference the hides of the goats slaughtered at a daughter's initiation. Given to a woman at her wedding and worn thereafter at every important rite; the *jocolo* is the single most diagnostic Ndebele garment.

**Mapoto** — The stiff rectangular beaded apron of the newly married woman, generally two-panelled and worn over a *linaga*; the fully beaded surface distinguishes it from the partly-beaded aprons of unmarried girls.

**Linaga (isiphephetu, pepetu)** — Goatskin apron forms worn by girls and young women; the *isiphephetu* is the beaded, often stiffened apron presented to an initiate at *iqude*, worn as the visible statement that she has completed seclusion.

**Nguba (Ndebele beaded blanket / cape)** — The married woman's woollen blanket, originally a trade blanket in striped grey or ochre, then laid over with beaded horizontal bands and, from the mid-twentieth century, appliquéd stripes of coloured wool and even safety pins and reflective strips. Worn as a full-body wrap; a beaded cape of the same logic covers the shoulders. The *nguba* is the clearest case of an imported industrial textile being absorbed into an indigenous grammar.

**Ndebele doll** — Beaded figures on a coiled or cloth core, dressed in miniature *jocolo* and neck rings. Made both as fertility figures for young women and, from the 1950s, for sale; the doll is the compressed encyclopaedia of Ndebele women's dress.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Razor blade / stepped-fret* — the stacked chevron and stair-step band that dominates both mural and bead; *isiqolo* concentric rings; *amasumpa* raised bead or clay knobs, literally "warts"; the outlined rectangular panel or "window"; the double-pitched roof and gable line; the flight of steps; the electric light bulb and telephone pole, twentieth-century additions; the aeroplane; the razor-edge white outline that bounds every block; the letters and number forms borrowed from shop signage.

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress is age-graded and reads as a life stage at a glance. A small girl wears a beaded string apron; at puberty she receives the *isiphephetu*; the newly married woman wears the *mapoto*, the established wife the five-flap *jocolo* and the *nguba* blanket. The *isigolwani* — grass or reed coils bound in beadwork — are worn stacked on neck, arms, legs and waist by young women and by initiates. Married women of high standing wear the brass or copper *isiqolo* neck rings (*idzila*), historically permanently fitted and understood as a sign of the husband's standing and of marital fidelity. Headwear runs from the beaded headband (*inyoga*) to the married woman's beaded *amacubi* headpiece and, for men, a simple blanket over the shoulder. Men's traditional dress is sparser — animal-skin apron (*isinyabe*) and later a beaded breastplate and blanket, with beadwork worn chiefly by initiates at *wela*.

### Architecture

The Ndzundza homestead (*umuzi*) is the canvas for the mural tradition. Rectangular sun-dried mud-brick or wattle-and-daub houses with pitched thatched or later corrugated-iron roofs are set behind a low front courtyard wall, the whole enclosure — gateposts, steps, courtyard, façade, and the curved *isiphephelo* entrance screen — treated as one continuous painted field. Women paint the exteriors freehand, without measuring or preliminary drawing, using a chicken-feather or finger-and-brush technique: bold black outlines defining flat fields of ochre, white lime, blue, green and pink. Pigment was originally earth ochre, cow dung, ash and lime; commercial acrylic house paint took over after the 1950s and drove the palette's sharpness and saturation. Repainting is seasonal, before spring or before a wedding or initiation, and is a woman's obligation and reputation. Motifs move from the mural to the bead and back; the stepped gable, flight of steps and razor-blade fret appear in both. The tradition's canonical documented site is the Ndzundza settlement at KwaMsiza near Pretoria, and the painter Esther Mahlangu is its best-known practitioner.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Hand-built coiled earthenware — beer pots, water jars and cooking vessels — is burnished, low-fired and often decorated with incised bands or applied *amasumpa* knobs, then blackened by reduction; the household beer pot is central to ancestral libation. Metalwork centres on drawn brass and copper: the *idzila* rings for neck, arm and leg, worked by specialist smiths and fitted so as not to be removed, plus brass wire bindings on grass coils. Wooden objects include carved headrests, milk pails, meat platters and long-handled spoons, and the woman's staff. Grass and palm-leaf coiled basketry — beer strainers (*intluzo*), sieves and lidded storage baskets — is utilitarian and rarely beaded. Trade goods were absorbed wholesale: enamel basins, safety pins, razor blades and plastic strapping all enter the ornamental vocabulary.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment is quantitative and public: the more brass, bead and coil a woman carries, the more visibly her household's cattle and her husband's standing are declared. Beyond the *idzila* neck, arm and ankle rings and the beaded grass *isigolwani* hoops, women wear beaded headbands, long beaded neck strips (*ithulwana*), beaded leg bands and the married woman's shoulder train (*linga koba*, "long tears") — twin beaded strips hanging from the head to signify a son's departure for and return from initiation. Initiates are ochred and secluded; male initiates at *wela* are shaved and rubbed with fat and red ochre. Ear-piercing was a childhood rite, the pierced lobe (*ukuqhumbuza*) a sign of readiness for full social participation. Beaded blankets, aprons and rings are inherited, and a woman's set accumulates across her life rather than being replaced.

## Music & performance

Ndebele music is Nguni in its foundations — unaccompanied or lightly accompanied group song built on call-and-response between a leader (*umsumo*) and a chorus, with overlapping entries, hand-clapping and stamping carrying the pulse rather than a drum battery. Initiation is the densest musical context: the *iqude* seclusion of girls and the *wela* circumcision school of boys each carry their own repertoire of songs, some restricted to initiates and their age-mates and not sung outside. Wedding song (*ukuthomba* and marriage-negotiation repertoire) accompanies the procession of the bride and the display of her beaded regalia. Instruments include the end-blown and side-blown animal-horn trumpet (*iphalaphala*), reed flutes, the *umrhubhe* mouth-bow played by scraping and voiced with overtone whistling, and the *makhweyana* braced gourd-bow; German-derived concertina and later guitar were absorbed into secular song during the labour-migrancy era. Beer-drink gatherings (*utshwala*) at the homestead are the ordinary setting for adult song, and ancestral rites (*ukuphahla*) pair song with libation at the cattle byre. Migrant men's choral and stamping styles developed in the Witwatersrand mine compounds and returned to the rural homestead as performance repertoire.

## Dance & theatre

There is no masked or puppet theatre tradition among the Ndebele; performance is sung dance tied to rite. Women's dance is predominantly upright and undemonstrative below the waist, the weight of beadwork and the stiffness of the *jocolo* and *nguba* constraining movement into shuffling processional steps, pivots and a rhythmic bow of the torso, so that the dance's visual effect is carried by the swing of the beaded flaps and the *linga koba*. The high-kicking stamping style (*indlamu* in the wider Nguni field) belongs to men and to age-set display, performed in lines with shield or stick, and is danced at initiation graduations and at the installation of chiefs. Girls' initiation ends in a public coming-out dance in which each initiate appears in her new *isiphephetu*, walked through the homestead by her female kin — the single most photographed Ndebele performance. Wedding processions are themselves choreographed, the bride veiled and led, with her attendants dancing ahead.

## Festivals & rituals

The Ndebele calendar is built on life-cycle rite rather than annual festival. Male initiation, *wela* (also *ingoma*), is held roughly every four years in winter, gathering a whole regiment (*iintanga*) of boys into a bush lodge for circumcision and instruction; the cohort takes a regimental name and remains an age-set for life, and the sequence of Ndzundza regimental names is the people's own chronology. Girls' initiation comes in two stages: *iqude* (also *ukuthomba*) at first menstruation, a short seclusion, and *iqhude*/*ukwemula* — the longer seclusion of some months in a painted room, ending in the public presentation of the initiate in new beadwork and the slaughter of goats whose hides are recalled in the *jocolo*'s five flaps. Marriage proceeds by *lobola* cattle transfer in instalments, sometimes completed only after children are born, with the bride's beaded trousseau assembled by her mother. Funerals are followed by the *ukubuyisa* rite, bringing the deceased back as an ancestor after a year. Ancestral propitiation (*ukuphahla*) with sorghum beer and the slaughter of a beast at the cattle byre is the recurrent household ritual. **Ndebele Cultural Day** and the annual commemorations of the Ndzundza defeat of 1883 — when the Mapoch stronghold fell and the polity was broken up and indentured — now function as the calendrical public festivals, and it is to that dispersal that the intensification of mural painting as a marker of identity is generally traced.

## Foodways

The staple is maize, which displaced sorghum and millet as the main grain during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Stiff maize porridge — *phuthu* / *isitjhwala* — eaten with the hand from a shared bowl is the daily base, served with *imifino* (wild and cultivated greens: amaranth, pumpkin leaves, cowpea leaves) boiled and sometimes mashed into the porridge, and with sour milk (*amasi*), curdled in a calabash or hide bag. Meat is occasional and ritual: a goat or beast slaughtered for initiation, marriage or ancestral offering, the meat boiled or roasted on coals and distributed by kin relation, with offal and specific cuts reserved for particular categories of relative. Sorghum beer (*umqombothi*) is brewed by women from malted sorghum and maize meal, fermented in earthenware pots, strained through a coiled grass *intluzo* and drunk from a shared pot passed by seniority — it is simultaneously the everyday sociable drink, the medium of hospitality, and the substance offered to the ancestors. Pumpkin, beans, groundnuts and, later, potatoes round out the field crops. There are no religious dietary prohibitions, but clan animal avoidances and the rules governing who may eat which part of a slaughtered beast are observed.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ndebele oral genres follow the Nguni pattern: *iinganekwane*, the folktale cycle told by older women to children in the evening, frequently featuring the trickster hare and the ogre figure; *iimbongo*, praise poetry, declaimed by a praiser (*imbongi*) for chiefs and for the Ndzundza royal line and reciting the succession of rulers — Musi, Mabhoko, Nyabela — and their regiments; proverbs (*iinlomani*) and riddles used didactically. Clan praise-names (*iimbongo zesibongo*) are recited at ancestral offerings and at greeting. Because Southern Ndebele was long treated administratively as a variety of Zulu and had no substantial literature before the twentieth century, written collection is thin; standardisation of isiNdebele orthography, its recognition as one of South Africa's eleven official languages in 1994, and school publishing since then have driven a modest literary revival, with the Pan South African Language Board supporting terminology and text development.

## Language & religion

IsiNdebele (Southern Ndebele) is a Nguni language of the Bantu branch of Niger-Congo, closely related to Zulu and Swati but with heavy lexical and phonological borrowing from Northern Sotho, a legacy of long coexistence on the highveld; it is written in Latin orthography and standardised on the Ndzundza variety. Sumayela Ndebele, spoken further north, is a separate and more Sotho-influenced variety. The religious landscape is predominantly Christian — mission churches from the late nineteenth century, and a strong presence of African Initiated Churches, notably the Zion Christian Church, whose Easter gathering at Moria draws Ndebele pilgrims — layered over an unbroken ancestral practice (*ukuphahla*, *ukubuyisa*) that coexists with church membership rather than replacing it. Diviners and herbalists (*izangoma*, *izinyanga*) remain consulted for illness, misfortune and the timing of rites.
