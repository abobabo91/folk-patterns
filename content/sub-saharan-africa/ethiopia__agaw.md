## Overview

The Agaw, also called Agew, are a Cushitic ethnic group native to the northern highlands of Ethiopia and neighboring Eritrea. They live in scattered communities extending from Eritrea to Gojjam, including areas around Keren, Gondar, Lake Tana, Dangila, Soqota, Lasta, Tembien, and Abergele. Their languages belong to the Central Cushitic branch of the Afroasiatic language family. The Agaw include several linguistic groups: the Northern Agaw or Bilen, Western Agaw or Qemant, Eastern Agaw or Xamir, and Southern Agaw or Awi. Their historical importance includes the Cushitic Agaw rule of the Zagwe dynasty of Ethiopia from about 1137 to 1270.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources state that Agaw peoples were historically noted by travelers and outside observers as practicing what some described as a “Hebraic religion”; some also practiced Ethiopian Orthodoxy, and many were Beta Israel Jews. Thousands of Agaw Beta Israel converted to Christianity in the 19th and early 20th century, voluntarily and forcibly, becoming the Falash Mura.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that there is a deep tradition of folklore in the Awngi language. Literature in Agaw is widely dispersed, ranging from medieval texts containing passages in the Qimant language, now mostly in Israeli museums, to the modern Bilen language with its own newspaper based in Keren, Eritrea. Historical material is also available in the Xamtanga language.

## Language & religion

The Agaw languages, also called the Central Cushitic languages, are Afroasiatic languages spoken by several groups in Ethiopia and, in one case, Eritrea. They form the main substratum influence on Amharic and other Ethio-Semitic languages. The languages include Awngi, Bilen, Xamtanga, and Qimant, as well as poorly recorded or extinct varieties identified in the source. Awngi is spoken southwest of Lake Tana and has over 350,000 speakers. Bilen is spoken in Eritrea around Keren and in eastern Sudan around Kassala; Xamtanga is spoken in the North Amhara Region; and Qimant is nearly extinct. Many Agaw also speak Amharic, Tigrinya, and/or Tigre. The sources describe historical Agaw religious practices involving a “Hebraic religion,” Ethiopian Orthodoxy, Beta Israel Judaism, and later Christianity.
