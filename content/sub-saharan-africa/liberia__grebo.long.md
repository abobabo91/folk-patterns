## Overview

The Grebo, also called Glebo, are an ethnic group or subgroup within the larger Kru group of Africa. In Liberia they are found primarily in Maryland County and Grand Kru County in the southeastern part of the country, and also in River Gee County and Sinoe County. Grebo communities in Côte d’Ivoire are known as the Krumen and occupy its southwestern corner. A 2001 estimate gives approximately 387,000 Grebo in Liberia and an estimated 48,300 in Côte d’Ivoire, not counting refugees. Precise numbers are lacking because many people were displaced by the civil war in Liberia during the late 20th and early 21st century. Grebo culture includes ritual passages, shrines, divine beliefs, carved masks, age-group organization, bush schools, ceremonial clothing, drums, and practices associated with ancestors and the surrounding world.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Lappa* is a ceremonial wrap-around cloth used in Grebo culture. The source compares it with material made from raffia, but does not describe its production technique or provide a fuller account of its patterns.

The museum catalogue records include Grebo masks decorated with cowrie shells, metal inlays, raffia-like material, vegetal fibre, animal hair, animal skin, beads, nails, and painted markings. These records document adornment on masks rather than a separate textile tradition.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name spirals, zig-zag lines, parallel lines, spots, stripes, and incised motifs. They do not identify a specifically Grebo textile motif vocabulary.

### Clothing & dress

The source describes *lappa* as a ceremonial wrap-around cloth, similar to material made from raffia. It contrasts this clothing with the pants that most common men had access to wear. The sources do not provide a fuller account of everyday clothing, women’s garments, head coverings, belts, footwear, or clothing distinctions between different social groups.

Bodios and Jide are described as wearing black attire. The source states that the Bodio was a high priest guided by the gods and that his wife, the Jide, was subject to restrictions concerning public appearance and contact with objects. The museum records describe masks with raffia-like or vegetal-fibre attachments, wooden beards, animal hair, and animal skin, but do not establish these as ordinary dress.

### Architecture

The sources describe a building known as a *takae*. It was built by the followers of a Bodio and served as his place of seclusion and residence. The source does not specify its construction materials, roof type, plan, decoration, or relationship to other Grebo houses. The sources used do not otherwise cover Grebo domestic architecture, settlement form, workshops, or urban traditions.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Grebo ritual and communal objects include carved wooden masks, stools, stones, brass rings, drums, trumpets, horns, chairs, and mortars. Shrines may include stools, stones, and brass rings known as *nitien*. These rings were associated with protection, healing, punishment, and sacred power. Large brass rings were used as ritual objects for protecting villages from “spirits, sicknesses, war and other people’s witchcraft.”

The museum catalogue records a bronze ring from Grand Cess, Liberia, with four raised elements, spiral, zig-zag, and parallel-line decoration, and a surface partly covered by green corrosion. The catalogue also records wooden masks and a wooden human-head figure. The sources do not describe Grebo ceramics.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Bodios often wore a heavy brass ankle ring placed on them by a blacksmith and worn until death. These anklets were considered animate and were regularly fed human blood. Large brass rings more generally served as ritual objects for village protection and were reportedly given power through human sacrifice.

Grebo masks and head-dresses were decorated with horns, beads, cowrie shells, nails, metal studs, raffia-like material, vegetal fibres, animal hair, fur, and painted or incised details. Museum records describe a wooden mask from Maryland County with a beard, metal inlays around the eyes, and cowrie shells on the forehead. The sources do not document tattoos, henna, or specific hair practices apart from hair-like and fibre attachments on masks.

## Music & performance

Instruments crafted in Liberia are described as connecting communal life with communication, entertainment, battle, and the signaling of danger. Grebo communities are associated with some of the largest drums described in the source. These drums are painted and carved, can be used for a town and for war, and are still maintained in some villages for communication. They may measure up to six or seven feet tall and are commonly used in pairs, sometimes with chairs or mortars used to reach them.

The source distinguishes a lower hum associated with male sound from a higher pitch associated with female sound. Trumpets and horns also signal and communicate with laborers beyond their entertainment use. The sources do not give names for particular drum types, songs, musical genres, or dances, and they do not describe musical notation, performance ensembles, or occasions such as weddings.

The Grebo language is a Kru language of Liberia. Tone is extremely important: *né* with a high or high-mid tone is the first-person pronoun “I,” while *nè* with a low tone is the singular form of “you.” The Grebo music article supplied with the sources concerns a United Kingdom indie-rock subculture rather than Grebo people and is therefore not used as evidence for Grebo musical culture.

## Dance & theatre

Grebo masks were worn in ceremonies to mediate or propitiate spirits. Dancers wearing carved masks were daubed with white clay, which marked a *ku* or spirit. Masks most often appeared at funerals and dances and were especially associated with warriors and warfare. Their representations embodied designated members even into death and were intended to appear striking and frightening to enemies while empowering those around them.

Most masks were not worn to conceal the wearer’s identity. They were placed on basket-like structures above the head and functioned more as helmets than as coverings. They could have horns, fibrous hair-like material, carved teeth, round protruding eyes, multiple sets of eyes, and sharp, flat, projecting noses. The sources do not identify named dances, dramatic genres, theatrical performances, or a separate theatre tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do not describe an annual Grebo festival calendar or provide named festivals and dates. They do describe ritual practices connected with shrines, masks, priests, divination, warfare, funerals, and initiation.

Traditional Grebo people had bush schools known as *poro* for males and *sande* for females. These schools and their associated societies continued as part of Grebo culture. Historically, poro societies adapted to new conditions by establishing Masonic lodges of the European-American style of fraternal society.

Grebo masks were used in ceremonies connected with spirits, funerals, dances, warriors, and warfare. White clay applied to participants denoted a *ku* or spirit. Ritual masks were made by highly initiated specialists who retained detailed knowledge of their forest-zone myth culture. Performers wore the masks within the forest, where rituals and ceremonies took place, and only occasionally displayed them to outsiders.

Divination was practiced by scrying. Spirits were believed to exist only in natural objects, and shrines could use large rocks, medicine, offerings, rituals, and sacrifices. Food was prepared for spirits and alcohol was poured at shrine sites to appease them and provide luck and fortune. A person accused of witchcraft, usually a woman, could be subjected to trial by ordeal through forced drinking of a decoction made from the bark of the sasswood or sassywood tree or vine.

## Foodways

The sources do not describe Grebo staple foods, cooking methods, named dishes, ceremonial meals, tea or coffee traditions, or dietary rules. They state only that offerings at shrines could include preparing food for spirits and pouring alcohol. These offerings were intended to appease spirits and provide luck and fortune.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources do not describe Grebo folktales, epic poetry, proverbs, riddles, named cycles of stories, or contemporary literary revivals. They state that the makers of ritual masks retained detailed information about their forest-zone myth culture. The sources also refer to oral histories in connection with the classification of Grebo language varieties, but they do not record their contents.

## Language & religion

Grebo is a Kru language of Liberia. Grebo speakers live in the extreme south-west of Liberia, on the coast and inland, between the rivers Cavally and Cess. In Côte d’Ivoire, Krumen is the usual name for the Grebo languages. The Grebo language area contains speech varieties forming an extensive language continuum with cultural centers of gravity usually organized around town clusters. The internal classification of these varieties is difficult to delineate precisely.

Tone is extremely important in Grebo. A high or high-mid tone in *né* marks the first-person pronoun “I,” while a low tone in *nè* marks the singular form of “you.” Bilingualism or bidialectalism is common for exogamy and commercial interaction.

Grebo religious practice includes divine beliefs, ancestors, shrines, spirits, ritual priests, offerings, sacrifices, and divination. The Bodio was a high priest guided by the gods. Shrines were associated with natural objects, especially large rocks, and with ancestors and deities. The sources describe ritual objects and sacred places but do not assign Grebo religious practice to a named world religion.
