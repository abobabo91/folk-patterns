## Overview

The Tutsi are a Bantu-speaking pastoral people of Rwanda and Burundi who historically held dominant positions in society as warriors and aristocrats. Before independence in 1962, they controlled the upper levels of a sharply divided society through a clientship system. Their material world centers on cattle: milk vessels, basketry, iron weapons, and stringed instruments reflect a culture that valued poetry, weaving, and music as leisure pursuits.

## Material culture

Tutsi material culture is built around pastoralism, featuring milk storage and dairy equipment carved from wood, coiled grass basketry with geometric patterns, iron weapons, and stringed musical instruments.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Tutsi textiles are primarily coiled and plaited grass basketry and mats, worked with natural fibres dyed black or brown in geometric patterns.

- **Coiled grass basketry** — Round-bottomed baskets, dishes, bowls, and drinking cups built from grass and vegetable fibre, worked in natural colour against brown or black dyed fibre, often with conical coiled lids.
- **Coil-sewn mats and covers** — Grass mats sewn in coils with patterns like mauve rings, zigzags, or alternating squares, sometimes used as bottle covers.
- **Plaited fibre work** — Distinct plaited pieces including pot-stands, dishes, basketwork stands, and shields, often with black diagonal stripes or cross-plaited decoration.

### Clothing & dress

Few garments are documented; what survives shows royal and ceremonial dress with head-gear, jewellery, and cloth.

- **Girl's apron** — An apron hung with wooden pendants and attached to a plaited hide girdle.
- **Bamboo head-ring** — A head ornament fringed with bamboo tubes, each topped with an attached banana seed.
- **Royal dance costume** — Worn by Prince François Karemera, a Tutsi royal dancer and nephew of King Mutara, including costume, head-gear and jewellery, documented in a 1958 drawing.

### Architecture

No sources describe Tutsi house form, building materials, or architectural decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Tutsi dairy life and warrior culture are reflected in wooden milk vessels and iron weapons; pottery is shunned as part of pastoral tradition.

- **Wooden milk bottle** (*inka*) — A necked wooden vessel with a flat base, used for storing milk, sometimes with a coil-sewn grass top.
- **Wooden butter pot** — A carved wooden container with a lid for storing butter.
- **Socketed spear** — An iron weapon with a leaf-shaped ogee blade and short iron butt on a wooden shaft.
- **Composite sword** — An iron sword with a decorated hilt.
- **Iron hoe blade** — A leaf-shaped iron blade for agricultural work.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Personal ornament is sparse but specific, including tooth ornaments, head-rings, and bells worn with performance dress.

- **Hippopotamus tooth neck ornament** — A personal ornament made from a hippopotamus tooth worn around the neck.
- **Bamboo head-ring with fringe** — A head ornament with bamboo tubes and banana seeds attached, worn as personal adornment.
- **Circlet of iron bells** — A performance ornament consisting of iron bells connected by skin, worn with dance.
- **Iron bell on hide strip** — A single iron bell attached to a strip of hide, used in accompanied movement and performance.

## Music & performance

The Tutsi cultivated music as a leisure pursuit, creating stringed instruments including trough zithers and harps.

- **Trough zither** (*cithare à huit cordes*) — A composite wooden instrument with eight strings made from twisted sinew wound on a stick spool, the body sometimes perforated with crosses and knife-stitches.
- **Harp** — A stringed instrument made of wood and fibre string.
- **Iron bells** — Percussion instruments used as accompaniment, including a circlet of bells connected by skin and a single bell on a hide strip.

## Dance & theatre

Royal dance tradition is documented through a 1958 portrait of Prince François Karemera in full costume with head-gear and jewellery.

- **Royal dance** — Prince François Karemera, identified as a Tutsi royal dancer and nephew of King Mutara, wore costume, head-gear and jewellery for accompanied movement.

## Festivals & rituals

Few ritual observances are documented, but restrictions on women's contact with cattle and royal burial customs are recorded.

- **Women's prohibition from milking** — Women and especially menstruating women are strictly forbidden to milk cows, a practice shared with neighbouring Hima and traced to Central Sudanic-speaking societies.
- **Royal burial customs** — The burial practices of lacustrine kingdoms resemble those of former Cushitic Sidama states of Ethiopia, suggesting possible Nilotic ancestry before Bantu settlement.

## Foodways

Tutsi diet is pastoral by design and narrow: relying on milk, blood and meat from cattle while avoiding grains, fish, hunting and pottery.

- **Milk and dairy products** (*inka*) — The Tutsi rely heavily on cow's milk and butter, stored in wooden vessels and accessed through a strict system of taboos restricting who may handle cattle.
- **Blood and meat** — Cattle blood and meat are central to the Tutsi diet, with no reliance on cultivated grains or hunting.
- **Tube drinking** — Drinking through tubes is practiced, suggested by a wooden container made for beer tubes, though the drink itself is not described.

## Oral tradition & literature

Poetry is named as a valued pursuit in Tutsi leisure culture, but no epic, folktale, proverb or contemporary literary tradition is recorded.

## Language & religion

The Tutsi speak Kinyarwanda (Rwanda) or Kirundi (Burundi), both similar Bantu languages shared with Hutu neighbours, and carry loanwords from extinct Cushitic and Sahelian languages.

- **Kinyarwanda** — The language spoken by Tutsi and Hutu in Rwanda.
- **Kirundi** — The language spoken by Tutsi and Hutu in Burundi, similar to Kinyarwanda.
- **Loanwords from Tale South Cushitic** — The language carries words for 'bull', 'cow dung' and 'lion' borrowed from an extinct Cushitic language associated with pastoral traditions.
- **Loanword for cattle camp** (*inka*) — The Western Lakes Bantu word for cow, borrowed from Sog Eastern Sahelian, originally meant 'cattle camp'.

## Glossary

- *inka* — Cow; a Western Lakes Bantu word borrowed from Sog Eastern Sahelian, originally meaning cattle camp
- *cithare à huit cordes* — Eight-stringed zither, a stringed musical instrument
- *trough zither* — A composite stringed instrument with a wooden trough body and twisted sinew strings
- *plaited fibre work* — Basketwork and mats made by plaiting vegetable fibres
- *coil-sewn* — Constructed by sewing coils of fibre together
- *Kinyarwanda* — The language spoken by Tutsi and Hutu in Rwanda
- *Kirundi* — The language spoken by Tutsi and Hutu in Burundi
- *clientship* — A hierarchical system binding Tutsi aristocrats to Hutu commoners
- *Tale South Cushitic* — An extinct Cushitic language, source of pastoral vocabulary
- *Sog Eastern Sahelian* — An extinct language related to Nilotic and Surmic, source of cattle-keeping terms
- *lacustrine kingdoms* — Kingdoms of the Great Lakes region
- *Nilotic* — Relating to peoples of the Nile region
- *Bantu* — A language family and people of central and southern Africa
- *Hima* — A neighbouring pastoral people north of Rwanda sharing cattle-related taboos with the Tutsi
