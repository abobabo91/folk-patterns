## Overview

Hausa communities live in northern Nigeria, southern Niger and elsewhere in West Africa. The objects selected here come mostly from British Museum records attributed to Hausa makers or users. They include clothing, household goods, horse equipment, writing boards, instruments and amulets. The Smithsonian also documents dyers in Kano and mud-brick builders in Katsina. An individual museum attribution does not mean every Hausa community made or used the same thing.

## Material culture

The selected objects show how textile, leather, wood, clay and metal work intersect in household life and formal display.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Narrow-strip weaving, indigo dyeing and embroidery appear in the museum record. The Smithsonian describes a Hausa robe whose embroidery was planned by a calligrapher and could take months to complete.

- **Embroidered robe** — A Smithsonian example joins handwoven cotton strips and embroidered motifs, including triangles and a small Qur'an-board shape.
- **Embroidered hat** — A Smithsonian cap carries colored geometric and curving designs; the museum notes that the form spread beyond Hausa communities.
- **Strip-woven trousers** — A British Museum pair uses 75 handwoven strips sewn together and geometric embroidery.
- **Indigo cloth** — Selected wrappers and blankets carry indigo-dyed strips or bands; Smithsonian photographs show dyers at Kano's pits.
- **Weaving loom** (*turkudi*) — A recorded horizontal loom shows equipment used to make cloth.
- **Embroidery plans** — Two drawings record designs for the front and back of a man's gown; they are design documents rather than garments.

### Clothing & dress

The collection includes formal robes and trousers alongside footwear, hats and a child's garment. Materials and construction vary by object.

- **Men's gown** (*riga*) — A British Museum gown is sewn from dozens of handwoven cotton strips.
- **Riding boots** — A pair of yellow-dyed leather boots has colored stitching and decoration.
- **Embroidered slippers** — Leather slippers are lined in red and embroidered with colored silk.
- **Decorated straw hat** (*malfa*) — A plaited hat has leather trim and painted details.
- **Women's dress portrait** — A museum photograph shows a Hausa woman with wrapped cloth and a head covering; it does not define dress for all Hausa women.

### Architecture

Smithsonian photographs identify mud-brick construction and geometric surface relief in Hausa settings. Commons images show decorated gates in particular cities.

- **Mud-brick wall** — A 1959 Katsina photograph records a Hausa mason and apprentice at work.
- **Raised house decoration** — A 1974 Kano photograph identifies a Hausa house with patterned relief.
- **Decorated entrance** — Photographs from Zaria and Kano document palace and compound gateways.
- **Door screen** — A selected British Museum screen combines wood and a grass fringe.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The selected household goods include basketry, pottery, leather, wood and metal. Museum descriptions often give a use more precise than the object's broad category.

- **Ablution container** (*shantali*) — A decorated metal water vessel was used for washing.
- **Cooking and serving** — A pottery hearth, cup, coiled trays and a probable food or kola-nut cover survive in the selection.
- **Patterned basketry** — Coiled mats, platters and baskets combine natural and dyed fibers.
- **Leatherwork** — Saddlebags, purses, sheaths, cases and horse equipment include worked and painted leather.
- **Horse gear** — Stirrups, a saddle, forehead ornament and quilted armor document equestrian equipment.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment includes beads and bracelets; horse ornaments and protective cases have different uses and should be read through their own records.

- **Leather-tab necklace** — Tabs are sewn onto a flat leather strip.
- **Beads and bangles** — Selected museum pieces include glass beads and metal ornaments.
- **Charm case** (*rufin laya*) — A worked leather case encloses paper.
- **Protective amulet** — A Europeana catalogue describes one Hausa amulet as protection against thieves.
- **Horse forehead ornament** — Decorated leather bars and raised elements belong to equestrian display.

## Music & performance

The British Museum records several instruments without consistently naming performers or repertoires.

- **Shawm** (*algaita*) — A wood-and-leather double-reed instrument is identified by name.
- **Gourd drum** — A leather membrane is stretched over a gourd body.
- **Stringed instruments** — Selected pieces use gourd resonators, skin, wood and strings.
- **Rattle and whistle** — Decorated and carved examples appear in the collection.

## Dance & theatre

UNESCO describes the Durbar in Kano as four processions of riders and people on foot, with different timings and costumes. Its participants come from several ethnic communities. The sources here do not establish a separate named Hausa dance or theatre form.

## Festivals & rituals

The Durbar takes place around Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha. UNESCO inscribed it in 2024 and describes the crafts and skills passed on through the event.

- **Durbar in Kano** — The emir, court, riders and walkers process through the city with distinct costumes and horse equipment.
- **Craft transmission** — UNESCO names leatherwork, weaving, dyeing, embroidery, smithing, riding and drumming.
- **Religious objects** — Qur'an boards, a leather Qur'an cover and ablution vessels document learning and worship.
- **Amulets** — Several selected leather cases are catalogued as charms; their meanings require the individual record.

## Foodways

A brass cover probably served food or kola nuts. The collection also contains a cup, cooking hearth, baskets and a market photograph. These objects do not establish a named Hausa recipe.

## Oral tradition & literature

The selected Qur'an boards carry written passages, and a leather cover protected loose sheets. The museum records consulted here do not establish a particular folktale or literary work for this profile.

## Language & religion

Hausa is a Chadic language of the Afro-Asiatic family. Qur'an boards, ablution vessels and a book cover in the collection strongly document Islamic practice, while the selected objects cannot describe every form of religious life.

## Glossary

- *algaita* — shawm identified in a British Museum record
- *malfa* — plaited hat identified in a British Museum record
- *riga* — one name recorded for a broad men's gown
- *rufin laya* — leather charm case in a British Museum record
- *shantali* — metal ablution water container
- *turkudi* — horizontal loom in a British Museum record
