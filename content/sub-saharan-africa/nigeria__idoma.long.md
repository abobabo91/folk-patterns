## Overview

The Idoma are communities of central Nigeria, particularly the Benue region. This page focuses on material that collecting institutions specifically attribute to Idoma makers. It draws chiefly on the British Museum's object records and on the Smithsonian National Museum of African Art's discussion of Idoma masks. Museum records capture the objects and the information collected with them; they do not describe the full range of Idoma life today.

## Masks, sculpture, and performance

The selected wooden masks differ markedly. A British Museum example collected by C. A. Woodhouse around 1908?20 is painted black and white, has filed teeth, and retains a cord through holes along its edge. Other masks in the collection have carved facial markings, beards made of dyed cotton, or horns. The museum attributes a headdress carrying male and female figures beneath a black-and-white patterned animal to Idoma makers. A separate horned head is catalogued as a figurine; its metal teeth and painted face are visible in a historical museum photograph.

The Smithsonian's label for an Idoma face mask is careful about function. It explains that masks and associated practices moved across ethnic divisions in the region. The mask may have been used at a formal funeral, for entertainment, or to maintain order, but a definite use is not known. This caution also applies when looking at a museum mask without a documented performance history. A mask's form is evidence of carving and decoration; it does not by itself prove a specific named rite.

A seated woman carrying a child is another form of Idoma sculpture in the British Museum collection. Its record describes the figure and its condition but does not supply a secure ritual interpretation. The accompanying Commons museum photographs add other views of Idoma-attributed sculpture and masks; one sculpture is cautiously described by its museum as possibly connected with Anjenu.

## Clothing, furnishings, and metal

The British Museum records a fringed barkcloth dress painted with geometric forms in red, yellow, and black. A separate apron is made from vegetable-fibre string with attached seeds. These two surviving examples give concrete evidence of materials and surface decoration, while leaving broader questions about who wore them and on which occasions open.

Two wooden stools show different solutions to the same everyday form. One has a circular seat and six bent supports; the other rests on a carved central support and spherical base. The collection also includes a cast brass bell with a hide-attached clapper, a decorated brass arm ring, a smoking pipe with a cast-metal bowl, and an iron arrowhead. Several similar arrows were excluded from this selection to keep the gallery useful rather than repetitive.

## Collection context

Many selected British Museum objects came from C. A. Woodhouse, a colonial official who worked in northern Nigeria from 1908. The museum states that his 1920 donation drew from the Bassa and Kabba Provinces and other central Nigerian areas. The period and place are collection history, not proof that every type remained unchanged or was made throughout all Idoma communities.

The Smithsonian's mask label is a useful reminder that historical attribution can be uncertain in this region. For this page, the object gallery uses explicit Idoma maker attributions. Records marked uncertain or attributed to other groups were left out, as were Commons items whose captions said ?Idoma or Igbo? or ?Igala or Idoma.? Contemporary dance photographs are included only where their captions directly identify an Idoma cultural event.
