## Overview

The Makonde are an ethnic group in southeast Tanzania, northern Mozambique, and Kenya. Their culture developed on the Mueda Plateau in Mozambique, and the group is now distributed throughout Tanzania and Mozambique, with a small presence in Kenya. The Ruvuma River roughly divides the group: members in Tanzania are called Makonde, while those in Mozambique are called Maconde. The two groups speak separate languages that share a common origin and culture. The Tanzanian population was estimated in 2001 at 1,140,000, while the 1997 Mozambique census recorded 233,358, giving an estimated total of 1,373,358. Makonde culture is especially associated with wood carving, initiation rites, masks, sculpture, and performance.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used do not document a Makonde textile or pattern tradition.

**Mapiko** — Mapiko masks are made from hard or soft wood and may include hair or fibers. Some include beeswax patterns representing scarification.

**Ujamaa** — Also called the “Tree of Life” style, *ujamaa* consists of connected human forms climbing over one another in postures of support. The forms refer to family, unity, and reciprocal relationships.

**Shetani** — *Shetani* woodcarvings are abstract or semi-abstract spirit figures with distorted human, animal, or otherworldly features. The style expresses Makonde mythology and the spiritual realm.

**Binadamu** — *Binadamu* is a naturalistic style depicting human forms, everyday life, work, family, and social roles. Common subjects include men smoking and women performing household chores.

**Dimoongo** — The style formally known as *Dimoongo* means “power of strength” or “tree of life.” Its sculptures traditionally portrayed connected wrestlers holding up a winning victor, before the central figure came to represent tribal heads or people united with family and community members.

**Motif vocabulary.** Tree of Life, connected human forms, wrestlers, family, unity, supernatural beings, ancestors, distorted facial features, distorted body features.

### Clothing & dress

The sources used do not provide a general account of everyday Makonde clothing, footwear, belts, or ordinary dress. In initiation contexts, however, clothing marks a change in social status. At the conclusion of the boys’ months-long initiation process, they return to their families as men with new names, clothing, and celebrations. Girls are anointed with oil and dressed in new garments created for the occasion after their ceremonial bath. Makonde masks may depict male or female figures: female masks include lip plugs and ear spools, while male masks include facial hair. One museum catalogue records a *kofia*, an embroidered Islamic skull cap, on a painted wooden *lipiko* mask representing the head of a Swahili trader.

### Architecture

The sources used do not describe Makonde house forms, roofs, settlement layouts, named building types, or architectural decoration. They state only that wood was traditionally used for building houses as well as for ritual figures, masks, household objects, and fertility and lineage sculptures.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Wood is a major material in Makonde daily and artistic life. Traditionally, carvers used wood available on the Mueda Plateau, including the soft wood *njala*, to make ritual objects, figures, masks, stools, containers, ornamental boxes, pipes, and other domestic objects. Carving was typically monoxylous, using a single block of wood, with an adze or wood chisel, knives, rasps, and sanding. Oils or waxes could be applied to preserve the surface and sheen. Museum catalogues record a wooden club, an adze haft carved as a human bust, a wooden shield covered with black and white cow hide, a wooden snuff-box with decorative engraving, a wooden spear sculpture, and wooden staffs and figures.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources document body adornment primarily in relation to initiation and masks. Traditionally, tattoos were applied to the face and body after initiation rites to mark young men and women as Makonde adults, although fewer Makonde now undergo tattooing because standards of beauty have changed. Museum catalogues record wooden lip-plugs, and Makonde female masks are described with lip plugs and ear spools. Masks may have human hair, vegetable fibers, aluminium earrings, coloured pigment, or scarification represented with applied black beeswax. A female wooden figure in a museum catalogue has glass beads inset for eyes.

## Music & performance

Makonde performance is closely connected with initiation rites. Children learn appropriate behavior and community relationships through routines, stories, music, and art, while initiation brings them together to complete this teaching through dance, sculpture, and song. The sources name no musical instruments or song genres.

For boys, the initiation period includes seclusion, instruction, and circumcision. Fire symbolizes new life and the continuity of the cultural group. Community leaders organize the event, while medicine-men hold spiritual authority and responsibility for sacred objects associated with the ceremony. Lessons focus on discipline, endurance, respect, and community responsibilities; clay and wooden figures represent proverbs and morals.

Girls undergo the *ciputu*, a puberty rite held openly rather than in the secrecy associated with the boys’ rite. After several days of seclusion and instruction, the girls take a ceremonial bath while the community sings and drums outside the house. A performance follows their emergence, with *mapiko* dancers wearing elaborate wooden masks. A museum catalogue also records a photograph of a Makonde dancer at a performance in the Village Museum carrying a wooden mask on top of the head.

## Dance & theatre

The principal named dance tradition is *Mapiko*, performed by masked dancers during Makonde rites of passage. The masks are called *lipiko* in the singular and *mapiko* in the plural. They represent *shetani* spirits, ancestors, dead people, or living characters, whether real or idealized.

In the male initiation performance, a masked dancer represents a dead man who has returned to haunt the village. Another represents the *Mashapilo*, an evil spirit associated with malice and disrupted health. The young man undergoing the transition to adulthood must conquer these entities. The masked dancers symbolize evil that must be faced and defeated. The sources also state that ceremonies for girls include ritual dance and isolation, while woodcarvings become part of a woman’s life once she is married.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not describe an annual Makonde festival calendar or seasonal festivals. They document several life-cycle and ritual practices.

Initiation rites mark the transition from childhood to adulthood and reaffirm cultural values, secrecy, respect, and community knowledge. Boys undergo seclusion, instruction, circumcision, and a months-long process that ends with their return to their families as men. Fire symbolizes new life and continuity. Their instruction includes discipline, endurance, respect for maternal figures, and the use of clay and wooden figures to convey proverbs and morals.

Girls undergo the *ciputu*, which marks puberty and prepares them for womanhood. They spend several days in seclusion inside a closed house, where female relatives instruct them about the duties of a wife and basic sexual biology. At the end, they take a ceremonial bath, are anointed with oil, and put on new garments. The community sings and drums outside, and a ceremonial *mdimu* dance is performed by masked *mapiko* dancers.

The sources also describe a spirituality in which life-force connects the living and the dead. Illness and misfortune can result when this balance is disturbed, while magic, ritual, and education are used to protect individuals and maintain harmony.

## Language & religion

Makonde, also known as ChiMakonde, is a Bantu language closely related to Yao. The language source calls Makonde a central Bantu language and records the names *Kimakonde* in Swahili and *Maconde* in Portuguese. The Matambwe or Matembwe and Mabiha or Maviha dialects are described as divergent and possibly not Makonde. Makonde speakers also use other languages, including English in Tanzania, Portuguese in Mozambique, and Swahili and Makua in both countries.

Makonde traditional religion is an animistic form of ancestor worship. It continues alongside other religious affiliations: Makonde in Tanzania are nominally Muslim, while those in Mozambique are Catholic or Muslim. Makonde ritual practice understands life-force as a continuum joining the living and the dead. Sorcery and witchcraft remain active systems, and sorcerers may be regarded as healers, protectors, community leaders, and holders of spiritual authority.
