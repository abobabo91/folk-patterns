## Overview

The Lega — also spelled Rega, and calling themselves *WaRega* — are a Bantu-speaking people of the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, numbering about 250,000 in 1998. By the 1970s they lived mainly in the middle and upper Elila valley and the upper Ulindi valley, two rivers that rise in the east of South Kivu and run northwest through Maniema to join the Lualaba below Kindu. Administratively this falls in the Mwenga and Shabunda territories of South Kivu and the Pangi Territory of Maniema. The country is deep rain forest, hot and humid all year, with mountains in the east rising above 1,800 m and 1,500–2,000 mm of rain annually. In folk-culture terms the Lega matter above all for the art of the *Bwami* association: small, intensely handled sculptures in wood and ivory whose meaning lies in the proverbs and initiation teaching attached to them rather than in the object alone.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources describe one textile among the objects shown, and no named weaving tradition.

**Raffia cloth** — a large panel, apparently a skirt, woven from raffia palm fibre and made up of seven sections stitched together. Each long edge carries a border of narrow hand-sewn raffia strips trimmed with red cotton cloth; the strips are worked with purple dye and with dark brown triangles, some printed, some sewn on. One border ends in a narrow blue-purple raffia strip fringed with tiny pompoms, the other in a wide and a narrow blue-purple strip edged in red cotton and likewise finished with pompoms.

**Motif vocabulary.** Triangles in dark brown along the raffia borders; on carved figures and ivories, circular motifs placed on the head, forehead, neck, torso and back, sometimes arranged in an X across the chest or in rows along the spine, and small circular holes forming an M on one figure's forehead.

### Clothing & dress

The sources say little about Lega dress beyond two items held in the museum collection. A cap is made of skin with the hair left on, its edge finished with a decorative band of woven fibre and cowrie shells, and fitted with a woven fibre strap also set with cowrie shells. The raffia panel described above — seven machine-stitched sections with dyed and pompom-trimmed borders — is catalogued as a skirt. What Lega people otherwise wore day to day, and how men's and women's dress differed, is not covered by the sources used here. One point that bears on dress is made about the art rather than the clothing: Bwami objects take their striking surface from being handled or worn on the body, so sculpture and adornment were not wholly separate categories.

### Architecture

The sources mention Lega buildings only in passing, in connection with cooking and eating. Villages are small and without central authority. Women cook outdoors when the weather allows, on a hearth built from slow-burning logs arranged to support a pot over the fire; in rain they cook in kitchens inside the long houses. Men eat together in the men's house, women with the young children in or beside the kitchen — so at least three kinds of space are named: the long house with its interior kitchen, the men's house, and the outdoor hearth. House form, materials, roofing and any architectural decoration are not described in the sources used here. The colonial record adds only the founding of towns by outsiders: Micici on the Elila, Shabunda on the Ulindi, and Mulungu in the mountains.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Lega ivory and bone carving dominates the object record. Spoons are cut from elephant ivory with a leaf-shaped bowl; one has an openwork handle, another a handle bearing two raised ovals, an ovoid suspension hole, and circular motifs front and back. A spiked implement is carved from elephant bone and surmounted by two human heads. In iron and wood there is the *mugusu*, a sword or bill-hook with a shaped steel blade and a wooden handle wrapped in reptile skin held by an iron ring and two leather thongs. Hunting whistles are made of wood with three sound holes, the body bound in woven vegetable fibre coated with a reddish pigment, with a hide carrying strap and a binding of cane — and in one case cane and iron — near the base.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Cowrie shells are the one ornament material the sources name, set in rows along the edge and strap of the skin cap. Body marking appears indirectly, through the ivory mask carved as a human face with triangular scarification at the bridge of the nose and linear marks across both cheeks — a record of how faces were marked, carried on the object rather than the person. Surface treatment of the objects themselves is closely tied to handling: Bwami pieces acquire their patina from being held or worn against the body, ivory taking on russet or yellow tones, while masks are rubbed with white clay at every use until the whiteness sets into the wood. Reddish deposits survive in the carved recesses of several ivory figures and across the face of the ivory mask.

## Music & performance

The only instruments the sources name are hunting whistles: wooden, with three sound holes, wrapped in fibre coated with reddish pigment and slung from a hide strap. Their context is the hunt rather than performance. Song, however, is central to Bwami. The association's objects are tied to proverbs, and those proverbs — delivered together with dance, poetry and song — are how wisdom passes to members. Beauty, knowledge and power are treated as a single bound thing rather than separate qualities. Because the teaching is graded, performance is also graded: the Bwami has seven levels for men and four for women, and some objects may only be seen, handled or owned by the highest grades, so what a member hears sung and sees performed depends on how far they have advanced. Membership is open to all, but advancing means instruction, payment and initiation at each step. Instrument names, song genres and the occasions of non-Bwami music are not covered by the sources used here. The colonial suppression of Bwami in 1933 and again in 1948 drove the association underground; by the time it was recognised again in 1958, much of the tradition of art production had been lost.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is named in the sources only as one of the three media — dance, poetry and song — through which Bwami proverbs are conveyed to members during initiation. Masks and figures are used during initiation rites and stand for the owner's rank within the association, so the mask is a badge of grade as much as a performance object. Individual dances are not named, and the sources used here do not distinguish ceremonial from entertainment forms.

## Festivals & rituals

Lega ritual life as the sources describe it runs through Bwami rather than an annual calendar. The association counterbalances the hereditary side of Lega society: a chief inherits his place patrilineally and his close relatives rank highest, but Bwami is open to all and regulates social and political life on its own terms. Advancement through its seven male and four female levels comes by a complex system of instruction, payment and initiation, each step raising the member's status; whoever reaches the top grade is recognised as a *Kindi*, a social leader of great moral authority. Masks and figures are brought out during initiation rites, their meaning carried by the proverbs attached to them, and the most restricted pieces are reserved for the highest grades. Religion is polytheistic. The gods include *Kalaga*, the promiser; *Kenkunga*, the reassembler; *Ombe*, the hidden; and *Kaginga*, the incarnation of evil, who assists sorcerers — and membership of Bwami is itself protection against the workings of witches. How much the association cost is legible in the wage economy: young men took short stints in the mines opened from 1923 specifically to raise the money for Bwami initiation or for marriage. No annual festivals or birth and funeral rites are described in the sources used here.

## Foodways

The Lega lived traditionally by hunting and gathering the forest, which is described as abundant in wildlife and in plants and trees that provide food and other uses; the upper Ulindi valley holds many monkey species, chimpanzees, leopards, buffaloes, elephants and antelopes. Roasting is the preferred method whenever it is possible. Cooking is women's work. In dry weather they cook outside on a hearth of slow-burning logs laid so as to hold a pot above the flame; in poor weather they move into the kitchens inside the long houses. Eating is segregated: the men eat as a group in the men's house, the women eat with the young children in or outside the kitchen. Colonial demands changed the diet and the work behind it — villagers were compelled by the administration to grow manioc, bananas and rice to feed the miners, which disrupted the older pattern considerably. The hunting whistles in the collection belong to this food economy. Named dishes, ceremonial food and drink traditions are not covered by the sources used here.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Lega oral form the sources document is the proverb, and it is inseparable from sculpture. Bwami masks and figures stand for moral or social values, and each is associated with proverbs; the proverbs, carried by dance, poetry and song, are the vehicle by which wisdom reaches members of the association. This makes the objects unreadable on their own. The Lega assign a category and a significance to each carved figure in a way an outsider cannot work out, and the distinction between a full figure, a head and a bust — obvious to a museum — is not meaningful to the Lega. Once a figure leaves the community without its name and meaning being asked for and written down, there is no later way to recover how the Lega would have classified it. That is a statement about a whole body of oral knowledge already detached from a large part of the surviving sculpture.

## Language & religion

Lega is a Bantu language or dialect cluster of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, with two major varieties: Shabunda Lega and Mwenga Lega. Mwenga speakers, about 10% of the total, find Shabunda difficult to understand. The name is also spelled Rega, Leka, Ileka, Kilega and Kirega; Shabunda is likewise called Igonzabale and Mwenga Shile or Ishile, and Gengele is reported to be a Shabunda-based creole. Ethnologue places Bembe in the same dialect continuum, and Nyindu is a dialect of Shi heavily influenced by Lega. Religiously the Lega are polytheistic, their gods including Kalaga, Kenkunga, Ombe and Kaginga. Catholic missionaries reached the area in the 1910s and the first Protestant mission was founded at Shabunda in 1922.
