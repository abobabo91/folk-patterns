## Overview

The Ngoni are a hybrid people formed from Nguni migrants out of what is now KwaZulu-Natal in South Africa, mixed with Tumbuka and Chewa populations among whom they settled. The sources place their present communities in the Mzimba (formerly Kathibi) and Ntcheu Districts of Malawi and the Chipata District of eastern Zambia, with the northward migration route running through Mozambique and Zimbabwe to the region around the Viphya Plateau. The sources used here do not describe a Tanzanian Ngoni population or its localities, though they note that a distinct Ngoni language is spoken in Tanzania. In folk-culture terms the Ngoni matter as a case of conquest identity: a small band — probably no more than 1,000 people crossed the Zambezi in the 1830s — absorbed so many subject peoples that by 1921, in Nyasaland alone, 245,833 people claimed Ngoni membership while few spoke the Zulu dialect called Ngoni.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here name no weaving, dyeing or printed-cloth tradition for the Ngoni. What the museum catalogue records show instead is a body of worked skin, hide and feather that carries the visual identity a woven tradition carries elsewhere.

**Warrior's skin skirt** — a garment assembled from a series of strips of skin taken from various long-haired animals, attached to a rope belt. The patterning is made by the contrast and sequence of the pelts themselves rather than by any applied design.

**Two-tone hide shield** — an oval convex shield of hide with two rows of lighter-coloured hide threaded through slits on each side of the central line, so that the pale insertions read as a pair of stripes flanking the shield's axis. The staff passes through loops at the back.

**Fur-strip ornament** — a wickerwork ball with a V-shaped hide pendant, from which hang strips of monkey skin; the collection holds two examples of the same type.

**Feather belts and bands** — belts of skin with clipped feather tufts set along the entire length, and a warrior's head-band of feathers combined with cotton cloth and string.

### Clothing & dress

The catalogue records document dress mainly in its warrior and ceremonial register. A man going armed wore the skin-strip skirt described above, or a belt of skin carrying a large feather bustle; at the head, a head-dress of brown hide sewn thickly with cock's feathers, with a tuft of cow's hair hanging at the front, or a head-band of feathers, cotton cloth and string worn together with a separate plume of iron wire, feather, wood, glass beads and thread. Ornaments were distributed over the whole body: an armlet of haired animal skin and cloth, a knee-band of cotton cloth, iron and haired hide hung with bells, a waist girdle of glass beads, haired skin, hide and fibre, leglets of white-haired hide with tie-on strings, and an anklet of hide, feathers and string. A girdle of fibre wound with copper wire and a penis-sheath made from a gourd with burnt ornament are also recorded.

### Architecture

The sources record only that Zwangendaba, having led his followers north, established a state in the region of the Viphya Plateau, in what is now Chipata District in Zambia and Mzimba and Ntcheu in Malawi.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The household objects in the collection are wood, gourd, basketry, bone and iron. Wood is turned to bowls, including one standing on four legs and one blackened on the exterior and hung with a bead-covered cord, to spoons cut from a single blank, to a smoking pipe, and to a headrest with carved decoration. Basketry appears as a spirally woven basket with a broad rim and a circular bowl woven from stiff cane. Gourds serve as containers: a stoppered receptacle open at the larger end, and a vessel ornamented with incised and burnt triangular decoration whose crack has been repaired by sewing. A bottle-shaped wooden snuff-box with gourd stoppers at both ends carries carved ornament and a binding of tin and copper wire. Earthenware is represented by a jug. Metalwork runs to weapons and to an iron sweat-scraper with a cloth loop; a comb is cut from bone.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Glass beads are the dominant ornamental material. A bracelet is made of white glass beads worked on a four-ply thread loosely twined around a string core; a pendant, apparently a quill, is encased in beadwork spiralling in white and green; the warrior's waist girdle and the plume worn with the head-band both incorporate glass beads. Beadwork aprons appear on both carved figures in the collection. A lip ornament made of stone (spar) is recorded, indicating lip piercing. Copper wire is wound around a fibre girdle, and iron wire enters the construction of head plumes.

## Music & performance

The instruments held for the Ngoni are a musical bow of wood and fibre with a separate gourd resonator; a drum of wood with a skin membrane that retains its hair, laced with hide thongs; a gourd rattle with a stick handle, catalogued as part of the contents of a diviner's bag and so tied to divination as much as to music; and whistles. Two of the whistles are near-identical: horn-like lengths of dark brown hard wood, perforated through and bound at intervals with rattan, one with a leather band near the smaller aperture, both with a two-stranded fibre suspension cord. A third whistle is tubular with a slightly flaring end, covered in skin from the lower end to the midpoint, with a band of reptile skin beneath the skin covering, a simple angled mouth hole, and twisted cotton cord run through a pierced band at the base. Beyond this inventory of instruments, the sources used here describe no song genres, no performance occasions and no named musicians. They record only that the Ngoni dialect of Zulu survives in a few ritual praise poems and songs.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used here name no Ngoni dance or dramatic tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

One festival is documented: **Nc'wala**, a celebration of first fruits held in late February at Mutenguleni, about 25 km from Chipata, by the Ngoni of Zambia. Ritual practice is otherwise visible through a single divination kit in the collection, the contents of one bag, which gives an unusually concrete picture of a diviner's equipment: two gourds ornamented with strips of cat and monkey skin, one of them beaded round the neck and each containing further objects; a bush buck horn holding powdered red wood, with a wooden spindle attached; a horn stopped with fibre; and the gourd rattle with a stick handle noted above. Two carved wooden figures — a standing woman carrying a pot on her head and a standing man wearing a headring, both in glass beadwork aprons — are the only figurative sculpture recorded, and the catalogue assigns them no ritual role. The sources record that the Ngoni dialect of Zulu is now reserved for ritual praise poems and songs, so the ritual register is where the ancestral language survives.

## Foodways

Beyond the late-February first-fruits festival, the sources used here say almost nothing about Ngoni food. They record that the Ngoni were primarily agriculturalists but that cattle were the main object of their raiding expeditions and of the migration north — a herding economy pursued by raid rather than by trade. No staple grain, dish, cooking method or drink is named.

## Oral tradition & literature

Fifteen Ngoni folk tales were written down by Margaret Read from an old Ngoni man who had heard them from his grandmother, edited by Geraldine Elliot and published in London in 1939; the collection has been reprinted many times in both Britain and the United States. The sources name no individual tale, no epic and no riddle or proverb tradition. The other surviving verbal form they record is the ritual praise poem, now one of the last uses of the ancestral dialect.

## Language & religion

The name "Ngoni language" covers several distinct tongues: a Ngoni language of Tanzania, a Ngoni language of Mozambique, the Ngoni dialect of Zulu spoken in Zambia and Malawi, a Ngoni dialect of Nyanja in Malawi, and a Ngoni dialect of Tumbuka. This spread follows directly from the group's history: integration and acculturation in the post-colonial states led the Ngoni to adopt local languages, and the Zulu-derived dialect is now used only for a few ritual praise poems and songs. The sources used here describe no Ngoni religious system, cosmology or spiritual office; the diviner's bag in the museum collection is the only trace of religious practice they contain.
