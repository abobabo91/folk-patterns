## Overview

The Bilen, also transcribed as Blin and formerly known as the Bogos or Northern Agaw, are a Cushitic ethnic group in Eritrea. They are concentrated in central Eritrea, particularly in and around the city of Keren and farther south toward Asmara, the country’s capital. A 2006 estimate placed their total population at around 91,000, approximately two percent of Eritrea’s population. Most live in Eritrea, while others live in Ethiopia and Djibouti. The Bilen speak Bilen, an Agaw language of the Central Cushitic branch of the Afro-Asiatic language family. Their folk-culture profile in the supplied sources is defined mainly through pastoralism, farming, clan organization, multilingualism, religious diversity, and the history of their written language.

## Music & performance

The sources do record several historical religious and institutional contexts that affected Bilen society. The permanent presence of the Catholic Lazarist mission in Keren influenced the Bilen and made Keren one of the centers of the Apostolic Vicariate of Abyssinia. Bilen religious life includes both Islam and Christianity. A 2020 source cited in the supplied material indicated that two-thirds of the Bilen were Muslim and that the remainder were Christians, while other sources described Sunni Islam as followed by half of the Bilen and Christianity of various denominations by the other half.

## Festivals & rituals

The historical material does describe religious change and political pressure. Many Bilen later converted to Islam in response to Egyptian raids, while the population currently practices both Islam and Christianity. The sources state that Muslim Bilen mainly inhabit rural areas and have intermingled with adjacent Tigre populations, while Christian Bilen tend to reside in urban areas and have intermingled with the Tigrinya People, also called Biher-Tigrinya. They further state that religious diversity has supported peaceful coexistence, with little conflict arising from religious differences. No more detailed ritual practices are recorded.

## Foodways

They mention farming and pastoralism.

## Oral tradition & literature

According to local oral tradition, the Bilen migrated to the Eritrean plateau from Lasta around the 9th or 10th century under the leadership of Gebre Tarqe. The supplied historical account also records a possible second migration during the fall of the Zagwe dynasty in 1270, and states that local traditions preserved memories of Bilen recognition of the authority of some Ethiopian Emperors. The period of Funj domination over neighboring areas was remembered as a time of stability.

The Bilen are first mentioned in the 14th-century text the Acts of Ewostatewos.

## Language & religion

Bilen is a Cushitic language belonging to the Afro-Asiatic family and is the only Agaw, or Central Cushitic, language spoken in Eritrea. It is spoken by about 72,000 people in and around Keren. The language is also called *b(ɨ)lina* or *b(ɨ)lin* in the supplied linguistic description. English usage includes Blin, Bilin, and Bilen, while the Ethnologue report lists additional alternative names including Bogo, Bogos, Bilayn, Balen, Beleni, Belen, Bilein, Bileno, and North Agaw.

Missionaries first developed a Bilen writing system using the Geʽez script, and the first text was published in 1882. In 1985, the Eritrean People’s Liberation Front decided to use the Latin script for Bilen and other non-Semitic languages in Eritrea. In 1993, the government established a committee to standardize the language and its Latin-based orthography. As of 1997, the alphabetic order included e, u, i, a, é, o, b, c, d, f, g, h, j, k, l, m, n, p, q, r, s, t, v, w, x, y, z, ñ, ñw, th, ch, sh, kh, kw, khw, qw, and gw.

The Bilen practice Islam and Christianity. Muslim adherents mainly inhabit rural areas and have intermingled with Tigre, while Christian Bilen tend to reside in urban areas and have intermingled with Biher-Tigrinya.
