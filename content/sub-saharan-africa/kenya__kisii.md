## Overview

The Kisii, also known as the Gusii or Abagusii, are a Bantu-speaking ethnic group indigenous to Kisii and Nyamira counties, as well as parts of Kericho and Bomet counties, in Kenya. Their language is Ekegusii, a Great Lakes Bantu language with Rogoro and Maate dialects. The language was spoken natively by 2.2 million people as of 2009, mostly among the Abagusii. Kisii Town, also called Bosongo or Getembe by local people, is their major native urban centre. The Abagusii are particularly noted for soapstone sculpture called *chigware*, concentrated around Tabaka town. Their culture also includes the eight-stringed *obokano* bass lyre, distinctive coming-of-age rituals, clan-based social organisation, cattle herding, agriculture, and a cuisine centred on *obokima*.

## Material culture

### Architecture

A typical Gusii house has a conical grass-thatched roof and is usually round, although rectangular houses also occur. Contemporary houses remain similar, but corrugated iron sheets and stone are sometimes used for roofs and walls. Traditional compounds included elevated granaries for storing millet and other crops. Homesteads were fortified with walls and trenches to protect against cattle rustling and raids. The household was divided between the homestead, called *Omochie*, and cattle camps, called *Ebisarate*, which were situated in grazing fields and protected by male warriors. In 1913, the British abolished the cattle camps and forced Abagusii families to live in dispersed homesteads.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Before colonisation, the Abagusii produced iron tools, weapons, decorations, wooden implements, pottery, and baskets. They also imported pottery from the neighbouring Luo community. Blacksmiths and other workers associated with iron and iron ore were respected and influential, although they did not form a distinct caste; smithing was largely carried out by men. Ox-drawn plows and iron hoes were used in cultivation.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They mention decorations produced by metalworkers.

## Music & performance

The Abagusii traditionally play the *obokano*, a large eight-stringed bass lyre. It is described as the largest such lyre in Kenya, and instruments may be as large as six feet tall. The *obokano* accompanies singing and dancing, while Gusii music is usually vocal. When played solo, it generally serves as an introduction to singing. The instrument is usually reserved for male members of the Gusii.

Women may play drums known as *Ekonu* and rattles during circumcision ceremonies. Flutes are also identified as traditional instruments. The sources connect music with singing, dancing, and circumcision ceremonies.

## Dance & theatre

The sources state that the *obokano* is used to accompany singing and dancing.

## Festivals & rituals

Circumcision of boys is an important Abagusii rite of passage. The ritual traditionally takes place without anesthesia at around 10 years of age, usually during November and December. After circumcision, the boys enter a period of seclusion and are led through activities by older boys. Only older circumcised boys may visit the initiates, and other visitors are forbidden during this period.

During seclusion, male initiates are taught their roles as young men and the code of conduct expected of a circumcised man. They are also taught the rules of shame, called *chinsoni*, and respect, called *ogosika*. The rite is a time of celebration for families and the wider community. Candidates invite family, friends, and neighbours several days in advance.

Traditionally, marriage was arranged by parents through intermediaries called *chisigani*. After the parents negotiated the dowry, the wedding was organised. A mentor called an *omoimari* provided continuing support to the newly married couple. Marriage between members of the same clan was forbidden, and marriage was formally established through payment of cattle to the wife’s family.

## Foodways

Before colonisation, the Gusii diet consisted of meat, milk, and blood from livestock; cereals made from millet and sorghum; fruits, vegetables, birds, edible insects called *chintuga*, and wild meat obtained through hunting and gathering. The Abagusii also kept cattle, goats, sheep, and poultry, with livestock providing an important basis for food and economic life.

The staple meal is *obokima*, made from millet flour or sorghum flour cooked with water into a hardened dough-like consistency. It is commonly served with *rinagu*, *chinsaga*, *rikuneni*, *enderema*, *emboga*, *omotere*, *risosa*, and *egesare*, among other local green leaves eaten as vegetables. It is served with milk, especially sour milk from livestock, or with another stew. The Ekegusii word *ragera*, meaning “having a meal,” usually refers to a meal centred on *obokima*.

Maize was introduced to Gusiiland by the 1920s and replaced finger millet and sorghum as the staple crop and cash crop. Maize is now largely used to prepare *obokima*. *Ritoke*, plural *amatoke*, is a dish of cooked and flavoured bananas. It is a popular snack but is considered supplemental food rather than a proper meal.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Abagusii preserve oral traditions describing an ultimate origin in a location called *Misri*, often associated with Egypt. These traditions recount a long, staged migration into the present homeland in Kisii and Nyamira counties. The ancestors in this account are credited with founding six principal Gusii clans: the Abagetutu, Abanyaribari, Abagirango, Abanchari, Abamachoge, and Ababasi.

The sources describe scholarly debate over the interpretation of the *Misri* tradition. Gideon Were located *Misri* generally north of Mount Elgon, while William Ochieng later argued that the name might preserve memories of movements from the Nile Valley region. Gusii oral traditions also record relationships with Kalenjin groups, particularly the Kipsigis, and with other Bantu-speaking communities.

## Language & religion

Ekegusii is a Great Lakes Bantu language spoken in Kisii and Nyamira counties in Nyanza Province, Kenya. It has two dialects: Rogoro, meaning “upper-side,” and Maate, meaning “lower-side.” The dialects differ phonologically in the articulation of /t/ and show lexical differences. Maate is spoken in Tabaka and Bogirango, while most other regions use Rogoro, the standard dialect of Ekegusii. The sources state that Gusii has seven vowels and contrastive vowel length.

Before Christianity and Islam, the Abagusii were monotheistic and believed in a supreme God called *Engoro*, also popularly called *Nyasae*, a loanword from Dholuo. The sun, *Risase*, and the stars were important in Abagusii religion. Ancestor spirits were called *Ebirecha*. Medicine men were revered, and divination was practised by a diviner called an *omoragori*, who could identify displeased spirits and prescribe ways to placate them.

Today, most Abagusii practise Christianity. The four major denominations are Catholicism, the Seventh-day Adventist Church, Swedish Lutheranism, and Pentecostal Assemblies of God. A minority follow the traditional religion, while others observe a syncretic form combining traditional religion and Christianity.
