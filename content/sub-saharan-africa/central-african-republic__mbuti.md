## Overview

The Mbuti (*BaMbuti*, singular *MuMbuti*), together with the closely related Efe and Sua, are a forest-dwelling people of the Ituri rainforest, whose homeland straddles the north-eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo and spills across the border into the south-eastern reaches of the Central African Republic and adjacent zones of South Sudan and Uganda. They are one of several Central African forager peoples grouped popularly under the term "pygmy" — a label of external and often pejorative origin that scholars increasingly replace with *Bambuti* or the specific band-name (Efe, Sua, Aka, Baka) — and are distinguished from their Bantu and Sudanic-speaking farming neighbours by an average adult stature well under 150 cm, an economy centred on net- and bow-hunting, honey-collecting and the gathering of forest tubers, and a mobile camp-based social organisation without chiefs, clans or centralised authority. The Mbuti themselves speak no ancestral language of their own; each band has instead absorbed the tongue of the neighbouring cultivators with whom it maintains a hereditary trade partnership — most Mbuti proper speak varieties of KiBila or KiNdaka (Bantu), the Efe speak Lese (Central Sudanic), and the Sua speak KiBudu. Population estimates for the whole Bambuti complex range from 30,000 to perhaps 40,000, of whom only a small fraction — commonly cited in the low thousands — live within the borders of the Central African Republic proper, most Mbuti communities being concentrated in Congolese Ituri around the Epulu River. In folk-culture terms they matter above all as the makers of *pongo* barkcloth painted with soot-and-gardenia pigment in the free-hand abstract idiom now known internationally as *Mbuti painting*, and as the custodians of one of Africa's most sophisticated polyphonic vocal traditions, in which the forest itself is addressed as a singing partner through the *molimo* ceremony.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Mbuti do not weave. Cloth in the true woven sense reaches the camp only through trade with village neighbours, and the ancestral fabric of the forest is instead beaten bark — *pongo* (also transcribed *lupongo* or *murumba* depending on band and neighbouring language). The raw material is the inner bast of certain fig and *Ficus*-related trees, notably species locally called *lombi* and *bongungu*; a section of trunk is stripped, soaked, and beaten on a hardwood anvil with a grooved ivory or wooden mallet until the fibres felt out into a broad, supple, buff-coloured sheet. Once dried, the cloth is decorated by the women of the camp: the pigment is a black paste of soot mixed with gardenia-fruit juice or forest-vine sap, and it is applied with a finger, a twig, or a chewed stick, freehand and without preliminary drawing, in the improvisational graphic idiom that Western collectors from the 1930s onward labelled *Mbuti painting* or *Ituri barkcloth*. The vocabulary of marks is unmistakable: parallel and cross-hatched line bundles, isolated dots and circles, lozenges, chevrons, "ladders", meanders, star bursts, and irregular grids that break rhythm and re-form across the sheet. Some pieces are read locally as evocations of forest phenomena — leaf-litter, animal tracks, spider webs, the play of light through canopy — while others are frankly abstract, and the same maker rarely repeats a composition. In some communities the painted cloths were worn as loincloths or aprons and only later, under the pressure of the twentieth-century collector market, became stand-alone objects; the parallel with contemporaneous European post-war abstraction has been noted often, though the Mbuti tradition is manifestly indigenous and predates that contact.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional Mbuti dress is minimal and calibrated to the humidity of the forest. Both men and women wear a short *pongo* barkcloth loincloth passed between the legs and tucked into a bark or liana waist-cord; women's cloths are typically the more elaborately painted, and a second broader cloth may be wrapped as a skirt or slung as a baby-carrier. Upper bodies are usually bare, though a shoulder cape of barkcloth or, latterly, of trade cotton is worn in cold rain. Ornament — see below — carries much of the visual load that clothing carries in neighbouring farming societies. Since the mid-twentieth century industrially printed cotton wrappers, T-shirts and shorts obtained from Bila, Ndaka and Lese village markets have progressively displaced *pongo* for everyday wear, and painted barkcloth today survives in most camps primarily for ceremony, for sale to outsiders, and for the girls' *elima* seclusion. Footwear is essentially absent; the forest floor is walked barefoot.

### Architecture

The Mbuti have no monumental or permanent architecture, and this absence is itself diagnostic of their culture rather than a lack. Because a hunting camp (*apa* in some dialects) is moved every few weeks as game and honey shift and as ritual or social pressure demands, the dwelling is designed to be built in an afternoon by a single woman from materials gathered within a few metres of the chosen spot. The classic Mbuti hut (*endu*) is a small hemispherical dome, roughly two metres in diameter and barely tall enough to stand in at the centre, made by planting flexible saplings in a rough circle, bending them inward and lashing their tips with vine, then thatching the resulting armature with large *mongongo* or *phrynium* leaves laid in overlapping courses from the ground up like tiles. A low doorway faces the hearth. Huts are arranged in a loose ring or crescent around a central clearing (*bopi*) that functions as communal space, dance ground, and — during the *molimo* — ceremonial arena. When the band moves, the huts are simply abandoned to the forest, which reclaims them within a season. Longer-term "roadside" settlements at the forest edge, established under Belgian colonial and later Congolese and Central African state pressure to sedentarise, use rectangular wattle-and-daub houses borrowed directly from village neighbours, and are regarded by many Mbuti as places of illness and social decay rather than as home.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Mbuti make no pottery and smelt no iron. Both ceramics and metal blades reach the camp through the hereditary trade relationship with village cultivators, in which forest meat, honey and *pongo* are exchanged for iron spear- and arrow-heads, machetes, cooking pots and cultivated starches (plantain, cassava, rice). The objects that are unambiguously Mbuti-made are those of forest materials: the great hunting net (*bo*), plaited from the bark-fibre of the *kusa* liana in sections up to a hundred metres long and joined into a communal drive-net; bows of springy *ngbi* wood strung with twisted plant fibre; small-game and monkey arrows tipped with iron and, for large game and formerly for war, smeared with a *Strophanthus*-based cardiac poison; the honey-gathering kit of smouldering leaf-torch, bark ladder and bark bucket; carrying baskets of coiled or twined liana; and the barkcloth-beating mallets themselves, sometimes of ivory in older assemblages. Musical instruments — the small arched harp, the wooden slit-drum, the raft-zither, the *molimo* trumpet — belong equally to this material register (see below).

### Jewelry & body adornment

If cloth is sparse, the Mbuti body is not. Personal adornment is dense, playful, and mostly self-made from forest materials: necklaces and belts of seed beads (especially the shining black *Canna* seed and the red-and-black *Abrus precatorius*), fibre bracelets and anklets, cane armlets, feather and leaf head-ornaments assembled fresh for a dance, and the teeth of small game strung as pendants. Body painting is a signature Mbuti art in its own right — the *nkumbi* initiation of boys and, more distinctively, the *elima* first-menstruation seclusion of girls both involve extensive patterned application of gardenia-black and, in some bands, white clay or red *ngula* camwood paste to the face, torso and limbs, in dot, line and lattice motifs that echo the graphic language of *pongo* painting. Scarification is not a Mbuti practice in the elaborated sense found among neighbouring farmers, though light cicatrisation and ritual filing of the upper incisors to a point are reported in some communities, commonly under influence of the village partner group. Coiffure ranges from close-cropped to elaborately shaved patterns and, on ceremonial occasions, decorated with fresh flowers, leaves and feathers.

## Music & performance

Mbuti music is one of the most studied vocal traditions in Africa and one of the earliest well-documented cases of dense contrapuntal polyphony outside the European art tradition. The characteristic texture is a hocketed, interlocking polyphony sung by the whole camp — men, women and children — in which each singer contributes a short repeated motif, often only one or two pitches, offset in time from the others so that the composite melody exists nowhere in any single voice but only in the aggregate. Yodelling between chest and head voice is a defining device, and the underlying scale is commonly anhemitonic pentatonic. The best-known genre is the *molimo* — both a ceremony and the long wooden (formerly, and now sometimes metal drainpipe) trumpet through which the voice of the forest is sung back to the camp during the nightly fire-side ritual convened in times of crisis, prolonged bad hunting, or the death of an adult. The *elima* songs of the girls' first-menstruation seclusion and the honey-season songs form parallel repertoires. Instruments accompanying the voice include the small five-string arched harp *(kundi* in neighbouring Zande usage, borrowed and reshaped by the Mbuti), the earth-bow, the musical bow struck with a stick, the log slit-drum obtained by trade, and rattles of seed pods lashed to the ankles. The Aka Pygmies of the neighbouring western forest (Central African Republic and Congo-Brazzaville) hold the UNESCO inscription "Polyphonies of the Aka Pygmies of Central Africa" (proclaimed 2003, inscribed on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2008); the closely comparable Mbuti and Efe repertoires are not separately inscribed but are widely regarded by ethnomusicologists as part of the same broader Central African forager polyphonic complex.

## Dance & theatre

Dance among the Mbuti is participatory rather than presentational, and it is not readily separable from music: there is no dedicated performer class and no audience distinct from the singers. Circle and line dances around the central hearth of the *bopi* accompany honey songs, hunting-success songs and the girls' *elima*. Movement vocabulary emphasises small, rapid steps, deep flexion at the knee, shoulder isolations and a torso lean that lets the singer keep breathing polyphonically while dancing. Mimetic hunting dances — imitations of elephant, forest buffalo, duiker or monkey — are performed by men, sometimes with a leaf-and-liana costume; comedic and satirical impersonations of village neighbours or of colonial officials form a folk-theatrical genre in their own right and are often improvised at the fire. There is no masking tradition of the elaborate West or Central African kind; when a masked figure appears — for instance the *nkumbi* initiation masker in bands influenced by Bantu neighbours — the form is understood as borrowed from the village partner group rather than as ancestrally Mbuti.

## Festivals & rituals

Mbuti ritual life is anchored not to a solar or agricultural calendar but to the life of the band and the mood of the forest. The two great ceremonies are the *molimo*, convened by the men after a death or a run of misfortune and sung nightly for weeks until the forest is "reawakened", and the *elima*, the collective first-menstruation seclusion of girls held in a specially built leaf hut, during which the initiates are painted, taught songs, and courted at the hut door by the young men of surrounding camps. Boys in many bands undergo *nkumbi* circumcision initiation jointly with the sons of their village trade partners — an institution that binds the two peoples ritually and is administered largely by the villagers, using masks and secret-society apparatus that the Mbuti themselves do not otherwise employ. Marriage is by sister-exchange between bands, with no bridewealth in the cattle-and-cash sense of the neighbouring farmers, and is marked simply by the couple's setting up a hut together. Funerary practice is brief; the body is buried in or near the abandoned hut, the camp is moved, and the *molimo* is sung.

## Foodways

The Mbuti diet is one of the most protein-rich of any forager population on record and rests on a tripod of net-hunted game, wild honey and gathered plant food, complemented by cultivated starches obtained by trade. Net hunts (*bo*) are collective and take up most of the day: the women and children drive game into a long semicircle of vertical nets where the men wait with spears; the Efe further east rely more heavily on the individual bow-and-arrow hunt of arboreal monkeys. Blue duiker (*Cephalophus monticola*) is the staple quarry; larger duikers, forest pig, and occasionally elephant are also taken. Honey — of stingless bees and of the more formidable *Apis mellifera adansonii* — is the seasonal luxury and the subject of a rich song and ritual repertoire; the two-month honey season is a period of dispersal into small family camps. Gathered foods include wild yams (*Dioscorea* species), *itaba* mushrooms, forest nuts, caterpillars (a major seasonal protein source), termites, and palm hearts. Plantain, cassava and rice reach the camp by trade with the village partner and are boiled or pounded into a stiff porridge; palm wine and, latterly, distilled village spirits are drunk on social occasions. Cooking is by direct fire, leaf-wrapping (*en papillote* in effect) and boiling in the traded clay or aluminium pot. There is no salt production; salt too comes by trade.

## Oral tradition & literature

Mbuti oral tradition is transmitted almost entirely through song and through informal fireside narration; there is no epic tradition of the West African griot type and no professional class of reciters. The largest verbal genre is the etiological and trickster tale, with the chameleon and the small forest antelope recurring as protagonists and with the forest itself as a moral agent that rewards the generous and punishes the loud, the wasteful and the quarrelsome. Proverbs are short, image-dense, and often built on hunting and honey imagery. Song texts — especially of the *molimo* and *elima* — are frequently vocable-based rather than lexical, and their meaning resides in the collective act of singing rather than in a paraphrasable content; this is one reason Mbuti music has proved so resistant to conventional song-text transcription. Since the mid-twentieth century a small written literature about the Mbuti, rather than by them, has accumulated — most influentially the ethnographies of Colin Turnbull — and Mbuti individuals increasingly appear as co-authors and consultants in contemporary Congolese and Central African ethnographic and advocacy publications.

## Language & religion

As noted, the Mbuti retain no ancestral language of their own and speak the tongue of their village trade partners, most often a Bantu language of the Bila–Ndaka group, with Efe bands speaking Central Sudanic Lese. Whether an original "pygmy" language ever existed is an open scholarly question; a small residual vocabulary of forest-specific terms — plant names, honey terminology, ritual words — is shared across Bambuti groups regardless of their present host language and may be a relic of such a substrate. Religion is centred on the forest itself, addressed as *ndura* or, in more explicitly personified usage, as a benevolent parental being sometimes glossed as *Jengi* or *Khonvoum* depending on band and neighbouring tradition. The forest is understood to sleep at times, and misfortune is read as its inattention; the *molimo* ceremony exists precisely to sing it awake. There is no priesthood, no shrine, no idol, and no ancestor cult of the elaborated village kind. Christian mission activity — Catholic and various Protestant denominations — has been present at the forest edge since the early twentieth century and is nominally accepted in many roadside communities, generally as a layer added to rather than replacing the forest cosmology; Islam is largely absent among the Mbuti themselves though present among some of their trading partners.
