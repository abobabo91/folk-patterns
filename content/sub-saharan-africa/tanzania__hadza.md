## Overview

The Hadza are a small hunter-gatherer group living in Tanzania's Rift Valley and Serengeti Plateau. They speak Hadzane, a unique language with click consonants unrelated to any other language family. Their material world is built around hunting, gathering, and seasonal movement, with camps of 20 to 30 people that relocate to follow game, water, and plant foods. Egalitarian in structure, their culture centers on the epeme rite, which marks a man's transition to adulthood through large-game hunting.

## Material culture

Hadza material culture is a portable foraging toolkit of wood, hide, bone and traded goods, rebuilt as camps move with the seasons.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Hadza do not weave cloth but create patterns in beadwork applied to hide and imported cloth, and work with animal skin itself.

- **Beaded hide skirt** — A married woman's skirt of impala hide decorated with beads, cowrie shells and a bell.
- **Beaded cloth apron** — A married woman's apron of cloth strip decorated with coloured beads strung on cord.
- **Feather-and-leather head-dress** — A man's dance head-dress of ostrich feathers mounted on leather with glass-bead decoration.
- **Skin garments** — A married woman's upper garment of antelope hide and a man's apron of genet skin with natural markings.

### Clothing & dress

Hadza dress is made of animal skin decorated with traded glass beads, with practical hunting gear worn as part of everyday dress.

- **Impala-hide skirt** — Married women wore impala-hide skirts decorated with beads, cowrie shells and a bell.
- **Genet-skin apron** — Men wore a pubic apron of genet skin with natural spotted markings.
- **Leather sandals** — Both sexes wore sandals made of leather.
- **Dance bells** — Metal bells tied to a leather strap around the ankle for ceremonial dance.
- **Beaded head-bands** — Women's head ornaments made of glass beads, including ones in white, red and blue.

### Architecture

Hadza shelters are built for seasonal camps of 20 to 30 people that move frequently to follow water, game and plant foods.

- **Portable hut** (*hamakwanebee*) — A shelter that can be put up in a few hours and abandoned when camps relocate.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Hadza make no pottery and do not smelt iron, but work metal acquired through trade into hunting tools and everyday objects.

- **Axe** (*ato*) — An iron blade in a wooden handle, with the socket burned through the shaft using red-hot iron.
- **Poisoned arrow** — An arrow with an iron head, wooden shaft and vulture-feather fletching, poisoned with branches of Adenium coetaneum.
- **Digging stick** — A wooden stick with a fire-hardened point used by women to gather tubers and roots.
- **Carrying bag** (*mbogoshi*) — A bag made of impala or dik-dik leather for carrying gathered foods and small items.
- **Smoking pipe** — Made from soft sedimentary rock, shaped with knife and axe, bored with an awl and reinforced with a hide cylinder.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Hadza ornament combines glass trade beads with materials from the hunt, such as bone, cartridges and animal skin.

- **Baboon bone necklace** — A necklace of beads strung with tail bones from a baboon.
- **Beaded arm-band** — An ornament worn by women, made of glass beads.
- **Zebra-mane head ornament** — A head ornament made from the hair of a zebra's mane, worn by men and boys.
- **Cartridge-case necklace** — A necklace of red and white beads strung on cord with empty cartridge-cases.

## Music & performance

The Hadza use only objects associated with dance to carry sound; no other musical instruments or song genres are recorded.

- **Dance bells** — Metal bells tied to a leather strap around the ankle, worn during ritual dance.
- **Dance rattle** — A rattle made from a gourd, used in dance performance.

## Dance & theatre

The only recorded Hadza dance is the monthly epeme, a ritual performance by men at the new moon that marks manhood and hunting prowess.

- **Epeme dance** (*epeme*) — A monthly men's dance held at the new moon that celebrates hunting, manhood, and the relationship between the sexes.
- **Epeme man** (*epeme*) — A man who has killed large game and become a true adult, allowed to eat certain parts of large animals.

## Festivals & rituals

Hadza ritual life is tied to the moon and the hunt, with two main ceremonies: the monthly epeme dance for men and the circumcision and coming-of-age ceremony for women.

- **Epeme rite** (*epeme*) — A monthly ritual dance and rite of passage for men, founded according to oral history by the hairless giants of the second epoch.
- **Maitoko ceremony** (*maitoko*) — A circumcision and coming-of-age ceremony for women, held less frequently than the epeme dance.

## Foodways

The Hadza forage selectively for tubers, berries, honey and meat, adjusting their diet by season and working with honeyguides to find wild honey.

- **Honeyguide partnership** (*Indicator indicator*) — The greater honeyguide leads hunters to wild bees' nests in exchange for a portion of the honey left behind.
- **Baobab ladder** — Pointed sticks driven into a baobab trunk to climb up and reach fruit high in the tree.
- **Poisoned hunting** — Men hunt in pairs during the dry season using poisoned bows and arrows, lying in wait at waterholes overnight.
- **Tubers and baobab** — Women gather tubers and baobab fruit using digging sticks and grass baskets, providing about 100 to 150 grams of fibre daily.

## Oral tradition & literature

Hadza history is told as a sequence of four epochs, each with different inhabitants and tools, preserved entirely in oral form in the Hadzane language.

- **First epoch giants** (*akakaanebee or geranebee*) — Hairy giants with no tools or fire who ran down game until it dropped and slept under trees.
- **Second epoch giants** (*xhaaxhaanebee*) — Hairless giants who cooked with fire, hunted with dogs, lived in caves and began the epeme rite.
- **Third epoch people** (*hamakwanebee*) — Smaller people who invented the bow and arrow, built huts, traded for iron and invented the gambling game lukuchuko.
- **Honeyguide folk tales** — Tales featuring the honeyguide in both bird and human form, and a woman harvesting honey despite the assertion that honey-gathering is men's work.

## Language & religion

Hadzane is a language isolate with click consonants and no known relatives, spoken by the Hadza as the primary marker of ethnic identity.

- **Click consonants** — Hadzane is one of only three East African languages with click consonants, and clicks appear inside words.
- **Ishoko the Sun** (*Ishoko*) — A deity to whom the Hadza pray during hunts and to whom they believe they go when they die.
- **Haine the Moon** (*Haine*) — A deity to whom the Hadza pray, and around whose phases ritual life is structured.
- **Native numerals** (*itchâme, piye*) — Hadzane has native words for one (itchâme) and two (piye); higher numbers are borrowed from neighbouring languages.

## Glossary

- *Hadzane* — Language of the Hadza, a language isolate with click consonants
- *epeme* — Monthly men's dance at the new moon; also manhood and hunting prowess
- *maitoko* — Circumcision and coming-of-age ceremony for women
- *hamakwanebee* — Third epoch in Hadza oral history; people who built huts and invented the bow
- *xhaaxhaanebee* — Second epoch; hairless giants who used fire and began the epeme rite
- *akakaanebee* — First epoch; hairy giants with no tools or fire
- *ato* — Axe with iron blade in wooden handle
- *mbogoshi* — Carrying bag made of impala or dik-dik leather
- *lukuchuko* — Gambling game invented by the third-epoch people
- *Ishoko* — The Sun; deity to whom the Hadza pray and believe they join after death
- *Haine* — The Moon; deity central to Hadza ritual and prayer
- *Indicator indicator* — Greater honeyguide bird that leads hunters to wild bees' nests
- *Adenium coetaneum* — Shrub whose branches are used to poison arrows
- *itchâme* — Hadzane word for one
- *piye* — Hadzane word for two
