## Overview

The Kongo (*Bakongo*, singular *Mukongo*) are a Bantu-speaking people of west-central Africa whose homeland straddles the lower Congo River and the Atlantic coast from southern Gabon through the Republic of the Congo, the Cabinda enclave, the western provinces of the Democratic Republic of the Congo (Kongo Central, formerly Bas-Congo), and northern Angola. Their language, *Kikongo* (*Kikoongo*), belongs to the Bantu H10 group and is one of the four national languages of the DRC; a simplified trade form, *Kituba* or *Kikongo ya leta*, serves as a lingua franca across much of the lower Congo basin. Population estimates for the wider Kongo cluster — including sub-groups such as the *Yombe*, *Vili*, *Woyo*, *Bembe*, *Sundi*, *Ndibu*, *Manianga* and *Solongo* — range between ten and sixteen million. Historically the Kongo formed one of the great early-modern polities of Atlantic Africa: the Kingdom of Kongo (*Kongo dya Ntotila*), founded around 1390 with its capital at *Mbanza Kongo* (later São Salvador), converted to Catholicism under Nzinga a Nkuwu in 1491 and maintained diplomatic relations with Portugal and the Vatican for four centuries. From this long convergence of Bantu cosmology and lusophone Christianity emerged the material tradition for which the Kongo matter in world folk-culture terms: the power figures *minkisi* and their nailed and mirrored subset *minkondi*, the *mangaaka* judicial guardians of the Chiloango basin, the seated soapstone ancestor effigies *mintadi* (singular *ntadi*), the raphia (raffia) pile-cloths and geometric mats of the Kuba-adjacent Yombe and Woyo, the maternity figures *pfemba*, the woven prestige *mpu* cap of chiefs, and the syncretic brass and wood *Kongo crucifix* (*nkangi kiditu*) that grafts Christ onto an older cosmogram.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Kongo textile tradition centres on **raphia** (*mbadi*), the fibre stripped from the young fronds of the *Raphia vinifera* palm. Warp and weft are worked on a single-heddle upright loom, historically operated by men, producing squares roughly 50–60 cm on a side that are then joined into larger cloths, mats and skirts. The finest historical grade — the seventeenth-century *libongo* and the pile-woven *mpusu* — was recorded by Capuchin missionaries as comparable in touch to velvet, and was used as both currency and prestige dress in the Kingdom of Kongo. Cut-pile decoration (*ntsak*), in which supplementary raphia tufts are inserted and clipped, produces the plush surfaces most often associated with the neighbouring Kuba but present also among the Yombe and Bembe. The design vocabulary is emphatically geometric: interlace (*makanda*), lozenges, zig-zags (*nsansi*), chequerboards, and the ubiquitous cross-in-circle *dikenga dia Kongo* — the Kongo cosmogram whose four points mark sunrise, zenith, sunset and midnight and, by extension, birth, maturity, death and rebirth. On the coastal Woyo side the same grammar is transposed into carved wooden pot-lids (*taampha* or *mataampha*), in which each incised motif encodes a proverb used by a wife to signal grievances to her husband at meals. Printed cotton cloth — the Dutch-wax *pagne* introduced through Matadi and Boma in the colonial period — has largely displaced raphia for daily wear, but the older palette of indigo, tukula-red (from powdered camwood, *Pterocarpus soyauxii*) and kaolin-white survives in ritual contexts.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday male dress in the historical kingdom consisted of a knee-length raphia wrapper (*nlele*) tied at the waist, with the torso bare or draped in a second cloth over the left shoulder; women wore a longer wrapper knotted above the breasts or at the hip. Chiefs and titled elders were distinguished less by cut than by regalia: the woven basketry cap **mpu** — a close-fitting knotted-fibre bonnet often studded with leopard claws, brass tacks or cowries and worked in interlace patterns that read as coded genealogies — the leopard-skin (*ngo*) shoulder cape, an ivory or wooden staff (*mvwala*) with figurative finial, and a fly-whisk (*mfunya*) of buffalo or elephant tail set into a carved handle. Ceremonial anklets and wristlets of woven raphia or twisted brass wire (*nlunga*) marked initiates of the *kimpasi* and *khimba* societies. Post-conversion Kongo elites layered Portuguese-derived elements — capes, brimmed hats, iron swords — over this indigenous grammar; the seventeenth-century royal portraits from São Salvador show precisely this hybrid. Contemporary Kongo dress in the DRC follows the general Central African pattern of tailored *pagne* ensembles for women and the *abacost* or European suit for men, with *mpu* caps and raphia sashes reappearing at coronations of customary chiefs and at funerals of notables.

### Architecture

The Kongo have no monumental stone architecture of the kind associated with the Great Lakes or the Sahel, and the built environment of the pre-colonial kingdom impressed European visitors chiefly by its scale and orderly plan rather than by permanent construction. The typical village (*vata* or *mbanza* for a chiefly seat) was arranged as parallel rows of rectangular houses flanking a central plaza (*lumbu*) containing the chief's compound, the men's palaver shelter (*mbongi*), and often a shrine enclosure with a sacred tree. Houses were built of a timber frame lashed with lianas, walled in raphia-palm mat panels (*tsivu*) or wattle-and-daub, and roofed with layered palm thatch in a steep hip or gable; interior floors of packed earth were sometimes finished with a slurry of cow dung and kaolin. The most architecturally distinctive Kongo constructions were the enclosed royal necropoli (*mbanza ya bafwa*) and the open-air *nzo a nkisi* shrine houses that sheltered the great communal *minkisi*, marked externally by planted *nsanda* (fig) trees and by earth cones or termite mounds re-used as altars. Along the Atlantic coast the Vili port town of Loango, described by Dutch traders in the seventeenth century, extended this vernacular into planned quarters for foreign factors, but the buildings themselves remained perishable. The material continuity of Kongo architecture therefore lies less in surviving structures than in the persistent spatial logic of the *dikenga* cosmogram, which orients graves, shrines and chiefly compounds along an east–west axis.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Kongo ceramics are hand-built by women using coil-and-scrape technique, fired in open bonfires, and blackened with a post-firing reduction in leaves. Forms include the wide-mouthed cooking pot (*nzungu*), the water jar (*mvungu*) with a constricted neck, and the small ritual bowl used to hold libation palm-wine at ancestor shrines. Surface decoration is restrained: incised bands of hatching, rocker-stamping, and cordoned rims. Far more elaborate is the tradition of carved soft-stone funerary sculpture from the Boma region of the lower Congo — the **mintadi** (singular **ntadi**), seated or kneeling figures cut from a soft chloritic schist, placed on the graves of chiefs and notables from the sixteenth century onward, with attributes (crossed legs, hand to cheek, *mpu* cap, drum) encoding titles and virtues. Iron was smelted historically in bloomeries throughout the Manianga region and worked by hereditary smiths (*mfudi*) into hoes, spears, throwing knives, and the twin iron gong (*ngonge* or *ngongi*) whose double strike calls chiefs to council. Brass, arriving via Atlantic trade, was cast by lost-wax method into anklets, crucifixes and the small standing or seated *santu* figures that fused Kongo *minkisi* logic with Iberian Catholic iconography. Everyday objects worth noting are the carved wooden **taampha** proverb-lids of the Woyo, the double-cup palm-wine goblet, and the slit-drum (*ngoma nkonko*), hollowed from a single log.

The **minkisi** (singular *nkisi*) themselves stand at the centre of Kongo material religion and deserve their own note: they are containers — a gourd, a shell, a bundle, or most famously a carved wooden figure — into which a ritual specialist (*nganga*) has packed *bilongo*, empowering substances drawn from the *mpemba* world of the dead (white clay, graveyard earth, seeds, mirrors sealing an abdominal or forehead cavity). The subset **nkondi** (plural *minkondi*), literally "hunter," is activated by driving iron nails, blades and wedges into its body with each oath or grievance sworn before it; the most imposing type, the **mangaaka** judicial figures of the Yombe and Vili from the Chiloango river basin, stand up to a metre and a quarter tall, arms akimbo, tongue protruding, eyes inset with glass or porcelain. The maternal counterpart is the **pfemba**, a seated Yombe mother-and-child figure associated with the *pfemba* fertility cult, carved with fine scarification patterns and a *mpu* cap. Smaller portable protective *minkisi*, called **nduda**, guard households and travellers against night-witches (*ndoki*).

### Jewelry & body adornment

Kongo body adornment historically favoured brass and copper over gold. Twisted or coiled brass wire anklets (*nlunga za malu*), often massive enough to require the wearer to walk with a swaying gait, marked the wealth of chiefly wives; matched armlets and neck-rings completed the ensemble. Small brass or iron crucifixes and *santu* pendants, worn on a fibre cord, doubled as protective amulets after the Christianisation of the kingdom. Cowrie shells (*nzimbu*) — which also served as the currency of the historical kingdom, harvested off Luanda island — were sewn onto caps, sashes and initiation belts. Scarification (*mfindu*), still visible in early twentieth-century photographs, followed sub-group patterns: parallel keloid ridges on the temples and abdomen among the Yombe, dotted rosettes among the Bembe, and the vertical median forehead line of the Woyo. Filed incisors, particularly the sharpening of the upper front teeth to a point, was practised in some communities into the colonial period. Hair was dressed into elaborate crested, braided or shaved geometric styles that themselves referenced the *dikenga* cross.

## Music & performance

Kongo music is built on layered polyrhythm, call-and-response vocals, and a rich lamellophone and drum ecology. The lamellophone (*sanza* or *likembe*), a small hand-held instrument with tuned iron tongues over a wooden or gourd resonator, is closely associated with Kongo migrant labour and, in the twentieth century, became one of the source instruments of Congolese urban popular music. Drums include the tall single-headed *ngoma*, the double-headed *mpungi*, the friction drum *puita* used at funerals and initiations, and the slit-log *nkonko*, still employed for coded long-distance messaging in the lower Congo forests. Wind instruments include the side-blown antelope-horn trumpet (*mpungi a mpakasa*) sounded at chiefly appearances, and end-blown flutes of raphia stalk. The song repertoire distinguishes between *makinu* — social dance-songs — and *nkunga* — ritual songs performed at initiations, funerals and *nkisi* consecrations, often in an archaic register understood only by specialists. From the syncretic Christian-Kongo tradition emerged the twentieth-century Kimbanguist church choirs, whose brass-band-accompanied *bafiote* hymnody in Kikongo remains a defining sound of Kongo Central. In urban Kinshasa and Brazzaville the Kongo substrate underlies the *rumba congolaise* of Wendo Kolosoy and later Franco, whose guitar interlocking (*sebene*) is traceable to *likembe* patterns.

## Dance & theatre

Dance among the Kongo is inseparable from ritual and from the display of chiefly and initiate status. The most codified forms belong to the men's *kimpasi* and the women's *kumbi* initiation enclosures, in which novices emerge after seclusion in a public procession of masked and painted figures whose choreography — a low, driving hip-drop over bent knees, arms held wide — recapitulates the crossing of the *kalunga* line between the world of the living and the *mpemba* world of the ancestors. Masked performance is regionally uneven: the coastal Yombe and Vili developed a substantial masking corpus, including the polychrome helmet mask of the *ndunga* police society among the Woyo — striped in red, black and white and worn with a full raphia costume to enforce chiefly edicts — while inland Kongo groups relied more on figural *minkisi* than on wearable masks. Funerary dances (*matanga*) may extend over several nights and include the mimed re-enactment of the deceased's occupations. In the Christian syncretic tradition, the annual Kimbanguist commemorations at Nkamba (*Nkamba-Jérusalem*) include processional choreographies that fuse hymn-singing with older Kongo circle-dance forms.

## Festivals & rituals

The Kongo ritual calendar is anchored less in a solar-agricultural cycle than in life-cycle events and in periodic renewal of chiefly and *nkisi* authority. The investiture of a chief (*mfumu*) — historically the *Manikongo* himself, today the customary *nkuluntu* of a village or clan — is the most elaborate public ritual, involving seclusion, the consumption of leopard and python meat, the receipt of the *mpu* cap and *mvwala* staff, and a formal circumambulation of the ancestor shrine. Funerals of notables extend over weeks and culminate in the erection of a *ntadi* on the grave or, in modern practice, of a cement or tile-work grave-house. Twin births (*bansimba*) trigger their own cycle of ceremonies. On the Catholic side, the feast of Saint Anthony (*Toni Malau*) — patron of the seventeenth-century Antonian movement of the prophetess Beatriz Kimpa Vita — remains observed, and the pilgrimage to Nkamba on 6 April and 25 May marks the birth and death of Simon Kimbangu for the millions of Kongo affiliated with the Église Kimbanguiste, an independent African Christian church inscribed by UNESCO in various intangible-heritage inventories at national level.

## Foodways

The Kongo diet is built on cassava (*madioko*), introduced from Brazil in the sixteenth century and now the dominant staple, prepared as fermented paste steamed in leaves (*chikwangue* or *kwanga*), as boiled sticks (*mbala*), or as coarse flour (*fufu*) mixed with hot water into a stiff porridge. Cassava leaves (*saka-saka* or *mpondu*) are pounded and slow-cooked with palm oil, onion and dried fish to produce the signature national relish. Plantain (*makemba*), yam, taro and maize round out the starch complex; the protein side draws on freshwater fish from the Congo and its tributaries (smoked or dried), bushmeat where still available, chicken, and the goat slaughtered for guests. Palm oil (*mafuta ma mba*) is the primary cooking fat and colours most stews a deep red. Palm wine (*malafu ma mbila*), tapped from the raphia or oil palm and drunk fresh within a day of fermentation, is the ritual and social beverage; a distilled version, *lotoko*, appears at funerals. Peanuts (*nguba*) are ground into a sauce (*muamba wa nguba*) that thickens chicken or fish stews, and *pili-pili* chilli is the near-universal condiment. Communal eating is done by hand from a shared bowl, with women and men in many rural communities eating separately.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kikongo oral literature is unusually well documented for a sub-Saharan tradition, thanks to the early literacy that followed the 1491 conversion — the *Kongo Catechism* of 1624 was among the first books published in a Bantu language — and to the massive twentieth-century collections of the Swedish missionary Karl Laman. The corpus distinguishes *kingana* (proverbs and riddles), *nsamu* (historical narrative), *lusamunu* (praise recitation), and *nkunga* (song-poetry). Proverbs are dense with cosmological reference and are formally deployed in judicial palaver (*mambu*), where a chief's authority is measured by his command of them; the Woyo *taampha* pot-lids are a material extension of this genre. Historical narrative preserves the founding myth of the kingdom — the ancestor Ntinu Wene crossing the Congo to found Mbanza Kongo — and the epic of the seventeenth-century wars culminating at the battle of Mbwila in 1665. The prophetic tradition, from Beatriz Kimpa Vita in 1704 to Simon Kimbangu in 1921, has generated its own literature of visions, hymns and testimonies, much of it now printed in Kikongo. Writers in the modern DRC, notably the poet Antoine-Roger Bolamba and, in French, the novelist Sony Labou Tansi (of Congo-Brazzaville), draw on the same substrate.

## Language & religion

*Kikongo* is a Bantu H10 language with several major dialect clusters — Kikongo-Kikwango, Kimanianga, Kiyombe, Kivili, Kiwoyo, Kisolongo — mutually intelligible with effort. The vehicular *Kituba* (*Kikongo ya leta*), grammatically simplified and lexically enriched from French and Lingala, is spoken by well over five million as a first or second language across Kongo Central, Kwango and the Republic of Congo. Religiously the Kongo present one of Africa's densest palimpsests: an indigenous cosmology structured by the *dikenga* cosmogram, the *kalunga* water-line separating the living from the *bakulu* ancestors, and the mediating role of the *nganga* and his *minkisi*; overlaid by Roman Catholicism since 1491 and by Protestant missions (Baptist, Swedish Evangelical) from the late nineteenth century; and cross-cut by independent African prophetic churches, above all the Église Kimbanguiste founded by Simon Kimbangu in 1921 and now claiming several million adherents worldwide. Contemporary practice moves fluidly between these registers: a Kongo Catholic may still consult an *nganga* at illness, receive baptism at Nkamba, and be buried under a cement grave-house facing east in the direction of the rising sun and the ancestral *mpemba*.
