## Overview

The Susu, also called Soussou, are a Mande-speaking people living primarily in Guinea and northwestern Sierra Leone, especially Kambia District, with smaller communities in Guinea-Bissau and Senegal. In Guinea they are associated with the coastal plains, Conakry, Kindia, Dubréka, and the Fouta Djallon highlands and plateau. Their language, Susu or Sosoxui, belongs to the Mande family and is closely related to Yalunka; it has also served as a trade language along the Guinean coast. Susu society is patrilineal and predominantly Muslim, with extended-family networks, caste distinctions, farming, fishing, salt production, blacksmithing, and trade shaping social life. Music, dance, and oral storytelling remain important means of preserving histories, social values, and religious practices.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources used mention traditional clothing and bazin in connection with the celebration following Ramadan. Women are described as wearing or using clothing within a social life organized around extended families, while the sources identify no named ceremonial dress beyond the reference to traditional clothing and bazin after Ramadan.

### Architecture

Ancient Susu houses were typically made from mud or cement blocks, depending on the resources available. They do describe extended families living close together, with women often having a room or hut next to their husband’s lodging, where they stayed with their children.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do identify blacksmithing as an old Susu association and describe smiths, carpenters, jewelers, and leatherworkers as artisan castes. Rice fields, fishing, gardening, palm nuts, trade, and salt production are documented livelihoods.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention jewelers as a Susu artisan caste. They likewise provide no named personal ornaments or documented functions for jewelry.

## Music & performance

Traditional music remains central to Susu cultural life and is described as preserving histories, social values, and religious practices. Musicians and bards, called *Yeliba*, belong to artisan castes within Susu social organization. The sources also identify musicians as a distinct caste alongside smiths, carpenters, jewelers, and leatherworkers.

The sources name several Susu musicians: Mohamed Bangoura, Momo Wandel Soumah, and Maciré Sylla. Mohamed Bangoura is described as a Guinean drummer, while Momo Wandel Soumah and Maciré Sylla are described as Guinean musicians. They state that music, dance, and oral storytelling remain part of cultural life and that Susu musicians and bards participate in the transmission of historical and social knowledge.

## Dance & theatre

Traditional dance is identified as an important part of Susu cultural life, alongside music and oral storytelling. They therefore do not distinguish ceremonial dance from entertainment or provide a documented account of dance costumes or instruments.

## Festivals & rituals

Susu religious life is predominantly Muslim, and over 99% of Susu are described as Muslim in the supplied source. Most Islamic holidays are observed. The most important celebration mentioned is the celebration that follows Ramadan, a month of prayer and fasting. After Ramadan, Eid is celebrated, and people wear traditional clothing and bazin.

Marriage is typically arranged within extended-family networks, and polygyny is practiced in accordance with Islamic traditions. Islamic law is described as allowing men to have as many as four wives, although the sources note that this is not always practiced because maintaining multiple households requires greater means. The sources also describe the Bondo secret society, which gradually establishes attitudes related to adulthood in girls and discusses fertility, morality, and proper sexual comportment. The society is said to maintain an interest in the well-being of its members throughout their lives.

## Foodways

Rice and millet are the two principal Susu crops. The sources also mention mangoes, pineapples, and coconuts, as well as vegetables grown in women’s gardens. Men provide for their families through work in rice fields, fishing, or trade, while women cook food, care for children, and often engage in small commerce involving garden vegetables.

Palm nuts are used by women to make various kinds of palm oil. Fishing and salt production are also identified among traditional Susu activities. They describe Susu society as predominantly Muslim.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral storytelling remains central to Susu cultural life and is described as preserving histories, social values, and religious practices.

Portuguese explorers and chroniclers of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries supplied some of the earliest written records concerning the Soso and Yalunka peoples. The sources refer to writings by Valentim Fernandes and Duarte Pacheco Pereira from 1505–1508, which describe a kingdom called “Jalo Concho,” regional trade, caste organization, animism, and conflicts with neighboring groups. Earlier Arabic chronicles are also said to mention the Sosso Empire. In the Susu language, the first literature identified in the sources is a translation of the first seven chapters of the Gospel of Matthew, published in London as “Lingjili Matthew” in 1816 by John Godfrey Wilhelm of the Church Mission Society.

## Language & religion

Susu, also called Sosoxui, Sosokhoui, or Soussou, is a Mande language spoken mainly in the coastal region of Guinea and in Sierra Leone. Its closest relative is Yalunka, and it belongs to the Soso-Yalunka branch of the Mande languages within the Niger-Congo language family. Susu has served as a trade language in coastal Guinea and Sierra Leone, especially around Conakry. It has been influenced by Mandinka, Soninke, and Kpelle.

Susu has been written in the Ajami variant of the Arabic script, Latin-script orthographies, N’ko, and Adlam. The sources also describe Koré Sèbèli or Wakara, an alphabetic script developed by Mohamed Bentoura Bangoura from traditional symbols used by secret societies and adopted by a small community of users since 2009.

Susu society is predominantly Muslim. The sources also describe an earlier period when Susu people were traditionally animist, followed by conversion to Islam during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. The Bondo secret society is associated with adulthood, fertility, morality, and sexual comportment.
