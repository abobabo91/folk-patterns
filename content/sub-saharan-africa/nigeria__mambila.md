## Overview

The Mambila live on a high, river-cut plateau in northeastern Nigeria and adjacent Cameroon, speaking related dialects of Mambiloid. They are known above all for a unique sculptural tradition in raffia pith and soft wood tied to the Suu masquerade-and-oath complex, which functions simultaneously as art, law, and religion. The plateau's isolation by rivers has kept villages and dialects distinct, and ritual secrecy—particularly the exclusion of women from seeing Suu masks—has limited outside documentation. Today they are Christian and Muslim, with ancestral practice persisting through the Suu complex.

## Material culture

Mambila material culture centers on soft-wood and raffia pith sculpture painted in red, white, and black, rather than on loom weaving or dense metalwork.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The signature pattern language is not woven cloth but the annual red-white-black repainting of ritual figures and masks.

- **Raffia fibre mask costume** — Full-body dress worn beneath Suu helmet masks, built from shredded raffia palm frond knotted onto a netted yoke, shaggier and less finely plaited than southern grassfields versions.
- **Bark-fibre cloth** — Beaten bark cloth from fig species, worked with grooved mallets and used for wrappers and ritual coverings before cotton trade cloth arrived.
- **Red-white-black painted scheme** — Annual repainting of tadep figures, kike pith figures, and Suu masks in camwood/ochre red, kaolin white, and charcoal black, applied in banded zones as an act of reactivation.
- **Cowrie applique** — Cowrie shells stitched in rows and rosettes onto hide, netting, and costume, marking wealth and ritual office while also serving as currency.
- **Tikar-style beadwork** — Netted glass-bead cladding over carved wood and calabash, reaching the Mambila through lowland trade but used sparingly on prestige objects only.

### Clothing & dress

Daily dress is now generic northern Nigerian style, but ceremonial dress remains distinctive: the masquerader is entirely enclosed in a raffia costume and helmet mask.

- **Suuburr helmet mask** (*Sua Burr*) — A first-rank mask that rests on the shoulders and completely encloses the head, with the wearer invisible beneath a full raffia costume.
- **Suu Dua mask** — A second-rank mask that sits on top of the head rather than over it, appearing in numbers around a single first-rank mask.

### Architecture

Villages sit on hilltops and in valleys separated by steep terrain, with circular compounds as the basic unit and the granary as a shrine structure.

- **Cane hut** — Circular dwelling of wattle-and-daub or cane construction arranged around a swept yard, often enclosed, with conical thatch roof of grass.
- **Bamboo-mat wall panelling** — Split cane or bamboo woven into panels that screen sleeping areas and enclose verandas within compounds.
- **Compound granary** — A raised, capped storage hut that housed kike pith figures out of general sight while serving as both barn and shrine, with access restricted by sex.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery, calabash, and soft-wood carving form the core, with deliberately faceted surfaces rather than polished finish.

- **Sorghum-beer pottery** — Coil-built, open-fired jars for brewing and serving grain beer, with burnished shoulders and roughened bodies for grip and heat.
- **Chevron-incised calabash** — Decorated gourds with zig-zag bands cut and darkened with soot, used as universal containers.
- **Iron hoe currency** — Hoe-blade-shaped iron bars circulating as bridewealth and store of value alongside cowries.
- **Mambila brass anklet** — A heavy cast ring worn in pairs, one of the documented metal forms.
- **Mambila stool** — A low single-block seat carved from one piece of wood, belonging to the soft-wood carving tradition.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Brass and iron anklets, cowrie strings, and imported glass beadwork constitute the documented body ornament.

- **Brass and iron anklets and armlets** — Metal jewelry worn on the body as status markers.
- **Cowrie strings and body cowries** — Cowrie shells worn on the body that doubled as portable wealth and currency.

## Music & performance

Performance is organized around the Suu association, which combines masquerade with oath-taking, and the sound of the masquerade itself is restricted to initiated men.

- **Suu masquerade** (*Sua, Shua, Shuaga*) — An association that conjoins masquerade with oath-taking, functioning as art, law, and religion simultaneously.
- **Mbà Sùu festival** — The occasion when Suu masks appear in performance, with the masquerader's voice and sight withheld from women.
- **Inter-village performance circuit** — Men travel between villages to perform, making the circuit itself as important as the music.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is embedded in the festival circuit and serves to maintain inter-village relations across a landscape where rivers isolate settlements.

- **Suu masquerade theatrical form** — A first-rank helmet mask attended by several second-rank masks, moving in full raffia concealment, its appearance restricted to initiated men.
- **Dance, sport, and friendship-making visits** — Men travel village to village for festivities combining these three elements, the mechanism by which inter-village relations are maintained.

## Festivals & rituals

The Mambila year runs on a named festival calendar that structures agricultural and social time, with Suu oath-taking serving as an instrument of dispute settlement.

- **Bol festival** (*Lùm on the Ndòm Plain*) — November festival marking agricultural and social time.
- **Mbà Sùu festival** — April–June masquerade festival when Suu masks appear, the central ritual occasion.
- **Tadep figure** — A male healing sacra of associations concerned with illness, carved with a small abdominal opening that receives food offerings.
- **Kike figure** — A pith figure serving as embodiment for visiting ancestral spirits and stored in the compound granary shrine.
- **Suu oath-taking** — To swear on Suu is a legal act used for dispute settlement and truth-testing.

## Foodways

Agriculture on the plateau concentrates in valley bottoms, with highlands used for grazing, and sorghum beer anchors ritual hospitality.

- **Guinea corn and maize staples** — Sorghum and maize form the basis of the diet, pounded and cooked as stiff porridge eaten with leaf and groundnut sauces.
- **Sorghum beer** — The ritual drink brewed for festivals, work parties, and Suu association events, requiring specialized brewing pottery.
- **Irish potato and tea** — Colonial-era crops now grown on the cool, reliably wet plateau at places like Kakara and Nguroje.
- **Fulani dairy and wara** — Fresh milk and cheese-like wara from Fulani herds arrived in the mid-20th century, entering everyday diet from local production.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral tradition centers on migration and settlement history, particularly the descent from the Mambilla Plateau to the Ndòm Plain around A.D. 1700.

- **Migration and settlement narratives** — Accounts of descent from the Mambilla Plateau onto the Ndòm Plain and of the displacement of the pre-Tikar Twumwu population.
- **Toponymy** — Place names themselves carry historical record, with Ndòm transplanted from Nyö Heights and lowland Mbèrr named from the Gwalì area.
- **Li Fa New Testament** — The New Testament in Common Mambilla (Tungbo) dialect with language-study texts, the Nigerian literary standard.
- **Ju Ba New Testament** — A separate New Testament based on the Songkolong dialect serving Cameroonian speakers.

## Language & religion

Mambila is not one language but a cluster of dialects within Benue-Congo, with Common Mambilla as the Nigerian standard and Christianity and Islam layered over the Suu complex.

- **Common Mambilla (Tungbo) dialect** — The most widely understood variety in Nigeria and its literary standard, written in an orthography established by Mona Perrin.
- **Suu oath-taking and dispute settlement** — The Suu complex persists as the framework for oath-taking, healing associations, and ancestral practice.
- **Kike pith figure as spirit embodiment** — A key practice at the centre of Suu religion, embodying visiting ancestral spirits.

## Glossary

- *Suu* — Masquerade-and-oath complex combining art, law, and religion
- *Suuburr* — First-rank helmet mask that encloses the head completely
- *Suu Dua* — Second-rank mask worn on top of the head
- *Tadep* — Male healing figure with abdominal opening for food offerings
- *Kike* — Pith figure embodying visiting ancestral spirits, housed in granary
- *Norr* — The people (Mambila self-designation in Nigerian dialects)
- *Ba* — The people (Mambila self-designation in Cameroon)
- *Ndòm Plain* — Lowland area in Cameroon where Mambila settled from A.D. 1700
- *Mbà Sùu* — April–June masquerade festival when Suu masks appear
- *Twumwu* — Pre-Tikar population displaced by Mambila on Ndòm Plain
- *Bol* — November festival marking agricultural and social time
- *Wara* — Cheese-like product made from Fulani milk
- *Li Fa* — New Testament in Common Mambilla dialect
- *Ju Ba* — New Testament in Songkolong dialect for Cameroon
- *Tungbo* — Common Mambilla dialect, the Nigerian literary standard
- *Songkolong* — Mambila dialect serving as literary standard in Cameroon
- *Mambiloid* — Language cluster within Benue-Congo (Niger-Congo)
