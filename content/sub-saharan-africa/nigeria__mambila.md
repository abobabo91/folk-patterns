## Overview

The Mambila live on a high grassland plateau at Nigeria's south-eastern edge and into Cameroon. They are politically autonomous villages without a central kingdom, each with its own elders and masquerade society. They are known far beyond their numbers for distinctive carved wooden figures and masks with heart-shaped faces, and for a famous spider oracle used to settle disputes. Today the plateau is tea and coffee country, with maize, guinea corn, and cassava as staples.

## Material culture

Mambila material culture uses soft wood, raffia, bark, bamboo and cane for masquerade and dwelling, with imported brass, iron and cowries from long trade links.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Mambila apply textiles and ornament rather than weaving them, using beaten bark, raffia, cowries and painted geometric patterns.

- **Bark-fibre cloth** — Inner bark of fig trees soaked and beaten into soft sheets, historically used for wrappers, bedding and masquerade dress before cotton trade cloth took over.
- **Raffia fibre mask costume** — Shredded and knotted raffia hung from a netted armature so the dancer's body is entirely concealed and the fibre swings as the dancer moves.
- **Cowrie appliqué** — Cowrie shells stitched in rows and patterns onto caps, belts, bags and mask collars, signalling the wealth of the owner.
- **Tikar-style beadwork** — Glass seed beads couched onto cloth or carved wood in geometric patterns, used on stools, calabashes, headdresses and containers.
- **Painted geometric field** — Chevrons, triangles and concentric bands in red camwood, white kaolin and black soot, applied to masks, figures, shields and house posts.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress follows West African cotton wrappers and tailored gowns; what remains distinctively Mambila is ceremonial and masquerade dress.

- **Wrapper and skin** — Older men combined a waist wrapper of bark cloth or trade cotton with a cured goat or antelope skin slung over the shoulder, worn by elders for ritual occasions.
- **Kirdi dress** (*Kirdi*) — A style label for borderland fibre skirts, beaded aprons and ornament, derived from a Kanuri and Fulfulde word for non-Muslim peoples.
- **Masquerade dress** (*suaga*) — The mask is worn with a fibre cape, a netted body-suit, ankle rattles and a stick or whisk to completely conceal the human wearer.
- **Beaded cap and collar** — Prestige headgear and shoulder collars worked in cowries and glass beads, worn by title-holders and senior masquerade members.
- **Body pigment** — Camwood red rubbed on the skin and white kaolin drawn in lines on face and torso mark participants in initiation and mortuary rites.

### Architecture

The Mambila compound is a cluster of round rooms and stores around a swept courtyard, walled in woven cane and roofed with a steep cone of thatch.

- **Conical thatch roof** — A tall steeply pitched cone of grass thatch lashed to a radial bamboo frame, lifted clear of the wall so smoke escapes at the eaves.
- **Bamboo-mat wall panelling** — Split bamboo or raffia midribs plaited into stiff mats and set between posts as walling, sometimes doubled and packed with earth.
- **Cane hut** — A lighter round structure built entirely of cane and grass, used as a sleeping room, boys' house or field shelter and rebuilt routinely.
- **Compound granary** — A raised cylindrical basketry or mud-plastered store standing on stones or short posts, capped with a miniature thatch cone.
- **Stone-walled compound** — Dry-stone enclosure walls at older ridge-top and defensive sites, a legacy of the nineteenth-century raiding period.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery and smithing were village-level crafts carried by specialist lineages, dominated by objects tied to beer, grain and masquerade.

- **Sorghum-beer pottery** — Large globular jars with restricted necks, hand-built by coiling and open-fired, blackened in use and central to work parties, funerals and masquerade feasting.
- **Tadep figures** (*tadep*) — Small standing wooden figures, usually male-and-female pairs, with heart-shaped concave faces and tubular eyes, repainted in red, white and black, kept in compounds for protection and lineage wellbeing.
- **Mambila stool** — Low carved stool cut from a single block, either plain cylindrical form or supported by figures or animal struts, sometimes beaded, used as a seat of office.
- **Chevron incised calabash** — Gourd bowls and bottles pyro-engraved and incised with bands of chevrons and lozenges, darkened with soot and oil, used for beer and medicines.
- **Iron hoe currency** — Forged iron blades that circulated as a standard of value in bridewealth and fines alongside cowries.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ornament is predominantly brass, iron and shell rather than silver or gold, with weight and quantity carrying the message of status.

- **Mambila brass anklet** — Heavy cast or hammered brass rings worn in numbers at the ankle by women, both ornament and audible accompaniment to dance, and a store of household wealth.
- **Brass and iron armlets** — Plain or twisted bands worn on the upper arm by men of standing and by masquerade officers.
- **Cowrie strand** — Strings and belts of cowries worn at the waist and neck, their value historically both monetary and decorative.
- **Bead necklace** — Strands of imported glass beads obtained through trade, worn layered at the throat for marriage and initiation.
- **Scarification and dentition** — Raised cicatrisation on torso and face, and in some communities the filing or removal of incisors, marking adulthood in older generations and now largely lapsed.

## Music & performance

Mambila music is functional before it is concert music, built on percussion and voice rather than melody instruments.

- **Masquerade drumming** — Ensembles of hollowed single-headed drums of graded size, played with hands and sticks, providing interlocking rhythms that govern the masked dancers.
- **Slit gong** — A hollowed log idiophone struck to summon villagers, announce a death and signal the stages of a masquerade.
- **Horns and whistles** — Antelope-horn and gourd whistles used as voice disguisers and signalling instruments, the 'voice' of the masquerade heard before it is seen.
- **Ankle rattles** — Seed-pod and metal rattles bound at the ankle, making the dancer's step part of the musical ensemble.
- **Antiphonal song** — Lead-and-chorus singing at work parties, beer-drinks and mortuary rites, with texts that praise, mock and record village events.

## Dance & theatre

Performance is organised through masking associations whose membership, secrets and costumes are held at village level, so repertoires differ from settlement to settlement.

- **Suaga masquerade** (*suaga*) — The principal masquerade complex associated with harvest, purification and mortuary observance, in which masked figures in fibre costume dance in the open village ground.
- **Sua institution** (*sua*) — The ritual and initiatory institution behind the masking, structuring men's ritual knowledge, its grades and its secrecy.
- **Heart-shaped face mask** — The best-known Mambila form with a concave heart-shaped face, cylindrical projecting eyes, jutting toothed jaw and crested superstructure, painted in red, white and black.
- **Zoomorphic masks** — Buffalo, bush-cow and bird-headed forms danced alongside anthropomorphic masks, their animal references tied to bush, strength and unpredictability.
- **Gbanga and mbamaga dances** (*gbanga, mbamaga*) — Local masquerade and dance names recorded in particular communities, with varying glosses between villages and no single agreed translation.

## Festivals & rituals

The Mambila ritual calendar is agricultural and mortuary rather than fixed to a solar date, with major gatherings falling after harvest when grain and beer are plentiful.

- **Harvest masquerade** — The post-harvest cycle of masked performance, feasting and beer-drinking that closes the farming year and cleanses the village.
- **Second funeral** — The delayed commemorative rite held months or years after burial, at which masquerades appear, beer is brewed in quantity and the deceased is formally removed from the world of the living.
- **Initiation into sua** — Staged instruction of young men in the secrets of the masking association, with seclusion, ordeal and the revelation of the mask's construction.
- **Spider divination consultation** (*ŋgam dù*) — Routine recourse to the spider oracle before building, marriage, travel, planting and at illness or death, making divination a near-daily practice.
- **Christian and Muslim festivals** — Christmas, Easter and the two Eids are now the largest public gatherings in most plateau towns, frequently absorbing the food, dress and dance conventions of the older calendar.

## Foodways

The plateau's altitude and rainfall support a cooler, wetter agriculture built on grains and tubers grown in ridged fields cleared from grassland.

- **Maize and guinea corn porridge** — Maize and guinea corn (sorghum) are the staples, pounded and cooked into a stiff porridge eaten with soups and relishes of leafy greens, beans and okra.
- **Plateau vegetables and tubers** — Cassava, yam, cocoyam and Irish potato fill out the year; beans and potatoes grown on the plateau are traded down to the lowlands.
- **Honey** — Honey from forest and hive is a valued sweetener and medicine.
- **Sorghum beer** — Brewed by women from malted and fermented guinea corn in large earthenware jars, it is the currency of hospitality, payment for farm labour and the necessary accompaniment to funerals and masquerades.
- **Coffee and tea** — Introduced commercially in the twentieth century, these are now the plateau's cash crops and have entered local consumption.

## Oral tradition & literature

Mambila oral tradition is carried in migration narratives, genealogies, proverbs, riddles and masquerade song, with the spider oracle as the outstanding recorded institution.

- **Spider divination system** (*ŋgam dù*) — A burrowing spider housed in a pot is drawn out by bait to disturb marked leaf cards laid over its mouth; the diviner reads the arrangement to answer a question put in binary form.
- **Origin narratives** — Migration narratives tracing Mambila movement onto the plateau from the north-east and explaining the dispersal of villages.
- **Trickster and animal tales** — Evening stories of tricksters and animals told in the village.
- **Masquerade song** — Allusive and often satirical song texts performed at masquerades and second funerals, serving as a village's running commentary on its own affairs.
- **Written Mambila** — Bible translation and orthography work have produced written Mambila in several varieties, though the language remains primarily spoken.

## Language & religion

Mambila is a chain of closely related Mambiloid varieties within Bantoid, with the plateau now largely Christian alongside continuing ancestral and divinatory practice.

- **Mambiloid language cluster** — Mambila varieties are tonal and divided broadly between a Nigerian cluster around Gembu and Warwar and a Cameroonian cluster including Ju Ba, far enough apart that speakers from opposite ends may not understand each other easily.
- **Bantu homeland reference** — The position of Mambiloid languages close to the presumed Bantu homeland has made them a standing reference point in comparative historical linguistics.
- **Trade and school languages** — Hausa, Fulfulde and English function as languages of trade, herding and school in Nigeria, with French playing the last role in Cameroon.
- **Christian affiliation** — The plateau is now largely Christian, the result of sustained twentieth-century mission activity on the Nigerian side.
- **Continuing ancestral practice** — Ancestral attention, masking associations, ritual specialists and the spider oracle continue to operate, often alongside church or mosque membership rather than in opposition to it.

## Glossary

- *tadep* — Small wooden guardian figures with heart-shaped faces, kept in compounds for lineage protection and wellbeing
- *suaga* — The principal masquerade complex and the full-body fibre costume worn with masks
- *sua* — The ritual and initiatory masking association structuring men's ritual knowledge and secrecy
- *ŋgam dù* — The spider divination oracle used to settle disputes and answer questions before important undertakings
- *Kirdi* — A style label for borderland dress and ornament, derived from a word for non-Muslim peoples
- *gbanga* — A local masquerade and dance form recorded in particular Mambila communities
- *mbamaga* — A local masquerade and dance form recorded in particular Mambila communities
- *Mambiloid* — A language group within Bantoid, Benue-Congo, to which Mambila and related varieties belong
- *Mambilla Plateau* — The high grassland tableland above 1,500 metres on Nigeria's south-eastern edge where the Mambila live
- *guinea corn* — Sorghum, a staple grain used for porridge and beer on the plateau
- *bark cloth* — Cloth made by soaking and beating inner bark of fig and related trees
- *raffia* — Shredded palm fibre used for masquerade costume and textile work
- *cowrie* — Shell used as both currency and ornament, stitched onto cloth and worn as jewellery
- *camwood* — Red pigment used to colour bodies and mask surfaces
- *kaolin* — White clay used to draw lines on skin and paint masks
