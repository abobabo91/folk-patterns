## Overview

The Gedeo are an ethnic group in southern Ethiopia, associated administratively with the Gedeo Zone in the South Ethiopia Regional State. They live in the highland area southwest of Dila and east of Lake Abaya, with members also living in adjacent parts of the Oromia Region. According to the 2007 Ethiopian national census, the group had 986,977 members; 75.05% lived in the SNNPR and 24.84% in adjacent parts of the Oromia Region, while 6.24% lived in urban areas. The Gedeo speak Gedeo, a Highland East Cushitic language of the Afro-Asiatic family. Their documented folk-cultural significance centers on the *baalle*, a system of ranks and age classes, and on an agricultural economy based on cultivating *ensete*.

## Material culture

### Architecture

They mention a Qallu compound, or *galma*, in Wenago, but provide no architectural description of it.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention spears only in an account of the 1960 clash at Michille hill near Dilla, where Gedeo used traditional weapons against modern firearms.

## Music & performance

They do describe the *baalle* as a social and ceremonial institution. The Gedeo tradition is characterized by ranks and age classes similar to the Gadaa system of the Oromo people. Beckingham and Huntingford describe seven grades spanning a 10-year period of birth and creating a 70-year cycle. Before incorporation into the Ethiopian Empire, the Gedeo lived in a federation of three territories called *Sasserogo*, or “three Roga”: Sobbho, Ributa, and Rikuta. These territories shared one Aba Gada, and every eight years the office passed to a new office holder in the next age set at a ceremony also known as *baalle*. According to Gedeo tradition, leadership positions from Aba Gada down to the office of Hyiticha were assumed at the *baalle* ceremony.

## Dance & theatre

The *baalle* ceremony is described as an important institution through which leadership positions were assumed.

## Festivals & rituals

They describe the *baalle* as a ceremony connected with the transfer of leadership and the organization of age sets. The system comprised seven grades, with each grade associated with a 10-year period of birth and the full structure forming a 70-year cycle. Before the Gedeo conquest by the Ethiopian Empire in the 1890s, the three territories of *Sasserogo* shared one Aba Gada, whose office passed every eight years to a new office holder in the next age set during the *baalle* ceremony.

The sources also describe ritual and social roles associated with the seven Gedeo clans: Doobba’a, Darashsha, Gorggorshsha, Hanuma, Bakarro, Henbba’a, and Logoda. These clans were organized into two houses, *shoole baxxe* and *sase baxee*. Specific clans or sub-clans supplied people for leadership, ritual, traditional medicine, and other duties. The Aba Gada was chosen from the Logoda and Henbba’a clans.

## Foodways

The sources used identify *ensete* cultivation as the basis of the Gedeo agricultural economy. They compare this practice with that of the neighboring Sidama people, whose language is closely related to Gedeo.

Coffee is also documented as an important crop. After incorporation into the Ethiopian Empire, landlords emphasized coffee production because of its importance as a cash crop, benefiting the landlords rather than the Gedeo peasantry. Later changes in land measurement and control pushed ordinary Gedeo toward peripheral areas, where they cleared forested areas for mixed coffee and *ensete* cultivation.

## Oral tradition & literature

The origin of the Gedeo is described as not well known. One account, associated with Tadesse Kippie Kanshie, traces the Gedeo to an aboriginal tribe called Murgga-Gosallo, perhaps the earliest people to have lived in the area. Another Gedeo tradition traces their origin to Daraso, described as the older brother of Gujo, ancestor of the Guji Oromo, and Boro, ancestor of the Borana Oromo. Daraso is said to have had seven sons from two wives, from whom descended the seven Gedeo clans: Doobba’a, Darashsha, Gorggorshsha, Hanuma, Bakarro, Henbba’a, and Logoda.

The sources state that these traditions may be connected with an Oromo practice of mass adoption of indigenous ethnic groups known as *guddifacha*. They also mention oral traditions concerning the Gedeo acquisition of the *baalle* practice from the Guji Oromo.

## Language & religion

Gedeo is a Highland East Cushitic language of the Afro-Asiatic family. Alternate names given for the language are Derasa, Deresa, Darassa, Geddeo, Derasanya, and Darasa. It is spoken by the Gedeo people in the highland area southwest of Dila and east of Lake Abaya. The language has subject–object–verb word order. Its verbs are marked for person, number, and gender of the subject, and for active, causative, middle, and passive voice. The New Testament was published in Gedeo in 1986 using the Geʽez script.

The sources describe Mageno as the Supreme Being of the Gedeo. Protestant missionaries arrived in the early 1950s and established the Ethiopian Kalehiywot Church and Ethiopian Evangelical Church Mekaneyesus. The Ethiopian Kalehiywot Church attracted the bulk of the Gedeo population and exerted a far-reaching influence. Missionaries established Bible and elementary schools, including evening classes for adults.
