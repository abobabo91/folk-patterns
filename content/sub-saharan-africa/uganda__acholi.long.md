## Overview

The Acholi (also spelled Acoli) are a Nilotic people of the Luo (Lwo) group living in northern Uganda and in Magwi County, Eastern Equatoria, in South Sudan. Their home region, Acholiland or the Acholi sub-region, covers roughly 28,500 km² near the Uganda–South Sudan border and comprises the districts of Agago, Amuru, Gulu, Kitgum, Lamwo, Nwoya, Pader and Omoro. Acholi in Uganda were estimated at 1.9 million in 2024, with over 45,000 more in South Sudan in 2000; the sub-region's population is put at about 2,155,000, some six percent of Uganda's total. Locally the people call themselves Lwo or Luo Gang — "people of the home" in the usage of their Lango neighbours — and the name "Acholi" derives from *Shooli*, a term applied to them by Arabic-speaking traders from the north in the second half of the nineteenth century. In folk-culture terms they matter as a hunting, herding and millet-farming people whose smithing, personal ornament, harp and drum music, and elaborate reconciliation rituals are all documented in collections and in the literature.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here describe no woven or dyed textile tradition. What they do document is a body of fibre, bead and hide garment work held in the British Museum:

**String trains** — Women's *trains* (garments) made of twisted vegetable-fibre string; one example combines the string with cane, gum and glass beads.

**Hide aprons and belts** — Aprons of hide worked with twine and brass wire. A woman's belt is a flap of reddish leather, one end served with three iron-wire tags, the other hung with leather strings attached by iron wire, its outer edges ornamented with iron rings.

**Human-hair head-dresses** — Head-dresses built over a felt or basketry foundation and surfaced with human hair: one is decorated with discs of red and white beads and red feather tufts, another is spherical, ornamented with a brass plate and with rings sewn to the exterior.

**Painted house walls** — Not textile but the collection's clearest surface-pattern tradition: women daubed hut walls with mud and decorated them with geometrical or conventional designs in red, white or grey.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no individual motifs, only the geometrical and "conventional" wall designs and their red, white and grey palette.

### Clothing & dress

The catalogued garments are chiefly women's and chiefly of hide, fibre and bead: string trains, hide aprons stitched with twine and brass wire, and leather belts finished with iron rings and wire-bound tags. Headwear includes plain conical caps and the two human-hair head-dresses described above, whose beadwork, brass plate and feather tufts mark them out as display pieces rather than daily wear. A forehead ornament of leather set with cowrie shells belongs to the same register. One object is explicitly ritual dress: a rain-maker's belt of leather and iron, showing that specialist religious office had its own regalia. For men the sources record dress only indirectly, through equipment — the wrist-knife, a strip of iron worn on the arm with its edges protected by a narrow strip of hide and, in a second example, kept in a leather sheath. Footwear is not covered by the sources used here.

### Architecture

Acholi settlements were organised hamlets of circular huts with high peaked roofs. The interior fittings are specified: a mud sleeping-platform, jars of grain, and a sunken fireplace. Wall finishing and decoration were women's work — they daubed the walls with mud and then painted geometrical or conventional designs on them in red, white or grey, which makes the house exterior the principal patterned surface in the documented material culture. The social frame around these hamlets was the chiefdom: from the late seventeenth century the Luo of northern Uganda formed chiefdoms headed by *rwodi* (singular *rwot*, "ruler"), each chiefdom containing several villages made up of different patrilineal clans, and by the mid-nineteenth century about sixty small chiefdoms existed in eastern Acholiland. The sources used here do not describe granaries, shrines, fencing or any other named building type, nor any urban building tradition.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Iron dominates the catalogued object set. There are axes with iron heads on wooden handles, an iron-bladed sword mounted with wood and snake skin, a wooden club whose rounded end is strengthened with strips of iron, iron razors, and the wrist-knives described above. Defensive gear is hide: shields of hide, and in war men carried spears and long, narrow shields of giraffe or ox hide, while hunting used nets and spears. Woodwork covers the household: carved wooden head-rests or pillows, one fitted with leather thongs, and a wooden burden-carrier. Basketry and plant fibre appear in a double-thickness winnowing fan of twigs set at right angles and joined with bast strips in a wrapped twine weave. No pottery is present among the objects shown, and the sources do not describe Acholi ceramics.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The ornament holdings are the most varied part of the collection. Necklaces are made of giraffe hair strung with glass beads, and one composite piece assembles hide string, seeds, cowrie shells, glass beads, wood, iron wire, bone and horn, and comes with two bracelets, tweezers and a bell — a single wearable kit combining adornment with grooming and sound. A neck ornament is built from ostrich-eggshell beads on hide and fibre string with iron wire and cowrie shells. Arm and wrist pieces include a circular armlet cut from a section of ivory tusk, bracelets of iron wire, and a wristlet of iron with leather. There is also a collar of iron wire and wood, and the cowrie-set leather forehead ornament. Human hair is itself an adornment material, used as the surface of the two head-dresses. Tattooing, scarification and hair-dressing practice are not covered by the sources used here.

## Music & performance

The instruments held for the Acholi cover string, percussion and wind. The string instruments are a **bow harp** — an arched wooden neck with six peg-holes and five surviving pegs, a roughly triangular sound-box of leather stretched over a wooden frame with two circular sound-holes cut into the upper and lower corners, and five strings (one dyed red) anchored to a rod fixed beneath the sound-box surface — and two **trough zithers**, one of wood and cane with hide strings, the other of wood and cane alone. Percussion comes as combined drum-rattles: one of wood with hippopotamus hide and stones, another of wood with cow hide, stones and what appears to be cotton string. A separate body rattle is made of four sticks each threaded with five seed pods, the stick ends bound and secured with bark fibre and twisted fibre cords knotted at the corners so the frame can be tied to the player's body; some pods are pierced and all still hold their seeds. Wind instruments are a cane flute with four finger-holes and its end bound with vegetable fibre, an antelope-horn hunting whistle on a cord, and a war horn or trumpet of wood and hide. Uganda has no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions, and the sources used here name no song genres or performance occasions.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used here document no Acholi dance or dramatic tradition. The body-tied seed-pod rattle, whose cords are knotted at each corner for attachment to the wearer, is the only object in the collection that implies dancing, but its catalogue record does not name an occasion or a dance.

## Festivals & rituals

Ritual, especially ritual of reconciliation, is the best-documented intangible domain. Before colonial rule the Acholi maintained a government rooted in religious belief and custom and administered by anointed chiefs, the *rwodi moo*, who were credited with supernatural powers but ruled through a Council of Clan Elders whose representatives mediated between clans and handled what would now be civil and criminal matters. Killing was prohibited; where it occurred, the victim's family led negotiations for blood money, and agreement was followed by a reconciliation ceremony that restored the killer to the community and re-established peace between the clans. A wider cycle of purification rites covers cleansing homes and sites, clearing spirits from places where killings have occurred, welcoming home people long absent, and receiving those who have been held captive. Two named practices carried into the recent past: *nyono tong gweno*, "stepping on the egg", performed for returnees to their home villages, which some 12,000 former Lord's Resistance Army returnees underwent; and *mato oput*, the indigenous accountability and justice process whose revival was urged during the 2005–2007 peace talks, and which Uganda's then minister of internal affairs Ruhakana Rugunda judged effective. Acholi elders still perform purification and atonement in some communities. The system is described as valuing peace over justice and as having both retributive and restorative aspects. The rain-maker's belt of leather and iron in the collection points to a further ritual office. The sources used here describe no annual festival calendar and no birth, initiation, marriage or funeral rites.

## Foodways

The sources used here cover crops rather than cooking. Women were accomplished agriculturists who grew and processed millet, *simsim*, groundnuts, peas, sorghum and vegetables; men kept goats, sheep and cattle and hunted with nets and spears. Grain was stored in jars inside the house, beside a sunken fireplace, and a double-thickness winnowing fan of twigs and bast survives among the objects shown. Tonally, the language distinguishes *bél* "corn" from *bèl* "wrinkled" and *kál* "millet" from *kàl* "place enclosed by a palisade". No dishes, preparations, ceremonial foods or drinking traditions are named in the sources used here.

## Oral tradition & literature

Acholi verbal art is documented both traditionally and in print. The sources record numerous trenchant proverbs, among them *"Ange tyene lit, koko ange"* — "Regret has sore legs, that's why it arrives late" — and *"Lit wic"*, "pain of the head", meaning stubbornness; Okot p'Bitek published a collection, *Acholi Proverbs* (Nairobi: Heinemann Kenya, 1985). The same author wrote *Song of Lawino* in Acholi, published in 1966 and later translated into English, one of the most successful works of African literature; its sequel *Song of Ocol* was written in English. Other Acholi writers named in the sources include the playwright, poet and educator Lubwa p'Chong, and Harriet Anena, author of the 2015 poetry collection *A Nation In Labor* and winner of the 2018 Wole Soyinka Prize for Literature in Africa. Documentation of the language itself runs from the missionary grammars of Crazzolara (1938) and Malandra (1955) to Janet Lakareber's nine-volume *Acoli Accented Orthography*, which argues that a monosyllabic Acholi word has fourteen distinct pronunciations. A Luo-language newspaper, *Rupiny*, serves the region.

## Language & religion

Acholi — *Leb Acoli* or *Leb Lwo* — is a Western Nilotic language of the Luo (Lwo) group, spoken across the eight districts of Acholiland and in Magwi County, South Sudan; the Dhopaluo (Chope) sub-dialect is spoken in Kiryandongo District in the kingdom of Bunyoro. It shares 84–90 per cent of its vocabulary with Alur and Jo Padhola and is mutually intelligible with them, the three being counted separately because their speakers are ethnically distinct. It has vowel harmony across two five-vowel sets distinguished by [±ATR], and is tonal, with high, low, downstep high and double downstep high tones plus rising and falling contours, tone also functioning in verb conjugation. Religiously, the 2002 Census of Uganda recorded 72.3% Roman Catholic, 23.6% Anglican, 1.7% Pentecostal and 0.8% Muslim; before colonialism all the Acholi believed in one superior being, *YA Latwer*. Catholic, Anglican, Muslim and later Orthodox leaders formalised the Acholi Religious Leaders' Peace Initiative in 1997.
