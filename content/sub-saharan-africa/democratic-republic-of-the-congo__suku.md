## Overview

The Suku are a matrilineal Bantu people living mainly in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Their culture includes hunting, cultivation, initiation, masks, divination, leadership objects, and carved figures. Mukhanda is a major initiation process for boys. Suku culture has similarities with Yaka culture.

## Material culture

Suku material culture uses wood, raffia, vines, animal skins, metal, charms, and medicines in masks, drums, swords, figures, and initiation structures.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The profile does not describe a distinctive Suku textile tradition, but raffia is important on masks and boys learn to weave mats during initiation.

- **Raffia fringe** (*raffia*) — Raffia forms full fringe on the kakungu, frames the face of the hemba, and covers the m-bawa as cloth.
- **M-bawa mask** (*m-bawa*) — The m-bawa is a vine sphere covered with raffia cloth, with two wooden or animal horns and white spots.
- **Hemba mask** (*hemba*) — The hemba is a wooden helmet mask with a human face, coiffure, triangular bands, and raffia fringe.
- **Kakungu mask** (*kakungu*) — The kakungu is made from mengela or m'tsenga wood, animal skins, and raffia fringe, with red and white as its main colors.
- **Mats** — During initiation, boys learn useful work such as weaving mats.

### Clothing & dress

The sources do not describe everyday clothing, but initiation includes nakedness, mats, and ceremonial masks.

- **Initiation dress** — Boys remain naked for several days and sleep close together on mats on the ground.
- **Hemba mask worn by initiates** (*hemba*) — Near the end of initiation, newly initiated boys wear the hemba mask.

### Architecture

The temporary mukhanda initiation camp is the only built setting described.

- **Initiation camp** (*mukhanda*) — The mukhanda has special structures, masks, and sculpted posts covered with charms and medicines that block the site.
- **Burned camp** (*mukhanda*) — After the boys leave, the mukhanda camp is burned.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The profile describes wooden, metal, and mixed-material objects, but no Suku pottery.

- **Diviner's slit drum** (*N’koku ngoombu*) — The N’koku ngoombu is a wooden slit drum with a carved human head on its handle, used by diviners and in preparing medicines.
- **Leadership sword** (*mbeedya phoko*) — The mbeedya phoko is a broad double-bladed sword with a pointed metal hilt and an unusual sheath, representing leadership and powers of life and death.
- **Carved figures** — The sources describe carved wooden statuettes and female wood figures.
- **Initiation objects** — Initiation objects use wood, vines, animal skins, raffia, charms, and medicines.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources do not describe jewelry or tattoos, but they record distinctive hats and headgear on leadership figures.

- **Bweni hat** (*Bweni*) — Bweni hats on leadership statuettes have a rising crest with knobs or a projecting brim.
- **Chief's skull cap** (*Mpu*) — Mpu are skull caps shown on regional chiefs in Suku.
- **Miter-like headgear** (*kambaandzia*) — The kambaandzia is a pointed, cone-like head form.

## Music & performance

Music appears mainly in initiation and divination, with singing, drums, and ceremonial objects.

- **Initiation mourning songs** — Initiates sing mourning songs about separation from their families and the possible chance of death.
- **Circumcision drums** — Drums begin the circumcision ceremony around 5 am.
- **Diviner's slit drum** (*N’koku ngoombu*) — The N’koku ngoombu is a distinct diviner's drum used while preparing medicines.
- **Lunda war-dance sword performance** (*mbeedya phoko*) — The mbeedya phoko is used ceremonially in Lunda war-dance, which recalls legendary battles and ends by pointing toward the land of origin.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is central to initiation, and masks appear at different stages of the ceremony.

- **Gift dances** — Before entering the camp, young candidates visit relatives and dance for traditional gifts.
- **Hemba mask dance** (*hemba*) — The hemba dance is taught to initiates and is performed when they return from camp.
- **Kakungu and m-bawa dances** (*kakungu; m-bawa*) — The kakungu and m-bawa appear during circumcision, departure from camp, and other initiation events.
- **Return celebration** (*mukhanda*) — When initiates return, the hemba masks dance as the camp burns, followed by dancing and celebration.

## Festivals & rituals

Mukhanda is the main ritual described: a long initiation through which Suku boys reach manhood.

- **Mukhanda initiation** (*mukhanda*) — When enough uncircumcised boys aged 10–15 are available, elders announce the initiation and send them to a special camp.
- **Mukaamba tree opening** (*mukaamba*) — Before circumcision, participants gather by a mukaamba tree and carry away the charm specialist's arrow if it remains there.
- **Circumcision rules** — After circumcision, boys follow rules about food, silence, bodily conduct, and obedience.
- **Healing and strengthening** — Boys clean their incisions on the third day and receive strengthening charms the next day.
- **Peace welcome** (*kaolin*) — When they return, the boys seek an elder and receive kaolin as a sign of peace and welcome.

## Foodways

Women grow several crops and gather wild foods, while initiation imposes many unspecified food restrictions.

- **Cultivated crops** — Women cultivate yams, beans, peas, pineapple, and peanuts.
- **Gathered foods** — Women collect and forage fruits, berries, and roots.
- **Initiation food restrictions** — Camp leaders impose many food restrictions during initiation, but the sources do not name the restricted foods.

## Oral tradition & literature

The profile names storytelling during initiation but gives no Suku tales, poetry, proverbs, or written literature.

- **Initiation storytelling** (*mukhanda*) — Storytelling is taught to boys during the period after circumcision at the mukhanda camp.
- **Legendary battle stories** (*mbeedya phoko*) — The Lunda war-dance recalls legendary battles, but the sources do not give the stories.

## Language & religion

Suku is a Bantu language, and Suku society is matrilineal, with belief in a creator god and practices of divination.

- **Suku language** — Suku is a Bantu language of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, traditionally placed in the Yaka branch of Bantu.
- **Creator god** (*ndzambyaphuungu*) — Ndzambyaphuungu is a creator god who lives in the sky and travels the breeze, explaining life, death, and unanswered questions.
- **Diviner** (*ngoombu*) — A ngoombu helps people who face misfortune, strange events, sickness, or death.
- **Shell money offering** (*nziimbu*) — A diviner requires nziimbu shell money, a coin, or cloth belonging to the victim to find the source of a problem.
- **Matrilineal society** — Suku society traces social descent through the mother's line.
