## Overview

The Antambahoaka are the least numerous ethnic group in Madagascar, numbering around 50,000 people in 2013. They inhabit a small region along the southeastern coast near Mananjary, their largest town, and share origins with the partially Arab Antaimoro people. Their community was established in the fifteenth century by Ravalarivo, who founded Masindrano, later incorporated into Mananjary. The Antambahoaka speak a Malagasy dialect belonging to the Malayo-Polynesian branch of the Barito language group. Their folk-cultural significance is especially associated with the **Sambatra**, a ritual group circumcision ceremony held every seven years, as well as ancestor veneration, ancestral taboos, distinctive community houses, traditional clothing, polyharmonic singing, and the use of the conch shell and large drum in festival music.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*tafitsihy* — Clothing was traditionally made from sedges called *harefo* or from beaten bark. The materials were woven into mats and stitched together.

*harefo* reed mats — Women traditionally wore reed mats stitched into a tube dress, knotted at one shoulder or belted at the waist.

*mahampy* — Mahampy reeds were woven into a bandeau top worn by adolescent and adult women beneath the dress.

Beaten bark cloth — Men wore a beaten bark loincloth beneath a woven sedge tunic or jacket.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no motifs or pattern vocabulary.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional clothing included *tafitsihy*, made from sedges or beaten bark woven into mats and stitched together. Women wore *harefo* reed mats formed into tube dresses, which were knotted at one shoulder or belted at the waist. They also wore bandeau tops made from woven *mahampy* reeds beneath their dresses. Men wore a beaten bark loincloth under a woven sedge tunic or jacket.

During special occasions, the *mpanjaka*, or kings, wore traditional Islamic-inspired dress consisting of a red fez and a loose garment with wide red and black stripes.

### Architecture

The homes of local *mpanjaka*, called *tranobe*, or “big houses,” serve both as community meeting spaces and as temples for ancestor worship. They are unusually large by Malagasy standards and have a rectangular form without a veranda. The buildings are raised on low stilts and have gabled roofs covered with *ravinala* leaves. Floors are typically made from *ravenala*, while the walls are constructed from braided *falafa* leaves.

The sources state that there are ten *tranobe* in Mananjary and twelve in the historic village of Ambohitsara. Most Antambahoaka villages have three or four.

## Music & performance

The *antsa* is a traditional polyharmonic singing style performed by women and is popular among the Antambahoaka. At major festivals, music is played on an *antsiva*, a conch shell drilled with finger holes. Music is typically accompanied by the *hazolahy*, a large, deep-toned drum.

The most prominent performance context described by the sources is the **Sambatra**, which takes place every seven years and lasts for an entire month. During the festival, the Antambahoaka commemorate their origins and Raminia’s circumcision of his son Ndohanina. War reenactments are performed between “armies” led by an Antambahoaka general for each clan. These groups consist of the boys’ fathers and maternal uncles dressed as soldiers. The reenactments can contain heightened violence connected with the settling of old scores.

The sources also describe two short films about the Sambatra. The first, *Sambatra*, is a 52-minute film directed by Ronan Leroy and Solo Randrasana and released in 2008. The second is a 24-minute film directed by Quitterie Bienvenu and released in 2010 under the title *Les Enfants de Raminia*.

## Festivals & rituals

The principal Antambahoaka festival is the **Sambatra**, a ritual group circumcision ceremony held every seven years. It takes place during the “Friday year” and culminates in the circumcision of all boys born during the previous seven years. The rite marks their transition to adult male status and to membership as *Zafiraminia*, or son of Raminia. The boys are then integrated into their paternal clan and initiated into the life of the true Antambahoaka man.

The festival lasts for an entire month. It commemorates the Antambahoaka origins and Raminia’s circumcision of his son Ndohanina, who passed the practice down to his people. One Friday includes a long march by military units toward the mouth of the Mananjary river, recalling Raminia’s exodus.

An annual festival called *hosin'ny mpanjaka* brings villagers together to prepare the local king’s paddy field for planting rice. Twins are forbidden from participating in the Sambatra and cannot join this annual ritual.

The sources describe grandparents performing a child’s first haircut. They also state that Antambahoaka society has a strong taboo against raising twins, called the *fady kamamba*, particularly around Mananjary. Historically, twins were strangled immediately after birth by the village sorcerer or abandoned in the wilderness to die of exposure. Those who raise twins can be ostracised and forced to leave their home village. Twins are excluded from the family tomb and are not considered familial ancestors after death.

The dead are buried in a *kibory*, a house in the forest far from the village.

## Foodways

The principal economic activities of the Antambahoaka are oceanic and freshwater fishing, subsistence farming, and, for some, the cultivation of cash crops such as coffee. The sources mention rice cultivation in connection with the annual preparation of the king’s paddy field. They state that village chiefs may not eat pork and that animals must be fully bled when butchered for eating. During some Sambatra ceremonies, grandparents eat the foreskin of their newly circumcised grandchild.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe oral tradition concerning Raminia, who is believed by the Antaimoro and Antambahoaka to have been an Arab who migrated from Mecca to Madagascar between the tenth and twelfth centuries. Two explanations are given for his emigration: that he left because of an increasingly rigid application of Islamic law, or that he was searching for fertile land to cultivate.

The Antambahoaka split from the Antaimoro in the fifteenth century when Ravalarivo established Masindrano. Among some Antambahoaka, Ravalarivo is said to have become known as Ratiambahoaka, meaning “beloved by his people,” and the name Antambahoaka is said to derive from a deformation of that title.

## Language & religion

The Antambahoaka speak Antambahoaka, a Malagasy dialect close to Southern Betsimisaraka and Southeastern dialects. Malagasy is described as a branch of the Malayo-Polynesian language group derived from the Barito languages spoken in southern Borneo. The local dialect was originally written using *sorabe*, an adaptation of the Arabic alphabet for transcribing Malagasy. Today, only a small number of specially educated Antambahoaka have learned to read and write in the *sorabe* script. A historical “secret language” blending Arabic, Malagasy, and Creole words was used among *ombiasy* to communicate confidential knowledge and rites.

Antambahoaka society is strongly patrilineal and patriarchal. Ancestors are venerated through rituals and rites, especially the Sambatra. The Antambahoaka believe that a deceased community member becomes an ancestor who can watch over living descendants and intervene for good or ill according to the respect shown and the rites and *fady* observed. Catholicism was introduced by French missionaries during the French colonial period, from 1896 to 1960, but had limited effect. Certain Islamic practices have also been incorporated into local culture.
