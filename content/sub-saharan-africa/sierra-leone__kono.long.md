## Overview

The Kono (pronounced *koh noh*) are a Mande-speaking people of eastern Sierra Leone, making up 5.2% of the country's population. Their homeland is Kono District, the diamond-bearing east of the country, and most Kono work as diamond miners and farmers. They speak Kono as a first language, and many younger Kono use Krio when dealing with other Sierra Leonean groups. Unlike most of the country's peoples the Kono rarely settle outside the east, so few are found in Freetown or in the north. Kono tradition holds that they descend from Mali-Guinean migrants who reached the area in the mid-sixteenth century as peaceful hunters, although archaeology puts settlement in Kono District as far back as 2200 B.C. Colonial partition split the group, and part of it remains in neighbouring Guinea.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Body marking appears in the collection only as sculpture. The **Sowei helmet mask** held by the British Museum carries three parallel scarification marks on each cheek and three short incised lines radiating from the outer corner of each eye, and its carved figure wears three neck rings, two of which continue around to the front. The mask's coiffure is dressed in three lobes framed by carved panels worked to imitate basketry, with a semi-circular projection at the back of the head bearing a flower at its centre, two large horns curving around the sides, and a rectangular amulet with four projecting "legs" set on top. Whether these represent scarification, neck ornament and hairdressing as practised by living Kono women, or a sculptural convention, is not something the sources used here settle.

## Festivals & rituals

The one institution the sources document is the **Bondo** society, a women's association whose practices the Kono use to establish adult attitudes in girls gradually but firmly, with instruction covering fertility, morality and sexual comportment; the society keeps an interest in its members' welfare throughout their lives. Alongside it, Kono spiritual practice centres on the ancestors: they are invoked and prayed to for protection, health, guidance and good fortune, and are held to be present during every activity, from eating and sleeping to important events. Curses, omens, charms and magic are used in daily life by some Kono. The sources used here give no annual festival calendar, and describe no birth, marriage or funeral rites.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used here preserve no Kono folktale, epic, proverb or riddle. What they do carry is historical memory held as tradition: Kono history claims the people were once powerful in Mali and Guinea, that they migrated south as hunters, and that attacks by the neighbouring Mende drove them to take refuge in Koranko territory to the north, where they were permitted to farm, until the Mende moved further south and the Kono returned east to their own land. The articles also point to *Alatangana* as related material without describing it.

## Language & religion

Kono belongs to the Mande group and is the most widely spoken language among the Kono people; Krio serves many younger speakers as the language of contact with other Sierra Leoneans. The name "Kono language" is ambiguous across West Africa: the Sierra Leonean language (ISO *kno*) is the one spoken here, distinct from Kono of Guinea (*knu*), a variety of Kpelle, and from Kono of Nigeria (*klk*), a Kainji language. Most Kono are Muslim or Christian, and some also practise traditional religion, with the ancestral invocation and protective charms described above coexisting with both.
