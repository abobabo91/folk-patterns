## Overview

The Edo — also called the Benin people or Bini — are an Edoid-speaking people of southern Nigeria, concentrated in the seven local government areas of the Edo South senatorial district and centred on Benin City, the walled capital that gives the group its exonym. They number in the low millions and speak Edo (Bini), a Volta-Niger language of the Edoid branch; neighbouring Esan, Etsakọ, Owan, Isoko and Urhobo all trace their descent from the same Benin polity. The Edo are the descendants of the founders of the Benin Kingdom, first under the *Ogiso* dynasty and then under the *Oba*. For folk-culture research they are exceptional because their court arts — the Benin Bronzes, ivory pectoral masks, coral regalia — were produced by hereditary palace guilds under state supervision, so the material record is unusually dense, datable, and internally consistent from the fifteenth century onward.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Ododo cloth** — the deep scarlet flannel-weight cloth that is the Edo court colour, wrapped as a skirt or draped over the shoulder by chiefs and by the *Oba* at Igue. Red is read as apotropaic and as the colour of danger and of authority; *ododo* is worn where a Yoruba chief would wear indigo-blue *adire*, and the chromatic contrast is one of the clearest visual borders in southern Nigeria.

**Ukpon-ivie** — literally "cloth of coral": a cloth-and-bead composite in which coral beadwork is stitched in dense rows onto a textile ground, producing a garment whose surface is effectively lapidary rather than woven. The technique is the basis of nearly all Edo beaded regalia.

**Ewu-ivie** — the beaded cape or blouse worn by titled women and by brides, built as a net of coral and, latterly, glass beads across the shoulders and chest. The netted-bead construction reappears at smaller scale in the beaded cap, the *ekpa-ivie* handbag, and the *ivie-obo* hand ornament.

**Ebe-ame** — patterned wrapper cloth associated with water and with Olokun devotion; white and blue-white cloths, together with imported white "river cloth," are the standard offering-dress at Olokun shrines, where the god's wealth is figured as sea-borne trade goods.

**Ivbiotu** — raffia and plant-fibre work, the everyday counterpart to imported cloth: plaited mats, fibre skirts, and the raffia elements still required in certain shrine and masking costume even where flannel or velvet is available.

**George, velvet and lace wrappers** — the modern ceremonial trio for Edo women, adopted through Atlantic and Indian Ocean trade but now fully conventionalised: the heavy George wrapper pleated at the waist, velvet for chiefly occasions, lace for the second wrapper or blouse.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Ebo ame* (the guilloche or interlace "water" band, a running double plait read as river current); *ododo* red field; coral-bead lattice (*ivie* netting); river-leaf and mudfish forms, the mudfish standing for the *Oba*'s amphibious dual nature; frog and crocodile forms tied to Olokun; the quatrefoil "river-leaf" rosette used as a ground-filler on plaques and ivories; the *ukhurhe* ring-and-segment banding derived from rattle-staff turning; rope or *eben*-edge borders framing figural panels; and the cast-metal rosette punch used to fill the background of Benin plaques.

### Clothing & dress

Edo ceremonial dress is built around coral. Men wear a wrapper at the waist over a plain white shirt with a striped or multicoloured cloth over it, a beaded or fabric cap (the *headdress*, sometimes a mesh of coral beads), coral necklaces, anklets, and frequently a carved walking stick; black hats are a common modern alternative for grooms. Chiefly and royal dress escalates this into full *ukpon-ivie*: a coral-bead high collar or *odigba* closing the throat and chin, coral crown-cap, beaded cuffs, and *ododo* wrapper, with the *eben* ceremonial sword carried and tossed in salute. Women wear wrappers of velvet, lace or George, the *ewu-ivie* beaded cape or blouse, *ivie-uru* coral necklaces, *ivie-obo* on the hands, beaded earrings, and the coral *ekpa-ivie* handbag; the crested or wing-shaped coiffure of court women, familiar from the Queen Idia masks, survives as a ceremonial hairstyle and as a beaded cap form. Coral itself (*ivie*) is charged material: tradition ties its arrival to Oba Ewuare and to early Portuguese trade around 1485, and its use was regulated by the palace.

### Architecture

The Edo built in laterite mud. The classic Benin City house is a rectangular compound of ridged clay walls enclosing an *impluvium* courtyard — a sunken, hard-plastered rain basin open to the sky, which collected water and lit the surrounding rooms; roofs were pitched in palm-leaf thatch and later corrugated iron, pitched inward toward the basin. Walls were burnished to a glossy red-brown finish by hand, and the finest were horizontally fluted or grooved in relief, a treatment carried up onto the **iy'uzo pillar relief**: the interior verandah posts and wall piers of the palace and of chiefs' houses, modelled in clay or faced with cast bronze plaques. The palace of the *Oba* was a stacked series of such courtyards, its roof turrets crowned with cast-bronze birds and serpents; the Benin Bronze plaques that filled European museums were originally nailed to these pillars as a historical register of reigns and campaigns. Around the city ran the Benin walls and ditch system, an earthwork of banks and moats extending far into the surrounding countryside and enclosing satellite settlements.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Edo metalwork is guild work. **Igun eronmwon**, the brasscasters' guild on Igun Street in Benin City, holds the hereditary right to cast by **cire perdue** (lost wax) — the technique behind the commemorative *uhunmwun elao* ancestral heads, the relief plaques, and figures of messengers, warriors and Portuguese soldiers. Beside them, **igbesanmwan** are the ivory and wood carvers, responsible for pectoral masks, tusks carved in registers of kings and attendants, and **ikegobo** or "altar of the hand," a cylindrical altar in wood, brass or terracotta dedicated to individual achievement and the power of one's own hand. Ritual wood includes the **ukhurhe** rattle staff, a segmented ancestral staff struck on the ground to summon the dead, and the **oro** bird-of-prophecy staff. **Ama** are the clay or brass altar and shrine forms of household worship; the **ezuzu** ceremonial fan and the leaf-bladed **eben** sword complete a chief's equipment. Pottery is women's work, unglazed and hand-built, for water storage and shrine offerings.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Coral and red jasper dominate: cylindrical and barrel coral beads strung into the *odigba* choker, multi-strand *ivie-uru* necklaces, crossed bandoliers over the chest, coral anklets and wristlets, and the coral-mesh cap. Brass and bronze anklets, armlets, and bells are cast by the same guild that makes the plaques; agate and imported glass beads supplement coral in non-royal dress. Body marking is documented in the form of *iwu* — the vertical keloid scarification once cut on the chest and torso of Edo men and women as a mark of Benin identity, abandoned in the twentieth century but visible on cast figures and plaques. Women's hair is dressed in the high crested and forward-curving styles that the pectoral masks record, often bound with beads.

## Music & performance

Edo music is drum-led and closely tied to the palace and to shrine worship. The core ensemble pairs the *ema* membrane drums with the slit and hourglass forms, iron clapperless bells (*ukho*-type single and double bells, cast by the brass guild), rattles, and the *akpata* — a plucked bow-lute or trough-zither whose players are professional praise-singers and narrators. Praise-singing to the *Oba*, performed by court musicians attached to the palace wards, recites royal genealogy and the deeds of past reigns; the *ukhurhe* rattle staff, struck rhythmically at ancestral altars, marks liturgy rather than entertainment. Olokun worship supports its own repertoire of women's songs and hand-clapping in the shrine, and masquerade societies keep drum languages specific to their outings. Edo musical practice is also the root of a major twentieth-century popular form: Victor Uwaifo, an Edo musician, sculptor and instrument inventor, built *ekassa* and *akwete* highlife rhythms directly out of Benin ceremonial patterns, and Benin City remains a productive centre of Nigerian popular music (Rema, Johnny Drille, Shallipopi are all Edo).

## Dance & theatre

The best-documented Edo dances are palace dances and masquerade. *Ekassa* is the dance associated with royal rites of succession, danced at the installation and death of an *Oba*; its rhythm was later secularised into highlife. Chiefs perform the *eben* salute — a leaping turn in which the leaf-bladed ceremonial sword is thrown and caught before the *Oba* — which functions as dance, oath and homage at once. Masked performance is organised by age-grade and by ward: masquerades appear at the annual festivals and at second burials, their costumes combining raffia (*ivbiotu*), appliquéd cloth and carved wooden headpieces, and the mask is understood to bring an ancestral or spirit presence rather than to represent one theatrically. Women's dance at Olokun shrines is distinct again: slow, in place, in white cloth, with the shoulders and hands carrying the movement.

## Festivals & rituals

**Igue** is the central annual festival, held in December–January at the close of the year: a sequence of rites in which the *Oba*'s head — the seat of his power — is ritually fortified, the ancestors are addressed, and blessings are pronounced on the kingdom for the coming year. Subsidiary rites in the same cycle include *Ugie Erha Oba*, honouring the *Oba*'s father, *Otue* homage visits by chiefs and palace societies, *Ugie Ewere*, in which the *ewere* leaf is carried through Benin City as a token of prosperity, and *Ugie Oro*, when the bird-of-prophecy staffs are struck to commemorate Oba Esigie's defiance of an ill omen. Olokun devotees hold their own periodic shrine festivals, marked by white cloth, chalk and river offerings. Life-cycle rites: naming takes place about a week after birth, with the child presented to the lineage; marriage proceeds through the *Oba*'s or the elders' approval, bride-gift and the installation of the bride in coral and *ewu-ivie*; death of an elder requires a first burial and a later, larger **second burial**, at which the heir erects an ancestral altar with a carved *ukhurhe* staff and, for chiefs and royals, a cast *uhunmwun elao* memorial head — the practice that generated the great series of Benin brass heads.

## Foodways

The Edo staple is pounded starch eaten with a thick, dark soup. *Pounded yam* (from white yam) and cassava-based *eba* or *akpu* are the standard swallows. The signature dish is **owo** soup — a palm-oil and potash soup, sharply flavoured and thickened without vegetables, eaten with plantain (*ukpo*) or with pounded yam and regarded as the Benin dish proper. **Ogbono** (wild mango-seed) and **banga** (palm-fruit) soups are staples, as is **black soup** (*ebe-ebe* / *omoebe*), a Benin speciality in which a mix of bitter and aromatic leaves is ground to a near-black liquor and cooked with dried fish, snail and meat. Palm oil, ground crayfish, dried fish, bush meat, land snail and periwinkle from the creeks are the characteristic proteins. Melon-seed *egusi*, okra and pepper soups are common. Drinks are palm wine (*ogogoro* when distilled) and kola nut, both of which are also liturgical: kola and palm wine are the standard offerings poured at ancestral altars and at Olokun shrines, and no rite of naming, marriage or burial opens without them.

## Oral tradition & literature

Edo oral tradition is dominated by dynastic history rather than by a single epic. The *Ogiso* king-list and the *Oba* genealogy are transmitted as recited chronicle by palace praise-singers and by ward chiefs, and the Benin plaques functioned as a parallel visual archive of the same material — the *Oba*'s campaigns, embassies, and the arrival of the Portuguese. Folktale (*okhuae*-type animal and trickster narrative), proverb and riddle are the domestic genres, performed at evening gatherings; proverbs are a required register of chiefly and courtroom speech. The great documentary achievement is Chief Jacob U. Egharevba's *A Short History of Benin* (1934 and later editions), which fixed the recited dynastic tradition in writing and remains the reference point for Benin chronology; Egharevba also published on Edo proverbs, names and institutions. Contemporary Edo literature continues in English — Jane Igharo and Suyi Davies Okungbowa both draw on Benin material — and Edo-language publishing and broadcast keep the vernacular in literary use.

## Language & religion

Edo (Bini) belongs to the Edoid group of the Volta–Niger branch of Niger-Congo, with Esan, Etsakọ, Owan, Urhobo and Isoko as close relatives; it is written in a Latin orthography with subdot diacritics for open vowels and tone marking in careful writing. Most Edo today are Christian — Benin City is a major centre of Nigerian Pentecostalism, and Archbishop Benson Idahosa founded the Church of God Mission there — with a Muslim minority and a widely practised substratum of Edo religion. In that system, alongside the human world stands an invisible world of supernatural intercessors, served at their own shrines: *Osanobua*, creator and Supreme God, whose epithet *Osanobua Noghodua* means God Almighty and who is described by *orhiole* (omnipresence), *ajoana* (omniscience) and *udazi* (omnipotence); *Olokun*, his child, ruler of all waters and giver of fertility and wealth; *Ogun*, patron of metalworkers; and *Shango* of thunder and *Osun* of medicine. Ancestral veneration at household and lineage altars is the practical core, and it is what the *ukhurhe*, *ikegobo* and *uhunmwun elao* objects exist to serve.
