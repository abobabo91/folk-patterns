## Overview

The Ekoi, also known as Ejagham, are an ethnic group in south east Nigeria extending eastward into the southwest region of Cameroon. In Nigeria, communities identified as Ejagham occur in Akamkpa, Etung, Ikom, Odukpani, Calabar municipality, Ogoja and nearby areas; in Cameroon they are mainly found in the Eyumojock subdivision of Manyu division. They speak Ejagham, also called Ekoi, an Ekoid language within the Bantoid branch of the Niger–Congo dialect cluster. The sources used do not provide a population figure. The Ekoi are associated with Nsibidi ideograms, Ekpe headdresses, skin-covered masks, sculpture, body-painting, weaving, pottery and ceremonial societies.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Nsibidi* — Nsibidi is an ideographic writing tradition associated with the Ekoi and is visible on surviving objects. Museum records describe vessels, bowls, a dance-sword or dance-staff, stands and masquerade headdresses carved or decorated with nsibidi designs, signs or inscriptions.

**Body-painting** — Body-painting is described as important to men, who are presented as both warriors and artists. Nnimm initiates wore cursive body-painting together with material dresses of calabash and shells.

**Weaving** — Both men and women participated in weaving. Museum records include mats made of vegetable fibre with geometrical patterns and bags made from palm leaf or vegetable fibre.

**Palm-leaf and fibre work** — Museum objects include bags made of palm leaf, a bag of vegetable fibre with a check pattern, an undyed palm-leaf bag with a fringe, and a climbing rope combining woven cane with fibre from the outer bark of the mid-rib of the oil palm frond.

**Calabash decoration** — Calabash vessels and bowls carry geometric decoration, pictographic nsibidi characters, burnt nsibidi signs and other decorative patterns.

**Basketry** — Basketry appears in masks and headdresses, including circular basket-work bases, basket-work circlets and vegetable-fibre basketry cylinders.

**Motif vocabulary.** Nsibidi characters and ideograms, geometric patterns, pictographic signs, roaring fish, leopard, bird’s heads, horns, human heads, monkey.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe ceremonial clothing more fully than everyday dress. Nnimm initiates were unmarried young girls who wore cursive body-painting and material dresses of calabash and shells, leather necklaces, feather headdresses, and a cowrie-fringed wrapper. Bones of monkeys were matched with feather headdresses, and the single feather at the back of the head was identified as the Nnimm feather. Museum records include a “Jujudräkt,” described as probably Ekoi and associated with Nyang in the Calabar area, as well as a wooden mask attached to a coarse cloth dress. The sources also describe wooden dance headdresses and head-dresses made from wood, skin, metal and basketry. They do not describe ordinary daily garments, footwear, belts or specific men’s clothing.

### Architecture

The sources used do not describe Ekoi house forms, settlement architecture, roof types, building materials or named building traditions. They state that God’s first man and woman lived in a hut, but provide no ethnographic account of Ekoi domestic architecture. The sources mention sanctuaries in connection with mural paintings, without describing their construction.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Ekoi material culture includes pottery, calabash vessels and wooden household objects. The sources describe pottery vessels, bowls, a vessel with a long narrow neck and loop handles, a pedestal bowl, a calabash bottle with geometric decoration, a composite calabash bowl with a cover, and a calabash bowl or ladle decorated with burnt nsibidi signs. Museum records also include an iron lamp, a composite knife with an iron blade and copper-wrapped wooden handle, a forked ceremonial blade, a rectangular palm-mid-rib shield, a wooden tripod seat and a crocodile-shaped mancala board with two times six holes.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources describe leather necklaces, cowries, beads, feathers, monkey bones and animal teeth as forms of adornment. Nnimm initiates wore leather necklaces, feather headdresses and a cowrie-fringed wrapper. A museum-recorded standing female figure has strings of small black, white and pink beads around the neck. Leopard’s teeth were used in skull-caps and necklaces associated with ntuis, while a staff could be bound with leopard’s skin. The sources do not document tattoos, henna, hair practices or named jewelry types beyond these descriptions.

## Music & performance

Music is represented in the sources through drums, a xylophone and rattles. The Ekpe story says that Tanze became the body of a female drum after dying and becoming a fish. The museum catalogue includes a drum whose body takes the form of the lower half of a female figure, a large drum with a cylindrical wooden body standing on four mammalian feet, and a double drum with two membranes, one of snake-skin and the other of antelope skin. It also records an incomplete xylophone made from a trough crossed by slats, with a human figure attached to one end and a beater, as well as a pair of wicker rattles with gourd bases containing seeds.

The Ekoi used music within social and ritual life. The Ekpe Society referred to the roaring fish and the leopard as signs from God, and these signs were said to be invoked in every Ekoi court. The sources do not identify song genres, named instrumental ensembles, musical scales or performance occasions such as weddings and funerals.

## Dance & theatre

The Ekoi are described as having mastered skin-covered masks and as being the first in the account to make skin-covered masks and dance in them. Nigerian and Cameroon regions are said to share similar traditional masks and tribal organizations. Museum records include wooden dance headdresses, double-headed headdresses, Janus-faced masks, bird-headed masquerade headdresses, head-dresses covered with skin, and a wooden mask attached to a coarse cloth dress.

The Nkwa-mbuk was a mask-wearing society that performed rituals including human sacrifices and head hunting. Ekoi masks could be realistic, with shiny eyes, human hair, carved teeth and coverings of animal or human skin. Two-sided masks used a darker side for the male force and a whiter side for the female force. The sources do not provide the names of dances, theatrical plots or entertainment genres.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not describe an annual festival calendar or festivals with dates. They do describe ritual institutions, initiation and funerary practices. The Mgbe and Nnimm societies were respectively for males and females. Nnimm initiates were unmarried young girls and wore distinctive body-painting, dresses, necklaces, feather headdresses and wrappers.

The Nkwa-mbuk performed rituals including human sacrifices and head hunting. After a battle, the skin of enemies was taken and made into a mask to proclaim victory. When a village received a blessing, offerings were presented to the ancestors. The current chief and previous chiefs were honored, and the chief who selected the settlement became village chief. When a ntui died, people entered the jungle to bring back the ngbe as the ntui’s spirit returned to God. The sources also state that the dead were buried in the ground with important belongings, while the soul continued under the earth with Obassi Nsi.

## Foodways

The sources state that Ekoi women traditionally engaged in agriculture and raised yams, plantains and corn, while men hunted and people in coastal areas practiced fishing. Domestication of sheep, goats and fowls is also mentioned. Obassi Nsi is associated with ripening the food planted in the ground and with teaching humans to hunt and grow food.

Museum records document pottery and calabash vessels, bowls, a bottle, a ladle and a platter, but do not identify particular dishes or cooking recipes. The sources do not describe staple preparations, named foods, beverages, dietary rules, ceremonial meals or tea and coffee traditions.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Ekoi have a large number of spoken stories. One creation tale tells of God creating the first man and woman, allowing them to live in a hut, and instructing them to care for their child; the tale concludes that all people descend from this man and woman. Another story explains the natural world through Eagle and Ox playing hide-and-seek. Eagle hides on Ox’s horns, Fowl reveals his location, and Eagle then swears to take Fowl’s children; the story explains why eagles eat younger fowls.

Ekoi folklore also says that mermaids taught the Nsibidi script. The story of Tanze describes an old king who died, became a fish, was caught by a woman, and became the body of a female drum after a man killed the woman and created the Leopard Society. The sources do not identify an epic poem, written literary tradition, proverb collection or contemporary literary revival.

## Language & religion

Ejagham, also called Ekoi or Jagham, is an Ekoid language of Nigeria and Cameroon. The Ekoid languages belong to the Bantoid branch of the Niger–Congo dialect cluster. The sources divide Ejagham dialects into Western and Eastern groups. Western varieties include Bendeghe, Northern and Southern Etung, Ekwe and Akamkpa-Ejagham; Eastern varieties include Keaka and Obang. Ekin is also listed as an Ejagham dialect.

The Ekoi are associated with Nsibidi ideographs, which convey concepts through symbols and are used in reading and writing. A Jagham alphabet was developed by John R. Watters and Kathie Watters in 1981. Traditional religion names Obassi Osaw and Obassi Nsi as gods who created everything. Obassi Osaw is associated with the sky, moisture, light, droughts and storms; Obassi Nsi is associated with the earth, nurture, hunting and food-growing. Ancestors, natural forces, farming cults, traditional medicine and secret societies such as the Egbo are also described.
