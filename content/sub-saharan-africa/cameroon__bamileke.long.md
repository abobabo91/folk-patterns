## Overview

The Bamiléké are a Central African people of the Western High Plateau of Cameroon, the region colloquially called the grassfields. Dr John Feyou de Hapy glosses the name as "people of faith". Their languages are Bamileke languages, a group of Eastern Grassfields languages within the Southern Bantoid branch of Niger-Congo. Bamiléké oral history traces an origin along the Nile in what is now Sudan, with arrival in Western Cameroon in several waves along two routes — one from between the Lake Tchad area and the Nile Valley, the other from the Cross River area of Nigeria. In folk-culture terms they matter for a court art built on beadwork, stitch-resist indigo *ndop* cloth, masquerade and carved royal furniture, produced under a chiefdom system whose ruler, the *fon*, stands at its centre. The sources used give no population figure.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Ndop** — a stitch resist-dyed indigo cloth, woven in strips of white hand-spun cotton warp and weft, the strips then hand-sewn together, and decorated throughout with indigo stitch-resist patterns. Museum examples are narrow-strip or wide-strip, and range from display cloths — one a very long roll carrying a repeating pattern, several left unhemmed — to garments cut from the same cloth: a wide rectangular tunic with a V-shaped neck opening lined inside with sackcloth, a rectangular tunic with a round neck, short capped sleeves and pockets, and a dance-skirt with a single wide strip inserted at one side and red wool baize strips sewn on at both ends.

**Beadwork** — beads worked over cloth and over wooden sculpture. Beadwork is treated as the element that distinguishes Bamileke art from other regions of Africa, and working beadwork over wooden sculpture is a technique unique to the Cameroon grasslands. No two pieces are alike, colours are used to catch the eye, and the kind of bead can indicate status. Before colonisation beads came from sub-Saharan countries such as Nigeria and were made of shells, nuts, wood, seeds, ceramic, ivory, animal bone and metal; colonial-era trade with Europe and the Middle East introduced brightly coloured glass beads, pearls, coral and stones such as emeralds, exchanged for slaves, gold, oil, ivory and fine woods.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Isosceles triangles* — the known symbol of the leopard, and part of the geometric beadwork on elephant masks; *lizards*, *equal-arm crosses enclosed within circles* and other amuletic designs — recorded on a stitch-resist indigo *ndop* roll.

### Clothing & dress

Dress documented in the collection is largely ceremonial and made of *ndop*. Two tunic forms survive: a wide rectangular one with a V-shaped neck opening, woven in narrow strips and lined on the inside with sackcloth, and a narrower-cut rectangular tunic with a round neck, short capped sleeves and pockets, woven in wide strips. A *ndop* dance-skirt, likewise strip-woven, carries red wool baize cloth strips sewn on at both ends.

Headgear is the other developed category. A basketry hat worn with the elephant mask has its whole surface covered with dyed cotton cloth and intricate glass beadwork. Other head-dresses are built on a cane or wicker frame: one decorated with strips of a long-haired animal and with coloured strips of twisted red, black and natural-coloured fibre knotted at the ends; one covered with feathers and fitted with a string strap; one covered with a fur crest and a piece of cotton cloth. A circular woven fibre cape for covering the head, adorned with cowrie shells and feathers, is recorded as worn by a witch doctor. On occasion the *fon* permits community members to perform in an elephant mask together with a leopard skin, a statement of wealth, status and power.

### Architecture

Traditional homes begin as a raffia-pole frame set into four square walls; the holes left in the frame are stuffed with grass, the whole building is covered with mud, and the thatched roof is shaped into a tall cone. That construction is now mostly reserved for barns, storage buildings and the gathering places of the various traditional secret societies. Present-day Bamileke houses are built of bricks of either sun-dried mud or concrete, and roofed with metal sheeting.

Settlement follows a well-organised and structured pattern: the houses of family members are grouped together and surrounded by small fields. The settlement unit is the chiefdom, headed by the *fon*, and below the *fon* and his advisers sit several ward heads, each responsible for a particular portion of the village.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Black pottery is the recorded ceramic tradition: a flat-bottomed vase with rounded sides tapering to a neck that flares out for the rim, a single looped handle from rim to shoulder and the bottom third rouletted with diagonal lines; a vase with a bell mouth and two lifting lugs; and bowls raised on openwork cylindrical pedestals, one with three holes evenly spaced near the top, concentric circles on the shoulder and geometric rouletting around the pedestal base. A pottery fragment forms the head of a male figurine, black, wearing a hat.

Iron work runs to weapons and tools — a ceremonial sword whose hilt is surmounted by a cat-like animal, socketed spearheads with two barbs (one socket finished with an iron coil), axes, a curved-edged knife whose edge is wrapped for protection, and a circumcision-knife. Brass is cast by the lost-wax method; one sample pairs a brass face with the clay core still inside, and a wax model of a woman seated on a stool holding a bag was made for casting.

Wood and plant fibre carry the household and court objects: a chief's throne whose back represents a human figure and whose seat is supported by six figures, a stool with carved heads, a pedestal bowl supported by two figures, a cylindrical box on flaring tripod feet with a looped lid handle and bands of pyro-decoration, wooden bowls, gourd vessels with rattan bases and binding or with incised patterns and a leather suspension strap, a waisted coiled-cane basketry dish, and a square shield frame of palm and cane built on a criss-cross frame.

## Music & performance

The instruments held for the Bamileke are mostly idiophones, aerophones and drums. Two *sansa* (lamellophones) survive in contrasting builds: one a rectangular wooden box with fifteen cane tongues, the outer layer of cane stripped away at the playing ends to reveal a smooth surface and three tongues stripped along their whole length, the tongues held by a single cane-bound metal rod and supported by two bridges — cane at the lower end, a wood quadrant at the playing end; the other two pieces of bamboo bound with fibre, with nine teeth over a bridge. Two bamboo flutes each have two stops. A stringed instrument is made of wood. Two iron gongs are joined by a handle covered with reed, and a slit drum is carved in human form. One wooden trumpet is catalogued as funerary equipment, tying wind instruments directly to burial.

The Bamiléké developed their own musical style, **Tsamassi**, popularised by André-Marie Tala.

## Dance & theatre

Masquerades are an integral part of Bamileke culture and expression. Colourful beaded masks are worn at funerals, important palace festivals and other royal ceremonies; they are performed by men, and aim to support and enforce royal authority. The best known is the elephant mask, or *mbap mteng*, with protruding circular ears, a human-like face and decorative front and back panels hanging to the knees, covered throughout in geometric beadwork including triangular imagery. Elephant masks are used in dance ceremonies and at funerals to show the importance of the deceased; at the homegoing celebration of King Njoya's mother in 1913 they were worn by those in attendance. Buffalo masks are also very popular and present at most functions across Grassland societies, including the Bamileke, representing power, strength and bravery and associated with the *fon*. The power of the *fon* is represented by the elephant, buffalo and leopard, and oral tradition holds that he may transform into an elephant or leopard at will. The carved wooden masks in the collection include one with a textile mask attached through holes to cover the back of the head, one with an elaborate hairstyle and open mouth painted dark brown, one with an openwork headdress and white pigment in the carved recesses around ears, eyes and mouth, a man's face mask with animal skin and hair, and one blackened with a hot iron.

## Festivals & rituals

Funerary practice is the rite the sources describe in most detail. Death is always met with mystery, and the family must turn the body over to an examiner to determine the cause. The family then gathers at the home, and each member steps up to the totem and swears they were not involved in the death; the belief is that if the murderer is in the room, the totem will trap their spirit forever. The person believed to be the murderer must satisfy the ancestors by a special ritual of pouring libation during the burial ceremony, after which the family gathers the wet earth and shapes it into a circle — a metaphorical skull of the deceased, blessed by libation.

Ancestral veneration is the dominant form of worship. Because the ancestor's spirit is held to remain within the actual skull, families keep possession of them: the oldest male in the family keeps the skulls of both male and female ancestors in a dwelling the family has built and a diviner has blessed, and a special libation ritual must be performed if a skull is not well preserved. Funerals, important palace festivals and royal ceremonies are the occasions for beaded masquerade. Marriage involves a bride price paid to the bride's family, and polygyny is practised; some important individuals may have hundreds of wives. A circumcision-knife in the collection points to that rite without the sources describing it. There is no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription for Cameroon in the sources used, and no annual festival calendar is documented there.

## Foodways

Staple crops are cocoyams, groundnuts and maize. Men typically clear the fields and women largely work them, using tools such as machetes and hoes. Palm wine and other drink customs would need a source that treats them; libation is documented as a funerary ritual act rather than as a described foodway.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral tradition carries Bamiléké history. Narratives collected by Alexis Maxime Feyou de Happy and his son Joseph describe arrival in Western Cameroon in multiple waves along two routes, from between the Lake Tchad area and the Nile Valley, and from around the Cross River in Nigeria. Some traditions hold the Bamiléké to descend from a Mbum princess named Wouten, also called Betaka, who helped establish the Tikar fondom in the 13th century after being expelled from Ngan Ha, the capital of Mbum, following a succession dispute. Another strand of oral tradition proclaims that the *fon* may transform into an elephant or a leopard whenever he chooses. Sculpture is understood in these terms too: an analysis of Bamileke sculpture found that artists do not produce but create, that statues, reliefs and paintings represent life forces safeguarding intangible attributes of death so that they live eternally, and that sculptors act as intermediaries charged with realising God's vision through symbols and signs. The sources used name no epic cycle, proverb collection or riddle tradition.

## Language & religion

The Bamileke languages are Eastern Grassfields languages within the Southern Bantoid branch of Niger-Congo, possibly forming two branches. Western Bamileke comprises Mengaka (Məgaka), Ngombale, Ngomba (Nguemba or Ngemba), the "Bamboutos" dialect cluster of Yɛmba, Ngyɛmbɔɔŋ, Mmuock and Ŋwe; Eastern Bamileke comprises Feʼfeʼ, Ghɔmáláʼ, Kwaʼ, Ndaʼndaʼ and Mədʉmba. During the colonial period parts of the Bamileke region adopted Christianity, and some Bamileke practise Islam, but the dominant form of worship remains ancestral, centred on veneration of the ancestors and the keeping of ancestral skulls. Diviners bless the dwellings in which skulls are kept. The sources used mention no historical script for these languages.
