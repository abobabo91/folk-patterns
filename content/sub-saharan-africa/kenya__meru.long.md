## Overview

The Meru, also called Ameru or Amîîrú, are a Bantu ethnic group in Kenya. They inhabit the Meru region, including Meru and Tharaka-Nithi, on the fertile northern and eastern slopes of Mount Kenya and across the Nyambene ranges. The nine subgroups named in the sources are Igoji, Imenti, Tigania, Miutini, Igembe, Mwimbi, Muthambi, Chuka and Tharaka. The Tharaka live in the semi-arid part of Greater Meru; Tharaka, Mwimbi, Muthambi and Chuka form Tharaka-Nithi County. The Meru speak Meru, also called KiMîîrú or Kimeru, a Bantu language with geographically varying but mutually intelligible dialects. Their documented folk-culture materials include oral histories of migration, ritual specialists, age sets, clan relationships, agricultural and pastoral life, and museum objects associated with Meru in Kenya.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The catalogue records describe three engraved ornament fields on a white-metal ear ornament, an ornament band and central ornament on a finger ring, and two rows of glass beads on an earring.

### Clothing & dress

The sources provide limited information about clothing. A British Museum photographic print describes an adult male wearing cloth tied at the right shoulder, with a long straight object over the left shoulder and long plaited hair tied at the back. In the historical account of the Ngaa, the invaders called Nguo Ntuni are described as wearing a single red cloth tied around the waist and at one shoulder, with another cloth bound around the head; this description concerns the remembered coastal conquerors rather than a documented Meru dress tradition.

### Architecture

The historical account says that the migrating Ngaa probably lived in simple, smaller huts while constantly moving. It also describes the Nguo Ntuni as normally living on the mainland behind a log stockade and using mainland gardens.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum records document several objects associated with Meru or with East Africa. A British Museum spear has an iron, ribbed, leaf-shaped head. A composite arrow has a fibre construction, a leaf-shaped iron blade, a cane shaft, leather feathering and a decorated end. Another British Museum object is a wooden spoon. The historical account also mentions iron knives, wood-tipped arrows, small bows, woven goat-hair lassos used to catch fish, wooden hooks, spears and a forge. These references document tools and weapons.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The Museum of Ethnography catalogue records several objects attributed to Meru in Kenya. An ear ornament is a thin, rounded white-metal plate with three engraved ornament fields on each side and part of a brass chain; the catalogue says it was worn by “manlig medelålder,” translated in the record as a man of middle age. Another earring has a hanging section of copper chains, two rows of glass beads on metal wire and a brownish substance described as probably red earth mixed with fat. A shield-shaped, narrow oval finger ring is made of white metal and has ornament bands along the edges and a central ornament; the record calls it a modern reproduction of an original type. The photographic print describes an adult male with long, straight, plaited hair tied at the back.

## Music & performance

The historical account does describe ritual specialists and public actions connected with crisis and migration. Koome Njoe is remembered as a *Muga*, or curse remover, who advised the Kiama during the demands imposed by the Nguo Ntuni. Mururia is described as another ritual specialist who proposed that the Ngaa flee the island. The Kiama is identified as a council of elders and appears in the account as a body that met, interpreted difficult tasks and directed collective action. These references concern ritual authority and oral historical narrative, not music. The Meru language article states that a Kenyan musical group called High Pitch Band Afrika made a Kimeru cover of “Despacito,” uploaded to YouTube on July 10, 2017, but the sources provide no further account of Meru musical practice.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe ritual and historical actions associated with migration, conflict and collective identity. During the remembered escape from Mbwaa, specific clans were said to carry objects of importance: Abwekana carried yams, Kiniamburi carried the goat, and the Omo carried objects of religious significance because of its association with the Meru God Ngai and with the Mugwe. The migrant population was divided into groups of warriors, older and middle-aged men, and women, children and elderly people.

The account of the crossing says that the prophet requested three men to participate in a sacrifice: Muthetu was to serve as an altar, Gaita was to have his stomach opened so that omens could be read, and Kiuna was to replace him if his courage failed. The historical narrative also records the establishment of the Nkuthuku age set at Kirorero, probably around the 1720s, and the Mukuruma age set probably in the 1730s. Later, the Ngaa divided into groups whose relationships were commemorated through kinship rituals, military alliances and bonds.

## Foodways

The sources document food and subsistence during the migration remembered in Meru oral history. The Ngaa mainly lived on goats and sheep brought with them, supplemented their diet by raising crops and fishing, and used wooden hooks. They also made woven goat-hair lassos that caught fish by snagging their gills. During the trek, the migrants sought swamps and other water sources and dug for water when it was not readily available. Traditions also state that they hunted Grévy's zebra and the Reticulated giraffe when food and hydration became scarce.

The account describes several unusual preparations connected with the demands of the Nguo Ntuni. Koome Njoe advised that a deep hole be filled with water so that a small round fruit would float out. He supplied an “eight-sided cloth” in the form of a cob of maize whose peeled husk had eight sides. To produce a calf with white dung, he starved it for a week and then fed it milk and lime for four days. At the elders’ final feast, each family was to present a goat, and blood from the slaughtered animals was allowed to flow toward the Nguo Ntuni camp as part of a deception.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources give substantial space to Meru oral history concerning the Ngaa, described as proto-Meru by the Igoji, Imenti, Tigania, Mwimbi, Muthambi and Igembe traditions. The narrative places their earlier settlement on the coast, possibly around today's Manda Island, followed by defeat by the Nguo Ntuni and an escape from Mbwaa. It recounts impossible tasks imposed on the elders, including producing a dog with horns and forging a spear long enough to reach the clouds. The story continues through the crossing, the trek along rivers and swamps, the movement toward Mount Kenya and the eventual division into Meru subgroups.

The sources preserve named sayings and explanations of identity. The groups later remembered that “Mwimbi, Tigania and Murutu were once one” and that “Igoji, Igembe, Imenti, and Muthambi had one father.” A prophecy attributed to Kiringo M'Munyari of Igembe speaks of leaving the desert for better land and defeating its inhabitants in battle.

## Language & religion

Meru is a Bantu language spoken by the Ameru on the eastern and northern slopes of Mount Kenya and on the Nyambene ranges. It is also called KiMîîrú or Kimeru. The sources describe seven mutually intelligible dialects: Kiimenti, Tiania or gitiania, kiigembe, Kimwimbi, Kïmüthambï, Gicuka and Kitharaka. Imenti is described as commonly used in Meru and as a lingua franca among the nine Meru subtribes. Kimeru is written in a Latin alphabet, does not use the letters f, p, q, s, v, x and z, and adds ĩ and ũ. The language has sixteen noun classes and follows Dahl's law.

The historical traditions refer to the Meru God Ngai, the Omo clan, the Mugwe, prophets, blessings of the ancestors, omens, sacrifice and ritual specialists.
