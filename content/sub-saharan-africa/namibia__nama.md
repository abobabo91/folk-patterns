## Overview

The Nama are an African ethnic group living in South Africa, Namibia, and Botswana. Many Nama clans live in central Namibia, while smaller groups live in Namaqualand, which crosses the Namibian border with South Africa. The Nama traditionally speak Nama, also called Khoekhoe, Khoekhoegowab, or Nama/Damara, a Khoe-family language; many also speak Afrikaans. The Nama were the largest group of the Khoekhoe people, much of which disappeared as a group. Their history includes pastoralism, nomadic movement, colonial dispossession, resistance to German rule, and the Nama and Herero genocide of 1904–1908. Music, poetry, oral storytelling, leatherwork, mats, clothing, and the mobile reed-mat hut remain important subjects in the sources.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*|haru oms* — The traditional Nama dwelling is covered with portable rush mats. The mats are made from organic materials and form the covering of a domed hut.

**Matjieshuis** — The matjieshuis is a round hut made from reed mats on a skeleton of sticks, woven into a beehive shape by women. Women and men collect materials, prepare rugs, and construct the hut in a meticulous process described as a Nama art.

**Leatherwork and skin karosses** — Nama crafts include leatherwork and skin karosses.

**Mats** — Mats are among the crafts associated with Nama culture.

**Reed flutes** — Reed flutes are listed among Nama musical instruments.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no Nama textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The traditional dress of Nama women consists of formal dresses resembling Victorian traditional fashion. These long, flowing dresses developed from the style of missionaries in the 1800s and remain an integral part of Nama culture.

### Architecture

The Nama hut, called matjieshuis or *haru oms* in the Nama language, is a round dwelling made from reed mats attached to a skeleton of sticks and woven into a beehive shape by women. It reflects the Nama’s nomadic history and remains part of life in the Richtersveld, a region of mountainous deserts in the northwest of South Africa. The hut is used as a dwelling, store, kitchen, additional sleeping place, and tourist accommodation. It is cool and ventilated in summer, insulated by reed carpets in winter, and protected from rain by porous stems that swell with water. It can be dismantled in less than an hour and reused.

Traditionally, Nama camps had 5–30 circular domed huts whose doors faced the centre. The chief’s hut stood to the west and faced east; other huts were arranged according to seniority. Adult livestock slept in front of their owners’ huts, while calves and lambs were kept in an enclosed central area.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Nama crafts include clay pots, tortoiseshell powder containers, leatherwork, skin karosses, mats, jewellery, and musical instruments such as reed flutes.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Jewellery is listed among Nama crafts.

## Music & performance

Music, poetry, and storytelling are very important in Nama culture, and many stories have been passed down orally through generations. Reed flutes are listed among Nama musical instruments.

The sources describe singing during funerals. Mourning takes place for three days before burial. During the first two nights there is singing of hymns, preaching, and praying. On the last day of mourning and the day of burial, speeches are made and messages of condolence are presented.

The sources also state that the Nama settlement at |Ae||gams, modern-day Windhoek, developed a communal society centred on cattle, trade, Christianity, and Tsui-||goab and Heitsi-Eibib worship.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do describe wedding and funeral rituals.

A Nama marriage begins when a man discusses his intentions with his family. If they agree, his family accompanies him to the bride’s home. Animal hides are placed in the yard for different groups to sit and discuss. The groom’s family asks for the gate to be opened and questions him about the bride, including their first meeting and her body marks. If the bride is pregnant or already has children, a “door cleansing” ceremony takes place, involving the slaughter and consumption of a snow-white goat.

The wedding process continues when the bride’s family visits the groom’s clan. At the engagement, the groom’s family brings live animals to the woman’s family home. The animals are slaughtered and hung on three sticks, and each part is offered to the bride’s family. Bags of sugar or flour are offered in quantities of two or four to indicate continuing abundance of food. White flags are mounted on both families’ houses and remain until they wither or are blown away.

Preparations can take up to a year. The groom’s family traditionally gives the bride’s mother a cow and a calf. On the wedding day, both families provide animals and other food. The wedding takes place in a church, followed by several days of festivities. The couple spends the first night separately and leaves for their own home the next morning.

Nama funerals were formerly small and involved burying the body without speaking about the deceased because of fear of spirits. Today funerals are described as occasions of social solidarity. Close relatives prepare the grave for a week. Mourning lasts three days before burial and includes hymns, preaching, prayer, speeches, and condolences.

## Foodways

The sources mention pastoral farming, cattle, small livestock, and the use of animals in wedding rituals. At engagements, animals are slaughtered and offered to the bride’s family. The sources also mention bags of sugar and flour, offered in quantities of two or four as a sign that food will remain abundant.

The “door cleansing” ceremony involves slaughtering and consuming a snow-white goat.

## Oral tradition & literature

Music, poetry, and storytelling are important in Nama culture. Many stories have been transmitted orally through the generations.

The sources also describe Hendrik Witbooi as styling himself as a biblical prophet and gaining the support of prominent families in Gibeon.

## Language & religion

Nama, also called Khoekhoe, Khoekhoegowab, Nama/Damara, or Namagowab, belongs to the Khoe language family and is spoken in Namibia, Botswana, and South Africa. It is spoken primarily by Namakhoen, ǂNūkhoen, and Haiǁomkhoen. Modern scholars generally identify the Nama–Damara, Haiǁom, and ǂĀkhoe dialects; Eini is described as extinct. Khoekhoe is a national language in Namibia, and state-owned broadcasting corporations in Namibia and South Africa produce radio programmes in it. Around 167,000 Khoekhoe speakers are estimated to remain in Africa, making it an endangered language.

The Nama largely abandoned their traditional religion through the sustained efforts of Christian and Muslim missionaries. Most Nama people in Namibia are Christian, while Nama Muslims make up a large percentage of Namibia’s Muslims. The sources also mention Tsui-||goab and Heitsi-Eibib worship in the communal society developed at |Ae||gams.
