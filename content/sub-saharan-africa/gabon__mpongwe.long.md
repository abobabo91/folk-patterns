## Overview

The Mpongwe are an ethnic group in Gabon and a subgroup of the Myènè people, a Bantu-speaking population. They were the earliest known dwellers around the estuary where Libreville is now located. Myene varieties are spoken in Gabon by about 46,000 people; the more distinctive varieties include Mpongwe, Galwa and Nkomi. The Mpongwe became intermediaries between the coast and interior peoples such as the Bakèlè and Séké, and engaged in extensive coastal trade across the Central African coast. In the 1840s, they consisted of 6,000–7,000 free persons and 6,000 slaves, organized into about two dozen clans. Their history, language, coastal commerce and contact with Europeans are the principal subjects covered by the sources used here.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The British Museum catalogue describes a broad beadwork belt with lozenge and other conventional patterns in blue, red, green, amber, pink and black.

### Clothing & dress

A British Museum catalogue record describes a photographic print of an adult female in Gabon wearing long, patterned cloth and a head-cloth formed into two points. Another record describes a broad beadwork belt made of beads in several colours, with eight cords of twisted blue beads at each end. The catalogue records do not identify these objects as specifically Mpongwe.

### Architecture

The sources state that King Louis, Anguilè Ré-Dowé, ceded his village of Okolo and moved, leaving the French to establish Fort d’Aumale on the village’s site in 1843.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum records include a dagger associated with the Mpongwe. The Museum of World Culture catalogue describes a pointed, leaf-shaped dagger with a sheath, associated with Gabon or the Ogove region and identified in the catalogue commentary as probably Mpongwe rather than Fang. Its recorded length is 28.5 cm, although the sheath was already missing in 1891. The same record identifies *Lumbuetul* in connection with the object.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The museum catalogue records several objects made or partly made from beads, ivory and tooth. They include a necklace of six groups of four tusk-shaped ivory pieces separated by strands of small black beads, a beaded necklet made of beads and tooth, and a necklace of black and yellow glass beads with a central cylindrical pendant.

## Music & performance

The Mpongwe language article records a Pongwe dictionary published by Jean-Rémy Bessieux in 1847 and refers to an archive documenting the Myene language cluster, including Mpongwe, but it provides no musical description. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription concerns this ethnic group.

## Festivals & rituals

The Wikipedia account does describe social relations between Mpongwé women and European men in the mid 19th century: unions were accepted in Mpongwé communities when family consent and a bridewealth were given, although they were not considered legitimate marriages under French law. These unions provided an avenue for women to acquire property and French citizenship.

## Foodways

The historical account states only that, in the 1830s, Mpongwe trade included slaves, dyewood, ebony, rubber, ivory and gum copal in exchange for cloth, iron, firearms and various forms of alcoholic drink.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources record that Jean-Rémy Bessieux, a missionary and linguist, published a Pongwe dictionary in 1847. The Mpongwe language article also mentions an online Myene resource containing proverbs, tales, courses in audio mp3, stories, rites and legends.

## Language & religion

Mpongwe is identified as a subgroup of the Myènè people, who belong to the Bantus. Myene is a cluster of closely related Bantu varieties spoken in Gabon. Mpongwe, also called Pongoué, is described alongside Galwa, also called Galloa, and Nkomi as one of its more distinctive varieties. The language article discusses phonological variation, including the realization of /ɡ/, voiced sounds, affricates, /w/ and a nasalized labio-velar sound in the Mpongwe dialect. Jean-Rémy Bessieux published a Pongwe dictionary in 1847.
