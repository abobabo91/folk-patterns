## Overview

The Embu, who call themselves *Aembu* (singular *Muembu*, sometimes rendered Waembu), are a Bantu people of Embu County on the southern windward slopes of Mount Kenya, in Kenya's former Eastern Province. The Kikuyu lie to their west, the Meru to the north, and the Kamba to the east and south. They comprise two subgroups: the Embu proper and the Mbeere, who occupy the semi-arid part of the county. Their language, *Kĩembu*, belongs to the Bantu branch of the Niger-Congo family and is closely related to Kikuyu, Kimeru and Kamba. The sources used here give no population figure. In folk-culture terms the Embu are chiefly documented through a body of weaponry, personal ornament and ritual practitioners' equipment collected into museums, and through an agrarian life built on the fertile Mount Kenya highlands.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here document no Embu textile or weaving tradition. The pattern-bearing objects they record are ornaments rather than cloth: leather headdresses and head-rings faced with cowrie shells, glass beads and shell beads, a head-band hung with bead-covered pendants, a headdress fringe of reeds and seeds strung on a plaited fibre cord, and a medicine gourd bound with bands of hide. An oval shield of bark covered with red pigment carries the only recorded surface colouring.

### Clothing & dress

The catalogue records reach Embu dress only through the head. Two **head-dresses or head-rings** are built of leather and faced with cowrie shells, glass beads and shell beads — a combination of marine shell and imported glass on a hide ground. A third, a **head-band**, is of leather hung with pendants covered in red and pink beads. A separate **head-dress fringe** is made of reeds and seeds threaded onto a plaited vegetable-fibre cord, a lighter and entirely local construction beside the beaded leather pieces. A wooden **ear-plug** completes the recorded personal ornament.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The recorded Embu objects are overwhelmingly of wood, hide, horn, gourd and bamboo rather than fired clay. Weapons dominate: a **sword** of iron, wood and hide; **bows** of wood strung with vegetable fibre, one bound with haired skin; **game arrows** of wood, reed, feather and vegetable fibre; an oval **shield** of bark under red pigment and a wooden **parrying shield**. Three striking weapons are recorded, all of the same composite construction — a **club or knobkerrie** with a stone head sheathed in hide on a wooden handle, and a **throwing-club** whose stone head is covered by sewn leather over a wooden shaft. Domestic and utility pieces include a wooden **medicine spoon**, a female **fire-stick** of wood, and a stopper apparently made of cow tail.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Body adornment in the museum record is concentrated in the beaded leather headgear described under dress: cowrie shells, glass beads and shell beads worked onto leather rings and bands, and pendants covered with red and pink beads. A wooden ear-plug indicates pierced and distended earlobes. The sources record no metal jewellery, tattooing, scarification, henna or hair practice for the Embu.

## Music & performance

Two instruments appear in the museum record, both wooden side-blown horns: a **war horn** of wood, and a second **carved war horn** — signalling instruments tied to the defence of territory rather than to entertainment. Wikipedia describes the Aembu love of music and dance as evident in performances at occasions of regional and national importance and at many events of daily life, but names no song genre, no ensemble and no other instrument. It notes that cultural loss, urbanisation and modernisation have heavily affected indigenous knowledge in this domain.

## Dance & theatre

Embu dance is documented in general terms only. In their dances the Aembu display agility, performing acrobatics and body movements, and their dance techniques resemble those of the Akamba of Kenya. The sources name no individual dance, no masquerade and no dramatic or puppet tradition, and do not distinguish ceremonial from entertainment repertoire.

## Festivals & rituals

What the record does document in depth is the equipment of ritual and healing specialists — the largest single group of Embu objects held by the British Museum, catalogued as witchdoctors' equipment. It includes a **'wizard's bag'** cut from a single piece of folded hide, stitched up at the sides with a carrying strap attached; bamboo **tubes** holding medicinal powder, and another bamboo tube holding chalk powder for ceremonies; gourd **vessels** of medicine and medicinal powder, variously mounted with skin, haired skin, hide thongs and goat dung, one bound with decorative bands of hide; a gourd and skin **vessel containing divination powder**; a wooden **container** of powder and possibly snuff; and a set of **charms** made of horn — one of gerenuk antelope horn, others combining horn with hide, wood, skin, claws and conch shell. A wooden **medicine spoon** belongs to the same complex. Divination powder and medicine kept in the same kit indicate practitioners who both diagnosed and treated.

## Foodways

Embu land on the windward slopes of Mount Kenya takes two growing seasons a year, and drought or famine is rare. The staple food crops are maize, beans, yams, cassava, millet, sorghum, bananas and arrowroot, supported by cattle, goats, sheep and chicken. Typical dishes include *nyeni*, traditional vegetables; *mukimo*, mashed banana with traditional vegetables or potatoes; *githere* or *muthere*, unfettered corn seeds cooked with beans or peas and traditional vegetables; *nyama cia gwakia*, roasted meat; *irio*, mashed dry beans, corn and potatoes; and *mutura*, a sausage made from goat intestines, meat and blood. Drinks and porridges are *ucuru*, also called *ukie* or *kirurio*, and *kimere*, a fermented porridge made from corn, millet or sorghum flour. Yams, cassava and sweet potatoes are eaten alongside these. Colonial-era cash crops — coffee, tea, *muguka* (khat) and macadamia nuts — are grown mainly for sale with little processed for domestic use.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Embu preserve an origin myth set at the Mwenendega grove in the interior of Embu, near Runyenjes town. Ngai created *Mwenendega* and gave him a wife at the Gogo River Salt Lick in Mukuuri — hence her name *Ciũrũnjĩ*, also given as *Nthara*. In the version recorded by the historian Mwaniki Kabeca, Mwenendega brought his cattle to drink at the salt lick and found a girl who at first refused to speak; when she did, she made him swear never to speak ill of her or abuse her, warning of consequences. Her parents were unknown, so she was held to have been sent by God. At a later ceremony Ndega broke his oath and reproached her; heavy rain fell and floods drowned the wealthy old couple, but their children survived and their descendants filled the land of Embu. A parallel naming tradition explains Mount Kenya as the seat of *Mwenenyaga* or *Mwenenjerũ*, the lord and owner of the snow, from *Nyaga* and *njerũ*, "white". Ciarunji Chesaina's *Oral Literature of the Embu and Mbeere* is the standing collection.

## Language & religion

*Kĩembu*, or Embu, is a Bantu language of the Niger-Congo family with two dialects, Embu proper and Mbeere (Mbere, Kimbeere); speakers near Mount Kenya are recognised by a further variety called *Kirũgũrũ*. Swahili and English serve as lingua francas. The Aembu hold Ngai, also called Murungu, to be a single invisible and transcendent God living in the sky — omnipotent creator of life on earth, merciful but distant — and regard the spirits of the departed as intercessors between the living and Ngai, remembered in family offerings and libations at individual altars. Most Aembu are Christians today across many denominations, including syncretic forms; the main schools were founded or sponsored by Catholic, Anglican, Salvation Army and Methodist missions. A minority practise Islam, arriving through Arab, Indian and Persian missionaries with East African trade.
