## Overview

The Kalabari are a sub-group of the Ijaw people of the eastern Niger Delta in Nigeria, originally known as the Awome. Their name derives from an ancestor, Perebo Kalabari, son of Meinowei, and their settlements have always been sited close to a river, because they held that their powers came from deities in the water. Elem Kalabari grew into a kingdom of roughly thirty-five settlements, among them Bakana, Abonnema, Buguma and Tombia. Before Portuguese ships reached the West African coast the people were fishermen; contact with Europeans made them, like other Nigerian coastline groups, wealthy. Kalabari is an Ijo language spoken in Rivers State and Bayelsa State, recorded at 258,000 speakers as of 2005. In folk-culture terms the Kalabari are known for cut-thread cloth, water-spirit masquerade and carved ancestral screens.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Pelete bite** — Imported machine-woven cotton cloth reworked by the Kalabari, who cut out sections of the warp threads to open a new design in the ground pattern. The British Museum holds several examples: one is an imported cotton with a warp-faced pattern of alternating blue and white warps divided by thin red lines and a blue weft, cut to make a repeating stepped pattern of diamonds; the museum keeps an uncut piece of the same imported pattern for comparison. The distinction from neighbouring textile traditions is that the Kalabari do not weave the cloth — they subtract from a finished trade cloth to make it theirs.

**Injiri** — A named cut design worked on an imported machine-woven cotton whose red, blue, brown and white warps and wefts formed a regular plaid chequered pattern; sections were cut away to create the design known as *injiri* (glossed in the catalogue as "wine glass").

**Motif vocabulary.** Named cut designs recorded on Kalabari cloths: *Glass Tail*, *Kiene Uncut*, *Sangolo*, *Wine Glass*, *Alu*, *Injiri*, *Cross* and *Draught*. The last two appear on a plaid of brown warps with yellow, purple and white wefts, which before cutting carried two panels of yellow and white grids on a purple ground bordered by check. The "Wine Glass" and "Alu" design was cut from a black-and-white cloth whose warps and wefts formed a white grid on a black background. Cloths in the collection are unhemmed.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe Kalabari dress chiefly through two nineteenth- or early twentieth-century photographs and through the masquerade head-dresses. In a portrait of Chief Amachree with five other men, three elderly men are seated and three younger men stand behind; the seated men wear tunics, wrappers and hats and carry walking sticks, one wearing shoes and one wearing neck ornaments and wrist ornaments, while the standing men wear shirts, jackets and wrappers. A second portrait shows Chief Wilbraid seated in an admiral's uniform with his feet raised on a platform, flanked by two elderly men in tunics and wrappers, hats and neck ornaments and holding walking sticks; behind them stand soldiers in wrappers, waistcoats, shirts and head-dresses, carrying breech-guns and shields. Ceremonial head-dress is treated below under dance. The sources used do not otherwise distinguish men's from women's daily dress, nor name garment types.

### Architecture

The sources used do not describe Kalabari house form, building materials or settlement layout, beyond the fact that settlements were sited close to a river and that the kingdom comprised about thirty-five towns, villages and fishing settlements — among them Abonnema, Buguma, Bakana, Kula, Ke, Soku, Ido, Ifoko, Obonoma, Sangama, Tombia and Usokun.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Kalabari pottery in the British Museum includes a globular vessel with a narrow neck and everted rim carrying a snake in relief on the upper body; a water vase of earthenware with incised decoration around rim and neck; and an earthenware cooking pot and bowl. Woodwork covers cylindrical openwork vessels with lids, and carved stools: one cylindrical stool has four panels of looped decoration and a human face in low relief; another carries a stylised bearded human face at the front with sub-cylindrical projections at the sides and a handle at the back; a third has a stylised head of a horned animal at the front with openwork angular loops at the sides and traces of yellowish pigment; a fourth a stylised standing man at the front with dentate ornaments at the sides. Several are recorded as heavily damaged by white ants.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used do not describe Kalabari jewelry, tattooing, hair practice or other body adornment as traditions. The only adornment they record is worn in the two photographic portraits, where seated elders wear neck ornaments and wrist ornaments with their tunics and wrappers, and one masquerade head-dress is set with glass mirrors and another with plastic buttons and a mirror at the front.

## Music & performance

The collection holds five Kalabari wooden drums, described only by their form: one heavy with a solid base, one with incised geometrical decoration on the body, two small, one undecorated. The sources used name no other instruments, no song genres and no performance occasions apart from the masquerade contexts described below.

## Dance & theatre

Masking is the best-documented Kalabari performance tradition in these records. Several wooden dance masks are catalogued as **Sekiapu** masks: one a stylised human face of semi-cylindrical form with three horn-like projections above, coloured pale red, with lizards in relief on the cheeks and tortoises on the brow; one of triangular form with rectangular projections above, blackened probably by soot; and two stylised human faces of sub-rectangular form with some cloth attached, in a natural greyish colour. An **Agiri** mask, recorded as used for *Owu*, is a carved wood face-mask encircled by a snake and surmounted by a bird, with bared teeth and protruding eyes. Animal masks include a naturalistic tortoise head mask painted in ochre, white, blue and brown; two masks called "son of tortoise", elongated human faces with high foreheads carrying a small carved tortoise; a hippopotamus face with pursed lips and blue-painted eyes, ears and lips; and a carved wooden crocodile masquerade head-dress with a slot on the underside. A distinct set of cardboard-and-wood head-dresses carry carved wooden fish or birds mounted on top — one fish painted green with red accents holding a smaller fish in its mouth, another red with white markings, a metal eye and gold cardboard discs; one bird painted red with black markings, a paper eye and feathers glued to its head — and are finished with mirrors, multicoloured feather dusters, ribbons and hanging cloth strips with ties. One head-dress carries a wooden plank across the top with a four-piece "claw" shape set with mirrors at one end and, at the centre, two tall curls and several human figures in boats.

## Festivals & rituals

Ritual objects dominate the Kalabari holdings. **Chi shrines** take the form of baskets of wood and split palm stem divided into compartments: a square one of sixteen compartments holding three pottery vessels and two pottery dishes, a rectangular one of twenty-eight compartments holding seventeen pottery vessels, and a square one of nine compartments. Ancestral screens are represented by separate carved figures — a central figure from a screen, a carved human head and stylised body pierced at the shoulders and towards the lower end with traces of red, white and black pigment, and a bird figure with the same pigment traces; one lot comprises five shrine figures, two carved human heads, two birds and a model tusk. Carved and painted wooden pillars and posts carry snakes and other animals in relief, one with three large and two small snakes on a flat background, and one post is itself a stylised human figure coloured red. Ritual equipment also includes a wooden paddle with a leaf-shaped blade, openwork handle end and incised cross-hatching, and a carved elephant tusk ending in a crocodile head, decorated with a human face, snake and cowrie-shell motifs. Marriage takes three forms — *Iya*, *Igwa* and *Waribiobesime* — of which *Iya* is the highest and most expensive; within it the rite of *bibife*, "buying the mouth", must be performed before the wife may eat in her husband's house, marking the man's undertaking to feed and care for her. The simplest recognised marriage is *Ari Ibara emi*, "she is with me".

## Foodways

Three dishes are named as the popular traditional foods of the Kalabari. *Onunu* is pounded yam with ripe plantain and palm oil. *Tominafulo* is made with fresh fish, prawns, periwinkle and oyster alongside other local ingredients. *Odo'fulo*, also called Native Soup, is made with fresh seafood and other local ingredients. The predominance of fish, prawn, periwinkle and oyster matches a people who were fishermen before European contact and whose settlements stand on the water. The sources used do not describe cooking methods, ceremonial food, drink traditions or dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used do not record Kalabari folktales, epic poetry, proverbs or storytelling occasions. What they do record is lexicographic and documentary work on the language: a Kalabari–English dictionary, a *Kalabari Children's Dictionary* by Pastor Daiwari Braide, and published orthographies for the Ibani and Okrika dialects prepared by Isaac Eyi Ngulube. Scholarly writing on Kalabari-area masquerade includes Anna Hlaváčová's studies of Ijo masquerade in the Niger Delta.

## Language & religion

Kalabari is an Ijo language of Nigeria, spoken south of Port Harcourt in Rivers State and Bayelsa State by the Awome people. Its three mutually intelligible dialects are Kalabari proper, Ibani (spoken southeast of Port Harcourt in the Bonny local government area and in Opobo) and Kirike (spoken in Port Harcourt and in the Okrika and Ogu–Bolo local government areas). Kalabari proper is among the best-documented varieties of Ijo and is the standard example of Ijo in linguistic literature. The language was reported endangered in 2005 because of the relocation caused by oil-industry development around Port Harcourt. Berbice Creole Dutch, recently extinct in eastern Guyana, was spoken by descendants of Kalabari speakers and its African element is predominantly Kalabari. The Kalabari located their power in water deities, and the *chi* shrines and ancestral screens above belong to that practice.
