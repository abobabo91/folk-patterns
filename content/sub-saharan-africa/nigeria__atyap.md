## Overview

The Atyap (*A̱tyap*; singular: *A̱tyotyap*) are an ethnic group found mainly in Zangon-Kataf, Kaura and Jema'a Local Government Areas of southern Kaduna State and Riyom of Plateau State, Nigeria. They speak Tyap, classified in the sources among the Central Plateau languages and also discussed within the Kwa and Benue-Congo language families. The Atyap occupy part of the Nok cultural complex in the upper Kaduna River valley, an area associated with terra-cotta figurines, iron-smelting sites and iron-ore mining pits. Their recorded history includes barter trade, long-distance commercial routes, relations with Hausa traders, resistance to the Zaria emirate, and opposition to British taxation and forced labour. The group includes several subgroups whose cultural and linguistic similarities do not erase their separate identities.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources mention woven mats, raffia mats, raffia fronds, red caps, clothes, bangles and necklaces in connection with trade.

### Architecture

 They mention the Zangon Katab market, the Hausa settlement known as the Zango, permanent marketplace land, trader residences, shallow caves and a rock shelter at Bakunkung Afang and Tswoɡ Fwuam.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Iron production is documented through smelting sites near Gan and nearby settlements. Archaeological remains include slag, tuyeres and furnaces, while iron-ore mining pits were identified in the area. Before currency was introduced, people took iron ore to blacksmiths to have tools made and paid in grains or meat. Trade records also mention pots, mortars and pestles, agricultural tools, swords, bangles and necklaces.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention bangles and necklaces as goods imported by Hausa traders and traded across the region.

## Music & performance

 They do record *tuk cyia̱ga̱vang*, identified as the Tyap term for tuwon shinkafa, but provide no musical information about it.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources mention the *A̱nak Festival*, during which the people were described as less ready for war when Kwassau attacked in 1899. No calendar date, ritual sequence or ceremonial practices are provided. The sources also describe the *A̱kabe* or Amana, an agreement between Atyap and Hausa communities concerning the safety of traders and their wares, land for a marketplace and settlement, and the appointment of officials to administer the arrangement.

## Foodways

The sources name grains, rice, honey, palm oil, ginger, locust bean cakes, goats, meat and tuwon shinkafa. Before the introduction of currencies, blacksmiths were paid in grains or meat. Atyap trade with neighbouring peoples included palm oil, ginger, locust bean cakes and honey, while Hausa traders obtained rice and honey from Atyapland. During Richard Lander's visit to Zangon Katab in 1827, the ruler gave him enough *tuk cyia̱ga̱vang*, identified as tuwon shinkafa, for at least 50 hungry men, together with a sheep and two bullock humps.

## Oral tradition & literature

Atyap traditions of origin include accounts of migration from the north, but the sources state that these versions contradict one another and that the tradition is unknown to most Atyap elders. The authenticity of the northern origin is described as questionable. The sources connect traditions of migration to avoidance of slavery and taxation, while distinguishing *Tyong A̱kpat*, “escape from the Hausa,” from *Tyong Kwasa̱u*, “escape from Kwassau.” The sources also state that there were no written records in an account by Human Rights Watch.

## Language & religion

Tyap is the language of the Atyap. The sources describe it as one of the Central Plateau languages and discuss its classification within the Kwa group of the Benue-Congo language family and the eastern Plateau. They state that the Kataf Group includes Tyap and that related groups include Gyong, Hyam, Duya and Ashe (Koro), which are little intelligible to Tyap speakers. The sources describe long-term dialect differentiation and cultural interaction in the region. They also state that Christian missionaries found support among Atyap people who had rejected the Moslem religion, while cautioning that not all Atyap people are Christians and not all Hausa people are Muslims. No script or detailed spiritual practices are documented.
