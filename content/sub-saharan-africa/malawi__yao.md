## Overview

The Yao are a Bantu ethnic group living at the southern end of Lake Malawi, with homelands in Malawi, Tanzania, and northern Mozambique. They are predominantly Muslim and number about two million. Their traditional homeland lies between the Rovuma and Lugenda Rivers in northern Mozambique, while many speakers live near the southeastern tip of Lake Malawi and along the Mozambican border. At least 450,000 Yao people live in Mozambique, where they largely occupy the eastern and northern parts of Niassa province and form about 40% of the population of Lichinga. Most Yao are subsistence farmers and fishermen, although historical livelihoods also included ivory and slave trading. Their folk-culture significance in the sources lies in farming, fishing, honey-hunting, Islamic practice, language, and resistance to colonial rule.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources mention that Arab traders exchanged clothes and weapons with the Yao.

### Architecture

The Yao adopted some elements of Swahili culture, including architecture, during their close relations with Swahili communities on the coast in the late 19th century.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources mention firearms obtained through cooperation with Arabs and weapons exchanged by Arab traders for ivory and grains.

## Music & performance

 They record a traditional *brrrr-hmm* call used by Yao honey-hunters in northern Mozambique to attract greater honeyguide birds. Hunters learn the call from their fathers and pass it on to their sons; a 2016 study reported that the chances of finding a beehive increased when hunters used it.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention “Dances of the Yao” as a related topic but provide no description of named dances, dramatic traditions, ceremonial distinctions, or entertainment contexts.

## Festivals & rituals

 They state that Islam reached the Yao gradually during the 19th century, that chiefs and village headmen tended to convert first, and that Makanjila III adopted Islam as his personal and court religion in 1870. The sources also describe Yao Islam as folk Islam syncretized with traditional, animistic belief systems.

## Foodways

The Yao are predominantly subsistence farmers and fishermen. Arab traders exchanged clothes and weapons for ivory and grains, and Yao people in Mozambique preserved subsistence agriculture despite the expansion of Portuguese plantation production. The sources also describe a honey-hunting practice: Yao hunters follow greater honeyguide birds to find honey, smoke bees from the beehive, collect the honey, and leave the wax for the birds, which consume it together with honeybee larvae.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They state that Yao chiefs employed Islamic teachers and scribes who provided literacy, and that the Yao speak Chiyao. The sources also identify Yohanna Barnaba Abdallah as a Yao linguist and historian and Shaaban bin Robert as a Tanzanian poet.

## Language & religion

Yao is a Bantu language spoken in Malawi, Mozambique, and Tanzania. It has an estimated 1,000,000 speakers in Malawi, 495,000 in Mozambique, and 492,000 in Tanzania. The main dialect in Malawi is Mangochi; the main dialects in Mozambique are Makale and Massaninga. Yao speakers in Tanzania also speak Swahili, those in Malawi speak Chichewa, and those in Mozambique speak Portuguese. Yao is an SVO language and an agglutinative Bantu language with noun classes, verbal inflection, and tonal distinctions. Its orthography differs among the three countries: Tanzania bases it on Swahili, while Malawi bases it on Chewa. The Yao are primarily Muslim, and Islam spread through chiefs, village headmen, Arab and Swahili traders, and Islamic teachers. Yao folk Islam is syncretized with traditional, animistic belief systems. Yao culture also associates illness with physical causes, curses, and violations of cultural taboos.
