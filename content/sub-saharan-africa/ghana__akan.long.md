## Overview

The Akan (*Akanfoɔ*) are a Kwa-speaking people of roughly 20 million living in the forest and forest–savanna transition belt of southern and central Ghana and the adjacent southeast of Côte d'Ivoire, between the Volta in the east and the Tano and Bia rivers in the west. Their subgroups — Asante, Fante, Akyem, Akwamu, Bono, Denkyira, Adanse, Akuapem, Kwahu, Sefwi, Wassa, Ahanta, Nzema, Anyi and Baoulé, among others — share matrilineal descent (*abusua*) for the inheritance of property and succession to office, so that all Akan are royal in status even where they hold no title. Their languages belong to the Tano branch of Niger–Congo. In folk-culture terms the Akan are among the most densely documented peoples in Africa: gold-weight sculpture, *kente* and *adinkra* cloth, stool regalia, terracotta funerary portraiture and a proverb tradition in which almost every object carries a sentence.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Kente** (*nwentoma*) — narrow-strip cloth woven on a men's double-heddle horizontal loom at Bonwire, Adanwomase and Wonoo near Kumasi, and at Agotime and Anlo in the Ewe borderland; strips 8–10 cm wide are cut and hand-sewn edge to edge so that warp stripe (*nsaduaso*) and weft-float block (*babadua*, *nkyimkyim*) align into a whole-cloth grid. Silk unravelled from imported European taffeta, later rayon and now cotton-viscose, gives the saturated yellow-red-green register. Every named design (*Oyokoman*, *Sika futuro*, *Emaa da*, *Toku kra ntoma*) is a titled composition with an owner and a proverb, not a generic plaid.

**Adweneasa** — "my skill is exhausted," the most demanding *kente*: every weft block across the whole cloth is filled with supplementary inlay figuring, leaving no plain ground. Reserved historically for the Asantehene and senior chiefs.

**Asasia** — the royal *kente* type woven in twill on a third heddle, producing a diagonal rib no commoner weaver was permitted to make; a handful of patterns exist and the Asantehene alone wears them.

**Nwomu** — the inlaid weft-float technique itself, the supplementary-thread "embroidery in the loom" that distinguishes patterned *kente* from plain striped *ahwepan*.

**Adinkra** — stamped cloth from Ntonso on the Kumasi–Mampong road. Calabash stamps (*adinkra aduru* is the dye: bark of *Bridelia ferruginea* boiled down with iron slag to a tarry black) are pressed in grids over cloth divided by comb-drawn bands. Worn chiefly for funerals and thanksgiving. **Kuntunkuni** (dark brown-black, deep mourning), **birisi** (russet-brown) and **kobene** (brick-red, for the bereaved closest to the dead) are the mourning grounds; white *adinkra* is for celebration.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Adinkrahene* — concentric rings, "chief of the adinkra symbols," greatness and leadership; *Gye Nyame* — "except for God," omnipotence; *Sankofa* — the backward-looking bird, retrieve what was left behind; *Duafe* — wooden comb, feminine care and beauty; *Nkyimkyim* — zigzag, the twists of a life's journey; *Nsoromma* — child of the heavens, star; *Akoma* — heart, patience; *Epa* — handcuffs, law and captivity; *Aya* — fern, endurance and independence; *Abusua pa* — the good matriclan; *Nyame dua* — God's tree, the altar post; *Funtunfunefu-denkyemfunefu* — the conjoined crocodiles sharing one stomach, unity in difference.

### Clothing & dress

Men wear a single large cloth, roughly 2 × 3.5 m, wrapped under the right arm and thrown over the left shoulder, leaving the right shoulder bare — the greeting-posture that requires a hand free. Chiefs add *ahenema* sandals (never barefoot on bare earth: the feet of an *ɔhene* must not touch the ground), a headband, and a battery of gold: *akrafokonmu* soul-washer's discs, cast rings, and anklets. Women wear two or three pieces — *ntama* wrapper, a second cloth over the shoulder or slung as a baby-carrier, and the tailored *kaba* blouse with *slit* underskirt for formal dress. Headties (*duku*) are elaborately knotted; in mourning they are worn low and dark. Colour is legible: *kuntunkuni* and *kobene* for funerals, white clay and white cloth for thanksgiving, purification, and for the mother of a newborn. Priests (*akomfoɔ*) wear raffia skirts and white clay body-marking; the *ɔkyeame* (spokesman) carries a gold-leafed staff (*akyeamepoma*) whose finial is a proverb in sculpture.

### Architecture

The classic Asante courtyard house (*abrono*) is a rectangle of four open-fronted rooms around a beaten-earth court, built in wattle-and-daub on a raised earth plinth with steep thatch hipped roofs; the front room is a raised open platform (*pato*) for receiving. Walls carry the *nkyinkyim*-derived low-relief plasterwork: bold spiral, arabesque and coiled-serpent motifs modelled in wet clay along the dado and painted in red ochre, white kaolin and black. Ten **Asante Traditional Buildings** around Kumasi — shrine houses at Besease, Asawase, Ejisu, Patakro, Bodwease and others — survive as the last examples and are inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list; the 1874 and 1900 British campaigns destroyed the palaces. Coastal Fante towns are dominated by *posuban*, the multi-storey concrete-and-stucco shrine-forts of the *asafo* military companies, painted in company colours and crowded with figurative sculpture — cannon, ships, lions, biblical scenes, Adam and Eve — each a boast against a rival company. Corrugated iron replaced thatch through the twentieth century; swish-and-mud construction survives in villages.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**Abrammuo** — brass gold-weights, cast by the lost-wax method in geometric series (based on Islamic *mithqal* and Portuguese *ounce* standards) and in figurative form: shields, drums, sandals, birds, scorpions, fish, human scenes staging a proverb. Tens of thousands survive; they were the working currency measure of a gold economy. **Kuduo** — lidded cast-brass vessels holding gold dust, beads and *nkrawoo* for the soul-washing rite and buried with their owner. **Forowa** — sheet-brass repoussé boxes with chased register bands, for shea butter. **Sika dwa kofi**, the Golden Stool, is the soul of the Asante nation; ordinary stools (*dwa*) are carved from a single block of *osese* wood with a curved seat over five pillars and a named central support — *mmaa dwa* is the women's form, *asipim* the brass-studded imported-form chair of office. Potting is women's work and hand-built without a wheel: burnished blackware cooking pots and the *nsodie* — terracotta commemorative heads with scarified cheeks, ringed necks and pursed lips, made at Ahinsan and Adanse for funerary groves.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Gold is the Akan medium: cast-gold *akrafokonmu* soul discs worn at the throat by the king's attendants, finger-rings in the form of birds, beetles, snails and clenched fists, hollow-cast pectorals, and gold-leaf-covered wood ornaments for sandals and staffs. *Bodom* and *aggrey* beads — some Venetian and Bohemian trade glass, some powder-glass — are strung as waist beads (*toma*) put on girls in infancy, and as the priest's *ahenewa*. Brass and gold *akuaba* dolls are rare; the wooden **akua'ba** with its flat disc face, high domed forehead and ringed neck is carried at the waist by a woman seeking conception and then kept as a shrine object. *Nsaa* amulet packets and Islamic-derived leather talismans came south with Dyula traders. Cicatrisation of the cheeks and temples is recorded on *nsodie* heads and in nineteenth-century accounts; white clay (*hyire*) marks purification, thanksgiving and priestly possession.

## Music & performance

Akan music is layered drum ensembles with bell and rattle timeline. **Fontomfrom** — the pair of huge open-ended barrel drums of the chief's court, played with curved sticks for *fontomfrom* royal dance and for war praise. **Atumpan** — the "talking drums," a tuned male/female pair whose slack and taut heads reproduce the tone-and-quantity pattern of Twi so that praise-poetry and proverbs are drummed as speech. **Mpintin**, **kete**, **adowa** and **sikyi** are further named ensembles, each with its own drum set, repertoire and dance. Melody instruments: *seperewa* (harp-lute, 6–14 strings over a calabash-and-skin body — the direct forebear of Ghanaian highlife guitar picking), *mmɛnson* (an ensemble of seven ivory or antelope-horn side-blown trumpets that "speak" the king's appellations), *donno* hourglass pressure-drum, *dawuro* iron clapperless bell, *frikyiwa* castanet, *ntorowa* gourd rattle. Vocal genres are gendered and occasion-bound: *nnwonkorɔ*, women's polyphonic entertainment song with commentary on marriage and morals; *ɔsoode* and *nsuae* funerary song; *abofoo* hunters' songs; and *adwera*, the dirge-poetry sung by women at funerals and analysed by Kwabena Nketia as the core Akan literary genre. Twentieth-century highlife, palm-wine guitar and the church *ebibindwom* (Fante lyric hymnody) all grow from this base.

## Dance & theatre

**Adowa**, the most widespread Akan dance, is performed by both sexes at funerals and celebrations; the dancer's hands speak — a raised index finger, cupped palms, a hand drawn across the throat each carry an utterable sentence, so that adowa is read as well as watched. **Fontomfrom** (*akantam*) is the chief's own dance, broad and martial, danced to the big drums with a sword or gun; **kete** is court dance of restrained elegance; **sikyi** and **apatampa** are young people's flirtation dances; **atsiagbekor**-influenced war dances appear in the Ewe borderland. **Asafo** companies of the Fante coast parade with flags (*frankaa*) whose appliqué scenes are insults aimed at rival companies, and drill in formations descended from seventeenth-century militia. Masking is not central to Akan performance as it is among their northern and eastern neighbours; the display genres are regalia procession, *akwasidae* durbar and the *ɔkyeame*'s oratory. **Ananse akuamoa** — dramatised Ananse storytelling with sung *mmoguo* interludes into which the audience breaks — became the base for the Ghanaian "concert party" travelling theatre of the 1930s–70s.

## Festivals & rituals

The Akan calendar is the *adaduanan*, a 42-day cycle of six seven-day weeks, punctuating the year with **Akwasidae** (the Sunday *adae*) and **Awukudae** (the Wednesday *adae*), on which the chief sits in state, blackened ancestral stools are fed with mashed yam and sheep's blood in the stool-house (*nkonguafieso*), and drummers recite the dynastic roll. **Odwira**, the Asante and Akuapem purification and first-fruits festival, falls in September–October: the ban on new yam is lifted, the state's shrines and stools are cleansed, and the dead are fed before the living may eat. The Fante **Bakatue** at Elmina (first Tuesday of July) opens the Benya lagoon fishing season; **Aboakyir** at Winneba (first Saturday of May) sends two *asafo* companies to catch a live bushbuck bare-handed for the god Penkye Otu; **Fetu Afahye** at Cape Coast, **Kundum** among the Ahanta and Nzema, **Papa Nantwi** at Techiman, and **Apoɔ** at Techiman and Wenchi — a licensed-insult rite in which commoners sing the faults of chiefs — complete the cycle. Life-cycle: *outdooring* (*abadinto*) on the eighth day, when the child is named and given water and liquor to teach truth-telling; puberty seclusion for girls (*bragoro*), ending in public presentation with clay and beads; marriage by *tiri nsa*, the head-drink presented to the bride's *abusua*; and the funeral — the largest of all Akan events, with *kobene* cloth, dirges, *adowa*, and a final rite after which the deceased may be installed as a blackened stool.

## Foodways

Starch is the meal and soup is the accompaniment. **Fufu** — cassava and plantain (or cocoyam) boiled and pounded together in a wooden mortar to a smooth elastic ball — is the Akan dish, swallowed in pinched pieces with soup: *nkatenkwan* (groundnut), *abenkwan* (palm-nut, from the pounded pericarp of *Elaeis guineensis*), *abunuabunu* (green cocoyam-leaf), light tomato soup with smoked fish or snail. **Ampesi** is plain boiled yam, plantain and cocoyam with *kontomire* stew of cocoyam leaves, melon seed and palm oil. **Ɛtɔ** — mashed yam or plantain with palm oil and egg — is ritual food: given at outdoorings, puberty rites, and offered on the ancestral stools. **Kenkey** (*dokono*) and **banku** are fermented-maize doughs, boiled and eaten with pepper and fried fish on the coast. *Aprapransa*, toasted corn flour stirred into palm soup with crab, is Fante festival food; *mpotompoto* a soft cocoyam pottage for children and the sick. Palm wine (*nsafufuo*) and distilled *akpeteshie* are poured as libation before any drinking, and *nsa* (drink) is the word for the bride-price itself. Kola (*bese*) — grown in the forest and traded north across the Sahara since Begho's fourteenth-century heyday — is chewed, given in greeting and presented in formal petitions.

## Oral tradition & literature

The central narrative cycle is **Anansesɛm**, the Ananse stories: the spider trickster, small and greedy and endlessly clever, who steals all the world's wisdom in a pot and drops it; who wins the sky-god Nyankopon's stories; who explains why hens scratch and why the moon is far away. Told at night with call-and-response openings and interrupted by *mmoguo* — sung audience interludes that need not relate to the plot — the genre gave its name to storytelling itself, so that any tale is *anansesɛm* whether the spider is in it. **Ɛbɛ** (proverb) is the higher register: proverbs govern chiefly oratory, and the *ɔkyeame* speaks almost entirely in them; they are carried on staff finials, gold-weights, stools and *adinkra* stamps, so that material culture is a proverb lexicon. **Apae** is the drummed and declaimed praise-poetry of the stool, reciting the king's ancestry; **adwera** the funeral dirge, a women's poetic form. J. B. Danquah's *The Akan Doctrine of God* (1944) and R. S. Rattray's *Akan-Ashanti Folk-Tales* (1930) are the early written collections; Kwabena Nketia's dirge and drum-language studies and Kwesi Yankah's work on proverb speech are the scholarly foundations.

## Language & religion

Akan is a Tano language of the Kwa branch of Niger–Congo, written in a Latin orthography with ɛ and ɔ and marked tone; the chief literary dialects are **Asante Twi**, **Akuapem Twi** (the Bible dialect) and **Fante**, mutually intelligible and collectively a lingua franca for some 20 million speakers, with Anyi–Baoulé and Nzema–Ahanta at the western edge. Notably, Akan's Kwa affiliation — unrelated to the languages of northern Ghana apart from Gonja — was Peter and Ama Shinnie's decisive argument against the colonial-era theories of an Akan migration from the Sahara, North Africa or the Near East advanced by Bowdich, Balmer, Danquah and Meyerowitz, and dismantled by David Tait and Dennis Warren's fieldwork at Techiman. Religion is now largely Christian (Methodist, Presbyterian, Catholic, and a very large Pentecostal/charismatic sector) with a Muslim minority, overlaying an intact indigenous cosmology: *Nyame* / *Onyankopɔn* the sky-god, *Asaase Yaa* the earth, the *abosom* (river and forest deities — Tano, Bona, Bosomtwe) served by possession-priests, the *nsamanfo* ancestors resident in blackened stools, and the person composed of matrilineal *mogya* (blood), patrilineal *ntoro*, and *ɔkra*, the soul which is washed in the *akrafokonmu* rite.
