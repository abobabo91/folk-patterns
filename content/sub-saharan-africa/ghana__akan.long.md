## Overview

The Akan are a cluster of closely related peoples occupying the forest and coastal belt of southern Ghana and adjacent south-eastern Côte d'Ivoire, unified less by a single polity than by a shared language continuum, a shared matrilineal descent system, and a shared political grammar of stools and paramountcies. Their language, Akan, comprises the mutually intelligible Twi and Fante literary dialects and belongs to the Central Tano group of the Kwa languages within Niger–Congo; Asante Twi, Akuapem Twi and Fante are all standardised in writing, while Bono, Akyem, Akwamu, Kwahu, Wassa, Ahanta, Nzema-adjacent and Baoulé-Anyi varieties extend the continuum westward across the Ivorian frontier. Subgroups conventionally named include the Asante, Fante, Akyem, Akuapem, Akwamu, Bono, Kwahu, Assin, Denkyira, Adansi, Sefwi, Ahafo and Wassa, with the Baoulé and Anyi of Côte d'Ivoire as western Akan branches. Population estimates place the wider Akan-speaking world at some twenty to twenty-five million, making them one of the largest ethnolinguistic blocs in West Africa and, in Ghana, a plurality of roughly half the national population. Akan history in the second millennium CE turns on gold and on the forest: early states such as Bono Manso and Begho grew on the northern trade toward the Niger bend, Denkyira and Akwamu consolidated in the seventeenth century, and from 1701 the Asante confederacy under Osei Tutu and the priest Okomfo Anokye rose to dominate the region, its unity symbolised by the *sika dwa kofi* — the Golden Stool said to have descended from the sky, containing the *sunsum* or soul of the nation and never sat upon. Asante's long collision with British power ran through five wars to the 1900 Yaa Asantewaa war and the exile of Prempeh I, and the Asantehene's restored office remains one of Africa's most consequential traditional institutions. The Akan contribution to world material culture is disproportionate for their numbers: *kente* strip-woven silk and rayon cloth, now a global emblem of African identity; *adinkra* stamped cloth with its lexicon of proverb-glyphs; lost-wax brass goldweights and the *kuduo* vessels that accompanied them; the carved wooden stool as portable throne and soul-seat; and the *akua'ba* fertility figure, whose disc-faced abstraction entered the twentieth-century Western canon of sculptural form.

## Material culture

Akan material culture is organised around chieftaincy and around gold. The objects that carry the greatest technical investment — woven cloth, cast brass and gold ornament, carved and blackened stools, linguist staffs and umbrella finials — are regalia, made to be displayed on a chief's body or carried in his procession, and their meanings are generally proverbial rather than merely decorative. A second register of domestic and funerary objects — pots, terracotta heads, combs, dolls — shares the same visual vocabulary at smaller scale.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The two great Akan textile traditions are complementary: *kente*, a narrow-strip loom weave assembled into a large wrapper, and *adinkra*, a stamped or printed surface applied to a plain ground cloth. Both encode named designs, and in both the name is a proverb, a historical allusion or a moral statement rather than a description of the shape.

- **Kente** (*nwentoma*) — Narrow strips of about 10 cm woven on a double-heddle horizontal loom, then hand-sewn edge to edge into a large rectangular wrapper; the weaving centres of Bonwire, Adanwomase and Wonoo near Kumasi are the Asante heartland, with Agotime-Kpetoe among the neighbouring Ewe. Named patterns run into the hundreds — *Oyokoman*, *Sika futuro*, *Emaa da*, *Nkyimkyim* — and are historically rank-restricted.
- **Adweneasa** — The most prestigious kente class, meaning roughly "my skill is exhausted" or "my ideas are finished": every weft block in every strip carries a different supplementary-weft design, so that no square repeats across the whole cloth. Historically reserved for royalty.
- **Nwomu** — The supplementary-weft float technique itself, in which extra weft threads are laid in over selected warps to build the geometric blocks that read as pattern; distinguished from the plain warp-striped ground on which they sit.
- **Asasia** — A rare royal kente woven in a twill structure on a loom with additional heddles, producing a diagonal rather than plain-weave ground; traditionally the Asantehene's exclusive prerogative and commonly said to be the most technically demanding weave.
- **Adinkra** — Cotton cloth stamped with carved calabash blocks using *adinkra aduro*, a thick black dye boiled down from the bark and roots of the *badie* tree with iron slag; the cloth is divided into panels by comb-drawn lines and each panel filled with repeated glyphs. Centred on Ntonso in Asante. Associated above all with mourning.
- **Adinkra colour codes** (*kuntunkuni*, *birisi*, *kobene*) — Mourning grounds dyed to grade of grief and relation: *kuntunkuni* a deep brown-black from the *kuntunkuni* root, *birisi* a dark blue-black, *kobene* a red-brown worn by close relatives of the deceased; white or light *fancy* adinkra is worn for thanksgiving and festival instead.
- **Adinkra symbols** (*adinkrahene*, *gye nyame*, *sankofa*, *duafe*) — A corpus of several hundred glyphs, each with a name and attached proverb: *adinkrahene*, concentric circles, "chief of the adinkra symbols", greatness and leadership; *gye nyame*, "except for God", the supremacy of *Nyame* and the most widely reproduced of all; *sankofa*, a backward-turning bird, "go back and fetch it", the propriety of retrieving what has been left behind; *duafe*, the wooden comb, feminine care and desirable qualities.

### Clothing & dress

Akan dress is fundamentally the draped rectangle rather than the tailored garment, and its grammar is one of proportion, colour choice and the manner of wrapping, all of which are legible statements about status and occasion.

- **Men's toga wrap** (*ntoma*) — A large cloth of roughly two by three metres worn wrapped around the waist and thrown over the left shoulder, leaving the right shoulder and arm bare; the right shoulder is lowered as a gesture of respect before a chief or elder.
- **Women's two- or three-piece** (*ntama ne kaba*) — A wrapper skirt worn with a fitted blouse (*kaba*), often with a second cloth used as a shoulder wrap or for carrying an infant on the back, and a head-tie (*duku*).
- **Mourning cloth** (*ayitoma*) — Red, russet, brown or black cloth worn to funerals, with the darkest grounds and *kobene* red marking closest kin; the widespread Akan preference for black-and-red funeral dress remains strongly observed.
- **Chiefly sandals** (*ahenema*) — Leather-soled sandals with ornamented straps, often gilded; a chief's feet should not touch bare earth, and specific motifs on the straps are themselves rank markers.
- **Royal regalia in procession** — A chief appears in adweneasa or asasia kente under a great state umbrella (*kyinie*) with a carved finial, attended by sword-bearers, stool-carriers and the linguist with his staff.

### Architecture

Traditional Akan building is earth architecture: wattle-and-daub or swish walls on a timber frame, raised platforms and steeply pitched thatch, organised as rooms around an open courtyard. Because the material is perishable in a high-rainfall forest environment, almost nothing pre-colonial survives, and the one substantial exception is protected as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

- **Courtyard compound** (*adan*) — Four or more rectangular rooms set around an open rectangular courtyard, several open-fronted onto it as verandas, housing a matrilineal household under its senior woman and elder.
- **Asante traditional buildings** — Ten surviving shrine houses in the Kumasi hinterland, inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1980 as the last material testimony to Asante civilisation; their walls carry bold low-relief bas-relief arabesque and spiral motifs in mud plaster beneath steep thatch.
- **Shrine house** (*abosomfie*) — The typical plan of four rooms around a courtyard, one raised and screened as the resting place of the *obosom*, one for drumming, one for cooking and one for storage.
- **Posuban shrine** (*posuban*) — Elaborate multi-storey cement-and-plaster military shrines built by Fante *asafo* companies in coastal towns such as Elmina, Anomabo and Mankessim, crowded with brightly painted figurative sculpture — ships, lions, officers, biblical scenes — asserting each company's history and rivalry. A distinctively coastal, distinctively modern form, largely twentieth-century.
- **Palace architecture** (*ahemfie*) — The Manhyia Palace at Kumasi, built 1925 for the returning Prempeh I and now a museum, marks the transition from earth-and-thatch royal compounds to colonial-era masonry.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Brass and gold casting by the lost-wax method is the technical summit of Akan craft, and the weighing of gold dust as currency generated one of Africa's largest bodies of small-scale figurative sculpture. Pottery, by contrast, is a women's craft using hand-building rather than the wheel.

- **Goldweights** (*abrammuo*) — Thousands of small brass weights cast by lost wax to a calibrated system for weighing gold dust, in two broad classes: geometric weights with adinkra-like surface patterning, and figurative weights depicting animals, tools, people and proverb scenes. In use from roughly the fifteenth century until currency reform in the later nineteenth.
- **Kuduo** — Cast brass lidded vessels, often with figurative lid groups and bands of engraved and repoussé ornament, used to hold gold dust, beads and valuables, employed in *kra* soul-washing rites and sometimes buried with their owners; early forms show clear debt to Islamic trans-Saharan metalwork.
- **Forowa** — Sheet-brass containers built from hammered and riveted plates with embossed and chased decoration, lighter and more common than the cast *kuduo*, used to keep shea butter, ointments and gold dust.
- **Golden Stool** (*sika dwa kofi*) — The Asante nation's palladium: a gold-adorned stool held never to be sat on and to contain the nation's soul; the 1900 British demand for it precipitated the Yaa Asantewaa war. Blackened ancestral stools (*nkonnwafieso*) of past rulers are kept in a stool house and fed offerings.
- **Asipim stool** (*asipim*) — A leather-and-wood chair of European derivation studded with brass tacks and fitted with cast brass finials, used as a chief's seat of state alongside the older carved wooden stool forms.
- **Women's stool** (*mmaa dwa*) — Smaller carved stools whose central support columns carry distinct named designs — the crescent-moon *srafo dwa*, the *kotoko* porcupine and others — given to girls at puberty and to women at marriage, and central to female life-cycle rites.
- **Terracotta funerary heads** (*nsodie*) — Hand-modelled clay commemorative heads and half-figures, made largely by women potters, placed at sacred groves near cemeteries to receive offerings for the dead; concentrated historically in the Adanse, Twifo and Fante areas and in the Anyi–Baoulé west.
- **Akua'ba** (*akua'ba*) — Carved wooden figures with a large flattened disc head, high forehead, small features and a ringed cylindrical neck, carried at the waist by women hoping to conceive or to bear a beautiful child; the name is glossed as "Akua's child", after a woman who conceived after carrying one.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Gold is the definitive Akan adornment, worked in cast, repoussé and gold-leaf-over-wood techniques, and its display on the body is directly regulated by office. Beads, both imported glass and local, carry ritual functions that gold does not.

- **Soul-washer's badge** (*akrafokonmu*) — A large repoussé gold or gilt disc with radiating and concentric ornament, worn on the chest by the *okra*, an attendant whose soul is bound to the chief's and who performs the *kra* cleansing rites; among the most recognisable Akan gold forms.
- **Cast gold ornaments** (*sanbra*) — Rings, pectorals, hair ornaments and pendants cast by lost wax, often figurative and proverbial, worn in profusion by chiefs and queen mothers on state occasions.
- **Gold-leaf regalia** (*abosodee*) — Carved wooden ornaments and staff, sword and umbrella finials sheathed in beaten gold leaf, allowing large emblematic sculpture to be carried in procession at manageable weight; the linguist's staff (*akyeampoma*) finial is the classic instance.
- **Beads** (*ahene, bota*) — Glass trade beads and *bodom* beads worn in strands at the neck, wrist, waist and knee; waist beads are worn by women from girlhood, and specific bead types appear in puberty rites, naming ceremonies and funerary dress.
- **Body marking and hairdressing** — Cosmetic white clay and *hyire* markings are applied for ritual and mourning states, and named coiffures, including the shaved and patterned styles worn by mourners and by court attendants, signal role and condition.

## Music & performance

Akan music is drum-centred and speech-linked to an unusual degree, since the language's tonal system permits instruments to reproduce utterance directly. The court ensembles are simultaneously musical and archival: the talking drums and horns of a chief's household transmit appellations, praise names and dynastic history, and a drummer's opening invocation to the forest, the mahogany tree and the dawn is a fixed text as much as a performance.

- **Fontomfrom** (*fontomfrom*) — Pair of tall carved royal drums played with curved sticks, associated with Asante court and with the dance of the same name; capable of speech-surrogate praise texts.
- **Atumpan** (*atumpan*) — The paired talking drums, one male and one lower-pitched female, whose two pitches and rhythms render Twi speech tone for tone; used to summon, announce and recite appellations.
- **Ivory trumpets** (*ntahera*, *mmenson*) — Side-blown elephant-tusk horn ensembles, the seven-horn *mmenson* set being a chiefly prerogative, which similarly render proverbs and praise phrases in tone.
- **Seperewa** (*seperewa*) — Akan harp-lute with a calabash or wooden resonator and typically ten to fourteen strings, historically a court instrument accompanying praise song and today undergoing a documented revival.
- **Adowa and Nnwonkoro ensembles** — Drum-and-idiophone ensembles using *donno* hourglass drums, *dawuro* iron bells and gourd rattles; *nnwonkoro* is a women's song genre of commentary, complaint and praise, often performed in call-and-response by a female chorus.

## Dance & theatre

Akan dance is closely tied to occasion and to rank, with the most codified forms belonging to funerals and to court. Gesture matters as much as step: the hands and the orientation of the body carry statements, so that a chief dancing before his people is understood to be speaking.

- **Adowa** (*adowa*) — The best-known Akan funeral and social dance, danced by both sexes with small measured steps and an elaborate vocabulary of hand signs conveying proverbs, questions and states of feeling; named for the antelope whose movement it is said to echo.
- **Kete** (*kete*) — A court dance suite in several named sections performed by chiefs and their retinue, more stately and more restricted than adowa.
- **Fontomfrom / Akom** — *Fontomfrom* is a martial, weighty royal dance to the great drums; *akom* is the possession dance of *akomfo* priests, in which the *obosom* is understood to mount and speak through the dancer.
- **Asafo companies** (*asafo*) — Fante militia companies whose parades combine drumming, dance, flag-dancing with appliqué banners and display at the *posuban* shrine; competitive and satirical in register.
- **Concert party** (*concert party*) — A twentieth-century Ghanaian travelling popular theatre of comic sketches, cross-dressed stock characters and highlife music, performed largely in Twi and Fante and carrying Ananse-style moral plotting into the modern commercial stage.

## Festivals & rituals

Akan ritual time runs on the *adaduanan*, a forty-two-day cycle of six seven-day weeks, on which the principal ancestral observances fall. The purpose of the major festivals is the feeding and purification of stools, ancestors and *abosom*, and the public reconstitution of the chief's authority.

- **Akwasidae and Awukudae** (*adae*) — The ancestral rites closing each forty-two-day cycle, one falling on a Sunday and one on a Wednesday, in which blackened stools are fed with mashed yam and drink; Akwasidae at Manhyia in Kumasi is the great public durbar of the Asante calendar.
- **Odwira** (*odwira*) — The Akuapem, Akyem and formerly Asante annual purification festival: a period of ban on yam-eating and on drumming, followed by cleansing of stools, the yam-eating rite, and a final procession of the paramount chief and sub-chiefs in full regalia.
- **Aboakyir** (*aboakyir*) — The Effutu deer hunt festival at Winneba, in which two asafo companies compete bare-handed to capture a live antelope for presentation to the chief; among the most widely attended coastal festivals.
- **Bakatue and Fetu Afahye** — Coastal Fante festivals at Elmina and Cape Coast marking the opening of the lagoon fishing season and the purification of the town, combining canoe regatta, asafo display and durbar.
- **Life-cycle rites** (*abadinto*, *bragoro*) — The eighth-day outdooring and naming (*abadinto*), at which the child receives a day-name from the day of birth; *bragoro*, the girls' puberty rite involving seclusion, ritual bathing and public seating on a stool, now much reduced but still performed in some communities. Funerals remain the largest and most costly Akan ceremony, with printed cloth, brass band, and multi-day wake.

## Foodways

Akan cooking is a forest cuisine of starchy staples pounded or boiled into a swallowed mass, eaten with a soup or stew whose base is palm fruit, groundnut or garden-egg, and whose seasoning depends on fermented fish, smoked fish, and chilli. The signature technique is pounding in a wooden mortar with a long pestle, and the signature meal structure is a single dense starch portion submerged in soup, eaten by hand from a shared bowl.

- **Fufu** (*fufuo*) — Cassava and plantain, or cocoyam, boiled and pounded to a smooth elastic mass, served in soup; the definitive Akan meal and the reference point against which other staples are ranked.
- **Palm nut and groundnut soups** (*abenkwan*, *nkatenkwan*) — *Abenkwan*, palm fruit pulped and simmered to an orange soup, and *nkatenkwan*, groundnut paste soup, both typically with smoked fish, snails, goat or bushmeat; *nkrakra* light soup is the tomato-based third member.
- **Kenkey and banku** (*dokono*, *banku*) — Fermented maize dough, steamed in plantain or maize leaves as *dokono* (Fante kenkey) or cooked stirred as *banku*, eaten with fried fish and ground pepper; the fermentation is the distinguishing character.
- **Ampesi and kontomire** (*ampesi*, *kontomire*) — Boiled yam, plantain or cocoyam served with a cocoyam-leaf stew of palm oil, egusi melon seed and fish, one of the oldest and least commercialised of Akan dishes.
- **Palm wine and akpeteshie** (*nsafufuo*, *akpeteshie*) — Tapped palm sap drunk fresh or fermented, and the distilled spirit *akpeteshie*; both are poured as libation to ancestors and *abosom* before any formal gathering, a ritual obligation as much as a refreshment.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Akan oral canon is organised around the proverb, and the proverb is the connective tissue between speech, cloth, gold and drum: a goldweight, an adinkra stamp, a staff finial and a drum phrase may all cite the same saying, so that Akan material culture functions as a visible literature. Formal speech is a specialist art, mediated at court by the *okyeame*, conventionally translated "linguist" or spokesperson, who receives a chief's words and re-delivers them in heightened, proverb-laden form while holding a gold-leafed staff whose finial announces the message of the office. Beyond the court, the dominant narrative genre is *Anansesem* — "spider stories" — a cycle of tales about *Kwaku Ananse*, a trickster spider whose greed, cleverness and repeated overreach explain the origins of things and puncture the pretensions of the powerful; the cycle travelled with the Atlantic slave trade and survives as Anansi in the Caribbean and as Aunt Nancy in parts of the American South, making it one of the most widely diffused African narrative traditions. Alongside these sit *ebe* (proverbs), *aboadwe* riddles, dirges (*nsuae* and funeral *kwadwom*) sung by specialists at royal and family funerals, drum appellations that function as dynastic chronicle, and day-name and praise-name systems (*Kwame*, *Akosua*, and the accompanying *mmerane*) that carry an individual's character and lineage. The great documentary tradition on this material begins with R. S. Rattray's Asante ethnographies of the 1910s–1930s and continues through J. B. Danquah and the philosopher Kwasi Wiredu, whose work treats Akan proverb and concept language as a formal philosophical corpus rather than folklore.

## Language & religion

Akan is a Central Tano language of the Kwa branch of Niger–Congo, tonal, with a lexicon and grammar shaped by extensive verbal serialisation and a productive system of nominal prefixes; Asante Twi, Akuapem Twi and Fante are separately standardised orthographies of a single continuum, and Akuapem Twi served as the language of the nineteenth-century Basel Mission Bible translation, giving it an outsized literary role. Akan is a major lingua franca in Ghana well beyond ethnic Akan speakers, and the Baoulé and Anyi of Côte d'Ivoire represent its westward extension. Religiously the Akan are today overwhelmingly Christian, with Methodist, Presbyterian, Catholic and a very large Pentecostal-charismatic presence, and a Muslim minority principally in the northern and urban Asante zones. Beneath and alongside this, Akan religion remains a functioning cosmology rather than a historical memory: *Nyame* (also *Onyankopon*, *Odomankoma*) is the remote creator, approached indirectly; *Asase Yaa* is the earth, to whom Thursday is owed and on whom no farming is done that day; the *abosom* are lesser deities, many associated with rivers such as Tano and Bosomtwe, served by *akomfo* priests through possession dance and divination; the *nsamanfo* ancestors are the immediate object of ritual attention, fed at every Adae through the blackened stools. The person is composed of plural elements — *okra* or *kra*, the soul bound to a day and to *Nyame*; *sunsum*, personality or spirit; *mogya*, the blood inherited from the mother and the basis of the matrilineal *abusua*; and *ntoro*, transmitted from the father — and this anthropology underwrites both the matrilineal descent system and the *kra* purification rites at which the *akrafokonmu* is worn. The moral ideal *abusua pa*, "a good lineage", names the reputation a matriclan holds and defends, and it is one of the concepts adinkra cloth is used to assert.
