## Overview

The Betsimisaraka live along Madagascar’s eastern coast. They are divided into northern Betsimisaraka, or Antavaratra, and southern Betsimisaraka, or Antatsimo. Their cultural life includes ancestor worship, tromba spirit possession, zebu sacrifice, raffia clothing, funeral customs, taboos and music such as basesa and valse. Rice farming, fishing, forest gathering and communal ceremonies are important parts of life.

## Material culture

Raffia fiber, basketry, wood, bamboo, paper, metal and animal products shape much of Betsimisaraka material culture.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Raffia was combed, made into strands and woven into clothing and household goods.

- **Raffia cloth** (*raffia*) — Raffia leaves were combed, knotted end to end into strands and woven into cloth.
- **Raffia weaving** — Women separated raffia fibers, formed strands and wove them on wooden and cane looms.
- **Basketry** — Basketry was used for hats, baskets, eating mats, a baby cover and a handbag.
- **Betsimisaraka fabric** — A 1922.13.0006 textile from Tamatave was made of raffia fiber.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional dress centered on raffia clothing for women and men.

- **Short wrapper** (*simbo*) — Women traditionally wore a short raffia wrapper called a simbo.
- **Bandeau top** (*akanjo*) — Women generally wore an akanjo bandeau top with the simbo.
- **Raffia smock** (*raffia smocks*) — Men traditionally wore smocks made from raffia.
- **Mourning dress** — Women unbraided their hair and stopped wearing their akanjo, while men no longer wore a hat.
- **Raffia hat** — Hats were made from raffia or basketry.

The sources show a bamboo-and-paper hut model and a house inauguration custom.

- **Model hut** — A museum catalogue includes a model hut made of bamboo and paper.
- **House inauguration** — The inauguration of a newly constructed house is a communal celebration.

## Music & performance

Basesa and valse are important musical forms, while tromba ceremonies use music and spirit possession.

- **Basesa** — Basesa is closely associated with tromba and is performed on the accordion.
- **Kaiamba shakers** (*kaiamba*) — Kaiamba shakers accentuate the rhythm of traditional basesa for tromba ceremonies.
- **Valse** — Valse is a Malagasy interpretation of European seafarers’ waltzes performed on accordion.
- **Tromba** — Tromba is a widespread ritual of spirit possession with men and women acting as mediums and spectators.

## Dance & theatre

Basesa dance uses side-held arms and heavy foot movements, with newer influences from Reunion Island.

- **Traditional basesa dance** (*basesa*) — The traditional dance uses arm movements held to the sides and heavy foot movements.
- **Sega-influenced dance** (*sega*) — Contemporary basesa dance has been influenced by sega music from Reunion Island.
- **Kwassa kwassa-influenced dance** (*kwassa kwassa*) — Contemporary basesa dance has been influenced by kwassa kwassa music from Reunion Island.

## Festivals & rituals

Communal rituals follow the agricultural year and mark birth, marriage, death, houses and major public celebrations.

- **Circumcision** (*sambatra*) — Sambatra is a major custom involving circumcision.
- **Tenth-child rite** (*folanaka*) — Folanaka is associated with the birth of a tenth child.
- **Reburial ceremony** (*famadihana*) — Some Betsimisaraka practice famadihana, a simpler reburial ceremony.
- **Birth house** (*komby*) — During a woman’s first childbirth, she is secluded in a special birthing house called a komby.
- **Bath of the leaves** (*ranom-boahangy*) — On the fifteenth day, the mother and baby are bathed in water soaked with lime or lemon leaves in a ritual called ranom-boahangy.

## Foodways

Rice, crops, fishing, forest foods, homemade drinks and food taboos shape Betsimisaraka foodways.

- **Sugarcane beer** (*betsa*) — Betsa is homemade sugarcane beer.
- **Rum** (*toaka*) — Toaka is homemade rum.
- **Sacred eel** — Among many Betsimisaraka, eel is sacred and must not be touched, fished or eaten.
- **First-childbirth diet** (*saonjo*) — During the first-childbirth ritual, the mother is restricted to saonjo greens and a specially prepared chicken.
- **River fishing** — Betsimisaraka catch and sell river crabs, shrimp and fish.

## Oral tradition & literature

Folktales and taboos focus especially on lemurs and crocodiles.

- **Lemur ancestor stories** — Stories describe lemurs assisting prominent Betsimisaraka figures or frightening enemies who mistake their sounds for human voices.
- **Lemur taboo** — The spirits of Betsimisaraka ancestors are believed to reside in lemurs, so it is generally forbidden to kill or eat them.
- **Crocodile offerings** — Villagers may offer crocodiles zebu hindquarters, geese and other food at river banks.
- **Crocodile amulets** — Amulets for protection may be worn or thrown into the water.
- **Ibonia** — Ibonia is a national epic in the broader Malagasy tradition.

## Language & religion

Betsimisaraka speak Malagasy dialects and combine ancestor worship, traditional spirits and, in some communities, Christian practice.

- **Northern Betsimisaraka** (*Antavaratra*) — Northern Betsimisaraka is closer to Tsimihety.
- **Southern Betsimisaraka** (*Antatsimo*) — Southern Betsimisaraka is more similar to Antambahoaka.
- **Traditional officiant** (*tangalamena*) — Traditional rites are presided over by a tangalamena.
- **Ghosts** (*angatra*) — Angatra are ghosts recognized by Betsimisaraka communities.
- **Mermaids** (*zazavavy an-drano*) — Zazavavy an-drano are mermaids recognized by Betsimisaraka communities.
