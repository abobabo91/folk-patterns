## Overview

The Balanta are the second largest ethnic group in Guinea-Bissau. Their social life is shaped by farming, initiation rites, music and family-based communities. They speak two related Bak languages with several dialects. Indigenous spiritual practices are followed alongside Islam and Catholic Christianity.

## Material culture

Balanta material culture includes ceremonial clothing, pottery, palm-leaf and fibre objects, wooden objects and farming tools.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The supplied sources do not document Balanta textile traditions or named patterns.

### Clothing & dress

Ceremonial clothing is especially linked to Fanado initiation.

- **Fanado initiation costume** (*Fanado*) — Young Balanta men wear a costume that includes a hollowed tortoise shell on the back during Fanado.
- **Brave and wise red hat** (*Lante Ndang*) — After initiation, a man wears a bright red hat to show that he has become Lante Ndang, or brave and wise.
- **Horned man’s hat** — Museum records include a man’s hat with two horns made from wood and palm leaf.
- **Men’s arm-bands** — Museum records include men’s arm-bands made from vegetable fibre and wood saplings.

### Architecture

Balanta houses are normally built according to clans.

- **Clan-based house** — Balanta houses are normally built based on clans.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Balanta everyday objects include pottery, palm-leaf tools, fibre baskets and a rice-farming tool.

- **Water-pot** — Museum records include pottery water-pots, including one with a decorated neck.
- **Palm-wine pot** — A pottery vessel is used for water or palm wine.
- **Palm-wine funnel** — A funnel made from palm leaf is used for palm wine.
- **Fibre basket** — Museum records include a basket made from vegetable fibre.
- **Soil-preparing tool** (*kebinde*) — The kebinde is an agricultural tool used to prepare soil for planting, usually rice.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources do not document Balanta jewelry or named adornment traditions.

- **Fibre arm-bands** — Men’s arm-bands are made from vegetable fibre and wood saplings, but their ritual use is not described.

## Music & performance

Balanta music includes the kusunde gourd lute and a large 24-layer balafon.

- **Gourd lute** (*kusunde*) — The kusunde is a gourd lute with three strings arranged in an unusual order.
- **Balafon** — The Balanta balafon has 24 layers, compared with 12 or 14 in the Mandingue community.
- **Harvest celebration music** (*Kussundé*) — After the harvest, the Kussundé includes dances in which non-initiated men compete.

## Dance & theatre

Balanta wedding dancing combines courtship, courage and spirit masks.

- **Wedding dance** — In a Balanta wedding dance, young women compete through beauty and skilled dancing for a prince’s hand.
- **Cow-stealing challenge** — A Balanta man must steal a cow to prove his courage to his future wife.
- **Large spirit mask** — A large spirit mask covered with cowry shells is used when the community gathers for music and dance.
- **Old woman mask** — An old woman mask represents family and spiritual connection.
- **Young people masks** — Young girl and young man masks are used with the other masks in community music and dance.

## Festivals & rituals

Fanado is the most important Balanta initiation and takes place every four years.

- **Childhood age group** (*Nwatch*) — From early childhood to age 15, a child belongs to the Nwatch category.
- **Young adult stage** (*Fuur*) — Around age 18 to 20, a person enters the Fuur stage.
- **Adult stage** (*Nghaye*) — Around age 25, a person enters the Nghaye stage.
- **Marriage-authorising rite** (*Kgness*) — Around age 30, the rites of Kgness authorise a man to take a woman.
- **Major initiation** (*Fanado*) — Fanado lasts two months in sacred woods and includes circumcision, hut building, endurance tests and adult training.

## Foodways

Balanta foodways center on farming, fishing, herding, cashews, rice and palm wine.

- **Cashew apple wine** (*Cadjou*) — The Balanta make an apple wine called Cadjou from cashews.
- **Cashew farming** — Cashew culture is a specialty, and exporting cashew nuts to India is important in Balanta villages.
- **Rice farming** — Rice is one of the main crops and is supported by intricate irrigation systems.
- **Mixed-water irrigation** — Balanta irrigation systems mix salt and fresh water to maintain soil nutrients and achieve good yields.
- **Cattle sacrifice** — Cattle sacrifice is important at initiation, marriage and death.

## Oral tradition & literature

Balanta oral traditions describe an ancient migration from the east and identify Nhacra as the homeland.

- **Migration from the east** — One tradition says the Balanta migrated westward from the region of present-day Egypt, Sudan and Ethiopia to escape conflict.
- **Sudan origin tradition** — Another tradition says that the Balanta originated in Sudan.
- **Nhacra homeland** (*Nhacra*) — The Balanta call the region between the Rios Mansôa and Geba Nhacra and say they migrated there from somewhere in the east long ago.

## Language & religion

Balanta life combines related Bak languages with indigenous spiritual customs, Islam and Catholic Christianity.

- **Balanta-Kentohe** — Balanta-Kentohe is spoken in Guinea-Bissau and The Gambia and has northern Kəntɔhɛ and southern Fora dialects.
- **Balanta-Ganja** — Balanta-Ganja is spoken in Senegal and has Fganja and Fjaalib dialects.
- **Spirit worship** — Balanta spiritual customs use practices and traditions to communicate with an Almighty believed to be far away.
- **Djon Cago worship** (*Djon Cago*) — Djon Cago is worshipped through necromancy and sacrifice.
- **Islam alongside spirit worship** — Islam is more prevalent than Christianity and is often practiced alongside Balanta spirit worship.
