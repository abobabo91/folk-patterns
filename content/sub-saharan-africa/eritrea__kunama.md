## Overview

The Kunama are an ethnic group native to Eritrea and northern Ethiopia. Most of the estimated 260,000 Kunama live in the remote area between the Gash and Setit rivers near the border with Ethiopia. They constitute 4% of Eritrea’s population and are one of its smallest ethnic communities. The Kunama speak Kunama, a language included in the proposed wider Nilo-Saharan language family. Formerly nomadic, they are now mainly settled agriculturalists and pastoralists who raise cattle. Their matriarchal and matrilineal social organization gives women a prominent role, and membership in Kunama society follows the mother’s lineage.

## Music & performance

The Kunama language has appeared in publications, including a translation of the Gospel of Mark first published in 1906.

## Festivals & rituals

They report that the Kunama traditionally worshipped a god called Anna and were ruled by a community of elders. Although some Kunama still practice traditional beliefs, most have adopted Islam or Christianity.

## Foodways

The Kunama live in the fertile plains of the Gash-Setit, also known as the Gash-Barka region, which are sometimes called the “breadbasket of Eritrea.” They are settled agriculturalists and pastoralists who live mainly from cattle. Historically, many Kunama were hunters and gatherers and also practiced hoe-farming; as agricultural land became scarce, some took up pastoralism.

## Oral tradition & literature

The earliest written mention of the Kunama comes from Ya'qubi, writing around 872 AD, whose account was based on travelers’ reports. In local oral traditions, the Abyssinians of Wolkait, Adiyabo and Seraye are remembered for raids against the Kunama. The documentary film *Home Across Lands* follows Kunama refugees resettled in the United States and their attempts to adapt to life in their new home.

## Language & religion

Kunama is spoken by the Kunama people of the Gash-Barka Region in western Eritrea and just across the Ethiopian border. It belongs to the Kunama language family and is included within the proposed wider Nilo-Saharan language family, although it is distantly related to the other languages, if at all. Its dialects include Barka, Marda, Aimara, Odasa, Tika, Lakatakura, Sokodasa, Takazze-Setit and Tigray. Ilit and Bitama are not mutually intelligible and may be considered distinct languages. In 1985, the Eritrean People’s Liberation Front decided to use the Latin script for all non-Semitic languages in Eritrea, including Kunama. Most Kunama have adopted Islam or Christianity; a minority practices Roman Catholicism. Conversion to Christianity and Islam took place in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.
