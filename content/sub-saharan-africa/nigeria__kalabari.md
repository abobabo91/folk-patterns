## Overview

The Kalabari are an Ijaw people of the eastern Niger Delta who were originally fishermen and became wealthy through contact with European traders. They settled in about thirty-five river towns and believed their power came from water deities. They are best known for reworking imported cloth by cutting patterns into it, for elaborate masquerade traditions, and for carved ritual objects.

## Material culture

Kalabari material culture centers on textiles, carved wood objects, and ritual items, shaped by their connection to the river and trade with Europeans.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Kalabari cut designs into imported machine-woven cloth to make it their own, creating named patterns with geometric forms.

- **Cut-thread cloth** (*pelete bite*) — Machine-woven cotton cloth imported from Europe, reworked by cutting out sections of warp threads to create new geometric designs.
- **Wine glass design** (*injiri*) — A named cut design worked on imported cloth with red, blue, brown and white pattern, created by cutting away sections.
- **Glass Tail design** (*Glass Tail*) — One of the named cut patterns found on Kalabari cloths in museum collections.

### Clothing & dress

Men wore tunics, wrappers, hats and carried walking sticks, with variation by age and rank shown in historical photographs.

- **Tunic and wrapper** — Worn by seated elders in nineteenth-century portraits, often paired with hats and walking sticks.
- **Admiral's uniform** — Worn by Chief Wilbraid in formal dress, reflecting Kalabari engagement in trade and governance.
- **Ceremonial head-dress** — Elaborate masks and decorated head-dresses used in masquerade performance, some set with mirrors, buttons and cloth.

### Architecture

Kalabari settlements were always built close to rivers, reflecting their belief that power came from water deities.

- **Elem Kalabari kingdom** — A kingdom of roughly thirty-five settlements including Bakana, Abonnema, Buguma, Tombia, and Usokun, all sited on or near rivers.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Kalabari made pottery vessels and carved wooden objects including stools, vessels and decorative items.

- **Pottery vessels** — Earthenware globular vessels with narrow necks, water vases with incised decoration, and cooking pots.
- **Carved wooden stools** — Cylindrical stools featuring human or animal faces, openwork loops, and sometimes traces of yellow pigment or damage from insects.
- **Cylindrical wooden vessels** — Openwork containers with lids, serving as everyday objects or possibly ritual use.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Kalabari elders wore neck and wrist ornaments; masquerade head-dresses were decorated with mirrors, buttons, glass and feathers.

- **Neck ornaments** — Worn by seated elders in formal dress, marking status and rank.
- **Masquerade decoration** — Head-dresses finished with mirrors, glass discs, multicoloured feather dusters, ribbons and hanging cloth strips.

## Music & performance

Kalabari used wooden drums in performance, though detailed musical traditions are not documented in available sources.

- **Wooden drums** — Five drums recorded in museum collections, varying in size and decoration, some with incised geometric patterns.

## Dance & theatre

Masquerade is the main documented performance tradition, with elaborate carved masks and head-dresses representing spirits and animals.

- **Sekiapu mask** (*Sekiapu*) — A stylised human face mask, sometimes with horn-like projections, painted pale red and decorated with lizards and tortoises in relief.
- **Agiri mask** (*Agiri*) — A carved wood face-mask encircled by a snake and topped with a bird, used for the rite called Owu.
- **Animal masks** — Naturalistic masks of tortoises, hippopotamus, and crocodile, as well as stylised 'son of tortoise' masks with high foreheads.
- **Fish and bird head-dresses** — Cardboard-and-wood structures with carved fish or birds mounted on top, decorated with mirrors, feathers, ribbons and painted details.

## Festivals & rituals

Kalabari ritual life centers on chi shrines, ancestral screens, and three forms of marriage reflecting social rank and obligation.

- **Chi shrine** (*chi*) — Wooden baskets divided into compartments, holding pottery vessels and dishes, used in worship of water deities.
- **Ancestral screen** — Carved wooden figures of humans and birds, painted with red, white and black, representing ancestors in ritual practice.
- **Carved ritual posts** — Wooden pillars decorated with snakes and other animals in relief, or carved as human figures.
- **Iya marriage** (*Iya*) — The highest and most expensive form of marriage, requiring the rite of bibife before the wife may eat in her husband's house.
- **Buying the mouth** (*bibife*) — A marriage rite marking the husband's undertaking to feed and care for his wife.

## Foodways

Traditional Kalabari food centers on pounded yam and dishes made with fish, prawns, periwinkle and oyster from the water.

- **Pounded yam and plantain** (*onunu*) — Pounded yam with ripe plantain and palm oil, a popular traditional dish.
- **Fish and seafood stew** (*tominafulo*) — Made with fresh fish, prawns, periwinkle, oyster and other local ingredients.
- **Native Soup** (*odo'fulo*) — Made with fresh seafood and other local ingredients, reflecting the Kalabari fishing heritage.

## Oral tradition & literature

Recorded Kalabari oral traditions focus on language documentation rather than folktales or epic poetry.

- **Kalabari-English dictionary** — Lexicographic documentation of the Kalabari language.
- **Children's Dictionary** — Kalabari Children's Dictionary compiled by Pastor Daiwari Braide.
- **Dialect orthographies** — Orthographies for the Ibani and Okrika dialects prepared by Isaac Eyi Ngulube.

## Language & religion

Kalabari is an Ijo language with three mutually intelligible dialects; religious practice centers on water deities and ancestral veneration.

- **Kalabari language** (*Kalabari*) — An Ijo language spoken in Rivers State and Bayelsa State by about 258,000 speakers, among the best-documented varieties of Ijo.
- **Ibani dialect** (*Ibani*) — A mutually intelligible dialect spoken southeast of Port Harcourt in the Bonny local government area and in Opobo.
- **Water deities** — Kalabari believed their power came from deities in the water, reflected in chi shrines and ancestral screens.

## Glossary

- *pelete bite* — Cut-thread cloth; imported machine-woven cotton reworked by cutting out warp threads
- *injiri* — A named cut design on imported cloth, glossed as 'wine glass'
- *Sekiapu* — A type of stylised human face mask used in masquerade performance
- *Agiri* — A carved wood face-mask encircled by snake and topped with bird
- *chi* — Shrine in wooden basket form, holding pottery vessels for worship of water deities
- *Iya* — The highest and most expensive form of marriage
- *bibife* — 'Buying the mouth' — marriage rite marking husband's obligation to feed wife
- *onunu* — Pounded yam with ripe plantain and palm oil
- *tominafulo* — Dish made with fresh fish, prawns, periwinkle and oyster
- *odo'fulo* — Native Soup made with fresh seafood and local ingredients
- *Owu* — A rite associated with the Agiri mask
- *Kalabari* — An Ijo language with three mutually intelligible dialects; also the people who speak it
- *Ibani* — One of three dialects of Kalabari, spoken in Bonny area and Opobo
- *Elem Kalabari* — The main Kalabari kingdom comprising about thirty-five settlements
- *Ijaw* — The larger ethnic group of which Kalabari are a sub-group
- *Niger Delta* — The region in Nigeria where Kalabari settlements are located
