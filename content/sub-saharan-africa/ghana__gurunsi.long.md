## Overview

The Gurunsi — also written Grunshi — are not a single people but a set of related ethnic groups spread across northern Ghana and southern and central Burkina Faso. They occupy an arc of village communities running from the Koudougou area west and northwest of Ouagadougou to the northeast of present-day Ghana, settlements that were mostly organised as defensive strongholds. Their languages belong to the Gur family, and the name itself is an outsider's label: the Mossi kingdoms, to whose states these communities were historically subject, used *Gurunsi* as an umbrella term for peoples who share neither a common language nor common cultural practice. Oral tradition holds that they came from the western Sudan by way of the Sahel, and they are thought to have been in their present lands by 1100 AD. In folk-culture terms they matter as a cluster of stubbornly autonomous village societies — never fully subjugated by Mossi horsemen who raided them for slaves after the 15th century — whose surviving material record runs to painted animal masks, brass anklets, fibre-and-leather aprons and hide quivers of poisoned arrows.

The largest of the groups classed as Gurunsi are the **Frafra**, **Nabt** and **Talensi** in Ghana and the **Ko**, **Lyele**, **Nuna** and **Sisaala** in Burkina Faso; the **Kassena** and **Nankani** live on both sides of the border.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources document a small number of specific pattern-bearing garments rather than named weaving traditions.

**Fibre aprons** — Aprons worked in vegetable fibre using twined, braided and plain techniques, one of them embroidered with strips of black and white leather; another combines vegetable fibre with leather. The patterning is structural, arising from the twining and the contrasting leather insertions rather than from applied dye.

**Woman's front apron** — A hand-spun, hand-woven cotton garment with a narrow woven band along the top and a hanging fringe of cotton woven in red and blue vertical stripes.

**Motif vocabulary.** Chevron and angle patterns, striated triangles and applied circular bosses on cast brass; incised squares and diamonds on carved wood, filled alternately with black pigment and left bare so the pattern reads as a checker; horizontal rows of stitched cowries on braided fibre; red and blue vertical stripes in woven cotton.

### Clothing & dress

Headgear is the best-documented element of Gurunsi dress in the objects shown here, and it spans the everyday and the display piece. One hat is basketry, made from coiled leaf strip, with a plume of white hair set in the centre. Another is a monkey-skin cap fitted with a leather ornament of buttons and projecting 'horns' at the front, a metal chain pendant hanging at the side, a skin chin strap and a cloth lining. Body garments in the collection are aprons — the fibre-and-leather types described above and the striped hand-woven cotton front apron worn by women. A braided vegetable-fibre band, catalogued uncertainly as either a leglet or a head-band, is covered in horizontal rows of stitched cowrie shells with white feathers massed at the front, and belongs to the same register of feather-and-shell display as the plumed basketry hat. The sources used do not describe footwear, belts, or a distinct ceremonial costume beyond these pieces.

### Architecture

The Kassena are known for clay houses whose construction and decoration are divided by gender: the men build the houses, and the women decorate the exteriors with geometric patterns. Beyond this, what the sources record of the built environment is settlement form rather than house form — Gurunsi villages across the arc from Koudougou to northeastern Ghana were mostly organised as defensive strongholds, a pattern consistent with centuries of Mossi slave-raiding from the north and with the fierce local resistance that met Djerma warlords and the three colonial armies in the 1890s. The sources used do not describe roofing, materials other than clay, or the internal plan of the compound.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Archery equipment dominates the everyday material record. Quivers are made of hide, bound with vegetable fibre and slung from plaited leather carrying straps, one with a leather ring attached; they hold poisoned arrows in sets of five, eight and sixteen, sometimes bundled together in fibre cloth. The arrows combine cane or reed shafts, iron heads and vegetable-fibre bindings. Bows are made of bamboo or wood and strung with cane or with hide; one wooden bow has its cane string ornamented with bands of leaf and string binding. Two wooden whistles are recorded: one with a terminal mouthpiece and strips of metal attached, the other a tubular instrument with a wide angled mouth-hole, an openwork triangular form pierced below it, a suspension hole at the lower end, incised line decoration and a series of small notches cut around the outer edge of the mouth-hole. The sources used do not cover Gurunsi ceramics.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Cast brass anklets are the signature ornament in this collection. They are penannular — open rings — with two flaring triangular terminals, and their surfaces carry applied circular bosses, striated triangles, and chevron and angle patterns, with a further protruding circular boss at the centre back. A matching pair survives, indicating they were worn as a set. The braided fibre band sewn with horizontal courses of cowrie shells and fronted with white feathers is the other documented adornment; the same use of feathers appears in the white plume set into the coiled-leaf basketry hat. The monkey-skin cap's metal chain pendant, hanging at the side of the head, and its leather button ornament extend the same vocabulary of metal, skin and shell. The sources used do not cover tattooing, scarification or hair practices.

## Music & performance

The only instruments in the record are the two carved wooden whistles described above — one with metal strips attached to the body, one a notched tubular whistle with a suspension hole so it could be carried on a cord. The sources used do not name Gurunsi song genres, ensembles or performance occasions.

## Dance & theatre

Masking is clearly central, and the surviving masks are overwhelmingly zoomorphic. Several take the form of an animal head: a semicircular crown with two long ears projecting backwards and a long snout thrust forward, a serrated ridge carved down the centre between round eyes, the sides of the head incised with squares and the muzzle with diamonds, each field alternately blackened and left bare, the ears painted red and open jaws showing carved teeth. A variant of the same form is studded with metal and has a metal band wrapped around the left ear. A large mask identified as a bull carries tall yellow-painted horns over a black face striped in red and white, an open snout with white teeth, and mirrors set as eyes, bordered with metal plates at each side of the jaw. Another animal mask has crocodile jaws, is painted white, red and black, and hangs a fibre fringe below the face. Fibre rope threaded through holes at the base shows how the masks were tied on the wearer. The sources used do not name the dances these masks were worn for.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not cover the Gurunsi festival calendar or life-cycle rites, though they record one ritual belief tied to the group's name: during the late 19th-century Djerma invasions the jihadist leader Baba Ato Zato — better known by the Hausa corruption of his name, Babatu — recruited a battalion of local men who, after consuming traditional medicines, were said to be invulnerable to iron.

## Oral tradition & literature

Gurunsi oral tradition carries the group's migration history: that they came out of the western Sudan and passed through the Sahel to reach their present lands, though the tradition does not date the movement. The group's own name is itself an etymological tradition recorded by Salif Titamba Lankoande in *Noms de famille (Patronymes) au Burkina Faso*, who derives *Gurunsi* from the Djerma words *guru-si*, "iron does not penetrate" — the phrase attached to Babatu's iron-proof battalion and to the Zabarima Emirate the Djerma established within Gurunsiland. The sources used do not record Gurunsi folktales, epics, proverbs or riddles.

## Language & religion

The *Grũsi* or Gurunsi languages, also called the East Mabia languages, form a branch of Gur comprising roughly twenty languages spoken in northern Ghana, adjacent parts of Burkina Faso, and Togo. They divide into an Eastern group (Lukpa, Kabiyé, Tem, Lama, Delo, Bago-Kusuntu, Chala), a Northern group (Lyélé, Nuni, Kalamsé, Pana, Kasem) and a Western group (Winyé, Deg, Phuie, Paasaal–Sisaala, Chakali, Siti, Tampulma, Vagla). The largest is Kabiye, spoken by about 1.2 million people in central Togo, 550,000 of them as a first language. Kleinewillinghöfer (2002) notes Kwa influence on the western Southeastern Gurunsi languages Cala and Dulo, while the eastern members Bago and Kusuntu show less outside influence but some Yoruboid. The sources used do not describe the current religious landscape.
