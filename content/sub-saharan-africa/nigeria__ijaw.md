## Overview

The Izon people, also known as the Ijaw people, are an ethnic group found mainly in the Niger Delta in Nigeria, with significant population clusters in Bayelsa, Delta, and Rivers. They are also found in Ondo State, Edo State, Akwa Ibom State, Abia State, and Ogun State. Migrant fishermen live in camps as far west as Sierra Leone and as far east as Gabon. Population figures are placed at just over 4 million, accounting for 1.8% of the Nigerian population. The Ijaws have long lived near sea trade routes and were connected to other areas by trade as early as the 15th century. Their folk culture, as described by the sources, is closely associated with fishing, farming, river environments, water-spirit beliefs, masquerades, foodways, and maritime exchange.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

 They do describe ceremonial masquerades connected with celebrations honoring water spirits. Men wear elaborate outfits and carved masks while dancing to the beat of drums. Particularly spectacular masqueraders are understood to be possessed by the particular spirits on whose behalf they dance.

## Architecture

 They mention villages and communities, including Opia and Ikiyan, but provide no architectural description.

## Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They do, however, identify several objects connected with traditional occupations and maritime life. Kin-based trading lineages developed into corporations known as “houses,” each with an elected leader and a fleet of war canoes used to protect trade and fight rivals. The sources also mention wooden boats and canoes, fishing equipment, timber, and palm kernels. No named ceramic, metal, woodworking, or household forms are recorded.

## Music & performance

The sources describe music and performance primarily in connection with water-spirit celebrations and masquerades. Each year, the Ijaw hold celebrations honoring the spirits that last for several days. At the center of these festivities are masquerades in which men wearing elaborate outfits and carved masks dance to the beat of drums. The dancing manifests the influence of water spirits through its quality and intensity. Particularly spectacular masqueraders are taken to be in the possession of the spirits on whose behalf they dance.

The sources also describe singing and dancing in the context of the Ijaw Youth Movement’s “Operation Climate Change.” On the morning of December 30, 1998, two thousand young people processed through Yenagoa dressed in black, singing and dancing. The Ijaw language article records that translations of poetry and *The Call of the River Nun* by Gabriel Okara were underway, and that the Izon Fie instructional book and audio CDs were launched in June 2013.

## Dance & theatre

The named performance tradition in the sources is the Ijaw masquerade. Men wearing elaborate outfits and carved masks dance during celebrations honoring water spirits. The performance is religiously significant: the dancers are understood to manifest the influence of the spirits, and especially spectacular masqueraders are taken to be possessed by particular spirits.

The sources also describe public procession, singing, and dancing during “Operation Climate Change,” a campaign of celebration, prayer, and direct action organized by the Ijaw Youth Movement beginning December 28, 1998.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe annual celebrations honoring water spirits. These celebrations last for several days and include masquerades, elaborate outfits, carved masks, dancing, and drums. Traditional religious practices center on “Water spirits” in the Niger river and on tribute to ancestors. Veneration of ancestors plays a central role in Ijaw traditional religion.

Marriage is completed through payment of a bridal dowry. The dowry increases when the bride comes from another village, compensating that village for the loss of her children. Funeral ceremonies, particularly for people who have accumulated wealth and respect, are often described as very dramatic.

The sources also describe *Igbadai*, a form of divination in which recently deceased individuals are interrogated about the causes of their death. They state that humans dwell among water spirits before birth and that prayer maintains the living in the good graces of those spirits. No coming-of-age ceremony or detailed birth rite is recorded. “Operation Climate Change,” beginning December 28, 1998, was a modern campaign involving celebration, prayer, direct action, singing, dancing, and public procession.

## Foodways

Ijaw foodways described in the sources center on fish, seafood, yams, plantains, cassava, rice, palm oil, and other crops. The Ijaw live by fishing supplemented by farming paddy-rice, plantains, cassava, yams, cocoyams, bananas, vegetables, guava, mangoes, and pineapples. Smoke-dried fish, timber, palm oil, and palm kernels are processed for export.

Named foods include *Polofiyai*, a soup made with yams and palm oil; *Kekefiyai*, a pottage of chopped unripened plantains, fish, other seafood or game meat, and palm oil; and fried or roasted fish and plantain. *Gbe* is the grub of the raffia-palm tree beetle, eaten raw, dried, fried in groundnut oil, or pickled in palm oil. Kalabari “sea-harvest” *fulo* is a mixed seafood soup or stew eaten with foofoo, rice, or yams.

*Owafiya* is a beans pottage made with beans, palm oil, fish or bushmeat, yam or plantain, and processed cassava or starch. *Geisha Soup* is prepared from geisha fish, pepper, salt, and water. *Opuru-fulou*, also called prawn soup, includes prawn, Ogbono, dried fish, table salt, crayfish, onions, fresh pepper, and red palm oil. *Onunu* is made with pounded yams and boiled overripe plantains and is mostly enjoyed by the Okrikans.

Other recorded foods are *Kiri-igina*, prepared without cooking on fire with Ogbono, dried fish, table salt, and crayfish; *Ignabeni*, a watery soup made with yam or plantain, teabush leaves, pepper, goat meat, and fish; *Pilo-garri*, a Bille meal of dry garri, red palm oil, and salt eaten with roasted seafood; *igbugbai fiyai*, a soup without oil containing fish, onion, periwinkle, bush leaves, and other seafood; and *kpanfaranran*, or *fry fiyai*, prepared by frying palm oil before adding fish, meat, crayfish, periwinkle, and other seafood.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They do record a scholarly bibliography concerning Ijọ proverbs and a lexicostatistic classification of Ijọ dialects. The language article also notes translations of poetry and *The Call of the River Nun* by Gabriel Okara.

Gabriel Okara is identified as a poet and novelist, while J.P. Clark is identified as a poet and playwright. The Izon Fie instructional book and audio CDs were launched in June 2013 at a ceremony attended by officials of the government of Bayelsa State. The Niger Delta University was working to expand the range of books available in the Ijo language.

## Language & religion

The Ijaw languages are spoken by over 14 million Ijaw people in Nigeria. They are associated with the Niger-Congo family, although the sources record a scholarly debate about whether the Ijoid languages should instead be considered an independent family. The Ijoid languages are classified into Eastern Ijo, Western Ijo, and Central Ijo, with Central Izon (Ijaw) described as the most common. Another source describes nine closely related Niger-Congo languages belonging to the Ijoid branch. The languages are not mutually intelligible, so Ijaws use English or Pidgin language in pan-Ijaw gatherings.

The Ijaw are primarily Christians, with 65% professing Christianity; Roman Catholicism, Zion Church, Anglicanism, and Pentecostalism are identified as prevalent varieties. Traditional religion remains important. Water spirits known as *Owuamapu* figure prominently in the pantheon, and *Egbesu* is described as a deity of justice whose totems are the leopard, panther, and lion. The sources also record a small number of converts to Islam.
