## Overview

The Krobo are an ethnic group in Ghana and part of the Ga-Adangbe ethnolinguistic group. They are the largest of the seven Dangme ethnic groups and the largest of the Ga-Adangme-speaking peoples. The Krobo occupy the Accra Plains, Akuapem Mountains and Afram Basin, and originate from Ghana’s Eastern Region. They are divided into the Manya and Yilo groups, administered as separate traditional states. The Krobo are a farming people whose historical importance also rests on commercial export crop production and beadmaking. Their cultural centre was Krobo Mountain, which served as a settlement, religious centre and site of significant rituals until their eviction by the colonial government in 1892.

### Architecture

Krobo Mountain contained stone houses, including structures several stories high with up to 20–30 rooms. Visiting missionaries considered the architecture unprecedented in their experience in Africa. The settlement also had an innovative water system that supported its growing population. As the mountain’s capacity became insufficient, surrounding settlements were established.

The mountain remained the Krobo religious and cultural centre until the eviction. Ancestors were buried in family homes there, making the mountain both a spiritual and literal ancestral home. In 1892, the colonial government ordered the population to leave and soldiers destroyed houses, shrines, pots and ancient trees.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources mention pots among the possessions destroyed during the forced evacuation from Krobo Mountain. They also mention houses, shrines and ancient trees, but provide no further description of Krobo ceramics, metalwork, wooden objects, tools or household goods.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources identify beadmaking as an important Krobo activity and state that the people became known for their role in it.

## Festivals & rituals

The Krobo cultural and ritual centre was Krobo Mountain. Girls undergoing the *Dipo* rite of passage traditionally spent one to three years on the mountain completing their customs. The Djemli, or priests, were forbidden to leave the mountain overnight. The mountain also served as an ancestral home because ancestors were buried in family homes there.

After the Dangme people separated into seven groups, the Krobo settled on a mountain later named “Klo yo,” or Krobo Mountain. Two hunters, Aklo Muase, also called Aklo Natebi, and Madja, were sent by priests to assess whether it was suitable for settlement.

In 1892, Governor Griffiths enacted the Native Customs Ordinance to remove the Krobo from the mountain. Residents were given three days to evacuate, and soldiers destroyed possessions that people could not carry away. This forced evacuation remains a significant historical trauma. The Manya and Yilo Krobo communities commemorate it through annual pilgrimages during their Ngmayem and Kloyosikplem festivals.

## Foodways

 They state that the Yilo underwent acculturation rites after returning from Krobo Denkyera, including instruction about foods taboo to the Krobo. The expression *wa yilɔ*, meaning “we don’t eat this,” became associated with the Yilo because they repeatedly asked whether various Akan foods they had learned to eat were acceptable.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe oral traditions concerning the division between Manya and Yilo Krobo. The two traditional areas were originally known as “Nɔwe” and “Nyέwe.” *Manya* is derived from *maonya*, meaning “keep your mouth shut,” associated with the saying *nɔ bi nya me tee*, meaning “one does not need to talk about everything one sees.” *Yilo* comes from *wa yilɔ*, meaning “we don’t eat this.”

The Dangme people are said to have travelled as one group before separating into seven groups. The place of separation was named “Lɔlɔvɔ,” an Ewe term meaning “love is finished/ended,” and is now known as the Tagologo Plains.

## Language & religion

The Krobo are part of the Ga-Adangbe ethnolinguistic group and are among the Ga-Adangme-speaking peoples. Their traditional areas were associated with the names “Nɔwe” and “Nyέwe,” while the names Manya and Yilo have explanations connected with Krobo expressions and oral traditions.

Krobo Mountain was their religious and cultural centre. Priests, known as the Djemli, were associated with the mountain and were forbidden to leave it overnight. The colonial government labelled the mountain a “fetish mountain” and claimed that its seclusion enabled warrior cults and violations of colonial laws.
