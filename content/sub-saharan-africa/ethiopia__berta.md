## Overview

The Berta, also known as the Bertha, Benishangul, Bela Shangul, Shangul, or Funj, are an ethnic group living along the border of Sudan and Ethiopia’s Benishangul-Gumuz Region. The Ethiopian-Berta population in Ethiopia is 208,759, while Sudanese-Bertas number around 180,000. They speak Berta, a Nilo-Saharan language not related to the languages of their Nilo-Saharan neighbors, Gumuz and Uduk. Their folk culture includes distinctive wedding music, ritual specialists, rain-making ceremonies, scarification, sorghum foodways, ceramic vessels, raised architecture, and cooperative working parties.

## Material culture

### Architecture

Berta settlement history is associated with naturally defended hills and mountains, rocky outcrops, and villages established partly to protect communities from slave raids coming from Sudan. Because of the harsh topography, houses and granaries were raised over stone pillars. German traveler Ernst Marno described Shangul architecture and villages in *Reisen im Gebiete des Blauen und Weissen Nil*, published in Vienna in 1874. After conflicts and raids receded during the 20th century, the Shangul moved to the valleys, where their villages are located today.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Ceramic vessels are used to prepare the Berta staple food, sorghum porridge. Sorghum beer is prepared in large ceramic containers called *awar* and *is'u*. The sources also mention houses and granaries raised over stone pillars.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Berta scarification usually consists of three vertical lines on each cheek. These marks are considered symbols of God, with each line interpreted as the initial letter of Allah, the Arabic *alif*.

## Music & performance

Music is played by males with large calabash trumpets called *waz'a* during Berta wedding ceremonies. Field recordings from 1980 of traditional music of the Ingessana and Berta peoples in Sudan’s Blue Nile State are listed as an external resource in the Berta people article.

## Festivals & rituals

 They describe rain-making rituals among the Berta and other Nilo-Saharan and Nilotic communities. Ritual specialists called *neri* are associated with healing and divination and are described as knowing how to deal with evil spirits called *shuman*.

Wedding ceremonies include male performance on large calabash trumpets called *waz'a*. The groom arrives at the wedding on a donkey while carrying a *bang*, described as a throwing stick. After the wedding, the husband builds a hut and lives in his wife’s village for a year or more while tilling his father-in-law’s land. Divorce is accepted.

## Foodways

The Berta are mostly mixed farmers who also raise livestock, trade, keep bees, and cultivate coffee. Sorghum is their staple food, and they make porridge with it in ceramic vessels. They also make beer from sorghum. The beer is prepared in large ceramic containers called *awar* and *is'u*. Working parties are important in Berta society: when somebody wants to build a house or cultivate a field, he calls his neighbors for help and provides beer and food. They state that most Berta practice Islam, often together with traditional customs.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They identify Berta language resources including a website maintained by the language community, with published literature and an online dictionary, but provide no details about particular literary works or contemporary preservation efforts.

## Language & religion

Berta, also known as Gebeto, is spoken by the Berta people in Sudan and Ethiopia. As of 2006, it had approximately 180,000 speakers in Sudan. The three Berta languages—Gebeto, Fadashi, and Undu—are often considered dialects of a single language. Berta proper includes the dialects Bake, Dabuso, Gebeto, Mayu, and Shuru; the name Gebeto may also be extended to all of Berta proper. Berta has a standard verb–subject–object order and is generally head-initial.

Berta is described as a Nilo-Saharan language unrelated to those of the neighboring Gumuz and Uduk. After centuries of influence from Arabic-speaking regions of Sudan, the Berta are mostly Muslim, and many speak fluent Sudanese Arabic. Traditional practices continue alongside Islam, including the work of *neri* ritual specialists, healing and divination, dealings with *shuman* evil spirits, and rain-making rituals.
