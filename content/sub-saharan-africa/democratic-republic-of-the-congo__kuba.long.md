## Overview

The Kuba — also called Bakuba or Bushongo — are a cluster of nineteen ethnic groups living in the savanna and gallery forest between the Sankuru, Lulua, and Kasai Rivers in the Kasai region of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, roughly 250,000 to 350,000 people today. The dominant group is the Bushoong, whose clan supplies the king (*nyim*); the confederation also includes the Kete, Coofa, Mbeengi, Ngeende, Pyaang, Shoowa, and the Kasai Twa. They speak Bantu languages of the Niger-Congo family, Bushong being the prestige tongue. The Kuba matter disproportionately in the study of world folk art because a merit-based court title system — half of all Bushoong men held titles in the 1880s — turned ordinary household objects into competitive displays of virtuosity, and because Kuba geometric design constitutes one of the most elaborated non-representational ornamental systems documented anywhere.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Kuba cloth** — the collective name for raffia textiles woven from the fibre of the *Raphia vinifera* palm. Men strip, dress and weave the panels on a single-heddle oblique loom; women then embroider, appliqué and dye them. The characteristic finish is a heavy, matte, slightly stiff hand quite unlike bark cloth or imported trade cotton.

**Shoowa cut-pile embroidery** — the technique Western collectors call *Kasai velvet*, produced above all by the Shoowa subgroup. Dyed raffia is threaded through the ground weave, pulled up as a loop, and sheared flush, producing a plush surface in which pattern is carried by the contrast between cropped pile and flat stitching. No two panels repeat; the embroiderer improvises, deliberately breaking symmetry as the design advances.

**Ntshak** — the wrapped dance skirt, a long rectangular raffia panel assembled from multiple woven lengths joined end to end, worn by both sexes at ceremonies and funerals. The finest are metres long and wrapped in bulked folds around the hips.

**Mbal** — appliqué and patchwork cloth in which cut shapes of contrasting raffia are stitched down over the ground, often over holes deliberately opened in the base panel. The technique is associated with women's mourning and dance skirts.

**Tukula-dyed cloth** — raffia coloured with *twool* (*tukula*), red powder ground from camwood and mixed with other pigments. Red is central to the Kuba idea of beauty: it ornaments face, hair and chest for dance, and anoints the body for burial.

**Cache-sexe panels** — small embroidered aprons, densely patterned, worn suspended from the waist cord; among the most concentrated fields of Kuba design because the whole vocabulary must resolve inside a hand-sized rectangle.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Imbol* (interlace / knot, the most prestigious pattern class), *mikomingoong* (interlocking stepped hooks), *woot* (the zigzag named for the culture hero), *namba* (nested chevrons), *nnaam* (concentric lozenge), *mishiing* (guilloche band), *bindusha* (checkerboard of alternating pile and flat ground), *bitaadi* (triangle fill), *mwiiny* (spiral or coiled scroll), *bushoong* interlace grids used on royal regalia.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress is a wrapped raffia or, since the colonial period, cotton cloth at the waist for both sexes, with the torso bare. Ceremonial dress is by contrast among the most encumbering in Central Africa. A titled man appears in a wrapped *ntshak* skirt, a beaded and cowrie-covered belt, sashes crossed over the chest, arm and leg rings, and a fibre or beaded cap; the *nyim* in full regalia wears a garment so loaded with cowrie shells, glass beads and leopard skin that he must be supported to stand. Women dance in embroidered *ntshak* and *mbal* skirts bulked at the hips, cowrie-strung waistbands, and a *cache-sexe* apron. Hats are status markers rather than weather protection: knotted and coiled fibre caps sewn with cowries and beads, some with a projecting brim, reserved by title. The *mukyeem* headdress — a beaded, cowrie-covered form with a swelling forehead and a trunk-like projection referencing the elephant — belongs to the highest ranks. Faces, chests and hair are reddened with *tukula* before performance.

### Architecture

Kuba villages are laid out on a rectangular plan, houses in rows flanking a central street or plaza, with the *nyim*'s compound and the men's meeting houses at the ceremonial centre. The house is a rectangular single-storey structure with a ridge roof thatched in palm leaf, framed on a pole armature. Its distinction lies in the walls: whole panels are woven and plaited from raffia palm ribs and split canes into geometric lattices — chevrons, lozenges, interlace — the same vocabulary embroidered onto cloth, executed here at architectural scale so that the surface of a village reads as a continuous patterned field. Nineteenth-century European visitors, including the missionary William Henry Sheppard, remarked on the woven mat walls as the signature of Kuba settlement. Doorways are low; interiors are divided by hung mats. Royal enclosures are screened with tall plaited palisades, again patterned, which control sightlines onto the king and mark the graded approach to the court.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The best-known Kuba objects are carved in wood for court competition. **Palm-wine cups** (*mbwoongntey*) are cut as cylinders, horns, or heads and half-figures, their surfaces incised with interlace; a titled man drank from his own. **Kuba boxes** (*ngedi mu ntey*) hold *tukula* powder and paste, razors for cutting raffia, hairpins and ritual items, and come in square forms with faceted lids, rectangles, mask shapes, and the crescent "half-moon". **Ndop figures**, introduced after 1700 by King Misha mi-Shyaang a-Mbul, are seated commemorative portraits of individual kings, each identified by the *ibol* — a personal emblem carved on the plinth before the figure. Also characteristic: ridged and incised drinking horns, wooden headrests, divination instruments, iron and copper ceremonial blades and knives with openwork blades, and finely carved combs.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Kuba adornment is overwhelmingly a matter of cowrie shells, imported glass beads, and copper worked onto fibre grounds rather than cast metal. Belts, baldrics, cuffs, anklets and caps are built by netting and stitching shells and beads into dense polychrome geometry, each configuration licensed by title. Copper and brass rings, neck collars and arm bands signal wealth; leopard-tooth and claw pendants are royal and chiefly. *Tukula* red is applied to skin and hair as cosmetic and ritual colour, and *tukula* paste was also moulded into commemorative blocks. Scarification in raised keloid patterns — lozenges and interlace on the belly, chest and temples — repeats the textile vocabulary on the body itself, making cloth, skin, wall and cup a single design system. Hair is dressed with carved pins and combs.

## Music & performance

Kuba music is court music first. The royal ensembles combine slit drums and tall carved single-headed drums, the largest being the royal drum surfaced with beads and cowries in the manner of regalia; iron double bells, rattles, and struck clappers carry the pulse. Melodic instruments include raffia-and-gourd resonated xylophones, the lamellophone or thumb piano, side-blown ivory and antelope-horn trumpets whose calls announce the *nyim*, and hunting whistles. Song genres divide by occasion: praise songs recounting the royal genealogy and the deeds of Shyaam a-Mbul a Ngoong, performed before the throne by titled singers; funeral laments for titled dead; initiation songs restricted to men of the association lodges; and women's work and grinding songs. Performance is intensely tied to office — who may play, sing, wear, and dance is graded by the same title system that governs cups and boxes. Drum languages encode proverbs and names, so the instrument speaks as well as sounds. Ceremonial performance clusters around royal accession, the reception of embassies, funerals of titleholders, and the periodic public appearances of the king, at which the massed ensembles, beaded regalia, and masked dancers were assembled as one composed spectacle.

## Dance & theatre

Kuba masquerade is the outstanding performance tradition, and its core is a triad of royal masks danced together as a mythic drama. **Mwaash aMbooy** (*mwaash amboy*) is the king mask — leopard skin, cowries, beads, and raffia — representing Woot, the first human and founder. **Bwoom** is a broad helmet mask with a bulging forehead and copper sheet over wood, standing for the commoner, the subversive prince, or the Twa; in performance he contests with Mwaash aMbooy. **Ngady aMwaash** (*ngady amwaash*) is the female mask, painted in triangles and stripes with beaded lines running from the eyes to signal tears, danced by a man as Woot's sister and wife. Their interaction stages incest, rivalry and the founding of the royal line. **Mukyeem**, the elephant-trunked beaded helmet, appears at royal funerals and accessions. Other masks belong to the *nkaan* men's initiation, where they police and instruct novices in the forest enclosure, and are not danced for entertainment. Non-masked dance accompanies title-taking, harvest and reception of visitors, women dancing in bulked embroidered skirts with hip-driven movement.

## Festivals & rituals

The Kuba ceremonial year is keyed to the court and the agricultural cycle rather than to a fixed calendar of named feast days. The largest events are accession rites for a new *nyim*, which involve seclusion, investiture with regalia, and the commissioning of a new *ndop*; and royal funerals, at which the king is buried with court treasures, masked dancers perform, and the whole kingdom's title hierarchy assembles. Title-taking ceremonies punctuate adult life and are the occasion for which a man commissions a carved cup or an embroidered skirt. **Nkaan** is the men's initiation: novices are secluded in a bush enclosure, undergo ordeals, encounter masked beings, learn the secrets of the association, and emerge reddened with *tukula* and treated as reborn. Birth is marked by naming rites and seclusion of mother and child; twins receive special ritual status. Marriage proceeds through bridewealth transfer and the gift of embroidered cloth, which women accumulate as heritable property. At death the body is anointed with *tukula* and wrapped in raffia — cloth is the necessary burial equipment, and a person's textiles are assembled for their funeral, which is why so much Kuba embroidery survives in quantity. First-fruits observances mark the maize and cassava harvests, crops introduced from the Americas that underwrote the kingdom's 18th-century wealth.

## Foodways

The staples are cassava and maize, both New World crops adopted early and thoroughly, alongside sorghum, millet, plantain, groundnuts, beans, yams, and leafy greens. Cassava is soaked, fermented, dried and pounded into flour, then stirred into stiff porridge — *fufu* — eaten by hand in balls dipped into sauce, the everyday centre of the meal. Cassava leaves (*pondu* / *saka-saka*) are pounded and simmered long with palm oil into a dense dark relish; palm oil, palm nut pulp, groundnut paste, and dried or smoked fish from the Kasai and Sankuru furnish the sauces. Protein comes from river fish, game from the gallery forest, goat, chicken, caterpillars and termites in season. Dishes are steamed in leaf packets, boiled, or grilled over coals. **Palm wine**, tapped from the raffia and oil palm and drunk lightly fermented, is the ceremonial drink and the reason the carved cup is the signature Kuba object: drinking is a public, ranked, competitive act, and the vessel announces the drinker's title. Palm wine accompanies title-taking, oath-swearing, dispute settlement and funerals. Kola nut and pipe tobacco — tobacco another American introduction — are offered in hospitality and exchange.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kuba oral tradition is unusually historical: the court maintained a remembered king list and an elaborated narrative of the state's founding, transmitted by titled specialists and recorded in the twentieth century by Jan Vansina, whose *The Children of Woot* takes its title from the Kuba's own self-description. The central cycle concerns **Woot**, the first human, who named the animals and brought civilization, and whose incest with his sister underlies the royal masquerade; and **Shyaam a-Mbul a Ngoong**, the culture hero who around 1625 travelled west to the Pende and Kongo kingdoms, learned what they knew, returned, usurped a local ruler, and founded the confederated state with its councils, courts and offices. **Bomazi** appears in the origin traditions as a founding ancestor figure. Alongside these are animal trickster tales, riddles exchanged as evening entertainment, and proverbs — many of them also encoded in drum language and carved as emblems on cups and *ndop* plinths, so that a proverb can be told, drummed, or displayed.

## Language & religion

The Kuba speak Bantu languages of the Benue-Congo branch of Niger-Congo; Bushong (Bushoong) is the court and prestige language, with Kete, Ngeende, Coofa, Mbeengi and other varieties spoken across the nineteen constituent groups, and Kikongo-based Kituba and Lingala serving as regional linguae francae. There was no indigenous script; the design vocabulary of cloth, wall and cup functions as a parallel visual system of identification and status. Cosmology centres on *Bumba*, the sky father who produced sun, moon, stars and planets and, with the earth mother, life — distant creators, invoked but not closely served. Immediate religious attention goes to *Woot*, the first human and bringer of civilization. Nature spirits, ancestral intervention, divination, and charm-making remain active practice; Christianity, Presbyterian and Catholic, has been present since the late nineteenth century and is now widespread, coexisting with masquerade and royal ritual.
