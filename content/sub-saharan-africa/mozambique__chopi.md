## Overview

The Chopi are a Bantu ethnic group of Mozambique who have lived primarily in the Zavala region of southern Mozambique, in Inhambane Province. They traditionally practiced subsistence agriculture and lived in rural communities, although civil war following Mozambique’s liberation from Portuguese colonial rule in 1975 displaced or killed many people. Drought also forced many away from their homeland and into the nation’s cities. The Chopi speak Chichopi, a tonal Bantu language; many also speak chiTonga and Portuguese. They are related to the Tsonga people of Mozambique and South Africa, and their western neighbors include the Shangaan ethnic group. Chopi cultural identity is associated with the elephant, while their music, especially the timbila orchestra tradition, is their best-documented contribution to folk culture.

## Material culture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources describe Chopi expertise in manufacturing with wood, iron, and clay from an early period. They made wooden instruments and smelted iron; the sources also state that they hunted elephants and other animals. No specific ceramic vessel, metal object, household form, or workshop tradition is described.

## Music & performance

The Chopi are famous for traditional music, especially the *mbila*, a xylophone whose plural form is *timbila*. Timbila are played in large groups, with musicians forming an orchestra. The music and dance of the timbila are performed by a large orchestra, and Henri-Alexandre Junod described the Ba-Chopi as especially accomplished musicians and as “masters” of the instrument. The sources associate this tradition with marimbas, xylophones, and other native instruments.

Other instruments named in the sources include panpipes, whistles, animal horns, rattles, drums of various sizes, musical bows, and a globular flute with three holes made from the dried shell of the *nkuso* fruit, identified as a bush orange. The Chopi music tradition was proclaimed a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity by UNESCO in 2005.

## Dance & theatre

The **timbila music and dance** tradition combines orchestral performance with dancing. Dancers put on a show whose style bears much similarity to the dance of the Tsonga people of South Africa, particularly the *Xibelani* dance and other footwork dance styles.

## Festivals & rituals

 They state that the Chopi identify culturally with the elephant.

## Foodways

The Chopi’s traditional foods include cassava, also called manioc, and cashew nuts. They produce traditional alcoholic beverages from fermented tangerines or cashews.

## Oral tradition & literature

According to oral traditions of the people themselves, the Chopi are part of the original Bantu peoples who migrated from Central Africa between 100AD and 200AD and settled in parts of Tanzania, Malawi, Northern Zambia, and Mozambique. These traditions also describe them as reputedly the first Bantu tribes to establish contact with the San people of South-East Africa. The sources connect the name Chopi with the use of bows and arrows, which they adopted from the San people after early contact. They mention Chopi poetry only in connection with Hugh Tracey’s documentation of Chopi musicians, their music, poetry, and instruments.

## Language & religion

Chopi, also spelled Copi, Tschopi, and Txopi, is a Bantu language spoken along the southern coast of Mozambique. The Chopi language is tonal, and many Chopi also speak chiTonga and Portuguese as secondary languages. Maho lists the possibly extinct Lenge dialect as a distinct language.
