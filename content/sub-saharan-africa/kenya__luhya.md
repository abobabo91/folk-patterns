## Overview

The Luhya are Kenya's second-largest ethnic group, spread across the fertile Lake Victoria basin in western Kenya and related areas. Rather than a single tribe, they are an ethnolinguistic constellation of more than twenty subgroups, each with its own dialect and clan system but sharing a common identity. Their material culture centers on worked iron, hide, pottery and plant fibres, with a rich tradition of instrumental music performed at funeral gatherings.

## Material culture

Luhya crafts emphasize iron metalwork, leather work, and plant fibres, with pottery and basketry serving specialized purposes.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Luhya worked hide and plant fibre rather than woven cloth, creating articulated surfaces through seams and construction.

- **Segmented antelope-skin cloak** — A man's cloak made of six separate pieces of antelope-skin leather sewn together so the surface is articulated by seams.
- **Banana-fibre and banana-bark work** — Banana plants supplied string for women's girdles strung with iron beads and sheet material for ring-shaped head-pads that cushioned pots.
- **Split palm-stem basketry** — Baskets with handles made from split palm stem; grass was worked separately into fish traps.
- **Cow-dung basket lining** — The inside of a basket was smeared with cow dung to turn an open weave into a container for loose or wet contents.
- **Fibre mask** — A mask made of fibre finished with a fringe of long twisted strings.

### Clothing & dress

Men wore leather; women wore dresses and body ornament that carried much of the work of dress, with imported cloth appearing later.

- **Antelope-skin cloak** — A six-panel cloak worn by men, photographed in fields during work.
- **Woman's waist girdles** — Iron beads threaded on string or banana fibre, worn at the waist as ornament and dress.
- **Woman's necklace** — A leather thong hung with a knife, rings, china beads and glass beads, combining tool and ornament on the same cord.
- **Wanga elder's Muslim dress** — A turban, robes and shoes worn by the Wanga elder Bakairi, photographed outside a thatched building.

### Architecture

Buildings used a framed construction sequence of branches and sticks with thatched roofs; agricultural structures included the quail pole.

- **Framed dwelling** — A house constructed with a frame of branches and sticks rather than walls built up first.
- **Quail pole** — A tall pole standing above the bush with baskets hanging from it, used in Maragoli country for catching birds.
- **Grain drying ground** — Sorghum heads spread in a circle on the ground to dry, watched over during processing.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery was specialized by contents; iron working was well developed and dominated functional and decorative forms.

- **Cooking bowl** — A pottery vessel specialized for cooking vegetables and meat.
- **Beer vessel** — A pottery vessel dedicated to fermentation, giving beer its own ware.
- **Spear** — An iron weapon with a socketed head on a wooden shaft.
- **Sword** — An iron blade mounted on a wooden handle.
- **Shield** — Thick buffalo hide with a carved wooden stem down the centre, lashed with thongs to leave a gap for the hand.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ornament was overwhelmingly iron, including rings, armlets and beads, with imported material entering alongside traditional forms.

- **Iron neck ring** — A simple ring worn at the neck.
- **Armlet of coiled iron wire** — An arm ornament made from coiled wire.
- **Iron beads for waist girdles** — Beads strung on plain string or banana fibre for women's waist ornament.
- **Lip-plug** — A cylinder of quartz cut, rounded at one end and tapering to a slight point, inserted in the lip.
- **Composite head ornament** — A crescent-shaped iron blade mounted on a thong of leather and hair.

## Music & performance

Two stringed zithers and an iron bell are documented; music was performed at funeral dances as a whole-community gathering.

- **Reed harp** — A stringed instrument of seventeen thin reeds.
- **Composite cane zither** — An instrument of hide and canes bound together with vegetable-fibre string, with strings formed of lifted cane bark and three iron rings fixed at one end.
- **Iron cattle bell** — A bell hanging on a leather strap, used in herding.
- **Funeral band** — An ensemble of Luyia men playing at a funeral dance, photographed with a crowd of men, women and children standing behind the musicians.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name only the funeral dance; no other dramatic tradition or dance genres are described.

- **Funeral dance** — A dance occasion performed outdoors among trees and buildings with an ensemble band and whole-community attendance.

## Festivals & rituals

Precolonial belief centred on Nyasaye/Were and ancestral spirits; Christian missions arrived in the early twentieth century and converted the population; the Dini ya Msambwa movement combined Christian and older elements with anti-colonial protest.

- **Nyasaye/Were** — The precolonial deity and focus of ritual belief.
- **Ritual specialists** — Experts who held authority alongside councils of elders and lineage leaders in clan-based polities.
- **Friends Africa Mission** — A Quaker mission established at Kaimosi in Tiriki by Arthur Chilson, who took the local name Shikanga.
- **Dini ya Msambwa** — A movement led among the Bukusu by Elijah Masinde in the 1940s and 1950s, combining Christian and older elements with anti-colonial protest and repressed by the colonial state.
- **Funeral rite** — The one life-cycle rite described as an occasion for music and mass attendance.

## Foodways

Cultivation combined shifting agriculture with herding and hunting; sorghum, millet, cowpeas and bananas were staple crops, processed and cooked in specialized vessels.

- **Sorghum** — Heads spread in a circle on the ground to dry, watched over during processing.
- **Finger millet** — Winnowed by pouring from the hands into a basket by girls during processing.
- **Porridge** — The grain staple in its cooked form, stirred with a wooden spoon.
- **Beer** — Fermented in a dedicated pottery vessel.
- **Quail pole** — A tall pole with hanging baskets in Maragoli country for catching birds.

## Oral tradition & literature

Genealogy worked as history; clans traced origins to legendary ancestors, migration leaders or occupational specialisations, with names remembered in sequence.

- **Marachi genealogy** — Marachi, son of Musebe, son of Sirikwa, forming the namesake of a clan.
- **Ababere lineage** (*Ababere*) — A lineage remembered through Ng'ono Mwami and his descendants.
- **Isukha leadership line** — Nkotsi Liyami Makaka Shitsuxane of the Mahalia clan, son of Nkotsi Lijoodi Makaka, son of Nkotsi Liyami Makaka Muchina.
- **Kabras etymology** (*Kabras*) — The name glossed from Avalasi, meaning warriors or mighty hunters.
- **Keyah naming** (*Keyah*) — A name given to Luhya children born around the Second World War, after KAR, the King's African Rifles.

## Language & religion

The Luhya speak a cluster of distinct but related Bantu languages with shared noun classes, verbal morphology and prefixes; most are multilingual in Luhya varieties plus Swahili and English.

- **Lubukusu** — A Luhya language variety, though Bukusu dialect is a variety of Masaba.
- **OluLuyia** — The collective term for the Luhya cluster of Bantu languages.
- **OmuLuyia** — The term for an individual Luhya person, using the aba-/ava- prefix for people.
- **AbaLuyia** — The collective term for Luhya people.
- **EbuLuyia** — The Luhya homeland, using the olu- prefix for languages and places.
