## Overview

The Toucouleur, also called Tukulor or *Haalpulaar*, are a West African ethnic group native to the Futa Toro region of Senegal. They live primarily in the Senegal River valley, with smaller communities in Guinea, Gambia, Mali and Mauritania. There are an estimated 1 million Toucouleur people in West Africa, including about 100,000 in Mali. Their language is Pulaar, a dialect of Fula belonging to the Atlantic branch of the Niger-Congo language family. The Toucouleur were Islamized in the 11th century and were among the first Muslims in the area that became Senegal. Their historical importance includes the spread of Islam in West Africa and the Toucouleur Empire, which expanded from Senegal into much of Mali during the 19th century. Farming, fishing and raising cattle are central activities.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used do not document named Toucouleur textile traditions or pattern vocabularies.

The museum catalogue records a British Museum cloth composed of 29 very narrow strips in two different widths. It was hand-woven from imported European thread and hand-sewn along the selvedges. The strips alternate between two patterns: blue weft squares alternating with white squares, with a blue stripe along the centre of the warp; and a warp arrangement of two blue threads and a green thread, with supplementary bundles of weft threads at regular intervals. ## Festivals & rituals

The Toucouleur were Islamized in the 11th century, and Islam is described as a defining feature of their heritage. The sources do not provide an annual festival calendar or name particular religious festivals. They describe several life-cycle practices. Marriage requires a bride price payable to the bride’s family, and the marriage is validated by a mosque. The bride comes to live with her husband’s joint family. Before the marriage is consummated, the bride’s aunt traditionally checks whether the girl is a virgin, then bathes and massages her. The bride and groom join a wedding feast with village members, marking the beginning of the new couple.

One week after *pémbougale*, meaning childbirth, the baby is named. A *gorgol*, the sister of the father, cuts the child’s hair. The father tells the *marabout* the name he has chosen; the marabout whispers it into the infant’s ear and prays. The marabout then informs a *gawlo*, or griot, who announces the chosen name to the village. The sources do not describe coming-of-age rites, funerals or other annual ceremonies.

## Foodways

The Toucouleur are traditionally settled in the Senegal River valley, where farming, fishing and raising cattle are their main activities. The Pulaar language article likewise describes an economy consisting mainly of raising stock, crop cultivation and fishing. It identifies noun classes connected with bovines, including *naage*, meaning cow, and *wiige*, meaning heifer. The sources do not name staple grains, dishes, cooking methods, ceremonial foods, tea or coffee traditions, or dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

According to the oral traditions of the Toucouleur and Serer, the Toucouleur descend from Serer and Fula ancestors. The sources also describe a mutually acceptable bantering-style interaction between Serers and Toucouleurs, called the joking relationship by anthropologists.

The naming ceremony includes the *gawlo*, or griot, who announces the infant’s chosen name to the village. The sources do not name a Toucouleur epic, folktale cycle, proverb tradition, riddle tradition or written literary genre. They do not document contemporary literary preservation projects, except for efforts connected with Pulaar literacy and education.

## Language & religion

Pulaar is the language of the Toucouleur and is a dialect of Fula in the Atlantic branch of the Niger-Congo language family. The Toucouleur also call themselves *Haalpulaar’en*, meaning “those who speak Pulaar.” Pulaar is spoken primarily in Mauritania, Senegal and Gambia and is one of the national languages of Mauritania, Senegal and Gambia. It is written primarily in the Latin script. Historically, it had a literary tradition using the Arabic script, an orthographic tradition now known as *Ajami*. Adlam script has also been gaining ground.

The Toucouleur are Muslims and were Islamized in the 11th century. Arabic words have entered Pulaar through Islamic influence. The sources do not identify a particular Islamic sect, saint tradition or form of religious syncretism among the Toucouleur.
