## Overview

The Baggāra, also known as Chadian Arabs, are a nomadic confederation whose members have mixed Arab and Arabized indigenous African ancestry. They inhabit part of the Sahel, mainly between Lake Chad and the Nile river near south Kordofan, with the largest populations in Chad and Sudan and smaller minorities in Niger, Central African Republic, South Sudan, Cameroon, and northern Nigeria. The source gives a population of over six million. They are known as Baggara and Abbala in Sudan and as Shuwa Arabs in Chad, Cameroon, and Nigeria. Their common language is Chadian Arabic, and their traditional livelihood is nomadic cattle herding, although many now lead settled lives. Baggara groups are associated with seasonal movement between wet-season grazing lands and river areas during the dry season.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Jibba* — A man’s cotton tunic recorded in the British Museum catalogue, with applique panels in dyed cotton and wool on both sides and embroidery at the neck, cuffs, and pockets.

*Tob* — A patterned cloth worn by women in several photographic records from South Darfur. The photographs show women wearing patterned or light-coloured floral tobs.

*Darangal* — A large roll-up bed made from numerous thin sticks of *bashom* wood bound with strips of cowhide called *seyur*.

*Dabia* — A bottle-shaped leather storage bag with a long, narrow neck, a plaited hanging loop, and a wider body made from sewn sections of red and plain leather. The catalogue records its use for millet, sorghum grain, or their flour.

*Boursa* — A round gourd transformed into a storage vessel, with a separate lid. Its low neck and lid use plaited vegetable fibre and leather thongs.

The sources do not identify named Baggara motifs or provide comparative pattern terminology.

### Clothing & dress

The catalogue records a man’s *jibba*, made of cotton cloth and decorated with applique panels in dyed cotton and wool, together with embroidery at the neck, cuffs, and pockets. Photographs from South Darfur show women wearing patterned *tob*, including light-coloured floral cloth. Other photographs show women wearing cloths, neck-ornaments, ear-ornaments, and nose-ornaments. A photographic record shows a bearded man wearing a *jellabiya* and turban, while another shows an older man wearing a turban and carrying a cane under his left arm. The sources do not describe everyday footwear, belts, trousers, or a separate ceremonial dress system.

### Architecture

The museum photographs show thatched roof buildings, wooden supports, and woven mats in South Darfur. One photograph places a young woman outside a thatched roof building, while another shows wooden supports and woven mats behind a woman. The sources do not provide a vernacular name for the house form, a description of its complete construction, or information about urban architectural traditions.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum records include two long spears associated with the Baggara. One has a broad, leaf-shaped iron blade, a wooden shaft made of reed or bamboo, and an iron spiral at the butt; its recorded length is 264 cm. A second spear has a broad, leaf-shaped iron point, a reed shaft, and a spiral-like metal fitting; its recorded length is 256 cm. The British Museum catalogue also records two drums with skin coverings suspended from wooden stakes, a tray with a jug and glasses, and household storage objects made from leather, gourds, vegetable fibre, and wood. The sources do not describe Baggara ceramics.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The photographic catalogue records women wearing neck-ornaments, ear-ornaments, and nose-ornaments. Neck-ornaments are described as carrying amulets. A decorative *gilada* is recorded as a rectangular red-leather neck-piece for a riding bull, decorated with lines of cowrie shells, plaited leather edges, and long leather tassels. The sources do not describe tattoos, henna, hair practices, or named human jewelry types beyond these catalogue descriptions.

## Music & performance

The sources do not describe Baggara musical genres, named songs, or performance contexts. Museum photographs show groups of men and boys gathered around two skin-covered drums suspended from wooden stakes. Two men hold wooden sticks used to beat the drums, and the stakes also carry a string of amulets and a piece of light-coloured fabric. Another photographic record shows a woman speaking or singing with her eyes closed while wearing a patterned *tob*. These records document drums and vocal activity but do not identify the instruments by a Baggara vernacular name, explain their construction in detail, or state whether the performances belong to ceremonies, festivals, weddings, funerals, or other occasions.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do not provide an annual Baggara festival calendar or describe birth, coming-of-age, wedding, or funeral rites. They do record seasonal movement: nomadic groups migrate between grazing lands during the wet season and river areas during the dry season. The historical account also describes the Mahdist period from 1883 to 1898, when tens of thousands of Baggara migrated to Omdurman and central Sudan and supplied troops for the Mahdist armies. These movements are historical and subsistence-related rather than descriptions of ritual festivals.

## Foodways

The museum catalogue records the *dabia* leather storage bag as an object usually used for storing millet and sorghum grain, or flour made from these grains. The sources also describe Baggara subsistence as nomadic cattle herding and identify cattle ownership as central to the political use of the term Baggāra in Sudan. They state that Baggara groups migrate seasonally between wet-season grazing lands and river areas during the dry season. The sources do not name prepared dishes, cooking methods, beverages, ceremonial foods, or dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources do not describe Baggara folktales, epic poetry, proverbs, riddles, or named storytelling traditions. They do record historical claims of ancestry from the Juhaynah Arab tribe and a documented letter from 1391 concerning Arab settlements and raids in the region. Braukämper dates the formation of Baggara culture to the 17th century in Wadai, between Bornu and Darfur, where camel-herding Arabs encountered cattle-rearing Fula people migrating east. These are historical accounts rather than recorded oral-literary genres.

## Language & religion

The Baggara mostly speak a distinct dialect known as Chadian Arabic. The language has also been called Shuwa Arabic, although the source describes *Shuwa* as a geographically and socially parochial term that has fallen into disuse among linguists specializing in the language. The term is said to be of Kanuri origin. Baggara communities in Southern Kordofan have Sudanese Arabic influence in their dialect because of contact with sedentary populations and Sudanese Arab camel herders. The sources used do not describe the group’s religious landscape, religious institutions, spiritual practices, or historical scripts.
