## Overview

The Tetela live in central Democratic Republic of the Congo between two rivers, hunting, fishing, and farming cassava, bananas and kola nuts. They speak Tetela, a Bantu language that classifies nouns into many categories. The name "Tetela" itself is recent, arriving only in the late 1800s as a corruption of Arab traders' words for the region. Many Tetela and related groups trace descent from earlier ancestors like the AnKutshu.

## Material culture

Tetela material culture emphasizes ironwork with decorative openwork, carved wooden figures for ritual use, and woven raffia and string goods.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Raffia mats and two-ply string aprons are the documented textile traditions.

- **Raffia mats** — Mats woven from raffia and finished with fringe along the edges.
- **Two-ply string aprons** — Women's aprons made of pale brown or black two-ply string worked in bunches.
- **Loom weaving** — Wooden looms with grass textiles document the weaving apparatus itself.

### Clothing & dress

Clothing includes string aprons, belts, and elaborate feathered headgear, with filed teeth historically marking ethnic identity.

- **String aprons and belts** — Aprons of plied string bunches and belts of black string or hide, some with impressed patterns.
- **Feathered headgear** — Head-dresses combining feathers, hair, glass beads, fibre, bamboo and cord.
- **Chief's knife sheath** — Rank-bearing regalia made of skin with fur, animal tail, wood, copper and iron.
- **Wooden combs and hairpins** — Personal grooming tools carved from wood.

### Architecture

Almost no architectural information is recorded in the sources.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Red earthenware pottery and decorative ironwork with openwork designs and copper inlay are the strongest crafts.

- **Red earthenware pottery** — Bowls and vases with relief bands, incised rows of triangles, and dark coatings on the upper body.
- **Openwork iron axe** — An axe blade with circular and diamond-shaped perforations, copper inlay, and a flared tip.
- **Javelin with copper fitting** — A socketed iron-and-wood javelin with copper closing one side opening.
- **Wooden food bowls** — Bowls with either straight knobbed handles or loop handles for serving.
- **Gourd oil vessels** — Gourds decorated with human figures or studded with brass nails.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ornaments use animal materials, iron and copper equally, with feathers holding particular historical importance.

- **Banana seed armlet with boar tusks** — A circlet hung with five pendant wild boar tusks.
- **Leopard tooth necklace** — Leopard's teeth strung on cord.
- **Iron bangles and bracelets** — Iron bangles with pendant spatulate blades and bracelets wrapped in lizard skin.
- **Iron gong-shaped pendants** — Five gong-shaped iron pendants with a small horn hung together on a ring.
- **Parrot feather wearing** (*kasuku*) — Nineteenth-century wearing of parrot feathers that led Arab traders to name nearby peoples the Kusu or Wakussu.

## Music & performance

Three drum types drive music and communication, with the slit drum being the most intricate, carrying poetry and messages.

- **Ngomo skin drum** (*ngomo*) — A skin drum used for dancing, usually played together with the slit drum.
- **Lukumbi slit drum** (*lukumbi*) — A six-toned slit drum that is a highly developed art form for music, verse and communication.
- **Ekuli cylindrical drum** (*ekuli*) — A small two-toned drum formerly used to signal battle victory and now used to call people to church and classes.
- **Stick zither** — A wooden zither with quill and fibre bindings and a gourd resonator in two parts.
- **Iron gong** — An iron gong with a rubber-headed beater.

## Dance & theatre

Dancing is accompanied by the ngomo and lukumbi drums, and masks indicate masquerade performance.

- **Painted masks** — Masks painted red, white and black with feather ornament, some fringed with grass and fitted with leaf girdles.
- **Carved wooden helmet mask** — A mask with a ridge across the head, protruding eyes and pursed lips, painted in black-and-white crosshatching with vegetable-fibre fringe.
- **Buffoon's basket** — A basket holding charms assembled from bamboo, cane, calabashes, gourds, shells, cord and wood, indicating a buffoon performer role.

## Festivals & rituals

No annual calendar or life-cycle ceremonies are documented, but ritual objects including charms and iron bells are well attested.

- **Carved wooden figures** — Human figures carved from wood, some wrapped in cotton cloth or with inlaid eyes, used as charms.
- **Figures with glass beads** — Head and neck figures on circular bases with white pigment around eyes or seed pods set with glass beads and blue-beaded cord.
- **Iron bells as charms** — Iron bells catalogued as charms rather than instruments, some with copper or iron wire and hide loops.

## Foodways

The Tetela live by hunting, gathering, fishing and farming cassava, bananas and kola nuts.

- **Wooden food bowls** — Bowls with straight knobbed or loop handles for serving food.
- **Gourd oil vessels** — Gourds for oil, some with human figures and one studded with brass nails.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Tetela have a large corpus of proverbs used by adults for rebuking and encouraging, with published collections of hundreds of sayings.

- **Tetela proverbs** — A wide-ranging set of proverbs deployed by adults for purposes from rebuking to encouraging.
- **A Collection of 100 Tetela Proverbs** — A published collection assembled by Elysee Meta Okubo in 2016.
- **Great Collection of Tetela Proverbs on the African Wisdom** — A published collection of 283 proverbs across 107 pages by Ndjeka Elizabeth Mukanga, Empenge Albert Shefu and Ambaye Albertine Tshefu in 2020.

## Language & religion

Tetela is a Bantu language with nouns sorted into multiple classes; religion includes traditional beliefs with competing deity names and Christian mission presence since 1910.

- **Tetela language** (*Otetela, Kitetela, Kikitatela, Sungu*) — A Bantu language of northern Kasai-Oriental Province with nouns sorted into classes inherited from an ancestral system of twenty-two.
- **Supreme deity names** (*Motetela, Djungumanga, Winya, Unya Shungu*) — Four competing names for a supreme being recorded by different sources and in different communities.
- **Methodist mission** — The Methodist Episcopal Church South established a mission at Wembo-Nyama in 1910 and began recording the Otetela language in 1914.

## Glossary

- *Batetela* — The Tetela people in plural form
- *Otetela* — The Tetela language; also used for the people
- *Kitetela* — The Tetela language; variant name also used for the people
- *Sungu* — Alternative name for Tetela language and a Luba title used by some Tetela groups
- *AnKutshu* — An ancestor group from whom many Tetela traced descent before the name Tetela came into use
- *ngomo* — A skin drum used for dancing, usually played with the slit drum
- *lukumbi* — A six-toned slit drum used for music, poetry and communication
- *ekuli* — A small cylindrical two-toned drum formerly used to signal battle victory, now used to call people to church
- *Motetela* — A name for a god, meaning either 'he who laughs not' or 'he at whom one may not laugh'
- *Djungumanga* — The Kitetela term for a supreme deity recorded by American missionaries
- *Winya* — A Luba term for god used by some Tetela groups
- *Unya Shungu* — A later development of Winya, reportedly meaning 'father of the heavens'
- *kasuku* — Parrot; the source of the name Kusu or Wakussu for peoples seen wearing parrot feathers by Arab traders
- *Wakussu* — A name Arab slave traders gave to Kitetela and related speakers wearing parrot feathers, a corruption of kasuku
- *Kusu* — A name still carried by Kitetela speakers of Maniema, derived from kasuku (parrot)
