## Overview

The Gisu people, also called the Bamasaba people of Elgon, are a Bantu-speaking ethnic group of the Masaba people in eastern Uganda. They live mainly in Mbale District, on the slopes of Mount Elgon. The 2014 National Census of Uganda estimated their population at 1,646,904, or 4.9% of the total population. They are closely related to the Bukusu people of Kenya, and their language is mutually intelligible with Bukusu. The Gisu are agriculturalists whose cultural life includes the Imbalu initiation ceremonies, a two-year cycle of circumcision ceremonies, Lumasaba language traditions, banana-based foodways, bamboo-shoot gathering, and material objects such as pottery vessels, wooden tools, shields, ornaments, and rattles.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

These include a reddish pottery vase with one handle, a spheroidal pottery beer pot with five mouths, a pottery vessel with a black interior and everted lip, a pottery vase with three necks, a vase with two necks, a pottery vase with a spout and strainer, and plain or reddish pottery bowls. The leglet has an attached iron rattle containing two metal balls. ## Festivals & rituals

The principal named ritual in the sources is *Imbalu*, the initiation ceremony undergone by men among the Bagisu. The initiation ceremonies among the Bamasaaba are held every two years during August. The source describes circumcision as an old culture practiced by the Bamasaaba in eastern Uganda and states that the culture was adopted from the Maasai people, who were described as the Bamasaba’s in-laws. The supplied account connects the adoption of circumcision with the story of Masaba, who migrated from the Ethiopian Mountains via Lake Turkana to Sironko and settled around Bududa. He fell in love with a Maasai girl known as Nabarwa, and her family required him to undergo their rite of circumcision before marrying her. The sources do not describe birth, wedding, funeral, annual harvest, or calendrical festivals beyond the August timing of Imbalu.

## Foodways

Banana is the staple food of the Gisu people and is commonly referred to as “Matoke.” The sources also state that the Bamasaba ancestors lived on bamboo shoots, known as *Malewa* in the Lumasaba language. These shoots are collected from bamboo trees on top of Mount Elgon. The sources do not describe recipes, cooking methods, ceremonial foods, dietary rules, beverages, or meal customs. Agricultural communities among the Bagisu grow Arabica coffee, cotton, tobacco, maize, beans, millet, sorghum, yams, and cassava. Those living as far as 5000 feet above sea level grow Arabica coffee, much of which is sold to Bugisu Co-operative Union.

## Oral tradition & literature

The supplied sources preserve an origin tradition concerning the ancestors Mundu and Sera, whom the Masaba, Bukusu, and Luhya people believed to be their ancestors. A related account tells of Masaba’s migration from the Ethiopian Mountains via Lake Turkana to Sironko and his settlement around Bududa. Masaba’s first son with Nabarwa was Mwambu, who was nicknamed Nkisu by his Maasai uncles after they had stolen his father’s cows. Masaba failed to pronounce the nickname and called his son Mugisu; the account presents this nickname as the origin of the name Bagisu. The sources do not describe a named epic, proverb corpus, riddle tradition, or contemporary literary revival.

## Language & religion

Masaba, also called Lumasaaba and sometimes Gisu or Lugisu after one of its dialects, is a Bantu language spoken by more than two million people in East Africa. The Gisu dialect in eastern Uganda is mutually intelligible with Bukusu, spoken by ethnic Luhya in western Kenya. Varieties listed in the source are Gisu, Kisu, Bukusu, Syan, Tachoni, Dadiri, and Buya. Dadiri is spoken in the north, Gisu in the center, and Buya in the center and south of Masaba territory in Uganda. Masaba has a five-vowel system and around eighteen noun classes. The Bamasaba speak a dialect of Lumasaba that is fully understandable by other dialects and is also understood by the Bukusu.

The majority of the Bagisu are Christians, mainly Anglican through the Church of Uganda, estimated at 45.7%. Roman Catholics account for an estimated 29.1%; around 14% follow Islam according to the 2002 Census of Uganda, and 5.3% are Pentecostal. The sources do not describe traditional religious practices beyond the ancestor tradition and the Imbalu initiation ceremony.
