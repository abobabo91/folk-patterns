## At a glance
| | |
|---|---|
| Who | The Haya, also called Bahaya or Wahaya, are a Bantu ethnic group. |
| Where | Kagera Region in northwestern Tanzania, west of Lake Victoria. |
| How many | Over one million people, about 4% of Tanzania’s population. |
| Language | Haya, also called Oruhaya, a Bantu language. |

| Known for | Banana cultivation · Early iron smelting and carbon steel · Mushonge houses · Coffee traditions · Kingdoms ruled by bakama |

## Overview

The Haya live in northwestern Tanzania near Lake Victoria. Agriculture, especially banana growing, is central to life. Haya society was once organized into kingdoms ruled by bakama. The Haya are also known for early iron smelting and carbon steel production.

## Material culture

Haya material culture includes bark cloth, leaf and grass basketry, special houses, iron tools and weapons, and musical instruments.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Haya textile traditions use bark cloth, vegetable fibres, leaves, grass, and woven patterns.

- **Bark cloth** — A painted bark-cloth fragment from Buhaya shows a four-legged predator.
- **Vegetable-fibre dress** — Museum records include vegetable-fibre dresses and garments with leaf fringes.
- **Plaited leaf girdle** — Plaited leaf girdles could support a fringe of leaves.
- **Coffee-bean case** (*olushansha*) — An olushansha is a red-patterned woven case for coffee beans.
- **Winnowing tray** (*oluhungo*) — An oluhungo is a woven tray used to separate coffee beans from chaff.

### Clothing & dress

Recorded Haya clothing includes vegetable-fibre garments and leaf-fringed dress.

- **Men’s vegetable-fibre garment** — Museum records identify vegetable-fibre garments for men.
- **Girl’s skirt** — The records include a small girl’s skirt made from vegetable fibre.
- **Boy’s garment** — The records include a boy’s vegetable-fibre garment.
- **Vegetable-fibre dress** — The records include vegetable-fibre dresses, some with leaf fringes.

### Architecture

The traditional mushonge is a circular house whose form and size showed social rank.

- **Traditional round house** (*mushonge*) — A mushonge is circular, has a conical peak, and is built from the top down with reeds, poles, banana fibre, and grass.
- **Royal palace** (*nyaruju*) — Haya kings lived in a similar structure called a nyaruju.
- **Mud-walled house** (*kiteti*) — The mushonge later gave way to mud-walled kiteti houses.
- **Mudbrick house** (*banda*) — Mudbrick banda houses also replaced the mushonge.
- **Royal compound** (*kikale*) — The kikale was the royal compound where the king’s burial estate was located.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Haya metalwork is especially known for iron smelting, while everyday objects include tools, weapons, and carved wood items.

- **Forge shrine tree** (*Kaiija*) — Kaiija was a sacred shrine tree beneath which an iron furnace was found.
- **Iron furnace** — An iron furnace beneath Kaiija was carbon-dated to the 1st millennium BC.
- **Iron knife** — Museum records include iron knives and a finger knife.
- **Iron-pointed spear** — Haya-associated objects include spears with iron points.
- **Wooden shield** — One shield has a flat wooden core surrounded by a plaited edge.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources do not document Haya jewelry or named body-adornment traditions.

## Music & performance

Haya-associated instruments include bow harps, a monochord, and an oil gourd instrument.

- **Bow harp** (*bågharpa*) — A bågharpa is a bow harp with a calabash resonator.
- **Seven-string harp** (*trågharpa*) — A trågharpa has seven nylon strings.
- **Horn monochord** (*Hornfela*) — A Hornfela is a monochord made with a skin-covered horn and one string.
- **Oil gourd instrument** — The catalogue records an oil gourd with a reed straw.
- **Age-set training** (*omuteko*) — Omuteko included competitive sports, games, dancing, and singing for boys aged 10 to 12.

## Dance & theatre

Dancing and singing were competitive parts of royal training, but no named dances or theatre forms are recorded.

- **Royal training activities** (*omuteko*) — Within omuteko, boys competed in dancing and singing alongside sports and games.

## Festivals & rituals

Haya ritual life included new moon ceremonies, ancestor offerings, royal rites, and child-growth rituals.

- **Family ancestor shrine** (*ekikalo*) — An ekikalo was a family shrine where people offered banana beer, unripe bananas, and dried coffee beans.
- **Child-growth ritual** (*koshobekera*) — Koshobekera used bananas and was believed to help a child grow.
- **Royal burial estate** (*gashani*) — The gashani was the royal burial estate within the royal compound.
- **Regalia house** (*buchwankwanzi*) — The buchwankwanzi house held the former king’s regalia and ritual objects.
- **New moon ceremony** — Monthly new moon ceremonies included feasting, alcohol, offerings, and royal regalia.

## Foodways

Bananas, coffee, maize, cassava, sweet potatoes, beer, and fish or beef shape Haya foodways.

- **Banana grove** (*kibanja*) — The kibanja is the banana grove around the homestead.
- **Family plot** (*musiri*) — The musiri provides maize, cassava, sweet potatoes, and other staple foods.
- **Communal grassland** (*rweya*) — The rweya provides grazing, grass for mulching, thatching, and flooring.
- **Cooking bananas** (*matoke*) — Matoke are cooking bananas prepared with beans and either beef or fish.
- **Banana beer** (*orubisi*) — Orubisi is beer made from bananas.

## Oral tradition & literature

Haya oral traditions tell of kingdoms, royal clans, and ancient ironworking.

- **Divining house** (*buchwankwanzi*) — The buchwankwanzi was a divining house where kings and advisers made decisions based on diviners’ predictions.
- **Spitting pearls** (*buchwankwanzi*) — The term buchwankwanzi refers to words of wisdom spoken by prophets.
- **Bankango kingdom tradition** — Oral history links the Bankango clan with Kyamtwara and Kiziba.
- **Bahinda royal tradition** — Oral accounts describe the Bahinda royal clan entering Kyamtwara and ruling under King Rugomora Mahe.
- **Ironworking tradition** (*Kaiija*) — Traditions say ancestors smelted iron beneath the sacred shrine tree Kaiija.

## Language & religion

Haya is a Bantu language, and traditional religious life centered on clans, ancestors, royal rites, and Bacwezi beings.

- **Haya language** (*Oruhaya*) — Oruhaya is the Haya language, with high and low tones and nine tenses.
- **Clan** (*oluganda*) — Haya society is patrilineal and organized through clans called oluganda.
- **Bacwezi gods and goddesses** (*Bacwezi*) — Ancient Bacwezi gods and goddesses were linked with prosperity, fertility, famine, and disease.
- **Spirit medium** — Spirit mediums communicated with or channelled ancient Bacwezi beings.
- **Diviner** — Diviners helped guide royal decisions and religious life.
