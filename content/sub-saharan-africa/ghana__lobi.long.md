## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| Who | Lobi communities of the Black Volta border region. |
| Where | Mainly southwestern Burkina Faso, with communities in northwestern Ghana and northeastern Cote d'Ivoire. |
| Language | Lobiri, a Gur language; neighboring Birifor and Dagara communities have related customs but distinct linguistic identities. |
| Known for | Earthen compounds, shrine figures and vessels, basketry, pottery, and xylophone music. |

## Overview

The Lobi live across the borders of Burkina Faso, Ghana, and Cote d'Ivoire. Historical research places migration from present-day Ghana across the Black Volta in the late eighteenth century, followed by settlement in southwestern Burkina Faso. This page is mapped under Ghana, but several objects shown here were collected or attributed to Burkina Faso. Museum labels sometimes use "Lobi" for a wider cultural area; that does not make Birifor or Dagara synonymous with Lobi. Cecile de Rouville's field research describes Lobi communities across Burkina Faso and Cote d'Ivoire, while the University of Ghana's linguistic study separates Lobiri from Birifor. The different meanings of an ethnic label, language name, and museum attribution should remain visible when reading the gallery.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The selected museum objects document coiled plant-fiber baskets with leather-covered sections, including a set catalogued as wedding baskets. Their tight coils and repeated leather strips create visible geometric bands. The evidence here does not establish a single named Lobi textile or motif system. Cleveland dates its basket set only approximately and marks the Lobi attribution as uncertain. The weave and leatherwork are visible facts about the object; its precise workshop and original use require more specific documentation.

### Clothing & dress

A historical photograph in the gallery documents Lobi women's lip ornaments in 1958. It records particular people at a particular time, rather than a universal or present-day dress rule. The selected museum objects also include jewelry and waist adornments; their original wearers and uses should be read from each object's catalogue entry.

### Architecture

Lobi domestic architecture includes earthen compounds with household and storage spaces. Photographs in the gallery show shrines within and around these compounds. Shrine placement varies: a family shrine may occupy a room, roof terrace, or courtyard. Granaries and roof access are also documented in studies of Lobi architecture.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery can carry ritual as well as household functions. A pot documented by the Lam Museum was used to hold protective medicines and stood in a family shrine; its raised spikes discouraged casual handling. The gallery also contains wooden figures, metal and leather objects, baskets, and stools. Their functions vary and should not be inferred from appearance alone. The Cleveland headpost is identified as a *baathil*: the museum describes a temporary sanctuary for a dangerous spirit, with its lower pole buried in an altar. The Palmer Museum, by contrast, explains *bateba* as figures for family shrines that embody living guardian spirits. These museum categories should not be merged merely because both objects are carved wood.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The gallery's ornaments show the range of materials in museum collections, including metal and cowrie shells. A photograph of two women with double lip ornaments offers more direct evidence of adornment in use than an isolated museum object, though it cannot represent every Lobi community.

## Music & performance

The Royal Museum for Central Africa documents a 1962 recording identified as Lobi from Ghana, with *gyil* xylophones accompanying dance. Its instrument catalogue also links the gyil to Lobi, Dagati, and Sissala musicians. Funeral music is documented in the recordings of the Ghanaian xylophonist Kakraba Lobi. This evidence supports a real musical tradition, while also showing why a stage name or a regional instrument must not be used to collapse Lobi, Birifor, and Dagara identities.

## Dance & theatre

The same recording describes xylophone music providing a rhythm for dancing. The sources used here do not establish a separate named Lobi theatre form.

## Festivals & rituals

Divination, shrine care, and offerings form part of documented Lobi religious practice. A *thil* is a spirit; a diviner may receive instructions for a wooden *bateba* figure and other objects to be placed in a family shrine. Research on the *bur* initiation ceremonies describes a specific Lobi community in the Iridiaka region of Burkina Faso; practices need not be identical across the border region.

## Foodways

The sources used for this page document household pottery and compounds, but do not establish a distinct named Lobi dish. Shrine vessels and offerings should be understood in their ritual context, not presented as ordinary food preparation.

## Oral tradition & literature

Historical accounts of migration across the Black Volta provide regional context, but the available sources do not justify a single origin story for every community labelled Lobi. Diviners also convey instructions attributed to *thila*; these form part of a living oral religious practice.

## Language & religion

Lobiri is distinct from Birifor, despite frequent overlap in older ethnographic labels. The University of Ghana study notes that the two languages are not mutually intelligible. *Bateba* are associated with living guardian spirits in family shrines, not simply portraits of deceased ancestors. The Cleveland Museum describes a different Lobi-style headpost, *baathil*, with its own documented ritual use; it should not be treated as an ordinary *bateba*.

## Glossary

- *bateba* - wooden shrine figures associated with guardian spirits.
- *thil* (plural *thila*) - spirit.
- *gyil* - xylophone used in the wider Black Volta region.
- *baathil* - a ritual headpost identified by the Cleveland Museum.
