## Overview

The Gogo live mainly in central Tanzania. Their history includes hunting, gathering, farming, cattle keeping, caravan trade, famine, and changing relations with neighbouring peoples. Society values homesteads, kinship, neighbourliness, cattle, ritual leadership, and age-group defence. Museum records include beadwork, leather goods, pottery, baskets, weapons, smoking equipment, a drum, and a cowbell.

## Material culture

Gogo material culture includes beadwork, leather adornment, pottery, fibre baskets, wooden weapons, metal objects, smoking equipment, and a drum.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources name no Gogo textile or pattern traditions.

### Clothing & dress

The sources do not describe everyday clothing but record beadwork, leather adornment, and a warrior’s headdress.

- **Beaded leather belt** — A man’s belt used a hide base covered with beads.
- **Beaded armlets** — Beadwork and leather armlets are recorded.
- **Warrior’s hide headdress** — A composite headdress used an oval of hide with part of an animal’s tail projecting from it.
- **Beaded necklet** — A necklet used black, blue, red, and white beads with a leather pendant at the front.

### Architecture

The sources do not describe Gogo buildings or settlement plans, but the homestead was central to society.

- **Homestead group** — A homestead group was a basic social unit, and its young men could escort a strange traveller for many miles.
- **Baobab burial place** — People who died in unusual circumstances were sometimes thrown into the bush or into a baobab trunk because they were believed to lack a homestead.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Recorded everyday objects include pottery, baskets, weapons, smoking equipment, and a cowbell.

- **Pottery bowl** — The collection includes a dark-coloured, sub-globular pottery bowl.
- **Fibre basket** — A conical fibre basket had a small flat, concave base.
- **Iron axe** — An iron axe had a wooden handle.
- **Sword** — A sword had an aluminium hilt, black horn tip, iron blade, and painted wooden sheath.
- **Smoking water pipe** — A composite pipe had a pottery bowl, wooden stem, and gourd water-cooler with leather and iron.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Gogo adornment includes beaded belts, armlets, necklets, ear ornaments, and wire girdles.

- **Copper-wire ear ornament** — Some ear ornaments used copper wire twisted into a hairpin shape with biconical bead tips.
- **Wooden ear-plugs** — Wooden ear-plugs are recorded.
- **Wood and brass ear ornaments** — Some ear ornaments were made from wood and brass.
- **Wire pendant stick** — A wooden stick carried coiled and plaited wire pendants.
- **Women’s wire girdles** — Women’s girdles were made from coiled copper wire or white metal wire around a central core.

## Music & performance

The sources record Gogo musicians and a wooden drum but no named Gogo musical style.

- **Wooden drum** — A wooden drum with a skin membrane appears in the museum records.
- **Nyati group** — Mzee Mchoya Malogo is described as a musician and leader of the Nyati group from Nzali.
- **Hukwe Zawose** — Hukwe Zawose is named as a Gogo musician.
- **Patrick Balisidya** — Patrick Balisidya is named as a Gogo musician.
- **Ben Pol** (*Benard Michael Paul Mnyang'anga*) — Benard Michael Paul Mnyang'anga, also known as Ben Pol, is named as a Gogo musician.

## Festivals & rituals

Ritual leaders, age-group defence, and livestock bridewealth are important documented practices.

- **Ritual leadership** — Ritual leaders controlled rainmaking and fertility, provided medicines and protection, led initiation, and arbitrated serious disputes.
- **Age-group defence** — Warriors organized by age groups defended against the Kisongo, Maasai, and Wahehe and sometimes raided cattle.
- **Alarm response** — When an alarm sounded, able-bodied men were expected to take up arms and run toward the call.
- **Livestock bridewealth** — Marriage agreements included a number of livestock, and bridewealth was conventionally paid entirely in livestock.
- **Polygyny** — Older, well-established men sometimes had two or three wives.

## Foodways

The Gogo became farmers with cattle, but drought and famine repeatedly shaped food life.

- **Hunting and gathering** — The Gogo had an earlier tradition of hunting and gathering before becoming agriculturalists with cattle.
- **Agriculture with cattle** — By 1890, the Gogo had become agriculturalists with cattle.
- **Mutunya** — From 1917, the central region suffered three years of mutunya, described as a scramble for food and the worst famine in recorded Gogo history.
- **Drought famine** — Unreliable rainfall and drought caused repeated famines in Ugogo.

## Oral tradition & literature

- **Historical accounts** — European writers including Edward Hore, Henry M. Stanley, and Emin Pasha recorded accounts of the Gogo.
- **Stanley’s travel account** — Stanley’s In Darkest Africa preserves a long passage about travel through Ugogo.
- **Clan histories** — The sources describe the movement of Wagogo clans and the growth of new family and clan names, customs, and ritual roles.

## Language & religion

Gogo is a Bantu language with three dialects and is spoken by both Christians and Muslims.

- **West Gogo** (*Nyambwa; Cinyambwa*) — West Gogo is spoken west of Dodoma and in Manyoni.
- **Central Gogo** (*Nyaugogo; Cinyaugogo*) — Central Gogo is spoken around Dodoma.
- **East Gogo** (*Tumba; Citumba*) — East Gogo is spoken to the east.
- **Anglican Gogo** — Gogo is a major language of the Anglican Church of Tanzania.
- **Caravan-trade Islam** — Islam influenced the Gogo through nineteenth-century caravan trade, while more people also learned Swahili.
