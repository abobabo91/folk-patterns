## Overview

The Yoruba are a West African people of southwestern Nigeria and the adjoining parts of Benin and Togo — a contiguous cultural zone called Yorubaland, running from the Ana-Ife country of central Togo east to the Ilaje coast of Ondo State, and from the Niger River south to the Atlantic lagoons. They number more than 50 million in Africa, making them one of the continent's largest ethnic groups and 20.7% of Nigeria's population; their language, of the Edekiri branch of Yoruboid within Volta-Niger (Niger–Congo), has more native speakers than any other Niger–Congo language. They were historically among Africa's most urban peoples, living in walled, concentric cities built around an *Ọba*'s palace and the *Ọjà Ọba* (king's market) — Ile-Ife, Ọyọ-Ilé, Ijebu Ode, Abẹokuta, Ibadan, Èkó (Lagos). That urbanism underwrote craft guilds of exceptional depth: the naturalistic brass and terracotta heads of 12th–14th-century Ile-Ife are among the technical high points of world sculpture, and the *òrìṣà* pantheon carried across the Atlantic seeded Santería, Candomblé, Umbanda and Trinidad Orisha.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Adire** — indigo-resist cloth, historically an Egba and Ijebu women's craft centred on Abeokuta and Ibadan. Three techniques are distinguished: *adire oniko* (raffia-tied and stitched resist), *adire alabere* (needle-stitched resist), and *adire eleko*, in which cassava-starch paste is painted freehand with a feather or palm rib, or stencilled through zinc sheet, before immersion in *elu* (Indigofera) dye vats. The freehand *eleko* work is what separates Yoruba indigo from the tied-and-dyed cloths of neighbouring peoples: it is drawing, not just binding.

**Aso oke** — narrow-strip cloth woven by men on the double-heddle horizontal treadle loom, sewn edge-to-edge into gowns and wrappers. The three classic grades are *sanyan* (beige-grey, from wild Anaphe silk), *etu* ("guinea fowl," dark indigo with fine white weft stripes) and *alaari* (crimson, from imported magenta waste silk). Supplementary-weft float patterning and openwork *eyelet* effects date the prestige weaves.

**Ijebu cloth** (*itagbe*, *ikale*) — the Ijebu kingdom's dense indigo-and-white strip cloths, including the fringed *itagbe* shoulder cloths worn as insignia by Ogboni society members, and the figured cloths traded east to the Niger Delta where they entered Igbo and Ijaw ritual use as prestige imports.

**Yoruba beadwork** — glass-bead embroidery over cloth-covered cane armatures, the exclusive prerogative of the *adigba* bead-workers' guild. Products include the fringed *ade* crown, *ileke* necklaces, *apo* bags for Ifa diviners, beaded footstools, staffs and *òrìṣà* garments. Ile-Ife's industrial-scale glass-bead production around 1300 CE is the deep root of this craft.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Olokun* (the sea-goddess grid of Ijebu cloth); *ibadandun* ("Ibadan is sweet," a stencilled adire design of forks, spoons and Mapo Hall); *olokun* and *eleko* stencil bands of interlace; *oniko* concentric rings; *etu* guinea-fowl speckle stripes; *sanyan* ribbed beige bands; *aguntan* (ram) heads; *igbin* (snail-shell spiral); *opon* interlace borders framing Ifa trays; *oju* (eye) and *oju-ona* ("eye of design," the cross-hatched ground on carved surfaces); *ejo* (serpent) and *ejo meji* paired snakes on crowns; the surmounting *eye* (bird) of the *ade* crown.

### Clothing & dress

Men wear the *agbada*, a wide-sleeved flowing gown of four to eight *aso oke* strips, over a long-sleeved shirt (*buba*) and drawstring trousers (*sokoto*), with the *fila* cap — the crushable *fila abeti-aja* ("dog-ear cap") and the cylindrical *gobi* are the standard forms. Formality is read from the gown's weight and the grade of cloth: *sanyan*, *etu* and *alaari* mark the highest ranks. Women wear the *iro* (wrapper) with a matching *buba* blouse, the *gele* head-tie tied high and structured for ceremony, the *ipele* or *iborun* shoulder sash, and, among market women and titled elders, additional wrappers layered as a display of wealth. Coral and carnelian strands (*ileke*) signal chiefly status; *Ọbas* add the beaded *ade* crown with its veil of bead fringes, beaded slippers and a beaded fly-whisk or staff. Ogboni members wear *itagbe* cloth over the left shoulder. Ceremonial dress diverges sharply from daily wear in the masquerade context, where *egungun* performers wear stacked appliqué panels of imported and local cloth that fly outward as the wearer spins.

### Architecture

The Yoruba compound (*agbo ile*) is a rectangular courtyard house: ranges of rooms built of puddled mud (*amo*) laid in courses, roofed with thatch and later corrugated iron, enclosing one or more open *ile-ita* courtyards where cooking, grain-drying and lineage gatherings happen. Verandahs run along the courtyard face, their roofs carried on carved hardwood posts (*opo*) — the signature Yoruba architectural sculpture, bearing equestrian figures, mounted warriors, kneeling mothers with children, and caryatid forms; the Ekiti and Ìgbómìnà carving schools produced the most elaborate. The *Ọba*'s *aafin* (palace) is the same plan expanded to dozens of courtyards at the town's centre, facing the king's market, with carved doors (*ilekun*) in horizontal registers of figural relief. Defensive works were substantial: the Ife Empire's *iyàrà* moats and trenches, the Sungbo's Eredo earthwork ringing the Ijebu kingdom, and Ọyọ-Ilé's outer wall, reported at over twenty feet with ten gates. In 19th-century Lagos, returnee communities from Brazil and Cuba — the *Amaros*/*Agudas* — introduced a distinct stuccoed Afro-Brazilian townhouse style.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Ile-Ife's bronze-casters worked the lost-wax process to a naturalism unmatched in sub-Saharan Africa; the tradition is credited to **Obalufon II**, venerated as patron *òrìṣà* of brass-casting, weaving and regalia. **Ife terracotta** heads and figures share that portrait sensibility in fired clay, as do the puzzling **Esie soapstone** figures of Kwara State — over a thousand seated stone figures of unknown attribution. **Owo ivory** carving, at the Yoruba–Benin frontier, produced bracelets and condiment vessels in relief. Ritual metalwork centres on **Ogboni edan** — paired male-and-female brass staff figures joined by a chain, emblems of the earth-cult society — and on the iron staffs (*opa osoro*, *osun*) of Osanyin, topped with birds. **Opon Ifa** divination trays are carved with a face of Eshu at the top edge and a border of figural registers; tapping ivory *iroke* keeps time during divination. Household ceramics include the *isaasun* cooking pot, *amu* water jar and *ikoko* storage vessel; calabashes (*igba*) are pyro-engraved.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Glass and coral beads carry rank rather than mere ornament: red *iyun* (coral) and *segi* blue glass beads were royal prerogatives. *Ileke* strands are worn at the neck, wrist and waist — the waist-bead is a women's intimate adornment. Diviners wear the *ide Ifa* wrist-band and *ileke Ifa* of alternating green and brown *Orunmila* beads. Brass and copper anklets and bracelets were cast by the same guilds that made *edan*. Facial scarification (*ila*), in lineage- and town-specific sets of vertical or horizontal cheek cuts (*abaja*, *pele*, *keke*, *gombo*), identified origin, and *pele* marks are still read as Yoruba signature. Women's hair is braided and threaded into named coiffures — *suku* (gathered into a crest), *kolese* (cornrows to the nape), *ipako-elede*, *koroba* — which the terracotta and bronze heads of Ife and the carved *ibeji* figures record in detail. **Ibeji** twin figures themselves are washed, dressed, beaded and camwood-rubbed as bodies.

## Music & performance

Yoruba music is built on talking drums, whose tonal contours reproduce the pitch patterns of the language. The *dundun* ensemble is led by the hourglass tension drum *iya ilu* with leather tension cords squeezed under the arm to bend pitch, supported by *kerikeri*, *gudugudu* (a small kettle drum struck with leather thongs) and *kanango*. **Bata drum** ensembles — conical two-headed drums, *iya ilu bata*, *itotele*, *kudi*, *omele* — belong to Sango and Egungun worship and their repertoire is the drummed praise-poetry of the thunder god. Other instruments: the *sekere* gourd rattle, *agogo* iron bell, *agidigbo* thumb-plucked lamellophone, *sakara* frame drum, *gbedu* royal drum reserved for the *Ọba*, and *kakaki* trumpets at northern courts. Vocal genres are deeply verbal: *oriki* (praise-name chains recited for persons, lineages and *òrìṣà*), *ijala* (hunters' chant for Ogun, delivered in Ẹgbẹ Ọdẹ contexts), *ewi* (declaimed poetry), *rara* (a slow praise-chant), *esa* (Egungun ancestral chant), and *iyere Ifa* (the sung verse of the Ifa corpus). From the 1920s these fed the popular genres of *sakara*, *apala*, *juju* and *fuji*, which remain the music of Yoruba weddings, naming ceremonies and funerals.

## Dance & theatre

**Gelede** is the best-documented Yoruba masquerade theatre: performed in the Ketu and Egbado west by male dancers in paired masks with superstructures carrying carved scenes of daily life, it honours *awon iya wa* ("our mothers") and the spiritual power of elder women, with a night vigil (*efe*) of satirical singing preceding the afternoon dance. **Egungun** masquerades embody returning ancestors; the *paka* dancer spins so that layered cloth panels fly out, and the masker's voice is altered to mark him as non-human. **Epa** masquerades of the Ekiti and Ìgbómìnà carry massive helmet masks surmounted by equestrian or maternity groups, danced in leaping displays that prove the wearer's strength. The Ìgbómìnà **Elewe** masquerade is renowned in its own right. Sango priests dance with the double-bladed *oshe Sango* wand (**Shango staff**) balanced on the head; Ogun devotees dance with iron. Distinct from these ritual forms is the 20th-century **Yoruba travelling theatre** — Hubert Ogunde, Duro Ladipo, Kola Ogunmola — which took *egungun* aesthetics, *oriki* declamation and drumming into professional touring opera.

## Festivals & rituals

The Yoruba year is a cycle of *òrìṣà* festivals, each tied to a town. **Osun Osogbo** (August) takes the *Arugba* votive-calabash bearer to the sacred grove on the Osun river. The **Olojo** festival at Ile-Ife marks the day of first dawn, when the Ooni wears the *Are* crown. **Eyo** in Lagos brings out white-robed, hatted *Adamu Orisa* masqueraders with *opambata* staffs to honour a departed *Ọba*. **Egungun** festivals run in the dry season across Ọyọ and Egba country; **Gelede** is danced at the onset of the rains and in response to drought or epidemic. **Ogun** festivals involve dog sacrifice and a display of blacksmiths' iron; the **Ifa festival** (*Odun Ifa*) at Ile-Ife renews the divination year. New-yam rites (*Odun Ijesu*) precede permission to eat the harvest.

Life-cycle rites: *ikomojade*, the naming ceremony on the seventh, eighth or ninth day, where the infant is touched to water, salt, palm oil, honey, kola, bitter kola and pepper, each with a spoken wish; consultation of Ifa (*esentaye*, "stepping into the world") to learn the child's destiny; twin birth, which requires *ibeji* honours and, on a twin's death, the commissioning of a carved *ere ibeji* to house the soul. Marriage proceeds through *itoro* (formal request), *idana* (bride-gifts including kola, honey, salt and cloth) and the bride's *ekun iyawo* — the "bride's lament," sung as she leaves her father's compound. Funerals for the aged are celebratory: drumming, *egungun* appearance, and *isinku* burial within the compound floor, followed by the second burial that installs the deceased among the ancestors.

## Foodways

The staples are yam, cassava, maize, cocoyam and plantain, pounded or fermented into stiff porridges eaten with soup by hand. *Iyan* (pounded yam) and *amala* — a dark swallow made from *elubo*, dried yam flour, a specifically Ọyọ–Ibadan marker — are eaten with *ewedu* (jute mallow, whisked with a broom-whisk *ijabe* to its characteristic draw), *gbegiri* (skinned-bean purée) and *obe ata* (red pepper stew); *amala, ewedu, gbegiri* served together is the emblematic Ibadan plate. *Eba* is granulated cassava *gari* stirred with hot water. Steamed bean puddings — *moinmoin* (in leaves) and *ekuru* (undressed, with *ata dindin* oil-sauce) — and fried bean fritters *akara* are the morning foods, with *ogi* (fermented maize gruel, *eko* when set firm). *Efo riro* is the spinach-and-locust-bean stew; *iru* (fermented locust bean) is the defining seasoning, as is smoked fish and *egusi* melon seed thickener. Palm oil (*epo pupa*) dominates over groundnut oil. *Isu sisun* (roast yam) with palm oil is street food; *asaro* is a yam porridge. Kola nut (*obi*) and bitter kola (*orogbo*) are offered to guests, used in divination and in every rite of passage; *emu* (palm wine) and *ogogoro* (distilled palm spirit) are libation drinks. Muslims abstain from alcohol and pork; snail (*igbin*) is a ritual food of Obatala devotees, who also avoid palm wine.

## Oral tradition & literature

Yoruba culture was an oral tradition, and its literary core is *itan* — the whole complex of songs, histories, myths and cultural concepts, including the *Odu Ifa*: 256 divination chapters, each with its own body of verses (*ese Ifa*) which the *babalawo* memorises and chants to interpret the figure cast with palm nuts (*ikin*) or the divining chain (*opele*) on the **opon Ifa** tray. *Oriki* praise-poetry, *ijala* hunters' chant, *rara*, *ewi* and *esa* are performed genres with named practitioners. Narrative folklore turns on *Ijapa* the tortoise, the trickster whose greed and cunning carry the moral, and on Eshu at the *oríta méta* crossroads. Proverbs (*owe*) are the mark of rhetorical competence — "*owe l'esin oro*," the proverb is the horse of speech. Written Yoruba literature began with missionary work in Abeokuta and Bishop Samuel Ajayi Crowther's 1850s grammar; D. O. Fagunwa's *Ogboju Ode Ninu Igbo Irunmale* (1938) created a literary idiom out of hunters' tales, and Amos Tutuola, Wole Soyinka and Duro Ladipo drew on the same material.

## Language & religion

Yoruba belongs to the Edekiri group of Yoruboid, within Volta-Niger (Niger–Congo), with three dialect areas: North-West (corresponding to the Ọyọ Empire and linguistically the most innovative), Central, and South-East (associated with Benin Empire expansion after c. 1450). Literary Yoruba rests on Ọyọ–Egba speech: the missionaries' orthography follows Abeokuta phonemes while the morpho-syntax follows Ọyọ–Ibadan. It is written in Latin script with subdot vowels and tone marks. Most Yoruba today are Muslim or Christian — Islam entered from the north, Christianity from the coast — but *òrìṣà* concepts remain operative: *Olodumare* as supreme being, *ase* as the empowering force, *òrìṣà* (Obatala, Ogun, Orunmila, Osun, Eshu, Olokun, Yemoja, Osanyin, Sango) as mediators, and ancestral veneration through Egungun. Divination by Ifa, the *Ogboni*/Osugbo earth cult, and Aladura Christian churches that absorbed possession and vision practice all coexist. Across the Atlantic the same pantheon survives as Santería, Candomblé, Umbanda, Kélé and Trinidad Orisha.
