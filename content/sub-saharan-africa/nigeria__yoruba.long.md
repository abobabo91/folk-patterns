## Overview

The Yoruba (*Yorùbá*, self-designation *Ọmọ Odùduwà*, "children of Oduduwa") are a Volta-Niger people of West Africa whose homeland — *Ilẹ̀ Yorùbá* or Yorubaland — occupies the southwestern quadrant of Nigeria (the states of Oyo, Ogun, Osun, Ondo, Ekiti, Lagos and parts of Kwara and Kogi) and extends westward across the Beninese border into central and southern Benin and eastern Togo, where cognate populations are known as Nagot, Anago, Itsha, Idaisha and Ketu. With a total population commonly estimated at 45–55 million, they are one of the largest ethno-linguistic clusters on the African continent and, through the transatlantic slave trade, the demographic and ritual seedbed of major diasporic religions in Cuba (Lucumí/Santería), Brazil (Candomblé Ketu), Trinidad (Shango) and Haiti (Nago rites of Vodou). Their language, *Yorùbá*, is a tonal three-register tongue of the Volta-Niger branch of Niger-Congo, with a rich literary standard descended largely from the Ọyọ dialect. Yoruba civilisation crystallised around a network of sacred city-states — *Ilé-Ifẹ̀*, mythic point of the world's creation; Ọyọ, the imperial cavalry power of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries; Ijebu, Ekiti, Owo, Ondo, Egba and Ketu — each headed by a crowned ruler (*ọba*) whose regalia and beaded veil (*adé*) mediate between the town and the ancestral world. In folk-culture terms the Yoruba matter as makers of the naturalistic bronze and terracotta heads of Ile-Ife, the indigo resist cloths of Abeokuta, the beaded crowns of the *ọbas*, the *gèlèdé* masquerade inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, the *Ifá* divination corpus (also UNESCO-listed), and the *bàtá* and *dùndún* talking-drum orchestras that carry the tonal language into music.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

***Aṣọ òkè*** — "cloth of the top country," the prestige narrow-strip weave of Yorubaland. Handspun cotton and silk (*ṣànyán*, a beige wild-silk) are woven on a men's double-heddle loom into strips roughly 10–15 cm wide, then edge-sewn into wrappers, robes, head-ties and men's *agbádá* gowns. The three canonical grades are *ṣànyán* (natural beige silk), *alaári* (magenta, historically from waste silk dyed with imported cochineal or camwood), and *ẹtù* (indigo-and-white pinstripe worn for chieftaincy and burial).

***Àdìrẹ*** — indigo-dyed resist cloth, historically a women's industry centred on Abeokuta and Ibadan. Two main techniques: *àdìrẹ ẹlẹ́kọ*, in which cassava-starch paste is hand-painted with a feather quill or stencilled over the cloth as a resist before dipping in the *ẹlú* (indigo) vat; and *àdìrẹ oníkò*, tied and stitched resist using raffia. Named patterns — *Olókun* ("goddess of the sea"), *Ìbàdàn dùn* ("Ibadan is sweet"), *ọlẹ́* ("frogs"), *ẹdan* — carry proverbs and city loyalties.

**Ijebu *itagbe* and *aṣọ olóna*** — the Ijebu weave a distinctive dark blue cloth patterned with white weft-float figures (crocodiles, birds, ceremonial staffs) used as regalia of the Ogboni society and as prestige wrappers; *itagbe* shoulder cloths signal senior title.

***Kìjìpa*** — a coarser handwoven cotton cloth used for everyday wrappers and formerly as the ground for *àdìrẹ*.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Ọba* (king) and crown imagery, *ẹyẹ* (bird, especially the paired birds of the *adé*), *ejò* (snake, associated with Ọ̀ṣùmàrè the rainbow), *àkùkọ* (cockerel), *ọ̀pá Ṣàngó* (Shango's double-axe thunderbolt *oṣé*), *ìlẹ́kẹ̀* (bead strings), *ẹ̀wà* (beans of prosperity), and interlace patterns read as *ọwọ́ l'ọwọ́* ("hand in hand," social bond).

### Clothing & dress

Men wear the three-piece *agbádá* — a flowing embroidered outer gown over a long tunic (*bùbá*) and drawstring trousers (*sòkòtò*) — with a soft cap (*fìlà*), most commonly the folded *fìlà abẹtí ajá* ("dog-eared cap") or the cylindrical *gọ̀bí*. For less formal wear the *dàṣíkí* or *bùbá* is worn alone. Women wear a wrapper (*ìró*) tied at the waist over a matching blouse (*bùbá*), a shoulder sash (*ìborùn* or *ìpèlé*) and, most conspicuously, the sculpted head-tie (*gèlè*), tied for weddings and church into elaborate architectural forms in stiff *aṣọ òkè* or damask. The *ìyàwó* (bride) and the age-mates of a celebrant wear coordinated *aṣọ ẹbí* ("family cloth"), a defining Yoruba social practice by which kin and friends dress in a single announced fabric to mark an event. Chiefs and *ọbas* wear the great conical beaded crown (*adé*) with its fringe of beads (*ìboju*) veiling the face — the veil being the point, since the *ọba*'s gaze is held to be too potent for direct human sight — together with beaded slippers, staff and horse-tail flywhisk (*ìrùkẹ̀rẹ̀*). Priests of specific *òrìṣà* mark themselves by cloth colour and bead: white for Ọbàtálá, red-and-white for Ṣàngó, blue for Yemọja, yellow-and-brass for Ọ̀ṣun.

### Architecture

The classical Yoruba town is a dense compound-based settlement radiating from a central palace (*àáfin*) and market (*ọjà*), enclosed historically by earthen walls and moats (the seventeenth-century walls of Ijebu-Ode and the vast *Sungbo's Eredo* earthwork are the most extensive surviving examples in sub-Saharan Africa). The domestic unit is the *agbo ilé*, a rectangular multi-family compound of mud-wall (*amọ̀*) rooms opening onto a single interior courtyard (*àkodì*) that catches rain and light, roofed originally in thatch and by the twentieth century in corrugated iron pans. Palaces and important shrines carry the signature Yoruba veranda supported on carved hardwood posts (*ọ̀pó*) — the veranda post is one of the great genres of African sculpture, worked by named masters such as Olowe of Ise (Ekiti, d. 1938) with superimposed equestrian, maternity and captive figures. Doors (*ìlẹ̀kùn*) of palaces and Ogboni houses were carved in low relief with narrative panels of processions, battle and sacrifice. The sacred grove of Osun-Osogbo, replanted and rebuilt from the 1950s onward by the Austrian-Yoruba artist Susanne Wenger and local shrine-priests, preserves and reinvents Yoruba shrine architecture in monumental cement sculpture and is inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Yoruba brass and copper-alloy heads and full figures of Ile-Ife, made by the lost-wax (*cire perdue*) method between roughly the twelfth and fifteenth centuries, are among the technically most refined castings of the pre-modern world — thin-walled, naturalistically modelled, and finished with fine parallel striations that may indicate scarification or veiling. The related Ife terracotta heads share the same idiom in fired clay. Owo, on the Yoruba-Edo frontier, developed a distinguished ivory-carving tradition — pendant masks, bracelets, ceremonial tusks — closely related to Benin court art. At Esie in present-day Kwara State stands a group of over eight hundred soapstone figures (*ère Èsìè*) of uncertain origin, now the object of an annual festival. Ogboni society elders carry paired brass *edan Ògbóni* — a linked male-and-female staff joined by a chain, driven point-first into the earth in judgement. *Ifá* diviners work over a carved wooden divination tray (*ọpọ́n Ifá*) whose raised border carries figurative registers (Èṣù face, birds, kolanuts) around a central meal-dusted field where the sixteen-cowrie or palm-nut oracle is cast; the diviner's tapper (*ìróké Ifá*) summons Ọ̀rúnmìlà. Twin figures (*ère ìbejì*) — small standing carved wooden statuettes commissioned when one or both of a twin pair dies, then fed, washed and clothed by the mother — form the single largest genre of Yoruba sculpture in world collections, the Yoruba having the highest twinning rate on earth. Ṣàngó devotees carry the *oṣé Ṣàngó*, a wand or dance staff surmounted by a double-headed axe representing the thunder-stones the god casts to earth. Everyday ceramic ware includes water pots (*ìkòkò*), palm-wine coolers and the black-burnished cooking pots of Ilorin and Ijebu; blacksmiths (*aláágbẹ̀dẹ*), under the patronage of Ògún, forge the hoe (*ọkọ́*), cutlass (*àdá*), and the iron staffs of Ọ̀ṣun and Ọ̀ṣọ́ọ̀ṣì.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Beadwork (*ìṣẹ́ ìlẹ́kẹ̀*) is the pre-eminent Yoruba luxury art. Glass beads — historically imported through trans-Saharan and Atlantic trade and reworked locally — are threaded and appliquéd over cloth or leather armatures to make the *adé* crown, the *ìlẹ̀kẹ̀* neck-strand of each *òrìṣà* priesthood (colour-coded to the god), diviners' bags (*apo Ifá*), flywhisk handles, boots and even full-body dance costumes. Colour reads as invocation: white beads for Ọbàtálá, red-and-white for Ṣàngó, amber-yellow for Ọ̀ṣun, blue-and-clear for Yemọja, red-and-black for Èṣù. Women wear waist beads (*bèbè ìdí*) under the wrapper as an intimate marker of femininity and, historically, of reproductive stage. Facial scarification (*ìlà*) — sets of vertical or horizontal cheek marks such as the three *pélé* of Oyo or the *àbàjà* of Egba — until the mid-twentieth century identified lineage and town of origin; the practice has largely lapsed. Camwood paste (*osùn*) is rubbed on the bodies of new mothers, brides and initiates as a red cosmetic and blessing.

## Music & performance

The tonal structure of the Yoruba language is inseparable from its music: the hourglass pressure drum *dùndún* ("talking drum"), whose leather tension cords are squeezed under the drummer's arm to slide pitch, literally speaks Yoruba proverbs, praise-names (*oríkì*) and *Ifá* verses, and is answered by a battery of supporting drums (*gángan*, *ìsáájú*, *ìkẹ̀hìn*, *gúdúgúdú*). The *bàtá* — a set of three double-headed conical drums (*ìyáàlù*, *omele abo*, *omele akọ*) sacred to Ṣàngó — carries the liturgy of that *òrìṣà* and, transported across the Atlantic, became the ritual drum of Cuban Santería. Other instruments include the *ṣẹ̀kẹ̀rẹ̀* (netted-gourd rattle), the *agogo* iron bell, the *gòjé* single-string fiddle, and the *kàkàkí* long metal trumpet of northern Yoruba courts. Court music (*ìlù ọba*) is organised around the *dùndún* ensemble at the palace gate playing the *ọba*'s praise-poem at fixed hours. In the twentieth century a series of secular popular genres grew out of these roots: *jùjú* (Tunde King, I. K. Dairo, King Sunny Adé, Ebenezer Obey) built on *dùndún* and Christian hymnody; *fújì* (Ayinde Barrister, Kollington, Wasiu Ayinde) grew from Muslim *wéré* wake-music into a percussion-driven mainstream; and Fela Anikulapo-Kuti fused highlife, jazz and Yoruba percussion into Afrobeat. *Ifá* divination chant, and the *ẹsà* funeral chant of the Egúngún society, are considered the most demanding sung repertoires.

## Dance & theatre

Yoruba dance is polyrhythmic and segmented — shoulders, torso, hips and feet each answering a different drum in the ensemble — and is almost always the visible half of a masquerade or *òrìṣà* possession. The *gẹ̀lẹ̀dẹ́* spectacle of the western Yoruba (Ketu, Ohori, Egbado, and across the Benin border), inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List in 2001, honours "the mothers" (*àwọn ìyá wa*) — elderly women, ancestors and the goddess-figure Ìyá Nlá whose spiritual power (*àṣẹ*) is held to be at once creative and dangerous. Paired male dancers in carved superstructure headdresses depicting scenes of daily and satirical life (a hunter with prey, a woman with a sewing machine, a policeman, a European) dance from afternoon into night to a bàtá ensemble. The *Egúngún* masquerade, by contrast, embodies the collective ancestors: fully covered dancers in stitched panels of hundreds of layered cloth strips (*ẹkú*) whirl so that the cloth becomes a horizontal disc, the ancestor made visible without any human skin exposed. The *Ẹpa* masquerade of northern Ekiti features enormous helmet masks with tiered superstructure figures (mothers with children, warriors on horseback) leapt over a mound as a test of male initiates. The *Àgẹ̀mọ* cult of Ijebu and the water-spirit masks of the Lagos lagoon complete the repertoire. Out of the *aláàrìnjó* travelling masque-theatre grew the twentieth-century Yoruba folk-opera of Hubert Ogunde, Duro Ladipo (*Ọba Kòso*, on Ṣàngó) and Kola Ogunmola, and thence the Nigerian film industry, Nollywood, whose earliest video-films were Yoruba-language.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual calendar is organised around the *òrìṣà*. The annual *Ọ̀ṣun-Òṣogbo* festival in August, culminating in the procession of the *Arugbá* (the virgin votary bearing the calabash of offerings) from the palace to the sacred grove on the Oṣun river, draws devotees from across the Atlantic world. Ọyọ celebrates the *Bẹrẹ* festival of the *Aláàfin*; Ile-Ife the *Olójó* festival of Ògún, at which the *Ooni* wears the ancient *Aàrẹ* crown said to have belonged to Odùduwà; Ondo the *Odún Ọbà*; Ekiti the *Ẹpa*; and the Egba of Abeokuta the *Lísàbí* festival commemorating their eighteenth-century liberation from Ọyọ. The *Egúngún* festival, timed to each town's own calendar, brings the lineage ancestors out for several days of masquerade. Rites of passage — naming (*ìkọmọ*) on the eighth day (or seventh/ninth by sex), during which the child receives its personal name, its *oríkì* praise-string, and tastes salt, honey, kola, palm-oil, water and dried fish as symbols of life's flavours; marriage, with its *ìdána* bride-price ceremony and *aṣọ ẹbí* uniform cloth; and the elaborate second burial (*ìsìnkú*) which formally installs the deceased as an *egúngún* ancestor — organise the life cycle.

## Foodways

The Yoruba staple is a starchy "swallow" (*ọ̀bẹ̀ àti oúnjẹ*) eaten by hand with a soup. *Iyán* (pounded yam, made from boiled yam beaten in a wooden mortar to a smooth elastic mass) is the prestige form; *àmàlà* (dark yam-flour or plantain-flour paste, a signature of Ibadan and Ọyọ) and *ẹ̀bà* (garri, fermented cassava granules stirred in hot water) are the everyday alternatives. Soups include *ẹ̀fọ́ riro* (thick leafy-greens stew with palm oil, ground *ẹ̀gúsí* melon seed, smoked fish, dried crayfish and locust-bean condiment *ìrù*); *ilá alaṣepọ̀* (okra); *gbẹ̀gìrì* (yellow bean soup, characteristically served alongside *àmàlà* and pepper soup *ewédú* as the Ibadan trio *abula*); and *ọ̀bẹ̀ àtà dídín* (fried-pepper stew). *Jollof* rice — the tomato-and-pepper one-pot rice common across West Africa — is a mainstay of celebration, disputed with Ghana and Senegal. Street and festival foods include *àkàrà* (fried bean-cake), *mọ́ínmọ́ín* (steamed bean pudding wrapped in leaves), *ẹ̀kọ*/agidi (fermented maize custard), *dòdò* (fried plantain), *ṣuya* (Hausa-derived spiced grilled meat now ubiquitous), and *bọ̀lì* (roasted plantain with groundnut). *Ìrù* (fermented locust bean) and palm oil (*epo pupa*) give Yoruba cooking its characteristic aroma and colour. Palm wine (*ẹmu*) tapped from *Elaeis guineensis* and *Raphia* palms is the traditional drink, along with maize beer (*ọtí ṣẹ̀kẹ̀tẹ́*) and, under Muslim influence in the north, non-alcoholic *kunun*.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Yoruba oral corpus is one of the largest and best-documented in Africa. At its centre stands *Ifá*, the divination system of Ọ̀rúnmìlà, whose 256 *odù* (chapters) each contain hundreds of memorised verses (*ẹsẹ Ifá*) — mythic narratives, medicinal prescriptions, proverbs and prayers — recited by the *babaláwo* ("father of secrets") after casting sixteen palm nuts or the divining chain (*ọ̀pẹ̀lẹ̀*). *Ifá* was proclaimed a UNESCO Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2005 and inscribed on the Representative List in 2008. Alongside *Ifá* stand *oríkì*, the praise-poems attached to persons, lineages, towns and *òrìṣà*, chanted by women elders and by professional bards; *ìjálá*, the hunters' chant sacred to Ògún; *rárà*, the melismatic praise-chant of the *akéwì*; *ẹ̀sà egúngún*, the ancestral chant; *iwì*, and the moonlight *àlọ́ àpamọ̀* riddles and *àlọ́ àpagbè* animal-tale songs of children. Written Yoruba literature begins with Bishop Samuel Àjàyí Crowther's nineteenth-century orthography and grammar; D. O. Fágúnwà's Yoruba-language novels of the 1930s–50s (*Ògbójú Ọdẹ Nínú Igbó Irúnmalẹ̀*), translated into English by Wole Soyinka as *The Forest of a Thousand Daemons*; and, in English, the Nobel laureate Wole Soyinka, Amos Tutuọla (*The Palm-Wine Drinkard*) and Ben Okri.

## Language & religion

*Yorùbá* is a Volta-Niger language of the Niger-Congo family, tonal (high, mid, low, with contour glides), with about forty mutually intelligible dialects (Ọyọ, Ijebu, Egba, Ekiti, Ijesha, Ondo, Owo, Ikale, Ilaje, Awori, Ketu, Sabe, and others) grouped under a Standard Yoruba based on Ọyọ and used in press, church and school. Religion is threefold and, in practice, overlapping: indigenous *ẹ̀sìn ìbílẹ̀*, structured around the supreme being Olódùmarè/Olórun and the pantheon of some four hundred plus one *òrìṣà* (Ọbàtálá the sculptor of humans, Ògún the iron-god of war and craft, Ṣàngó the thunder-king of Ọyọ, Ọ̀ṣun the river-goddess of love and wealth, Yemọja the mother of waters, Ọya the tornado, Èṣù the trickster-messenger, Ọ̀rúnmìlà the diviner, Ọ̀ṣọ́ọ̀ṣì the hunter, Bàbáluàiyé of smallpox and healing); Sunni Islam, brought in from the north from the seventeenth century onward and dominant among the Yoruba of Ọyọ, Ibadan, Iwo, Ede and Ilorin; and Christianity — first Anglican and Methodist mission churches from the 1840s, then Roman Catholic, then the massive twentieth-century Aladura and Pentecostal movements (Cherubim and Seraphim, Christ Apostolic Church, Redeemed Christian Church of God) that originated in Yorubaland and now project globally. Most Yoruba families contain adherents of two or three of these strands; the *òrìṣà* framework saturates even nominally Muslim and Christian households through naming, proverbs, and festival calendar.
