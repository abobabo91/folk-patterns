## Overview

The Kissi are a West African ethnolinguistic group found in Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone. They are the fourth largest ethnic group in Guinea, making up 6.2% of the population. They speak Kissi, a Mel language of the Niger–Congo language family. The Kissi are associated with basket-making, weaving on vertical looms, ironworking and the production of the “Kissi penny.” Their material culture includes iron tools and weapons, pottery, spinning and weaving implements, musical instruments, masks, head coverings and objects connected with the *Bundu* or *Bandu* societies. Kissi traditional religion includes ancestor worship and prayer to deceased relatives.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Vertical-loom weaving** — The Kissi are well known for weaving on vertical looms. Museum records also document a *kpaba*, a weaving implement made of vegetable material, wood, rotang and plant-fibre cords.

**Kondoyila** — A *Kondoyila* is a grey cotton net shirt with large knotted meshes and cauris around the neck opening and sleeves. The catalogue identifies it as part of the dance costume of the *Bandu* society.

**Jo** — The *Jo* is a dance skirt made from plant fibre. Its waistband is made of twisted *Urèna* fibre, while the hanging section is probably pisang bast or similar material, braided at the top and loose below. Brown and black material alternate to produce stripes.

**Spinning implements** — The *temuo* is a round wooden stick that branches into four parts and holds cotton. The accompanying spindle, called *kbendeleng*, has a long heavy shaft with a conical soapstone weight, identified in the catalogue as *dindo*. Spinning yarn is described as women’s work for men’s looms.

**Fujó** — The *Fujó* is a head covering made from pisang fibre or *Urèna* fibre and decorated with cauris. Museum records associate it with girls of the *Bundu* or *Bandu* society after ceremonies.

**Kpaba** — The *Kpaba*, plural *kpabalang*, is a weaving implement. Its wooden frame holds parallel heddles, and its remaining sides and heddles are made of rotang.

**Material fibres** — Catalogue records name pisang fibre, *Urèna*, raffia and *Raphia* materials in clothing, head coverings, spinning and other objects.

### Clothing & dress

The sources document clothing connected with the *Bundu* or *Bandu* societies. Girls wore the *Jo* dance skirt over imported cotton cloth. The *Kondoyila* net shirt belonged to the dance costume and was decorated around the neck and sleeves with cauris. The *Fujó* was braided into a girl’s hair by an older woman after she left the *Bundu* bush following a ceremony; it was later removed. Other museum records describe a hunter’s or warrior’s tunic made from hand-spun and hand-woven cotton strips, with amulets wrapped in cotton and small horns sewn to the back of the neck. The sources do not describe ordinary everyday clothing, footwear or separate men’s and women’s dress beyond these records.

### Architecture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Kissi smiths were known for ironworking, and museum records identify knives, a fighting dagger, an offering knife, an axe and a razor for circumcision. One knife was forged from a *Kissi penny* and was chiefly intended for harvesting palm fruits. A curved iron dagger from Liberia was used in fighting. Pottery records describe a round black earthen vessel with an incised and fired pattern, and a small black earthen vessel. Other everyday objects include a raffia basket, a sieve for producing palm oil, a stool of white wood with incised decoration, and a sling called *fanda*, used to drive rice birds away from platforms in rice fields.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources document cauris as decoration on *Fujó* head coverings and on the *Kondoyila* net shirt. The *Fujó* is worn by girls after *Bundu* or *Bandu* ceremonies and is braided into the hair by an older woman. The sources do not describe tattoos, henna, permanent body modification or other jewelry traditions.

## Music & performance

Museum records document several Kissi musical instruments. The *sangbaa* is a goblet-shaped drum with a drumskin, a wooden drumstick and a network of cords used to tension the skin. Its foot is tubular and ends in a tripod. The *sansa* has seven metal tongues fastened to a wooden board with a characteristic plaited design. A *tjekó* is a rattle made from a rattan vine with a base formed from part of a calabash fruit and containing seeds of *Canna indica*. A *seláng* is a calabash rattle with an attached bead rattle; the catalogue places it in an ensemble including the *sangbáa*. Another instrument is a scraping stick made from *Raphia vinifera*, with fifty-one grooves over an area of thirty centimetres; a stick or palm-nut is drawn across the grooves in time with dance or song rhythm. A British Museum record also describes an ivory musical horn carved with a lizard, snake and crosshatched decoration. The sources do not name song genres or describe complete performance occasions beyond dance, song rhythm and musical ensembles.

## Dance & theatre

The sources document dance objects associated with the *Bundu* or *Bandu* societies. The *Jo* skirt and *Kondoyila* net shirt formed part of a dance costume, while the *Jajei* ankle rattle was made for girls of the *Bundu* society. A dance rattle made from a hollow bottle-like fruit and covered with seed beads was used by women at *Bundu* parties in connection with the appearance of a masked *Bundu* “devil.” The same catalogue record says that a similar rattle was seen among Kissi men at one of their special dances. A wooden mask with two horns, four teeth probably from chimpanzee and animal-fur decoration is also recorded. The sources do not describe named dances, theatrical plots or shadow-puppet traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not describe an annual festival calendar or provide dates for Kissi festivals. They do document *Bundu* or *Bandu* ceremonies for girls. After returning from the *Bundu* bush, girls wore *Fujó* head coverings braided into their hair, and the coverings were removed after some time. The *Jajei* ankle rattle and the *Jo* and *Kondoyila* dance garments are associated with this society. A razor is identified as an instrument for circumcision, but the sources do not provide a broader account of the rite, its participants or its timing. The sources also do not describe weddings, funerals, birth ceremonies or annual agricultural festivals.

## Foodways

The sources identify rice fields and rice birds in the description of the *fanda*, a sling used from a platform to drive away birds. They also record a sieve used for producing palm oil and a knife chiefly intended for harvesting palm fruits. The sources do not describe named dishes, drinks, cooking methods, dietary rules or ceremonial foods.

## Language & religion

Kissi, also called Kisi or Kisiei, is a Mel language of West Africa within the Niger–Congo language family. It has northern and southern dialects, and both are tonal. The northern dialect, *kisiduei*, is spoken in Guinea and Sierra Leone; the southern dialect, *kpekeduei*, is spoken in Liberia and Sierra Leone. Kissi is spoken in Guinea in Kissidougou and Guéckédou and their préfectures. The Kissi also use the names Assi, Bakoa, Den, Gihi, Gisi, Gissi, Gizi, Kisi, Kisia, Kisie, Kisiye, Kizi and Kalen. Many Kissi have converted to Christianity, while most continue practicing their traditional ethnic religion. Ancestor worship and prayer to deceased relatives are common. Carved soapstone figures and heads were produced before colonial contact; their purpose is uncertain, with interpretations connecting them either to ancestor worship or to gods associated with agricultural yields.
