## Overview

The Wolayta, also called Welayta, are an ethnic group who mostly inhabit southern Ethiopia. The most recent estimate given in the source, from 2017, places their number at 5.83 million in Welayta Zone. Their language, Wolaitta, belongs to the Omotic branch of the Afro-Asiatic language family. The group has its own historical kingdom, called the Kingdom of Wolayta, and its music, dance, cuisine, proverbs, written language, and food practices have influenced or shaped cultural life in Ethiopia.

## Material culture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do mention *Marchuwa*, a currency made from metal pieces, which the source says was equal to 18 Maria Theresa Thalers or 0.50 US dollars.

## Music & performance

Welayta music has a prominent role in national entertainment in Ethiopia. The source describes Wolayta tunes as unique and fast-paced and says that they have influenced several styles and rhythms while contributing to Ethiopian musical diversity. Traditional dance forms are also widely adopted by musicians and appear in Ethiopian music videos. Artists named in the source who incorporated Welayta musical style into their songs include Tibebu Workeye, Teddy afro, and Tsehaye Yohannes.

## Dance & theatre

Welayta traditional dance forms are described as influential and as frequently adopted by musicians. They are widely visible in Ethiopian music videos.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe several cultural and life-cycle contexts. Raw beef, commonly known as *kurt*, is served on special occasions, including holidays such as the “Finding of the True Cross” or Meskel holiday, which the source says is celebrated annually on September 27 in Ethiopia. Enset foods are traditionally incorporated into cultural events connected with births, deaths, weddings, and rites of planting, harvesting, and purification.

## Foodways

Raw beef, commonly known as *kurt*, is described as a deeply rooted cultural food of the Welayta people. The source says that minced raw beef is called *kitfo* by the Gurage people, while *kurt* is associated with the Welayta. These foods are mostly served on special occasions such as holidays, including the “Finding of the True Cross” or Meskel holiday. Enset foods are traditionally used in cultural events involving births, deaths, weddings, and rites of planting, harvesting, and purification.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Welayta people use many proverbs. A large collection was published in 1987, according to the Ethiopian calendar, by the Academy of Ethiopian Languages. Fikre Alemayehu’s 2012 MA thesis from Addis Ababa University analyzes Wolaytta proverbs and their functions. The sources also mention oral tradition in connection with the conquest of Wolayta: it holds that 118,000 Welayta and 90,000 Shewan troops died in the fighting.

## Language & religion

Wolaitta is an Omotic language spoken in Wolaita Zone and in some parts of the Southern Nations, Nationalities, and People’s Region of Ethiopia. The source estimates the number of speakers at 1,800,000 in 1991 and identifies it as the native language of the Welayta people. Zala is identified by Ethnologue as one smaller dialect region. Melo, Oyda, and Gamo-Gofa-Dawro have been described by some as dialects, although most authorities listed in the source treat them as separate languages. Wolaytta has existed in written form since the 1940s. The Sudan Interior Mission first devised a writing system, later revised by a team led by Dr. Bruce Adams. The New Testament was completed in 1981 and the entire Bible in 2002.
