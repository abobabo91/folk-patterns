## Overview

The Teke people, also known as Bateke, Tyo, or Tio, are a Bantu Central African ethnic group who speak the Teke languages. They mainly inhabit the south, north, and center of the Republic of the Congo, the west of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and a smaller area in the south-east of Gabon. Their historical territory included the area between Manyanga and the Malebo Pool, while later displacement pushed many Teke north into the Batéké Plateau. A Teke kingdom developed during the first millennium C.E., with a ruler called the Makoko. Teke material culture is represented in the sources by masks, figurative statues, textiles, metal ornaments, weapons, and ritual objects.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Raffia funerary textiles** — Teke textiles are made from raffia and can be dyed to add color. The sources state that three textiles accompany the dead into the tomb; rolled up, they serve as maps guiding the recently buried toward the village of the dead and the ancestors. The three maps represent the sky, earth, and water.

**Mask dresses** — Teke masks have holes along their edges for attaching a woven raffia dress with feathers and fibers. The raffia, fabric, and feather elements form part of the costume and conceal the wearer during traditional dancing ceremonies.

**Geometric mask decoration** — Teke masks are round, flat, disk-like wooden objects decorated with abstract patterns and geometric motifs. A horizontal line divides the mask into two parts, and the masks are painted in dark blues, blacks, browns, and clay tones.

**British Museum cloth garments** — The catalogue records a woman’s coat made of braided cloth, a woman’s silk head-cloth, a diamond-shaped boy’s bib with fringes, a small embroidered boy’s cap, and a pair of knitted child’s socks with geometric decoration. The records do not provide a broader account of these garments within Teke dress.

**Motif vocabulary.** Geometric motifs, horizontal dividing line, triangle-shaped noses, narrow eye slits, concentric strips, geometric pattern.

### Clothing & dress

The sources provide limited information about everyday Teke clothing. They describe the dress attached to masks: woven raffia, feathers, and fibers conceal the wearer during dancing ceremonies. The museum catalogues also record a woman’s braided cloth coat, a woman’s silk head-cloth, a diamond-shaped boy’s bib with long fringes, a small embroidered boy’s cap, and knitted child’s socks. The cap is red and decorated with black, white, and yellow designs; a metal chain with beads is attached to its top. The records do not explain whether these catalogue garments represent ordinary, ceremonial, male, or female Teke dress beyond the descriptions of the objects themselves.

### Architecture

The sources mention special huts associated with the Cult of Spirits and a special hut at the edge of each village where ancestor statues or Butti are kept, but they provide no architectural description of these buildings.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Teke material culture in the catalogues includes swords, a dagger, a leather scabbard, a spear with an iron point, arm rings, a neck ring, a bracelet, a ring, a headrest, wooden figures, and sculptures. The swords have brass fittings and ornamented handles, while the dagger has an openworked blade and a brass-mounted handle. One sword is identified in the catalogue as Bateke, and others as Tio, Bateke, or Teke. The sources also describe Teke figurative sculptures with hollowed areas for “medicines” or “magical substances.” Pottery and ceramic production are not covered.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The Teke practise scarification, with scars mainly on the face from the temple down the cheek; the sources state that these scars are given in childhood. The catalogues record brass arm and neck rings, a narrow brass bracelet, silver and gilt jewellery, and an iron ring described by its donor as a fetish that helps against diseases and belonged to a muteke. Teke ancestor figures can be decorated with metal necklaces to mark the prestige of the ancestor. The sources also describe facial striation and tattoo-like lines on Teke sculptures. Hair practices and other forms of personal adornment are not documented.

## Music & performance

The sources state that masks are used in traditional dancing ceremonies connected with weddings, funerals, and initiation ceremonies for young men entering adulthood. The mask functions not only as part of performance but also as a social and political identifier of structure within a tribe or family. The sources further describe a village chief as a religious leader who keeps potions and spiritual bones used in traditional ceremonies to speak to spirits and safeguard the people. No account is provided of musical accompaniment, court performance, professional musicians, or instruments.

## Dance & theatre

Teke masks are used in traditional dancing ceremonies, including weddings, funerals, and initiation ceremonies for young men entering adulthood. They are round, flat, wooden masks with abstract and geometric decoration, narrow eye slits, and a bite bar held between the wearer’s teeth. A raffia, fabric, and feather costume conceals the wearer. The masks also identify social and political relationships within a tribe or family.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources document wedding, funeral, and initiation ceremonies in which masks are used. Teke religious life includes the Ancestor Cult and the Cult of Spirits. The Ancestor Cult maintains the places where deceased ancestors are believed to inhabit after death, including caves, clearings, or waterfalls near villages, and provides food or sacrifices. The Cult of Spirits meets in temples and uses nearby huts containing spirit statues, where sacrifices take place. The sources state that human sacrifice was replaced by animal sacrifice by the nineteenth century. Teke funeral practice includes placing three rolled raffia textiles beside the dead as maps for the sky, earth, and water.

## Foodways

The Teke economy is mainly based on farming maize, millet, tobacco, and bananas. The sources also describe the Teke as hunters, skilled fishermen, and traders. Offerings of food and sacrifices are documented in connection with the Ancestor Cult, whose members provide them to deceased ancestors. The Cult of Spirits likewise uses offerings and sacrifices to keep spirits satisfied.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that the Teke kingdom was ruled by a Makoko and that religion formed the basis of royal legitimacy. The Makoko was regarded as both divine and human and ruled over local chiefs.

## Language & religion

The Teke languages are a group of Bantu languages spoken by the Teke in the western Congo and Gabon. They are classified in Zone B.70 in Guthrie’s classification. The sources list several Teke language varieties and related languages, including Tsege, Teghe, Ngungwel, Central Teke, Tio, East Teke, Kukwa, Fuumu, Wuumu, Teke-Kukuya, Teke-Tyee, and Teke-Tsaayi. Teke religion is monotheistic and centers on Nzambi, the supreme deity who created the earth, water, and sky and controls life and death. Ancestor and nature spirits act as intermediaries. Nkwimbali is associated with waterfalls and receives sacrifices. The village religious elder is called the Mpugu, whose authority is marked by the large wooden Tara-Mantsie.
