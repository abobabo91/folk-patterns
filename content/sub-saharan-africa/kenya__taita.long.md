## Overview

The Taita are a Bantu-speaking people of Taita-Taveta County in southeastern Kenya, living in and around the Taita Hills — the massifs of Dawida, Saghalla and Kasighau that rise out of the surrounding lowlands. They speak *Kidawida* (also *Kitaita*), a Bantu language closely related to the Chaga languages of Kenya and Tanzania. West-Bantu speakers reached the area around 1000–1300, settling in five groups across what is now Taita-Taveta. In folk-culture terms the Taita are notable for practices tied to their landscape: skulls of the dead "buried" in caves and rock shelters, sacred forests called *Fighi*, and a hill-country religion of ancestor offerings — together with a documented nineteenth-century tradition of beadwork, ear-plugs and metal ornament, which is what the museum objects in this atlas show.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

What the sources do document is bead- and fibre-work worn on the body, which carries the group's ornamental vocabulary in place of cloth:

**Beaded waist ornament** — a band of red beads hung with single-strand tassels of red, white or dark blue beads threaded onto fibre, recorded in the British Museum collection. The colour set — red with white and dark blue — matches the bead palette described for Taita women in the nineteenth century.

**Sem-sem bead collars** — *sem-sem* is the name given to the dark blue and red beads that Taita women wore in large quantities around the neck, massed thickly enough that the wearer's chin tilted backward.

No motif names appear in the sources.

### Clothing & dress

The nineteenth-century record describes women wearing a short cotton loincloth or kilt with bead girdles or further strands of beads. Heads were shaved but for a circular crown of hair, which was dressed with strands of beads; wealthier women wore several strands falling over the ears and shoulders. Around the neck came the mass of dark blue and red *sem-sem* beads. Skin was dressed with animal and coconut oils layered with clay against harsh conditions. A bright bandana or handkerchief was a prized possession. Some women followed styles seen among Zanzibari enslaved women, setting ivory or wooden studs into the nostrils and lips. Men wore pieces of calico with armlets and leglets of ivory, bone and metal, and the same kinds of ear ornament as women; they carried bows and arrows with a quiver of poisoned arrows, the poison a plant product obtained from the Giriama province through Arab traders or local emissaries. Both sexes filed and discoloured their teeth and wore simple body tattoos.

### Architecture

What the sources do document is the built and natural landscape of ritual: the prominent rocks called *Magamba* and the caves called *Mbanga* in the Taita Hills, treated as places of worship and as resting places for the dead. The *Mbanga* caves served practical ends as well — shelter, security, and isolation wards where people with contagious diseases were confined and fed, and readmitted to the community if they survived. Sacred forests called *Fighi*, comparable to the Mijikenda's *Kaya*, were closed to other uses and so functioned as conservation reserves. The Wabasi cult likewise held its own sacred forests and meeting places. The same hills and rocks carried a judicial use: those convicted of crimes were taken to the heights and thrown to their death.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum objects here are ornament and weapon rather than household ware. Ironwork appears in two **leg ornaments**: one a crescent-shaped iron bell with two iron balls as clappers and two iron beads, all threaded on a hide thong; the other a larger crescent iron bell with a single lead ball for a clapper on a hide thong. Woodwork appears in a **knobkerrie**, a club carved from ebony with an ovoid head.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ear ornament is the best-documented Taita form in this collection. Three **ear-plugs** are carved from wood, circular with a central hole, concave top and bottom and a concave outer edge — one with a deeply concave profile. A fourth **ear ornament** is carved wood inlaid with circular tin decorations. The nineteenth-century account matches this: women wore numerous wire hoops set with small beads in one ear and larger glass rings in the other, and ear decorations of sweet grass, ivory, bone and metal, the accumulation standing for wealth. Armlets were large, of pewter and beads. Men wore armlets and leglets of ivory, bone and metal. Teeth were filed and discoloured; tattoos were applied in a rudimentary manner; women artificially enhanced breast shape. The bead colours named are dark blue and red *sem-sem*, with red, white and dark blue in the waist tassels.

## Music & performance

The only sounding objects recorded are the two iron leg ornaments, crescent bells with iron or lead clappers threaded onto hide thongs and worn on the leg, so that movement produced sound — but the catalogue does not say in what context they were worn.

## Festivals & rituals

Ritual among the Taita centred on the dead and on the ancestors rather than on a festival calendar, and the sources name no annual festivals. The distinctive rite shared by the Saghalla, Kasighau and Dawida was the "burial" of skulls in caves and rock shelters, with variations between the groups: the Dawida kept only the skulls of elderly men over the age of seventy, while the Saghalla kept the skulls of women, children and men, and in some parts of Saghalla also stored the skulls of people from other communities who had died in their territory. Sacrifice was routine to the ancestors or household gods; the supreme god *Mlungu* was called on and given sacrifice only for appeasement or thanksgiving at times of calamity and misfortune. A secret cult, the **Wabasi**, was feared across Taita and held sacred forests and meeting places; a man who joined it could be buried only by another *Abasi* member. Marriage was mostly polygamous and pre-arranged, the groom typically a friend of the bride's family. Negotiation, called *Wupe*, was conducted by the bride's father and maternal uncles and turned chiefly on the dowry, usually livestock, paid in installments over time. When girls reached marriageable age a practice of "kidnapping" took place, the prospective in-laws taking them in the evening as they went out to fetch water or firewood.

## Foodways

The only food practice they record is the provisioning of people isolated in the *Mbanga* caves during contagious illness, who were confined there and had food brought to them.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources preserve one figure of historical memory: **Mwangeka**, Joseph Mwambi, described as a legendary figure among the Taita for resisting British colonists encroaching on Wataita land. Traditional Taita political life was organised not around a tribe but around lineages or clans, *vichuku* (singular *kichuku*), each holding a territorial area in the hills as an independent political unit; before colonialism there was no concept of a unified Taita tribe.

## Language & religion

Taita (*Kidawida*, *Kitaita*) is a Bantu language of the Taita Hills, closely related to the Chaga languages. Its Saghalla variety (Sagala, Sagalla) is distinct enough to count as a separate language from the Dawʼida and Kasigau dialects; Saghalla speakers' *Kisaghala* is closely related to Kigiriama or Mijikenda. Dawʼida and Saghala carry loanwords from two now-extinct South Cushitic languages termed Taita Cushitic, whose lateral obstruents were still pronounced as such within living memory before being replaced by Bantu sounds. Dawida dialects include Wusi, Kidaya, Mghange, Chawia, Mwanda, Kishamba, Werugha, Wumingu and Wundanyi, with Mbololo and Bura distinct again. Taita has two tones, high and low, and no phonemic vowel length. Most Taita are Christian; only a small number still practise the ancestral religion of *Mlungu*, the ancestors and household gods.
