## Overview

The Antaisaka, also known as Tesaka or Tesaki, are an ethnic group of Madagascar traditionally concentrated south of Farafangana along the south-eastern coast. They have since spread more widely throughout the island, and an estimated 600,000 Malagasy identified as Antesaka in 2013. The group forms about 5% of Madagascar’s population. Antesaka speak a dialect of Malagasy, a branch of the Malayo-Polynesian language group derived from the Barito languages spoken in southern Borneo. Their folk-cultural profile is documented especially through marriage taboos, funeral rites, rice cultivation, fishing, and the use of local plant materials in houses and objects.

### Architecture

Antaisaka villages contain rectangular, one-room houses made of local plant material. These houses have an extra door on the eastern side, used only for removing a corpse from the living quarters. Funeral architecture includes the *kibory*, a communal burial house hidden in a sacred forest called the *ala fady*. The *ala fady* is restricted to men. The sources do not describe roofs, decoration, settlement plans, or urban architecture.

## Music & performance

Music appears in the documented funeral rite. After a corpse has been dried for two to three years, it is moved to a separate house accompanied by the women of the village. The women cry together on cue and then begin to dance. Meanwhile, village children dance to drum music outside until morning, when the corpse is moved to the *kibory*. The men gather in the house of the village leader and take turns going to the corpse house, where they affix money to the deceased using a specified type of oil. The sources do not name the drums, identify song genres, or describe musical performance outside the funeral context.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is documented as part of the funeral ceremony. Women begin to dance after collectively crying beside the corpse, while children dance to drum music outside the corpse house until morning. The sources do not name these dances or describe theatre, mask performance, shadow puppetry, court dance, or entertainment traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do not describe an annual festival calendar or seasonal festivals. They do document several life-cycle and communal rites. Family life and marriage are regulated by numerous *fady*, or taboos. Twins are regarded as taboo and were traditionally killed after birth or left in the forest to die; although this practice has been outlawed, it persists among some traditional communities, and twins are not permitted to be buried alongside their family members.

Funeral customs involve drying a corpse for two to three years before transferring it to a communal burial house called a *kibory*. Before this transfer, the village performs a ritual called *tranondonaky*. Women accompany the corpse while crying and dancing, men attach money to the deceased with a specified oil, and children dance to drum music outside. Men finally transport the body to the sacred forest, where they privately speak their last words to the deceased. The sources do not describe weddings beyond marriage taboos, coming-of-age ceremonies, initiation, or annual celebrations.

## Foodways

The principal Antaisaka economic activities documented in the sources are the cultivation of coffee, rice, and bananas. Women are primarily responsible for the rice harvest in accordance with local tradition. People living along the coast often rely on fishing as a principal source of income. The sources do not name specific dishes, cooking methods, food restrictions, ceremonial foods, or coffee-preparation traditions. They also do not document a distinctive cuisine beyond the cultivation of these crops and coastal fishing.

## Oral tradition & literature

The history of the Antesaka is preserved in oral history. According to this tradition, the founder of the clan was named Andriamandresy. He was born Repila in Tsiarepioky, near Mahabo, later changed his name to Ihazorango, and finally adopted the name Andriamandresiarivo in anticipation of being named king. Oral history also recounts that Ratongalaza killed or exiled his brothers to secure the throne. The sources do not provide named folktales, epic poems, proverbs, riddles, literary works, or contemporary preservation projects.

## Language & religion

Antesaka speak a dialect of Malagasy. Malagasy is described as part of the Malayo-Polynesian language group and as derived from the Barito languages spoken in southern Borneo. The sources do not describe a historical script or a separate Antaisaka writing tradition.

The documented spiritual and ritual framework includes *fady*, or taboos, and sacred forests. The *ala fady* is a sacred forest restricted to men and contains the *kibory*, the communal burial house. Funeral practices include drying the corpse, collective crying and dancing, attaching money to the deceased with a specified oil, and privately speaking last words to the deceased. The sources do not identify a formal religious affiliation, denomination, priesthood, divination system, or named healing tradition.
