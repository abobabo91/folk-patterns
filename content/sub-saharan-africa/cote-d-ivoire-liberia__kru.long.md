## Overview

The Kru — also written Krao, Kroo or Krou — are a West African people indigenous to western Ivory Coast and eastern Liberia, settled along the forest belt where it meets the coastal lagoons, from the coast around Monrovia east to the Bandama River. They are not a single tribe but a cluster of Kru-speaking communities: Liberia alone counts 48 sub-sections, among them the Jlao Kru, and the wider grouping includes the Bété, Bassa, Guéré, Grebo, Klao/Krao, Dida, Krahn and Jabo. They make up about 7% of Liberia's population. Their livelihood rests on farming the forest, supplemented by hunting, and — for the men European writers called *Krumen* — on seafaring: from the 1790s onward Kru sailors crewed European ships from Sierra Leone to the mouth of the Congo, which made this one of the most widely dispersed and most frequently photographed coastal societies of nineteenth-century West Africa.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here name no Kru weaving, dyeing or patterned-cloth tradition. What they record of cloth is how it was worn and used: loincloths and lengths of cloth wrapped around the lower body in the nineteenth-century photographs, a sash worn with the uniforms of a Kroo procession, and strips of blue, white and red cloth binding the gourd body of a Kru harp in the British Museum.

**Motif vocabulary.** The one decorative scheme the catalogue records describes is on an hour-glass drum: blue zigzag lines with orange dots, painted around the drum's waist.

### Clothing & dress

Nineteenth-century photographs of Kru men show a working coastal dress of loincloths worn with personal ornaments, and — among the Kroo communities settled abroad — a deliberate mixture of European and local garments: a group photographed in Southern Nigeria stands in European jackets and wrapped cloths together, one man seated on a European-style chair, another holding a European hat. Ceremonial and civic dress is documented at one remove, in a photograph of Kroo boys processing along the Marina for the Diamond Jubilee wearing uniforms with caps, shirts, a cloth around the lower body and a sash, and carrying large flags. Among the elders of the older Kru commonwealths, the insignia of office were not garments but iron rings worn on the legs. The sources do not describe women's dress, footwear or headwear separately.

### Architecture

The only built environment these sources show is an aerial photograph from a British Museum album: a Kru village of several long rectangular buildings under thatched roofs, set in a clearing surrounded by woodland, with eight people standing and looking up at the camera. Nothing in the sources names the house type, its materials below the roof, or any ornamental treatment. The settlement pattern is better recorded than the building: the first coastal Kru towns established from the interior were Little Kroo, Setra Kroo (Welteh in the Klao language), Kroo-Bar, Nana Kroo and King Will's Town, and the element "Bar" or "bah" in such names denotes a river mouth rather than a town name. Offshoot Kru quarters later grew up at every major trading factory of the coast, at Freetown in Sierra Leone, and as far away as the Kru town in Liverpool.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The canoe paddle is the defining Kru object, and the collections hold it in a consistent form: carved from a single piece of wood, a rounded handle running into a wide flat blade, the blade painted black. These are the paddles of the small canoes in which Kru companies went as much as a dozen miles out to sea to meet arriving ships and negotiate their employment on the spot. Alongside them the catalogues record a metal knife with a wood-and-hide sheath, and a rectangular wooden object identified as a bell — hollowed out to a cavity, with a braided fibre cord threaded through two knobs carved on either side. The Metropolitan Museum holds a Kru currency piece of probably nineteenth-century date, cast in brass over a fired terracotta core. No pottery tradition appears in these sources.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The best-documented Kru body marking is political rather than decorative. Kru oral tradition holds firmly that the Kru were never enslaved, and to secure their standing as freemen they took up tattooing the forehead and the bridge of the nose with indigo dye, a mark that distinguished them at sight from people held as slaves. Rank carried its own metalwork: in the small Kru commonwealths described in the late nineteenth century, power lay with the elders, whose insignia were iron rings worn on the legs. Photographs of Kru men on the coast show them wearing personal ornaments, but the sources do not name or describe the types.

## Music & performance

Three Kru instruments survive in the British Museum with enough description to be specific about. The drum is hour-glass-shaped, carved from wood with a skin stretched over each end and held in tension by fibre strings running along the body, its waist painted with blue zigzags and orange dots. A stringed instrument is built from two lengths of wood with notched ends pierced by burnt holes, mounted on a calabash resonator and lashed together at the centre with vegetable fibre; its six strings are tied to one support and pass through the other. Two further instruments are catalogued as harps, both built on a gourd resonator with wood and vegetable fibre — one of them incorporating blue, white and red cloth, the other metal. Beyond the objects themselves, the sources record no song genres, no performer roles and no named performance occasions, so the repertoire and the contexts in which these instruments were played are not documented here.

## Dance & theatre

One photograph in the British Museum album shows a Kru masquerade in performance: three stilt masqueraders seated on a bamboo structure, with male adults and children watching from either side and banana trees behind. The sources give the masquerade no name, no calendar and no account of what it enacts, and they record no other dance or dramatic tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The annual cycle is not covered by these sources. What they do record is the ritual texture of Kru political and social life. Villages maintain their ties to one another on the basis of presumed common descent, reinforced by ceremonial exchanges and gifts. In the small commonwealths described in the late nineteenth century, each had a hereditary chief whose duty was simply to represent the people in dealings with strangers; real authority lay with the elders, and their president held religious as well as political office — he guarded the national symbols, and his house served as a sanctuary for an accused offender until guilt was proved. Property was held in common by each family, and land was communal, though a cultivator's rights in a plot lapsed when he stopped farming it. Ritual and medical crisis was handled by a class of practitioners called *deya*, found in some areas of Liberia, who underwent long specialized training and apprenticeship before taking up the office and who treated problems both medical and spiritual, using pharmaceutical and spiritual remedies together. Life-cycle rites — birth, initiation, marriage, burial — are not described in the sources used here.

## Foodways

The Kru rely on the forest for farming and supplement it with hunting. Two further details of the food economy appear: the ancestral migration tradition remembers that in coming down the Poor River from the interior the Kru "learned the value of salt", and the coast they settled was known to European traders as the Pepper Coast, a centre of production and export of Guinea and melegueta pepper. No dishes, cooking methods, ceremonial foods or dietary rules are recorded in these sources.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral tradition is the principal source for Kru origins, and the sources treat it as such. Traditions recorded in the mid-nineteenth century by the missionary James Connelly trace the coastal Kru back to an inland place called Claho, which they left "some three generations back — say one hundred to one hundred fifty years", descending the Poor River, founding the town of Bassa, moving on to Little Kroo, and being joined there by further Kru-speaking communities from the interior; these events are placed in the eighteenth century and connected to intensifying European trade on the coast. Such accounts reach us distorted, because the Europeans who wrote them down did not understand the Kru language — hence Anglicized town names, and the persistent false etymology deriving "Kru" from a ship's "crew" when the word is in fact an Anglicization of the people's own name for themselves, *Klao*. A body of Kru proverbs was collected in the 1930s from Sie Tagbwe, interviewed at length by the American anthropologist Melville J. Herskovits, and that record is what preserved them.

## Language & religion

The Kru languages are spoken from southeastern Liberia to western Ivory Coast, and their classification is genuinely contested: older work treated Kru as a primary branch of Niger-Congo, while Güldemann (2018) finds neither the lexical nor the noun-class resemblances needed for that relationship and Glottolog treats Kru as an independent family. The subgroups include Kuwaa, Grebo, Bassa, Belle, Belleh and Kwaa, with Neyo added by Ethnologue; the dialect count is high because each village was historically an independent state with little intercommunication. The tone systems are among the most complex in Africa. Word order is primarily SVO but frequently SOV. S. W. Koelle published a Kru grammar in 1854. Most speakers now also use English in Liberia or French in Côte d'Ivoire. The spiritual universe described by missionaries centres on *Nyesoa*, a high creator God, alongside spirits attached to territories and familial guardians or souls of the departed who remain nearby and can influence events.
