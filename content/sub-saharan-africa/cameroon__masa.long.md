## Overview

The Masa, also called Massa, Masana, Banana, or Yagoua, are a Chadic ethnic group living in Cameroon and Chad. Their estimated population is 266,000 to 469,000, with the majority residing in Cameroon. Most speak the Massa language, which is also known as Masa or Masana and has approximately 200,000 speakers. Masa is spoken in southern Chad and northern Cameroon, including areas along the Logone River and parts of the Far North Region. The sources describe a society in which cattle ownership, fishing, sorghum cultivation, weaving, warfare, marriage exchanges, and religious affiliation all have important social roles. The group is also represented in museum collections by fishing equipment, armour, clothing, musical instruments, jewellery, and other objects.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used do not document named Masa pattern traditions or distinctive motifs.

**Fishing devices** — The sources describe the Masa as good weavers who developed a broad range of fishing devices and techniques. Museum records include a fish trap made of reeds bound with string of vegetable fibre and a fishing net made of fibre netting fastened to a wooden frame.

**Woman’s dress** — A British Museum catalogue records a woman’s dress made of fibre string. The sources do not provide a vernacular name, pattern vocabulary, or comparison with neighbouring cultures.

**Basketry** — A greave in the museum catalogue is made in basketry using cane and leaf, while another recorded object is a basket made of leaf. The sources do not describe their patterns or production contexts.

**Loin-cloth** — A museum catalogue records a loin-cloth made of hide with metal fittings. No vernacular term, pattern, or ceremonial use is given.

**Cuirass** — The catalogue records a cuirass, or body armour, made of hide. The sources do not describe decoration or construction beyond its material.

**Shield** — The museum records include shields made of reed, and a shield made of reed, wood, and hide. No named designs or motifs are supplied.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no Masa motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources give limited information about Masa clothing. A British Museum catalogue records a woman’s dress made of fibre string and a loin-cloth made of hide with metal fittings. The same catalogue records armour and related equipment, including a hide cuirass, a bark-fibre war helmet, reed shields, and a greave made in basketry using cane and leaf. These records document materials and object types but do not distinguish everyday from ceremonial dress. They also do not provide vernacular garment names, information about footwear, belts, head coverings apart from the war helmet, or a detailed account of men’s and women’s clothing. The Wikipedia source states that men who participate in formalised fighting over livestock or honour have helmets and clubs, and that they fight in lines while encouraged by women.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not document an annual Masa festival calendar, named seasonal festivals, dates, or religious celebrations. They also do not provide detailed accounts of birth ceremonies, initiation, funerals, or wedding rituals.

The sources do describe social and ritualised practices connected with cattle. Cattle ownership is central to many social activities, including bride prices, and a wife will cost a man cows. The Masa also participate in cattle lending, called *golla*, and use traditional cow-fattening cures called *gurna*. Formalised fighting can be caused by the theft of cows or by adultery. The fighting has rules: the wounded must be collected from combat, a man on the ground or a wounded man must not be hit, and the fighting must end at dusk. Women who participate in this kind of fight acquire prestige in Masa society.

## Foodways

The Masa cultivate early red sorghum, identified in the source as *Sorghum caudatum*, as a staple. They also engage throughout the year in fishing in the Logone River and its affluent. Over the last thirty years, the introduction of cash crops influenced the food economy through monetarisation and the extensive cultivation of rice, which is cultivated mainly by northern Masa. Groundnuts are also grown.

The sources state that the Masa settled on river banks in order to practise fishing. Their fish sauces are accompanied by sorrel, okra, and false sesame, which the source describes as producing a monotonous diet. The sources also describe the Masa as good weavers who developed fishing devices and techniques that enabled them to become traders in fresh and dried fish. No named dishes, cooking vessels, beverages, dietary rules, ceremonial foods, or food-related festivals are documented.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used do not document Masa epic poetry, folktale cycles, proverbs, riddles, written literature, or contemporary literary preservation efforts. They do record an oral historical tradition stating that the Massa left Sudan and arrived in northern Cameroon before the Tikar people. According to the same account, when Muslims entered the region to convert local populations, the Massa stayed while the Tikar fled south.

The sources also state that the Massa are said to be related to the Tikar and Bamileke peoples. The Musgum claim a Massa origin through a union between a Kotoko prince and a Massa woman. These accounts are the only documented oral or traditional narratives in the supplied material.

## Language & religion

Massa, also called Masana or Masa, is a Chadic language spoken in southern Chad and northern Cameroon by the Masa people. It has approximately 200,000 speakers. Dialects listed in the source are Bongor, Bugudum or Budugum, Domo, Gizay, Gumay, Ham, Walia, Wina or Viri, and Yagwa. Masa is spoken in the southern part of Mayo-Danay department in the Far North Region, including the arrondissements of Yagoua, Kalfou, Wina, Yele, and Guéré. Central Masa is spoken along the Logone River, while Western Masa includes Gizay around Guéré and Viri or Wina. The source also identifies Muzuk as a Masa dialect and states that its speakers are bilingual in Muzuk and Munjuk.

The sources state that 45% of the population is Muslim and another 45% is Christian. Thirty percent are mainline Catholics or Protestants, while another fifteen percent are Evangelical Christians. The sources also report clashes between different religious groups.
