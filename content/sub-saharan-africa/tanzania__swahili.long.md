## Overview

The Swahili (*Waswahili*) are the Muslim, Bantu-speaking urban people of the East African littoral — a 2,000-kilometre seaboard running from southern Somalia through the Kenyan coast (Lamu, Pate, Malindi, Mombasa), the Tanzanian mainland coast and its islands (Zanzibar's Stone Town, Pemba, Mafia, Kilwa Kisiwani), to northern Mozambique and the Comoros. Their own older self-designation is *Waungwana*, "the civilised ones," marking town-dwelling Muslims off from inland neighbours. Kiswahili belongs to the Bantu branch of Niger-Congo; its nearest relatives are Comorian and Mijikenda. In folk-culture terms the Swahili coast matters because it is where Bantu Africa, the monsoon Indian Ocean, and Islam produced a single material idiom: carved doors, coral-rag townhouses, resist-printed cotton, plaited mats, and sewn ships, all made locally out of imported and indigenous vocabularies at once.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Kanga** — the defining Swahili cloth: a rectangular machine-printed cotton sheet, roughly 110 × 150 cm, worn in pairs by women (one at the waist, one over shoulders or head). Its diagnostic structure is a wide patterned border (*pindo*) framing a central field (*mji*, literally "town"), with a Swahili proverb or aphorism (*jina*, "the name") printed along one edge. That printed text is unique among African cloths — the kanga is read as much as worn, and is given as a message in courtship, marriage, and quarrel. Early kangas grew from *lesos*, kerchiefs sewn in sixes, in later nineteenth-century Zanzibar.

**Kitenge** — heavier waxed or roller-printed cotton, sold by the length rather than as a finished pair, without the kanga's proverb; used for tailored dresses, wraps, and baby-carrying slings, and shared with the wider East African interior.

**Kikoi** — a woven (not printed) men's waistcloth of striped cotton with a plain-weave ground and knotted or twisted fringe at both ends, made on the Kenyan and Tanzanian coast; the banded stripe layout distinguishes it from the printed kanga.

**Mkeka** — plaited floor and prayer mats of split doum or coconut palm leaf (*ukili* strip-plaiting), dyed in bands of madder red, black and natural straw, sewn spirally or edge-to-edge. Mats define Swahili domestic space — one sits, eats, prays, and is finally washed for burial on them — and Zanzibar and Pemba workshops also plait *jamvi* (coarse room mats), winnowing trays, and lidded baskets.

**Embroidered kofia (kufi cap)** — the cylindrical white cotton men's cap worked in dense white-on-white or coloured counted-thread embroidery, the openwork panels named and graded by fineness; Lamu and Zanzibar caps are distinguished by their stitch density and the crown medallion.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Pindo* (framing border), *mji* (central field), *jina* (the printed proverb), *mavi ya kuku* ("chicken droppings," a dotted scatter ground), *kisutu* (the deep red-and-black bridal/rite cloth pattern of Pemba), *chunga* (cashew- or paisley-like bud), *maua* (flower rosettes), *mkufu* (chain link), *shanga* (bead rows), *kombe* (shell), *rangi ya damu* (blood-red ground).

### Clothing & dress

Men wear the *kanzu*, a long white ankle-length cotton robe with a tassel cord at the neck, over the *kikoi* or trousers, topped by the embroidered *kofia*; for mosque and festival a tailored jacket or Omani-style waistcoat (*joho*, *bushti* cloak) is added, with a *khanjar*-style dagger or a silver-mounted cane among elders. Women dress in paired *kanga* at home, and in public in the *buibui*, the black full-length overgarment with a face veil or headscarf that became standard in Zanzibar and Mombasa in the early twentieth century; beneath it are a blouse, slip and printed wrap. Brides wear *kisutu* cloth, heavy gold or gilt jewellery, and are covered with a veil for the procession. Sandals of tooled leather or plain wooden clogs are worn indoors and to the bathhouse. Modern coastal dress adds the imported *jilbab* and *thob*, and men returning from Hajj or Umrah bring dress items that carry religious prestige.

### Architecture

The Swahili stone town is built of coral rag quarried from the reef, set in lime mortar burnt from coral, with mangrove-pole (*boriti*) ceiling joists whose fixed length dictates room width — hence the long narrow galleried rooms. The classic *nyumba ya mawe* is inward-facing: a raised street bench (*baraza*) at the door, an entrance vestibule (*sebule*), an open courtyard (*kiwanda*), then progressively private transverse galleries ending in the *ndani*, the innermost room used for childbirth and for laying out the dead, with plaster wall niches (*zidaka*) holding imported porcelain. Ibn Battuta, visiting Kilwa in 1331, described it as finely and substantially built. Ornament concentrates on the **Swahili door** — thick jambs and lintel carved in lotus, rosette, chain and Qur'anic inscription bands, with brass studs and a central boss, the **Lamu door** running to deeper foliate relief and the Zanzibar type to Gujarati-influenced square frames. Mosques (*msikiti*) carry carved coral-stone *mihrab* niches; pillar tombs are a distinctively coastal funerary form. Rural and poorer quarters build *nyumba ya udongo*, wattle-and-daub on a mangrove frame with a makuti thatch of plaited coconut leaf.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Swahili households historically bought rather than made their finest ceramics — Chinese celadon and blue-and-white, Persian and Gulf glazed wares, set into *zidaka* niches as display — while local potters turned unglazed earthenware cooking pots (*chungu*), water jars (*mtungi*), and incense burners (*chetezo*) for the daily burning of frankincense and *udi*. Woodwork is the prestige craft: the brass-studded **Zanzibar chest** (*sanduku*), a teak or mahogany dowry box with iron or brass strapwork and secret drawers, and its Lamu variants; carved *kiti cha mpingo* ebony stools and the *kiti cha enzi*, the high-backed chair of woven cord and turned finials that signified office. The **siwa** is the great side-blown horn of ivory or cast brass, the regalia instrument of Pate and Lamu, sounded at weddings and installations. Shipwrights built the sewn-plank **mtepe** — hull planks stitched with coir, no iron nails — and the larger nailed **jahazi** and *dau* lateen-rigged traders.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Coastal goldsmiths and silversmiths worked filigree and granulation for bridal sets: heavy gold chains and pendants (*mkufu*), forehead ornaments, nose studs (*kipini*), hinged silver anklets and bangles (*bangili*), and silver amulet cases (*hirizi*) holding folded Qur'anic verses, worn by men and children against *jini* and the evil eye. Coral, amber, carnelian and imported Venetian glass beads (*shanga*) are strung for waist strings and necklaces. *Henna* (*hina*) is the central body art: hands and feet of a bride are worked in fine floral and lattice *piko* patterns over several nights, and the black *khanga la wanja* eye-blacking and sandalwood-paste face masks (*liwa*, *msala*) are part of the same bathhouse regime. Women's hair is oiled, plaited in named cornrow styles, and scented with jasmine and *ylang-ylang* garlands (*asmini*); perfume (*marashi*, *dalia*) and incense are inseparable from dress.

## Music & performance

The signature coastal genre is **taarab**, the sung poetry orchestra of Zanzibar and Mombasa — Swahili verse in strict metre set over an ensemble of *oud*, *qanun*, violin, accordion, ney, double bass and *dumbak*, consolidated in Zanzibar clubs from the 1900s (Ikhwani Safaa Musical Club, 1905) and carried worldwide by Siti binti Saad in the 1920s and later Bi Kidude. Its rural Zanzibari cousin **kidumbak** strips the orchestra to small clay-pot drums, tin-box bass, violin and chorus, and is sharper-tongued. **Beni** brass-band music, **gungu** drumming for weddings, and *ngoma* (the general term for drum-and-dance events) fill the life-cycle calendar. Instruments include the *zeze* (bowl-lute), *siwa* horn, *zumari* reed shawm, *upatu* (metal tray gong), *kayamba* raft rattle, *ngoma* and *msondo* drums, and the *marimba* frame zither. Sufi ritual supplies its own repertoire: *maulidi* recitation in praise of the Prophet, the *tarabu*-adjacent *kasida*, and the **Maulidi ya Homu** with rhythmic body sway, plus *dhikri* chanting in Qadiriyya and Shadhiliyya lodges.

## Dance & theatre

*Ngoma* covers the whole spectrum from the stately to the raucous. **Lelemama** was the women's formal processional dance of Mombasa and Zanzibar town associations, danced in ranks with restrained upper-body movement; **chakacha** (also *kisimayo*) is the hip-driven wedding dance performed by women for women in closed courtyards, considered too explicit for mixed audiences; **unyago** dances instruct girls at initiation and are led by the ritual teacher (*kungwi*). Men's forms include the sword-and-stick **goma la mkwaju**, and Lamu's **kirumbizi** stick-fighting display and *goma* wedding dance in which elders dance with staffs. The **Lamu Maulidi** procession and the *Maulidi ya Homu* blur ritual and performance. Swahili spirit-possession (*ngoma ya sheitani*, *pepo*) ceremonies use drumming and dance as therapy rather than entertainment, with a *mganga* directing. There is no indigenous masked or puppet theatre; theatre on the coast is modern Kiswahili stage drama (*michezo ya kuigiza*).

## Festivals & rituals

The Islamic calendar governs the year. **Ramadan** dominates, closing in **Idd el-Fitr** (*Sikukuu*) with new clothes, visiting, and *ngoma* in the streets, and **Idd el-Hajj** (Eid al-Adha) brings the sacrifice. *Maulidi*, the Prophet's birthday, is the coast's greatest gathering — the **Lamu Maulidi** draws pilgrims to the tomb of Habib Swaleh with recitation, processions, donkey and dhow races. **Mwaka Kogwa**, at Makunduchi in southern Zanzibar, marks the Shirazi solar New Year (*Nauruz*) in late July with ritual banana-stalk stick fights that discharge the past year's grudges, the burning of a hut, and communal feasting — the clearest surviving Persian-derived rite on the coast. Life-cycle rites: the naming and head-shaving of a newborn (*akika*) on the seventh day, Qur'an-school graduation, boys' circumcision (*jando*) and girls' seclusion and instruction (*unyago*) under a *kungwi*; marriage runs from the contract (*nikah*) through the bride's henna nights and the *kupamba* display, to the *kisutu*-wrapped procession. Burial is same-day, shroud-wrapped, facing Mecca, with graveside *talkini* and later *hitima* readings. Divination through Qur'anic verses — including drinking or washing in water in which ink-written verses have been soaked — and protective amulets against *jini* remain ordinary practice.

## Oral tradition & literature

Swahili has one of Africa's oldest written vernacular literatures, in Arabic script (*ajami*) from at least the seventeenth century. The great form is the **utenzi** (*utendi*), a long narrative poem in four-line stanzas: the *Utendi wa Tambuka* (Herekali, 1728, Pate) on the wars of Byzantium, the devotional *Utendi wa Mwana Kupona* (Lamu, 1858), in which Mwana Kupona instructs her daughter on marriage and piety, and the *Al-Inkishafi* of Sayyid Abdalla bin Ali bin Nasir, a meditation on the ruin of Pate. Shorter lyric forms — *shairi*, *gungu*, *wimbo* — feed directly into taarab lyrics. Prose tradition runs to *hadithi* animal tales built around the trickster hare (*Sungura*) against hyena and elephant, *methali* (proverbs, the same corpus printed on kangas), and *vitendawili* riddles exchanged in call-and-response. The fifteenth-century **Kilwa Chronicle** and the Pate and Lamu chronicles preserve dynastic tradition. Contemporary work by Shaaban Robert and Euphrase Kezilahabi, and the Zanzibar International Film Festival and Sauti za Busara, keep the repertoire in public circulation.

## Language & religion

Kiswahili is Bantu (Niger-Congo), with heavy Arabic lexical borrowing, plus Persian, Hindi, Portuguese, German and English layers; Modern Standard Swahili derives from Zanzibar's *Kiunguja*, while older dialects such as *Kimrima*, *Kitumbatu*, *Kiamu* (Lamu) and *Kimvita* (Mombasa) have far fewer Arabic loans. Before Roman orthography, Swahili was written in Arabic script. The overwhelming majority of Swahili are Sunni Muslims, with the Shafi'i school predominant and Sufi orders — Qadiriyya, Shadhiliyya, Alawiyya — historically central to town religious life; there are Christian Swahili speakers as well. Islam coexists with pre-Islamic strata: belief in *jini*, spirit possession treated by *ngoma ya sheitani*, protective *hirizi*, the *mganga* healer, and shrine visits (*ziara*) at pillar tombs and saints' graves.
