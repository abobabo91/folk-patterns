## Overview

The Swahili (*Waswahili*, sing. *Mswahili*) are a Bantu-speaking, predominantly Muslim people of the East African littoral, whose homeland — the *Pwani* or coast — stretches in a narrow ribbon from the Benadir shore of southern Somalia through Kenya and Tanzania to northern Mozambique, together with the offshore archipelagos of Lamu, the Zanzibar group (Unguja and Pemba), Mafia and the Comoros. Their language, *Kiswahili*, belongs to the Sabaki subgroup of Northeast Coast Bantu and carries a heavy stratum of Arabic loanwords together with Persian, Portuguese, Hindi and, more recently, English contributions; it is the national language of Tanzania and Kenya, a working language of the African Union, and the mother tongue of perhaps 15–20 million people, with a total speaker community, as a lingua franca of East and Central Africa, reaching well over 100 million. Ethnic Swahili proper — those who identify by descent, dialect and coastal urban culture (*uungwana*, "urbanity") — number in the low millions, distributed across the historic stone towns of Lamu, Pate, Mombasa, Zanzibar's Stone Town, Kilwa Kisiwani, Bagamoyo and Kilifi. Their civilisation crystallised between the eighth and thirteenth centuries out of the fusion of indigenous Bantu farmer-fisher populations with Indian Ocean trading diasporas — Omani and Hadhrami Arabs, Shirazi Persians, Gujaratis, and later Portuguese — producing a distinctive coastal urbanism built on the monsoon *dhow* trade in ivory, mangrove poles (*boriti*), cloves, ambergris and, in its darker centuries, enslaved people. In folk-culture terms the Swahili matter as the makers of the carved coral-rag townhouse, the studded *mlango wa Kiswahili* (Swahili door), the *kanga* cloth with its printed proverbs, the sewn *mtepe* sailing vessel, the *taarab* orchestra, and a manuscript tradition of Arabic-script Swahili verse (*utenzi*, *utendi*) reaching back to the seventeenth century.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Kanga** — the printed rectangular cotton cloth, roughly 150 by 110 cm, sold and worn in identical pairs (*doti*), one wrapped around the waist (*pili*) and the other over the shoulders or head (*mkaja*). Its defining feature is the *jina* or *ujumbe*, a Swahili proverb, prayer, warning or flirtation printed in a cartouche along one border, framed by a patterned *pindo* edge and a central *mji* field. The form emerged in Zanzibar and Mombasa in the later nineteenth century, reputedly when women sewed together squares of imported Portuguese kerchief cloth.

**Kitenge** — a heavier, waxed or resin-printed cotton, usually sold by the length rather than in pairs, closer in weight and technique to the West African *wax hollandais* tradition but with East African motif vocabularies. It is tailored into dresses, headwraps and men's shirts.

**Kikoi** (*kikoy*) — a woven, striped or checked cotton wrap with knotted fringe, historically loom-woven on the coast and now most associated with Lamu and Pemba. Traditionally a man's waistcloth, worn under a *kanzu*, it also serves as a shawl and a beach wrap.

**Motif and text vocabulary.** Printed *kanga* draw on paisley (*mavui*), the cashew leaf, mango, cowrie, star-and-crescent, and stylised henna arabesques; the woven *kikoi* palette favours indigo, madder red, saffron and undyed cotton. The *jina* texts range from Qur'anic invocation (*Bismillahi*) through proverb (*Haraka haraka haina baraka* — "hurry hurry has no blessing") to sly commentary on co-wives and neighbours, giving Swahili women a socially licensed public voice.

### Clothing & dress

Men's coastal dress is the *kanzu*, an ankle-length white or cream cotton robe of Omani derivation, worn with a *kofia* or *kufi* — a stiff, flat-topped embroidered cap whose openwork whitework panels (*taraza*) are painstakingly hand-embroidered in Lamu, Siyu and Zanzibar and grade a man's means by their density and fineness. A tailored waistcoat or western jacket is added for Friday prayer and weddings; on secular days men wrap a *kikoi* around the waist over a shirt. Women wear a fitted or loose dress (*deraya*) in *kitenge* or printed cotton, over which a *kanga* pair is arranged; in public, and in more observant households at all times, a black outer covering — the *buibui*, a full-length overgarment, sometimes with a face veil (*ninja* or *shela*) — is added. Bridal dress in the stone towns retains gold-embroidered *badla* work on velvet, gilt filigree jewellery, and elaborate henna (*hina*) drawn on hands and feet at the *kesha* pre-wedding gathering. Perfume — the oils *udi* (aloewood) and *dalia* sandal-and-turmeric paste applied to the face — is treated as an integral component of dress rather than an accessory.

### Architecture

The signature Swahili building is the coral-rag stone townhouse of the *mji wa mawe*, or Stone Town, best preserved on Lamu (UNESCO World Heritage, 2001), on Kilwa Kisiwani and Songo Mnara (UNESCO, 1981) and in Zanzibar's Stone Town (UNESCO, 2000). Walls are built of rough coral rag set in lime mortar and faced with a fine lime plaster (*chokaa*) burnished to an ivory sheen; roofs are flat, laid on square-sectioned mangrove poles (*boriti*) whose limited length dictates the characteristic narrow room modules of roughly 2.5 metres. The house presents a plain façade to the lane, pierced by the celebrated **Swahili door** (*mlango wa Kiswahili*) — a heavy double-leaf hardwood door set in a carved rectangular frame, its lintel and jambs incised with a chain of rosettes, lotus, fish, chain and Qur'anic inscription, and its faces studded with rows of brass bosses (*misumari*) whose form the local tradition traces, plausibly if not provably, to Gujarati elephant-door prototypes. The Lamu school favours a squared frame with dense floral carving; the Zanzibar school, from the mid-nineteenth century, introduced the semi-circular Indian-influenced arched lintel. Inside, a long entrance vestibule (*daka*) with masonry benches (*baraza*) opens onto a sequence of parallel long rooms (*misana*) around a central open court (*kiwanja*), with plastered wall-niches (*zidaka*) framing displays of Chinese porcelain, Omani chests and Qur'ans. The **coral stone carving** tradition extends from doorframes to mihrabs — the pierced coral mihrab of the Great Mosque of Kilwa is the classic example — and to the pillar tombs of the northern coast. Alongside the stone town, the vernacular *nyumba ya makuti* is a rectangular timber-framed house with wattle-and-daub or coral-rubble walls and a steep hipped roof thatched in split palm leaf (*makuti*), the ordinary dwelling of village Swahili and of poorer urban quarters. The mosque (*msikiti*) — from the small neighbourhood *msikiti wa mtaa* to the congregational Friday mosque — anchors each quarter, its qibla wall carved with a coral mihrab and, in older examples, a stepped *minbar*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Swahili coastal households historically imported their finest ceramics — Chinese celadon and blue-and-white, Persian sgraffito, Islamic monochrome-glazed wares — and set them into wall niches and tomb façades as much as they used them for dining; locally produced earthenware is a plainer, low-fired red domestic ware, comprising the water jar (*mtungi*), cooking pot (*chungu*) and incense burner (*chetezo*) in which *udi* is smouldered on charcoal. The signature wooden object is the **Swahili chest** or **Zanzibar chest** (*sanduku*), a hardwood dowry and document chest — teak, rosewood or mahogany — with a flat or slightly domed lid, its front and lid faces studded and strapped in brass and often fitted with a concealed lower drawer for coin. Zanzibari chests of the nineteenth century, made partly by Indian craftsmen for the Omani court, are the most heavily embellished; the Bombay variant, still called a Zanzibar chest in the trade, is a diaspora offshoot. The **siwa horn** — a ceremonial side-blown horn of ivory (Pate) or cast brass and copper (Lamu, sixteenth–seventeenth centuries), used to summon the town and to sound at weddings and inaugurations — survives in the Lamu Museum as one of the most important pieces of pre-colonial East African metalwork. Everyday furnishings include the woven **mkeka mat** of dyed *mkindu* (wild date palm) or *ukili* (doum-palm) strips, on which one sits, prays and, at life's end, is wrapped for burial; the carved four-poster *kitanda* bed with coir rope webbing; the low three-legged stool (*kigoda*); the wooden mortar (*kinu*) and pestle; and the coconut grater (*mbuzi*) shaped as a small bench with an iron-toothed blade.

### Jewelry & body adornment

**Swahili silver** and gold — the *pambo* of the coastal woman — draw on a shared Indian Ocean vocabulary of filigree, granulation and repoussé, with Omani, Yemeni and Gujarati input. Characteristic forms include the *hirizi*, a cylindrical or rectangular amulet case worn on a chain and holding a folded Qur'anic verse; heavy anklets (*bangili za miguu*) and bangles (*bangili*); the nose stud (*kipini*); long chain-and-plaque necklaces set with coral, carnelian and, on Pemba and Lamu, imported garnet; and, for brides, a gilt forehead ornament and temple pendants worn under the *shela*. Henna (*hina*), applied in fine black *piko* line-work with tamarind and lime for the bride and in simpler red *hina ya mikono* for festival days, is the central body-adornment practice, complemented by the yellow *singo* paste of turmeric, sandalwood and rose water rubbed onto the bride's skin during the *somo* seclusion. Kohl (*wanja*) lines the eyes of women and infants.

## Music & performance

The best-known Swahili musical form is *taarab*, the sung-poetry orchestral genre that crystallised in late-nineteenth-century Zanzibar under Sultan Barghash and fuses Egyptian *takht* orchestration (*ʿūd*, *qānūn*, *nāy*, violin, riqq) with Indian harmonium and tabla and a Swahili sung text in *utenzi* metre. The classical Zanzibar orchestras — Ikhwan Safaa (founded 1905) and Nadi Akhwan Safaa's rival Culture Musical Club — perform seated in tarbush and *kanzu*; a women's parallel form, *taarab ya wanawake*, and its more raucous descendant *kidumbak*, are performed at weddings, where audience women respond to pointed lyrics by rising to tip the singer. Older layers include *ngoma* (literally "drum" but also "dance-event"), a broad family of drum-led coastal genres — *msondo* and *unyago* accompanying girls' initiation, *chakacha* the hip-swaying wedding dance of Mombasa, *goma* and *beni* processional forms. Devotional music centres on the *maulidi* — sung praise of the Prophet in Arabic and Swahili, most famously the *Maulidi ya Homu* and *Maulidi ya Rama* of Lamu, performed with frame drums (*matwari*, *duff*) — and on Sufi *dhikr* of the Qadiriyya and Shadhiliyya orders. Instruments beyond the *taarab* orchestra include the trough zither *zeze*, the tubular fiddle *kinanda*, the box lute *gambusi* (from the Yemeni *qanbūs*), and the ceremonial *siwa* horn.

## Dance & theatre

Swahili dance is largely social and ceremonial rather than staged. *Chakacha*, danced by women at weddings with rapid isolated hip movement over a 6/8 drum pattern, is the emblematic coastal wedding dance; *msondo* and *unyago*, danced during the seclusion and coming-out of initiates on Pemba and the Mrima coast, use a tall single-headed drum of the same names and teach, in gesture, the sexual and domestic instruction of the *somo* (female instructor). Men's *goma* — a slow, dignified stick or sword dance in *kanzu* and *kofia* — is performed at weddings and Islamic feasts, and shades into the Omani-derived *razha*. The colonial-era **beni** *ngoma*, a brass-band mimicry of European military parade that swept the coast between the 1890s and 1930s, gave rise to competitive dance-associations (*mashindano*) whose structure still underlies much Swahili festival performance. Formal theatre in the Western sense is a twentieth-century development, most vigorously in the Kiswahili plays of Ebrahim Hussein and Penina Muhando; the older performance tradition is closer to sung narrative and to the improvised comic dialogue (*vichekesho*) of festival evenings.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual year follows the Islamic *hijri* calendar. *Ramadhani* is marked by the nightly *futari* breaking of the fast, the *tarawih* prayers, and, in Lamu and Zanzibar, night processions of children with lanterns. *Idd el Fitri* and *Idd el Hajj* bring new clothes, the slaughter of a goat or sheep, and long visiting rounds. *Maulidi* — the Prophet's birthday — is the great coastal festival, celebrated most spectacularly as the **Lamu Maulidi**, a week-long gathering drawing pilgrims from across the western Indian Ocean, with sung *qasidas*, dhow races, donkey races, *goma* dancing and Sufi *dhikr* in the Riyadha Mosque. The Zanzibari Persian New Year festival **Mwaka Kogwa** at Makunduchi, of Shirazi derivation, opens with a ritualised stick-fight between the men of the northern and southern halves of the village and the burning of a symbolic hut, and closes with women processing through the fields singing bawdy songs — a rare surviving pre-Islamic layer. Life-cycle ritual is dense: the *aqiqa* naming and head-shaving on the seventh day; boys' circumcision (*tohara*) grouped into age-cohorts; the girls' *unyago* seclusion and instruction under a *somo*; the multi-day wedding sequence of *kupamba* (adornment), *kesha* (henna night), *nikah* (contract) and *arusi* (feast); and the burial rites of washing, wrapping in a *sanda* shroud (often a *kanga* pair kept for the purpose) and interment facing Mecca, with a *hitima* Qur'anic recitation on the third, seventh and fortieth days.

## Foodways

Swahili cuisine is the archetypal Indian Ocean creole: a Bantu staple structure enriched with coconut, spice and rice from the monsoon trade. Rice (*wali*) cooked in coconut milk (*wali wa nazi*) is the festive staple; everyday meals rest on maize *ugali*, cassava, plantain and the flat wheat-and-coconut pancake *mkate wa sinia*. Fish and shellfish — kingfish, tuna, octopus (*pweza*), prawns — are grilled, curried, or stewed in coconut with tamarind and green mango (*mchuzi wa samaki*). Signature dishes include *biriani* and *pilau* of clearly Persian and Indian lineage but with Swahili spicing (cardamom, clove, cinnamon, black pepper, cumin — much of it Zanzibar-grown); *mishkaki* skewered grilled meat; *supu* the pre-dawn Ramadan broth of goat trotter and tripe; *urojo* (Zanzibar mix) the tamarind-turmeric street soup with cassava crisps, potato bhajia and boiled egg; and the sweets *halua* (a starch-and-ghee confection of Omani origin), *kaimati* (fried dough balls in cardamom syrup) and *vitumbua* (rice-flour coconut cakes). Drinks include coconut water (*madafu*), the fermented palm wine *tembo* (in less observant villages), spiced coffee (*kahawa chungu*) served in tiny handleless cups from a long-spouted brass pot, and cardamom-spiced tea (*chai ya tangawizi*).

## Oral tradition & literature

The Swahili possess one of the oldest written literatures in sub-Saharan Africa, recorded in the Arabic-script *ajami* orthography from at least the seventeenth century. The core classical genre is **utenzi** (also *utendi*) — the long narrative poem in quatrains of eight-syllable lines with a rhyme scheme *aaab bbbc cccd*, its terminal *b/c/d* line serving as a refrain — used for religious epic (the *Utenzi wa Herekali* attributed to Bwana Mwengo of Pate, c. 1728, narrates the Byzantine wars of the early Muslims), for prophetic biography, for historical chronicle (the *Utenzi wa Al-Akida* on the 1875 defence of Mombasa) and, in the twentieth century, for political and didactic verse (Shaaban Robert). The shorter *shairi*, in four-line stanzas of two hemistichs each, is the medium of the coastal *malumbano* — sung poetic duel — and of the printed newspaper poetry column still read across East Africa. Prose genres include the town chronicle (*Kitab al-Sulwa* of Kilwa, the *Habari za Pate*, the *Habari za Lamu*), the folktale (*hadithi*) featuring the trickster hare (*sungura*) and the ogre (*zimwi*), and the vast proverb literature (*methali*) partly preserved on *kanga* borders. Handwritten *utenzi* manuscripts on European or Indian rag paper, bound in tooled leather with fore-edge flap in the Islamic manner, survive in the collections of SOAS, the University of Dar es Salaam and the Zanzibar National Archives.

## Language & religion

**Kiswahili** is a Bantu language of the Sabaki subgroup, with the northern Kiamu (Lamu), Kimvita (Mombasa) and Kipate dialects preserving archaic features and the southern Kiunguja of Zanzibar Town forming the base of the standardised written and broadcast language codified by the Inter-Territorial Language Committee in the 1930s. Loanwords — perhaps 20–30 per cent of the lexicon in classical registers, less in modern rural speech — come chiefly from Arabic (religion, law, commerce, timekeeping), with strata from Persian (*serikali* government, *bandari* harbour), Portuguese (*meza* table, *bendera* flag), Hindi/Gujarati (*duka* shop, *chai* tea) and English. Religion is overwhelmingly Sunni Islam of the Shafiʿi school, with a strong Sufi presence (Qadiriyya, Shadhiliyya, Alawiyya) whose *hadra* gatherings and *ziyara* pilgrimages to the tombs of local saints (*mashehe*) — Habib Saleh of Lamu, Sayyid Ahmad bin Sumayt of Zanzibar — remain central to popular practice. Ibadi Islam persists among Omani-descended families in Zanzibar. A pre-Islamic substratum survives in the recognition of spirits (*majini*, *pepo*) that possess and are propitiated in coastal healing seances (*ngoma ya pepo*), and in the calendrical *Mwaka Kogwa* at Makunduchi.
