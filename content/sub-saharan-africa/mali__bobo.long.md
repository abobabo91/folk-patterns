## Overview

The Bobo, sometimes called Bobo Fing, are a Mande ethnic group living primarily in Burkina Faso, with some communities in Mali. Their major southern community is Bobo-Dioulasso, the second-largest city of Burkina Faso and its old French colonial capital. Further north are Fô and Kouka, while Boura lies in the extreme north in Mali. The Bobo number approximately 110,000 people, with the great majority in Burkina Faso. They speak Bobo, a Mande language with Northern and Southern dialects. Bobo culture includes farming, masks, wooden sculpture, drums, and decentralized social organization based on lineages and elders.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Bobo masks** — Bobo masks have detailed wooden structures extending above the main body. They are traditionally painted red, black, and silver/white, and are repainted at the beginning of each season.

**Wooden stools** — British Museum records describe stools made of wood, with handles in the form of human heads, three or four legs, and, in some examples, incised geometric patterns.

**Palls** — A Museum of World Culture record describes a one-piece wooden pall from Bomborokuy, associated with Bobo Ule. It was owned by the same family for at least three generations and was carried hooked over one shoulder.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name incised geometric patterns, animal and human features, bird heads, triangles, rhombs, crosses, and checker patterns.

### Clothing & dress

One museum record states that a wooden pall from Bomborokuy was carried hooked over one shoulder.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The documented objects are chiefly wooden masks, stools, palls, drums, and sculptures. A Museum of Ethnography catalogue records a small metal human figure from Mali, described as copper or possibly bronze, with a round pointed hat and a bent implement over one shoulder; the record associates it with the Bobo Fing-folket. Another metal object is a reddish crocodile, probably copper, associated with the Bobo Fing-folket.

## Music & performance

The museum catalogue records several drums from Bomborokuy, Övre Volta, associated with Bobo Ule. They are made of wood and skin. Some are played with a drumstick, while another is described as a “tambourine” played with the hands. The records describe drums with heights of 24 cm, 45 cm, 47 cm, and 91 cm, and diameters of 15 cm, 16,5 cm, 20 cm, and 26 cm.

One Cleveland Museum of Art record describes Bolo masks as being danced for entertainment during communal occasions such as funerals, male initiations, and harvest celebrations, but the record does not provide a Bobo-language name for a musical tradition.

## Dance & theatre

A Cleveland Museum of Art record states that Bolo masks are primarily danced for entertainment during communal occasions such as funerals, male initiations, and harvest celebrations. The mask combines animal and human features and is always worn by a male dancer. A Museum of World Culture record states that a tall Bobo Fing mask with two faces and nine carved bird heads keeps evil spirits away.

Another Museum of World Culture record describes a horned helmet mask associated with Bobo Fing. The mask bearer personifies the protective spirit “Do”, acts as a police figure, and drives away those who threaten to disturb death rites or fertility ceremonies. Each clan makes its own particular type, and animal motifs are common.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe several ritual and communal contexts. Bolo masks are danced during funerals, male initiations, and harvest celebrations. The horned helmet mask associated with Bobo Fing is used in connection with death rites and fertility ceremonies.

Bobo religious belief describes two important epochs: the time of Wuro, when the universe was created, and the contemporary time, when Wuro gave humanity his son Dwo. Blacksmiths traditionally worship Dwo. Bobo masks honor Wuro and have a spiritual role in erasing evil and balancing the sun, earth, and rain.

## Foodways

Farming is of primary importance among the Bobo and is described as the essential component of day-to-day existence. Major food crops are red sorghum, pearl millet, yams, and maize. The sources state that the Bobo also cultivate cotton, which is sold to textile mills in Koudougou, while cotton and peanuts function as primary cash crops.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that the Bobo are descendants of an ancient aggregation assembled around a number of core clans, none of which preserved oral traditions of immigration into the area. They also state that more than 41% of Bobo lineages claim a foreign origin while saying that they are autochthonous.

Bobo cosmogony describes the creation of the world by Wuro and the ordering of his creations into opposing pairs, including man and spirits, male and female, village and bush, and culture and nature. According to legend, Wuro created the first man, who was a blacksmith and received the first mask.

## Language & religion

Bobo is a Mande language of Burkina Faso and Mali. It has Northern and Southern dialects; the Northern dialect is also known as Konabéré. Northern and Southern Bobo share only 20%–30% intelligibility according to Ethnologue and are considered separate languages by that standard. The terms Bobo Fing, meaning “Black Bobo”, and Bobo Madaré distinguish them from Bobo Gbe, meaning “White Bobo”, and Bobo Oule, meaning “Red Bobo”, of Burkina.

The Bobo traditionally follow a pagan, animist religion. Wuro is the creator god who formed the earth from a ball of clay. He cannot be described and is not represented by sculptures. Wuro’s son Dwo is important in the contemporary epoch, and blacksmiths traditionally worship Dwo. Some Bobo have converted to Christianity or Islam.
