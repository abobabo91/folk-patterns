## Overview

The Kikuyu (*Agĩkũyũ*, sing. *Mũgĩkũyũ*) are a Northeastern Bantu people of the central Kenyan highlands, farming the volcanic ridges and river valleys that fall away southwest from Mount Kenya — the counties of Nyeri (*Gaki*), Murang'a (*Metumi*), Kiambu (*Kabete*), Kirinyaga and Nyandarua, drained by the Chania, Thika, Sagana and Ragati rivers. At 8,148,668 in the 2019 census they are Kenya's largest ethnic group, about 17% of the national population. Their language, Gĩkũyũ, is Bantu, closest to Embu and Mbeere. In folk-culture terms they matter because their material world was built almost entirely around the ridge-farm homestead and the age-set: nine matrilineal clans descended from the daughters of Mũmbi, a generation-class system (*riika*) that dated history by initiation name, and a craft repertoire — iron, leather, gourd, clay, bead — organised around those passages rather than around courts or markets.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Kikuyu material culture is not loom-based. The highlands produced no cotton weaving before the colonial period, and cloth in the ethnographic sense means dressed skin, beadwork on leather, and plaited fibre. The pattern-bearing traditions are therefore hide, bead and bast.

**Kikuyu leather (*rũhiũ* / *nguo cia njũa*)** — Goat and sheepskins scraped with an iron blade, worked soft with fat and red ochre, and cut into the cloak and apron forms below. The surface is the pattern field: skins are incised, punched in dotted rows, or seeded with beads sewn directly into the hide, and the ochre-and-fat dressing gives the characteristic dull red that distinguishes Kikuyu skinwork from the harder, blacker Maasai finish.

**Kikuyu beadwork (*mũthanga wa thanju*)** — Glass trade beads, and before them iron chain, seed and cowrie, laid in bands on leather grounds for collars, aprons, belts and headbands. Kikuyu beadwork favours dense linear banding and panels on skin rather than the discrete flat discs of Maasai *emonyorit*, and the older strings are heavy with locally drawn iron and brass wire — wire drawing and chain making were named Kikuyu industries.

**Kikuyu basketry (*kiondo*)** — The twined bag of the highlands, made from *mũgĩo* and other bark-fibre string rolled on the thigh, worked in a close diagonal twine and carried from the forehead by a leather strap (*mũkwa*). Colour is banded — natural, ochre, black from bark and mud dyes — in horizontal registers. The *kiondo* is the single Kikuyu form that has travelled worldwide as a commercial craft; the older domestic versions are coarser, undyed and larger, for maize and sweet potatoes.

**String and cordage (*mĩkwa*)** — String making is listed among the small industries of the pre-colonial Agĩkũyũ, and the twisted bark-fibre carrying strap, beehive rope and granary lashing are technically the parent craft of the *kiondo*.

**Kikuyu textile (post-1900)** — Machine cotton *shuka* and the white calico wrap entered with the colonial trade stores and the missions; by the 1930s they had largely displaced dressed skin in daily dress, surviving as ochre-dyed cotton in ceremony.

**Motif vocabulary.** Banded horizontal registers (the organising principle of *kiondo* and beaded belts); dotted punch rows on hide; ochre-red ground; zig-zag and chevron on twined bags; alternating light-dark diagonal twine; concentric ring of beads at the neck of a gourd; incised chevrons and hatched triangles on calabash and stool; cowrie panels on aprons and headbands; wire coil on leather.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress was dressed skin. Men wore *nguo ya njũa*, a cloak of goatskins knotted on one shoulder, over a short skin apron, with an iron or bead belt. Women wore a two-part skin costume: *mũthuru*, a wrap-skirt of soft dressed goatskin reaching below the knee, and *nguo*, an upper cloak; married women added *mwengũ*, a small beaded or fringed front apron. Ochre and fat were rubbed into both skin garments and body. Ceremonial dress marked the *riika*. Initiates wore beaded headbands and, at the dances, *mathangu* leaf-and-skin skirts; warriors of the *anake* grade wore the colobus-monkey cape, ostrich-feather headdress and thigh-bells (*njũga*) that rang on the beat, and carried the *itimũ* spear and the painted shield. Elders were distinguished by two things rather than by garment: the brass ear ornament stretched in the lobe, and the *mũthĩgi*, the staff that signified the power to lead, paired with the *itimũ*, the power to call to war.

### Architecture

The Kikuyu homestead (*mũcii*) was a ridge-top cluster inside a thorn or *mĩthuthi* hedge, not a village. Its buildings are all round, mud-and-wattle-walled, with a conical thatch of grass or banana leaf on a pole ring, a single low door and no windows. The central distinction is between *nyũmba*, the woman's house — each wife had her own, with hearth, sleeping platforms, goat pen inside and grain store — and *thingira*, the man's hut, smaller, set apart, where he slept, ate the food brought by each wife in turn, and received other men. Boys past a certain age slept in their own *thingira*, girls in the mother's *nyũmba*. Around them stood the raised granary (*ikũmbĩ*) on stones or stilts against damp and rats, its thatch cap lifting off; the goat and cattle enclosure; and, outside the hedge, the *mũgumo* fig or *mũkũyũ* sycamore under which sacrifice was made. There is almost no applied ornament: the aesthetic is in thatch-coursing, the woven door, and the geometry of the hedge and the granary line along a ridge.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**Kikuyu pottery** was women's work, hand-built by coiling without a wheel, burnished with a pebble, and open-fired: rounded cooking pots (*nyũngũ*), water jars, and the small-necked *mũtũngi*. Decoration is restrained — a burnished neck, a combed or roulette band on the shoulder. **Kikuyu gourd and calabash** work is the more elaborate container art: *kinya* for milk and gruel, *kĩnandũ* for fermenting, each scraped, smoked to black inside, and often collared with beads and leather straps; **Kikuyu snuff containers** (*gĩcuhĩ*), small stoppered gourds or horn, hang on a beaded thong at the elder's neck. The **Kikuyu stool** (*gĩtĩ*) is carved from one block, three legs, a shallow dished seat, sometimes incised on the underside. Smithing was a specialist lineage art: spear blades, *rũhiũ rwa njora* swords, hoes, iron chain and drawn wire. The **Kikuyu shield** (*ngo*) is buffalo or cow hide on a wooden spine, painted in bold ochre, black and white fields, and the dance shield (*ndome*), a small oval of wood carried by initiates, is painted in the colours of the initiate's *riika*.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ornament was in wire and bead rather than precious metal. Women wore coiled brass and iron wire on the arms and legs, layered bead collars, and *hang'i*, the heavy coiled ear ornament that stretched the pierced and widened lobe. Both sexes pierced the upper ear and the lobe; extended earlobes with wooden or brass plugs were an elder's mark. Men carried the beaded snuff gourd and wire armlets. Body treatment centred on red ochre mixed with fat, rubbed over skin and hair, and on the initiation-time shaving and head-dressing of the *riika*. Girls' initiation was marked by new beads given by the mother; the marriage chain (*mũgathi*) and beaded belt identified married women. Scarification is not a Kikuyu tradition; the permanent body marks are the pierced and stretched ear and, historically, the circumcision of both sexes — the ban on female circumcision being one of the great political controversies of the 1920s Kikuyu Central Association.

## Music & performance

Kikuyu music is voice-led and percussive, built for the open ground of the homestead and the initiation field rather than for a court. The central instrument is *kĩgamba*, the leg-rattle of iron bells or seed pods worn by dancers, whose clash on the downbeat is the timeline. Drums include the *mũrĩĩthi* type of hide drum and, in the *mũgithi* and *nyĩmbo cia kũina* contexts, hand-beaten single-headed drums. Melodic instruments are few and small: the *wandĩndĩ*, a one-string bowed tube-fiddle played by herdboys and, later, by professional itinerant singers; the *kĩhembe ngoma*; the *coro*, an end-blown horn of antelope or kudu sounded to call people together or to war; and the flute (*mũtũrĩrũ*). The great genre is antiphonal: a soloist (*mwĩmbi*) lines out the verse and the age-set answers in a short refrain, with hand-clap and rattle. Song types are occasional and named for their function — *gĩcaandĩ*, the sung riddle-contest performed to a gourd rattle by two competing poets, is the most virtuosic and the one most often recorded by ethnographers; *mũthuũ* and *mũmbũro* belong to initiation; *nyĩmbo cia arĩĩtu* are girls' songs; and there is a distinct genre of *mbaki*, work and beer-party song. In the twentieth century *mũgithi* — the "train" dance-song, single-file, one guitar, Gĩkũyũ lyrics — became the dominant popular form, and Joseph Kamaru's benga recordings carried the old antiphonal structure into the guitar band.

## Dance & theatre

Kikuyu dance is age-graded, not theatrical: there is no mask tradition and no puppet or dramatic form. Dances are named for the grade that performs them. *Mũmbũro* and *mũthuũ* are the initiation-season dances of boys, danced with the small painted *ndome* shield and leg-bells; *kĩbaata* is the warriors' dance of Nyeri, a stamping, spear-and-shield formation with ostrich plumes, its pace set by the rattles; *mũchũng'wa* and *gĩthũngũri* belong to young unmarried people at beer-parties and markets. Women's dance is closer-formation and hand-clapped, with a bent-forward torso and shoulder shimmer. *Ndũmo* is the old chant-dance of the elders. All are circle or line forms with a caller at the centre, no audience-performer separation, and no fixed stage. The nearest thing to theatre is the *gĩcaandĩ* contest, where two poets improvise riddling verse against each other before a judging crowd, and the Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o and Ngũgĩ wa Mĩriĩ community play *Ngaahika Ndeenda* (*I Will Marry When I Want*, Kamĩrĩĩthũ, 1977), which drew on these song-and-dance forms and was banned for it.

## Festivals & rituals

The Kikuyu year had four seasons and two harvests: *mbura ya njahĩ*, the long rains, March to July; *magetha ma njahĩ*, the black-bean (*Lablab purpureus*) harvest, July to October; *mbura ya mwere*, the short rains, October to January; and *magetha ma mwere*, the millet harvest. Both harvests and the onset of each rain carried sacrifice at the *mũgumo* fig or *mũkũyũ* sycamore — the olive, *mũtamaiyũ*, being the women's sacred tree — offered by elders to Ngai, also called Mwene-Nyaga, who dwells on Mount Kenya and comes down to inspect the earth. The great political ritual was *Ituĩka*, the handover of government from one ruling generation to the next, held roughly every 35 years to prevent the return of despotism; the last complete one passed power from the Maina generation to Mwangi in 1898–99, and the one due in 1925–28 was suppressed by the colonial government. Life-cycle rites: *gũciarwa*, birth, with the ululation count that announces the child's sex and a naming after the grandparents; *irua*, the initiation that created the *riika* and, with it, the calendar — sets were named for the events of their year, so that smallpox or a famine is still datable by a man's initiation name; *ũhiki*, marriage, negotiated through *rũracio* bridewealth in goats, and the *ngurario* ceremony that made it final; and burial with offerings, since the dead return as *ngoma*, ancestral spirits, and may be reborn.

## Foodways

The staples are the products of the highland shamba: *mwere* (finger millet), *mũhĩa* (sorghum), *njahĩ* (the black lablab bean, the prestige crop and the harvest that named a season), *njũgũ* (pigeon pea), sweet potato, yam, arrowroot (*nduma*), and after the eighteenth century maize, which displaced millet as the everyday grain. The signature dish is *irio* (also *mũkĩmo*) — green peas, maize and beans boiled with potato and mashed with pumpkin or nettle leaves until green, served as a mound and eaten with the hand; the version with *njahĩ* mashed into banana was the food given to a woman after childbirth. *Gĩtheri* is the plain boiled maize-and-bean mixture that is the everyday meal. *Ũcũrũ*, fermented millet or maize gruel, is drunk daily and given to nursing mothers. Meat is goat, roasted (*nyama choma* in the modern form) and eaten at ceremonies, with the liver and specific cuts reserved for particular grades and for the elders' portion. *Njohi* — sugarcane or honey beer — is the ritual drink, brewed for bridewealth negotiation, elders' councils and sacrifice, and no elders' business proceeded without it. *Mataha*, a mash of maize, beans and greens, and *rũkũrũ* fill out the repertoire. There are no religious dietary prohibitions, but clan and grade rules governed who ate which part of a sacrificed animal.

## Oral tradition & literature

The foundational narrative is the origin story of *Gĩkũyũ* and *Mũmbi*, given the land at *Mũkũrwe wa Nyagathanga* in Murang'a by Ngai on Mount Kenya, and of their nine daughters — Wanjirũ, Wambũi, Wacera, Wanjikũ, Waithĩra, Wairimũ, Wangarĩ, Wangũi and Wangeci — from whom the nine clans (Anjirũ, Ambũi, Aacera, Agacikũ, Airimũ, Angarĩ and the rest) descend, each clan tracing to a single female ancestor. The prose genres are *ng'ano*, the animal and ogre tale, whose recurring figures are the hare (*mbũkũ*) as trickster and the *irimũ*, the man-eating ogre; *thimo*, proverb, the dense idiom of the elders' council where argument is made by quoting; and *ndaĩ*, riddle, exchanged competitively in the evening. The *gĩcaandĩ* sung riddle-contest is the elaborated literary form, and it used a mnemonic system of signs incised on the gourd rattle itself. Written Gĩkũyũ literature began with the mission orthographies and the KCA press; Jomo Kenyatta's *Facing Mount Kenya* (1938) is the first full insider ethnography, Gakaara wa Wanjaũ wrote both the clan history *Mĩhĩrĩga ya Aagĩkũyũ* and a detention diary, and Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o's turn to writing novels in Gĩkũyũ from *Caitaani Mũtharaba-inĩ* (1980) onward is the central act of the modern language revival.

## Language & religion

Gĩkũyũ is a Northeastern Bantu language (Niger-Congo), closest to Embu and Mbeere, with dialects matching the old districts — Kĩambu/Kabete, Mũrang'a/Metumi, Nyeri/Gaki, Ndia and Gĩchũgũ — differing chiefly in tone and in the *th* and *r* series. It has no indigenous script; the Latin orthography with its marked vowels (ĩ, ũ) dates from missionary printing in the early twentieth century. Most speakers also use Swahili and English. The traditional religion is animist and monotheistic at its apex: Ngai (Mwene-Nyaga), a distant creator who manifests in thunder, lightning, rainbows, comets and the great fig trees, is approached through sacrifice by elders under the *mũgumo*, with the *ngoma* ancestors as intermediaries and the belief that spirits may be reborn. Christianity is now overwhelmingly dominant, but the *mũgumo* remains a sacred site, its unprompted fall is still read as an omen, and the orientation to Mount Kenya in prayer survives in practice.
