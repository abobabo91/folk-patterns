## Overview

The Turkana are a Nilotic people native to Turkana County in northwest Kenya, a semi-arid region bordering Lake Turkana. Their territory lies near the Turkwel River and Kerio River, with Uganda to the west, South Sudan to the north-west, Ethiopia to the north, and Pokot, Rendille and Samburu territories to the south. According to the 2009 Kenyan census, they numbered 1,016,174, or 2.14% of Kenya’s population. They refer to themselves as *ŋiTurkana* and to their land as “Turkan.” Turkana is an Eastern Nilotic language closely related to Karamojong, Jie and Teso of Uganda, Toposa and Nyangatom. Turkana folk culture is documented through semi-nomadic pastoralism, livestock exchange, woven baskets, elaborate clothing and adornment, ritual specialists, dance objects, milk vessels, weapons and household goods.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Rectangular woven wraps** — Traditionally, men and women wear wraps made from rectangular woven materials and animal skins. Men commonly arrange the wrap in a tunic-like form, with one end connected over the right shoulder.

**Leather wraps with ostrich eggshell beads** — Women’s traditional undergarments included leather wraps covered with ostrich eggshell beads. The source states that these are now uncommon in many areas.

**Hide aprons** — Museum records document small aprons and a girl’s pubic apron made of hide. One British Museum record describes an apron attached to a broad belt and decorated with lines of circular ostrich shell beads; another records hide aprons trimmed with groups of large coloured beads and, in one case, copper and iron beads.

**Beaded clothing and accessories** — Museum objects include a girl’s hide hip-cloth decorated with cowrie shells and beads, a beaded purse made of hide, belts made with bones and blue glass beads, and head-dresses combining beads, hide and wire.

**Woven fibre baskets** — The Turkana are noted for weaving baskets. A museum catalogue records a circular basket with a flat base, straight sides and a shallow circular lid, made from interlaced strips of light-coloured fibre.

**Leather and hide containers** — Museum records include vessels with stretched hide bases, leather suspension thongs and containers used for fat or ghee. Other vessels are made from wood or gourd and combined with leather carrying cradles, plaited cords or bead decoration.

**Museum-documented Turkana objects** — Museum catalogues identify these textile and fibre objects with Turkana, Ng’aturkwana or Ng’aturkana records.

**Motif vocabulary.** zigzags and lines, circular ostrich shell beads, cowrie shells, groups of large coloured beads.

### Clothing & dress

Traditionally, both men and women wear rectangular woven materials and animal skins. Men arrange their wraps similarly to tunics, securing one end over the right shoulder. They also carry wrist knives made of steel and goat hide, stools known as *ekicholong*, and several staves: one for walking and balance when carrying loads, and another for prodding livestock during herding. Men wear their hair shaved. The sources also state that men wear a cap, whether they are Muslims or not.

Women wear two pieces of cloth, one wrapped around the waist and the other covering the upper body. They customarily wear necklaces and either shave their hair completely or attach beads to the loose ends of their hair. Traditional leather undergarments covered with ostrich eggshell beads are now uncommon in many areas. Museum records document hide aprons, hip-cloths decorated with cowrie shells and beads, belts, head-dresses, hair-pieces and lip ornaments. Many Turkana have adopted western-style clothing, especially men and women living in town centres throughout Turkana.

### Architecture

Turkana houses are constructed over a wooden framework of domed saplings. Fronds of the doum palm, hides or skins are thatched and lashed onto the framework. The house is large enough to accommodate a family of six. During the wet season, houses are usually elongated and covered with cow dung. Animals are kept in a brushwood pen.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources describe household objects made from wood, gourd, hide, fibre, leather and iron. Museum records include milk-pails made of wood and hide; milk-bowls made from hollowed wood or gourd; wooden milk vessels with leather carrying cradles; wooden drinking vessels; spoons with pokerwork decoration; and vessels used for fat or ghee. A small gourd container has pokerwork decoration, glass beads around its neck and an iron spoon attached by a thong.

Metal and hide objects include wrist-knives, finger-knives, fighting bracelets, bells and adzes. A wrist-knife is described as having a lunate iron blade and two leather guards, while another is recorded as a weapon worn around the wrist. Museum catalogues also document wooden clubs, shields made of crocodile skin, throwing sticks, head-rests and stools carved from wood.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Turkana women customarily wear necklaces, including necklaces made from ostrich eggshell beads. Museum records document belts made from rock hyrax digit bones linked with blue glass beads, waist belts made from the bones of the Dik-dik antelope, hide waistlets with beads, armlets of ivory, and ornaments made from hide, plastic beads and metal beads.

Hair and head adornment are significant in the sources. Women may shave their hair or wear beads attached to loose hair ends. Museum catalogues record composite chignons or head-dresses made from woven “ancestor’s hair,” feathers and coiled brass wire, as well as head-dresses made from hide, beads and wire. A head ornament made of copper wire and string is also recorded.

Other documented adornments include a man’s aluminium nose ornament, an aluminium lip-plate, and a male lip ornament worn on a wooden pin passing through the lower lip. An amulet made from pangolin scales is recorded as being worn around the leg as *dawa*, described as an amulet and medicine.

## Music & performance

The sources provide little information about Turkana musical genres, song forms or performance practice. They do document instruments and objects associated with performance. A composite trough zither has six strings and is formed from a trough-shaped piece of wood with a handle. A wooden flute is end-blown, has four finger holes, and is bound with fibre cord. A horn made from sable antelope or oryx horn, with a feather tassel and hide covering, is identified as being used during dances.

Museum records also include a dance-stick made from wood with a leather thong securing lengths of animal hair, a fly-whisk made of hair and leather, and a dance whisk worn tightly around the forearm between the wrist and elbow. A goat bell made of iron and hide is also recorded. The source describes music only through these catalogue records and does not name Turkana musical scales, song genres, ensembles or professional musicians.

## Dance & theatre

The sources document Turkana dance through museum photographs and objects. A 1922 catalogue record identifies a photograph of Turkana dance and describes it as *Turkana-ngoma*. The record places the photograph at Muruessi in 1922. Other museum objects associated with dance include a dance whisk worn on the forearm, a horn used during dances, and a dance-stick decorated with animal hair and leather thongs.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources identify several Turkana life-cycle and community rituals. Birth rituals are called *aikido*. Male and female initiation rituals are called *aspen* and *akinyonyo*; the source specifically states that these do not include circumcision. Marriage rituals are called *Akuuta*. Annual blessing sacrifices are called *Apiaret an awi*, and death rituals are called *Akinuuk*.

These rituals are overseen by clan elders, both men and women. Elders also oversee community-wide wedding rituals, while an *emuron* normally takes part in blessing a marriage. The *ngimurok* are religious specialists who help identify the source of evil, sickness or other problems and determine the cure or sacrifice needed to restore abundant life. They may communicate with ancestors, identify an animal sacrifice, bring rain, seek a remedy for a child’s illness or bless families at weddings.

## Foodways

The Turkana rely on their animals for milk and meat. Goats, camels, donkeys and zebu are primary herd stock. Milk is central to daily foodways: in the morning, people eat maize porridge with milk, while lunch and dinner commonly consist of plain maize porridge, *nang’aria*, with a stew. The Turkana buy tea from towns and make milk tea.

Wild fruits are gathered by women from bushes and cooked for up to 12 hours. Goats are often roasted over a fire, and roasting is described as the favoured method of preparing meat. Zebu is eaten during festivals, while goat is consumed more frequently. After hunting, men gather honey, described as the only natural sweetener available in traditional diets.

The Turkana trade with the Pokot for maize, beans and vegetables and with the Marakwet for tobacco. They also cultivate some food, including sorghum, although sorghum cultivation is described as more limited by environmental factors than pastoralism. Fish is taboo for some Turkana clans, called brands or *ngimacharin*. Development aid programmes have attempted to introduce fishing, with very limited success.

## Oral tradition & literature

Turkana oral traditions describe the people as the “people of the grey bull,” referring to the zebu. The cultivation of zebu cattle and the rise of diviners, *ngimurok*, are presented in tradition as important to the accumulation of Turkana wealth and power.

The sources describe *ngimurok* as religious specialists who receive revelations, interpret problems and communicate with ancestors. The most respected “true diviners,” also called “diviners of God,” are said to receive revelations directly from Akuj, normally through dreams. The sources name Lokerio and Lokorijem as famous Turkana *ngimurok*. They also describe specialists who read tea leaves, tobacco, intestines, shoes, stones and string.

## Language & religion

Turkana is an Eastern Nilotic language spoken by the Turkana people of Kenya and Ethiopia. It is primarily spoken in northwestern Kenya, especially Turkana County west of Lake Turkana. It is closely related to Karamojong, Jie and Teso of Uganda, Toposa of South Sudan and Nyangatom in the South Sudan–Ethiopia Omo valley borderland. These languages form the cluster of Ateker languages, whose collective group name is Ateker.

Turkana has two tonemes, high and low, and six cases: nominative, absolute, genitive, instrumental, locative and vocative. It is a verb-initial language with verb–subject–object and verb–object–subject word orders.

Turkana traditional religion does not sharply distinguish the sacred from the profane. Akuj is the Supreme Being who created the world and controls the blessings of life. Ancestors, called *ngipean* or *ngikaram*, are believed to require animal sacrifices when angry. Ekipe is described as an ancestor active in everyday life through negative effects. Some Turkana are Christians, while Islam was also brought to the people by Arabs.
