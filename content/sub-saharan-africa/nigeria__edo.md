## Overview

The Edo are an Edoid-speaking people of southern Nigeria descended from the founders of the Benin Kingdom. Their court arts—bronze plaques, ivory masks, and coral regalia—were made by hereditary palace guilds under state control, creating an unusually rich and consistent material record from the fifteenth century onward. Edo culture centres on the Oba (king), palace ritual, and the veneration of ancestors and water spirits. Today most Edo are Christian, though traditional Edo religion remains widely practised alongside.

## Material culture

Edo material culture is organised around the palace and hereditary craft guilds, with coral, brass, and cast bronze as the dominant materials of chiefly dress and ritual.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Edo cloth is dominated by scarlet ododo and coral-bead composites, with imported George, velvet and lace wrappers now conventionalised for ceremony.

- **Ododo cloth** (*ododo*) — Deep scarlet flannel worn by chiefs and the Oba, read as apotropaic and a mark of authority.
- **Coral-bead cloth composite** (*ukpon-ivie*) — Coral beads stitched in dense rows onto textile, creating a lapidary surface used for all beaded regalia.
- **Beaded cape** (*ewu-ivie*) — A net of coral and glass beads across the shoulders and chest, worn by titled women and brides.
- **Patterned wrapper cloth** (*ebe-ame*) — White and blue-white cloths associated with water and Olokun devotion, standard offering-dress at shrines.
- **Raffia and plant-fibre work** (*ivbiotu*) — Plaited mats and fibre skirts, the everyday counterpart to imported cloth and still used in shrine and masking costume.

### Clothing & dress

Edo ceremonial dress is built around coral beads, with men and women wearing wrappers, beaded caps, necklaces and anklets in graded styles from everyday to chiefly.

- **Coral high collar** (*odigba*) — A choker of cylindrical coral beads closing the throat and chin, worn by chiefs and royals.
- **Coral necklaces** (*ivie-uru*) — Multi-strand necklaces of barrel and cylindrical coral beads, worn by women as part of ceremonial dress.
- **Coral handbag** (*ekpa-ivie*) — A beaded bag carried by women, made with the same netted-bead construction as the ewu-ivie cape.
- **Hand ornament** (*ivie-obo*) — Beaded ornament worn on the hands as part of women's chiefly dress.
- **Ceremonial sword** (*eben*) — A leaf-bladed sword carried by chiefs and used in salute and in the eben leap-and-catch dance.

### Architecture

The Edo built in laterite mud with rectangular compounds centred on a sunken, plastered courtyard (impluvium) open to the sky, with hand-burnished walls and interior pillar reliefs.

- **Sunken courtyard** (*impluvium*) — A hard-plastered rain basin open to the sky at the centre of the house, which collected water and lit the surrounding rooms.
- **Interior verandah post relief** (*iy'uzo*) — Modelled clay or bronze-faced pillars and wall piers in the palace and chiefs' houses, originally filled with Benin Bronze plaques.
- **Palace of the Oba** — A stacked series of courtyards with roof turrets crowned by cast-bronze birds and serpents, housing the historical plaques.
- **Benin walls and ditch system** — An earthwork of banks and moats extending far beyond the city to enclose satellite settlements.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Edo metalwork is guild-organised, with hereditary brass-casters producing lost-wax plaques and commemorative heads, while ivory and wood carvers make masks and ritual staffs.

- **Brasscasters' guild** (*igun eronmwon*) — The hereditary guild on Igun Street holding the right to cast by lost-wax, making plaques, ancestral heads and figures.
- **Ivory and wood carvers** (*igbesanmwan*) — Responsible for pectoral masks, carved tusks in registers of kings, and wooden ikegobo altars.
- **Ancestral head** (*uhunmwun elao*) — A commemorative brass head cast by the hereditary guild, erected at the second burial of a chief or royal.
- **Altar of the hand** (*ikegobo*) — A cylindrical altar in wood, brass or terracotta dedicated to individual achievement and the power of one's own hand.
- **Rattle staff** (*ukhurhe*) — A segmented ancestral staff struck on the ground to summon the dead, also erected at second burials.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Little is recorded.

- **Cylindrical coral beads** (*ivie*) — The primary material for all high-status jewelry, including chokers, necklaces, anklets and wristlets, regulated by the palace.
- **Coral-mesh cap** — A cap of coral beads worn as part of royal and chiefly dress, a form of the beaded cap style.
- **Keloid scarification** (*iwu*) — Vertical scars once cut on the chest and torso of Edo men and women as a mark of Benin identity, abandoned in the twentieth century.
- **High crested hairstyle** — A forward-curving style bound with beads, recorded on pectoral masks and worn as a ceremonial hairstyle or beaded cap.

## Music & performance

Edo music is drum-led and tied to the palace and shrine worship, with praise-singers reciting royal genealogy and shrines supporting their own women's song traditions.

- **Membrane drums** (*ema*) — Core ensemble drums paired with slit and hourglass forms in Edo music.
- **Iron bell** (*ukho*) — Clapperless single and double bells cast by the brass guild, part of the core ensemble.
- **Plucked bow-lute** (*akpata*) — A trough-zither played by professional praise-singers and narrators who recite royal genealogy to the Oba.
- **Highlife rhythm** (*ekassa*) — A twentieth-century popular form built by Victor Uwaifo directly out of Benin ceremonial drum patterns.

## Dance & theatre

Palace dances and masquerades organised by age-grade and ward are the best-documented Edo dances, with masked performance understood to bring ancestral presence.

- **Royal succession dance** (*ekassa*) — Danced at the installation and death of an Oba, its rhythm was later secularised into highlife music.
- **Ceremonial sword salute** (*eben*) — A leaping turn in which the leaf-bladed sword is thrown and caught before the Oba, functioning as dance, oath and homage.
- **Masked performance** — Costumes combining raffia, appliquéd cloth and carved wooden headpieces, appearing at festivals and second burials.
- **Women's shrine dance** — Slow dance in place in white cloth at Olokun shrines, with movement carried by shoulders and hands.

## Festivals & rituals

The Igue festival in December–January is the central annual rite, in which the Oba's head is ritually fortified, ancestors addressed, and blessings pronounced for the kingdom.

- **Year-end festival** (*Igue*) — Held in December–January, a sequence of rites fortifying the Oba's head and addressing the ancestors.
- **Homage to the Oba's father** (*Ugie Erha Oba*) — A subsidiary rite in the Igue cycle honouring the previous Oba.
- **Prosperity rite** (*Ugie Ewere*) — A rite in which the ewere leaf is carried through Benin City as a token of prosperity.
- **Second burial** — A large funeral rite at which the heir erects an ancestral altar with a carved ukhurhe staff or cast uhunmwun elao memorial head.
- **Water spirit shrine festival** — Olokun devotees hold periodic shrine festivals marked by white cloth, chalk and river offerings.

## Foodways

The Edo staple is pounded starch eaten with a thick soup, with the signature dish being owo, a palm-oil and potash soup eaten with plantain or pounded yam.

- **Pounded yam starch** — Made from white yam, a standard swallow eaten with soup.
- **Cassava starch** (*eba*) — Also called akpu, a cassava-based swallow eaten with soup.
- **Palm-oil and potash soup** (*owo*) — The signature Benin dish, sharply flavoured and thickened without vegetables, eaten with plantain or pounded yam.
- **Wild mango-seed soup** (*ogbono*) — A staple soup made from wild mango seeds.
- **Black leaf soup** (*ebe-ebe*) — A Benin speciality in which bitter and aromatic leaves are ground to near-black liquor and cooked with dried fish, snail and meat.

## Oral tradition & literature

Edo oral tradition centres on dynastic history transmitted by palace praise-singers, with folktale, proverb and riddle as domestic genres, and Chief Jacob U. Egharevba's written history as the reference point.

- **King-list recitation** (*Ogiso*) — The first dynasty's king-list transmitted as recited chronicle by palace praise-singers and ward chiefs.
- **Royal genealogy** (*Oba*) — The genealogy of the later dynasty, transmitted as recited chronicle and recorded visually on Benin plaques.
- **Animal and trickster narrative** (*okhuae*) — Domestic folktale performed at evening gatherings.
- **Historical chronicle** — Chief Jacob U. Egharevba's *A Short History of Benin* (1934), which fixed recited dynastic tradition in writing and remains the reference for Benin chronology.

## Language & religion

Edo belongs to the Edoid group of Niger-Congo, written in Latin orthography with tone marking; most Edo today are Christian, with traditional Edo religion widely practised alongside.

- **Supreme God** (*Osanobua*) — Creator and Supreme God, whose epithets describe omniscience, omnipresence and omnipotence.
- **Water spirit and giver of wealth** (*Olokun*) — Child of Osanobua, ruler of all waters and giver of fertility and wealth.
- **Patron of metalworkers** (*Ogun*) — Supernatural intercessor served at shrines, patron of the metalworking guilds.
- **Thunder spirit** (*Shango*) — Supernatural intercessor of thunder and lightning.
- **Spirit of medicine** (*Osun*) — Supernatural intercessor of medicine and healing.
