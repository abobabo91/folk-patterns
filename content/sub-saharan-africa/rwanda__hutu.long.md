## Overview

The Hutu, also called the Abahutu, are a Bantu people of the African Great Lakes region. They live principally in Rwanda and Burundi, and also in Uganda and the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, where they are one of the three principal population divisions alongside the Tutsi and the Great Lakes Twa. Before 2017 the CIA World Factbook put them at 84% of Rwandans and 85% of Burundians; those figures were dropped that year and no replacement has been published. Their language, Rwanda-Rundi, belongs to the Bantu subgroup of the Niger–Congo family and is shared with Tutsi and Twa speakers. In folk-culture terms, the objects collected from them — twined grass mats and screens, ivory carving, iron blades, trough zithers and lamellophones — record a household and craft repertoire built on plaited plant fibre, worked iron and carved ivory.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources name no vernacular textile terms for the Hutu, and none of the search terms we indexed appear in them. What the museum catalogue does document is a plaiting and twining tradition in grass, straw and fibre, recorded in the British Museum's 1948 register.

**Twined grass mats** — Sleeping mats and bed covers of grass or straw, plaited and twined, some with the lengths of straw twined together using brown fibre and finished with braided borders on two sides. One example is a twill-plaited sleeping mat with an edge binding, its strips alternating between natural colour and black.

**Patterned mat work** — The same twined technique carries geometric design. One mat is patterned in large diamonds of natural colour set against a checkered black-and-white ground; another carries a design in squares of black on natural colour.

**Twined hut screens** — A portion of a hut screen survives as a curved section worked in fine twined fibre, black-dyed and natural coloured, over a coarser woven base — the same two-tone logic as the mats, applied to the fabric of the dwelling itself.

**Plaited vessels and wear** — Grass plaiting extends to small objects: an oval plaited dish in black and brown natural colours, and a plaited anklet of dull light brown grass.

**Motif vocabulary.** Large diamonds on a checkered black-and-white ground; squares of black on natural colour; alternating natural and black strips in twill plaiting.

### Clothing & dress

The catalogue records dress in skin rather than cloth. Two skin skirts survive, both made with the hair left on: one black, with brown "legs" and a fringe, the other white speckled with black, with a single brown "leg" and a fringe. The "legs" are the animal's own limbs left attached to the pelt, so the skirt keeps the outline of the hide it was cut from. A composite baby's neckband is made of plaited skin with two tassels of brown, black and white hair, hung with a string of wooden sticks.

### Architecture

The only architectural evidence in the sources is a fragment: a portion of a hut screen, a curved section made of fibre, its fine twined black-dyed and natural-coloured work laid over a coarser woven base. The curve indicates a rounded interior or enclosure. A composite rain-guard — an inner layer of grass stems and an outer layer of leaves, knotted together with twisted leaf rope — belongs to the same family of plant-fibre shelter, though it is portable rather than built.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery in the collection is unglazed and plainly worked: a load of three small pottery vessels with cord decoration, tied together with a twisted fibre rope, and a solid pottery container shaped like a cotton reel with a shallow cavity in its top. Ironwork is the larger body of evidence. There are composite swords with wooden hilts and iron blades, black except where filed; spears with wooden shafts, one with an iron spiked butt and an iron head blackened but filed along the edges and midrib; arrows, notched and feathered, with iron heads bound to the shaft with sinew, some barbed; knives, one cut from a single iron strip so that blade and handle are one piece, another with an iron blade in a cracked wooden haft. Tools include a composite adze whose tanged blade is shaped like a spear head, and a tanged iron bill hook with a wooden handle. A wooden club and a bamboo holder containing four drinking straws complete the household range.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment in the collection runs to four types. A bangle is a plain section of elephant tusk, dirty white. A bracelet is coiled brass. An anklet is plaited grass, a dull light brown. The baby's neckband of plaited skin, with two tassels of brown, black and white hair and a string of wooden sticks, is the one piece tied to a stage of life. Ivory is also worked figuratively rather than as ornament: carved human heads, a carved woman with a bag on her back, a carved bird, and a hippopotamus tusk carved into the shape of a crocodile.

## Music & performance

Three classes of instrument are documented. A **sansa** — a lamellophone — is made of wood with pokerwork decoration and carries ten keys with small rattles attached, the rattles of iron; the pokerwork, burnt into the body, is the same decorative technique used on the zither. A **trough zither** of wood, also with pokerwork decoration and a split repaired with wire, carries seven strings. Two **rattles** survive: one a seed mounted on a stick handle, the other a gourd filled with gravel and stopped with a stick that doubles as the handle, with further seeds preserved in an envelope attached to the object. The pairing of a plucked lamellophone and a strung trough zither with shaken idiophones suggests accompaniment for voice, but the sources record nothing about song genres, performance occasions or who played these instruments, and Rwanda has no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources document one ritual object and no festival calendar. A **divining board** of wood survives as a semicircular section, slightly hollow on its flat side and painted white, accompanied by six wedge-shaped counters, also painted white — the counters cast or read against the board. Nothing in the sources explains the procedure, the diviner's role, or the questions put to it. The composite baby's neckband of plaited skin is the collection's only object attached to a life-cycle stage. There are no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage elements listed for Rwanda, and the sources used describe no annual festivals, birth or coming-of-age rites, weddings or funerary practice.

## Foodways

Food appears in the sources only through vessels and implements. Three small pottery vessels with cord decoration are bound together with twisted fibre rope as a single load, for carrying. A solid pottery container shaped like a cotton reel has a shallow cavity in its top. A small oval dish is plaited from grass in black and brown natural colours. Most specific is a bamboo holder containing four drinking straws — a set kept together, for drinking from a shared vessel. The sources used name no staple grains, dishes, cooking methods or ceremonial foods.

## Oral tradition & literature

One question of oral and scholarly transmission they do record is the etymology of the name itself: Ernest Viaene argued in 1910 that *Hutu* derived from a word meaning slave, which René Bourgeois contradicted, holding that it originally meant "lords" in relation to the Twa. Bourgeois pointed to the Mongo people of the DRC, among whom Bantu rulers of local Twa were called *Bahoto* or *Bawoto* — terms cognate with *Bahutu* — and meaning lords, not serfs, because they governed over the Pygmies.

## Language & religion

The Hutu speak **Rwanda-Rundi**, a Bantu language of the Niger–Congo family, subdivided into the *Kinyarwanda* and *Kirundi* dialects, standardised as the official languages of Rwanda and Burundi respectively; Tutsi and Twa speak it as a mother tongue too. A small and shrinking number of Hutu use French, the other official language of both countries, as a lingua franca, its decline tied to poor relations between Rwanda and France. On religion the sources are near-silent: they note only that Belgian colonial authorities used church records, alongside cattle ownership and physical measurements, to designate people as Hutu or Tutsi, and that the Rwandan government no longer uses the distinction.
