## Overview

The Baoulé are one of the largest ethnic groups in Côte d'Ivoire. They traditionally farm in the country's centre, especially in the Baoulé “V” between the Bandama and N'Zi rivers. Their culture includes woven cloth, carved wood, masks, figures, goldwork, music, dance, naming practices, and spiritual beliefs. Baoulé is spoken at home, while children use French at school.

## Material culture

Baoulé material culture combines hand-woven cotton textiles, carved wood, metal ornaments, masks, figures, tools, and musical objects.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Baoulé textiles include hand-woven cloth, sewn wrappers, ikat, and carved loom parts.

- **Kente cloth** (*pagne*) — Traditional pagne is also known as Kente cloth.
- **Hand-woven textiles** — The Baoulé are well known for unique hand-woven textiles, and Sakiaré is described as a village where 95% of people are skilled hand-weavers.
- **Woman's wrapper or shawl** — Wrappers or shawls are made from twelve or eighteen narrow strips sewn together, using cotton, indigo, ikat, float patterns, twined threads, tassels, and fringes.
- **Ikat cloth** (*ikat*) — Museum records describe white cotton ikat and white-and-orange ikat on indigo.
- **Heddle-pulley** — Carved wooden heddle-pulleys may show opposing faces, bearded heads, antelope heads, top-knots, or a miniature face mask.

### Clothing & dress

Baoulé dress includes decorated cotton wrappers, a charm-covered hunter's cap, and masquerade clothing.

- **Woman's wrapper or shawl** — Women's wrappers or shawls are made from sewn cotton strips decorated with ikat, float patterns, twined threads, tassels, and fringes.
- **Hunter's cap** — A hunter's cap of coarse dark brown cotton has flaps, chain-stitch decoration, leather thongs, appliqué, leopard-claw charms, and sealed leather charm pouches.
- **Masquerade costume** — A masquerade costume includes a polychrome wooden mask, a raffia skirt, wrist and ankle bands, and ankle rattles.

### Architecture

The sources give almost no information about Baoulé architecture.

- **Carved wooden door** — A carved wooden door has two panels, with a lizard above and a tortoise below.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Baoulé objects include carved wood, gold and bronze work, tools, and household materials.

- **Carved stool** — A stool carved from one block of wood has a curved seat, a rectangular base, four circles, and a central pillar.
- **Lost-wax pendant** (*srala/boloa atre*) — A gold pendant made by lost-wax casting has two faces, triangles, angled lines, and openwork circles.
- **Bronze anklets** — Museum records include bronze women's anklets.
- **Machete** — A machete is used to clear vegetation and to make paddles or canoes from logs.
- **Snail shell** — A snail shell is used to grind and pound tobacco and to make snuffs.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Baoulé adornment includes gold, bronze, bead, bone, and carved-hair details.

- **Gold bead** — Gold beads include one shaped like a musical horn or leopard tooth and another shaped like a clove.
- **Gold-plated hair-pin** — Museum records describe a gold-plated hair-pin.
- **Gold-plated spectacles** — Museum records describe gold-plated spectacles.
- **Lost-wax gold pendant** (*srala/boloa atre*) — The srala/boloa atre is a bi-facial gold pendant with geometric decoration and openwork circles.
- **Bead and bone ornaments** — Figures wear bead necklaces, white bead anklets, bone bead necklaces, and small bone-bead anklets.

## Music & performance

Baoulé music objects include drums, bells, and wooden instruments, while masks support named performances.

- **Antelope-head bell** — An iron bell has a separate striker linked by string and a wooden end carved as an antelope's head.
- **Woman-head instrument** — A wooden musical instrument is shaped like a woman's head with an elongated neck.
- **Toumodi drum** — A drum from Toumodi is associated with the Baoulé and measures 21 centimetres across and 42 centimetres high.
- **Entertainment mask performance** (*gbagba/mblo*) — The gbagba or mblo mask is used in entertainment performances made up of a dance sequence.
- **Protective mask performance** (*bo nun amuin*) — The bo nun amuin mask is danced during trouble to protect the village and at important men's funerals.

## Dance & theatre

Baoulé mask dances range from playful performances to sacred dances restricted by gender and age.

- **Goli performance** (*goli glin*) — The goli glin mask resembles an animal with horns, a boxy muzzle, and coloured bars around the eyes.
- **Playful mask dance** (*kple kple/kouassi gbe*) — The kple kple, or kouassi gbe, is a mundane mask often worn by children for playful dancing.
- **Sacred men's mask dance** (*bo nun amuin*) — The boxy bo nun amuin mask represents a dangerous animal, and women and children must not see it.
- **Entertainment dance** (*gbagba/mblo*) — The gbagba or mblo mask is worn with cloth drapery covering the dancer.

## Festivals & rituals

Ritual life includes protective mask dances, spirit-spouse offerings, and names linked to birth circumstances.

- **Protective funeral dance** (*bo nun amuin*) — The bo nun amuin dance protects the village during trouble and at important men's funerals, helping the dead become protective spirits.
- **Spirit-wife figure** (*blolo bla*) — A blolo bla is a private sculpture representing a person's female spirit spouse.
- **Spirit-husband figure** (*blolo bian/blolo yasua*) — A blolo bian, also called blolo yasua, represents a person's male spirit spouse.
- **Offerings to spirit spouses** (*blolo*) — People offer food and money to spirit-spouse figures because these spirits can affect relationships and personal efforts.
- **Birth-order names** (*I'nsan/N'Goran/Brou/Loukou/N'Gbin*) — Names such as I'nsan, N'Goran, Brou, Loukou, and N'Gbin mark birth order and family circumstances.

## Foodways

Baoulé foodways centre on yam, cassava, maize, market fish, and shared meat.

- **Mashed yam** — Yam is boiled, cooled, and mashed, and it is the staple of the Ivorian diet.
- **Cassava** — Cassava is an important part of Baoulé cuisine.
- **Stored yams and maize** — Yams and maize are stored until they are needed.
- **Market fish** — Fish is especially important in local markets and is wrapped in palm leaves.
- **Shared meats** — Goat, sheep, lamb, and chicken are eaten by the whole community; goats provide milk and chickens provide eggs.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources give little information about Baoulé oral literature but record religious transmission and Bible translations.

- **Baoulé Bible** — Parts of the Bible appeared in Baoulé in 1946, the full New Testament in 1953, and the complete Bible in 1998.
- **Religious transmission through sculpture** — Religious ideas are passed on through sculpture, dance, naming, and interpretations of spiritual beings.

## Language & religion

Baoulé is a tonal Akan language whose religious system connects God, earthly life, spirits, and ancestors.

- **Baoulé language** (*Wawle*) — Wawle is an Akan language of the Kwa family, related to Anyin, Nzema, and Sehwi.
- **God's domain** (*Niamien*) — Niamien is the Domain of God in Baoulé religious belief.
- **Realm of ancestral spirits** (*blolo*) — Blolo is the beyond where ancestral spirits reside.
- **Protective spirits** (*zulu*) — Sculptures can house zulu, protective spirits.
- **Wooden beings** (*Waka sran*) — Waka sran, meaning “wooden beings,” connect zulu with the physical and spirit worlds.

- *Loukou* — Name for the eleventh child from one mother
- *N'Gbin* — Name for the twelfth child
