## Overview

The Mano — also called *Mano-Dan* and *Yacouba* — are a people of Liberia and Guinea who speak Mano (also recorded as *Mah*, *Mah-Yacouba* and *Mawe*), a language of the Mande family. In Liberia they hold the northeastern forest country of Nimba County, including Ganta, Yekepa, Sanniquellie and Scalepea; across the border they live in the Nzérékoré, Lola and Yomou Prefectures of Guinea. The county's name is their own: *Niemba* / *Nyamba Tun*, Mount Nimba, glossed as "hills on which young maidens will slip and fall", where the Mano formerly worshipped the creator god *Wala* (also *Ya'ala*, *Nyamba*). Together with the Dan — called their "small brothers" — they are the two major peoples of Nimba County. The sources used give no population figure. They matter here as carvers, musicians and farmers; the miniature masks and figures in this gallery come out of that reputation.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The catalogue records of the objects shown carry a small but concrete set of fibre techniques, none of them recorded under a Mano-language name.

**Plaited grass mat** — a mat worked in double-strand twill across the field, with the edge finished in three-strand twill, so the border is structurally distinct from the body rather than merely hemmed.

**Strip-textile apron** — the carved wooden male and female figures both wear aprons made of series of patterned coloured textile strips, the cloth assembled from narrow bands rather than cut from one piece.

**Raphia and indigo dress of objects** — a pottery vessel in the collection is a composite piece finished with a fringe of raphia and indigo cotton, and one mask carries fur and raphia around the neck with woven cloth attached: fibre is used to clothe objects, not only people.

The sources name no motif vocabulary, so none is listed.

### Clothing & dress

The sources used do not describe Mano everyday or ceremonial dress directly; what they document is dress as it appears on the collected objects. The carved female figure wears an apron of patterned coloured textile strips together with a beaded necklace and a bracelet on the left wrist, and the carved male figure wears an apron of the same strip construction — the one indication in these records of how the body was covered at the waist. Masquerade dress is better attested: one composite mask is mounted with a headdress of yellowish fur and feathers, with fur and raphia round the neck, hair and woven cloth, that is, a full head-and-shoulder costume rather than a face piece alone. Beyond this the sources are silent on head coverings, belts and footwear, and on how ceremonial dress differed from daily wear.

### Architecture

The only built environment they name is institutional and recent: the Ganta United Mission, founded by the American missionary Dr. George Way Harley after he settled at Ganta, which grew into high schools, a hospital and colleges, and whose mission station now houses the Winifred J. Harley School of Nursing, named for his wife. Harley spent over thirty-five years at Ganta, died on 7 November 1966, and his ashes were flown back to be buried near the station. The towns the Mano occupy — Ganta, Yekepa, Sanniquellie, Scalepea — are described in the sources as trading and commercial centres, busy in commerce alongside their Dan neighbours, but not architecturally.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The collection shows a pottery vessel turned into a composite object by the addition of a raphia and indigo cotton fringe, and an iron spearhead that is deliberately left unsharpened. Metalwork otherwise appears as brass: a penannular bracelet built from three strands of two-ply wire wound round a core and closed with knob terminals. Woodwork is the broadest category — a *mancala* board with two rows of six holes plus one hole at each end for the captured pieces; a comb-shaped object carved with a face on it; carved wooden Janus heads, each mounted on a small stand; and standing male and female figures. All were registered in 1954.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Two bracelets in the collection stand for Mano adornment in these records. One is brass, penannular, its hoop made of three strands of two-ply wire coiled around a core and finished with knobs at the open ends. The other is a composite, greenish in colour, covered in string and set with a single cowrie shell at one side. Beadwork is attested indirectly, through the carved female figure, who wears a beaded necklace and a beaded bracelet on her left wrist. On masks, adornment carries over into metal and bone: one composite mask has white metal studs set across the forehead, and a miniature mask with a forward-sloping forehead and closed eyes has two teeth of bone. The sources do not document tattooing, scarification on living bodies, or hair practices, although several miniature masks are carved with incisions on the forehead and cheeks and with bands across the forehead.

## Music & performance

The sources state that the Mano are skilled musicians and gifted in arts and crafts, but they name no instrument, no song genre and no performance occasion, so nothing more specific can be written here. What the collection contributes is the mask corpus that such performance would have used: a long series of miniature masks, most of them carved with bands across the forehead, one with incisions on both forehead and cheeks, two with a forward-sloping forehead, one of those with closed eyes and two bone teeth — and, at full scale, a single composite mask with metal studs on the forehead and a fur, feather, raphia and cloth headdress. Masks were being bought out of Mano hands in quantity by the middle of the twentieth century: Dr. George Way Harley purchased many from local people and established a museum in Cleveland, in the United States, with them, and the objects in this gallery were registered in 1954.

## Festivals & rituals

Two institutions are named: the *poro* for men and the *sande* for women, described as the Mano's two traditional schools. Older religious practice centred on *Wala* — also given as *Ya'ala* and *Nyamba* — the creator god, worshipped up the mountain now called Mount Nimba, which is why the Mano name for it, *Niemba* / *Nyamba Tun*, carries the god's name; the gloss recorded for the name is "hills on which young maidens will slip and fall". As Christianity spread through northern Liberia during the nineteenth century, many Mano abandoned these practices for Methodist and Catholic congregations. The sources describe no annual festival calendar and no birth, initiation, marriage or funeral rite in detail.

## Foodways

The sources used say only that the Mano are farmers, and name no staple crop, dish, cooking method or ceremonial food.

## Oral tradition & literature

There is no epic, folktale cycle or proverb collection in the sources used. What they do preserve is a transmitted origin narrative, credited to named Mano authorities rather than to an anonymous tradition. John Gbatu (1919–2010), a prominent Mano tribal leader, is the source for the etymology of Nimba. His grandson Stanley Delano Quaye (b. 1985), a Liberian historical economist and banker, recounts a migration from Sudan to a settlement in the Mali Empire, then to Guinea, where the Mano formed a kingdom in the Youmou area, and onward into present-day Liberia in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, with a larger movement at the turn of the sixteenth and seventeenth. In the Mali Empire the Mano and Dan are remembered as the Soumano / Danhou Maghan clan of the Gbara, helping defend the empire against invaders from North Africa. Quaye has also written on the political and economic governance of the Mano and Gio (Dan).

## Language & religion

Mano is a Mande language of the Niger–Congo-speaking region, spoken mainly in Nimba County in north-central Liberia and in the Nzérékoré, Lola and Yomou Prefectures of Guinea; it is also recorded as Mah, Mah-Yacouba and Mawe. It is heavily tonal, with nine register and contour tones. It is written in a Latin orthography with tone marks and special vowel characters — a published Gospel of John opens *"Kɛɛ pɛ séĩ é tĩã kɛɛ̀ ɓea, Wééa e ke ɓe"* — and God is rendered with the indigenous name *Wálà*. Mano and Dan are close in both language and culture. Religiously, the older worship of *Wala* on Mount Nimba gave way in many communities to Methodist and Catholic Christianity from the nineteenth century, while the *poro* and *sande* schools are named as the traditional institutions. Cross-border kinship remained live into the Liberian Civil War, when Guinean Mano — including high-ranking officers in the Guinean army during the siege of Ganta — sheltered and aided their Liberian relatives.
