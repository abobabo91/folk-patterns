## Overview

The Merina are the largest ethnic group of Madagascar, living on the central highlands with mixed Austronesian, Bantu, Arab, and Indian ancestry. They speak Merina, the national language of Madagascar, and their culture centers on rice farming, silk weaving, and reverence for ancestors. Their social order historically tied caste to tomb ownership, and cattle remain central to their rituals, diet, and economy.

## Material culture

Merina material culture emphasizes cloth, particularly silk lamba shawls, alongside practical tools for rice farming and cattle-herding, protective amulets, and family tombs.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Silk lamba shawls dominate Merina weaving, made in warp-striped bands with elaborate supplementary weft patterning.

- **Lamba shawl** (*lamba*) — The rectangular Merina shawl worn by men and women, typically silk with warp stripes in purple, blue, red, green, yellow, and black, sewn from two or three broad strips with fringed ends.
- **Figured silk shawl** (*lamba akotofahana*) — A brocaded class of silk lamba with supplementary weft float patterns in up to ten colours layered over the warp striping, sometimes used as burial cloth.
- **Supplementary single-heddle patterning** — A weaving technique using supplementary single-heddles to create geometric and rosette designs within pattern stripes on fine lamba.
- **Aloe-fibre and raffia cloth** — Coarser striped cloth used for noble's garments, woven from aloe fibre or raffia in brown, blue, tan, and beige.
- **Burial cloth** — Silk lamba used as shrouds, sewn from multiple lengths with multicoloured floral designs on striped grounds.

### Clothing & dress

The lamba shawl is everyday wear for all ages and social classes, worn with head-gear by men and over dresses by women, with elite dress distinguished by finer materials rather than cut.

- **Lamba as daily wear** (*lamba*) — Men wear the lamba over trousers or tunics with head-gear; women wear it over dresses; it appears across all age groups and settings in photographs and is ordinary daily wear, not a costume.
- **Plaited fibre cap with felt tail** — An elaborate head ornament of plaited vegetable fibre covered in red felt with a tapering tail, embroidered in silk and gold thread and appliquéd with felt floral patterns.
- **Noble's aloe-fibre garment** — A striped brown and blue aloe-fibre shirt and skirt sewn lengthwise with a pink cotton fringe, marking elite status through material rather than cut.
- **Raffia costume** — Two lengths of brown raffia striped in blue, light blue, tan, and beige, attributed to a Queen of Madagascar.

### Architecture

Merina settlement centers on engineered rice irrigation, family tombs in ancestral villages, and urban markets in the capital Antananarivo.

- **Irrigation works** — Vast systems built by early settlers to drain highland marshes, water arable land, and allow two rice harvests a year, underpinning the Imerina kingdom's wealth.
- **Family tomb** — Standing structures in the ancestral village of origin that hold the lineage dead and are central to Merina social life and identity.
- **Market structure** — Open-air gathering where sellers sit on the ground around flat baskets or under large white umbrellas.
- **Raised thatched shelter** — A structure reached by steps with a low brick wall and rectangular thatched shelters beside it, visible in historical photographs.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Merina everyday objects focus on iron farm tools, plaited-fibre baskets, and worked horn and brass for weapons and ritual use.

- **Iron spade and sickle** — Iron-and-wood agricultural implements reflecting rice, cassava, and potato cultivation.
- **Gunpowder-flask** — A black cow's horn vessel with decorated brass mounts and an iron carrying chain, used for carrying gunpowder.
- **Spear and shield** — A spear with leaf-shaped iron head and a circular wooden shield covered in brown and white ox hide with leather thong stitching.
- **Model loom** — A wooden loom with uprights and cross-pieces still holding an unfinished brown-and-blue striped textile, showing the weaving apparatus.
- **Carved bed panel** — Low-relief nineteenth-century woodwork depicting the Merina army, birds, and a formal aristocratic event.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Merina ornament is primarily silver, gilt metal, and beaded cloth, with many pieces serving protective or ritual functions.

- **Waist-ornament amulet** — A rectangular gilt-metal box-amulet studded with coloured glass and hung with five gilded crocodile-tooth ornaments, suspended from a silk brocade waistband.
- **Zebu amulet** — A silver charm modelled as a humped bull with shortened legs and long tapering horns, pierced for suspension and linking personal adornment to cattle.
- **Head-ornament chain** — A silver chain of ring and cable links from which shorter vertical chains hang, each ending in a silver coin.
- **Hat-ornament** — A silver ornament made in the form of animal claws.
- **Beaded bangles** — Cotton cloth covered with multicoloured glass beads.

## Festivals & rituals

Merina ritual life centers on ancestors and cattle, with family tombs serving as the foundation of social identity and caste distinction.

- **Family tomb burial** (*tanin'drazana*) — The dead are laid in family tombs in the ancestral village, and many hold that ancestors intervene in earthly events for good or ill.
- **Tomb-based caste distinction** (*fotsy–mainty*) — The white–black distinction denoted whether a family had an established tomb (fotsy) or not (mainty), determining social status and eligibility for marriage.
- **Circumcision rite** — Boys are circumcised within their first seven years, with relatives asking the ancestors for blessing and protection.
- **Cattle sacrifice** — The Merina ritually kill cattle with unusual violence, then cook and ceremonially consume the beef.
- **Caste-governed marriage** (*Andriana*) — Marriage was restricted by caste, with the Andriana nobility subdivided into six endogamous substrata, each holding an inherited occupation, and cross-caste unions historically taboo.

## Foodways

Rice is the foundation of the Merina diet and moral order, made possible by irrigation works that allow two harvests yearly, with beef from ritually killed cattle as the complementary staple.

- **Rice** (*masina*) — The holy staple so central to Merina life that the term for eating a meal is simply to eat rice, believed necessary for moral behaviour.
- **Double-harvest rice system** — Made possible by irrigation works draining marshes and watering arable land on the plateau, allowing two rice crops yearly.
- **Beef from ritual cattle** — Cattle are ritually killed with unusual violence and the meat is cooked and eaten ceremoniously, central to Merina diet and economy.
- **Root crops** — Cassava and potatoes are staple crops grown alongside rice, with onions and other vegetables grown as supplements.
- **Market food economy** — Sellers sit on the ground among flat baskets or under large white umbrellas, with buyers in lamba and head-gear moving between stalls.

## Oral tradition & literature

Merina oral history traces their kingdom to the sixteenth century and preserves stories of settlement, cattle discovery, and the supernatural Vazimba.

- **Oral history of Imerina kingdom** — Oral tradition credits early sixteenth-century king Andriamanelo with founding a united kingdom after fifteenth-century settlement wars.
- **Vazimba** — Held to be the island's original Southeast Asian population, sometimes viewed as supernatural beings possessing magical power rather than human.
- **Discovery of cattle as food** — Merina oral history credits a servant of King Ralambo with discovering that cattle were edible, passing the knowledge to the king.
- **Vy Vato Sakelika society** (*Vy Vato Sakelika*) — A secret society founded in Antananarivo in 1913 under pastor Ravelojoana dedicated to affirming Malagasy cultural identity, repressed in 1915–1916.

## Language & religion

Merina speak the Austronesian Malagasy language in its Highland form, and follow Protestantism with deep roots in ancestor veneration.

- **Merina dialect of Malagasy** (*Highland Malagasy*) — The Austronesian language of the Merina became Madagascar's official language and basis for other dialects, written in Latin script and medium of instruction through grade five.
- **Protestant Christianity** — King Radama I welcomed Christian missionaries in the 1810s and the Merina nobility were among the first converts; the upper classes became wholly Protestant by the mid-nineteenth century.
- **Ancestor veneration** (*mahery*) — Runs alongside Christianity, with tombs, belief in ancestral intervention in worldly affairs, and the magical power attributed to the Vazimba.
- **Catholic conversion** — French colonial influence led former Merina slaves to convert to Catholicism, creating religious divisions that track caste and ethnicity.

## Glossary

- *lamba* — A rectangular Merina shawl worn by men and women, typically of silk with warp stripes
- *lamba akotofahana* — A brocaded silk shawl with supplementary weft patterns in multiple colours, sometimes used as burial cloth
- *tanin'drazana* — The land of the ancestors; homeland of the Merina
- *fotsy* — White; historically denoting a family with an established tomb and higher social status
- *mainty* — Black; historically denoting a family without or with a recently established tomb and lower social status
- *Andriana* — The Merina nobility, subdivided into six endogamous substrata each with inherited occupations
- *Vazimba* — The original population of Madagascar from Southeast Asian seafarers, sometimes viewed as supernatural beings with magical power
- *mahery* — Magical power, especially attributed to the Vazimba
- *masina* — Holy; used to describe rice, the sacred staple of Merina diet
- *Imerina* — The Merina kingdom and region in the central highlands of Madagascar
- *Highland Malagasy* — The Merina dialect of Malagasy, the national language of Madagascar
- *Vy Vato Sakelika* — Iron and Stone Network; a secret society founded in 1913 to affirm Malagasy cultural identity
