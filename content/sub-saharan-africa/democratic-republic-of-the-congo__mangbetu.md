## Overview

The Mangbetu live in northeastern Congo. Their culture is known for developed art, music, metalwork, pottery, textiles, body adornment, and royal imagery. Their language is Mangbetu, also called Nemangbetu. The sources do not give a population figure or describe their religion.

## Material culture

Mangbetu material culture includes decorated textiles, pottery with human heads, iron and copper objects, carved wood, musical instruments, and elaborate hairstyles and adornment.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Mangbetu textiles used plant fibers, dye, and strong geometric designs.

- **Special-occasion garment** (*Egbe*) — An Egbe was a plant-fiber garment made by aristocratic women and decorated with bold geometric motifs.
- **Back skirt or sitting pad** (*negbe*) — A negbe was made from compressed leaf or bark and vegetal fibers in woven, zigzag, crisscrossing, or chequered patterns.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe special-occasion women’s garments, buttock covers, fiber skirts, and a feathered dance-cap.

- **Special-occasion garment** (*Egbe*) — An Egbe was worn over a skirt and tied to a girdle with a frontal apron.
- **Buttock cover** (*negbe*) — A negbe was a vegetal-fiber back skirt or sitting pad.
- **Feathered dance-cap** — A dance-cap was made from an elephant’s ear and feathers.
- **Fiber apron** — A carved male figure is shown wearing a vegetal-fiber apron.
- **Fiber skirt** — Another male figure is shown wearing a fiber skirt around the waist.

### Architecture

Western photographs and films documented Mangbetu architecture and royal courts, but the sources give no building details.

- **Royal courts** — Western photographs and films documented Mangbetu royal courts.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Mangbetu makers produced weapons, tools, pottery, musical objects, and carved wooden goods.

- **Iron weapons and tools** — Iron objects included knives, axes, arm-daggers, swords, arrow-heads, spear-heads, spears, and a chisel-shaped currency blade.
- **Elephant-ivory horn** — A musical horn was made from elephant ivory and was described as used in war.
- **Head-shaped pottery vessels** — Pottery vessels could have human heads, tall headdresses, hats or coiffures, earrings, loop handles, spouts, and string-like ornament.
- **Wooden household objects** — Wooden objects included stools, seats, boxes, pipes, combs, and fly-whisks.
- **Carved figures** — Mangbetu wooden objects included carved figures.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Mangbetu adornment included shell and seed necklaces, hair-pins, earrings, elaborate hairstyles, and the former Lipombo practice.

- **Shell-and-seed necklace** — Necklaces used pale bluish shell discs and brown seeds strung on vegetable-fiber cord.
- **Hair-pins** — Hair-pins were made from ivory, wood, or both.
- **Brass-wire earring** — A pottery figure shows a human head wearing a brass-wire earring.
- **Elaborate hairstyles** — Women’s hairstyles were elaborate, and figures often showed the traditional elongated-head appearance.
- **Head elongation** (*Lipombo*) — Babies’ heads were formerly wrapped tightly with cloth in the practice called Lipombo.

## Music & performance

Mangbetu music is highly developed and is especially associated with the Mangbetu harp or guitar.

- **Mangbetu harp or guitar** — The Mangbetu harp or guitar has a bow-shaped form, a wooden neck, a skin-covered resonator, sound holes, pegs, strings, and sometimes a human head or figure at the neck.
- **Named harp record** (*kundi*) — A Museum of World Culture catalogue includes a musical record identified as kundi.
- **Ivory musical horn** — A musical horn made from elephant ivory is recorded as a Mangbetu instrument.
- **Seed or metal rattle** — A rattle had a plaited vine-leaf cage containing dried seeds or metal clappers.
- **Conical ivory whistles** — Museum records include conical ivory whistles.

## Dance & theatre

Mangbetu dances were documented by Western photographers and filmmakers, but no named dance or theatre form is given.

- **Black-and-white dance mask** — A wooden dance mask painted black and white is held in a museum collection.
- **Feathered dance-cap** — A dance-cap made from an elephant’s ear and feathers is recorded.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources give no festival calendar but record Lipombo and political events linked to royal rule.

- **Head-wrapping practice** (*Lipombo*) — Babies’ heads were traditionally wrapped tightly with cloth in Lipombo, which declined in the 1950s with westernization.
- **Royal courts and dances** — The sources mention royal courts and dances but do not describe their rituals.
- **Political expansion through wives** — The sources mention local wives used in King Nabiembali’s political expansion.
- **Independent kingdoms** — Independent kingdoms were established after King Nabiembali’s death in 1859.

## Foodways

The sources do not describe Mangbetu foods or cooking, but they record a word for fish.

- **Fish** (*ná-mutali*) — The language source gives ná-mutali as a word meaning fish.

## Oral tradition & literature

Mangbetu and Meegye oral literature includes tale texts and works about storytelling and peace-building.

- **Narrative connector** (*bhe*) — The connective bhe marks discourse development in Meegye narrative texts.
- **Leopard and Azapane tales** — Robert Guy McKee’s work includes Mangbetu Tales of Leopard and Azapane: Trickster as Resistance Hero.
- **Death-compensation studies** — The sources include work on Meegye-Mangbetu death compensations as intergroup rites of passage.

## Language & religion

Mangbetu is a Central Sudanic language with several named dialects and links to regional trade languages.

- **Mangbetu language** (*Nemangbetu*) — Mangbetu, also called Nemangbetu, is spoken in northeastern Congo.
- **Alternative people names** (*Amangbetu*) — The speakers and language are also identified as Amangbetu.
- **Alternative people names** (*Kingbetu*) — The speakers and language are also identified as Kingbetu.
- **Alternative people names** (*Mambetto*) — The speakers and language are also identified as Mambetto.
- **Most widely understood dialect** (*Medje*) — Medje is the most widely understood Mangbetu dialect.
