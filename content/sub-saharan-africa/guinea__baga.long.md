## Overview

The Baga are a West African ethnic group living in the swampy mangrove lands along Guinea’s Atlantic coastline. Their communities extend along the coast north and south of the mouths of the Nunez and Pongo rivers, around the Îles de Los and the Conakry area, and toward the Konkouré River. The sources give no population total. The Baga include Mandori, Sitemu, Pukur, Bulunits, Kakissa or Sobané, Koba, and Kalum communities, and are related to the Landuma, Nalu, and Temne. Their languages belong to the Atlantic branch of the Niger-Congo family. Baga material culture is especially represented by wood and metal carving, agricultural symbolism, ritual masks, figures, drums, combs, and other objects held in international museums.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name chequerboard, leaf, diamond, chevron, geometric, and linear designs in descriptions of Baga objects.

### Clothing & dress

The sources provide little information about everyday Baga clothing. They state that men are typically fishermen and that most families live in rural villages. Ceremonial masking involves a male dancer wearing a wooden Nimba or D’mba mask on his shoulders. The mask is secured with a rope tied onto the torso, while the dancer is concealed in European cloth and a cover called raffia. Museum records describe a large Nimba mask with a raffia leaf fringe and another with raffia tassels attached to the ears and chin. A British Museum photographic print records a carved wooden figure wearing a loin-cloth, but the catalogue does not identify this as a general Baga garment.

### Architecture

Most Baga families live in clusters of cylindrical mud structures. These houses are roofed with thatch made from rice straw, and groups of structures may form small villages. The sources state that guinea’s nationalization of land and property through socialist legislation following independence ended the effective power of Baga elders, whose authority had traditionally operated through village councils.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Baga artistic production includes wood and metalwork, while agriculture, fishing, and the tending of palm and kola trees structure everyday life. The British Museum records a globular earthenware divination vessel encased in hide and encircled by tree bark, with two double-pronged wooden objects attached to its mouth. Other catalogued objects include wooden combs, drums, carved figures, a wooden bird figure with metal nails, and a helmet mask. One comb has a woman’s bust with crested hair and elaborate facial markings; another has a chequerboard and leaf-patterned handle.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources document facial markings, scarification, hair styling, and ornamented carved representations. Nimba or D’mba masks can include painted wooden ornaments attached to the ears or pendants attached to the nasal septum. Museum records describe figures wearing armlets, bracelets, necklaces, and belts, and a seated figure with a glass-beaded bracelet and glass-beaded skirt. Baga and zoomorphic museum masks are described with scar-like or tattoo-like markings. The Nimba’s elaborate coiffure may be defined by incised designs, including a raised median crest and chevron marks.

## Music & performance

The sources do, however, describe performance contexts for ritual objects. The D’mba or Nimba is danced by a male masquerader during harvest, planting, baby showers, weddings, and ritual ceremonies. Earlier uses included dancing the mask at least twice a year before the arrival of the rainy seasons. The performance was associated with fertility, pregnancy, agricultural continuity, and support for women. Paint and noise embellishments could be added before ceremonies.

The Baga also traditionally made the Bansonyi mask, consisting of a painted pole that could be 20 feet long, decorated with color, a calico flag, and a triangular icon. It was used in male initiation ceremonies. A British Museum record describes a composite drum in two parts, made of carved wood with a skin membrane, with a kneeling woman and child supporting a ring of six masks; a separate drum takes the form of a crown. Another drum has a kneeling female figure with an elaborate headdress, a skin stretched over its top, carved wooden pegs, red and black paint, and a green-painted band of diamonds.

## Dance & theatre

Baga performance centers on masquerade rather than a named theatrical genre. The Nimba, also called D’mba, is a large wooden dance mask in the form of a woman’s head and torso. It is worn on the shoulders of a male dancer, who is concealed by cloth and raffia. The mask can be at least eight feet tall and can weigh eighty pounds or more. Seeing holes are carved into the torso so that the performer can see.

The D’mba is danced in agricultural, life-cycle, and ritual contexts. Its breasts represent a woman who has given birth to and nursed healthy children, while its form is associated with fertility and the strength of Baga women during pregnancy. The sources state that the mask does not represent a particular spirit or goddess, but an idea. Other museum records describe helmet masks, zoomorphic masks, a crocodile-like composite animal mask, and a wooden figure identified as Anok, a protective spirit belonging to the Simo society.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources document seasonal and life-cycle ritual contexts. The Nimba or D’mba is danced for harvest, planting, baby showers, weddings, and ritual ceremonies. Earlier performances occurred at least twice a year before the arrival of the rainy seasons. The Bansonyi was used in male initiation ceremonies.

Baga funerary practice has included ritually exposing the dead for a period in a sacred grove, burning some possessions and the house of the dead person, and then carrying out a Muslim-style burial. A Museum of World Culture catalogue describes an Anok sculpture as a protective spirit of the Simo society: it is brought out when someone has died and follows the dead person to the grave.

## Foodways

Baga women are known in the sources for their skill in rice farming in the swampy lowlands of the southern Guinea coastline. Men are typically fishermen and also tend palm and kola trees. Historically, the Baga were principally involved in rice and kola-nut cultivation and salt production, which supplied traders along the coast. Rice farming is therefore the principal documented Baga food-related activity.

The available material supports discussion of rice agriculture, fishing, palm, kola, and salt production.

## Oral tradition & literature

According to Baga oral tradition, the Baga originated in Guinea’s interior highlands and were driven westward by aggressive neighbors toward the coastal swamplands. They are considered “first-comers” along many parts of the Upper Guinea coast and consequently acquired landlord’s rights in those areas.

The sources do document cultural revival and preservation through material practice. After the death of Sekou Toure in 1984, Baga culture began to reemerge as an affirmation of tribal identity. Since the early 1980s, some communities have revived the creation of ritual pieces such as the Nimba. The sources also identify Frederick Lamp’s *Art of the Baga People* as a work through which knowledge of Baga art progressed in the western world.

## Language & religion

Baga languages form a dialect cluster of the Atlantic branch of the Niger-Congo family. Varieties named in the sources include Baga Koga or Koba, Baga Manduri, Baga Sitemu, Baga Kakissa, Baga Marara, Baga Sobane, and Baga Kaloum. Baga Manduri is spoken at Dobale; Baga Sitemu is spoken in villages on the Campaces River; Baga Marara is spoken on islands in the Rio Pongo; and Baga Kaloum was originally spoken in the Conakry area and the Îles de Los. Many Baga also speak the Mande language Susu, a regional trade language.

The Baga were traditionally animist and converted to Islam during the eighteenth century under the influence of Muslim Mandé missionaries. They are now predominantly Muslim, while some continue to practice animist rituals. The Marxist government that took power after Guinea’s independence in 1958 pursued forced “demystification,” destroying or confiscating traditional religious icons. After 1984, Baga ritual art and identity began to reemerge.
