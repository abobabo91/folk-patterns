## Overview

The Ashanti (*Asante*) are the largest Akan-speaking people of southern Ghana, concentrated in the forest belt of the Ashanti Region between the Pra and Ofin rivers, with Kumasi as their capital and the seat of the *Asantehene*. Roughly nine to eleven million people identify as Asante or speak Asante Twi, a literary dialect of Akan within the Kwa branch of Niger-Congo. From about 1701 the Asante Empire — consolidated by Osei Tutu and the priest Okomfo Anokye — dominated the gold and kola trade of the Volta basin until the Anglo-Asante wars ended with the Ashanti Crown Colony (1901–1957). That courtly, mercantile past is why Asante folk culture is unusually dense in regalia: lost-wax gold casting, brass weights for measuring gold dust, narrow-strip silk weaving, stamped mourning cloth, and a whole grammar of proverb-bearing objects.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Kente** (*nwentoma*) — narrow-strip cloth woven by men on a horizontal double-heddle treadle loom in strips about four inches wide, then sewn selvedge to selvedge into a large rectangle. Warp stripe sequences (*ahwepan* when left plain) carry named identities; supplementary weft-float figures are called *adwini*. Bonwire, north-east of Kumasi, is the canonical weaving town. Asante kente is distinguished from Ewe *kete* by its heraldic, court-licensed patterns and its historical use of unravelled imported silk rather than locally spun cotton pictorial blocks.

**Adwinasa** — the most prestigious weft-float kente, its name glossed "my ideas are exhausted" or "all the motifs are used up": the entire ground is filled with *adwini* so that no plain warp shows. Reserved historically for royals and their close attendants.

**Asasia** — a rare twill-weave silk kente produced only for the *Asantehene*, woven on a loom with a third heddle; the diagonal structure is not permitted to commoners.

**Adinkra** — cotton cloth block-printed with stamps cut from calabash rind, using *adinkra aduru*, a viscous black dye boiled down from *badie* bark (*Bridelia ferruginea*) with iron slag. The cloth is first divided into a grid with a toothed comb, then each cell filled with a repeated symbol. Ntonso, on the Offin road, remains the printing centre. The name is read as "farewell," and the oldest use is funerary: *kuntunkuni* (deep brown-black) and *brisi* for mourning, *kɔbene* (russet) for close kin, and white *adinkra fitaa* for thanksgiving and festival.

**Nsaa** — thick wool blankets with lozenge and chevron bands, woven by Fulani and other Sahelian weavers and imported northward into Asante, where they were prestige gifts and litter coverings. The adinkra symbol *nsaa* encodes the proverb that whoever cannot recognise real *nsaa* buys imitations.

**Batakari kese** — the "great war smock," a heavy northern-style strip-woven tunic hung with leather-cased amulets (*suman*), worn by the *Asantehene* in war and at certain state rites; the Asante adopted the form from the savanna and loaded it with their own protective medicine.

**Asafo frankaa** — appliquéd company flags. These belong properly to the coastal Fante, the Asante's southern Akan kin, whose *asafo* militia companies commissioned figurative cotton banners with a Union Jack or Ghanaian canton; they are indexed here as an Akan cognate tradition rather than an Asante one.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Gye Nyame* (except for God — omnipotence); *Sankofa* (a bird turning its head back — retrieve what was left behind); *Dwennimmen* (ram's horns — strength tempered by humility); *Nkyinkyim* (zigzag — adaptability, the twists of life); *Adinkrahene* (concentric circles — leadership, the chief of symbols); *Akoma* (heart — patience and endurance); *Aya* (fern — defiance, independence); *Nsoromma* (child of the heavens, a star — reliance on God); *Funtunfunefu-Denkyemfunefu* (conjoined crocodiles sharing one stomach — unity in difference); *Mframadan* (the well-ventilated house — resilience against storms); *Duafe* (wooden comb — feminine care).

### Clothing & dress

Men wear a large uncut cloth wrapped toga-fashion, gathered over the left shoulder with the right shoulder and arm bare — the *ntoma*, in kente for state occasions and printed cotton or adinkra otherwise. Chiefs add gold armlets, knee and ankle ornaments, and *ahenema*, leather sandals with brightly wrapped straps and gilt medallions; an enstooled chief's bare foot must not touch the ground, so the sandals are as much taboo-equipment as footwear. Women wear two or three pieces — an ankle wrapper, a shorter overpiece, and a shoulder cloth — later formalised as the tailored *kaba* blouse with *slit* skirt. Queen mothers and senior royal women crop the hair close and dress it black with *dansinkran*, a soot-and-shea preparation, and bind the chest with a wrapper in mourning. Priests (*akɔmfoɔ*) are distinguished by raffia or cotton skirts, white clay (*hyire*) marks, and charm-hung bandoliers. Mourners at *ayie* funerals wear red, russet, or black; white is worn when the death is celebrated as a fulfilled old age.

### Architecture

The classic Asante building is the courtyard compound: four rectangular rooms set around an open rectangular court, one side left open-fronted as a dais. Walls are wattle-and-daub over a split-palm frame on a raised laterite plinth, capped originally by a steep pitched thatch roof of palm leaf, later by corrugated iron. The lower half of the open rooms carries bold low-relief bas-relief plaster in curvilinear arabesques — spirals, concentric circles, crocodile and cockerel figures — picked out in red ochre, white kaolin, and black. The surviving examples are the *abosomfie*, the shrine houses of deities, of which thirteen near Kumasi (Besease, Bodwease, Asawase, Patakro, Abirim and others) are inscribed as the UNESCO World Heritage site "Asante Traditional Buildings." Nineteenth-century travellers — Bowdich in 1817 — described Kumasi as a planned town of wide streets lined with such compounds, with the palace (*Manhyia*) as an aggregation of courts. The adinkra symbol *mframadan* explicitly praises this form: the cross-ventilated house that outlasts the storm.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**Akan goldweights** (*mrammuo*) — brass weights cast by lost wax, the earlier ones geometric, the later figurative: beetles, sandals, drums, guns, a bird with a snake, whole proverb tableaux. They were used with folding *nsania* scales, brass spoons, and blowpans to measure *sika futuro*, gold dust. **Kuduo** — lidded cast-brass vessels for gold dust, beads, and personal *suman*, used at soul-washing rites and buried with their owners; the grandest carry figural lids showing a chief surrounded by attendants. **Forowa** — sheet-brass containers with punched and repoussé bands, made for shea butter. **Abusua kuruwa**, the "family pot" — a blackened, coil-built terracotta vessel with a lidded, often figurally ringed neck, made by women potters and set after a funeral in the *asensie*, the grove of pots. **Asante stools** (*dwa*) are adzed from a single block of *osese* wood, a curved seat over a central column and four corner supports, the middle element a named emblem. A deceased elder's stool may be blackened with soot and egg and kept in the stool room as an ancestral shrine; above them all stands *Sika Dwa Kofi*, the Golden Stool, which holds the *sunsum* of the nation and is never sat upon. **Akuaba** — flat, disc-headed wooden figures with ringed necks, carried on the back by women seeking conception and later kept as shrine objects. **Okyeame poma** — the linguist's staff, its wooden finial gold-leafed and shaped into a proverb the *ɔkyeame* can then expound.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Asante goldsmithing works by lost-wax casting and by hammering gold sheet over carved wooden cores. The signature form is the *akrafokonmu*, the soul-washer's badge: a chased gold disc with a rosette or concentric-ring face worn at the throat by the boys who purify the king's *kra*. Cast gold rings (*mpetea*) carry miniature birds, snails, cannon, and human jaws; chiefs' toes, knees, wrists, and hat brims are hung with cast ornaments. Large moulded and drawn glass beads — the disc-shaped *bodom* above all — and imported *aggrey* stone beads are strung for priests and royals. Women wear waist beads from girlhood, and puberty and marriage rites are marked by the addition of strings. Shaved or close-cropped heads mark mourning; the *dansinkran* coiffure marks female office.

## Music & performance

Asante music is organised around drum ensembles, each with its own repertory, dance, and protocol. *Atumpan*, the paired peg-tuned talking drums, are the instrument of drum language: the drummer recites *ayan* appellations and historical praise for the chief in reproduced Twi speech tone. *Fontomfrom* — huge barrel drums played in pairs — accompanies the chief's own dance of state. *Kete*, a smaller court ensemble of drums, bell, and flute, and *mpintin* for processions, complete the palace complement. Away from court, *adowa* is the ubiquitous ensemble and dance at funerals and public gatherings, built on *petia* and *apentemma* drums, the hourglass pressure drum *donno*, the iron *dawuro* bell, and *ntorowa* gourd rattles. Aerophones are court instruments too: *ntahera* and the seven-horn *mmɛnson* ensembles of side-blown ivory and animal-horn trumpets sound the chief's appellations, and the short *abɛntia* answers them. String music centres on the *seperewa*, a six- to fourteen-string Akan harp-lute whose repertory fed directly into palm-wine guitar and Kumasi highlife; the *atɛntɛbɛn* bamboo flute was developed into a concert instrument in the twentieth century. Women's genres stand apart: *nnwonkorɔ*, sung in interlocking chorus with hand-clapping and *adenkum* gourds, and the *kwadwom* dirge chanted by royal minstrels. The Asante ethnomusicologist J. H. Kwabena Nketia documented this system in *Drumming in Akan Communities of Ghana* and *Funeral Dirges of the Akan People*.

## Dance & theatre

*Adowa* is the most widely danced Asante form: small stepping figures with the weight low, the arms and hands doing the real work — the dancer's gestures are a legible vocabulary, pointing upward to invoke God, crossing the wrists to signal captivity or dependence. *Fontomfrom* is the chief's heavy, martial dance, performed with sword or gun. *Kete* is courtly and restrained; *sikyi* is the young people's flirtation dance; *asafo* and *apirede* belong to warrior and palace contexts; *akom* is the possession dance of the *ɔkɔmfoɔ*, performed to *akom* drumming until the deity mounts the priest. Unlike their Guinea-Coast neighbours, the Asante developed no masquerade theatre; dramatic performance runs instead through *anansesɛm* storytelling, where narration is broken by *mmoguo* interlude songs that any listener may start, and through the twentieth-century Ghanaian **concert party**, a travelling comic theatre that rebuilt Ananse as a stage trickster alongside vaudeville routines and highlife bands.

## Festivals & rituals

The Asante ritual year runs on *adaduanan*, a forty-two-day cycle of six seven-day weeks. Its twin pivots are *Akwasidae*, falling on a Sunday, and *Awukudae*, on a Wednesday: on each, the chief enters the stool room, feeds the blackened ancestral stools with mashed yam and sheep's blood, and then sits in state at a *durbar* under tiered state umbrellas (*kyinie*) while the horns and *atumpan* sound his appellations. Every ninth cycle produces the grander *Akwasidae Kɛseɛ*. **Odwira**, the annual purification and new-yam festival, cleanses the stools and the state, forbids the eating of new yam until the ancestors have been served, and ends in a night of licensed mourning followed by feasting. Life-cycle rites: a child is named on the eighth day at the *abadinto* outdooring, receiving a soul-day name (*kradin*) — Kwasi, Kwadwo, Akosua, Yaa — and the father's *ntorɔ*; a girl's first menstruation is marked by *bragoro*, in which she sits in public under an umbrella, is bathed, fed *ɛtɔ*, and adorned with beads and gold. Marriage is sealed by *tiri nsa*, the "head drink" presented to the bride's *abusua*. The funeral, *ayie*, is the largest ceremony of Asante life: days of drumming, *adowa*, adinkra mourning cloth, contributions of drink, and afterwards the *abusua kuruwa* set at the *asensie*. Descent is matrilineal through eight clans — Oyoko, Bretuo, Agona, Asona, Aduana, Asenie, Asakyiri, Ekuona — so it is the mother's lineage that buries and inherits.

## Foodways

The forest staples are cassava, plantain, cocoyam, and yam. *Fufu* — cassava and plantain pounded together in a wooden mortar into a smooth elastic ball — is the daily centre of the meal, swallowed in pinches dipped in soup: *abenkwan*, palm-nut soup thickened with the boiled pulp of oil-palm fruit; *nkatenkwan*, groundnut soup; or clear *nkrakra* light soup with tomato and pepper. *Ampesi*, plain boiled yam, plantain, or cocoyam, is eaten with *kontomire* — cocoyam leaves stewed down with palm oil, egusi seeds, onion, and smoked fish. *Ɛtɔ*, mashed yam or plantain worked with palm oil and served with boiled eggs, is the ritual dish: fed to a girl at *bragoro*, to a newborn's mother, and eaten on one's *krada*, or soul-birthday. Protein comes from smoked and fermented fish (*momone*), land snails, and bushmeat, above all the cane rat (*akrantie*). Pepper, garden eggs, and grated ginger dominate the seasoning. Drink means *nsafufuo*, tapped palm wine, distilled into *akpeteshie*; imported schnapps has replaced palm wine for *mpaeɛ*, the poured libation that opens any serious gathering, addressed first to *Nyame*, then to the earth, then to the ancestors.

## Oral tradition & literature

The great Asante narrative cycle is *anansesɛm*, tales of Kwaku Ananse the spider — a small, greedy, verbally brilliant trickster who outwits the sky-god and the larger animals and is periodically humiliated by his own cleverness. The term covers the whole genre, so that even stories without Ananse in them are "spider stories." Performance is nocturnal, dialogic, and interrupted by *mmoguo* songs. Alongside it stand *ɛbɛ*, proverbs, of which R. S. Rattray published 830 in 1916 and which are the medium of the *ɔkyeame*'s art, encoded visually in goldweights, adinkra stamps, and staff finials; *apae*, the fast praise-poetry shouted for the king by the *afenasoafo*; *kwadwom*, the archaic royal dirge; and the drummed histories of the *atumpan*. Court tradition is not merely remembered but curated: the Manhyia Palace in Kumasi keeps a museum and archive, and the Asante Collective Biography Project assembled by Ivor Wilks and T. C. McCaskie turned oral stool histories into a documented political record.

## Language & religion

Asante Twi belongs to Akan, in the Kwa group of the Atlantic-Congo branch of Niger-Congo; it is one of the three literary Akan dialects alongside Akuapem and Fante, and is mutually intelligible with both. Akan is tonal, and that tonality is what makes drum speech on the *atumpan* possible. The indigenous religion recognises *Onyankopɔn Nyame*, a remote creator invoked in every libation; below him the *abosom*, deities of rivers and forests, chief among them Tano and the crater lake Bosomtwe; the *nsamanfo*, the ancestors, who are fed at the blackened stools; and *asuman*, manufactured charms. Personhood is split between the patrilineal *ntorɔ*/*sunsum*, spirit, and the *kra*, the soul that carries its day-name. Most Asante today are Christian — Methodist, Presbyterian, Catholic, and increasingly Pentecostal and charismatic — with a Muslim minority in Kumasi's *zongo* quarters; the stool rites, the *adaduanan* calendar, libation, and funeral custom continue in parallel rather than in place of church membership.
