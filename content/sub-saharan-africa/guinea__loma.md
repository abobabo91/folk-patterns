## Overview

The Loma live mainly in sparsely populated mountain areas near Guinea and Liberia. They speak Loma and are closely related to the Mende. Their documented culture includes shifting rice farming, patrilineal and exogamous social organization, traditional religion, and Poro rites of passage. Large wooden masks and a writing script are important named features.

## Material culture

The documented material culture centers on large wooden masks, ritual figures, mixed-material objects, and a medicine-covered composite hat.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources do not document Loma textile or pattern traditions.

### Clothing & dress

The sources give only one detailed example of Loma dress: a composite ritual hat.

- **Composite hat** — The hat had a woven textile cap, cowrie shells, plaster, horns, black fur, and lumps of medicine.

### Architecture

The sources do not cover Loma buildings, houses, roofs, settlements, or named building types.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Museum records describe wooden ritual objects and a mixed-material implement or weapon.

- **Hooked implement or weapon** — It had a wooden handle wrapped in skin and two iron hooks.
- **Carved pall** — A pall from Lofa County in Liberia was made of wood and had a carved seat.
- **Ritual wooden figures** — Museum records describe wooden figures used as religious or ritual equipment, including one male figure covered with blood and another recorded as its pair.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources do not document named Loma jewelry or body-adornment traditions.

- **Cowrie shells** — Cowrie shells were used around the rim of a composite hat.
- **Amulets** — Amulets are listed among the materials or motifs of particular masks and hats.

## Music & performance

Loma hymn singing is documented through hymns written in Loma and later adopted by the Lutheran Church.

- **Loma hymns** — Billema Kwillia composed hymns in Loma in the 1960s, and they first appeared in print in Loma in 1970.
- **“A va de laa”** — “A va de laa” is the most widely used hymn and was translated into singable English in 2004 and also into German.

## Dance & theatre

The sources do not name Loma dances, drama, or theatre traditions.

- **Poro mask performance** (*Poro*) — Large wooden masks are associated with Poro secret rites of passage.

## Festivals & rituals

Loma ritual life includes traditional religion, Poro rites of passage, and large masks linked with forest spirits.

- **Poro rites of passage** (*Poro*) — Poro is a secret rite-of-passage tradition that uses large wooden masks.
- **Forest-spirit masks** — The largest masks were believed by the Loma to contain forest spirits.
- **Rolling war** — The religious conflict with Mandinka people was called a historic “rolling war.”
- **Ritual figures** — Male wooden figures were used as religious or ritual equipment.

## Foodways

The Loma farm rice in shifting farms, but the sources do not name dishes or cooking traditions.

- **Shifting rice farming** — The Loma grow rice in shifting farms.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources do not document a Loma literary canon, but they mention videos about a story and last names.

- **Jealousy story** — A Yale Linguistics Department video records a story about jealousy delivered in Loma.
- **Loma last names** — Another video concerns Loma last names, without giving the naming tradition itself.

## Language & religion

Loma is a Southwestern Mande language with several dialects, a Latin alphabet, and an older script developed in the 1930s.

- **Loma language** (*Löömàgòòi; Löghömàgòòi*) — The Loma call their language Löömàgòòi, or Löghömàgòòi in Guinea.
- **Loma people** (*Löömàgìtì; Löghömagiti*) — The Loma refer to themselves as Löömàgìtì or Löghömagiti.
- **Loma syllabary** — A syllabary with at least 185 characters was developed by Wido Zobo with help from the weaver Moriba.
- **Loma dialects** (*Gizima, Wubomei, Ziema, Bunde, Buluyiema*) — Dialects of Loma proper in Liberia include Gizima, Wubomei, Ziema, Bunde, and Buluyiema.
- **Toma regional language** (*Toma*) — In Guinea, the dialects called Toma are an official regional language.
