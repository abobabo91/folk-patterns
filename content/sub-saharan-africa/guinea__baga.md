## Overview

The Baga live along Guinea’s Atlantic coastline, around the Nunez and Pongo rivers, the Îles de Los, Conakry, and the Konkouré River. They include several communities related to the Landuma, Nalu, and Temne. Their material culture includes carved wood and metal objects, masks, figures, drums, combs, and ritual pieces. Baga ritual art and identity began to reemerge after 1984.

## Material culture

Baga material culture is especially known for carved wood and metal objects, ritual masks, figures, drums, combs, and agricultural symbols.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources do not document a named Baga textile tradition.

- **Chequerboard design** — Chequerboard designs appear on some Baga objects, but the sources do not identify them as textile motifs.
- **Leaf design** — Leaf designs appear on some Baga objects, but the sources do not identify them as textile motifs.
- **Diamond design** — Diamond designs appear in descriptions of Baga objects, but not as a named textile tradition.
- **Chevron design** — Chevron designs appear in descriptions of Baga objects, but not as a named textile tradition.

### Clothing & dress

The sources give little information about everyday Baga clothing.

- **Nimba or D’mba masking outfit** (*Nimba; D’mba*) — A male dancer wears the wooden mask on his shoulders and is hidden by European cloth and raffia.
- **Loin-cloth figure** — A British Museum record shows a carved wooden figure wearing a loin-cloth, but does not identify it as general Baga dress.

### Architecture

Most Baga families live in clusters of cylindrical mud houses with rice-straw roofs.

- **Cylindrical mud house** — Baga families live in cylindrical mud structures that may form small villages.
- **Rice-straw thatch roof** — The houses are roofed with thatch made from rice straw.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Baga everyday material culture includes wood and metalwork, combs, drums, vessels, and carved figures.

- **Divination vessel** — A globular earthenware vessel is encased in hide and tree bark, with two double-pronged wooden objects attached to its mouth.
- **Carved comb** — Baga combs include carved designs, such as a woman’s bust, crested hair, facial markings, chequerboard patterns, and leaves.
- **Composite drum** — One drum is made of carved wood and a skin membrane, with a kneeling woman and child supporting six masks.
- **Crown drum** — A separate drum takes the form of a crown.
- **Wooden bird figure** — A catalogued wooden bird figure has metal nails.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Baga adornment is shown through facial markings, hair styling, armlets, bracelets, necklaces, belts, and carved ornaments.

- **Glass-beaded bracelet** — A seated figure wears a glass-beaded bracelet.
- **Glass-beaded skirt** — A seated figure wears a glass-beaded skirt.
- **Painted ear ornaments** — Nimba or D’mba masks can have painted wooden ornaments attached to the ears.
- **Nasal pendants** — Nimba or D’mba masks can have pendants attached to the nasal septum.
- **Raised median crest** — The Nimba’s coiffure may include a raised median crest with incised designs and chevrons.

## Music & performance

The sources do not name Baga musical forms, but they describe ritual masks, drums, and performance settings.

- **Bansonyi mask** (*Bansonyi*) — The Bansonyi is a painted pole that can be 20 feet long, with color, a calico flag, and a triangular icon, and it was used in male initiation ceremonies.
- **Composite mask drum** — A composite drum has carved figures, masks, wood, and a skin membrane.
- **Crown drum** — One drum is shaped like a crown.
- **Female-figure drum** — Another drum has a kneeling female figure, an elaborate headdress, a skin top, wooden pegs, red and black paint, and a green band of diamonds.

## Dance & theatre

Baga performance centers on masquerade, especially the large Nimba or D’mba mask.

- **Nimba or D’mba masquerade** (*Nimba; D’mba*) — A male dancer wears the large wooden mask on his shoulders while concealed by cloth and raffia.
- **Nimba mask** (*Nimba*) — The mask has the form of a woman’s head and torso, can be at least eight feet tall, and can weigh eighty pounds or more.
- **Crocodile-like composite mask** — Museum records describe a zoomorphic mask shaped as a composite crocodile-like animal.
- **Anok figure** (*Anok*) — Anok is a wooden figure identified as a protective spirit belonging to the Simo society.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe seasonal, life-cycle, initiation, and funerary rituals but no annual festival calendar.

- **Harvest and planting dance** (*Nimba; D’mba*) — The Nimba or D’mba is danced during harvest and planting and was once performed before the rainy seasons.
- **Life-cycle dance** (*Nimba; D’mba*) — The Nimba or D’mba is danced at baby showers and weddings.
- **Male initiation ceremony** (*Bansonyi*) — The Bansonyi mask was used in male initiation ceremonies.
- **Funerary exposure** — The dead have been ritually exposed for a period in a sacred grove before burial.
- **Anok funerary rite** (*Anok*) — An Anok sculpture is brought out after someone dies and follows the dead person to the grave.

## Foodways

Rice farming is the principal documented Baga food-related activity.

- **Rice farming** — Baga women are known for their skill in rice farming in the swampy lowlands of southern Guinea.
- **Fishing** — Men are typically fishermen.
- **Kola cultivation** — The Baga historically cultivated kola nuts and tended kola trees.
- **Salt production** — The Baga historically produced salt for traders along the coast.
- **Palm tending** — Men also tend palm trees.

## Oral tradition & literature

Baga oral tradition says that the Baga came from Guinea’s interior highlands and moved west toward the coast.

- **First-comers tradition** — The Baga consider themselves first-comers along many parts of the Upper Guinea coast and acquired landlord’s rights there.
- **Cultural revival** — After the death of Sekou Toure in 1984, Baga culture began to reemerge as an affirmation of tribal identity.
- **Nimba revival** (*Nimba*) — Since the early 1980s, some communities have revived the creation of ritual pieces such as the Nimba.
- **Art of Baga** — Frederick Lamp’s Art of the Baga People helped knowledge of Baga art progress in the western world.

## Language & religion

Baga languages form an Atlantic Niger-Congo dialect cluster, and the Baga are now mostly Muslim after an earlier animist tradition.

- **Baga Koga or Koba** (*Baga Koga; Koba*) — Baga Koga or Koba is one of the named Baga language varieties.
- **Baga Manduri** — Baga Manduri is spoken at Dobale.
- **Baga Sitemu** — Baga Sitemu is spoken in villages on the Campaces River.
- **Baga Marara** — Baga Marara is spoken on islands in the Rio Pongo.
- **Baga Kaloum** — Baga Kaloum was originally spoken in the Conakry area and the Îles de Los.
