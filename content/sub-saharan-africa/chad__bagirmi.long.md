## Overview

The Bagirmi, also spelled Baguirmi or Barma, are a Central African ethnic group inhabiting the Chari-Baguirmi region of Chad. They are one of Chad’s major ethnic groups. Their language, Bagirmi, belongs to the Central Sudanic family and was spoken by 44,761 people in 1993, mainly in the Chari-Baguirmi Region and the Mokofi sub-prefecture of Guéra Region. Bagirmi society has a royal family whose king is known as *mbang*, a tradition dating to the Sultanate of Bagirmi. The available sources document agriculture, livestock raising, language, religious history, and museum objects associated with hunting, music, dance, and household use.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The catalogue records a jug made of pottery, as well as throwing-knives, throwing-clubs, a ceremonial axe, a ceremonial throwing-knife, a sheath for throwing weapons, and a curved wooden throwing-stick. The iron throwing-knives include examples with leather grips, while the sheath is made of leather and leopard skin. The ceremonial axe is made of wood with a two-pointed head that is lozenge-shaped in section. The catalogue also records small brass figures of a horseman, a giraffe, and an ostrich.

Four Museum of Ethnography records describe *Kastkniv*, a knife associated with Bagirmi in Chad. The catalogue states that it was used for hunting, thrown after running animals and aimed at their bones. Its blade is curved backward at a blunt angle, with a sharp edge along the front and a protruding handle. These records associate the objects with Gösta Moberg’s first expedition to North Africa in 1924–1925.

## Music & performance

The museum catalogue records a drum carved in wood with a hide membrane tied on with leather thongs. It also records a “flute” or clarinet made of tin, brass, cowrie shells, leather, and bamboo, and a harp made of wood with horse-hair strings. The sources do not provide vernacular instrument names, musical genres, song forms, performance contexts, or information about whether these instruments were used at court, in ceremonies, or for entertainment.

The sources do record dancing skirts, but they do not describe the associated music, choreography, occasions, or performers. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription concerns this ethnic group in the supplied material.

## Dance & theatre

The catalogue records two dancing skirts: one made of hide and decorated with cowrie shells, and another made of strings of reed. ## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not document an annual Bagirmi festival calendar, seasonal celebrations, life-cycle rites, wedding customs, funerals, initiation practices, or named rituals. They state that Islam was introduced to the Bagirmi by the start of the 17th century but did not fully replace their traditional beliefs. Sufi orders such as the Tijaniyyah and, to a lesser extent, the Sanusiyya, contributed to further conversions and the consolidation of Islam among the Bagirmi.

## Foodways

The Bagirmi mainly cultivate sorghum and millet and raise cattle, chickens, goats, and sheep. The Bagirmi language source gives *sa* as “eat,” but the supplied sources do not identify particular dishes, cooking methods, drinks, ceremonial foods, dietary rules, or food-related festivals.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used do not document Bagirmi folktales, epics, proverbs, riddles, or storytelling contexts. They do record efforts to develop written Bagirmi during the 1990s, when texts providing basic literacy instruction were composed through the work of Don and Orpha Raun, Christian missionaries of the Church of the Lutheran Brethren of America. In 2003, Anthony Kimball developed a font for the Bagirmi alphabet and a Keyman input method for Latin keyboards. The source states that published Baguirmi literature continued to expand and was distributed in Chad by David Raun.

## Language & religion

Bagirmi, also called Baguirmi, has the autonym *tàrà ɓármà*. It is the language of the Bagirmi people of Chad and belongs to the Central Sudanic family; it has also been tentatively classified as part of the Nilo-Saharan superfamily. In 1993 it was spoken by 44,761 people, mainly in the Chari-Baguirmi Region and the Mokofi sub-prefecture of Guéra Region. Bagirmi was the language of the Sultanate of Bagirmi from 1522 to 1871 and later of the Wadai Empire before the Scramble for Africa.

The Bagirmi mostly speak Bagirmi as their native language, although some speak Fula or Chadian Arabic. Their main religion is Islam. Islam was introduced by the start of the 17th century but did not fully replace traditional beliefs. The Tijaniyyah and, to a smaller extent, the Sanusiyya are identified as Sufi orders associated with further conversions and the consolidation of Islam.
