## Overview

The people now called Tetela — *Batetela* in the plural, *Otetela* as the language name — live between Lusambo and the Upper Congo River, in the provinces of Sankuru and Maniema in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, in the country between the Lomami and the Sankuru rivers. Most speak Tetela (*Otetela*, *Kitetela*, *Kikitatela*, also called *Sungu*), a Bantu language of northern Kasai-Oriental Province whose grammar sorts nouns into classes inherited from an ancestral system of twenty-two. They live by hunting and gathering, fishing and farming, raising cassava, bananas and kola nuts. Many Tetela, and some of today's Kusu, are subgroups of the larger Mongo group. The name itself is the group's most striking ethnographic fact: it is a late arrival, and many of the people it now covers understood themselves instead as descendants of *AnKutshu* or *AnKutshu-Membele*.

The sources make clear that "Batetela" as a clan or tribe did not exist before the colonial period. The first geographical journals, notes and books reporting a people of that name appear only between 1885 and 1887. The term was a corruption or mistranslation of the mid- to late nineteenth-century *Watetera*, itself derived from *Utotera*, the name Arab slave traders gave to a region in present-day Maniema inhabited by bilingual populations identifiable today as Songye, Luba and Kusu. Emil Torday, who studied the peoples of the Congo in 1908–09, recorded that "Batetela was not in general use," and that a Tetela-speaking group bearing the Luba title *Sungu* told him the Arabs had given them the name. The label spread across the northern Kasai basin up to Maniema in the early twentieth century, applied irrespective of people's original family names.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources document weaving equipment and finished mats rather than named cloth traditions, so the entries below are limited to what the catalogue records and Wikipedia articles actually attest. None of the vernacular textile names in our index appear in the sources, and they are not reproduced here.

**Raffia mats** — Mats woven from raffia, finished with a fringe along either of two sides. Three examples are held in the British Museum collection shown here, all of the same construction.

**Loom weaving** — A wooden loom with a partly completed grass textile still on it is among the collected objects, documenting the weaving apparatus itself rather than only its products.

**Two-ply string aprons** — Women's aprons made of pale brown two-ply string worked in three bunches, and a second apron of black two-ply string in four bunches. The bunching of the plied cords is the structural feature the catalogue records single out.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no motifs by vernacular term. The decorative devices they describe on collected objects are: bands of relief ornament around a pot's shoulder, rows of small nicked-out triangles, incised openwork with circular and diamond-shaped perforations, impressed patterns on leather, and black-and-white crosshatching painted on a mask.

### Clothing & dress

The record here is mostly the collected objects plus one photograph. Women's dress includes aprons of plied string, worn in bunched cords; belts and girdles appear in black string and in hide, and one leather belt carries impressed patterns and two attached pouches. A photographic print of a Tetela adult male at a large flat four-cornered drum shows adults and children in the background wearing cloths and personal ornaments.

Headgear is the most elaborated category. One head-dress combines feathers, hair — possibly elephant — glass beads, fibre, bamboo and fibre cord; another is built of feathers, fibre and string; a head ornament is made from cock feathers and cock skin. A chief's knife sheath, made of skin with fur, an animal tail, wood, copper and iron, is the one object the catalogue explicitly marks as rank-bearing regalia. Combs and hairpins of wood also survive.

A distinct nineteenth-century body practice is recorded in the historical sources rather than the objects: filed teeth were the ethnic identifier for many of the arabised groups of the region, first reported as a marker of cannibals and only later understood as a general custom not necessarily connected to it.

### Architecture

The sources contain almost nothing on built form. The single piece of evidence is the background of the British Museum photographic print, which shows a village setting with a large single-storey building, possibly thatch-roofed, behind the drummer. No house type, construction technique, granary form or ornamental scheme is described in the material used here.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery in the collection is red earthenware, sometimes finished with a dark coating. Decoration sits on the upper body: one bowl has a band of ornament around the shoulder, partly in relief; one vase of red clay is ornamented around the neck; another is ornamented very simply with three rows of small nicked-out triangles. Plain vessels and a bowl apparently containing seeds are also held.

Ironwork is the strongest technical showing. An axe has an incised openwork iron blade pierced with circular and diamond-shaped perforations and a flared tip, with a circular copper openwork section set into the blade and a wooden handle incised where shaft meets blade. Copper recurs as a finishing material: a socketed iron-and-wood javelin has its side opening closed with copper, and an adze's wooden handle is bound with copper wire. Other weapons and tools include spears of iron, wood and cane, a wooden club, an iron-and-wood sword, and a quiver of rattan, wood and string. Household and personal gear covers ivory powder horns, gourd oil vessels — one with a human figure, one with human figures, one studded with brass nails — wooden food bowls with either a straight knobbed handle or a loop handle, a bamboo snuff-box, and a hide box with a hinged lid.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ornament draws on animal material, iron and copper in roughly equal measure. There is a circlet or armlet of banana seeds hung with five pendant wild boar tusks, a necklace of leopard's teeth strung on cord, an iron bangle with a pendant spatulate blade, a bracelet of iron partly wrapped in lizard skin, and a hair ornament of copper wire that the catalogue also classes as a charm. A group of five gong-shaped iron pendants with a small horn is hung together on a ring. Feathers dominate the head ornaments described in the dress section above. Nineteenth-century parrot-feather wearing is the practice with the widest historical consequence: when Arab slave traders saw Luba, Songye and Kitetela speakers of the Barua lands in Maniema wearing parrot feathers, they began calling them *Kusu* or *Wakussu*, a corruption of *kasuku*, "parrot" — a name still carried today by the Kitetela speakers of Maniema.

## Music & performance

Three drum types are used by the Batetela of the country between the Lomami and the Sankuru. The *ngomo* is a skin drum used for dancing, and usually accompanies the *lukumbi*, a six-toned slit drum. The *ekuli* is a small cylindrical two-toned drum; it formerly signalled victory in battle and is now used to call people to church and to classes. The *lukumbi* is the most intricate of the three and constitutes a highly developed poetic and musical art form as well as a means of communication — that is, its repertoire is simultaneously music, verse and message. The British Museum photograph of a Tetela man "sending a message" on a large flat four-cornered drum, with small groups of men, women and children looking on in a village, documents that communicative use in performance.

Beyond the drums, the collected instruments include a stick zither of wood and quill with fibre bindings and a gourd resonator in two parts; a rattle of cane, gourd and seeds; a second reed-and-seed rattle that the cataloguer thought was apparently a zither; an iron gong with its rubber-headed beater; and two wooden whistles, one strung with cord and animal teeth, the other pierced with a single finger hole.

## Dance & theatre

Dancing is attested through its accompaniment: the *ngomo* skin drum is the drum used for dancing, normally played together with the *lukumbi* slit drum. Masks in the collection indicate masquerade without naming the performance. Two are painted red, white and black with feather ornament, one of them additionally fringed with grass around the lower edge and fitted with a leaf girdle. A third is a carved wooden helmet mask with a ridge across the top of the head, protruding eyes and pursed lips, painted in black-and-white crosshatching, with a vegetable-fibre fringe attached through holes around the rim. A further clue to performed roles is a "buffoon's" basket holding charms, assembled from bamboo, cane, calabashes, gourds, snail and other shells, vegetable-fibre cord and wood. The sources name no individual dance or dramatic tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used here describe no annual festival calendar and no birth, initiation, marriage or funeral rites for the Tetela. What they do document is a body of ritual objects. Charms take several forms: human figures carved from wood, one wrapped in cotton cloth, another with cotton wrapper and inlaid eyes, and a third simply recorded as a charm in the form of a wooden human figure. Two carved figures consist of head and neck on a circular base — one with white pigment around the eyes, the other with its hairstyle rendered by what appears to be a seed pod set with five white glass beads, and two lengths of cord at the neck, one strung with blue glass beads. Iron bells, some with copper or iron wire and one with a hide loop, are catalogued as charms rather than as instruments, as is a charm of cane and furred animal skin fitted with bells. The "buffoon's" basket of charms noted above belongs to this same class of ritual equipment.

Religious vocabulary is the one area where the sources are unusually precise about disagreement. Torday was told the name *Motetela* came from a god of that name, meaning either "he who laughs not" or "he at whom one may not laugh," but this was not known by most of the population in the early twentieth century; American missionaries working in the same period found the Kitetela term for a supreme deity to be *Djungumanga*. Torday recorded that the people bearing the Luba title *Sungu* arrived in Sankuru using *Winya* for god, not *Motetela*, and from the early twentieth century *Winya* developed into *Unya Shungu*, reportedly "father of the heavens."

## Foodways

The sources name no dishes. They state that the Tetela live by hunting and gathering, fishing and farming, and raise cassava, bananas and kola nuts. Two categories of collected object bear on food and drink: wooden food bowls, one with a straight knobbed handle and one with a loop handle, and gourd vessels for oil, two of them carrying human figures and one studded with brass nails.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Tetela have a large and colourful set of proverbs, used for a wide range of purposes from rebuking to encouraging, and usually deployed by adults. That corpus has been collected and published: Elysee Meta Okubo assembled *A Collection of 100 Tetela Proverbs* on a proverb website in 2016, and Ndjeka Elizabeth Mukanga, Empenge Albert Shefu and Ambaye Albertine Tshefu published *Great Collection of Tetela Proverbs on the African Wisdom* in 2020, running to 283 proverbs across 107 pages. The documentation of the language itself began with the Methodist Episcopal Church South, which established a mission at Wembo-Nyama in 1910 and identified its work with the "Otetela" people; Daniel and Edith Mumpower began recording and documenting the Otetela language in 1914. The sources name no epic cycle or narrative genre beyond the proverb corpus, and the poetic dimension of the *lukumbi* slit-drum repertoire is described in the Music section above.

## Language & religion

Tetela — also *Otetela*, *Kitetela*, *Kikitatela*, and *Sungu* — is a Bantu language of northern Kasai-Oriental Province. In its phonology, [ɡ] occurs as an allophone of /k/ between vowels. Like other Bantu languages it sorts nouns into classes; the ancestral system had twenty-two, counting singular and plural as distinct under the Meinhof system, and most Bantu languages retain at least ten. On religion, the sources record the competing divine names discussed under Festivals & rituals — *Motetela*, *Djungumanga*, *Winya* and *Unya Shungu* — alongside Christian mission presence from 1910 at Wembo-Nyama, and note that some of the nineteenth-century *Wakussu* groups were arabised, speaking Swahili and having adopted Islam; a group of *Binji* speakers later called *Zappo Zap* was reported in these terms, as was the slave raider Ngongo Lutete.
