## Overview

The Ebira are an ethno-linguistic group of North central Nigeria, concentrated in Kogi State and Nasarawa State. Their territory lies south-west of the confluence of the Niger and Benue Rivers, with communities also located north-east of the confluence. Ebiraland is north of Etsako, east of Yorubaland and west of Igalaland, and includes rocky hills and deciduous woodland in an open Savannah environment. The Ebira language is a Nupoid language within the Benue-Congo branch of the Niger-Congo language family and is spoken by around 2 million people. Ebira culture includes lineage-based social organization, farming, weaving, crafts, hair making, distinctive foodways, and the annual Ekuechi festival with masked performance, singing, drumming and chanting.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Cloth* is documented as part of Ebira material culture, and the sources identify weaving of clothing as an established activity.

**Clothing weaving** — Ebiras are known for weaving clothing.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs or pattern terms.

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that Ebiras are known for weaving clothing and hair making. They provide no named garment types, head coverings, belts, footwear or gender-specific dress.

### Architecture

They state that Ebira communities historically consisted of autonomous units managed by lineage leaders and that the family and lineage systems included compounds called *Ohuoje*, outer compounds called *Ovovu*, and lineages called *Abara*. Ajaokuta is described as cosmopolitan because of the construction of a steel mill.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention the Opete stool as a symbol of authority and identity, brought from Kwararafa and kept in Opete, in present-day Ajaokuta. The Opete is presently the title instrument of the Ozumi of Okene.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They mention hair making as an activity associated with Ebira culture but provide no further description of its techniques, styles or social significance.

## Music & performance

Music is documented most clearly through the Ekuechi festival. A major performance during the festival is a masked performance by Eku'rahu centered on singing, drumming and chanting. The festival is the most widely celebrated traditional festival in Ebira communities and is associated with ancestral masquerades and messages from the spirit world.

The supplied material identifies singing, drumming and chanting as components of Eku'rahu performance, but gives no further account of their forms.

The masquerades performing during Ekuechi are believed to have access to the spirit world in which dead relatives abide and observe the behavior of the living. During the festival, they deliver messages of good tidings and admonishment from the spirit world. The performance therefore combines music with masquerade, religious belief and communal observance.

## Dance & theatre

They document the Ekuechi masquerade and the masked performance by Eku'rahu, which involves singing, drumming and chanting. The masquerades represent ancestral spirits returning to earth and deliver messages associated with the spirit world.

They do, however, identify the Ekuechi masquerade as a major performance within the most widely celebrated traditional festival in Ebira communities.

## Festivals & rituals

**Ekuechi festival** is the most widely celebrated traditional festival in Ebira communities. It is held annually, beginning in late November and ending in late December or January. Its duration is long because different clans choose their own dates for marking the festival.

In Ebira, “Eku” represents an ancestral masquerade, while “Chi” means to descend. The festival is connected to the traditional belief in a land of the living and another land of the dead, together with the veneration of the dead by those among the living. Ekuechi can therefore be interpreted as ancestral spirits returning to earth.

The masquerades are believed to have access to the spirit world where dead relatives abide and observe the behavior of the living. They deliver messages of good tidings and admonishment from that world. The festival also marks the end of one year and the beginning of another.

They state that traditional Ebira religious practice included rites performed to appease Ohomorihi, a god associated with rain.

## Foodways

The principal occupation of the Ebiras is farming. They cultivate maize, yams, cassava and vegetables. In the nineteenth century, some communities cultivated and traded benne seeds, called “gorigo.”

The sources identify *Ọve* as a food associated with the Ebira people. It is made from water yam and usually prepared with a particular kind of leaf. The source compares it with moi moi but distinguishes it because Ebiras make it from water yam, called *Evina*, rather than from beans.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that the early migration of the Ebira to what is now Ebiraland is mostly surmised through oral history. Most versions trace the migration from the Jukuns of the Kwararafa state, north of the Benue River and in present-day Taraba State. Another account describes the Ebiras leaving Idah after the Idah-Benin War around 1519–1521 and stopping at Itobe.

The Opete stool is described as one relic of the trace from Kwararafa. It was brought along as a symbol of authority and identity and kept in Opete, a place whose name derives from the stool. The sources also preserve accounts of the settlement of Ebira groups among or near the Igalas and of later movements to Ebira Opete, Okengwe, Ubororo and Okehi.

## Language & religion

Ebira is a Nupoid language within the Benue-Congo branch of the Niger-Congo language family. It is spoken in Kogi State, Nasarawa State, Edo State, the Federal Capital Territory, Lapai in Niger State, Makurdi in Benue State, Kwara State and parts of Ondo State. The central dialect is known as Okene.

The sources identify Tao as the more prominent dialect used in media and publishing. Koto, also called Okpoto, is spoken north-east of the Niger-Benue confluence. Other classifications list Okene, Etuno, also written Tụnọ, and Koto, while another division gives Igara, Koto and Okene.

Before Christianity and Islam, Ebira people practiced a form of African traditional religion centered on Ohomorihi, a god and rainmaker who lives in the sky. Rites were performed to appease him. Other religious figures included ori, described as deities, and spirits. Ebira tradition also includes belief in a spirit world where dead ancestors live. Ibrahim, also called Attah Ibrahim or Attah of Ebiraland, was a Muslim convert who helped spread Islam in the region.
