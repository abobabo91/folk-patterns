## Overview

The Kalanga, or BaKalanga, are a southern Bantu ethnic group living mainly in Matebeleland in Zimbabwe, northern Botswana, and parts of the Limpopo Province in South Africa. In Botswana, the BaKalanga are the country’s second largest ethnic group, and Kalanga is the second most spoken language there, especially in the North. In Zimbabwe, TjiKalanga is the country’s third most spoken language and is recognized as a Western Shona branch. Kalanga communities are associated with the Shashe-Limpopo basin, the lower Shashe-Limpopo valley, and areas near major rivers. Their history is linked to Mapungubgwe, Khami, and Great Zimbabwe. Kalanga cultural life also includes rain-making practices associated with Mwali or Ngwali and the Njelele Shrine.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe ceremonial attire worn when Kalanga people plead for rain at Njelele Shrine in Zimbabwe. This attire consists of black skirts and white shirts. The black skirts represent dark clouds heavy with rain, while the white shirts represent rain droplets.

### Architecture

The sources state that prehistoric Bakalanga villages were usually built on terraced hilltops with stone walls around them. Many such villages have been discovered in Botswana and Zimbabwe, particularly in areas close to major rivers.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that early Bakalanga people traded ivory, furs, and feathers with the Indian Ocean coast in exchange for goods including glass beads and cotton clothes.

## Music & performance

They state that TjiKalanga is used in mass media in Zimbabwe.

The sources describe Mwali or Ngwali as the Supreme Being associated with Kalanga rain-making. Rain-making has historically been the duty of the Hosanna’s or Wosana, described as high priests in Mwali or Ngwali’s church. Njelele Shrine in Zimbabwe is identified as the headquarters for the Hosanna’s of Botswana, South Africa, and Zimbabwe.

## Festivals & rituals

They do, however, describe rain-making as an important ritual practice. Kalanga people are associated with rain-making through Mwali or Ngwali, and the duty of conducting this practice belongs to the Hosanna’s or Wosana, the high priests in Mwali or Ngwali’s church.

Rain is represented in ceremonial clothing worn at Njelele Shrine in Zimbabwe: black skirts signify dark clouds heavy with rain, and white shirts signify rain droplets.

## Foodways

They mention that early Bakalanga communities traded ivory, furs, and feathers with the Indian Ocean coast for glass beads and cotton clothes.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do describe a number of clan traditions and identities. Notable Kalanga clans include the boSungwasha, boMndambeli, boNeswimbo, boNtombo, boKumbudzi, boKadzasha, and boPhizha na boNebukhwa.

The Bo-Sungwasha are also known as BaWumbe or Tjibelu. The Mndambeli use the Shoko, Zhou, and Tshuma as totems in some areas, while other Mndambeli communities venerate the Ghudo or Tembo. The Ntombo, also called Baperi, are described as descendants of the Balobedu people and as a section of the Bapedi tribe. The BoKumbudzi are associated with spiritual gifts to communicate with Mwali at Njelele Shrine.

## Language & religion

Kalanga is a Bantu language within the Niger-Congo languages. It is placed in the western branch of the Shona group, also called Shonic or Shona-Nyai, together with Nambya. The language is called Ikalanga or TjiKalanga. It has varieties including Talaunda, Lilima, Jawunda, Nanzwa, Tjigwizi, and Tjindodondo, the latter described as dominant in Zimbabwe. The sources also identify TjiLilima, TjiGwizi, TjiTalaunda, TjiNanzwa, and TjiKalanga proper as dialects.

Kalanga has dialectal variation between Botswana and Zimbabwe, with slightly different orthographies. TjiKalanga is recognized as an official language by the Zimbabwean Constitution of 2013 and is taught in schools where its speakers predominate. The sources associate Kalanga religious practice with Mwali or Ngwali, rain-making, the Hosanna’s or Wosana, and Njelele Shrine.
