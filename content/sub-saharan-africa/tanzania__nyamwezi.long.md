## Overview

The Nyamwezi are a Bantu people of west-central Tanzania and the country's second-largest ethnic group. Their homeland, *Unyamwezi*, covers parts of Tabora, Singida, Shinyanga and Katavi regions — some 35,000 square miles (91,000 km²) of rolling plateau at roughly 4,000 feet (1,200 m). The name is Swahili and translates as "people of the moon" or, more meaningfully in context, "people of the west"; the Sukuma, with whom they were once a single group, called them *Dakama*, "people of the south." About 926,000 people speak *Kinyamwezi*, classified in the Sukuma–Nyamwezi group of Bantu. They matter to folk-culture study because a settled farming and cattle-owning society on the western plateau became, from about 1800, the porters and caravan traders of the whole central route to the coast — while its colonies abroad stayed conspicuously resistant to foreign culture.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources document no named weaving or dyeing tradition. What they do record is the collapse of one: a Nyamwezi **cloth industry** existed but was already ailing in 1857 under severe competition from Indian imports, and over the following sixty years it almost disappeared. Cloth nonetheless remained, with iron, one of the two goods of first importance in regional exchange networks.

The pattern-bearing work that survives in the collection is beadwork and coiled fibre rather than textile:

**Beaded waist-band** — a glass-bead band with plaited tying strings and a fringe, worked in white, red and black on a pale blue ground, its field laid out as squares and rectangles.

**Coiled bead-set basketry** — a basket of spirally coiled vegetable fibre, round-bottomed with nearly vertical sides, its outer wall studded with blue glass beads.

**Reed matting** — mats made of reed.

**Motif vocabulary.** The catalogued ornament is geometric throughout: squares and rectangles in bead fields; banded incision encircling pottery; punched marks and burn-staining on wood.

### Clothing & dress

The sources say very little about garments. Beaded body ornament is the documented element of dress: a waist-band of glass beads with plaited ties and a fringe, and bracelets and necklaces described below. On broader fashion, the record notes only that many Nyamwezi converted to Sunni Islam during the nineteenth century and that Islam influenced the dress even of those who did not convert. Nothing in the sources describes head coverings, footwear, belts other than the beaded waist-band, or a ceremonial costume distinct from daily wear.

### Architecture

Nineteenth-century settlements were large, compact and fortified for defence with strong wooden stockades, often placed in high, inaccessible rocky sites. The residential unit was the *tembe*; spouses generally came from outside the tembe and sons commonly moved away from their father's homestead. The sources name the tembe without describing its construction or roof.

Military architecture is documented in detail for one case. Anticipating foreign invasion, the Unyanyembe ruler Isike built a fortress around the royal courts at **Itetemia**: thick walls of stone and mud mortar strengthened with fire, roughly three feet wide and ten feet high, with holes cut at the top at chosen points so that *ruga-ruga* marksmen could set their weapons to defend the base of the wall.

When the Germans imposed peace in 1893 the fortified settlements did not empty at once. Dispersal took about fifty years, and the modern pattern of scattered settlement emerged only slowly. Under British administration many people were moved into new villages sited clear of sleeping sickness.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Crafts were a part-time occupation and were not hereditary. The goods that travelled furthest in regional trade were drums, ladles, stools, grain storage boxes and snuffboxes of horn. Ironwork came from localised settlements and was dispersed over wide areas: bows, arrows, spears, the metal used in paying fines, and above all the hoes that served as bridewealth, all produced by smiths with considerable ritual. The collected objects match this list closely — pottery vessels with geometric ornament, a long-necked pot in dappled light and dark brown with bands of incised decoration, a brass vase, an iron bell of unusual form with an opening at each side, a wooden ladle stained by burning and finished with punch marks, a snuff-box made from a seed pod, an ebony candlestick cut to a European form, and a wooden mask with an open mouth and teeth apparently of shell.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Glass beads strung on fibre account for most of the ornament collected: plain bead necklaces, one with two strings of black mingling with two black strips, and bracelets built as sections of small beads separated by larger ones. Metal and animal materials carry the more emphatic pieces — a necklace of biconical copper beads graded in size, a necklace of eleven rounded triangular plaques of ivory or rhinoceros bone strung together, an ivory armlet, and a bracelet made from the tail of an elephant. The last two connect ornament directly to the elephant hunters' guild, the most prestigious occupation in the society.

## Music & performance

Drums were among the wooden goods the Nyamwezi traded across the region, which places instrument-making inside their craft economy rather than outside it. For repertoire, the sources point to a single documented genre: greeting and installation songs for chiefs, recorded in the Tabora district and held by Smithsonian Global Sound. This fits what the sources say about chiefship, where the ruler's role was to receive tribute, bring prosperity, and take an active part in ceremonies; the *ntemi* was ritual and religious leader and priest, so song attached to installation belonged to the central political ritual rather than to entertainment. Beyond this the sources describe no song genres, no instrument names and no performance occasions.

## Dance & theatre

A carved wooden mask with an open mouth and shell teeth is held in the collection, but its catalogue entry records no performance context, and nothing in the written sources attaches masking to a named dance or masquerade.

## Festivals & rituals

No annual festival calendar appears in the sources; the year is described only as divided into two seasons, wet and dry, varying considerably by time and place. Ritual life as documented is ancestral, divinatory and political.

Offerings of sheep or goats are made to ancestors, with the help of the high god Likube invoked beforehand; goats were kept for ancestor sacrifice specifically, while the economic value of goats and sheep otherwise lay in meat and skins. Spirits are active in daily religious life, and *mfumu* — diviners and witchdoctors — serve as counsellors and medical practitioners. *Bulogi*, witchcraft, is a powerful force, and the **Baswezi** society recruits people possessed by the Swezi spirit.

Marriage is the main life-cycle institution described. Ideally every adult should be married, and every married wife should have her own household and bring her own utensils; bridewealth was paid in hoes, made with ritual by the smiths. Divorce was common — unlike among the Wagogo, a large majority of people had experienced at least one by age fifty — and entailed the return of bridewealth less an allowance for the number and sex of children born. Chiefdom courts recognised fixed grounds on each side. A widow was customarily inherited by her former husband's younger brother, though not against her will; among some, inheritance by the husband's sister's son was particularly favoured. The most striking political rite concerns the chief's death: rulers were strangled when they became seriously ill, because the wellbeing and continuation of the state were identified with the chief and his subordinate administrators.

## Foodways

The staple is *ugali*, a porridge made from hominy and served with meat and vegetables. Beer brewed from fermented corn, sorghum or millet was also common. The Nyamwezi are predominantly subsistence farmers and cattle herders, and were a settled agricultural and cattle-owning people from their arrival on the western plateau; cattle were important but their care was not part of tribal life, so herds were often placed with hired professional herdsmen, immigrant Tutsi. Livestock values were reckoned in a fixed scale: five goats or sheep equated to one bull, and two bulls to one cow. Men did the heavy work and women the recurring tasks and much of the everyday agricultural work; the sexes usually ate separately, and a domestic group's core was the husband, his wife or wives and their resident children. The sources describe no ceremonial dishes, no tea or coffee custom and no dietary prohibitions.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral tradition is the sources' own evidence for early Nyamwezi history: it holds that the people settled west-central Tanzania some time in the seventeenth century, with the earliest corroborating evidence coming from the Galahansa and confirming their presence there in the late seventeenth century. Outside testimony runs further back in name only — ancient Indian texts refer to the Nyamwezi as "the men of the moon," a term still used of them in Tanzania. The name entered European literature only in the nineteenth century, when travel taught Nyamwezi men that others called them this and almost all accepted the coastal people's term for themselves. The sources record no folktale cycle, epic, proverb collection or storytelling occasion.

## Language & religion

Kinyamwezi is a major Bantu language of central Tanzania, forming a dialect continuum with Sukuma while remaining more distinct from it; Konongo and Ruwila are sometimes counted as dialects. It has three tones — high, low and rising — and prenasalised voiceless stops occur frequently as a result of loanwords. Many speakers also use Swahili or English. Most Nyamwezi follow the traditional religion despite Islamic and Christian missionary effort, believing in a powerful god known as Likube (High God), Limatunda (Creator), Limi (the Sun) and Liwelolo (the Universe), with ancestor worship the more frequent daily practice. Sunni Islam spread in the nineteenth century, as did Moravian Protestantism; the Moravian Church in Western Tanzania now counts about 80,000 Nyamwezi adherents. Converts of both faiths retained much of their earlier custom and belief.
