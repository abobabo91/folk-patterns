## Overview

The Toposa are a Nilotic people of South Sudan, concentrated in the Greater Kapoeta region of Eastern Equatoria state, with a further population in the southeast of Jonglei State. They live beside the Singaita and Lokalyen rivers, keep a ritual centre at the Loyooro River, and take their herds to seasonal pasture at Moruangipi and sometimes east into the Ilemi Triangle; Kapoeta, Riwoto and Narus are their main settlements. The land is semi-arid and rugged — hills and ridges divided by shallow plains and seasonal streams, with vegetation limited to shrubs and short grass. A 1982 report counted 105,000 speakers of the Toposa language, 95,000 of them along both sides of the Zingietta and Lokalyen rivers and 10,000 in Ethiopia; more recent reports give a much higher figure. The language is Nilo-Saharan (Eastern Sudanic, Nilotic). Culturally the Toposa matter as a cattle-centred herding society whose knowledge is carried orally rather than in writing.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used give no account of Toposa weaving, dyeing, cloth-making or named surface-pattern traditions, and none of the search terms we indexed appears in them. The only worked surfaces the museum catalogue records describe are on objects of other materials: a cattle ornament carved from ivory as two crescent-shaped pieces, one of them with drilled decoration, both strung on a corded hide thong.

### Clothing & dress

The sources used do not describe Toposa everyday or ceremonial garments, and name no items of clothing. What the museum records hold from the dressed body is a hat of cane and raffia palm, and the wrist-knife — a blade of metal whose cutting edges are wrapped in leather so it can be worn on the arm, of which the collection holds two. The catalogue also records a man's ivory ring, identified as a thumb-ring worn during marriage ceremonies, which is the one item the sources tie to a specific ceremonial occasion. Beyond these, the sources note two things about appearance as status rather than as dress: possession of cattle and possession of a loaded gun are the main measures of standing and wealth among the Toposa, and during the civil war firearms — at least 50,000 small arms issued to the Toposa by the government of Sudan during the 1990s — became a symbol of wealth in themselves.

### Architecture

The sources used do not describe Toposa house form, building materials, roofing or built decoration. They give only the settlement pattern that houses serve: villages near which the animals graze in the wet season, and from which the men take the herds away to dry-season pasturage when the rains end, grazing them slowly back so as to arrive in the village as the next rainy season begins. Kapoeta, Riwoto and Narus are named as the main settlements, and the rivers and stream valleys as the places where limited cultivation happens.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The catalogue of objects shown here is almost entirely of wood, metal, horn and hide, and it sets out a herder's household. There is a vessel of pottery, and in wood a knobkerrie and a staff or walking-stick mounted with brass and aluminium. Metal and wood together make the spear and an axe or chopper. The best-documented piece is a composite: a wooden stool, which may equally be a neck-rest, carried with a metal knife in a skin sheath and a horn-and-hide container for chewing tobacco, sheath and container both tied to the stool by hide thongs; a second stool or neck-rest and a separate horn-and-hide container are held on their own. A drinking-horn of skin completes the set. The traditional Toposa weapon was a long throwing spear used together with a shield.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The worn objects in the collection are made from seed, fibre, cane, metal, brass and ivory. Four neck ornaments are recorded — two of seed and fibre, one of cane with seed and fibre, one of metal and fibre — alongside a brass nose-ring, a piece combining metal and ivory, and an ornament of feather and metal. Ivory also carries the one documented ritual use: a man's ivory ring worn during marriage ceremonies, catalogued alternatively as a pendant or charm. Ivory's prominence here is not incidental. The Toposa were deeply involved in the ivory trade in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and by the early 1930s were the main remaining ivory traders in the southern Sudanese lowlands. The sources used say nothing about tattooing, scarification, hair practices or body paint.

## Music & performance

The sources name no Toposa song genres, no singers and no performance occasions in detail, but they are clear that music is one of the carriers of the culture: Toposa culture is transmitted orally through songs, dance, music, poems and folklore. The instruments among the objects shown are all of metal, horn and skin. Three rattles are recorded, each made of metal and skin. Two trumpets are made from animal horn, one of them noted in the catalogue as possibly antelope. A cow-bell of horn, cane and leather belongs to the same sound-world and to the herd: bells and the animals they hang on are not separable in a society whose economy and social life revolve around herding cattle, camels, donkeys, goats and sheep. Beyond this the sources used do not describe how these instruments are played, by whom, or at which gatherings, and they name no melodic or stringed instrument at all. Nor do they record any professional or hereditary musician class. What the sources do fix is the setting in which speech and performance carry weight: most decisions about the clan or community are made in meetings attended only by men, traditionally held in the dark hours before dawn.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is listed in the sources as one of the forms through which Toposa culture is passed on, alongside songs, music, poems and folklore, but no individual dance is named and no dramatic or masking tradition is described. The sources used do not cover Toposa theatre.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not give a Toposa festival calendar, and they name no annual celebration. What they document instead is ritual authority and the ceremonial framing of conflict and peace. The Toposa believe in a supreme being and in ancestral spirits, who may be called on to help overcome drought or epidemics among the herds. The elders hold sacral power over rain and drought, and it is the sections' councils of elders that decide matters of war and peace. There is a ritual centre at the Loyooro River. One life-cycle transition is recorded directly: boys are first given the care of goats and sheep, and graduate to looking after cattle when they come of age, travelling considerable distances in search of water and pasture. Marriage is attested through an object rather than a description — the man's ivory thumb-ring worn during marriage ceremonies. Seasonal ritual of a practical kind governed relations with neighbours: the Toposa of Namorunyang used to drive their cattle to the Didinga Hills for dry-season water and pasture by agreement with the Didinga of Budi County, with a gift made in exchange for access, until the spread of guns and the breakdown of order during the civil war caused those protocols to be ignored. Women's peace-making has since become its own institution — a Didinga-Turkana-Toposa-Nyangatom women's workshop in February 2000 took on the responsibility of discouraging further raiding between the communities, and the sources call the role of women in crossing the boundaries to initiate peace talks unprecedented and crucial.

## Foodways

Toposa food comes first from the herds: cattle, sheep and goats yield milk, blood, meat and leather. Cultivation is limited and is women's work, carried out in the river valleys, with sorghum as the main crop, grown on fertile clay soils; depending on conditions a year may bring severe shortage or a large surplus, since rainfall is low and unpredictable and the streams are torrential, flowing only in the rainy season. Some good pasture cannot be used at all for lack of drinking water. Chewing tobacco is attested by the horn-and-hide containers in the collection, one of them tied to a stool alongside a sheathed knife so that the whole set travels together. The sources used name no prepared dish, no cooking method and no drink recipe, and they record no dietary prohibition. The division of labour around food is documented in an unusually specific episode: when an NGO introduced ox cultivation, it judged it easier to have men do the ploughing — though cultivation was women's work — than to have women enter the men's domain of animal husbandry; it later decided women should be allowed to plough, the councils of elders rejected this, and the NGO trained some women anyway, mostly widows and orphans. The experiment ended in 1985 when rebel forces reached the area, a disaster the elders attributed to having let women manage cattle.

## Oral tradition & literature

Toposa culture is orally transmitted through songs, dance, music, poems and folklore, and as of 2000 perhaps 5% of the population could read. The sources name no epic and give no storyteller's repertoire, but they preserve one cosmological narrative: men originally lived with "Nakwuge" in the sky, and many slid down a rope to earth; the rope then broke, separating them from heaven. Historical narrative is also carried orally. The Toposa say they came from the Losolia Mountains in Uganda and moved away during a severe drought that killed people and animals; their ancestors settled at Losilang for a time before drifting north for grazing, and at Loyoro one group, the Nyangatom or Dongiro, went east while the Mosingo and Kor sections moved west under Turkana pressure and had settled in Kapoeta by 1830. A neighbouring people's tradition names the Toposa in turn: the Lotuho tell how their kingdom of Imatari was destroyed around the start of the nineteenth century when an exiled Lotuho king, who had taken refuge in Toposaland with only his dog, returned with a band of Toposa who encircled the city and pushed over the strong walls of the palace where the people had taken shelter.

## Language & religion

Toposa — also called Akara, Kare, Kumi, Taposa and Topotha — is a Nilo-Saharan language of the Eastern Sudanic, Nilotic branch, belonging to the Turkana group that also contains Karamojong of Uganda, Nyangatom of Ethiopia and Turkana of Kenya; Turkana and Toposa are so close as to be mutually intelligible, and Jiye of South Sudan, Karimojong, Jie and Dodos of Uganda are likewise mutually intelligible varieties, while Teso is lexically more distant. The language has vowel harmony on two sets of vowels, advanced tongue root and not, with /a/ neutral; all nine vowels also occur devoiced, mainly before a pause, and some morphemes consist of nothing but a high voiceless vowel. Its tone is grammatical rather than lexical, marking case in nouns and tense in verbs. Toposa once served as a lingua franca west of Lake Rudolf and across most of Eastern Equatoria. Belief centres on a supreme being and on ancestral spirits; the Roman Catholic Diocese of Torit has proselytized among the Toposa with some success and has been active in resolving conflicts among them and with the Didinga and Buya.
