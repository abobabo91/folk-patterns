## Overview

The Nupe — called *Nufawa* by the Hausa and *Tapa* by the Yoruba — are a people of North Central Nigeria, dominant in Niger State and an important minority in Kwara, with communities in Kogi State and the Federal Capital Territory. They number roughly 4.5 million. Their homeland is a lowland of about 18,000 square kilometres in the Niger Basin, mostly north of the river between the Kontagora and Guara confluents, from Kainji down to below Baro; Bida and its surrounding towns are the centre of Nupe speech. The Nupe Kingdom emerged in the 14th–15th century between the Niger and the Kaduna, founded by conquest under the Igala leader Tsoede, who took the title *Etsu* (king). Nupe art is characteristically abstract, and the people are best known for wooden stools carved with surface patterns.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Saki / etu check cloth** — Men's large gowns (*boubou*, *tobe*, *riga*) and tunics are built from narrow hand-woven strips sewn together along the selvedges, using indigo-dyed cotton in both warp and weft with lines of white cotton run in regularly through both, producing a blue-grey "guinea-fowl" check known as *saki* to the Hausa and *etu* to the Yoruba. One British Museum gown is assembled from 161 uniform strips; a tunic in the same weave uses four-inch strips, is slit at the sides for the arms, and has its side and neck openings edged in red cotton.

**Indigo strip cloth** — Rectangular cloths made of very narrow strips (one example runs to 29), warp of indigo alternating with slightly narrower white lines, weft mainly indigo with narrow white lines. Because each strip's edges are indigo, the seams read as broader indigo bands; a thin light-indigo edging strip sewn across the warps at each end serves as the hem.

**Women's warp-striped wrapper** — A cotton wrapper woven in a single panel rather than assembled from strips, predominantly red-dyed warp stripes broken by narrow black and white stripes, with narrow white and black weft stripes at one side and twisted warp-thread fringes at both ends.

**Alaari-striped wrapper/shawl** — A six-strip hand-sewn cloth combining white and indigo-dyed cotton with *alaari* (magenta silk) warp stripes, and hidden white and indigo weft stripes.

**Warp-faced hair-band** — A woman's hair-band of a single woven width in white, red, brown and indigo-dyed handspun cotton, warp-faced, with four bands of colour set against four bands of white and the warp ends twisted into a fringe.

**Cut and appliquéd leather** — Pattern is carried in leather as well as cloth: a circular cushion cover of alternating beige and dark-brown patches appliquéd together within white leather borders, its beige fields worked with floral and lozenge patterns in metallic blue leather; and horse trappings whose patterns are cut through the dye into the red leather itself.

**Motif vocabulary.** Carved door panels carry gun, snake, cutlass, Koran board, lizard, hump-backed cow, fan, dagger with looped handle, ankle rattle, sickle, axe, saddled horse, pair of sandals, monkey, tortoise, key, bow and arrow — and, explicitly, designs based on embroidery patterns. Beaten brass adds hammered Zs in rings, a central rosette and trumpet-lobed scrolls in an S pattern; leather mats carry animal and geometric motifs in concentric bands; calabash lids are engraved with squares and lines; a hooded cloak is embroidered around a large central star. The British Museum also holds a Nupe pattern-sheet: a rectangle of paper on canvas backing drawn with coloured geometric designs.

### Clothing & dress

Men's dress centres on the strip-woven *saki*/*etu* gown, wide-shouldered and unshaped in the body with sleeves that fan out from a broad top, a large pocket sewn at the front, and heavy embroidery at shoulder and front; the lighter side-slit tunic is the same cloth in a smaller garment. Outerwear includes a hooded green cloak (*burnous*) cut from a single piece of dyed European cloth, lined with machine-woven pink and yellow cotton, edged with seven green silk tassels at the hood and five at the neck, and embroidered in pink and green around a central star. Riding dress runs to thigh-length boots of black Morocco leather ornamented with sewn-on green leather, and iron spurs. Headgear includes plaited straw hats with a coloured crown band and red and black cotton brim ornaments, and twisted straw head-rings, two-ply strands whipped around a circular core, for carrying loads. Women wear the single-panel red-striped wrapper and the woven hair-band; wooden clogs with carved raised soles and decorated leather straps are also recorded. Facial marks are widespread, worn to signal prestige and family, for protection, and as adornment — though the practice is dying out in places.

### Architecture

The sources describe Nupe building almost entirely through its carved doors. These are assembled from separate wood panels — typically three, sometimes four — originally fixed together by battens nailed across the back and hung on pegs at one side, reaching about 99 cm wide when complete. Each panel is carved in relief with the object repertoire listed above, the outermost panel often heavily patinated down its exposed edge from handling. At least one surviving door has an iron flat bar stapled to a panel, used with a modern padlock. Beyond the doors, house form, materials and roofing are not covered by the sources used here.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery includes a triple lamp painted glazy red and striped in black, yellow and white; a glazy black earthenware water-bottle; a black handled flask; a splay-ended stand with geometric decoration; and plain vases. Brass is the signature Bida medium — a tray bought directly from its maker in Bida in 1952 is catalogued as being in a characteristically Nupe, expressly non-Hausa style. The brass repertoire covers globular lidded bowls tinned inside and decorated with punched designs, hinged-lid teapots with a chain-tethered spout plug, beaten ewers, hammered dishes, copper-and-brass coffee pots, and repoussé spoons; also small cast figures of birds, a seated dog, an ox, a quadruped, a crocodile, a cobra, a balancing toy figure and a man at a camera. Woodwork includes carved stools with sub-rectangular incised seats, used by wives at market. Iron tools — hoe, adze, sickle, axe — are set into wooden handles finished with pokerwork bands and grooves. Household containers include coiled leaf baskets with lids, calabashes ornamented in relief, and plaited straw fans on wooden sticks, used mainly by women.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Glass and brass dominate. Recorded ornaments include a pale blue glass bangle with bosses around its outer circumference; an opaque blue imported glass bracelet of triangular section; and cylindrical glass beads — twelve dark brown with white spirals, eleven yellow-brown with yellow spirals. In metal there are brass finger-rings, brass pins with spatulate ends and engraved squared heads, brass hairpins used as hair curlers, and heavy cast manillas with splayed terminals incised along the outer curve with arcs, circles and dot patterns. Facial marking is the other body practice the sources name: worn to identify prestige and family, for protection, and, in the phrasing of the source, as adornment like jewellery.

## Music & performance

Nupe traditional music is sung by the *Ningba*, the musician or musicians, while the *Enyanicizhi* beats the drum. Remembered singers include Hajiya Fatima Lolo Alhaji Nda'asabe, Hajiya Nnadzwa, Hauwa Kulu, Baba-Mini, Ahmed Shata and Ndako Kutigi; Fatima Lolo (1891–1997) is named among notable Nupe. The instruments in museum collections are mostly aerophones and chordophones. A stringed instrument uses half a calabash covered with skin as its resonator, with a straight stick neck and three sinew strings, its calabash back richly carved with stylised animals and birds; a second skin-topped instrument with a wooden body is catalogued as a guitar. Horns and trumpets are prominent: an ivory horn cut from a tusk and slung on giraffe hair, a side-blown trumpet roughly trimmed from an antelope horn, and an Emir's brass war trumpet made in two parts, a relief-decorated bell-and-stem section and a separate mouthpiece stem that fits into it. A composite brass bell with an iron clapper completes the recorded set.

## Dance & theatre

The sources do not describe Nupe dance. Two performance objects are documented. A wooden face mask shows a bearded human face bearing facial marks, surrounded by oval "horns" that flank two human figures. A hunter's headdress is built from the head and beak of a ground hornbill (*Bucorvus abyssinicus*), covered with goat skin and bright red *Abrus precatorius* seeds, the beak open and the eyes accentuated with shell or metal inlay; Nupe hunters tied it around the forehead to hold it upright, imitating the bird's movements to approach game unseen and to secure protection. In screen drama the Nupe built their own industry from the late 1990s and early 2000s — **Nupewood**, founded by Sadisu Muhammad DGN, Prince Ahmed Chado, Prince Hussaini Kodo, M.B. Yahaya Babs and Jibril Bala Jibril, and since responsible for more than a thousand films for Nupe audiences.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used here do not set out a Nupe festival calendar or life-cycle rites. What they do record is *Dzana*, a practice preserved in oral tradition and described as a multifaceted cultural gesture involving the giving of gifts, blessings or symbolic items, primarily in the context of travel, transitions, and relational expressions of care.

## Foodways

The sources say nothing about Nupe staples, dishes or cooking; the only trace of the table is in the vessels themselves — brass lidded bowls tinned on the inside, hinged-lid teapots and coffee pots, beaten ewers, hammered dishes and repoussé spoons.

## Oral tradition & literature

Nupe cultural heritage is rooted in oral transmission, and Nupe speech distinguishes its forms by name: *egankogi* (parable), *gangba* (warning), *egancin* (idiom), *ecingi* (riddle or tale), and *eganmagan* (proverb, plural *eganmaganzhi*). The *eganmaganzhi* are didactic — they educate, entertain and teach morality — and carry the norms and ethics of Nupe society from one generation to the next through songs, stories, fables, folk tales, myths, legends, incantations, communal discussion and worship. Like other African proverbs, they tie people's actions to their immediate environment in order to explain or correct a situation, and they warn, advise and shape perception. Ethnographically, the Nupe were described in detail by Siegfried Nadel, whose *Black Byzantium* remains an anthropological classic.

## Language & religion

Nupe — also Anufe, Nyinfe, *Nupenci* in Hausa and *Tapa* in Yoruba — is a Volta–Niger language of the Nupoid branch of Benue-Congo, closest in structure and vocabulary to Kakanda, and grouped with Igbira (Ebira) and Gade. More than five dialects are spoken: Central Nupe, Nupe Tako/Bassa-Nge, Kupa, Kakanda and Dibo/Abawa/Gana-Gana, with Central and Tako the two most markedly distinct. Nupe has absorbed earlier groups, including the originally Kanuri-speaking Benu of Kutigi and the Gbagyi at Lemu. The Nupe are primarily Muslim, with Christian and African Traditional Religion minorities; Etsu Jibiri adopted Islam around 1770 under pressure from the Fulani advance from the north, and the Sokoto jihad of the 19th century changed many practices without displacing all of them. A Koran board is among the motifs carved on Nupe doors, and portions of the Book of Common Prayer were published in Nupe in 1955.
