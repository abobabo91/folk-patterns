## Overview

The Dyula live across several West African countries, including Ghana, Côte d’Ivoire and Burkina Faso. Since the fourteenth century, Dyula merchants built trading communities linked by wide commercial networks. Their history is tied to trade, Islamic learning and peaceful relations with non-Muslims. Their culture includes strip-woven cloth, oral traditions, Arabic writing and Muslim religious life.

## Material culture

Dyula material culture includes narrow-strip cotton cloth, wrappers, a smock, masks, a stool and brass bridles.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Dyula cloth is often made from narrow cotton strips sewn together and decorated with stripes, checks, ikat and supplementary weft patterns.

- **Strip-woven cloth** — These cloths use hand-sewn narrow cotton strips in indigo, white, red, yellow and green, with ikat blocks, stripes, checks and geometric designs.
- **Woman’s wrapper** — Women’s wrappers use ten, twelve or thirteen narrow strips, often with alternating indigo and white sections and supplementary weft decoration.
- **Suruku kawa** — This woman’s wrapper uses dark indigo, white cotton, a central red stripe and white, orange and yellow supplementary weft patterns.
- **Indigo and ikat cloth** — These cloths combine dark or mid indigo, white cotton and indigo ikat blocks with checks, rectangles and central stripes.
- **Eight-strip cotton cloth** — This cloth uses eight imported cotton strips with a white warp, dark-blue and white weft, a chequerboard pattern and colored supplementary weft threads.

### Clothing & dress

The profile names women’s wrappers and a smock made from sewn cotton strips.

- **Woman’s wrapper** — The wrappers are made from narrow cotton strips and may have indigo, white, red, yellow and green yarns, fringes and sewn hems.
- **Suruku kawa wrapper** (*Suruku kawa*) — Suruku kawa is a woman’s wrapper with dark indigo, white cotton, a red stripe and supplementary weft decoration.
- **Cotton smock** — The smock is made from one cloth folded in two, with a head opening and sides sewn below the armpits.

### Architecture

Dyula houses traditionally separate men’s and women’s living spaces, with roundhouses for men and rectangular houses for women.

- **Men’s roundhouse** — Men traditionally live in roundhouses made of mud or cement.
- **Women’s rectangular house** — Women traditionally live in rectangular houses made of mud or cement.
- **Kong Friday Mosque** — The seventeenth-century Kong Friday Mosque is part of Kong’s traditional Sudano-Sahelian architecture.
- **Qur’anic school** — Kong also features a Qur’anic school.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Named objects include a carved wooden stool, brass bridles and wooden masks.

- **Carved wooden stool** — This four-legged stool has a human head projecting from the end of the seat as a handle.
- **Brass bridle** — The records list two brass bridles with leather straps.
- **Horned mask** — The museum records include wooden masks with horns.
- **Cow-head mask** — The museum records include wooden masks shaped like cow heads.
- **Human-face mask** — The museum records include wooden masks shaped as human faces with carved or painted decoration.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources do not describe Dyula jewelry or other body adornment.

## Music & performance

The sources mention dance and storytelling but do not name Dyula instruments, songs or musical occasions.

- **Quranic instruction** — Dyula religious scholars taught the Quran and its commentary, hadith and the life of Muhammad.
- **Karamogo clerical study** (*karamogo*) — A karamogo studied under one sheikh for five to thirty years before possibly becoming an imam or qadi.
- **Teaching license** (*isnad*) — After study, a karamogo could receive an isnad, or teaching license.

## Dance & theatre

Dance and storytelling are part of Dyula oral tradition, but the sources name no specific dances or theatre forms.

- **Dance and storytelling** — Dyula cultural history and devotion are expressed through oral traditions of dance and storytelling.
- **Mask traditions** — The records document horned, cow-head and human-face masks, but do not explain how they were used.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe Islamic practice, pilgrimage and religious education but give no annual festival calendar.

- **Hajj to Mecca** (*hajj*) — Several rulers and Sheikh Al-Hajj Salim Suwari undertook the hajj to Mecca.
- **Suwarian tradition** (*Suwarian*) — The Suwarian tradition emphasized Muslim learning, piety, peaceful coexistence and opposition to military jihad unless believers were threatened.
- **Clan marriage** — Young people are often encouraged to marry within their own clan.
- **Polygamy** — Polygamy continues in Dyula society despite being illegal.

## Foodways

The profile focuses on trade goods rather than Dyula cooking or named foods.

- **Kola nuts** — Kola nuts came from the south and were important trade goods.
- **Millet** — Millet was among the important goods traded by Dyula merchants.
- **Salt** — Salt was exchanged through Kong.
- **Gold** — Gold was an important trade good and was exchanged through Kong.
- **Northern desert products** — Northern desert products were exchanged through Kong.

## Oral tradition & literature

Dyula culture includes oral dance and storytelling traditions and a substantial written Islamic literary tradition.

- **Arabic manuscripts** — Arabic manuscripts associated with Dyula-Wangara Muslims were collected in Ghana.
- **Quranic commentary** (*tafsir*) — Dyula scholars produced and circulated Quranic commentary, called tafsir.
- **Kitab Ghunja** — The Isnad al-shuyukh wa’l-ulama, also called Kitab Ghunja, was compiled by al-Hajj ‘Umar ibn Abi Bakr ibn ‘Uthman al-Kabbawi al-Kanawi al-Salaghawi of Kete-Krachi.
- **Jula folktales** — Nsiirin! Nsiirin! Jula folktales from West Africa is listed as further reading.

## Language & religion

Dyula is a Mande trade language, and Dyula people have been predominantly Muslim since the thirteenth century.

- **Dyula language** (*Julakan*) — Dyula, also called Julakan, belongs to the Mande language family and is mutually intelligible with Bambara and Malinke.
- **N’Ko script** (*N’Ko*) — The N’Ko script was invented in 1949 by Solomana Kanté.
- **Mande languages** (*Manding*) — Dyula is one of the Manding languages and may be written in Latin, Arabic or N’Ko scripts.
- **Muslim life** — Dyula have been predominantly Muslim since the thirteenth century.
- **Animistic traditions** — In rural areas, Islamic beliefs may be combined with beliefs in spirits and the use of amulets.
