## Overview

The Shona, also formerly known as the Karanga, are a Bantu people of Southern Africa, concentrated on the Zimbabwe plateau where they make up at least 70% of the country's population, with further communities in Mozambique, South Africa and a worldwide diaspora. Their estimated population is 22.6 million. They speak *chiShona*, a Bantu language of Guthrie's zone S.10, and are grouped conventionally by dialect cluster: Korekore in the north, Zezuru in the centre around Harare, Manyika in the east around Mutare, Buhera and Nyanga, Ndau in the southeast around Chimanimani and Chipinge, Karanga in the south-centre around Masvingo, and Kalanga in the southwest. They reached the plateau from the Congo River basin at the end of the first millennium. In folk-culture terms they matter for the drystone architecture of Great Zimbabwe, for *mbira* music, for decorated earthenware, for divining tablets, and for the twentieth-century stone-sculpture movement.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources document textiles chiefly through the museum objects rather than through named weaving traditions.

**Bark-fibre cloth** — Women's dress in the collection includes a rectangular strip of bark fibre woven in a pattern of saltires with a long fringe hanging from one of the longer sides, and a cap made of barkcloth fibre. Traditional clothing of animal skins later gave way to cloth once the Shona began trading for it with other groups such as the Tsonga, after which native cloths began to be manufactured.

**Woven vegetable-string cloth** — A piece of black cloth in the collection is woven from vegetable string with an unwoven pattern of zigzags and lozenges — the pattern produced by leaving areas unwoven rather than by added colour.

**Grass plaiting** — A cap of woven grass carries a zigzag pattern; leg ornaments are built from small rattles each made of woven strips of leaf, attached at both ends to two parallel fibre cords.

**Beadwork** — Girdles are strung from glass beads: one of three strings with alternating pink/red and white beads grouped in fours to form a quatrefoil or cross, another of seven strands of very small alternating red, black, pink and white beads. A bag is worked in multicoloured glass beads in a zigzag pattern.

**Spinning** — A spindle in the collection has a shaft of yellow jointed cane and a pottery whorl, still wound with cotton thread.

**Motif vocabulary.** Zigzag; lozenge; saltire (diagonal cross); quatrefoil or cross formed by grouped beads; triangle, in both incised and painted form; diamond; concentric circle; incised diagonal lines set in triangular patches.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional dress was animal skin covering front and back, the two pieces called *Mhapa* and *Shashiko*. Skin garments in the collection include a kaross, an apron of tanned leopard skin. Later dress used traded and then locally manufactured cloth. Women's garments recorded in the museum material include a dress made from a rectangular strip of bark fibre woven with saltires and fringed along one long edge, and girdles of glass beads worn in multiple strands. One woman's girdle is functional as well as ornamental: it carries skin scrapers and medicine phials attached to it. Headwear is represented by two caps, one of woven grass in a zigzag pattern and one of barkcloth fibre. For the limbs there are armlets of spirally twisted brass and copper wire, bangles of twisted brass wire, ear ornaments of brass wire, and leg ornaments of small woven-leaf rattles strung between two fibre cords — worn dress and dance equipment at once.

### Architecture

Traditional Shona housing, *Musha*, consists of round huts arranged around a cleared yard, the *Ruvanze*, each hut assigned a specific function such as kitchen or lounge. Beyond the homestead, Shona architecture includes drystone walling whose lineage runs back to the ancestors of the modern Shona and of the Kalanga and Venda. This tradition comprises drystone walls, drystone-walled stairs on hilltops and free-standing walls of the Great Zimbabwe type, with Great Zimbabwe and Chisvingo as examples. Great Zimbabwe, dated to about 1250–1450 AD, was a stone-walled town built of local granite and laid without mortar; excavation of its eastern enclosure produced six soapstone birds and a soapstone bowl. In the Rozvi state the Shona also built platform-terrace drystone walling. Zimbabwe's eastern province has ziwa-type drystone walling, with cattle kraals for an indigenous African cattle species that still exists, and underground homes with drystone-walled verandas. Construction at Great Zimbabwe, capital of the kingdom of Zimbabwe, began in the eleventh century when the Karanga formed kingdoms on the plateau.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Shona clay earthenware pots are *Hari*; storage and serving pots are the most decorative, in contrast to cooking pots. Museum examples show the decorative logic: a large buff earthenware pot with a spherical body blackened with plumbago and bearing red triangular patterns, its neck 8-shaped in section with a cross-piece at the mouth; another large buff pot whose incurved cylindrical neck narrows abruptly to a smaller mouth, patterned in plumbago and red; a circular burnished vessel with incised zigzags and red and burnished black graphite designs; black vessels with geometric designs, one with four relief projections. Ironwork in the collection includes daggers and knives with incised line or zigzag designs on wooden handles, a long sword or knife with a central ridge and a notched wooden hilt with conical pommel, and axes with wooden shafts, one handle carrying an incised triangle design. Precolonial Shona states drew substantial revenue from exporting mining products, particularly gold and copper. Wooden and horn objects include headrests with concentric-circle designs, combs, carved bowls with pointed handles or incised diamond borders, a carved spoon, combined knife-spoons in brass and in iron, grease holders bound with brass and copper wire, a decorated long-necked gourd dipper, and snuff containers of horn, ivory, wood and gourd, several bound or covered with brass wire.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment in the collection is dominated by drawn wire and glass beads. Armlets are made of spirally twisted brass and copper wire; bangles of twisted brass wire; ear ornaments of brass wire; and a pin is formed from brass wire with a spiral head. A cymbal-shaped disc pendant of brass hangs from a leather thong. Beaded girdles are worn in several strands, one of three strings alternating pink/red and white beads grouped into quatrefoils, another of seven strands of very small red, black, pink and white beads. Ivory is worked into a snuff-flask bound with brass wire, into clapper plaques and into a medicine phial with an incised square design, its beads of horn and glass.

## Music & performance

The two central instruments of Shona traditional music are the *ngoma* drum and the *mbira*. Drums vary in size and shape with the music they accompany and are played accordingly: large drums with sticks, smaller ones with an open palm, while the small drum of the *Amabhiza* dance is played with a hand and a stick, the stick rubbing or scratching the head to produce a screeching sound. Named drum pairs divide lead from response: *Shauro* and *Tsinhiro* for **Mhande**, *Mhito* and *Mitumba miviri* for **Dinhe**, and the two-headed *Chimudumbana* and *Chigubha* for **Chokoto**. The mbira has become something of a national instrument of Zimbabwe, with variants including the *Nhare*, *Mbira Dzavadzimu*, *Mbira Nyunga Nyunga*, *Njari* and *Matepe*; the 22–24-key mbira dzavadzimu is used to summon spirits, while the 15-key nyunga nyunga is taught from primary school to university. Mbira playing is built from two interlocking parts: the *kushaura* lead, whose player usually also sings, choosing a known melody or pattern under a phrase of text that is then commented on improvisationally, and the *kutsinira* second part, which interlocks to produce the repeated notes characteristic of the music, sometimes duplicating the kushaura one pulse behind. A further singer plays the *hosho*, a hollowed maranka gourd rattle containing hota seeds, and answers and embellishes the lead vocal. Other percussion includes the *marimba*, leg rattles, wooden clappers (*Makwa*) and the *Chikorodzi*, a notched stick played with another stick. Performer and audience are barely distinguished: both take part in the music-making. Ancient Shona music — multiple mbira dzavadzimu with hosho and ngoma — is played mainly at the *bira* ceremony. Museum holdings include two *sansa* thumb pianos, ivory clappers, seed-pod and double-gourd rattles, and a cane-framed rattle enclosing black spherical seeds between covers of thick corn stems.

## Dance & theatre

Drums are always associated with dance and serve various dances; music is very often accompanied by dancing with audience participation. The sources name **Amabhiza**, whose small drum is struck with hand and stick, and the drum-pair traditions **Mhande**, **Dinhe** and **Chokoto**. Leg ornaments of small woven-leaf rattles strung between parallel fibre cords belong to this performance apparatus.

## Festivals & rituals

The *Bira* ceremony, which often lasts all night, summons spirits for guidance and intercession; it is the principal context for ancient Shona music played on multiple mbira dzavadzimu with hosho and ngoma. Rain-making ceremonies, *Umbuyiso* and *Ukuthethela* are named among Zimbabwean indigenous religious ceremonies. Ritual is anchored in place as well as occasion: sacred places where spirits dwell and where the sacred practitioner communicates with the ancestors include hills, caves, rivers, trees and mountains — Njelele, Gulabahwe Cave, Silozwana caves, Diana's pool and Chinhoyi Caves among them. Death is understood not as loss but as promotion to a stage from which the dead represent the living through the clan spirits; the ancestral spirits, *Dzavadzimu*, are the only ones able to communicate with both the living and God, and are turned to in hard times as protectors of people and land, intermediaries, and guides to proper moral behaviour. Divination is well represented in the museum material: sets of four wooden divining tablets with incised line-and-dot or geometric designs, tied or strapped together; a single ivory tablet with an incised diamond design and another with incised geometric lines bound by leather string; and a set composed of three oblong brown nutshells each split into halves and strung on a leather thong. Marriage is regulated by the *Mitupo* clan-emblem system, which helps avoid incest and ensures people marry someone they know, expressed in the proverb *Rooranai Vematongo*.

## Foodways

Sorghum and maize are the grains behind the staple dish, a thickened porridge called *Sadza*, and the traditional beer *Hwahwa*. The Shona have traditionally practised subsistence agriculture, growing sorghum, beans and African groundnuts, with pumpkins added after the Columbian Exchange; sorghum was largely replaced by maize once that crop arrived. Cattle and goats are kept, livestock being an important food reserve in drought. Beef is a staple, given how prominent cattle rearing is in Zimbabwe, but historically its distribution was stratified: wealthy royals ate beef more than three times a week, usually dried, while commoners ate it at least once a week, also dried. Cattle were a prized resource normally reserved for other products such as milk, and preserved milk was eaten with sadza, then made from sorghum. Today beef is usually cooked mixed with greens, kale being the most common. The decorative hierarchy of Shona pottery follows use — storage and serving *Hari* are the decorated ones, cooking pots are not — and the museum material includes the associated utensils: carved wooden bowls, a carved spoon with a hollowed oval handle, combined knife-spoons in brass and iron, a long-necked gourd dipper decorated with black triangles and zigzag lines, and grease holders bound with brass and copper wire. Snuff is served from its own equipment: flasks and bottles of ivory, horn, wood and gourd, several wire-bound, with stoppers on thongs.

## Oral tradition & literature

Shona indigenous religion has no scripture or sacred text; it is transmitted orally, by word of mouth from one generation to the next. The proverb is a working genre within this transmission: *Rooranai Vematongo*, "marry or have a relationship with someone that you know", carries the marriage rule of the *Mitupo* system. Mbira song is itself improvisational verbal art — the kushaura player selects a known melody under a phrase or a few lines of text, which is then commented upon as the performance proceeds. Historical spirit mediums remembered by name include Mbuya Nehanda, Sekuru Kaguvi, Sekemutema and Kawanzaruwa. In written literature, chiShona was codified as a written standard in the early twentieth century on the recommendation of the South African linguist Clement Doke and fixed in the 1950s; Solomon Mutswairo's *Feso*, the first novel in Shona, appeared in 1957, and hundreds of novels, short-story collections and poetry volumes have followed. Shona is taught in schools, though after the first few grades it is not the medium of instruction for subjects beyond Shona grammar and literature.

## Language & religion

*ChiShona* is a Bantu language of Guthrie's zone S.10, spoken in Zimbabwe and Mozambique; the broader Shonic group includes Ndau (Eastern Shona) and Kalanga (Western Shona), and by the wider definition the language has over 14 million speakers. Standard Shona is based on Zezuru. The orthography is phonemic, the syllable structure open, the vowel system a simple five (a, e, i, o, u), and two tones — high and low — go unmarked in writing. Its whistled sibilants are the fricatives *sv* and *zv* and the affricates *tsv* and *dzv*. From 1931 to 1955 Unified Shona used Doke's special characters, later replaced by Latin digraphs. Traditional religion centres on *Mwari*, also called *Musikavanhu* (creator of people) or *Nyadenga* (one who lives high up), reached directly in prayer, through holy family members with powers to prophesy, heal and bless, or through the ancestral *Dzavadzimu*; life is guided by *Unhu*. Sixty to eighty per cent of Shona follow Christianity while these beliefs persist, with small Muslim and Jewish populations.
