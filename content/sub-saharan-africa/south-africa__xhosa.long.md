## Overview

The Xhosa (*amaXhosa*) are the second largest ethnic group in South Africa, numbering over ten million Xhosa-speaking people across Southern Africa, with the heaviest concentration in the Eastern Cape — the province formed in 1994 when the Transkei and Ciskei bantustans were folded back into the country. Their historic heartland lies on the mountain slopes of the Amatola and Winterberg ranges and in the basins of the Kei and Great Fish Rivers, where rainfall and soils supported the cattle herds that remain the axis of Xhosa wealth and ritual. Large communities also live in Cape Town, Gauteng and the Free State, and a Mfengu-descended Xhosa-speaking population in Zimbabwe retains isiXhosa as a recognised national language. Archaeological evidence places Xhosa-speakers in the Eastern Cape since at least the seventh century. For folk culture they matter above all for beadwork, ochred blanket dress, and a ritual vocabulary of cattle, ancestors and initiation that survives intact in modern urban life.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Umbhaco / isikhakha** — The defining Xhosa cloth: a heavy white or cream cotton skirt-and-wrap material, ochred to varying intensity and built up in horizontal registers of black braid, buttons and beaded panels. *Isikhakha* names the woman's wrapped skirt, *umbhaco* the broader cloth and costume complex. What separates it from neighbouring Nguni dress is the banded, ruled geometry of applied trim rather than woven pattern — the pattern is sewn on, not woven in.

**Xhosa blanket (ingcawa)** — The *ingcawa* is a white-and-black blanket worn draped over the shoulder by men, embellished through *ukurhaswa*, the beading technique by which beads are worked directly onto blanket cloth. The wider "red blanket people" tradition — ochre-dyed blankets whose red intensity varies from chiefdom to chiefdom — makes the blanket itself an identifying marker between Gcaleka, Thembu, Mpondo and Bhaca.

**Incebetha** — A small blanket worn as a breast-cloth, pinned in place and finished with beads through *uRhaswa*. Together with *ifulu*, an under-garment blanket worn below the belt, it forms the layered foundation beneath the *isikhakha*.

**Iqhiya** — The headcloth: fitted to the head and covered with beadwork. Every Xhosa woman covers her head as a mark of respect to the head of the household; elderly women are entitled to the most elaborate constructions, so the headdress reads as a direct index of seniority. *Uxakatha*, a light blanket, is worn at the waist with it.

**Beaded regalia panels (Thembu, Mfengu, Bhaca, Mpondo)** — Each cluster keeps its own palette. Thembu initiate regalia after circumcision runs dominantly white and navy blue with yellow for fertility and green for new life; Mpondo *inkciyo*, the beaded pubic apron worn at virginity-testing ceremonies, is turquoise and white. These colour codes are how the sub-groups are told apart at a glance.

**Xhosa doll** — Cloth-and-bead figures dressed in miniature *umbhaco*, headcloth and collar, made both as children's objects and as fertility and teaching pieces; museum collections hold large numbers, since the doll compresses an entire dress code into one object.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Chevron* (zigzag bands across collars and belts); *triangle*; *diamond* and *quadrangle* (lozenge fields, often age-grade specific); *circle*; *parallel lines* (ruled registers, the commonest structural device); *tree*; white grounds (purity, mediation, ancestral offering); navy-and-white Thembu banding; yellow (fertility); green (new life); turquoise-and-white Mpondo pairing.

### Clothing & dress

Dress encodes social standing and stage of life. Unmarried women wear wraps tied at the shoulder leaving the breasts exposed; engaged women redden their plaited hair and let it screen the eyes, a deliberate deference to the fiancé. Married women wear the *isikhakha* over *ifulu*, the *incebetha* breast-cloth, the *iqhiya* headcloth and the waist blanket *uxakatha*, with *intsimbi* or *amaso* — beaded bands on flexible wire — around ankles and waist, *imitsheke* at the wrist, and a small bag, *ingxowa*. Gcaleka women encase arms and legs in beads and brass bangles.

Men's dress descends from herding, hunting and warfare and uses hide: the goatskin bag skinned off in one piece, cured with the hair on and turned inside out, carries tobacco and knife. Over it goes the *ingcawa* blanket. Necklaces are graded — *isichebe* the short collar, *isidanga* the long multicoloured strand — with *amaso* at wrist and foot and *unngqa* or *igwala* on the head. For weddings and initiation men wear embroidered skirts with a rectangular cloth over the left shoulder, or a tunic with beaded strands. The long-stemmed pipe, *umbheka phesheya* ("look across the sea"), is beaded in the same *ukurhaswa* technique.

### Architecture

The Xhosa homestead, the *umzi*, is organised around the cattle byre, *inkundla* — the physical and jural centre of the household, where marriage negotiators are received and where they wait, excluded from the house, until the bride's family acknowledges them. Dwellings are round thatched houses on a timber-and-wattle armature with daubed walls, historically the beehive-framed Nguni form and later the cylindrical *rondavel* with a conical thatch. Interiors are lime-washed and frequently painted in broad bands and blocks of colour around door and window openings, echoing the register logic of the dress.

One interior space carries explicit ritual weight: the *indlu enkulu*, the main bedroom, which is emptied of furniture at a death so that extended family can be seated on the floor on *ukhukho* grass mats or mattresses to pay last respects. Homesteads are dispersed across hillsides rather than nucleated, each with its byre, granary pits and garden plots — a settlement pattern shaped by grazing rather than by street frontage.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery, woodwork, weaving and beadwork are the four crafts named in the Xhosa repertoire. Potters raise unglazed burnished vessels by hand for beer and milk; the beer pot for *umqombothi* is a household ritual object, since beer is poured on the ground of the bride's homestead to thank the ancestors. Woodwork produces milk pails, meat platters, headrests, spoons and the knobkerrie (*iwisa*), the club carried as weapon and status stick, alongside the *assegai* spear brought south by the migrating Nguni. Grass and palm coiling yields lidded baskets and beer strainers as well as the *ukhukho* floor mat. The snuff spoon — bone, horn or metal, often worn in the hair or on a beaded cord — and the beaded pipe complete the smoking and snuff-taking kit. Brass bangles, wire-wound arm and leg rings and buttons used as appliqué carry most of the metalwork.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Beads are the primary adornment and the primary information system. Before glass, Xhosa people obtained ostrich-eggshell beads from the San by trade — hemp exchanged for discs chipped, bored, polished and strung on sinew, scarce and highly valued; Portuguese glass beads arrived through trade in the 1930s. Beadwork fixes age, gender, age-grade, marital status, rank, role and spiritual state, and it links the living to the ancestors, which is why diviners (*amagqirha*) use white beads when communicating with them. Diviner regalia includes beaded spears associated with the inspiring ancestor, beaded horns, calabashes for medicine and snuff, and *amageza*, a beaded veil swayed before a person's eyes to induce trance. *Impempe*, a whistle strung on a necklace, marks entry into adolescence. Brass bangles, wire coils and beaded collars complete the assemblage; a diviner's hair is worn long and matted, while mourners shave their heads after burial as a sign that life continues to spring up after death.

## Music & performance

Xhosa music rests on drums, rattles, whistles, flutes, mouth harps and stringed instruments, but its centre is group singing with handclapping, organised around overlapping call-and-response entries rather than a single melodic line. The characteristic solo instruments are the mouth-resonated musical bows — the *uhadi* gourd bow, struck with a light stick, and the mouth bow *umrhubhe*, bowed and played with the lips selecting harmonics above a fundamental — whose overtone technique underlies the distinctive Xhosa vocal style in which a singer reinforces the partials of a drone. Women are the principal bow players and the principal composers of the songs that accompany work, beer-drinking and ritual.

There is a song for nearly every ritual occasion. The best-known is **Qongqothwane**, a wedding song about the knocking beetle, carried worldwide by Miriam Makeba as "Click Song #1" — its fame rests on the clicks isiXhosa took from Khoisan contact. Nineteenth-century missionaries introduced four-part Western choral singing, which was absorbed rather than displaced: **Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika**, written in isiXhosa by Enoch Sontonga in 1897 and now part of South Africa's national anthem, is the emblem of that hybrid, and the *makwaya* choral movement it founded remains a mass participatory tradition. Diviners' initiation ceremonies, *intlombe*, combine drumming, clapping and dance over long night-long sessions.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is embedded in ritual rather than staged. Diviner ceremonies (*intlombe*) turn on possession dancing by *amagqirha* over hard-driven clapping and drumming; the dance is the means by which the ancestor is made to speak, not an accompaniment to it. Initiation, marriage and beer-drink gatherings each have their own step and stamping patterns, with men's dancing built on the stamped, weapon-carrying vocabulary shared across the Nguni and women's on a lower, torso-driven idiom with clapping.

Formal theatre is a modern development, and isiXhosa has become one of its principal languages: the first plays in Xhosa appeared in the nineteenth century, and *U-Carmen eKhayelitsha*, shot entirely in isiXhosa in the Cape Town township of Khayelitsha, reworks Bizet's 1875 opera with African music. Xhosa also reached global audiences as a language of the film *Black Panther*.

## Festivals & rituals

The Xhosa ritual calendar is organised by the life cycle and by the ancestors rather than by a fixed annual festival. **Ulwaluko**, male initiation, is the decisive rite: the initiate (*umkhwetha*) is secluded, circumcised, smeared with white clay and emerges to be washed, ochred and dressed in new blanket and beadwork, a transition without which a man is not socially adult. Girls' rites include *intonjane* seclusion and virginity-testing ceremonies at which the beaded *inkciyo* apron is worn.

**Umtshato**, marriage, proceeds through named stages: *ukuthwalwa*, the declaration of intent (historically bridal abduction, today usually following courtship); the checking of *isiduko*, clan names, since two people of the same clan name are considered kin and may not marry; *ikhazi*, the *lobola* negotiation conducted by appointed negotiators kept in the *inkundla* until accepted, with the bride-price paid in cattle or, in the cities, money; and *ukuyalwa*, in which the bride is instructed by the elders on conduct and dress and given a new name by the women of the groom's family. Livestock is slaughtered and beer poured for the ancestors; *umqombothi* is brewed by the bride's family in thanks.

Death has its own sequence. **Umkhapho**, "to accompany," is performed by the male clan leader to escort the spirit to the ancestors and to keep the bond alive so the deceased can return and speak — a cow for a family head, an unblemished goat for others. The *indlu enkulu* is emptied for mourners, who need no invitation. **Ukuxukuxa**, cleansing, follows on the day of burial with a further slaughter, and the day after, the women wash the deceased's blankets at the river, the clothes are removed from the house, and the family shaves its hair.

## Foodways

The staple is maize, eaten in a family of preparations distinguished by texture. **Umngqusho** — white maize cooked with sugar beans — is the Xhosa staple dish proper. *Umphokoqo* is crumbly dry pap, eaten as *umvubo* when mixed with *amasi*, the fermented sour milk that is the other pillar of the diet. *Isidudu* is soft mealie-meal porridge taken at breakfast with sugar and milk; *iinkobe*, peeled boiled maize grains, is a salted snack; *umcuku* mixes the sour fermented porridge *amarhewu* with dry pap. *Umfino* folds wild spinach into mealie meal, and *umqa* does the same with pumpkin. Bread is *umbhako*, a round loaf baked in a pot.

Meat is beef (*inyama yenkomo*), mutton (*inyama yegusha*) and goat (*inyama yebhokwe*), eaten mainly at ritual slaughters; *umleqwa* is free-range chicken. Pumpkins (*amathanga*) appear as *amaceba*, unpeeled slices boiled in plenty of water, and as *umxoxozi*, cooked before full ripeness; *intyabontyi* is a white-fleshed citron melon eaten raw or cooked. Beans are *iimbotyi*, and *isophi* is a corn-and-bean or corn-and-pea soup. Gathered foods include *rhabe*, a sorrel-like wild spinach, *imvomvo*, the sweet sap of an aloe, and *ikhowa*, a mushroom that fruits after the summer rains. **Umqombothi**, beer fermented from maize and sorghum, is both everyday drink and the medium of ancestral offering. Funerals were once catered with *iinkobe* drawn from family reserves and donated by neighbours; in the twenty-first century that is considered shameful, and funeral catering has become an industry.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral performance centres on *iimbongi*, praise poets, who deliver *izibongo* — praise poems naming a chief's lineage, deeds and clan, improvised at high speed and in an archaic register, and licensed to criticise as well as to praise. Clan praises (*iziduko*) are recited as personal identity: to give one's *isiduko* is to state who one is and whom one may not marry. Alongside these run *iintsomi*, the folktale genre told by older women to children in the evening, built on sung refrains the audience joins; the *iintsomi* repertoire includes the cannibal-ogre and trickster-hare cycles shared across the Nguni world.

Written isiXhosa dates from the nineteenth century, with the first newspapers, novels and plays, and a literary line running through S.E.K. Mqhayi, whose praise poetry bridged the oral and printed forms, and the poet J.J.R. Jolobe. Rhodes University in Makhanda teaches isiXhosa to mother-tongue and second-language students with a cultural-studies component, and Russell H. Kaschula has published extensively on Xhosa oral literature and the modern *imbongi*.

## Language & religion

IsiXhosa is an agglutinative, tonal Bantu language of the Nguni group, written in a Latin-based orthography and spoken by about 18% of South Africans. It is partly mutually intelligible with Zulu, especially urban Zulu, and is notable for the three click series — dental *c*, lateral *x*, palatal *q* — absorbed through long contact with Khoisan speakers, whose communities the Xhosa polity incorporated as it expanded across the Cape. Many speakers are also fluent in Zulu, Afrikaans or English.

Religion is overwhelmingly Christian, and thoroughly interleaved with ancestral practice: the ancestors (*izinyanya*) are addressed through slaughter, beer and the *umkhapho* rite regardless of church membership, and the diviner (*igqirha*) is called to the vocation by ancestral affliction and trained through the *intlombe* apprenticeship. Herbalists (*amaxhwele*) handle materia medica; the African Independent Churches, strong in the Eastern Cape, formalise the blend.
