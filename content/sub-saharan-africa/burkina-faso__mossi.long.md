## Overview

The Mossi, or Mosi, are a Gur ethnic group native to modern Burkina Faso, primarily in the Volta River basin. They constitute 52% of Burkina Faso’s population, or about 11.1 million people in 2010; significant numbers also live in Benin, Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana, Mali, and Togo. The Mossi speak Mooré, also known as Mossi, a Gur language of the Oti–Volta branch and part of the Niger–Congo family. Their society is organized hierarchically around family and state, with the Mogho Naaba as the highest political position, the Nakomse as a ruling class, and the Tengabisi as descendants of earlier farming peoples. Their masks, dolls, royal figures, textiles, and ceremonies are central subjects of the available material.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Cotton shawl** — A man’s prestige garment made of cotton, woven in thirteen narrow strips and hand sewn together. The warp and weft are hand spun and dyed indigo throughout; supplementary weft stripes and twisted warp-thread fringes decorate both ends.

**Indigo cotton textile** — A cotton textile from Ouagadougou shows stages of plain weaving and indigo dyeing. The catalogue records that it was knotted and dyed by a Mossi woman.

The sources used do not document additional Mossi pattern-bearing textile traditions.

### Clothing & dress

The sources document a man’s prestige garment described as a cotton shawl. It is made from thirteen narrow strips sewn together, with hand-spun cotton warp and weft dyed indigo throughout, supplementary weft stripes along the borders, and twisted warp-thread fringes at both ends. The sources do not describe everyday clothing, women’s clothing, head coverings, belts, footwear, or named ceremonial dress beyond this garment.

**Motif vocabulary.** Supplementary weft stripes; incised geometric designs on a wooden cap mask.

### Architecture

The sources do not describe Mossi house forms, compounds, roofs, walls, or urban architecture. Museum records include a weathered wooden house-post and a wooden tethering post, possibly for a chief’s house, surmounted by a carved human head with iron nails driven around the neck.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum records include an iron-headed hammer-club with a wooden shaft, a leather pull-out pouch with red fringes and stamped ornament, and a wooden tethering post possibly associated with a chief’s house. Other recorded objects include a bronze dancing figure holding a fan, an antelope figure made of metal, a wooden female figure with incised line patterns and a small iron band around the wrist, and a metal figure of a Fulani woman with a child at a well. The sources do not describe Mossi ceramics.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources do not provide a general account of Mossi jewelry, hair practices, tattoos, or ritual body adornment. A museum description of a Mossi mask records a female figure with scar tattoos on the body and face, while the Mossi doll entry mentions decoration with beads, pearls, shells, fabric, metal earrings, pieces of bone, or animal hide.

## Music & performance

The sources do not identify Mossi musical instruments, song genres, or a specific musical tradition. They do document performance in ritual and public contexts. Masks appear at burials, funerals, memorial services, and annual public festivals, and museum records state that a *wan-balinga* mask was worn and danced with at the funeral of a male or female elder and during annual memorial services.

The Friday Mogho Naaba court ceremony is connected with Mossi political history and the authority of the royal court. Its narrative concerns the Moro Naba’s sister fleeing north to Yadega, the kingdom called Yatenga, while carrying the amulets of power, or *nam*. The Moro Naba mounted a white horse three times and returned to the palace each time. The sources also state that bridles, saddles, stirrups, and other horse-related objects are important to the Nakomse, while carved wooden figures represent royal ancestors.

## Dance & theatre

Mossi mask performance is associated with funerals, memorial services, religious observances, rituals, and public festivals. The Nyonyose, described as the ancient farmers and spiritual segment of Mossi society, use masks, while the Nakomse, or chief class, do not. Masks accompany the deceased, observe whether proper funeral procedures are carried out, honor the dead, and verify whether the spirit merits admission into the world of ancestors.

A museum catalogue identifies the proper name of one mask as *wan-balinga*, a mythical figure who was the mother of the first Mossi ruler. The sources also record masks with elongated projections, horns, geometric designs, and animal forms. They do not describe a Mossi theatre tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

Ceremonies and celebrations pace Mossi life and express joy or suffering, as well as duties to the memory of ancestors. The Friday Mogho Naaba court ceremony recalls the flight of the Moro Naba’s sister to Yatenga with the *nam*, or amulets of power. The Moro Naba’s repeated departures on a white horse and returns to the palace explain why the kings of Yatenga claim to hold the power of Mossi rule.

Royal ancestors are represented by carved wooden figures displayed each year at royal festivals called *na possum*. At these festivals, the heads of households reaffirm allegiance and loyalty to the chief. The sources also name the International Art & Craft Fair, or Salon international de l’Artisanat de Ouagadougou, Week of the Culture, and the Atypical Nights of Koudougou as annual public festivals where masks from different Mossi regions appear.

Funeral and memorial practices are especially important. Masks appear at burials and at funeral or memorial services held at regular intervals during the years after an elder’s death. Without a proper funeral, the spirit is believed to remain near the home and cause trouble for descendants. The sources also describe a girl’s doll being placed in a hut after her excision ceremony and given to her younger sister on her wedding night.

## Oral tradition & literature

Mossi history has largely been preserved through oral tradition, although Mossi records also exist and were primarily written using the Ajami script. Oral tradition tells of the marriage of Yennenga, a Mamprusi/Dagomba princess and warrior princess, and Rialé, a solitary Mandé hunter. Their son, Ouedraogo, is recognized as the father of the Mossi people.

The sources state that Yennenga was the daughter of Naa Gbewaa, a king of present-day northern Ghana. While exploring her kingdom on horseback, she lost her way and was rescued by Rialé. Gbewaa’s tomb is located in Pusiga in Ghana’s Upper East Region. The sources also mention *Contes du Larhallé*, followed by a collection of proverbs and devices of the Mossi country, as further reading.

## Language & religion

Mooré, also known as Mossi, is a Gur language of the Oti–Volta branch and one of the four official languages of Burkina Faso. It is spoken in Burkina Faso, Ghana, Ivory Coast, Benin, Niger, Mali, Togo, and Senegal, and is the main language in Ouagadougou. The language is closely related to Frafra and less closely related to Dagbani. Regional dialects are described for Yatenga, the northern region, Koupela, and Tenkodogo, though they are mutually intelligible. Mooré can be written with the national Burkinabé alphabet or the Goulsse alphabet.

Mossi spiritual life includes beliefs concerning ancestors, land, and ritual obligation. Ancestors are believed to influence life on earth and to help or punish descendants. Land is understood as a path to the ancestors and has a spiritual dimension. Masks are sacred links to the spirits of ancestors and nature. The sources do not provide a general account of contemporary religious affiliation.
