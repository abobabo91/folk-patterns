## Overview

This gallery follows Fon-attributed objects rather than treating the old kingdom of Dahomey as a complete portrait of present-day Fon life. The selected records span court insignia, ancestor remembrance, pottery, jewelry, musical instruments, and sculpture. Their museum labels and collecting histories vary; an object bought at an Abomey market was not necessarily made there.

## Material culture

The Met identifies *asen* as portable metal altars placed in family or royal shrine houses. People used them to address and remember the dead; their figures and symbols could refer to a particular person's life. One selected royal *asen* came from the Abomey court, while another was made for remembrance in Ouidah. The British Museum's Fon-attributed pottery includes a red vessel recorded for a royal ancestor cult and a two-headed serpent vessel recorded for the cult of Dã. These specific catalogue notes support those uses, not a single purpose for every pot.

Royal and ceremonial objects take several forms. A bird-headed *récade* in the Toulouse museum is explicitly identified as Fon and described as a sign of the ruler's authority. The British Museum's appliqué banner depicts a Dahomean victory in the reign of King Glélé. Its catalogue calls this particular cloth an early twentieth-century replica made by the traditional Yémadjé makers in Abomey, so it should not be mistaken for a banner carried in that battle. The selected metal figures, beaded ornaments, gongs, drum, and rattle staffs show other forms without assigning them all to the court.

Four separately reviewed Commons photographs show a Fon *récade*, an apron labeled Fon by its museum, a Vodun figure identified as Fon, and a royal or noble costume attributed to Fon by the Brooklyn Museum. They are photographs of objects, not a record of one contemporary performance.
