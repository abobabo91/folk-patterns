## Overview

The Tlokwa, also called Batlôkwa, Batlokoa, or Badogwa, are several Kgatla communities living in Botswana, Lesotho, and South Africa. In South Africa they occur in North West, Gauteng, Limpopo, the Free State, KwaZulu-Natal, and the Eastern Cape. Their communities include settlements near the Caledon River, the Tshwane region, the Nqutu Municipality, and areas around Polokwane, Potchefstroom, and the Herschel and Mount Fletcher districts. No census has established how many Batlôkwa there are. They form part of the Bakgatla and the wider Bantu-speaking Tswana peoples, commonly classified within the Sotho-Tswana grouping. Their folk-cultural significance lies in their royal traditions, clan histories, totems, customary law, settlement forms, language practices, and the continuing importance of regional Batlôkwa identities.

### Clothing & dress

The sources mention *mekgatlha*, a style of skin cloak associated with Sotho-Tswana peoples. The source also records that, in a Tlokwa tradition from Botswana, animal skins were used to keep people warm when blankets were unavailable. This account is connected with the naming of Taukobong. No documented distinction between men’s and women’s clothing, head coverings, belts, footwear, or other forms of everyday or ceremonial dress is supplied.

### Architecture

A characteristic architectural form associated with the Sotho-Tswana grouping is a round hut with a conical thatch roof supported by wooden pillars on the outside. The sources also describe dense and closely settled villages, larger than those of “Nguni” peoples, and a tradition of building in stone in regions that are less grassy or wooded. These features are presented as commonalities among related Sotho-Tswana groups rather than as an exclusively Tlokwa architectural style. No further information is supplied about house decoration, interior arrangements, courtyards, workshops, or named Tlokwa building types.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe several customs and historical practices connected with identity and social organization. The Tlokwa consider the Tlokwe-cat their original totem; the source says that this animal became extinct through over-hunting for its fur, which was used by clan chiefs. The Batlôkwa in Botswana are described as distinctive because their totem is the *thakadu*, or ant-bear. According to the account, this change followed a period in the wilderness when the people found water in holes dug by thakadu and decided that nobody should harm the animal.

The same account connects the birth of Marakadu with water obtained from one of these holes. Marakadu was said to have been named after the thakadu, described as the saviour, and the Batlôkwa then agreed to change their totem from *nkwe* to *thakadu*. The source also explains the names Mosima, Motlhabane, Mokgwa, and Taukobong through events in this genealogical narrative. These accounts concern clan memory, ancestry, and totemic identity rather than a documented festival cycle.

Customary law remains important in rural areas, including the distinctive practice of marrying paternal cousins.

## Foodways

They do record food-related episodes in historical narratives. During the period associated with Manthatisi’s military movements, the Tlôkwa army was described as living frugally because warfare and plundering had caused severe scarcity. Roots, bulbs, and berries are named as foods available in the veld, though the source states that these were insufficient to feed the large group.

The Botswana origin account says that Batlôkwa found water in holes dug by *thakadu* when they were thirsty and hungry. The same narrative explains that animal skins were used for warmth when blankets were unavailable. These details concern historical and genealogical traditions rather than a documented cuisine. No information is supplied about grain preparation, meat dishes, drinks, tea, coffee, dietary rules, or food served at life-cycle ceremonies.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tlokwa identity is strongly represented in genealogical and historical traditions. The Batlôkwa trace royal lineages to Kgwadi, son of King Tabane, who is described as the father and founder of the Batlokwa nation. The source records the division of Tabane’s descendants into groups associated with Bapedi, Makgolokwe, Batlôkwa, Maphuthing, and Basia. It also preserves genealogical accounts involving Mokotjo, Sekonyela, Manthatisi, Marakadu, Mosima, Motlhabane, Mokgwa, Taukobong, Makaba, Molefe, Bogatsu, Phiri, and Semele.

The history of Manthatisi and Sekonyela is presented through narratives of warfare, migration, regency, rivalry, and political change during the Mfecane or Difaqane. The sources also explain that English-language accounts called the Tlôkwa the Mantatees after Manthatisi’s name, while Sotho-Tswana raiders were called “boo-Mmanthatisi” or “Mantatee Horde” by the English. “Leboko la Batlokwa (Batlokwa Poem)” appears as a heading, but no text or analysis is supplied.

## Language & religion

The Batlôkwa belong to the Bantu-speaking Tswana peoples and are commonly placed within the Sotho-Tswana grouping. Depending on their region, they generally speak Setswana, Sesotho, or Northern Sotho, with Sesotho described as the language spoken by most. Sesotho, Northern Sotho, and Setswana are described as largely mutually intelligible. In Lesotho, the Batlôkwa are identified as one of the three main Sotho-Tswana clans who speak Sesotho. Regional identities also include Setswana-speaking Batlôkwa in the North West and Northern Sotho-speaking Batlôkwa in Limpopo.

They do document totemic traditions involving the Tlokwe-cat and the *thakadu*, as well as customary law and clan-based ideas of descent.
