## Overview

The Kuranko, also called Koranko, Kolanko, Kooranko, Koronko, Kouranko, Kulanko, Kurako, Kuronko, Kuranké, or Karanko, are a Mandeka people from Mande who occupy a mountainous region within northeastern Sierra Leone and southern Guinea. The Kuranko language, also called Koranko, is a dialect of the Mande branch of the Niger–Congo language family. The language is spoken by approximately 350,000 Kuranko people in Sierra Leone and Guinea. Kuranko is similar to the Manding languages and can be understood by neighbouring Mandinka and Susu people. Different dialects and distinct social groupings occur across the region. Kuranko villages are led by a chief and elders, while relative isolation has helped many people retain traditional cultural and religious practices.

## Festivals & rituals

Men undergo initiation rituals on reaching puberty. The initiation consists of circumcision and training sessions, after which they become members of a secretive club and may marry. Marriage involves paying a bride price to the family of the chosen woman, and Kuranko society is described as polygamous, with some men having more than one wife.

The sources also describe the Bondo secret society, which gradually establishes attitudes related to adulthood in girls and addresses fertility, morality, and proper sexual comportment. The society maintains an interest in the well-being of its members throughout their lives.

## Foodways

The sources describe the Kuranko primarily as a hunting and trading people, with these activities exceeding farming as their primary employment.

## Language & religion

Kuranko, or Koranko, is a Mande language and a dialect of the Mande branch of the Niger–Congo language family. It is spoken in Sierra Leone and Guinea; in Guinea it blends dialectologically into Eastern Maninkakan, although the people are ethnically distinct. Kuranko is similar to the Manding languages and is understood by the Mandinka and Susu.

The Kuranko are nominally Muslim, and their lands were among the first areas of Sierra Leone to adopt Islam. The sources state that many people do not follow all the formal protocols of Islam and continue to believe in aspects of an ancient religion. Forests, rivers, and mountains are believed to contain quasi-human beings known as *Nyenne*, described as “bush spirits” that influence Kuranko life. Ancestral spirits and the natural ecosystem form the core of Kuranko spiritual life.
