## Overview

The Nyoro people, also known as Banyoro or Abanyoro, are a Bantu ethnic group native to the kingdom of Bunyoro in Uganda. They live in settlements on a well-watered and fertile plateau. The Nyoro are closely related to the Batooro, Banyankole, Bakiga and Bahema peoples. Their society has historically been organized through a kingdom ruled by a king and a hierarchy of feudal and territorial chiefs. The sources describe about 150 totemic patrilineal clans, called *ruganda*, each headed by a *mukuru w'oruganda*. Formerly pastoral, the Nyoro lost most of their herds through war and disease. Their folk-cultural significance in the sources lies chiefly in their social organization, kinship system, language, family customs, royal traditions and recorded musical associations.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state only that a son could not wear his father’s clothes while the father was alive. The sources also mention that a father could formally hand over his spear to an heir in an inheritance ceremony.

### Architecture

They state that the Nyoro live in settlements on a well-watered and fertile plateau, but provide no architectural details.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention livestock, land, clothing and a spear in connection with family property and inheritance.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They do record that in the past the Nyoro practiced dental avulsion by removing the 4 lower incisors.

## Music & performance

They do, however, identify a recording titled *Royal Court Music from Uganda*, associated with Ganda, Nyoro and Ankole music, collected by Hugh Tracey and released by Sharp Wood Productions in 1998 from recordings made in 1950-1952. Another recording listed in the sources is *Music Of Africa Series No. 8 The Uganda Protectorate*, collected and recorded by Hugh Tracey and issued by DECCA as LF1173.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe several family and inheritance practices. In a family, the father is the head and is called the *mukama*, described as the master or owner of everyone in the household and of all possessions. A son could not sell or kill livestock purchased by him without his father’s approval. Disputes between a son and his wife were taken to his father for settlement, and a son could not give corporal punishment to his wife without his father’s permission.

There was only one heir in a family. Movable property could be divided among the sons, while the household and land were given to the heir. If the son was the heir, the father formally handed over the spear in an inheritance ceremony. The heir could also inherit his late father’s widows and take them as wives, except for his own mother. A widow who did not want to be inherited could live with another kinsman of the deceased; if she married someone else, the bridewealth paid for her was to be returned.

## Foodways

The Nyoro are described as traditionally pastoral, although war and disease mostly wiped out their former herds.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do state that the Nyoro refer to people in their clan through kin terms such as “brothers,” “fathers” and “sons,” and that kin may be distinguished through genealogical proximity, generation, sex, and whether the relationship is through the mother or father.

## Language & religion

Nyoro, or *Runyoro*, also called *Orunyoro*, is a Bantu language spoken by the Nyoro people of Uganda. It has two dialects: Runyoro proper and *Rutagwenda*. A standardized orthography was established in 1947, and the language is most closely related to *Rutooro*.

The Nyoro language acquired Central Sudanic loanwords from the *Ma'di* language in the 13-15th centuries during the *Bachwezi* era, when the early expanding Banyoro community absorbed Madi people whose territory extended south of the Nile into what is now northern Bunyoro. Runyoro also borrowed Madi words for bellows, ostrich, sugarcane and donkey. Later, it received Nilotic loanwords from the *Luo* languages after the migration of the *Babiito* and *Palwo* into Bunyoro.
