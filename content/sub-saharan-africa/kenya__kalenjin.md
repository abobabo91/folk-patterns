## Overview

The Kalenjin include more than 20 Kalenjin-speaking tribes and sub-groups. Most live in Kenya’s Rift Valley. Their culture includes pastoral foodways, beadwork, age-set organization, initiation, music, dance, sacred places, and oral traditions. They are also strongly associated with long-distance running.

## Material culture

Kalenjin material culture includes beadwork, leather clothing, hunting equipment, sacred places, and remains of fortified pastoral settlements.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Kalenjin visual art is known especially for regional styles of applique beadwork.

- **Kalenjin beadwork** — Traditional beadwork uses applique, and styles differ between regions.
- **Sirikwa holes and related structures** (*Sirikwa holes*) — These remains belong to a tradition of fortified homesteads, livestock pens, and structured gates.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe special adornments and leather clothing but not a complete everyday dress system.

- **Cowrie shell adornment** — Chemwal women were associated with cowrie shell adornment, which led the Karamojong to call them Siger.
- **Leather back piece** — Men from Amudat and the West Suk wore leather back pieces linked with the Suk, Pokot, and Kalenjin.
- **Leaf headdress and body paint** — Adult men in a dance photograph wore leaf headdresses, body paint, and American shorts.

### Architecture

Sirikwa settlements focused on security and keeping herds, while sacred places were usually open-air sites.

- **Sirikwa fortified homestead** — These highland settlements had fortified homesteads, internal livestock pens, and structured gate systems.
- **Tumuli and megaliths** — Tumuli and megaliths remain as traces of the Sirikwa tradition.
- **Hilltop sacred place** — Hilltops were used for worship because the Kalenjin did not want to limit the deity to one building.
- **Family altar or prayer tree** (*Mabwaita or Korosyoot*) — Family altars or prayer trees were traditional sacred places.
- **Crossroads sacred place** (*Sach ooraan or Kimwocho*) — Crossroads were also used as sacred places.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The documented everyday objects include bows, iron-tipped arrows, and one horn snuff-flask.

- **Bow** — Bows collected at Amudat measured 160 cm and 150 cm.
- **Iron-tipped arrow** — The arrows had iron points and measured between 78 cm and 93 cm.
- **Horn snuff-flask** — A museum record lists a snuff-flask made of horn, leather, and iron wire, with a chain and lid.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The clearest named adornment is the cowrie shell decoration associated with Chemwal women.

- **Cowrie shell adornment** (*Siger*) — This adornment was associated with Chemwal women and helped produce the name Siger used by the Karamojong.
- **Leaf headdress** — Adult men wore leaf headdresses during a dance shown in a British Museum photograph.
- **Body paint** — Adult men also wore body paint during the photographed dance.

## Music & performance

Kalenjin music ranges from rare traditional instruments to guitar-based contemporary music.

- **Sukutit drum** (*sukutit*) — Traditional music played on the sukutit drum is now rare and is mostly heard at cultural events and venues.
- **Stringed lyre** — Traditional music also uses various stringed lyres, which are now uncommon.
- **Benga-style music** (*benga*) — Contemporary Kalenjin music draws on benga, whose guitar playing mainly uses plucking rather than strumming.
- **Kericho recording studios** — Kericho became a recording center where musicians from western Kenya recorded on spool tapes.

## Dance & theatre

Traditional Kalenjin dances are now mainly seen at ceremonies, cultural events, and performances.

- **Warriors’ dance** (*kambakta*) — Kambakta is a warriors’ dance performed during the event with the same name.
- **Old people’s dance** (*sondoiyo*) — Sondoiyo is described as an old people’s dance.
- **Row dance** — A British Museum photograph shows adult men dancing in a row, possibly at a funeral.

## Festivals & rituals

Kalenjin ritual life centers on peaceful relationships, initiation, and marriage ceremonies.

- **Peaceful relations** (*Tiliet*) — Peaceful relations, especially between relatives, are highly valued and are called Tiliet.
- **Circle of relationships** (*Tilionutik*) — The wider circle of personal relationships is called Tilionutik.
- **Male initiation** (*tumdo*) — Tumdo is the initiation that marks the transition from boyhood to manhood.
- **Circumcision** (*yatitaet*) — Yatitaet is circumcision, a central part of male initiation.
- **Learning process** (*ibinwek*) — Ibinwek is linked with the learning process during initiation and is declining in some settings.

## Foodways

Kalenjin foodways reflect pastoral life, especially through fermented milk and celebratory meat.

- **Fermented milk** (*mursik*) — Mursik is a fermented milk delicacy and an emblem of Kalenjin culture.
- **Roast meat** (*nyama choma*) — Nyama choma is roast beef, goat, or less often mutton served at celebrations and gatherings.
- **Kipsundet roast** (*Kipsundet*) — The Kipsundet roast is an older celebration practice using four specific types of wood as fuel.
- **Maize** — Maize is the main starch and is usually served with vegetable relishes, meat stews, or roasts.
- **Shared food** — Family members commonly share food from a central serving dish.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kalenjin oral traditions tell of migration, origins, creation, and powerful beings.

- **Misiri origin tradition** (*Misiri*) — Some traditions connect Kalenjin ancestral origins with Misiri, often interpreted as ancient Egypt.
- **Nile Valley and Ethiopian highlands tradition** — Other traditions connect ancestral origins with the Nile Valley and Ethiopian highlands.
- **Pharaoh’s army tradition** — Kipsigis traditions link their origins with the Pharaoh’s army.
- **Nandi creation story** — The Nandi creation story concerns the sky and earth, the sun and moon, Ilet, an elephant, and an Okiek.
- **Tororut and Asis accounts** (*Tororut; Asis; Ilat; Ilet*) — Religious oral accounts concern Tororut, Asis, Ilat, Ilet, Ilet ne-mie, and Ilet ne-ya.

## Language & religion

Kalenjin languages form a Southern Nilotic dialect cluster, while religion centers on sky powers and sacred places.

- **Kalenjin language** (*Elgeyo; Kalenjin proper*) — Kalenjin is also called Elgeyo or Kalenjin proper and is described as a dialect cluster.
- **The name Kalenjin** (*Kalenjin*) — The name became prominent in the late 1940s and early 1950s and means “I tell you” in a Nandi expression.
- **Supreme sky God** (*Asis*) — Asis is described as the supreme God who lives in the sky.
- **Thunder and rain power** (*Ilat or Ilet*) — Ilat or Ilet is associated with thunder and rain.
- **Sacred places** (*Kaapkoros; Mabwaita; Korosyoot; Sach ooraan; Kimwocho*) — Traditional sacred places include Kaapkoros, Mabwaita or Korosyoot, and Sach ooraan or Kimwocho.
