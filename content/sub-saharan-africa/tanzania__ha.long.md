## Overview

The Ha — who call themselves *Abaha*, and are known as *Waha* in Swahili — are a Bantu-speaking people of the Kigoma Region in northwestern Tanzania, along the eastern shore of Lake Tanganyika and inland across a country of grasslands and open woodland they call Buha. In 2001 their population was estimated at between one and one and a half million, making them one of the largest of Tanzania's many ethnic groups; the 1948 Tanganyika census had already ranked them the territory's third largest. Their language, Ha (*Kiha*, *Ikiha*, *Giha*), belongs to the Bantu branch of the Niger-Congo family and is closely related to Kirundi and Kinyarwanda across the border in Burundi and Rwanda. They share the northwest with the Sukuma, Haya, Zinza, Hangaza and Subi. In folk-culture terms they matter here for a body of museum-documented work in beadwork, hide and woven dress, ironwork, and carved wood — including a decorated trough zither.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources name no vernacular terms for Ha weaving or cloth-making, and none of the seed traditions listed for this atlas appears in them. What the British Museum records do document are two pattern-bearing dress traditions held in the collection, described only in English.

**Ribbed and squared women's dress** — A woman's dress collected in two parts. The back is coarse woven cloth divided on one side into squares by raised cords and coloured pale red and black, finished with a fringe of dark fibre carrying a few strings of glass beads. The front is a square of cotton cloth divided into squares of red and black by raised ribs, fringed on three sides and fitted with a string for attachment. The raised cord or rib is what makes the grid: the pattern is structural as much as dyed.

**Hide dress** — A dress made of cow skin with tassels, the collection's counterpart in hide to the woven, ribbed cloth above.

**Beaded belts** — Two women's belts: one of glass beads on fibre, the other a fibre core covered with bands of black and white glass beads. The banding of black against white is the only colour scheme the belt records name.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Alternating natural and blackened triangles* — carved on the sloping sides and back of a trough zither, the natural triangles further cut with horizontal grooves; *four-pointed star* — three equally spaced stars cut through the base of the same zither; *rings* — bands of black paint around the stem of a wooden head-rest; *squares* — the red-and-black grid of the woman's dress, set off by raised cords or ribs.

### Clothing & dress

For women's dress the catalogue is specific. A dress was made and worn in two pieces: a front of cotton cloth and a back of coarse woven cloth, both divided into red and black squares by raised ribs or cords, both fringed, the front carrying a string for attachment and the back a fringe of dark fibre strung here and there with glass beads. A second dress is cut from cow skin and hung with tassels. Belts were worn with these — one made wholly of glass beads on fibre, another a fibre belt faced with bands of black and white glass beads. Unmarried women wore a distinct breast ornament, the catalogue's "maiden's ornament", built of vegetable fibre, glass beads, shell beads and wood; the collection holds two. Ha women also wore the *Kitindi*, coiled bracelets of copper wire worn near the elbow — a tradition they share with neighbouring peoples rather than one peculiar to them. The sources describe no men's garments and no head coverings or footwear.

### Architecture

The Ha live in dispersed homesteads rather than nucleated villages, typically as a joint family whose male members are related through their lineage. Beyond this settlement pattern, the sources used here say nothing about Ha house form, building materials, roofing or architectural decoration, and none of the building types in this atlas's search list is attested in them.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The British Museum holdings are strongest here. Ironworking is represented both by its products and by its equipment: an arm-dagger of iron and wood, arrows with barbed iron points and feathered shafts, a wooden bow strung with cord, and a bellows built of wood, cane and skin — the smith's own tool, collected alongside what smiths made. In wood there is a bowl cut round from the solid and coloured light red on the outside; a milk vessel with a separate lid; and a head-rest, described as a pillow, turned from light wood on a circular base with its stem painted in black rings. Containers in softer materials include a bag of bark fibre. Hunting equipment carries its own refinements: a wooden whistle formed as a tube with a crescent-shaped mouth, two ears at the sides and an expanded end, with a feather kept inside the tube and the vent left clear, plus two hunters' whistles of ivory. A charm or medicine container is made from horn. No ceramics appear among the objects shown, and the sources name no pottery tradition.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ha ornament in the collection is worked in copper and brass and in beads. For the arm and wrist there are an armlet of copper and another of brass; a copper ring flattened on one side where a short broad axe projects; a brass ring flattened on one side to a ridge; a penannular bracelet of brass; and a child's penannular bracelet, also brass — the open-ended penannular form recurring at both adult and child scale. The *Kitindi*, coiled copper-wire bracelets worn near the elbow, are worn by Ha women and shared with neighbouring ethnic groups. Beadwork appears on the body as well as on belts: the two maiden's breast ornaments combine vegetable fibre, glass beads, shell beads and wood, setting imported glass against shell in one piece. The sources record no tattooing, scarification, hairdressing or cosmetic practice.

## Music & performance

One Ha instrument is documented in detail. The **trough zither** in the British Museum is built from a single piece of wood with parallel long sides and rounded ends, its central section hollowed out to act as the resonator. A single length of fibre is secured at one end, stretched the length of the trough and over cane bridges at either end, then looped around deep serrations cut into the deep end to form seven strings — so the whole seven-course instrument is strung from one continuous cord. Three equally spaced four-pointed stars are cut through the base, and the sloping sides and back are decorated with alternating natural and blackened triangles, the natural ones incised with horizontal grooves. Alongside it the collection holds aerophones from a different world of use: a wooden whistle shaped as a tube with a crescent mouth, two side ears and an expanded end, holding a feather inside with the vent kept clear, and two hunters' whistles of ivory — signalling instruments of the hunt rather than of performance. The sources name no Ha song genres, singers, ensembles or performance occasions, and no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage element covers Tanzania.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used here document no Ha dance or dramatic tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

No annual festival calendar for the Ha appears in the sources. What they do record is the place of livestock in the marriage system: where the tsetse fly is only a minor problem the Ha keep cattle, goats and other livestock, which are highly valued in Ha society and are given as gifts at marriage — the subject of J. H. Scherer's 1965 Utrecht dissertation on marriage and bride-wealth in the highlands of Buha. Ritual life is grounded in an animist religion in which ancestors and nature spirits are revered and *Imana* is the supreme being and creator. A charm and medicine container made of horn is among the objects shown, the one item in the collection tied directly to ritual practice. Two missionary histories overlie this: Islamic missionary activity reaching the Ha from Arab traders in the pre-colonial period, and Christian mission — Roman Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran and others — during and after the German and British colonial administrations. The sources describe no birth, initiation or funeral rites in detail.

## Foodways

Ha cultivation covers sorghum, millet, corn (maize), cassava, yams, peanuts and other crops. Livestock keeping depends on the tsetse fly: where the fly is a minor problem the Ha raise cattle, goats and other animals, and in the northern parts of their territory, where the fly is a serious problem, they hunt and gather honey instead — a division that shapes what is eaten across their country. Two objects in the collection belong to this economy: a wooden milk vessel with a separate lid, and a wooden bowl cut round from the solid and coloured light red on the outside. The hunting whistles — one of wood with a feather kept inside the tube, two of ivory — belong to the same northern, tsetse-ridden zone where hunting supplements the fields. The sources name no Ha dishes, cooking methods, ceremonial foods, beverages or dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used here record no Ha folktales, epics, proverbs, riddles or storytelling occasions. What they do document is the language's written documentation: Lotta Harjula's *The Ha Language of Tanzania: Grammar, Texts and Vocabulary* (2004) includes a body of texts, and Bible recordings exist in Ha.

## Language & religion

Ha — *Kiha*, *Ikiha*, *Giha*, the prefixed forms also appearing as *Igiha* — is a Bantu language of the Niger-Congo family, spoken on the eastern side of Lake Tanganyika up to the headwaters of the Mikonga. It is closely related to the languages of Rwanda and Burundi, and neighbouring dialects are reported to be mutually intelligible with Kirundi. Its consonant inventory shows /ɾ/ heard as [l] among different dialects in free variation, and /b/ realised as either [b] or [β] in complementary distribution. Religiously the Ha are animists who revere ancestors and nature spirits, with *Imana* as supreme being and creator; Islamic missionary activity has reached them from Arabs since the pre-colonial era, and Roman Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran and other Christian missions arrived in the German and British colonial periods.
