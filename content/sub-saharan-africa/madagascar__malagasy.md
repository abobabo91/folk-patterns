## Overview

The Malagasy are an Austronesian-speaking ethnic group and nation indigenous to Madagascar. They live throughout the island, including the central highlands around Antananarivo, Alaotra and Fianarantsoa, and coastal regions associated with groups such as the Sakalava, Bara, Vezo, Betsimisaraka and Mahafaly. Malagasy society developed through interaction between Austronesian settlers from Maritime Southeast Asia and later Bantu migrants from Southeast Africa. The population was 2,242,000 in the first census in 1900; the Malagasy language is spoken by around 25 million people in Madagascar and the Comoros. Malagasy culture is marked by regional diversity, ancestor veneration, distinctive architecture, silk weaving, lamba production, music, oratory, rice-based foodways and named ethnic traditions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Lamba* — Madagascar’s national dress is a woven textile whose production and weaving can be traced to the island’s earliest settlers. Silk weaving is an established tradition, and fresh silk shrouds are used in the highlands practice of *famadihana*.

*Raffia weaving* — Raffia and other local plant materials are woven into floor mats, baskets, purses and hats.

*Antaimoro paper* — Among the Antaimoro people, paper is embedded with flowers and other decorative natural materials. The tradition is long-established and has also been marketed to eco-tourists.

*Embroidery and drawn thread work* — These hand techniques produce clothing, tablecloths and other home textiles for local crafts markets.

*Zafimaniry woodworking* — The decorative and functional woodworking traditions of the Zafimaniry people of the central highlands include architectural decoration and wooden objects. The tradition was inscribed on UNESCO’s list of Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2008.

### Clothing & dress

The woven *lamba* is Madagascar’s national dress and has developed into a varied and refined art. Silk is used in the textile tradition, including fresh silk shrouds for *famadihana*, the highlands rewrapping of a deceased family member’s remains. The sources also state that embroidery and drawn thread work produce clothing for sale in local crafts markets.

### Architecture

Traditional Malagasy houses are rectangular, have peaked or gabled roofs and commonly feature a central support pillar. Their form strongly resembles traditional architecture in southern Borneo, also known as South Kalimantan, where houses are raised on piles. In the central highlands, traditional wooden houses of the Merina aristocracy have a central pillar called *andry*, a steeply sloped roof and roof horns called *tandro-trano*. In southeastern Madagascar, actual zebu horns were traditionally attached to the gable peak. Houses and their dimensions can symbolically indicate the status of their occupants or the importance of their purpose. Tombs are culturally significant, often built from durable material such as stone, and may be more elaborately decorated than houses. Decorative wooden funerary posts are called *aloalo* in western Madagascar.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources describe wooden furniture, household goods, balcony decoration, wooden sculptures and *aloalo* funerary posts. Raffia and other plant materials are used for mats, baskets, purses and hats. The sources also mention clay jars in the preparation of *jaka*, a beef confit associated with the Merina Royal Bath, and metal sheeting used near Toamasina to construct box-form *marovany* instruments.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 They state that valiha players traditionally allowed their fingernails to grow long for plucking strings, and that, from the mid-19th century, long fingernails became symbolic of Merina aristocratic status.

## Music & performance

Malagasy music reflects traditions associated with Southeast Asia, Africa, Oceania, Arabia, England, France, Portugal and the United States. It is broadly divided into traditional, contemporary and popular music. The *valiha*, a bamboo tube zither considered Madagascar’s national instrument, was brought by Austronesian settlers and resembles instruments used in Indonesia and the Philippines. Other instruments include the *marovany*, *kabosy*, *mandoliny*, *djembe*, *sodina*, *antsiva*, *gorodo*, *ampongabe*, *langoroana*, *hazolahy*, *kaiamba* and *atranatrana*.

Traditional regional styles include coastal *salegy*, highland *hiragasy* and southwestern *tsapika*. The *hiragasy* is a day-long spectacle of music, song, dance and oration, performed by a troupe or between two troupes. *Salegy* is energetic dance music with electric guitars, accordion, call-and-response vocals, electric bass and percussion. *Tsapika* developed from southwestern traditional music and uses electric guitar, bass guitar and drum kit.

Music serves entertainment, personal expression, spiritual ceremonies, cultural events, political functions and the preservation of history. The *tromba* involves entering a trance state, typically induced by music, while music is also central to *famadihana*. The *hira gasy* and *mpilalao* bands perform at reburials, circumcisions and other traditional celebrations.

## Dance & theatre

The *hiragasy* combines music, song, dance and oration in a day-long theatrical spectacle. Its contemporary form began in the late 18th century, when Merina prince Andrianampoinimerina used musicians to draw crowds for political speeches. The troupes later incorporated political commentary and critique, while audiences expressed approval or disapproval through applause, cheers and other sounds.

Madagascar has numerous regional dance styles. In the 19th-century royal court, the quadrille was danced to Malagasy-influenced piano music. The *Afindrafindrao*, described as the last of these Malagasy quadrilles, accompanies a specific partner dance and was revived after national independence for weddings, concerts and other festivities. Western theater was introduced with French colonization in 1896, producing Malagasy theater that combined short musical and dance performances with piano or guitar accompaniment.

## Festivals & rituals

Ancestor veneration structures many Malagasy rituals. The highlands practice of *famadihana* involves exhuming a deceased family member’s remains, periodically rewrapping them in fresh silk shrouds and replacing them in the tomb. It is an occasion for remembering the ancestor, reuniting family and community, and maintaining a festive atmosphere. Music is central to the ceremony.

The Merina celebrated the new year with the Royal Bath, or *fandroana*. During Merina royal festivals, *hanim-pito loha*, seven highly desirable dishes, were eaten. The sources associate the ceremony with *jaka*, a beef confit prepared in a decorative clay jar and conserved underground for a year, and *tatao*, rice boiled in milk and drizzled with honey.

Other ritual practices include male circumcision, offerings to ancestors, the sacrifice of zebu, and the use of music at spiritual ceremonies. The sources state that *fady*, or taboos, regulate social life and may concern food, animals and other conduct.

## Foodways

Rice, called *vary*, is the cornerstone of Malagasy foodways and is typically eaten at every meal. The accompanying dish is *laoka*, often served with a sauce. Highland sauces are generally tomato-based, while coconut milk is frequently added in coastal areas. In the arid south and west, where zebu herding is important, families may rely more on cassava, maize, sweet potato, yams, taro root, millet and zebu milk or curds.

Named dishes include *romazava*, a broth made with beef and local greens; *ravitoto*, shredded cassava leaves; *trondro gasy*, freshwater fish; *henan’omby*, beef; *akoho*, chicken; *ritra*, meat simmered in its own juices; and *kitoza*, smoked strips of zebu meat. Common flavorings include garlic, onions, ginger, tomatoes, mild curry and salt. *Sakay* is a spicy condiment made from red or green chili pepper, while *lasary* can refer to pickled fruit or a highland salad.

Street foods include *mofo gasy*, a sweetened rice-flour cake; *menakely*, a fried doughnut; *koba akondro*, a steamed or boiled sweet of peanuts, bananas, honey and corn flour; and *sambos*, Indian-style samosas. Traditional beliefs produce food taboos called *fady*, including restrictions against pork among the Sakalava and prohibitions against eating cows or certain sea turtles among the Antandroy.

## Oral tradition & literature

Malagasy oral traditions include *hainteny* poetry, *kabary* public discourse and *ohabolana* proverbs. The epic *Ibonia*, concerning a Malagasy folk hero of the same name, has been transmitted in several forms across the island and offers insight into traditional mythologies and beliefs. Malagasy mythology is rooted in oral history and storytelling, including the *Ibonia* cycle.

The Betsileo preserve oral histories through the musical performance called *rija*. The sources describe the *rija* as a combination of an earlier single-verse form and an epic poem called the *isa*. In the late 19th century, the *Tantara ny Andriana eto Madagasikara*, a compilation of oral history concerning Merina sovereigns, became a major source about traditional highland society.

The first Malagasy historian identified in the sources is Raombana, who documented early 19th-century Merina history in English and Malagasy. Western literary arts developed during French colonization. Jean-Joseph Rabearivelo combined Western poetic forms with Malagasy oratory and published historical novels and a Western-style opera.

## Language & religion

Malagasy is an Austronesian language and dialect continuum, most closely related to the Ma’anyan language of Borneo. It is generally spoken throughout Madagascar and includes eastern, western, northern, central-eastern and southern varieties. The Merina dialect is the basis of Standard Malagasy. Malagasy and French were named official languages in the first national Constitution of 1958; the language is written in the Latin script, while the earlier *Sorabe* script was derived from Arabic and used for astrological and magical texts.

Most Malagasy adhere to Christianity, with Protestant practitioners slightly outnumbering Roman Catholics. Many Christians integrate Christian beliefs with ancestor-honoring practices. Traditional religion is known as *Fomba-gasy* and emphasizes links between the living and the *razana*, or ancestors. Malagasy traditions also refer to a creator god called Zanahary or Andriamanitra, while values include *fihavanana*, *vintana*, *tody* and *hasina*. Islam is practiced by around 3 percent of the population, and Hinduism was introduced through Gujarati immigration from India.
