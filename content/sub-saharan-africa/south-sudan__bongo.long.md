## Overview

The Bongo are a people of South Sudan, living in sparsely populated areas of Bahr al Ghazal. Their language — Bongo, also recorded as *Bungu* and known too as *Dor* — belongs to the Central Sudanic family. It is tonal, distinguishing high, mid, low and falling tones, has ten vowel qualities that may be long or short, and shows vowel harmony between a set of "heavy" and a set of "light" vowels; its counting system is quinary-vigesimal. The Bongo have been described by outside observers since the mid-nineteenth century: John Petherick published Bongo word lists in 1861, Theodor von Heuglin in 1869, and Georg August Schweinfurth contributed sentences and vocabularies in 1873. In folk-culture terms the sources here document the Bongo chiefly through objects — hunting and fighting gear, carved wooden seats and figures, ivory horns, iron currency, and personal ornament.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used do not cover Bongo weaving, netting, bark cloth or any other textile tradition, and name no motif vocabulary.

### Clothing & dress

The sources used describe no Bongo garments, head coverings, belts or footwear, and draw no distinction between everyday and ceremonial dress. What they do record is worn ornament rather than clothing: wooden lip ornaments for women, ivory armlets, necklaces, and a head ornament — a circle of skin with a ridge of sandy-coloured hair around it — which the museum record also identifies as a load-carrier, that is, a pad worn on the head for carrying. These are treated below under jewelry and body adornment.

### Architecture

House form, materials and roofing are not described in the sources used. One object, however, belongs to the built environment: an ornament for a hut, cut from solid wood in the form of a bird, with a projecting ruff or collar around the neck, two holes drilled in the breast to receive its legs, and a surviving eye bead of red with a central black spot. It was originally painted brown and yellow.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Iron and wood dominate the recorded household and working equipment. An oval iron knife carries punched decoration on one side. A hoe handle is carved from wood with a flat oval head. Two objects are currency in metal blade form: a wide, flat iron blade with pointed ends and a handle, and a **hoe currency** whose iron blade takes the shape of an ace of spades and carries a long handle. Seating is a carving tradition in its own right: a stool cut from solid wood with a curved oval seat on four feet and a horizontal handle looped at the end; a **warrior's stool** with a horizontal projecting handle; a stool with protuberances at the base of each of its four feet; and a piece recorded as stool or head-rest, four-legged, with a long neck in the form of a stylised animal. A smoking pipe combines a clay bowl, a bamboo stem and a gourd mouthpiece partly covered with skin. Ivory is worked into a seal-stamp made from the end of an elephant's tusk, its face engraved with intersecting lines.

Weapons and hunting gear form a matched set: bows of wood with fibre string, arrows of reed, iron and fibre, and quivers of hide and skin with the hair left on — one of hippopotamus hide, with hair and a tassel. A small shield is made of brown hide, slightly curved, with a handle behind and raised metal embossments punched out from the back.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Women's lip ornaments are carved in wood and painted black; a further wooden lip ornament for a woman is recorded without a stated finish. Ivory appears as armlets and in a necklace strung on fibre with ten flat oval pieces of ivory carved to resemble cowrie shells, together with a flat ivory disc. A second necklace is recorded on its museum slip as a "necklace of conjuring beads": five strings of twisted fibre strung with round brown berries, with two transverse pieces of hide strung about a foot apart in the middle — the one object in this group whose description points to ritual use. Tattooing, scarification, henna and hair practices are not covered by the sources used.

## Music & performance

Four kinds of instrument are documented. A drum, recorded as a tom-tom, is made from a hollowed wooden log open at both ends, with skin heads held by thongs of skin. A **harp**, catalogued also as a lyre, has a semi-circular sounding chamber with truncated ends; its back is wood covered with skin parchment, and its neck consists of two sticks joined by a transverse bar to which gut strings are attached. Ivory supplies both a trumpet and a series of side-blown hunting horns: one plain, one fitted with a loop of leather, one whose wide end is covered with coloured skin recorded as cow, and a short horn made from the pointed end of an elephant's tusk with incised lines and pricked dots. A further hunting horn is carved from wood, and one horn of uncertain function is recorded as bone or ivory. The sources used name no song genres, no ensemble types and no performance occasions, so nothing can be said here about the contexts — funerary, festive or otherwise — in which these instruments sounded.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used do not cover Bongo dance or dramatic performance.

## Festivals & rituals

No annual festival calendar and no birth, initiation or marriage rites appear in the sources used. Funerary commemoration, however, is the best-attested ritual domain in the object record. The Metropolitan Museum of Art holds a *ngya*, a commemorative post carved in mahogany and dated to the late nineteenth century. The British Museum holds a standing grave figure of a woman, carved in wood and nearly life-size, along with a figure of a man carved in red wood. Together these are the material trace of a practice of erecting carved human figures in connection with the dead — a tradition the sources name but do not further describe. The necklace of conjuring beads noted above is the only other object whose catalogue description implies a ritual role.

## Foodways

The sources used cover no Bongo staples, dishes, cooking methods or drinking customs. The only food-related implement recorded is the wooden hoe handle with its flat oval head, and the iron hoe blade that circulated as currency in the same form.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used document no Bongo folktales, epics, proverbs or riddles. What they do record is the written documentation of the language itself. Nineteenth-century travellers gathered vocabulary: Petherick in *Egypt, the Soudan, and Central Africa* (1861), von Heuglin in *Reise in das Gebiet des Weissen Nil, &c. 1862-1864* (1869), and Schweinfurth in his *Linguistische Ergebnisse, Einer Reise Nach Centralafrika* (1873). E. E. Evans-Pritchard published further Bongo word lists in 1937, and Eileen Kilpatrick a phonology of the language in 1985. More recently, a team of Bongo authors — Daniel Rabbi Moi, Mario Lau Babur Kuduku, Sister Mary Mangira Michael, Simon Hagimir John, Rapheal Zakenia Paul Mafoi and Nyoul Gulluma Kuduku — produced a *Bongo Grammar Book* and a *Bongo Consonant and Vowel Book* with SIL-South Sudan in Juba in 2018, and a sociolinguistic survey of the Bongo of South Sudan appeared in 2020. The Jesus Film has been translated into Bongo.

## Language & religion

Bongo, also called Bungu or Dor, is a Central Sudanic language of Bahr al Ghazal in South Sudan. It is tonal, with high, mid, low and falling tones; falling tones occur on long vowels, vowel clusters or glides, and where the fall is not caused by a preceding high tone it can be written as a high tone followed by a low. Ten vowel qualities occur long or short, and vowel harmony sets five "heavy" vowels against five "light" ones. Numerals follow a quinary-vigesimal system. The sources used give no dialect inventory and no historical script. They say nothing directly about religion; the existence of a Bongo translation of the Jesus Film and the involvement of a Catholic missionary scholar, Stefano Santandrea, in comparative Bongo vocabulary work are the only indications the record offers, and neither describes Bongo religious practice.
