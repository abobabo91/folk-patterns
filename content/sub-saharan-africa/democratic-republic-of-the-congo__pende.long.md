## Overview

The Pende live in west-central Democratic Republic of the Congo. Eastern and Western Pende have distinct artistic and ceremonial traditions while identifying as one people. Extended families and maternal descent have been important in social organization. There is no single Pende kingship that explains every object in a museum collection. Masks, performance, sculpture, textiles, vessels and musical instruments instead show several kinds of work made and used in particular places and periods.

Pende art is especially visible in the ensemble of a masquerade: a carved or fiber mask, raffia costume, the dancer's movement, music and an audience. These events may accompany initiation or public gatherings, and their characters can comment on relationships within a community. The 1931 Pende revolt against Belgian colonial rule is also part of this history; the Metropolitan Museum of Art notes that mask pendants later expressed solidarity and resistance.

## Material culture

The selected museum objects range from elaborate masks and carved figures to pottery, baskets, weapons, musical instruments and small ornaments. A museum label identifies the maker or cultural attribution of an individual piece; it does not mean that every Pende community made or used the same form.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Raffia fiber is central to surviving Pende masquerade dress. The Smithsonian's complete minganji ensemble includes a knotted fiber mask, body covering, fringed skirt and coverings for the limbs. Movement animates the hanging fibers and hides the dancer's everyday appearance. Other collected pieces include woven and dyed cloth, patterned bands, a head covering and raffia ornaments. British Museum textiles show geometric repetition and textured surfaces, but their catalogue records do not provide a complete account of who wove each piece or how a pattern was named.

The Metropolitan Museum describes raffia hair attached to a gambanda mask, made as part of an expressive figure rather than as an ordinary wig. Its account of the carved face connects the mask's coiffure to hairstyles admired by young women around 1900. Textile, hair and carved wood therefore work together in the appearance of a performed character.

### Clothing & dress

The strongest evidence in these sources concerns ceremonial costume. A minganji fiber suit can cover the body from head to ankle; other masquerades combine a wooden mask with raffia hair or fringe. The British Museum holds striped fiber costume pieces, skirts, hats and head ornaments attributed to Pende makers. Their shape and material are visible, while the records often leave the precise wearer, occasion and local name unspecified.

Small mask pendants are another kind of bodily display. The Met distinguishes wooden examples prescribed by healers from ivory examples carved by specialists as ornaments. These miniature faces became markers of Pende identity as well as personal adornment. Their history differs from that of a full-size dance mask even when the facial features are related.

### Architecture

The sources identify communities and ceremonial settings, but the objects selected for this atlas give too little evidence for a detailed architectural account. Carved figures and stools in collections should not be presented as doorposts or building panels without a record that identifies that use.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

British Museum records attribute lidded pottery vessels, carved cups and bowls, baskets, a fish trap, combs, axes and other tools to Pende makers. Some vessels have incised designs or fiber bindings; others are shaped as human heads or supported by figures. These pieces show skill in clay, wood, fiber, iron and ivory beyond the better-known masks. A carved chair or stool can also be a substantial work of sculpture, though its exact social use needs the individual catalogue record.

The Met's galukoji divination instrument documents a more specific practice. It joins a small mask-like head to an expanding bamboo mechanism. A diviner used its movement during consultations. The museum dates the recorded use of this particular form mainly to the 1920s through the 1950s, so it should not be described as an unchanging practice of all Pende communities.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The Met calls a small ivory pendant mask a gikhokho; ikhokho is its plural. It explains that ivory pendants were carved by professional sculptors and worn as decoration, while wooden miniature masks could be prescribed by healers. Their curved forehead, lowered eyes and narrow chin echo full-size masks. The Met also records their growing importance as signs of solidarity after the 1931 rebellion.

The selected British Museum pieces include a small face-shaped pendant or badge, decorated hairpins, feather ornaments and a fiber ring. They document a range of adornment, but the labels do not establish one common meaning for every object.

## Music & performance

The selected museum records identify Pende thumb pianos or sanza, panpipes, whistles, a drum and a rattle. They establish that these instruments were collected under Pende attribution, but rarely identify a tune, performer or occasion. Their presence complements museum accounts of masquerade as an event of costume, movement and sound. A list of instruments should not be taken as a fixed ensemble used in every initiation or masked dance.

At the Met, a gambanda mask represents the wife of a chief. The museum describes how restrained choreography and a carefully carved face expressed refinement, while the character could address tensions between individual wishes and communal expectations. This account gives a concrete example of how a mask acquired meaning in performance rather than only through its appearance on a wall.

## Dance & theatre

Mbuya wooden masks are danced with raffia costumes in performances linked to mukanda initiation. According to the Met, these masquerades may entertain, frighten and comment on power relationships. The Smithsonian describes minganji as a fiber mask and full costume, and records Pende masks at initiations, funerals, leadership ceremonies and other gatherings. These are related performance settings, not a single dance with an invariant script.

Pende mask characters can differ markedly in form. The Smithsonian's giwoyo cap mask sits on top of the head and has a long extension below the face. Its museum record says Pende people connect this form with an Angolan origin. The Met's gambanda has a quiet expression and elaborate raffia hair; other masks use strong color, sharp geometry or animal references. The dancer, costume and setting are needed to understand what each figure did.

## Festivals & rituals

Mukanda is a male initiation setting documented by the Met for masked performances. The Smithsonian describes fiber and wooden masks worn by adults at initiations and also at funerals and ceremonies concerning leadership. These records support the importance of masquerade in public and ritual life; they do not supply a single annual festival calendar for all Pende communities.

Ancestral relationships appear in summaries of Pende belief, and the Wikipedia account gives Mvumbi as a name for ancestors. The precise role of any particular museum object in those beliefs must be checked in its own record. The Met's galukoji account is unusually detailed about one divination practice and its period of use.

## Foodways

The sources used for this profile do not document a distinct Pende dish, recipe or food festival. An older ethnographic summary reports that artists could receive a first share of the harvest, reflecting their social standing, but it gives little detail about the foods or how widely this practice was followed. It would be misleading to fill the gap with dishes known only from neighboring communities.

## Oral tradition & literature

Museum descriptions preserve interpretations of mask characters and of their performances. The Smithsonian reports that Pende people connect the giwoyo masking tradition with an Angolan origin. The Met records how masks and pendants took on new political meaning in the wake of the 1931 revolt. These are examples of historical memory carried by objects and performance.

The Pende people's oral histories, contemporary literature and locally named genres need documentation from speakers and communities. The selected objects alone cannot supply a complete account.

## Language & religion

Giphende and Kipende are names used for the Pende language, part of the Bantu family. Eastern and Western Pende communities share an ethnic identification but differ in local traditions. Historical summaries describe extended families, maternal descent and no single centralized kingship. These are broad descriptions, not a substitute for local accounts of present-day social life.

The Wikipedia summary lists Christianity alongside traditional religion and names Mvumbi in relation to ancestors. The museum sources used here are much richer on art and performance than on current religious affiliation. Mukanda, masquerade and divination should therefore be described through the places and periods for which they are documented, without treating every collected practice as current or universal.
