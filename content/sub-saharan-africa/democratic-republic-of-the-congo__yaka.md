## Overview

The Yaka live in forest and savanna regions near the Angola border. They farm, hunt, fish, and make many wooden, fibre, and metal objects. Villages are led by chiefs, with a paramount chief called kiambv. Cultural life includes initiation, divination, ancestor reverence, masks, sculpture, basketry, drums, and whistles.

## Material culture

Yaka material culture uses wood, raffia, fibre, cane, beads, feathers, copper, iron, brass, and tin for objects of daily use, authority, ritual, and protection.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Raffia, fibre, beads, feathers, and geometric designs are important in masks, hats, baskets, and other objects.

- **Raffia composite masks** — These masks combine wood, cane, raffia, and raffia cloth, often with painted faces, patterns, projections, and fringes.
- **Nkanda masks** (*nkanda*) — These initiation masks may include mondo, ndemba, kapita, and mbala, and are judged for color, creativity, and execution.
- **Woven raffia headpiece** (*bweni*) — The bweni is a woven raffia headpiece with one to three crests, worn by earth chiefs as a sign of authority and as a communal charm.
- **Beaded noble hat** (*tsala*) — The tsala is a beaded conical hat with a feather tuft, and its feather color shows rank.
- **Ceremonial mask coverings** — Masks may have fibre-frame caps or conical cloth hats with projections, painted geometric designs, and raffia around the rim.

### Clothing & dress

The profile describes ceremonial and authority headwear more fully than ordinary clothing.

- **Authority rings** (*kazekedi; lukano*) — Paramount chiefs wore coiled rings around the ankle and arm, made with fibres, a sacrificial tendon, and copper.
- **Noble hat** (*tsala*) — Nobles wore beaded hats with conical tops and feathers.
- **Earth chief’s headpiece** (*bweni*) — Earth chiefs wore woven raffia headpieces.
- **Fibre chief’s hat** — Museum records include a chief’s hat made of vegetable fibre.
- **Crested hat or coiffure** — A museum figure has a crested hat or coiffure.

### Architecture

The sources give little information about Yaka architecture, but they mention chiefs’ villages, graves, and house imagery.

- **Chief-led villages** — Yaka villages are governed by chiefs.
- **Grave dwellings and enclosures** — Many graves are built in dwellings or placed in enclosures where n-saanda and n-yoombu trees are planted.
- **Pitched-roof house motif** — Some carved headrests show a house with a pitched roof.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Yaka makers produce many wooden, fibre, and metal tools, vessels, containers, and household objects.

- **Drinking cups and vessels** — Museum records include drinking cups, wooden double cups, and vessels with two spouts.
- **Baskets and winnowing trays** — Baskets and winnowing trays are among the recorded everyday objects.
- **Fish traps** — Fish traps are recorded among Yaka material objects.
- **Mukoku slit drum** (*mukoku*) — The mukoku is a carved slit drum used in ritual and divination contexts.
- **Metal tools and fittings** — Records include swords, spear points, adze fittings, brass studs, brass nails, copper wire, iron wire, and tin-plating.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment often marks rank or carries protective power.

- **Chiefly copper-wrapped rings** (*kazekedi; lukano*) — Paramount chiefs wore fibre and tendon rings wrapped in copper around their ankles and arms.
- **Feathered noble hats** (*tsala*) — Tsala hats use beads and feathers, with white and red feathers showing different ranks.
- **Beads and pendants** — Museum figures include beads, metal fittings, cords, cloth bags, and pendants.
- **Protective charms** — Headrests and other objects could carry charms meant to protect their owners from wicked influences.

## Music & performance

Yaka music uses drums, slit drums, whistles, and flutes for ritual, divination, and hunting signals.

- **Ritual slit drum** (*mukoku*) — The mukoku provides rhythm for ritual dances and diviners’ chants.
- **Hunt signal whistle** (*kasengo-sengo*) — Men are warned several days before a hunt by a whistle or miniature flute called kasengo-sengo.
- **Hunting leader’s whistle** (*yimbila*) — The yimbila is an elongated whistle used by the hunt leader to call and signal hunters.
- **Spherical whistle** (*ndemba*) — The ndemba is a more spherical whistle with a short side extension.
- **Other instruments** — Museum records include ivory and wooden whistles, friction drums, and many slit gongs.

## Dance & theatre

Masks, figures, and slit drums shape Yaka ritual dances and initiation performances.

- **Ritual mask performance** — Yaka masks are worn by dancers and performers and are believed to communicate with the spirit world.
- **Mbwoolo ritual figures** (*mbwoolo*) — Mbwoolo sculptures are used in ritual dances.
- **Nkanda initiation performance** (*nkanda*) — Nkanda uses masks in the initiation of young men after circumcision.
- **Leading mbala mask** (*mbala*) — The leading mbala mask is designed by its sculptor and wearer to amuse spectators.

## Festivals & rituals

The best-described Yaka ceremony is nkanda, a long initiation for boys after circumcision.

- **Coming-of-age initiation** (*nkanda*) — Nkanda prepares young men for adulthood after circumcision at puberty.
- **Initiates** (*tundansi*) — After circumcision, boys become tundansi and leave the village for one to three years to learn skills and proper manhood.
- **Initiation teachers** (*tulombusi*) — Tulombusi teachers and elders decide when the initiates are ready.
- **Ancestor offerings** — The sources describe offerings to ancestors, including offerings before group hunts.
- **Divination and funerary rites** — Yaka ritual life includes divination, grave practices, hunting rites, and reconciliation ceremonies between Kongo and Lunda groups.

## Foodways

Yaka food comes from farming, fishing, hunting, and gathered foods, with rules for sharing game.

- **Staple crops** — The Yaka farm cassava, sweet potatoes, and corn.
- **Hunted game** — Hunters pursue duiker, antelope, buffalo, and wild boar.
- **Hunting equipment** — Hunters use bows and arrows, rifles, lances, snares, traps, long nets, and dogs.
- **Hunt approval offerings** — Before a group hunt, hunters send palm wine and kola nuts to the earth chief for approval and ancestor offerings.
- **Game-sharing rules** — The first arrow determines ownership, while other hunters, the tracking dog, chiefs, dependents, and lineage headmen receive assigned portions.

## Oral tradition & literature

The profile records divination chants and historical accounts rather than named epics or written literature.

- **Divination chants** — During divination, the ngoombu chants about possible curses and relatives may repeat the ends of the chants.
- **Curse investigation** (*ngoombu*) — The diviner later reveals the curse believed to have caused an affliction.
- **Yaka kingdom history** — Historical accounts describe the founding of the Yaka kingdom, its political organization, and King Kabamba.
- **Kongo-Lunda histories** — The accounts describe Lunda movements westward and relations between Kongo and Lunda communities.
- **Colonial and independence histories** — The sources describe resistance to colonial administration and the later role of the Bayaka in independence movements.

## Language & religion

Yaka religious life centers on ancestors, divination, healing, protective power, initiation, and fears of witchcraft.

- **Yaka language** — The Yaka speak the Yaka language, a name also used for several other languages and a West Teke dialect.
- **Diviner** (*ngoombu*) — The ngoombu investigates strange events, misfortune, illness, and untimely death.
- **Protective statuettes** (*mbwoolo*) — Mbwoolo statuettes help interpret dreams, communicate with ancestors, heal, and protect.
- **Power figures** (*Biteki; minkisi*) — Biteki figures can hold magical ingredients that become minkisi, or power objects.
- **Phuungu protection** (*Phuungu*) — One Biteki figure is associated with Phuungu and protection against witches.
