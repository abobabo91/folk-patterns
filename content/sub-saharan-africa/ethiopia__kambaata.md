## Overview

The Kambaata are a Cushitic ethnic group in south-central Ethiopia, specifically in Kambaata Zone in Central Ethiopia Regional State. The name Kambata is also used more broadly for Kambata, Alaba, and Tambaro groups, which speak dialects of the same language and are associated with the heartland of the Hambaricho massif. These groups have been described as mingled and intermarried, making their boundaries difficult to specify. Ethiopian statistics recorded a Kambaata population of 630,236, with 90.89% living in the Southern Nations, Nationalities, and People’s Region and 18.5% in urban areas. Kambaata is significant in the sources for its Cushitic language, former kingdom, indigenous institutions, agriculture, cuisine, and annual festival traditions.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources do identify Hambaricho Mountain as an important landmark and as the residence of the Kambata kings.

## Music & performance

The sources also state that the king and Abba Sarecho, identified as the god of Kambaata, lived on Hambaricho Mountain.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources identify *Gifata* as an annual festival celebrated by the Kambaata people. No date, season, ritual sequence, or detailed account of the festival is provided. The sources also connect Hambaricho Mountain with Kambaata kingship and state that the king and Abba Sarecho, the god of Kambaata, lived there.

The historical sources describe Kambaata as a southern kingdom with an established monarchical system instituted in the 16th century. This system operated without interruption until the last decade of the ninetieth century, when Kambaata was incorporated by Emperor Menelik II. During the first period of incorporation, Kambaata province was largely Christianized. The kingdom was ruled by kings known as Woma. King Hamlmal, locally known as Hamo, was described as the first king of the Kambata Kingdom and founder of the ruling Oyeta clan. King Dagoye was known for expanding Kambata territories, while the last independent king, King Woma Delbato Degoye, was killed by the armies of Menelik II toward the end of the 19th Century.

## Foodways

The sources identify *kocho* as the most important of the Kambaata people’s indigenous traditional cuisines. It is made from ensete. The Kambaata also cultivate tubers, spices, coffee, crops, and vegetables.

Subsistence is also shaped by population density and migration. Kambaata is described as one of the most densely populated regions in Ethiopia. Because of overpopulation and a lack of economic opportunities in the region, Kambaata people migrate to large cities, industrial areas, and large plantation farms. In recent years, the sources state that they experienced a large influx of migration to South Africa and Middle Eastern countries.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that Kambaata was first mentioned in the chronicles of Emperor Yeshaq I, but provide no further account of oral or written narrative traditions.

The sources do record several historical and linguistic publications concerning Kambaata. These include works on the traditional institution of Kambata, the history and society of a southern Ethiopian peasant people, the sociocultural structures of Kambata and their economic development, occupational minorities in the Kambata ethnic group, and the significance of some Kambata values from an evangelical perspective.

## Language & religion

Kambaata, also called Kambatissa and Kambatigna in Amharic, is a Highland East Cushitic language within the Cushitic branch of the Afroasiatic language family. Closely related varieties named in the sources include Xambaaro, T’ambaaro, Timbaaro, Alaba, and Qabeena or K’abeena. The language has several dialects, including Tembaaro, Alaba, and Qabeena, and is closely related to Hadiyya and Sidama.

Kambaata has subject–object–verb order, five vowels that may be long or short, ejectives, a retroflexed implosive, and a glottal stop. Both Latin and Ge’ez scripts are used. The New Testament and parts of the Old Testament have been translated into Kambaata; the New Testament was published in the Ethiopian syllabary in 1992 and later republished in Latin letters. Amharic is the language of inter-ethnic communication and the national language of Ethiopia. English is described as the only spoken foreign language and as the language of teaching in secondary schools.

The sources state that Kambaata was largely Christianized during the first period of incorporation into Menelik II’s empire. They also identify Abba Sarecho as the god of Kambaata.
