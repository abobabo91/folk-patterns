## Overview

The Sakalava are an ethnic group primarily found along the western edge of Madagascar, from Toliara in the south to the Sambirano River in the north. They constituted about 6.2 percent of Madagascar’s population, or about 2,079,000 people, in 2018. Their territory extends over about 128,000 square kilometers, from the Onilahy River in the southwest to Nosy Be in the north. Sakalava society includes regional groups with distinct customs and two principal dialects: the northern dialect of Boina and the southern dialect of Menabe. Their history includes Austronesian and Bantu settlement, Arab and European trade, royal dynasties, cattle pastoralism, royal ancestor worship, Tromba ceremonies, and the historical formation of Sakalava kingdoms in western Madagascar.

## Music & performance

The sources used do not identify Sakalava musical genres or describe a detailed performance repertoire. They do record a guitar or zither made of wood, with a gourd resonator and vegetable-fibre strings.

The principal named performance tradition in the sources is **Tromba**. It is a ceremony in which dady priests divine the spirits of deceased royal ancestors and communicate their words to Sakalava people. Tromba has also historically involved ceremonies and processions for deceased royalty, functioning both as a religious event and as a form of community celebration and identity affirmation. The practice has a long association with the Sakalava royal dynasties. It also spread to Mayotte through Andriantsoly and farther to Tanzania through Comorian migration to Zanzibar.

The sources do not describe song forms, musical ensembles, dance accompaniment, instruments used in Tromba, or performance venues beyond the religious and processional contexts mentioned above.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do not provide an annual festival calendar or dates for seasonal celebrations. They describe several religious and royal practices. About 60% of the Sakalava are said to follow Fomba Gasy, a traditional religion centred on royal ancestor worship and the noble dady lineage, which preserved the remains of deceased rulers.

During Tromba, dady priests divine the spirits of dead ancestors and communicate their words. The Sakalava visit memorial sites of royal ancestors to clean and maintain them. A devoted *toñy* is a tree or stone assigned to a spirit in its honour. In a doany, visitors wait until a guardian guides them to the shrines, joins them in prayer, and helps summon ancestral spirits. Visitors may offer money and receive a small piece of limestone called a *tanifoty*, understood in the source as a physical manifestation of an ancestor’s blessing. A white dot is placed on the visitor’s chest before departure.

The sources also describe royal succession, exile, burial, and the transformation of rulers into ancestors who can possess mediums known as the *saha* during Tromba. They do not describe birth, marriage, initiation, or funeral rites in general.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources describe Sakalava history through local tradition, oral histories, and documents. According to local tradition, the founders of the Sakalava kingdom were the Maroseraña, also called Maroseranana, princes described as “those who owned many ports,” from Fiherenana, now Toliara. Merina oral histories and documents in Comoros mention annual expeditions by Sakalava slave raiders through the end of the eighteenth century.

The sources used do not record a Sakalava epic, named folktale, proverb, riddle, or specialist storytelling context. The Sakalava language article states that Malagasy has a tradition of oratory arts and poetic histories and legends. Its best-known national epic is *Ibonia*, about a Malagasy folk hero of the same name, but the source does not identify it as specifically Sakalava.

## Language & religion

Sakalava communities speak at least two distinct Malagasy dialects. The Sakalava of Boina speak a northern Malagasy dialect, while the Sakalava of Menabe speak a southern Malagasy dialect. Southern Sakalava is more closely related to Masikoro, while Northern Sakalava shares linguistic features with Tsimihety. Malagasy is an Austronesian language and dialect continuum, and Sakalava dialects belong to its western varieties.

Malagasy is written in the Latin script, which Western missionaries introduced in the early nineteenth century; the Sorabe script, a local development of the Arabic script, was previously used. The Sakalava religious landscape includes Fomba Gasy, royal ancestor worship, Tromba, Sunni Islam, and Christianity. Sunni Islam arrived through Arab traders and was adopted by Sakalava rulers in the eighteenth century in connection with military support from the Omani and Zanzibar Sultanates. Christianity arrived with European traders and missionaries. The sources state that many Sakalava converts continued traditional practices such as spirit worship.
