## Overview

The Dorze are one of the Gamo communities inhabiting the Gamo Highlands in southern Ethiopia. They speak Dorze, an Omotic tongue, and primarily live in the southern parts of the country, with many residing in villages near Chencha and Arba Minch. According to Ethnologue, they numbered 29,000 individuals in the 1994 census, including 9,910 monolinguals. Some communities live on the peripheries of Addis Ababa, mostly in the Burayu and Sebeta areas. Their material culture is associated with colourful textiles, bamboo architecture, enset fibres, leatherworking, and a polyphonic multi-part vocal music featuring hocket.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Weaving** — Weaving is a primary profession for a number of Dorze, and traditional Dorze textiles are colourful. Cotton spinning and textile technology were attributed to northern Christians who entered the Gamo Highlands from northern Ethiopia; the source identifies Christian Amharas as the primary agents of this transfer.

**Shemma** — During the period of integration into the imperial court system, Dorze craftsmen produced *shemma* cloth for the royal court, provincial governors, and clergy. The source associates this cloth with Amhara weaving and with *tibeb* border techniques.

**Dinguza** — *Dinguza* is described as a heavy, distinctive patterned cotton fabric with bold red, yellow, and black horizontal geometric stripes. It was used to construct wide traditional pants worn by men, as well as specialized ceremonial capes and scarves. The source states that the Dorze adapted the structural form of the Amhara *tiftif meqenet* to create it.

**Netela** — Dorze women used the *netela* as a full wrap skirt rather than adopting the Habesha kemis. The source presents this wrapping technique as an adaptation of northern garment structures.

**Bamboo and enset fibre work** — Before the widespread adoption of cotton, Dorze artisans used bamboo and the enset plant for structural and household purposes. Enset fibres were processed into ropes, livestock tethers, floor mats, and household seating pads.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe a transition from leather garments to handwoven cotton fabrics during the Dorze integration into the imperial court system. Before northern textile technologies were introduced, leather served as the primary material for everyday apparel and specialized ceremonial shields. Dorze women used the *netela* as a full wrap skirt, while men wore wide traditional pants made from *Dinguza*. The source also identifies *Dinguza* as material for ceremonial capes and scarves. The described fabric has bold red, yellow, and black horizontal geometric stripes.

### Architecture

Dorze huts are made from local bamboo and are described as towering, beehive-shaped residential dwellings. The huts can last up to 80 years. Bamboo was split to construct the large structures, and the material also supported domestic tools, fencing, baskets, and walking sticks.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They describe bamboo baskets, fencing, walking sticks, ropes, livestock tethers, floor mats, household seating pads, and leather shields as objects or materials associated with Dorze life.

## Music & performance

Dorze polyphonic multi-part vocal music features a sophisticated use of hocket. The sources identify this vocal practice as a characteristic of Dorze culture.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that Dorze people originally adhered to traditional African religions and that most are members of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, but provide no further account of ritual practice.

## Foodways

The core of the enset plant is described as a dietary staple called *Kocho*. During food processing, the dried leaf sheaths and outer fibres were also used to produce ropes, livestock tethers, floor mats, and household seating pads.

## Language & religion

Dorze is an Afro-Asiatic language spoken in the Gamo Gofa Zone of Ethiopia, and the Wikipedia source describes it more specifically as an Omotic tongue. Alemayehu Abebe reported that, during preliminary fieldwork in 1992, he found 14 kebeles in Chencha woreda with Dorze speakers. The Dorze originally adhered to traditional African religions, while most are members of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church.
