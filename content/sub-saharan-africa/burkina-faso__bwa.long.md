## Overview

The Bwa are a farming society of roughly 300,000 people living in central and western Burkina Faso and south-eastern Mali, in the savanna between the Bani River and the Mouhoun (Black Volta). Their principal towns are Houndé, Boni, Bagassi, Dossi and Pa. They speak Bwa languages, a tight cluster of Gur languages within Niger–Congo, and use Jula for trade and French in schools and administration. Early European travellers confused them with their western neighbours the Bobo, which produced the misleading colonial labels *Bobo Oule* ("Red Bobo" in Jula) and Eastern Bobo — the two peoples share religious forms but are ethnically distinct. In folk-culture terms the Bwa matter for two things above all: the towering polychrome plank masks of the southern villages, and the body scarification that earned the southern Bwa the exonym *Nieniegue*, "scarred Bwa."

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Bwa's decisive contribution to West African surface pattern is carried on carved and painted wood rather than cloth, so the mask corpus and the cotton corpus have to be read together — the same graphic grammar runs across both.

**Nwantantay** — the tall vertical plank mask of the southern Bwa, carved from a single length of light wood, mounted on a disc-like face base and finished in black, white and red pigment. Its surface is a stacked register of geometric fields — checkerboards, zigzags, concentric circles — and it is this planar, diagram-like organisation that separates Bwa masks from the volumetric helmet masks of the Bobo proper.

**Leaf mask** — the northern Bwa idiom, built of bundled and bound leaves and fibre rather than wood, worn in ceremonies for *Dwo*. Because it is made of living bush material and destroyed after use, it belongs to an older stratum of Bwa religion than the purchased wooden-mask complex.

**Do mask** — masks of the cult of *Do* (Dwo), the son Wuro sent to mediate between god and humans; among the Boni Bwa the leaf form is the ceremonial vehicle for Dwo's presence.

**Horizontal and heterogeneous masks** — animal masks whose pattern is iconographically fixed: the butterfly carries concentric circles, the hawk a plain white surface. Heterogeneous types set an ovoid head with round or diamond eyes beneath crescents or small standing human figures.

**Faso dan fani** — the narrow-strip handwoven cotton of Burkina Faso, woven on the men's double-heddle loom and sewn edge-to-edge so that the warp stripes step and offset across the seams. Bwa cotton is the country's most successful cash crop, and the cloth is worn as wrappers, tunics and mask costumes.

**Bogolan** — mud-resist cotton of the Bani–Mouhoun corridor, in which fermented iron-rich mud is painted over an alkaline-dyed ground and the unmudded areas bleached out, leaving pale geometry on a black-brown field.

**Indigo resist** — tie- and stitch-resist indigo dyeing on strip cloth, producing the concentric rings and grid-stamped fields used for women's wrappers.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Checkerboard* — alternating black-and-white squares, read on masks as a moral opposition between initiated knowledge and ignorance; *zigzag* — the crooked path, pattern of proper conduct; *concentric circle* — the butterfly, herald of the first rains; *crescent* — celestial sign on heterogeneous masks; *diamond eye* — spirit sight; *serpent* — the bush spirit of the long, sinuous form; *hawk* — white, unpatterned, speed; *double chameleon* — the paired cast-brass motif associated with fertility; *hornbill* — the commonest bronze figure; *scarification lines* — the raised facial and body registers whose graphics repeat on mask faces.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress is built from *faso dan fani* strip cloth: for men a wide-sleeved tunic over drawstring trousers, for women a wrapped skirt (*pagne*) with a matching shoulder cloth and head-tie. Indigo and bogolan wrappers are kept for market days and ceremonies. Ceremonial dress is inseparable from masking: a *nwantantay* dancer disappears entirely into a full-body costume of dyed and shredded hibiscus or bast fibre, cuffed at wrist and ankle, so that no human skin is visible and the wearer is read as spirit rather than person. Leaf-mask performers wear fresh-cut foliage bound directly onto the body in the same logic. Initiated age grades that accompany the masks wear plain fibre skirts and carry whisks and rattles. Body marking was itself a form of dress: the dense facial and torso scarification of the southern Bwa signalled ethnic belonging and completed initiation, a practice now largely abandoned, taking the exonym *Nieniegue* with it.

### Architecture

Bwa villages are built of *banco* — sun-dried earth, puddled and coursed, then rendered with a mud-and-vegetable-binder plaster that is renewed after each rainy season. The characteristic unit is the *soukala*, a walled family compound of flat-roofed rectangular rooms opening onto an interior court, with the roof terrace used for drying grain and for sleeping in the hot months. Adobe granaries stand inside the compound: round, corbelled, raised on stones against termites and rot, closed with a removable conical thatch cap. The Bwa's long-standing practice of storing grain against lean years — a system French taxation broke, contributing to the 1911–13 famines — was physically embodied in these granaries. Exterior walls carry incised and painted decoration in the same repertoire as the masks: bas-relief and painted registers of checkerboard, zigzag and chevron in white kaolin, black and red earth, applied by women. Sacred earth shrines (*bayiri*, the earth altar tended by the *labie* or earth priest) sit at the village centre as low mounded cones of accumulated libation.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Bwa society recognises three occupational castes — farmers, musicians and blacksmiths — and the smiths are the makers of masks and metal alike. They cast in bronze and brass by the lost-wax method, producing figures, utensils, jewellery and ritual staffs; iron staffs and hoes are forged for shrine and field. Animal iconography carries moral content: hyena for stupidity, bush cow for strength, with the *double chameleon* and the *hornbill* the two commonest cast forms. Household ceramics are hand-built and open-fired water jars, cooking pots and wide-mouthed beer jars. Calabashes are cut into bowls, ladles and drinking vessels and decorated by pyrography — burnt-in geometry using the same zigzag and grid vocabulary as the painted walls.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Cast brass and bronze ornament — rings, bracelets, anklets, pendants — comes from the blacksmith caste rather than from trade. The *double chameleon* pendant is worn specifically by women seeking to conceive, the chameleon standing for transformation and gestation. Cowrie shells are sewn in rows onto mask costumes, belts and dance aprons, and strung as necklaces. The most consequential adornment was permanent: the scarification that named the southern Bwa *Nieniegue*, cut in dense parallel and chevron registers across cheeks, forehead and torso at initiation, marking both ethnic identity and adult status. The same graphic sets are carved and painted onto the faces of wooden masks, so that mask and initiate share a skin.

## Music & performance

Music is not accompaniment to Bwa ceremony but its structural spine, and musicians form one of the three castes. The signature ensemble is a set of end-blown wooden flutes played in groups of five to nine, each player sounding a short repeating figure that interlocks with the others into a composite melody — a hocket technique in which the flutes reproduce the tonal contours of spoken Bwa and so literally speak, naming individuals and reciting phrases. Drums drive the mask dances, and rattles, iron bells and struck calabashes mark the dancers' entries. Mask performance is concentrated in the dry season between February and May, when the fields are empty; the flutes, by contrast, are played daily. Song is organised by age grade: the initiated cohorts sing the praise songs that honour the masks as they appear, and the repertoire of songs and dances was acquired together with the masks themselves when the Bwa purchased the wooden-mask complex from the neighbouring Nuna after the Volta-Bani War — rights to the songs, the costumes, the carving and the choreography were transferred as a single package.

## Dance & theatre

Bwa masquerade is theatre in which the spirit, not the man, is the performer. *Nwantantay* dancers move with the plank held vertical, then sweep it in great figure-eights and arcs that make the painted registers read as motion, the mask's height converting a step into a whipping curve overhead — the performance that gave Christopher Roy's study its title, *Land of the Flying Masks*. Animal masks are danced in character: the serpent mask is carried low and undulating, the hawk snapped and darting. Leaf masks in the northern villages move in processional, less virtuosic fashion, appropriate to Dwo's gravity. Performance is not gendered in the way it is among many of their neighbours — women take full part in mask ceremonies alongside men, praising and attending the masks as members of their age grades, and some wooden mask performances are staged specifically as entertainment for women. Masks appear at initiations, funerals, market festivals and the opening of the agricultural year.

## Festivals & rituals

The Bwa year turns on the agricultural cycle and the masks that punctuate it. The great masquerade season runs February to May, in the dry months between harvest and first planting: masks come out to close the year, purify the village and release the rains. The cult of *Do* is led by the *labie*, the village's eldest man and earth priest, who makes the sacrifices at the *bayiri* earth shrine on behalf of the community — Bwa villages are autonomous and recognise no outside authority, and the council of male elders plus the labie is the whole apparatus of governance and religion. Initiation is the defining life-cycle rite: children are taken out of the village, instructed in the meaning of the mask motifs — the checkerboard's opposition of knowledge and ignorance, the zigzag's crooked path of right conduct — and returned as adult members of a named age grade that will move through life together. Historically initiation also delivered the scarification that made a Bwa recognisable as Bwa. Funerals for elders bring out the full mask complement, since the masks escort the dead to the ancestors and the spirit Lanle, whose power the wooden masks manifest, must be present. Sacrifice at shrines, divination with carved figures, and fertility rites involving chameleon imagery fill the ritual year between the mask seasons.

## Foodways

Bwa cooking rests on savanna grains. Millet and sorghum are pounded and cooked into the stiff porridge that is eaten at every meal, dipped into sauces of baobab leaf, okra, groundnut or dried fish, spiced with *soumbala* — the fermented locust-bean condiment of the West African savanna, pressed into dark balls and sold in market. Rice is grown in the wetter lowlands, and yams, peanuts and maize supplement the grain. Shea butter from wild *Vitellaria* trees is the cooking fat and also a skin and medicinal preparation. Sorghum and millet are malted and brewed into a thick, mildly alcoholic beer (*dolo*), drunk from calabash bowls, poured in libation at the earth shrine and consumed in quantity at funerals and mask ceremonies — beer is a ritual substance before it is a beverage. Grains and fruits serve nutritional and medicinal purposes in the same breath, and the Bwa's granary technology existed to carry a household through a failed season. Islam and Christianity, adopted under the pressure of nineteenth-century Fulani raiding and French occupation, have introduced dietary restraint in some households without displacing the beer-centred ceremonial economy in the villages that keep the masks.

## Oral tradition & literature

Bwa oral tradition is anchored in cosmogony rather than in a long sung epic. The narrative core concerns *Wuro*, the creator who designed the earth in balance, was wounded by human action, and sent his eldest son *Dwo* to serve as the channel between himself and humanity; Wuro's other sons are *Soxo*, spirit of the wilderness and the bush, and *Kwere*, the lightning. These accounts are transmitted inside initiation, not performed publicly, and the mask motifs function as their mnemonic notation — a carver and an initiate can read a plank mask as a sequence of moral propositions. Alongside this sits the flute repertoire, which is itself a speech-surrogate literature: the interlocking flute patterns render the tones of Bwa sentences, so a phrase can be spoken by instruments to an audience that understands it. Christopher D. Roy's fieldwork in the Mouhoun valley over several decades remains the principal documentation of both the narrative and the visual system.

## Language & religion

The Bwa speak Bwa (Bwamu), a closely related set of Gur languages in the Niger–Congo family, dialectally differentiated between northern and southern communities — a split that tracks the leaf-mask/wooden-mask divide. Jula serves as the trade language and French as the language of state. The indigenous religion is animist, organised around Wuro and his sons, the earth shrine and the cult of Do under the *labie*; its material expression is the mask corpus, most of which is Nuna in origin and was purchased, with its songs and dances, in the aftermath of the 1915–16 Volta-Bani War when the Bwa sought the blessings they believed the Nuna enjoyed. Islam and Christianity arrived with Fulani domination and French colonisation respectively and are now both present, generally layered over rather than replacing shrine practice.
