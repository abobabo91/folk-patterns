## Overview

The Bwa are a Gur-speaking agricultural people of the savanna plains along the Black Volta (Mouhoun) in western Burkina Faso, with related communities across the frontier in eastern Mali; their heartland is usually described as the country around Dédougou, Boromo and Solenzo. Their language, Bwamu, belongs to the Gur branch of Niger–Congo and is unrelated to the Mande speech of their Bobo, Bamana and Marka neighbours — a distinction that matters because nineteenth- and early twentieth-century writers habitually conflated the Bwa with the Bobo, labelling them "Bobo-Oulé" or "red Bobo" as against the Mande-speaking "Bobo-Fing." Scholarship since the mid-twentieth century has firmly separated the two, but museum labels still carry the old confusion. Politically the Bwa were famously acephalous: authority rested with the eldest men of lineage segments and with village earth-priests rather than with chiefs, and the absence of a centralising state is usually read as the reason the Bwa mounted one of the most determined anticolonial risings in French West Africa, the Volta-Bani war of 1915–16, whose memory remains locally vivid. Their economy is millet, sorghum, maize, yams, groundnuts and, since the colonial period, cotton, farmed from densely built earthen compounds set amid bush. In material terms the Bwa are best known internationally for their masquerade: the great vertical plank masks, usually called *nwantantay* in the butterfly and abstract board forms, painted in hard-edged black, white and red geometry — checkerboards, zigzags, concentric circles and crescent hooks — that has become one of the most reproduced graphic idioms in African art. Alongside these wood masks stand the entirely different leaf masks of the *Do* cult, bundles of fresh foliage and fibre with no carved element at all, which many Bwa regard as the older and more authentically Bwa of the two traditions. Add to this a strong regional architecture of moulded adobe granaries and bas-relief-painted walls, the *bogolan* and indigo-resist textile complex of the Volta bend, and a robust smithing tradition in iron and lost-wax brass, and the Bwa contribution to the art of the West African savanna is disproportionate to their numbers.

## Material culture

Bwa material culture is overwhelmingly earth, wood, fibre and cotton rather than stone or precious metal: it is the art of a farming society whose most ambitious objects are made for masquerade and whose most enduring ones are dwellings. Its unifying feature is a graphic language — high-contrast geometric field patterns — that migrates freely between mask surface, mud wall, woven strip, pyrographed calabash and scarified skin.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Bwa weaving belongs to the wider Volta-basin narrow-strip tradition in which men weave long bands on double-heddle looms and the bands are sewn edge to edge into cloth, which women then dye, resist or paint.

- **Narrow-strip cotton cloth** (*faso dan fani*) — Hand-spun cotton woven in bands a hand's breadth wide and sewn into wrappers and blankets; the Burkinabè national name for the genre, promoted as a patriotic textile since the 1980s, covers Bwa production along with that of neighbouring peoples.
- **Mud-resist cloth** (*bogolan*) — Cotton cloth painted with fermented river mud over a tannin-treated ground, so the pattern emerges as pale motifs reserved against a dark field; the technique is centred on the Bamana and Marka regions immediately west but is practised and worn in the Bwa zone as well.
- **Indigo resist** — Locally grown indigo used with tied, stitched or folded resists to produce white-on-blue geometry, commonly on women's wrappers and on cloths kept for ceremonial use.
- **Fibre masquerade costume** — The body of most Bwa masks is not cloth but a dense fibre cape of dyed and undyed hibiscus or *Daniellia* fibre, with a woven fringe that conceals the dancer entirely; the cape is as much a part of the mask's identity as the carved plank.
- **Geometric field motifs** (checkerboard, zigzag, concentric circle) — A shared repertoire read as moral and cosmological shorthand: the checkerboard commonly glossed as old and new knowledge or the pairing of opposites, the zigzag as the difficult path of right conduct, the concentric circle as the sun or the eye of the spirit. Glosses vary by village and by initiation grade, and elders treat the fuller explanations as initiatory knowledge.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday Bwa dress is the modest cotton of the savanna farming village, with the visual extravagance reserved almost entirely for masquerade and for the transitional costume of initiates.

- **Men's wrapper and tunic** — Hand-woven cotton trousers and loose tunic, historically with a strip-woven blanket carried over the shoulder in the cold harmattan months.
- **Women's wrapper** (*pagne*) — Two or three strip-woven or indigo-dyed lengths wrapped at the waist, with a second cloth for carrying an infant on the back.
- **Initiate's fibre dress** — Young people undergoing initiation are commonly set apart by fibre skirts, ash or ochre body coating and restricted ornament, marking a period outside ordinary social life.
- **Masquerade fibre cape and hood** — The dancer's full-body covering of knotted and dyed plant fibre, worn with anklets of iron or seed rattles that make the footwork audible.
- **Imported and factory cloth** — Since the colonial period machine-printed cotton has largely displaced hand-woven cloth for daily wear, while hand-woven and resist-dyed pieces have gained ceremonial and identity value.

### Architecture

The Bwa build in puddled adobe, and their compounds are among the most photographed vernacular architecture of Burkina Faso — flat-roofed rectilinear rooms packed into a wall-enclosed cluster, the whole resurfaced each year after the rains.

- **Family compound** (*soukala*) — A cluster of adjoining flat-roofed adobe rooms enclosed by a continuous wall so that the outer face reads as a single fortified block, entered through one or two low doorways and housing an extended patrilineal household with its livestock.
- **Adobe granary** — Moulded earthen storage bin, usually a tapering cylinder with a small top opening and a fitted conical cap of thatch, raised on stones and often the most carefully finished structure in the compound.
- **Bas-relief wall painting** — Exterior walls dressed in low relief and painted in black, white and earth-red geometry by women, who replaster and repaint after the rainy season; the technique is best known from Kassena and Nankana compounds in the southeast of the country but is practised across the adobe belt, the Bwa zone included.
- **Banco earth construction** (*banco*) — The generic regional term for sun-dried earth mixed with chaff, dung or shea residue; the mosque at Banfora and the region's older mosques belong to the same Sudano-Sahelian earthen tradition of buttressed walls and projecting timber.
- **Flat terrace roof** — Beams of *Borassus* palm or hardwood carrying a packed earth terrace used for drying grain and for sleeping in the hot season, reached by a notched log ladder.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Craft specialisation among the Bwa followed the regional pattern: smiths formed an endogamous group with ritual as well as technical authority, and their wives were commonly the potters.

- **Blacksmith's forge** — The smith carved masks, forged hoes and ritual iron, and in many villages held responsibility for the objects of the *Do* cult, a position combining technical skill with ritual danger.
- **Lost-wax brass casting** — Small figures, rings, bells and pendants cast by the wax-thread method characteristic of the Volta basin; Bwa and neighbouring Lobi-Dagara brasswork share forms and probably workshops.
- **Iron staff and ritual iron** — Forged staffs, bells and altar irons set in shrines and carried in ritual, the material itself understood as powerful because of its transformation by fire.
- **Calabash pyrography** — Gourds scraped, cut and burned with a heated blade into fine geometric registers, used as bowls, dippers, seed containers and beer vessels, and carrying the same checkerboard and concentric-circle vocabulary as the masks.
- **Earthenware** — Coil-built and open-fired water jars, cooking pots and beer jars made by women, undecorated or lightly roulette-impressed, with large jars kept for millet beer brewing.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Bwa ornament is modest in material and emphatic in meaning: brass, iron, seeds and cowries rather than gold or silver, and permanent body marking as the primary statement of identity.

- **Scarification pattern** — Facial and body scarification formerly marked lineage and initiation grade, and the same raised linear patterns are cut into the surfaces of masks, explicitly linking the mask's face to a human one. The practice has largely ceased in living generations but remains legible on masks and on older people.
- **Cowrie beadwork** — Cowrie shells sewn to fibre bands, mask crests and belts, valued as former currency and as a sign of wealth and fertility.
- **Brass rings and bracelets** — Cast brass and forged iron bracelets, anklets and finger rings worn as everyday ornament and as protective objects.
- **Seed and cord anklets** — Rattling anklets of hard seeds or small iron bells worn by dancers, so that the mask's rhythm is heard as well as seen.
- **Lip and ear plugs** — Wooden or metal plugs worn by older women in some communities, now rare.

## Music & performance

Bwa music is percussion-led and made for masquerade, work and funerals rather than for courts — the Bwa had none — and its central sound is the paired drum and iron idiophone driving a masked dancer.

- **Drum ensemble** — Sets of single-headed and hourglass drums of differing pitch, played in interlocking patterns; specific rhythms belong to specific masks and are the signal by which spectators identify which spirit is entering the plaza.
- **Iron bell and rattle** — Struck iron bells and gourd rattles supply the metallic pulse that anchors the drum polyrhythm.
- **Transverse flute** — Simple side-blown flutes of reed or millet stalk played by herdboys and in some masquerade ensembles.
- **Xylophone** (*balafon*) — Gourd-resonated wooden xylophone widespread across western Burkina Faso and northern Côte d'Ivoire; strongly associated with Senufo and Mande players, and present in the Bwa region through them. The related Senufo–Bwa–Tagbana *balafon* practice of the Sudanese-Sahelian region has been recognised by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage.
- **Funeral song** — Sung lament and praise performed by women at funerals, the occasion at which masks most often appear.

## Dance & theatre

Masquerade is Bwa theatre: there is no separate stage tradition, and the performance combines choreographed dance, characterisation, drum dialogue and, in the leaf-mask ceremonies, ritual that spectators are not free to read as entertainment.

- **Plank mask dance** (*nwantantay*) — Danced with a tall vertical board above the face, the dancer whipping the plank in wide arcs and figure-eights so that the painted geometry reads as moving pattern; the mask is danced at initiations, funerals and market-season celebrations.
- **Butterfly mask** — A very wide horizontal disc-and-wing mask, spun on the axis of the dancer's head in rapid rotation, commonly associated with the swarming of butterflies after the first rains and thus with planting.
- **Hawk and serpent masks** — Bird masks with long lateral wings and tall serpent masks several metres high, each with its own rhythm, gait and lineage association; the serpent form is among the tallest masks made anywhere in West Africa.
- **Leaf mask** (*Do*) — A costume of fresh green leaves and fibre with no carved face, worn in the ceremonies of *Do*, the son of the creator god and the force of renewal in the bush; its dance is quieter and more processional than the wood masks and is bound to the agricultural calendar.
- **Masked initiation cycle** (*lanle*) — Young men's initiation into the mask society, in which initiates learn the rhythms, the carved vocabulary and the glosses of the painted motifs; knowledge is graded, and full explanation is withheld from the uninitiated.

## Festivals & rituals

Bwa ritual life turns on the agricultural year and on the earth shrine rather than on a royal calendar; the two great poles are the mask festivals of the dry season and the propitiation of the land before planting.

- **Earth shrine rites** (*bayiri*) — Offerings at the shrine of the land, made through the earth-priest of the founding lineage, to secure rain, fertility and the moral health of the village; the term is used across the Voltaic region for the land-cult complex.
- **Dry-season mask festival** — After the harvest and before the rains, masks emerge over several days for initiation, for the closing of mourning and for public celebration; since 1996 the biennial Festival International des Masques et des Arts (FESTIMA) at Dédougou has gathered masquerade troupes from across West Africa and has become the best-known public face of Bwa mask culture.
- **Funeral masquerade** — Masks appear at the second funeral of an elder to escort the dead out of the village and to restore order among the living.
- **Do ceremonies** — Leaf-mask observances marking planting and the renewal of the bush, in which the fresh, perishable costume is understood to embody wild vegetal life itself.
- **Initiation** — Seclusion, instruction and return of age-sets, historically accompanied by scarification and by the granting of rights to dance particular masks.

## Foodways

Bwa cooking is the millet-and-sauce cuisine of the savanna: a stiff grain porridge eaten with a vegetable sauce enriched with shea butter, dried fish or a little meat, and millet beer as the social and ritual drink. Sorghum and pearl millet are pounded and cooked into a thick paste, known regionally as *tô* (or *saghbo*), served with sauces of baobab leaf, sorrel, okra or the fermented seeds of the néré tree, whose pungent cakes, *soumbala*, are the standard savoury seasoning across Burkina Faso. Shea (*karité*) nuts gathered by women are boiled, roasted and pounded into butter used for cooking, lighting and cosmetics; groundnuts, cowpeas, yams, maize and sesame fill the rest of the field calendar. Guinea fowl, chicken and goat are eaten sparingly and usually in connection with sacrifice or with a guest. Millet beer, *dolo*, brewed by women in large earthenware jars and sold in courtyard drinking houses, is the indispensable substance of Bwa sociability and of ritual: no masquerade, funeral or work party proceeds without it, and the brewing calendar structures much of village life. Islamic observance among Bwa Muslims has produced local abstention from *dolo*, which has in places become a visible line between religious communities.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Bwa had no professional caste of praise-singers on the Mande model, and their verbal art is correspondingly domestic and initiatory rather than courtly. Narrative transmission rests with elders and with the officers of the mask society: origin accounts tracing village founding and the descent of lineages from the arrival of the first ancestor at the earth shrine; etiological stories explaining the gift of masks by the spirits of the wild and the relation of *Do* to the creator; and trickster cycles in which the hare and the hyena work out, comically, the boundaries of proper conduct. Proverb is the prestige verbal form, deployed in dispute settlement by lineage elders, and the painted motifs of the masks are themselves commonly described as proverbs made visible — each sign attached to a saying or an injunction taught progressively through the grades of initiation, which is why published glosses vary between villages and why elders resist flattening them into a fixed key. Funeral oratory and women's lament carry genealogical content, naming the dead into their lineage. The Volta-Bani war of 1915–16 has generated a substantial body of local historical narrative, transmitted orally and only later collected, in which the Bwa villages' resistance to French conscription and taxation is remembered with specific names and places. Bwamu-language literacy has developed mainly through mission translation and adult literacy programmes, and a modest printed literature in Bwamu now exists alongside the dominant French.

## Language & religion

Bwamu (Buamu) is a Gur language of the Niger–Congo family, with dialect clusters usually divided into northern, southern and western groups whose speakers do not always understand one another readily; French is the language of school and administration, and Jula (Dioula) serves as the market lingua franca across western Burkina Faso, so trilingualism is common. The indigenous religion recognises a remote creator, commonly named *Difini* or by cognate forms, and directs practical worship toward his son *Do*, the force of renewal manifest in the bush and embodied in the leaf masks; toward the earth, approached through the shrine and the earth-priest; and toward lineage ancestors. Blacksmiths hold a distinct ritual position, and in many villages the *Do* cult is theirs to administer. Bwa communities are notably divided in how they relate the two masking traditions: those that regard the leaf masks as the sole legitimate form generally treat the carved plank masks as a borrowing from Mande-speaking neighbours, while villages that dance wood masks hold both. Islam spread into the region through Marka and Jula trade and is well established in the towns; Catholic and Protestant missions worked in the area from the early twentieth century and have substantial followings. As commonly reported across the region, adherence is layered rather than exclusive: households may be nominally Muslim or Christian and still contribute to earth-shrine offerings and to the funeral obligations that bring out the masks.
