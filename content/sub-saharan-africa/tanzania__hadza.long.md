## Overview

The Hadza, or Hadzabe, are a hunter-gatherer people of southwest Arusha Region in Tanzania, based in Baray ward of Karatu District and living around the Lake Eyasi basin in the central Rift Valley and on the neighbouring Serengeti Plateau. In 2015 there were between 1,200 and 1,300 Hadza in Tanzania, of whom only about a third still subsisted exclusively by foraging. Their language, Hadzane, is a language isolate with click consonants, once grouped with the Khoisan languages but no longer thought related to anything; it has roughly 1,000 speakers and UNESCO classes it as vulnerable. In folk-culture terms the Hadza matter because their material world is a foraging toolkit — bows, poisoned arrows, digging sticks, honey containers, hide bags — carried on the back and rebuilt as camps move, and because their egalitarian camp life, oral history and *epeme* rite have been documented continuously since the early twentieth century.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources describe no loom weaving, dyeing or cloth-making among the Hadza. What the museum objects show instead is a pattern tradition in beadwork applied to hide and to imported cloth, and in the working of skin itself.

**Beaded hide skirt** — A married woman's skirt of impala hide, decorated with beads, cowrie shells and a bell: hide as the ground, trade goods as the ornament.

**Beaded cloth apron** — A married woman's apron consisting of a strip of cloth decorated with coloured beads strung together with cord. The cloth is the only textile in the collection, and it is a support for beadwork rather than a patterned fabric in its own right.

**Feather-and-leather head-dress** — A man's dance head-dress of ostrich feathers mounted on leather, with glass beads for decoration — feather, hide and glass bead combined in one ceremonial object.

**Skin garments** — A married woman's upper garment of antelope hide and a man's apron of genet skin: the pelt's own colour and marking, in the genet's case spotted, does the work that pattern does elsewhere.

### Clothing & dress

Hadza dress recorded in the museum collection is made of animal skin, with glass beads and other traded items as decoration. Married women wore an impala-hide skirt decorated with beads, cowrie shells and a bell, an upper garment of antelope hide, and an apron made from a strip of cloth beaded with coloured beads on cord. Men wore a pubic apron of genet skin. Both sexes wore leather sandals. Women's headwear includes head-bands of glass beads, one of them in white, red and blue. Men and boys wore a head ornament made from the hair of a zebra's mane and leg ornaments of soft hide. Ceremonial dress is distinguished by the man's dance head-dress of ostrich feathers on leather with glass beads, and by dance bells of metal tied to a leather strap around the ankle. Practical gear is part of dress rather than separate from it: the sources describe women carrying knives, shoes and clothing along with digging sticks and grass berry baskets, and small items kept in a pouch worn around the neck; men carried axes, bows, arrows, knives, small honey pots and fire drills.

### Architecture

Hadza shelter is built for a camp that moves. Shelters can be put up in a few hours, and most of what a person owns can be carried on their back. Camps of 20 to 30 people move to resolve conflict, to abandon a place where someone fell ill and died, to follow water and game between the seasons, to reach stands of tubers or berry trees in season, and to relocate temporarily to the site of a large kill such as a giraffe. In the dry season people sleep under trees. Hadza oral history frames house-building as a comparatively recent acquisition: the giants of the first epoch slept under trees as the Hadza do in the dry season today, the second-epoch people lived in caves, and only the third-epoch people of *hamakwanebee* built huts of the kind the Hadza build now. Early twentieth-century European visitors, among them Erich Obst, described the Hadza building houses in the same style as they do today. The sources do not describe the materials, roof form or decoration of the huts, and name no house type.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

No pottery appears in the sources. Iron the Hadza did not smelt: their third-epoch ancestors traded with non-foraging peoples for iron to make knives and arrowheads, and the Sukuma exchanged worn metal tools, which the Hadza reworked into arrowheads, for the right to hunt elephants in Hadzaland. An axe, *ato*, has an iron blade in a wooden handle, its socket burned through the shaft with a piece of red-hot iron. Hunting gear includes bows of wood and nuchal ligament, including a child's bow; wooden arrows fletched with guinea-fowl feathers and bound with nuchal ligament; a poisoned arrow with an iron head, wooden shaft and vulture-feather fletching; a harpoon-arrow or dart; a hide quiver; and a wooden sheath with a decorative skin band. The arrow poison is made from the branches of the shrub *Adenium coetaneum*. Women's digging sticks are wood with the point hardened in the fire. Other objects are a fire-drill of wood with an ostrich feather, a bird snare of nuchal ligament with tail-hair nooses, a leather impala-hide bag with a sewn-on patch sealed with beeswax, a carrying bag called *mbogoshi* of impala or dik-dik leather, and a smoking pipe of soft sedimentary rock roughed out with an axe, shaped with a knife, rubbed down on an abrasive rock, bored with an awl and strengthened with a cylinder of hide. Three stone dolls, two 'female' and one 'male', are decorated with trade beads, dried mud, spotted beads and cloth tied round the waist.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Hadza ornament combines glass trade beads with materials taken from the hunt. Necklaces recorded include one of beads with tail bones from a baboon, one of beads with two circles of beads worked into it, a child's necklace of beads and acacia pods, and one of red and white beads strung on cord with empty cartridge-cases. Women wore beaded head-bands, one in white, red and blue glass beads, and beaded arm-bands. An arm ornament is made of dik-dik skin, and two leg ornaments for a man or boy are of soft hide. Men and boys wore a head ornament of zebra mane hair. The sources record no tattooing, no henna and no hair practices.

## Music & performance

The sources name no Hadza musical instruments beyond items used in dance. The objects that carry sound are dance bells of metal tied to a leather strap round the ankle and a dance rattle made from a gourd. No song genres, singers or performance repertoires are described.

## Dance & theatre

Dance among the Hadza is recorded as ritual rather than entertainment, and the sources name one dance: the monthly *epeme* dance, held by men at the new moon. The museum collection preserves its equipment — a man's dance head-dress of ostrich feathers mounted on leather and decorated with glass beads, ankle bells of metal on a leather strap, a gourd rattle, and a dance container for animal fat catalogued as part of a set. *Epeme* is more than a dance: it covers the Hadza concept of manhood, of hunting, and of the relationship between the sexes. A man becomes an *epeme* man, a "true" adult, by killing large game, usually in his early twenties, and only *epeme* men may eat certain parts of large animals. Hadza oral history ascribes the founding of the *epeme* rite to the hairless giants of the second epoch, the *xhaaxhaanebee*, who were also the first to use medicines and protective charms. No dramatic, masking or puppet tradition appears in the sources.

## Festivals & rituals

The Hadza do not follow a formal religion and it has been claimed that they do not believe in an afterlife. Ritual life is tied to the moon and to the hunt rather than to an annual calendar. Prayers are offered during hunts to *Ishoko*, the Sun, or to *Haine*, the moon, and the Hadza believe they go to Ishoko when they die. Two named rites are recorded: the *epeme* dance, performed by men each month at the new moon, and *maitoko*, the less frequent circumcision and coming-of-age ceremony for women. Marriage is predominantly monogamous, with no social enforcement of monogamy; after marriage the couple is free to settle with either the husband's or the wife's family, an ambilocal pattern common among foragers, and among the Hadza slightly more couples live with the wife's kin. Death shapes settlement directly rather than through funerary ceremony: a camp may be abandoned when someone falls ill and dies there, because illness is associated with the place where it was contracted. Children are raised communally, with high-quality care given by many people, related and unrelated. The sources record no annual or seasonal festival.

## Foodways

The Hadza forage selectively and opportunistically, adjusting the diet by season and by what is locally available, so that some camps lean on tubers, others on berries, others on meat. In the wet season the diet is mostly honey, fruit, tubers and occasional meat; meat's share rises in the dry season, when game concentrates around water. Tubers and baobab fruit supply about 100 to 150 grams of fibre a day. Honey provides roughly 10 to 20 percent of calories, and its acquisition depends on a relationship with the greater honeyguide, *Indicator indicator*: the bird leads people to wild bees' nests, sometimes calling with a distinctive chatter, sometimes drawn and held by men whistling, striking trees and shouting. The hunter smokes the bees into submission and chops the nest open with his axe, then often burns, buries or hides the leftover wax to keep the bird hungry and willing to guide again. To reach honey or fruit high in a baobab, the Hadza drive pointed sticks into the trunk as a ladder — a technique that also appears in a folk tale. Men hunt in pairs in the dry season, lying in wait whole nights at waterholes with poisoned bows and arrows; game eaten includes impala, dik-dik, kudu, baboon, vervet monkey, bush baby, shrew, warthog, bushpig, various birds and fish. Women gather tubers, berries and greens with digging sticks and grass baskets. Only *epeme* men may eat certain parts of large game. Most Hadza can no longer sustain themselves in the bush without supplementary food such as *ugali*.

## Oral tradition & literature

Hadzane is an entirely oral language, and Hadza history is told as a sequence of four epochs, each with its own inhabitants. The world began with hairy giants, the *akakaanebee* (first ones) or *geranebee* (ancient ones), who had neither tools nor fire, ran game down until it dropped, ate meat raw and slept under trees. The hairless giants of the *xhaaxhaanebee* (in-between ones) cooked with fire, hunted with dogs because animals had grown wary, lived in caves, used medicines and charms, and began the *epeme* rite. The smaller people of *hamakwanebee* (recent days) invented the bow and arrow, cooked in containers, built huts, traded for iron and invented the gambling game *lukuchuko*. The present epoch, *hamayishonebee* (those of today), is discussed with named people and places and generations counted. Folk tales feature the honeyguide in both bird and human form, a woman harvesting wild honey while the tale asserts honey-gathering is men's work, and the Isanzu as favourable and sometimes heroic, in contrast to the Iraqw and the cattle-raiding Maasai. Hadzane also carries about a dozen "celebratory" or "triumphal" names for dead animals, used to announce a kill; grammatically they are imperative verbs rather than nouns. A lexicon was compiled by Kirk Miller with Mariamu Anyawire, G.G. Bala and Bonny Sands, and a practical orthography devised by Miller and Anyawire, though as of 2015 no Hadza speakers were using it.

## Language & religion

Hadzane is a language isolate with click consonants — one of only three languages in East Africa to have them — and is notable for clicks occurring inside words, a pattern shared with Sandawe and the Nguni Bantu languages but not with the Khoisan languages of southern Africa. It has five vowels, CV syllable structure, masculine and feminine gender, and flexible word order with a default of verb–subject–object; gender is used metaphorically, so that a normally feminine word becomes masculine if the thing is notably thin. There are no dialects, only some regional vocabulary, chiefly Bantu loans. Native numerals are *itchâme* 'one' and *piye* 'two'; 'three' is a Datooga loan and 'four', 'five' and 'ten' are Sukuma. Fluency in Hadzane is the most important marker of being Hadza; many now also speak Swahili. Religion is not formal: prayers go to Ishoko the Sun and Haine the moon, ritual centres on the *epeme* dance and *maitoko*, and repeated missionary efforts at conversion since the 1960s have been largely unsuccessful.
