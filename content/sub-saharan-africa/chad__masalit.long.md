## Overview

The Masalit — who call themselves *masala* or *masara*, and are also recorded as Kana Masaraka, Mesalit and Massalit — are a people of the Chad–Sudan borderland. Their heartland is the far west of Darfur around Geneina, the capital of West Darfur state; on the Chadian side of the frontier they live in the Ouaddaï Region, and smaller communities are found in South Darfur and in Al Qadarif State in eastern Sudan. Ethnologue counted 462,000 Masalit speakers in 2011, 350,000 of them in Sudan; a group known as the Massalat moved further west into central-eastern Chad and numbered 30,000 at the 1993 Chadian census, though only ten speakers of the language were reported there in 1991. Their language belongs to the Maban group of the Nilo-Saharan family. In folk-culture terms they matter as a millet-and-peanut farming people with their own sultanate tradition, a distinctive conical house form, and a Muslim practice layered over older custom.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used for this profile — the Wikipedia articles on the Masalit people and their language, and the catalogue records of the two Masalit objects shown here — describe no textile or weaving tradition, so none of the named cloth, mat or plaiting traditions indexed for this region can be documented for the Masalit from them. The only craft materials the object records name are iron, leather and wood, worked into an arm-dagger and its sheath.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources do not describe Masalit dress, everyday or ceremonial. One item of personal equipment is documented: the **arm-dagger**, held by the British Museum in two examples, each a blade of iron and leather paired with a sheath of wood and leather. A dagger worn on the arm is carried on the body rather than at the belt, which places it among the things a person wears rather than merely owns, but the catalogue records give no account of who wore it, on which arm, or on what occasions, and nothing in the sources connects it to a particular garment, headcloth or footwear.

### Architecture

The one building form the sources describe is the typical Masalit dwelling: **conical in shape and built of wood and thatch**. This is the house form of a settled farming population in the dry savanna west of Darfur, raised from the two materials that are locally at hand — timber for the frame and grass thatch for the cone that sheds the seasonal rain. The sources say nothing further about how such a house is laid out, whether dwellings are grouped into a fenced compound, how a homestead is divided between sleeping huts, granaries and shaded work space, or whether any part of the structure carries painted or incised decoration. Nor do they describe mosque architecture, the seats of the sultans who ruled the Masalit state between 1874 and the treaty of 1908, or any urban building tradition in Geneina, the town that is now the Masalit centre.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Metalwork is the one craft the objects in this atlas attest. Both British Museum pieces are **arm-daggers with sheaths**: the dagger of iron and leather, the sheath of wood and leather. The combination tells something about the workshops involved — a smith forging the blade, and a worker in hide and wood building the grip and the scabbard around it — but the catalogue entries are brief and record no maker, place of manufacture, date, blade profile or decoration. The sources describe no Masalit pottery, no basketry or matting, no gourd vessels, and no wooden household forms, and they name no agricultural implement used in the cultivation of the peanuts, millet and sorghum that the Masalit grow.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used here record no Masalit jewellery, beadwork, henna practice, scarification, tattooing or hairdressing.

## Music & performance

Neither Wikipedia article on the Masalit describes musical instruments, song genres or performance occasions, and Chad has no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription that could be cited for them, so the sources used do not cover Masalit music. What the sources do document, and what bears on how verbal art would be performed, is the sociolinguistic split within the language itself: Masalit is spoken in a **"heavy"** variety, used by higher-ranking people and in the countryside, with a complicated agglutinative grammar, and a **"light"** variety used at home and in the market, grammatically simplified and carrying many loans from Sudanese Arabic, the region's lingua franca and the language of education. Any sung or spoken performance among the Masalit therefore has two registers available to it, one associated with rank and rural speech and the other with everyday exchange, and most speakers command Arabic as well — except in the central part of Masalit country, where the Nilo-Saharan language is what is chiefly spoken.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used do not cover Masalit dance or dramatic performance.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe no festival calendar and no life-cycle rites for the Masalit. What they do record about the religious ground on which such observances rest is that the Masalit are Muslim, and that their religious practice incorporates older beliefs and customs alongside Islam. Islam reached the area around the seventeenth century, carried by wandering Muslim mystics rather than by conquest or state decree — an arrival by itinerant holy men that is the usual setting in which local practice and Islamic observance combine rather than displace one another. The sources do not say which observances belong to which layer, nor do they name any annual gathering, seasonal rite tied to the millet and peanut harvest, or ceremony connected with birth, initiation, marriage or burial. They also give no account of ritual life around the Masalit sultanate, whose rulers — Hajjam Hasab Allah from 1874, Ismail Abdel Nebi from 1883, and Taj al-Din Isma'il after 1903 — are described in the sources only in political and military terms.

## Foodways

The Masalit are primarily subsistence agriculturalists. Their two principal crops are **peanuts** and **millet**, and in the southern part of their territory they grow a wider range of crops including **sorghum** — the shift reflecting the higher rainfall as one moves south from the dry country around Geneina. That is the whole of what the sources used say about Masalit food. They name no dish, no method of grinding or fermenting the grain, no bread or porridge form, no ceremonial food, and no tea or coffee custom, and they state no dietary rule beyond the fact that the Masalit are Muslim.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources do not summarise any Masalit tale, epic or proverb, but they do point to where such material has been collected and published. John Edgar's "Masalit stories" appeared in *African Languages and Cultures* in January 1990, and his *A Masalit Grammar. With Notes on Other Languages of Darfur and Wadai* was published in Berlin in 1989 in the series *Sprache und Oralität in Afrika* — a series title that itself places Masalit among the languages studied for their oral as well as their grammatical dimension. Alsadig Adam Abdo's contrastive analysis of Masalit and English was submitted at the University of Khartoum in November 2013. One piece of tradition is itself an origin narrative rather than a written record: the Masalit trace their beginnings to Tunisia, hold that they migrated through Chad, and say they settled at last in what is now Sudan. The sources name two Masalit public figures, the activist Usumain Baraka, born 1994, and Khamis Abakar, 1964–2023, governor of West Darfur.

## Language & religion

Masalit belongs to the Maban group of the Nilo-Saharan family and is written in the Latin script. Its closest relatives are Marfa, Maba and Karanga, with which it shares 45%, 42% and 36% of its vocabulary respectively. It has several dialects: the variety spoken in South Darfur differs from that of West Darfur, and a northern dialect is spoken east and north of Geneina. Beside the dialects stand the two sociolects described above, "heavy" and "light". Most Masalit are bilingual in Arabic, and the sounds /z/ and /x/ enter the language only through Arabic loanwords; occasional click sounds are reported but considered rare. The Masalit are Muslim, and their practice incorporates traditional beliefs and customs, Islam having arrived in the area in about the seventeenth century with itinerant Muslim mystics.
