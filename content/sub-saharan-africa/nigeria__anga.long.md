## Overview

The Anga — more accurately the *Ngas*, also recorded as *Agas* and *Angas* — are a people of Plateau State in central Nigeria, numbering roughly a million according to recent studies. They speak Ngas, an Afro-Asiatic language. Their settlements lie on the lowlands and the south-eastern edge of the Jos Plateau, where they form the largest group in the Jos highlands; Pankshin is the major town, and the districts of Ampang (also called Gyangyan), Amper and Kabwir carry the older settlement pattern. Colonial-era photographs and collections distinguish *Hill Anga* from *Plain Anga* communities, a division still visible in the objects museums hold from them. For folk-culture purposes the Ngas matter as a plateau farming people whose granite-strewn terrain shaped both their fields and their houses, and whose narrow-strip weaving, reed zithers and harvest festival are documented in museum catalogues and ethnographic writing.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Mbat** — the narrow strip cloth that is the one named Ngas textile in the collection. Museum examples use cotton throughout: a warp of white and brown (or brown/black) yarn and a weft of white, with a single pattern band running down the centre of the strip formed by the darker warp threads. The warp ends at both ends of the strip are left loose as a fringe rather than hemmed. Both hand-spun and four-ply machine-spun versions exist side by side in the same collection, so the technique persisted across the shift to bought yarn without changing the format of the cloth.

**Strip-sewn man's bag** — narrow-strip weaving carried into a made-up object. One bag is built from seven hand-woven strips sewn together selvedge to selvedge, plain weave, with bleached white wefts and warps of bleached white and dyed green arranged so that each strip carries a single green stripe along its centre. The cut ends are hand-hemmed, and inside the bag the fringe of the original strip cloth survives — the maker reused finished cloth rather than weaving to shape.

**Coiled basketry** — coil-woven work in vegetable fibre or leaf strip, including a basket with a pattern worked into the coiling in red, a broad-rimmed grass hat, and circular food covers for bowls, one of them fitted with a leather handle.

**Netted fibre bag** — a closely worked net of white vegetable-fibre string, with a string carrying handle and loose pendent strings hanging at both sides and at the base.

**Motif vocabulary.** Central warp pattern band (a single dark or green stripe down the middle of a narrow strip), inwoven red pattern in coiled basketry, geometric ornament burnt into wood, black burnt bands and lines on pale wood, rows of applied punched bosses on pottery, relief patterning in the mud of house walls.

### Clothing & dress

The one garment in the collection is a dress of tanned skin, probably goat, whose central area has been peeled and dyed black in a pattern that follows the natural outline of the hide — the shape of the animal, not an imposed grid, sets the design. Photographs of Hill and Plain Anga show men in loin-cloths, with caps, head-cloths, and a cloth worn across one shoulder. Women are photographed wearing a waist ornament with bunches of string or leaves hanging at front and back, arm and neck ornaments, ear-plugs and a narrow head-band, and carrying infants in slings of cloth or animal hide on the back; one woman carries a calabash and balances a rolled reed rain cape on her head while walking with a stick. Dress marking rank is recorded in a single portrait, of a man identified as the chief of Garram: a tunic and cap, an ivory bracelet, a spotted cat hide across one shoulder, a whip in his hand and a long pipe in his mouth. The sources do not describe a distinct costume for weddings or funerals.

### Architecture

Ngas building answers directly to the plateau. On the plains of Amper the soil is strewn with granite, farmers work terraced fields, and the boulders themselves are used as foundations and as walling for houses. Photographs of compounds show clusters of circular buildings with thatched roofs, set close together and including structures that read as granaries; in one village the thatch has plants growing over it. The decorative element the photographs single out is relief patterning worked into the mud of exterior walls, on dwellings and on the granary-like structures alike, so that a compound presents several patterned surfaces at once. Doorways serve as sitting places: one image shows a man and a child seated in one, the man holding a young goat. The sources do not name Ngas house types in the vernacular or describe their interiors.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Ngas pottery in the collection runs from plain vases and a black burnished bowl to a pipe bowl and a water-pot in terracotta, circular with a pointed base and incised ornament. The most ambitious piece is a large ritual beer vessel: an open bowl above, joined by four tubes to a big spheroid receptacle below, with rows of applied punched bosses around the upper part of the lower chamber. A small solid pottery figure is also held. Ironwork is weaponry and edge tools — tanged axe blades set into wooden handles, some blades engraved, some handles decorated with pokerwork or sheathed in leather, one of them strip-plaited and fitted with a leather loop; one axe handle is weighted with three iron rings. Spearheads are forged iron, and bows are simple wooden staves with bevelled ends strung with hide or with a string fixed through drilled holes. In wood: a four-pronged comb, a four-legged stool with burnt geometric ornament, and a pale-wood spoon and food-stirrer carrying black burnt bands and lines.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ornament is documented photographically rather than by collected pieces. Women wear bead necklaces, other neck ornaments, arm ornaments and head-bands, with ear-plugs in both cylindrical and disc form and disc-shaped lip-plugs. Hair is dressed into a bowl-shaped style, and one photograph catches the work itself: two women dressing the hair of two others with small pointed tools, while a fifth holds a child. Men in the images wear arm and wrist ornaments; the chief of Garram is recorded wearing an ivory bracelet. The sources do not mention tattooing or scarification.

## Music & performance

Two instrument types are securely Ngas in the collection. The first is a reed **zither-rattle**, an instrument that is one object doing two jobs: lengths of reed or grass stem are bound together at top and bottom, five groups of three cane strings are set across two reed bridges, and the back carries a central woven panel of broad leaf strips worked into the cane. That panel closes a cavity filled with loose seeds or small pebbles, so the body of the zither rattles as it is played. The end lashings are tied in figure-of-eight form, and one example has a small piece of manufactured cloth attached at one end. The second is a **leg rattle** for dancing: six iron pieces hung with rings and strung on hide thongs, worn on the body so that the dancer's own steps sound it. Horn playing is the ensemble practice the photographs record — two men playing horns in one image, and in another a row of Hill Anga men and boys standing outdoors playing horns together, in loin-cloths and head-cloths, several with arm ornaments. The sources name no song genres, no vocal repertoire and no individual musicians.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is attested by its equipment rather than described. The iron leg rattle is catalogued explicitly as a dance rattle, and a masquerade helmet mask made of fibre and abrus seed shows masked performance among the Ngas. The sources do not name any dance, describe the masquerade's occasion or actors, or record a theatrical tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The Ngas keep one major festival, **Tsafi Tar**, also called **Mos Tar**, held at harvest time. Within it comes a brief rite called **Shooting the Moon**, which marks the close of one season and the opening of the next — the festival is a hinge in the agricultural year rather than a fixed calendar date. The ritual apparatus in the collection fits a festival economy of communal drinking and eating: the four-tubed pottery beer vessel is catalogued as ritual equipment, and the decorated skin dress is recorded as ceremonial. The masquerade helmet mask belongs to the same ceremonial register. A ritual belief rather than a rite is the best-documented piece of Ngas religious practice: *tuput*, "to die and come out", holds that a person who observed certain ritual practices in life may, after death, have their soul return to the body within two days and carry on living as though the death had not happened. The sources do not describe Ngas birth, initiation, marriage or funeral ceremonies.

## Foodways

Ngas farming is cereal farming on made ground. In the granite-littered plains of Amper district crops are grown on terraced fields, and the grains named are millet, guinea corn and maize. The household equipment collected from the Ngas is the equipment of grain and beer: a spoon that doubles as a food-stirrer, coil-woven circular covers for keeping bowls of food covered, coiled baskets, a netted carrying bag, and the compound photographs showing what read as granaries standing among the dwellings. Beer is the one prepared drink the catalogue identifies, through the large two-chamber pottery vessel made for it. Smoking is documented twice over — a pottery pipe bowl in the collection, and photographs of men smoking, one a long pipe. The sources name no Ngas dishes, no methods of preparation and no dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ngas origins are carried in local folklore rather than in a written chronicle. The tradition holds that the people came from Bornu, moved through a series of villages, and settled on the highlands of Plateau State, the migrating body splitting into sub-groups that took up the districts of Pankshin, Ampang, Amper and Kabwir. The narrative names leaders: Gwallam led the settlers at Kabwir, and Kendim was chief of the Amper people. Later movements populated the highlands of the Jos Plateau. The fullest documentation of Ngas oral material is Deirdre LaPin's *Sons of the Moon* (Institute for the Study of Human Issues, 1984), whose title points to the moon imagery that also gives the harvest festival its Shooting the Moon rite. The sources record no epic, no proverb collection and no modern literary movement in Ngas.

## Language & religion

Ngas is an Afro-Asiatic language, and the sources note no dialect divisions or writing tradition for it, though the collection's own distinction between Hill Anga and Plain Anga communities suggests a geographic split in usage. Many Ngas practise a syncretic form of Christianity in which pre-Christian belief has not been displaced but absorbed: the clearest instance is the survival of *tuput*, the conviction that the properly observant dead may return to the body within two days and resume life. Ritual objects — the beer vessel, the fibre-and-seed helmet mask, the ceremonial skin dress — indicate a body of practice the sources used here do not otherwise describe.
