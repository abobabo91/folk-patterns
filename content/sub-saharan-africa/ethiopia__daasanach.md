## Overview

The Daasanach, also known as the Marille or Geleba, are an ethnic group inhabiting parts of Ethiopia, Kenya, and South Sudan. Their main homeland is in the Debub Omo Zone of the South Ethiopia Regional State, adjacent to Lake Turkana, while the Daasanach language is spoken along the Lower Omo River and on the shores of Lake Turkana. According to the 2007 national census, 48,067 people lived in Ethiopia, including 1,481 urban dwellers. The Daasanach are a descendant group of the Maliri people and are traditionally pastoralists who have become primarily agropastoral. Their folk-cultural life is closely connected with cattle, goats, sheep, seasonal cultivation, migration, egalitarian social relations, age sets, and clan lineages.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Daasanach women wear a pleated cowskin skirt together with necklaces and bracelets. The sources state that women who are not circumcised cannot wear clothes and are called animals or boys. Boys are circumcised. The available information therefore documents women’s cowskin clothing and adornment, but not a fuller division between everyday and ceremonial dress.

### Architecture

Daasanach people who herd cattle live in dome-shaped houses. These houses are made from a frame of branches and covered with hides and woven boxes. The woven boxes are also used to carry possessions on donkeys when the Daasanach migrate. Inside, the huts have a hearth, and mats cover the floor for sleeping.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Daasanach women wear necklaces and bracelets. The sources also describe female and male circumcision: women are circumcised by removing the clitoris, and boys are circumcised. Women who are not circumcised cannot marry or wear clothes.

They provide information about the Daasanach language, including its Cushitic classification, large number of noun classes, irregular verb system, implosive consonants, and spelling traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe several life-cycle and social practices. Women are circumcised by removing the clitoris, and boys are circumcised. Women who are not circumcised are called animals or boys and cannot get married or wear clothes. Women often marry in their late teens, while men often marry in their early twenties. Men with large herds often take multiple wives.

Daasanach social organization involves age sets and clan lineages, both of which involve strong reciprocal relations. The sources describe the society as highly egalitarian.

## Foodways

The Daasanach are primarily agropastoral. When the Omo River and its delta flood, they grow sorghum, maize, pumpkins, and beans. Otherwise, they rely on goats and cattle, which provide milk and are slaughtered in the dry season for meat and hides. Sorghum is cooked with water into a porridge eaten with a stew. Corn is usually roasted, and sorghum is fermented into beer.

The Dies, or lower class, are people who have lost their cattle and their way of living. They live on the shores of Lake Turkana, where they hunt crocodiles and fish. Although their status is low because of their lack of cattle, the Dies help herders with crocodile meat and fish in return for meat.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record language-preservation and translation activity. Jim Ness and Susan Ness of Bible Translation and Literacy and Wycliffe Bible Translators devised a practical spelling and published a 1995 alphabet book. Yergalech Komoi and Gosh Kwanyangʼ published another alphabet book in 1995. An edition of the Gospel of Mark was published in 1997, and other Bible translations were published with this spelling in 1999. The alphabet was later revised, replacing the digraph ⟨dh⟩ with a bowl-struck đ.

## Language & religion

Daasanach is a Cushitic language of the Afro-Asiatic family, spoken by the Daasanach in Ethiopia, South Sudan, and Kenya. Its homeland is along the Lower Omo River and on the shores of Lake Turkana. The language is notable for its large number of noun classes, irregular verb system, and implosive consonants. The initial D in Daasanach is implosive and is sometimes written as <'D> or <Dh>. The sources also list numerous alternative names for the language, including Dasenech, Daasanech, Dathanaik, Dhaasanac, Gheleba, Geleba, Gallab, Galuba, Dama, Marille, Merile, Merille, Morille, Reshiat, and Russia.

They state that the Daasanach originally spoke a Nilo-Saharan language and later adopted the Afro-Asiatic language of a Cushitic-speaking population.
