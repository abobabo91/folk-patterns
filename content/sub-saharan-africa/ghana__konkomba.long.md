## Overview

The Konkomba are a Gur people of northern Ghana and north-west Togo, concentrated in the country around the Oti River and spread across the Northern and Volta Regions; they are the second-largest ethnic group in Ghana's Northern Region. The 2010 census counted 823,000 Konkomba in Ghana, and the CIA World Factbook reports that 3.5 percent of the national population spoke the Konkomba language in 2010. They call themselves *Bikpakpaam* and their language *Likpakpaln* (also written *Likpakpaanl*); a man is an *ukpakpaanja*, a woman an *ukpakpaanpii*, and "Konkomba" is the anglicization used for both people and language. Until the turn of the 21st century their primary occupation was farming and animal husbandry, and they remain largely subsistence farmers keeping poultry, small ruminants and cattle. In folk-culture terms the material record shown here is dominated by ironwork and by cowrie-encrusted gourd and fibre headgear.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here — the Wikipedia articles and the catalogue records of the objects in this atlas — describe no woven-cloth tradition for the Konkomba, so none of the strip-weaving or cloth names sometimes indexed under this people can be confirmed. Where pattern appears in the collection it is built up on the surface of headgear and ornaments rather than woven into cloth:

**Cowrie-covered gourd helmets** — hemispherical gourds or calabashes hollowed out and stitched over their whole surface with cowrie shells laid in concentric bands or circles, the shells interspersed with small coloured glass beads and the rim hung with pendant cowrie strings.

**Coiled fibre hats** — round hats worked in fibre by a coiling technique, one with a knob at the top and an extra-thick rim, another a coiled semicircle with an ear flap on each side and its outside completely covered with pieces of hide sewn together.

**Twined and stitched surface work** — one gourd hat carries a covering of twined string over the cowrie work, and a helmet crest of animal skin is held by a triple band of cotton stitching running through the middle of the hair.

**Motif vocabulary.** Concentric bands and circles of sewn-on cowries; herringbone, in strings of doubled cowries along a helmet rim; three stripes of cowries across the crown of a gourd hat; a single cowrie semicircle on the crown; a band of cowries around the rim; fringes of cowrie pendants each tipped with a bead.

### Clothing & dress

The catalogue records cover headgear and limb ornaments rather than garments, and the sources say nothing about everyday clothing. The headgear is of two kinds. Gourd hats and helmets are inverted semicircles of hollowed gourd or calabash, their outer surface sewn with white cowries and small glass beads in royal blue, red, pale blue and orange — later examples substitute red plastic beads and blue ceramic beads — and finished with tassels of red beads and wool tufts, or with strings of alternating cowries and beads hanging from the rim. Most carry two long horns, identified in one record as antelope horns, tied to the crown with string or fishing wire and passed through perforations in the calabash; on one hat the horns are wide and flat and curve inward until their tips meet, and on another a long tail of cowrie shells hangs between them. One helmet substitutes a crest of animal skin with the hair still attached, standing 9 cm high, and has a leather chin strap attached at both sides. Two of these calabash pieces are catalogued as head-dress and mask together, indicating ceremonial rather than daily use. Worn with them are a head-band of plaited vegetable fibre ornamented with cowries and ending in a tuft of hair, and cylindrical armlets, anklets and leglets described below.

### Architecture

The sources used do not describe Konkomba house forms, compounds or building materials; they note only that the Konkomba were settled in the eastern corridor of northern Ghana before the arrival of other groups in the 15th and 16th centuries, and that the main towns of the Dagomba divisional chiefs were outposts placed strategically on the east bank of the River Oti.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Iron is the medium the collection documents best, and the recurring feature is a weapon made to be worn on the body rather than only held. An **iron knife with a loop handle** is made for wearing on the hand and carries incised ornament with brass wire at the base of the blade; a second knife and a **loop dagger** are plain iron. A **fighting-claw** is a claw-shaped iron blade terminating in a ring for wearing on the finger, and a further piece is simply a hooked iron weapon. Archery is represented by an arrow with a barbed iron head on a reed shaft bound with vegetable fibre. The sources record no Konkomba ceramics, and no pottery centre named in connection with this people can be confirmed from them.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Konkomba limb ornament in this collection works in two materials. Plaited vegetable fibre is used for an armlet or anklet ornamented with cowrie shells and finished with a tuft of hair at the end — the same construction as the matching head-band, so that head and limb ornaments form a set. Leather hide is used for cylindrical armlets and leglets assembled from a number of separate rings sewn together, with looped thongs attached. Beadwork is confined to the small glass, ceramic and plastic beads set among the cowries on the headgear and to the tassels of red beads with wool tufts that hang from their rims. The sources say nothing about tattooing, scarification, hairdressing or cosmetic practice.

## Music & performance

The sources used carry no information on Konkomba instruments, song genres or performance occasions, and none of the drum, horn or flute names sometimes indexed under this people appears in them.

## Dance & theatre

Nothing in the sources used describes Konkomba dance or masquerade performance, although two of the cowrie-covered calabash head-dresses are catalogued as masks as well as head-dresses, which places them in some performative or ceremonial context the records do not specify.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not describe a Konkomba festival calendar or life-cycle rites; Ghana has no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions that bear on this people.

## Foodways

The sources record the productive base but no cuisine: the Konkomba are primarily subsistence farmers who raise poultry, small ruminants and cattle, and until the turn of the 21st century farming and animal husbandry were their primary occupation. Their twentieth-century movement out of Ghana's eastern corridor was driven partly by the search for fertile farmland, alongside colonial pressures. No staple grain, dish, brewing practice or ceremonial food is named in the sources used.

## Oral tradition & literature

Konkomba historical memory is transmitted orally and is explicitly set against neighbouring accounts. Oral tradition holds that the Gonja under **Ndewura Jakpa** defeated the Dagomba under **Ya Na Dariziogo** and forced them to move their capital — believed to have been at Tamale — to its present site at Yendi. Where Martin (1976) writes that the Dagomba pushed the Konkomba back and installed divisional chiefs over them, the Konkomba themselves have insisted they moved on voluntarily when the Dagomba arrived; David Tait records a Konkomba elder saying: "When we were growing up and met our fathers, they told us they (our forefathers) stayed in Yaan/Chare (Yendi) with the Kabre and Bikwom. The Dagombas at the time lived mainly in Tamale and Kumbungu from where they rose, mounted their horses and moved towards Yendi. We saw the horses and had to move further east." Fynn (1971) likewise notes that the ancestors of the Dagomba met a people akin to the Konkomba already living in northern Ghana. A written literature now exists alongside this: teaching primers, a dictionary, storybooks and folk tales, and a complete Bible in Konkomba produced through GILLBT and GIL, RILADEP (formerly KOLADEP, the Konkomba Literacy and Development Project), and Mary Steele, who began the translation in 1962 after arriving to work with the Wycliffe Bible Translators.

## Language & religion

Konkomba, natively *Likpakpaanl*, is a Gur language of the Gurma subgroup, related to Bimoba in Ghana, Moba in Togo and Burkina Faso, Bassari in Togo and Ghana, and more distantly Gourmanché and Miyobe. It is spoken in Ghana's Northern, Volta, Brong Ahafo, Eastern and Accra areas, in Togo's Savanes, Kara and Plateaux Regions, and by smaller numbers in Burkina Faso. Its dialects — among them *Lichaboil*, *Ligbeln*, *Likoonli*, *Limonkpeln*, *Linafeel*, and the further groupings *Linankpel* (Nankpantiib), *Likpalil* (Bikpalib), *Linandeln* (Binandim), *Lisagmaln* (Sagmantiib) and *Linalol* (Binalob) — arose as different families and groups settled together and developed their own pronunciation and vocabulary; Lichaboil is the written variety. The Latin orthography includes the digraphs *gb*, *kp*, *ny* and *ŋm* and the letters *ŋ* and *ɔ*, doubles vowels for length, leaves tone unmarked, and adds an *h* to separate tone-only pairs such as *upii* 'woman' from *upiih* 'sheep'. On religion the sources say only that the Bible has been fully translated into the language.
