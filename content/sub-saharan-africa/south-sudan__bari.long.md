## Overview

The Bari are a Nilotic people of the Karo group living along the White Nile in South Sudan, occupying the savanna on both banks and reaching up to forty miles east and west of the river. Their country holds the historical settlements of Mongalla, Lado, Gondokoro and Rejaf, as well as Juba, the capital of South Sudan, which lies ten miles south of Gondokoro and seven miles north of Rejaf. They speak *Bari*, a Nilotic language also called *Kutuk* ("mother tongue"), shared in distinct dialects with the Pojulu, Kakwa, Nyangwara, Mundari and Kuku. As sedentary agropastoralists they combine subsistence mixed farming with livestock held less for daily food than as social and financial capital — exchanged in marriage, sacrificed at celebrations and funerals, and sold for cash when needed. The sources consulted do not give a population figure.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources consulted document Bari fibre and hide working through museum objects rather than through named weaving traditions, so no vernacular tradition names can be given here. What the catalogue records show is a repertoire in which skin, plaited hide and vegetable fibre carry most of the decorative work.

**Fibre-fringe aprons** — Girls' aprons are made of plant fibre with a hanging fringe and leather straps; one old example is finished with hide straps and a single red bead.

**Beaded hide garments** — A buttock garment of leather stained red was repaired in four places with glass beads, so that the mending itself became part of the surface.

**Chain-hung leather skirt (*rahat*)** — A petticoat or skirt formed from a broad band of leather from which iron chains hang free.

**Spun cotton** — A wooden spindle with its whorl survives still wrapped with cotton thread, and a beaded purse is carried on a cotton girdle.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no motif vocabulary for Bari textiles. On the body, however, initiation marks are named: girls received scarifications on the belly, flank, back and temple in the form of arrow shapes or simple flowers.

### Clothing & dress

Bari dress in the catalogued collection is built from hide, fibre and metal. Women and girls wore fibre-fringed aprons on leather straps, skirts of fibre textile, the leather *rahat* petticoat hung with iron chains, and red-stained leather buttock coverings mended with glass beads. Sandals come as a pair in red-coloured leather with a strap and button. Headwear ranges from a plain hat to a conical basketwork head-dress four and a quarter inches across, topped with split black feathers, and to head-dresses assembled from hide and string. Plumes mounted on bamboo with string, using what the register records as weaver-bird feathers, formed separable parts of larger head-dresses. A mask of leopard skin is the only mask recorded. Photographs from the collection show women wearing frontal and rear coverings with wrist and neck ornaments, one man in shorts with arm ornaments, and a woman in cloth with neck ornaments.

### Architecture

The sources used do not describe Bari house forms, building materials or roofing. What they do record is settlement pattern and its destruction: Bari oral tradition holds that the land flanking the Nile once carried strings of villages spread to the horizon, and Samuel Baker, who first saw the area in the 1860s, confirmed this had been so before the slave trade reduced the Bari villages to a miserable few by the time of his later expedition. Photographs in the museum collection show a shelter with an entrance where a man and woman kneel holding pots, a screen standing beside a woman at her potting, and a fence of woven sticks behind a seated woman — the only built fabric the sources put on record.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery appears in the collection only through photographs: two portraits show a woman seated on the ground making a pot with two finished pots beside her, and another shows a man and a woman holding pots at a shelter entrance. Woodwork includes an oval bowl cut from a single block, head-rests with forked legs and two cross-supports each bored with three holes, a second head-rest combining wood and hide, a staff of twisted wood, a staff with an iron bell at the head of the shaft, and a carved figure of a very attenuated woman painted ochre-red. Smoking equipment includes a red clay pipe-bowl with incised ornament and a pipe stem of cane and metal. Metalwork runs to iron tweezers and a probe held together on an iron ring. Weapons are numerous: giraffe-skin shields stiffened with a central stick bound in hide strips, iron-bladed spears on wooden shafts banded with brass, spears of iron and bamboo, an ebony *knobkerrie* grooved with long lines, longitudinally channelled wooden clubs, a club whose handle carries four bands of parchment skin, an iron-bladed axe with an iron ring at the top of its long handle, arrows of vegetable fibre, iron and wood, and a hide quiver partly bound with snake skin holding nine plain wooden sticks.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ornament is dominated by iron and brass. An armlet of iron carries a central knob bound with brass; another armlet is ivory. A head-band of iron closes at the back with a chain. A leglet worn below the knee is cut into seven strips and decorated with brass bands. One bracelet is functional as well as decorative: worn on the right wrist as a rattle, with the rattle held inside a projecting central flange. Brass wire twisted into circles makes a pair of ear-rings; a brass finger ring is engraved; a neck pendant joins two brass pieces, one crescent and one oblong. A mourning necklace is made of twisted strands of vegetable-fibre cord. Beadwork survives on a hide purse with a flap covered in coloured glass beads. Body modification was formalised at initiation: both boys and girls had the lower front teeth removed, and girls additionally received arrow-shaped or flower-shaped scarifications on the belly, flank, back and temple.

## Music & performance

The instruments in the collection are strings, wind and percussion. A **lyre** is built on a tortoise carapace covered with crocodile skin, with wooden supports and five fibre strings. A **drum** has a cylindrical wooden body tapering towards the base, a skin membrane and hide tension thongs, accompanied by two joined leather pieces that are either ornaments or beaters. Two **antelope-horn whistles or nose-flutes** are partly covered with skin: one sewn with fibre thread and pierced with two holes at the pointed end, one of which may be a finger hole, and carrying a leather strap threaded through the skin; the other with a single hole cut near the tip, a metal ring through the suspension hole and a plaited leather strap. A **musical horn** used for dancing is a cow's horn capped with hide on a sling of twisted hide. Thirty **dancing bells** are mounted on plaited hide thongs, and the rattle-bracelet described above would have sounded with the right hand. Beyond these objects, the sources record that the marriage feast which follows successful bride-price negotiation involves drinking and dancing, and that visits to another tribe were marked by a ceremony of singing and chanting.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name no Bari dances or dramatic forms, but they attach dance to specific objects and occasions: the cow-horn trumpet is catalogued explicitly as a horn used for dancing, thirty bells are strung on plaited hide thongs for dancing, and a rattle-bracelet was worn on the right wrist. Dancing follows the blessing of a marriage by both families. In the visiting ceremony between tribes, participants sang and chanted while swinging back and forth on hammocks, men in hammocks slung closer to the ceiling and women in hammocks hung lower to the ground, after which gifts of equal value were exchanged within each gender — arrows or arrowheads between men, articles of clothing and above all woven skirts between women.

## Festivals & rituals

No annual festival calendar appears in the sources. Life-cycle ritual is documented in detail for marriage. A suitor declares his intent to marry (*nyara*) by presenting himself at the house of the girl's parents with a few close relatives and friends. Some months before the wedding (*budu*), the families meet at the bride's parents' house to negotiate the bride price, with the parents themselves never directly involved in the talks. Payment is traditionally in live animals — a typical bride price might be two dozen cattle and forty head of sheep and goats — with cash accepted when drought or disaster makes livestock impractical. Premarital sex, eloping and aggression towards in-laws can all raise the price. Once terms are agreed, both families bless the marriage and a feast of drinking and dancing follows; the bridegroom returns home alone, and the bride arrives about two weeks later to take charge of her house. Christian Bari add a church blessing, though the cost of holding both ceremonies pushes many couples to delay the church wedding by a year or more. Initiation in the past involved removing the lower front teeth of both boys and girls and scarifying the girls. Death is marked by mourning necklaces of twisted fibre cord and by small animal sacrifices to the spirits of the dead, and livestock are sacrificed at celebrations and funerals.

## Foodways

The Bari economy rests on subsistence mixed farming, with small and large domestic livestock raised mainly to supplement food but held above all as a socio-economic and financial investment. The sources consulted do not name Bari staple crops, dishes or cooking methods.

## Oral tradition & literature

Bari folklore carries the group's own account of its origins: that the Bari came from the north, that their history reaches back to the fifteenth century, and that they arrived in their present homeland in the 1650s or the early eighteenth century, where they found the Pari people and drove them away. Place names of Bari form surviving in southern Dinkaland are read as evidence that the Bari held those areas before the Dinka arrived. Oral history also preserves the killing of Chief *Gubek lo Gore* of the Dung Kaliri clan and his son *'Doggale lo Gubek* at Rejaf East, beheaded by the Turkish slaving army after Gubek sent his villagers into the bush and refused to say where they had gone, and it carries the succession that followed through Nyarsuk lo Gore to the present chief Philip Jada. A further remembered image is of a land whose Nile-flanking villages once stretched to the horizon — a memory Samuel Baker recorded as accurate in *Ismailia* (1874). A Bari grammar and dictionary were published in the 1930s but are now very hard to find; more recent doctoral work covers Bari tonal phonology and syntax.

## Language & religion

Bari belongs to the Nilotic family and is spoken across Central Equatoria state in South Sudan, into the northwest corner of Uganda and into the Democratic Republic of Congo. It is a tone language with vowel harmony, subject–verb–object order and agglutinative verbal morphology with some suppletion. Its dialects are Bari proper (Beri), Pöjulu, Kakwa, Nyangbara, Mandari, Kuku, Nyepu and Ligo. The Bari alphabet, shared with Kakwa, Pojulu and Kuku, has the digraphs ʼB, ʼD, ʼY and Ny plus the letter eng (Ŋ). Most Bari today are Christian, many combining Christianity with the older religion; some worship the traditional gods alone. One god is nameless and двух-sided, half living in the sky and sending rain, half living in the ground and quickening agriculture. *Nun*, a supernatural power, inhabits big strong trees; bark was stuffed with tobacco leaves and sometimes anointed with animal blood and fat, and Nun was believed to visit at night to feast. *Nun-loki* is the most forgiving, ending the life of one he judges to have lived long enough; *Nun-lukak*, the "spirit of food", is prayed to for a plentiful season, with sacrifice following if prayer fails. The cultural hero *Sabaseba* is known as "Old Wind". The Bari hold the world to be layered, with their own plane in the middle, and expect the dead to pass to a next life on the horizon much like this one.
