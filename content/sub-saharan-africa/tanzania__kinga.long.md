## Overview

The Kinga are an ethnic and linguistic group native to Mbeya Region and Makete District of Njombe Region in Tanzania, in the great Kipengere Range northeast of Lake Malawi. Their population was estimated at 140,000 in 2003. The Kinga speak the Kinga language, a Bantu language closely related to Magoma, although mutual intelligibility is low. They are primarily agriculturists who cultivate millet, beans, bananas, wheat and potatoes, and they also keep cattle, sheep and goats. Their mountain settlements, ironworking, agricultural practices and beer production are among the cultural subjects covered by the sources. Kinga structures were built around common spaces used for public meetings.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Kinga ironworking was regarded by the Nyakyusa as requiring secret skills. Kinga smiths produced iron-tipped spears, hoes and knives. Iron was heated, layered in charcoal until the slag drained off, and reduced to a solid spongy mass called bloom; the bloom was reheated and hammered in a forge. Each operation required a new furnace, charcoal, labor and skill. The production of only three hoes or spears could require a ton of charcoal and a long apprenticeship. The Nyakyusa made shields, while seeking Kinga iron-tipped spears after wood-tipped spears proved ineffective against tough shields during the invasion of the Ngoni.

The museum catalogue records an iron axe with a wooden handle and a wooden carved staff bound with iron at one end. It also records grass-plaited baskets used for beer, including close-weave baskets with orange or red decorative bands, openwork lattice-patterned baskets, and a natural-colour basket. A grass basketry bottle is closely plaited and patterned in red. A bamboo mug or cup has pokerwork in bands of triangles and possibly contained a maize-cob.

## Festivals & rituals

Kinga priests claimed an ancient heritage older than their chiefs. The priests also seemed to interpret subterranean water movement. The Nyakyusa watched with fear and dismay as pilgrims descended the mountain paths each year toward “Lwenbe’s” shrine. Beyond this account, the sources used do not document an annual festival calendar, seasonal celebrations, life-cycle rites, weddings or funerals.

## Foodways

The Kinga cultivate millet, beans, bananas, wheat and potatoes. They cultivate bamboo for a strong beer, and the sources describe a very pleasing beer made from bamboo. Museum catalogue records identify several grass-plaited baskets as used for beer. One catalogue record describes a bamboo mug or cup with pokerwork in bands of triangles, possibly containing a maize-cob. ## Language & religion

Kinga is a Bantu language spoken by the Kinga tribe in Tanzania. It is closely related to Magoma, but mutual intelligibility is low. The Kinga speak Kinga almost exclusively at home and usually know Swahili, although they are not fluent. The sources state that Kinga priests claimed an ancient heritage older than their chiefs and were associated with interpreting subterranean water movement. They also describe annual pilgrimages toward “Lwenbe’s” shrine. No further religious system, script, denomination or current religious landscape is covered by the sources used.
