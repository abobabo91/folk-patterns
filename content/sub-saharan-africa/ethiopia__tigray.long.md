## Overview

The Tigrayans (Tigrinya: ተጋሩ, *Təgaru*) are a Semitic-speaking people indigenous to the northern Ethiopian highlands, the plateau country between the Tekeze (Tacazzé) river and the Eritrean escarpment, centred on Mekelle, Adwa, Axum, Shire, Tembien, Enderta and Agame. The 2007 Ethiopian census counted roughly 4,483,000 Tigrayans, 6.07% of the national population. They speak Tigrinya, a North Ethio-Semitic (Afroasiatic) language written in the Geʽez script, and the overwhelming majority belong to the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church. Their folk-cultural importance is inseparable from place: Tigray holds Yeha, Aksum and Debre Damo, the monumental and monastic core of the Kingdom of D'mt and the Aksumite Empire, and Tigrayan craft — carved and cast crosses, parchment manuscripts, painted icons, hand-woven cotton mantles — descends in visible continuity from that Christian civilization of late antiquity.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Shamma** (*shämma*) — the broad white mantle of handspun, hand-woven cotton worn draped over the shoulder, woven on a pit treadle loom by the *shämane* (weaver). The Tigrayan version tends to be finer and narrower-bordered than the heavy Shewan cloth, and the plain field is the point: ornament is concentrated in a single woven band.

**Netela** (*nätäla*) — the light women's shawl, a single width of gauzy cotton with a coloured border, worn over the head in church with the decorated edge folded to the front. The border band, *tibeb*, is the carrier of pattern.

**Gabi** (*gäbi*) — a thick mantle of four to six layers of cotton pressed and stitched together, worn for warmth in the cold highland nights and as a marker of adult male dignity; undecorated or edged with a narrow stripe.

**Tibeb border weaving** — the supplementary-weft and warp-float bands worked into the selvedge of shamma, netela and *kemis* dresses. Tigrayan tibeb favours narrow, densely repeated geometry and cross-derived figures over the wide polychrome panels of central Ethiopian work.

**Ge'ez manuscript decoration** — parchment codices from Tigrayan monastic scriptoria carry *harag* ("tendril") interlace headpieces, canon-table arcades and full-page ornamental crosses; the same vocabulary migrates onto textile and metalwork.

**Magic and healing scrolls** (*kitab*, *telsem*) — strips of goatskin parchment, cut to the client's height, painted by a *debtera* with talismanic guardian faces, eight-pointed *telsem* seals and Geʽez prayer text, then rolled into a leather cylinder and worn. A Tigrayan and Amhara speciality with no close parallel in neighbouring Cushitic traditions.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Meskel* (the equal-armed Ethiopian cross, in dozens of interlace variants), *harag* (vine tendril interlace), *telsem* (geometric talismanic seal), lattice and diamond fret, the stepped Aksumite stele profile, the "monkey-head" beam-end square borrowed from architecture into painting, eight-pointed star, guardian eye-faces, and alternating red-black-yellow banding in border weaves.

### Clothing & dress

Women wear the *kemis*, an ankle-length white cotton dress with a tibeb border at hem, cuff and neck opening, over which the netela shawl is arranged; the head-covering is folded differently for market, mourning and mass. Tigrayan women are known for elaborate *albaso* / *shuruba* braided coiffures — rows of fine braids tight to the scalp opening into a loose mass behind — dressed with butter. Men wear white trousers cut narrow at the calf, a long white shirt, and the shamma or heavier gabi thrown over the shoulder; on ceremonial occasions a *jano*, a mantle with a broad red stripe, replaces the plain cloth. Priests and *debtera* wear the *kaba*, a coloured velvet or brocade cope, with a turban, and carry a *maqwamiya* prayer staff. Deacons and choristers appear in white with embroidered stoles. Silver-studded leather belts, amulet cords at the neck, and open leather sandals complete both men's and women's dress; the *kuba* or crown form appears only in royal and ecclesiastical regalia, as votive crowns given to churches.

### Architecture

Highland Tigray builds in stone. The characteristic dwelling is the *hidmo*, a rectangular drystone or mud-mortared house with massive walls, few openings, and a flat roof of timber joists, brushwood and rammed earth, weighted and rolled to shed the summer rains; internal stone piers carry the beams, and a raised platform separates living space from the byre. The flat roof doubles as a threshing and drying floor. In Aksumite and Aksumite-derived building, alternating recessed courses of stone and timber produce the "monkey-head" projecting round beam-ends, which survive as a decorative motif on church façades and in the rock-hewn churches. Tigray's most distinctive monuments are those rock-cut churches — **Abreha we-Atsbeha**, **Wukro Cherkos**, **Debre Damo** (a built basilica on a sheer-sided amba, reached by rope), and the cliff churches of Gheralta — carved into sandstone with cubical capitals, cruciform piers and coffered ceilings. Above ground, the giant granite stelae of Aksum, including the Obelisk of Axum and the Ezana Stone, render multi-storey timber-and-stone palace architecture in monolithic rock.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The *jebena*, a round-bellied black or red burnished clay coffee pot with a long neck and straw-lid stopper, is thrown or coil-built by women potters and is the centre of every household. Cooking pottery includes the large *mogogo* clay griddle for injera and the *dist* stew pot. Metalwork is the prestige craft: Tigrayan smiths cast and chase **processional crosses** (*meskel*) in bronze and brass by lost-wax, with openwork interlace arms and lateral "ears," and file **hand crosses** (*mäshaf mäsqäl*) for priests to offer for kissing. Silver is worked into **Tigrayan neck crosses** in dozens of regional patterns — the Axum, Adwa, Lalibela and Gondar types are named and collected. Church treasuries hold *sistra* (*tsenatsel*), the jingling metal rattles shaken in liturgy, drums (*kebero*), censers, and gilded votive crowns. Basketry is women's work: the **mesob**, a tall coiled hourglass table-basket of dyed grass, and flat *sefed* serving trays, worked in red, black and natural spirals.

## Music & performance

Tigrayan music divides between the liturgy and the secular tradition. Church music follows *zema*, the Ethiopian Orthodox chant system attributed to the sixth-century Aksumite deacon Saint Yared, notated with *melekket* neumes and sung in three modes — *geez*, *ezel* and *araray*. Its instruments are the *tsenatsel* (sistrum), the *kebero* drum, and the *maqwamiya* staff struck on the flagstones, with *debtera* performing *aquaquam*, the measured liturgical dance-step, in the outer court of the church. Secular music centres on the *krar*, a five- or six-string bowl lyre, and the *masinqo*, a single-string spike box fiddle with a diamond-shaped soundbox bowed by the travelling *azmari* minstrel, who improvises praise and satire in *qene* couplets. The *begena*, the large ten-string lyre, is played for penitential song during Lent. Flutes include the *washint*, a notched cane flute of herders, and the long *malakat* trumpet sounded at feasts and, historically, in war. The Tigrayan secular repertoire is distinctive for the drum-driven dance song known as *guayla*, performed at weddings and *tezkar* memorial feasts with massed clapping, and for *awzena*-style call-and-response singing. Hydraulic-tempo *eskista* shoulder dancing accompanies both.

## Dance & theatre

**Guayla** is the signature Tigrayan dance: participants form a ring or arc, moving counter-clockwise with a shuffling step while the shoulders and upper chest work in rapid isolated pulses, led by a *krar* or *kebero* and driven by handclapping; a solo pair breaks into the centre in turn. The shoulder tremor, called *eskista* in Amharic, is regionally inflected — Tigrayan style is more vertical and bouncing, with less of the head-flicking of Gondar and Gojjam. Weddings involve competitive dancing between the two families and a *shilela* boasting chant. Liturgical performance takes the form of *aquaquam*: priests and *debtera* in two facing rows advance and retire in slow synchronized steps with sistrum and staff, a dance tradition traced to Aksumite-era practice and the Davidic dance before the Ark. There is no indigenous masked or puppet theatre; the dramatic function is filled by the azmari's improvised satirical song.

## Festivals & rituals

The Ethiopian Orthodox calendar governs the year. *Ledet* (Gena, Christmas) falls on 7 January, *Timkat* (Epiphany) on 19 January, when the *tabot* (Ark replica) is carried under canopy from church to a water source for the renewal of baptism — the Aksum Timkat, held at the Mai Shum reservoir beside the Church of Our Lady Mary of Zion, is among the largest in Ethiopia. **Meskel**, 27 September, marks the finding of the True Cross with the burning of the *damera* bonfire of poles and *meskel* daisies. *Fasika* (Easter) closes the fifty-five-day *Hudadi* fast. *Enkutatash*, 11 September, is the New Year. Distinctively Tigrayan is **Ashenda**, the girls' festival at the close of the two-week Filseta fast in mid-August: girls dress in new kemis with elaborate braids and a bunch of tall *ashenda* grass tied at the waist, and go from house to house singing and drumming for gifts. Local *tabot* feasts (*ts'ebel*) fill the calendar with parish pilgrimages. Life-cycle rites: baptism at 40 days for boys and 80 for girls, circumcision in infancy, church-crowned *qal kidan* marriage or the more common *sämanya* civil form, and burial followed by *tezkar*, the memorial feast at 40 days and one year.

## Foodways

Tigray is the heartland of *taff* (teff) cultivation, and the staple is *injera*, the large spongy sourdough pancake fermented three days and baked on a clay *mogogo* griddle, served as both plate and bread. *Tsebhi* stews are ladled onto it: *tsebhi dorho* (chicken and hard-boiled egg in berbere), *tsebhi derho* variants, *zigni* (spiced beef), *alicha* (mild turmeric stew), and *shiro*, a ground chickpea purée that anchors fasting days. Lentil *birsen* and collard *hamli* carry the two long Orthodox fasts, when all animal products are forbidden — roughly 180 fasting days a year for the devout. Distinctively Tigrayan dishes include *tihlo*, stiff balls of roasted barley dough dipped by a two-pronged wooden fork into a meat sauce, a Tigray–Enderta speciality, and *himbasha*, a sweet decorated bread scored into wedges for holidays. *Qwanta* is sun-dried spiced beef; *ayib* is fresh curd cheese. The drink is *sewa* (*suwa*), home-brewed barley beer, and *mes*, honey wine. Coffee is prepared as the *buna* ceremony: green beans roasted in a pan and passed for the smoke to be inhaled, pounded in a *mukecha* mortar, brewed in the *jebena* and poured in three successive rounds — *awel*, *kalaii*, *bereka* — with popcorn or roasted barley.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tigrinya oral tradition rests on *mesalat* (proverbs) and *qene*, a compressed couplet form built on deliberate double meaning — a "wax and gold" surface sense concealing a barbed inner one — the same technique that governs Geʽez ecclesiastical poetry and the azmari's satire. Narrative genres include animal trickster tales, saints' legends drawn from the *Gädl* hagiographies (the *Gädlä Ewostatewos*, recording the saint's travels in Tigray, is read aloud on his feast), and historical narrative attached to named places: the Aksumite dynastic legend of the *Kebra Nagast* and its Solomonic descent, the local Yodit/Gudit destruction cycle, and above all the memory of **Adwa**, the 1896 defeat of Italy on Tigrayan ground, sung in *shilela* war-boast and *fukkera* verse. Written literature descends from Geʽez: Tigray's monastic libraries at Debre Damo, Abba Garima and Gunda Gunde hold the parchment codices, including the Garima Gospels, that carry the tradition. Modern Tigrinya print literature developed from the 1940s.

## Language & religion

Tigrinya is a North Ethio-Semitic language of the Afroasiatic family, the closest living relative of Geʽez along with Tigre, and one of Ethiopia's official working languages; it is also the principal language of highland Eritrea. It is written in the Geʽez *fidäl* syllabary. Principal dialects follow the old districts — Enderta, Tembien, Agame, Adwa/Axum, Raya — with Raya showing Oromo contact features. Geʽez survives as the liturgical language. The great majority of Tigrayans are Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Christians, with smaller Muslim (Jeberti) and Ethiopian Catholic communities, the latter concentrated in Adigrat and Agame. Folk religion runs alongside orthodoxy: the *debtera*, church-trained but working outside the priesthood, writes healing scrolls and casts talismans against the *buda* (evil eye) and *zar* spirits; *ts'ebel* holy-water cures and *adbar* tree shrines persist.
