## Overview

The Esan are an ethnic group native to Nigeria, primarily residing in Edo Central Senatorial District, a plateau region approximately 136 metres above sea level. The term Esan refers to the people, their culture, and their language. Esanland traditionally consisted of a loose confederation of 35 independent kingdoms, each governed by an Onojie. The Esan are traditionally agriculturalists, trado-medical practitioners, mercenary warriors, and hunters. Their language is classified among the Niger-Congo languages and is related to Edo, Urhobo, Owan, Isoko, and Etsako. The sources estimate that Esan people residing in Esanland number about one million to 1.5 million citizens of Nigeria, with a strong Esan diaspora. Their folk-culture documentation includes agricultural practices, oral traditions, traditional religion, scarification, and the acrobatic dance Igbabonelimhin.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They state only that Esanland produced cloth for world trade and that, in the pre-colonial era, Esans carried a crow’s foot tribal scar below their eyes.

### Architecture

Archaeological evidence described in the sources records enclosures made by pre-Esan and pre-Edo societies around family properties. These moats and walls were at maximum three to five kilometres in diameter and demarcated residential and agricultural property. The properties enlarged into villages, and by 800 these villages had coalesced into kingdoms with hierarchies.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They mention cloth, ivory, peppers, and palm-products in connection with trade.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They do record a crow’s foot tribal scar below the eyes as a pre-colonial Esan practice.

## Music & performance

The sources identify music and performance mainly through the dance tradition Igbabonelimhin. The sources state that Igbabonelimhin is an acrobatic dance performed mostly by young males and that it involves spinning and somersaulting to a timed beat. Its mode of operation varies among Esan villages, and differences can be seen on fixed days for performances.

The dance is normally performed every two weeks of the Esan calendar and on the market days of the villages and towns that make up Esan. It can also be danced annually to mark end-of-year celebrations, new yam festivals, and organized social functions such as burial ceremonies. The sources describe it as a unique symbol for Esans everywhere.

## Dance & theatre

**Igbabonelimhin** is an acrobatic Esan dance performed mostly by young males. Its movements include spinning and somersaulting to a timed beat. The dance varies among Esan villages and is associated with fixed performance days, market days, annual end-of-year celebrations, new yam festivals, burial ceremonies, and other organized social functions. They state that Esan folktales and folklore serve learning and entertainment, but provide no further account of their staging or narrative performance.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources mention new yam festivals and annual end-of-year celebrations as occasions for dancing Igbabonelimhin. They also associate the dance with market days, which occur in the villages and towns that make up Esan, and with organized social functions including burial ceremonies. No festival calendar, festival dates, or detailed ritual sequence is provided.

Esan traditional religion includes worship of ancestral spirits and other gods. The sources identify Osanobua as the main Edo-Esan god and state that the name was brought into Christianity and its missionaries, making Osanobua the translation for God in Esanland. They also name Olokun and Osun, the Esan god of medicine. Èṣù is described as a god shared with Yoruba and Edo myth and as incorrectly categorized as the Esan trickster god; the sources state that Èṣù is not Satan or the Devil. They state that a large percentage of Esan remain traditional and practice beliefs involving ancestral spirits and other gods, despite the long-term impact of Christianity. A large percentage of Esan are Christians, mostly Catholic and recently of other denominations.

## Foodways

The sources describe the Esan as agriculturalists who cultivate palm trees, Irvingia gabonensis, cherry, bell pepper, coconut, betel nut, kola nut, black pear, avocado pear, yams, cocoyam, cassava, maize, fluted pumpkin, rice, beans, groundnut, bananas, oranges, plantains, sugar cane, tomato, potato, okra, pineapple, paw paw, and various vegetables. They state that Esanland produced peppers and sold them to European merchants, and that the nineteenth century brought increasing European influence as the English demanded palm-products.

The sources also state that Oba Ewuare passed laws of mourning in 1460 that prohibited sexual intercourse, bathing, drumming, dancing, and cooking. These laws were described as restrictive and are connected in the account with the flight of citizens to Esanland. No further Esan food preparation or ritual food practice is documented.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that Esan origins involve multiple waves of migration and that oral traditions and historical research connect the earliest settlers with the Nok Cultural Zone in north-central Nigeria, around the Niger-Benue confluence. Oral tradition also supports the account of migration from Igodomigodo, the early Benin Kingdom, including the return of the three sons of Ogiso Owodo with their mother, Queen Oakha.

Esan folktales and folklore, including Igbabonelimhin and akhuẹ, serve as forms of learning and entertainment. The sources state that oral tradition has heavily supported the theory of migration from the Benin Empire and that prominent Esan and Edo historians have collected stories about this migration. They do mention that the Esan language is taught in primary schools and that it is broadcast on radio and television.

## Language & religion

Esan is a language in Nigeria with many dialects, including Ogwa, Ẹkpoma, Ebhossa, Ewohimi, Ewu, Ewatto, Ebelle, Igueben, Irrua, Ohordua, Uromi, Uzea, Ubiaja, and Ugboha. The language is classified among the Niger-Congo languages and is related to Edo, Urhobo, Owan, Isoko, and Etsako. It is described as regionally important, taught in schools throughout Esanland, and used on radio and television.

Romanized Esan is the most commonly used alphabet and has a total of 25 letters. The language has many dialectal differences: people from Uromi, Irrua, and Ewu speak slightly differently from people from Uzea, although Uromi and Uzea are documented as having common ancestry. The sources state that Esan has various dialects stemming from Bini and that there remains a close affinity between the Esan and Benin people.

Esan religious life combines traditional beliefs with Christianity. Traditional practice includes worship of ancestral spirits and other gods. Osanobua is identified as the main Edo-Esan god, Olokun is named among the deities, and Osun is identified as the Esan god of medicine. A large percentage of Esan are Christians, mostly Catholic and recently of other denominations.
