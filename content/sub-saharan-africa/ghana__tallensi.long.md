## Overview

The Tallensi (also spelled Talensi) are a people of northern Ghana whose language belongs to the Gur branch of the Niger-Congo family; Ethnologue records *Talni* as a dialect of Farefare. They farm millet and sorghum as staples and keep cattle, sheep and goats on a small scale. The ordinary domestic unit is the polygamous joint family — a man and his sons, sometimes his grandsons, together with their wives and unmarried daughters; married daughters move to their husbands' communities, usually nearby. Descent and inheritance are patrilineal, and the sources used here give no population figure. In folk-culture terms the Tallensi are known above all through Meyer Fortes's ethnography of clanship, kinship and personhood, which made the father–first-born relationship and the sacred crocodiles of the Tong Hills country a standing reference point in anthropology.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources available here describe no named weaving or dyeing tradition; what follows is drawn from the catalogue records of the Tallensi objects this atlas shows, which name techniques and colours but no vernacular terms.

**Raffia band weaving** — a hand-woven weft-faced plain-weave band with a fringe, worked in undyed raffia with dark brown and pink, bordered by alternating check and banding, with a row of triangles in alternating colours running down the centre.

**Bast-fibre dress** — a garment of vegetable fibre in red, white and black, built as a central woven portion with a loose fringe hanging on either side of it.

**Straw-twine belting** — belts twisted from straw twine, made of separate red and black strings (in one case black alone) that are wrapped at intervals with yellow, so the colour reads as banding along the belt rather than as a woven pattern.

**Ornamented leatherwork** — a tassel of leather carrying woven and sewn ornament in red, white and black leather, and a horse's head-band of leather and cloth with a fringe and sewn and peeled leather ornament.

**Motif vocabulary.** Hatched diamonds and girth grooves on pipe bowls, chevron banding around the ends of a cast brass ring, serrated notches cut near the bend of a throwing club, three lines of notches on a black pipe bowl, alternating check and banding with a central row of triangles on raffia, and on a terracotta figure a cross over the stomach and four sets of three diamonds across the chest.

### Clothing & dress

Two garment classes appear in the object record: the fringed bast-fibre dress described above, and belts of wrapped straw twine in red, black and yellow. The taboo system reported for first-born children names two further items of men's dress — the cap and the tunic — which carry ritual weight rather than decorative meaning: from the age of five or six a first-born son may not wear his father's cap or tunic, and he puts both on for the first time at his father's funeral, when he assumes the ritual standing of a mature man.

### Architecture

The sources touch the built environment only through the ritual geography of the homestead. Households live in a walled compound whose entrance is itself a ritually charged place: once a first-born son reaches adolescence he may not meet his father there. The granary is the other marked structure — a son may not look inside his father's granary during the father's lifetime, and at the funeral a tribal elder carrying the dead man's bow leads him to it and shows him the inside. A woman's storage pot works the same way between mother and first-born daughter.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery smoking pipes are the most numerous single class in the collection, and they are sculptural: bowls modelled as a human head or a human face, one as a seated female figure with cicatrices on face and body, and plainer red and black bowls carrying girth grooves, hatched diamonds, three lines of notches, or a hoof-shaped foot. Weapons make up the rest. Throwing clubs are carved from wood with a three-knobbed crooked head, a "pineapple" head, or as a flat L-shaped piece notched with four serrations at the bend. A quiver of wood covered in leather, snake skin and panels of red-dyed woollen cloth holds more than twenty arrows with cane shafts, barbed iron heads and bindings of fibre and reptile skin, some shafts painted along their length with patterns in red or black pigment; loose barbed iron arrow-heads are held separately. A terracotta standing female figure with hands on the hips completes the record.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The metal ornaments recorded are a cast brass leg-band in the form of an oval ring bent upwards at both ends, with a conical projection at one end and chevron patterning around its upper and lower edges, and a brass finger-ring with an upright hollow bezel, terminating in a button bound with brass wire and flanked by applied and serrated bands. Scarification is documented not on living bodies in these sources but on two objects: a pipe bowl modelled as a seated female figure with cicatrices on face and body, and a terracotta female figure whose chest marks and arm band are painted dark brown, with black wool tied around the waist.

## Festivals & rituals

Tallensi ritual is organised around the ambivalence between a man and his first-born. A man must have a son to reach fulfilment and to be venerated as an ancestor after death, yet the birth of a first-born son — and to a lesser degree a first-born daughter — marks the peak of his rise and the beginning of his decline, since the son grows to supplant him. The resulting tension is channelled through avoidances that begin when the child is five or six: the son may not eat from his father's dish, wear his cap or tunic, carry his quiver, use his bow or look into his granary; in adolescence he may not meet his father in the compound entrance. A daughter is similarly barred from looking into her mother's storage pot. At the father's funeral the first-born son and daughter lead the rites, the son dons the cap and tunic, and an elder carrying the dead man's bow shows him the granary's interior. From then on he is ritually adult and responsible for sacrificing to the ancestors — foremost his own father, who as the recently dead acts as intermediary between the living and the more remote ancestors.

A second body of belief concerns crocodiles. Particular crocodiles in particular pools are held to be persons (*ni-saal*) and the incarnations of important clan ancestors; no Tallensi would kill or injure one, since to do so would be murder of the most heinous kind and would bring disaster on the whole clan. The distinction is precise rather than general: crocodiles in rivers fished during the dry season are not persons and not sacred, and may be killed and eaten.

## Foodways

Millet and sorghum are the staple crops, supplemented by cattle, sheep and goats kept on a small scale; crocodiles taken from the dry-season fishing rivers are eaten, while sacred crocodiles are not. Food also carries ritual prohibition: a first-born son may not eat from the same dish as his father from the age of five or six. The sources used name no dishes and describe no cooking methods.

## Language & religion

Tallensi speak a Gur language of the Niger-Congo family; Ethnologue's report on Farefare lists *Talni* as one of its dialects. Religious life as documented here is ancestral: a man's fulfilment lies in being venerated as an ancestor, sacrifice to the ancestors is the duty of the ritually adult first-born son, and the recently dead father mediates between the living and the older ancestors. Clan ancestors are also present in the world as sacred crocodiles inhabiting particular pools. *Tongnaab*, a fertility deity, is associated with the Tallensi.
