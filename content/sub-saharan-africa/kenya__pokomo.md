## Overview

The Pokomo live along the Tana River in southeastern Kenya. They farm and fish, relying on the river’s floods for crops. Their culture includes songs, dances, wedding customs, food traditions and folklore. Upper Pokomo are mainly Muslim, while Lower Pokomo are mainly Christian.

## Material culture

Recorded material culture includes beadwork, girdles, mats, ornaments, wooden tools and musical objects.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Museum records show girdles, beadwork and raffia mats, but no named Pokomo textile patterns.

- **Women’s girdle** — Women’s girdles were made from glass beads on vegetable fibre or cotton thread, sometimes with red, white and blue patterns.
- **Cowrie-shell girdle** — Another girdle was made of hide with cowrie shells arranged in four parallel strings.
- **Beadwork girdle** — A recorded girdle used red, white and blue beads coiled on a vegetable-fibre base.
- **Raffia mat** — Museum records describe mats made from raffia.

### Clothing & dress

The profile records a grass skirt for mwaribe and several kinds of personal ornaments, but not everyday clothing.

- **Doum-palm grass skirt** (*Mkoma*) — A grass skirt made from Doum Palm fronds is worn especially by boys during mwaribe, a dance by young girls at circumcision ceremonies.
- **Dance costume** (*mwaribe*) — Mwaribe is a dance costume tradition linked to circumcision ceremonies.
- **Women’s girdle** — Women’s girdles used glass beads, fibre, cotton thread, hide and sometimes cowrie shells.

### Architecture

Pokomo settlements used raised flood-season structures and villages on small hills near river farms.

- **Raised flood shelter** (*Mahandaki*) — At Nkozi, residents built Mahandaki above the water during floods and lived there.
- **Sacred village** (*Nkozi*) — Nkozi was the mother village of Kitere and the centre of the Kijo.
- **Sacred place** (*Mji wa Walevu*) — Mji wa Walevu was one of the sacred places named in the profile.
- **Sacred place** (*Laini Keya*) — Laini Keya was one of the sacred places named in the profile.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Recorded objects include wooden tools, a palm-leaf lamp stand, a zither and metal ornaments.

- **Wooden comb** — Museum records list wooden combs.
- **Food stirrer** — A wooden food stirrer had three spikes projecting radially from one end.
- **Palm-leaf lamp stand** — A lamp stand used a split palm-leaf midrib and twisted leaf to form a hollow cone.
- **Composite zither** — A composite zither had a sounding trough that narrowed at the waist and pokerwork decoration.
- **White-metal ear ornament** — Some ear ornaments were made from wire twisted into a double spiral.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Beads, cowrie shells, basketry, metal and coins were used for neck, ear and waist ornaments.

- **Cowrie-shell neck ornament** — Neck ornaments used glass beads with pendant fringes of cowrie shells.
- **Beaded ear pendant** — Ear pendants used red, white, blue and green beads.
- **Basketry and coin ornament** — A personal ornament combined basketry, glass beads and metal coins.
- **Beaded waist ornament** — Women’s waist ornaments used glass beads in red, white, black, green and blue.
- **Shell disc** — The catalogue also lists a shell disc.

## Music & performance

Pokomo songs and performances mark birth, weddings, work, correction, welcome and joyful events.

- **Birth song and dance** (*Kingika*) — Women perform Kingika when a mother leaves the house after confinement for healing and breastfeeding.
- **Wedding competition** (*Miri*) — Young men and women perform Miri at weddings and birthdays while competing with one another.
- **Welcome performance** (*Beni*) — Beni is performed mainly by Wema’s Ngatana subtribe to welcome visitors, with a large drum and utasa.
- **Men’s songs** (*Mela ya Walume*) — Mela ya Walume consists of men’s songs.
- **Work songs** (*Mela ya Kitoko*) — Mela ya Kitoko is sung while working in fields or paddling a canoe in the evening.

## Dance & theatre

Pokomo dances are tied to weddings, births, circumcision, visitors and joyful celebrations.

- **Miri dance** (*Miri*) — Miri is performed by young men and women at weddings and birthday ceremonies.
- **Kingika performance** (*Kingika*) — Kingika is a women’s performance linked to childbirth and the end of confinement.
- **Beni dance** (*Beni*) — Beni welcomes visitors in Wema village.
- **Circumcision dance** (*Mwaribe*) — Mwaribe is danced by young girls during circumcision ceremonies.
- **Joyful community dance** (*Kitoko*) — Kitoko is performed by everyone during weddings, births and joyful celebrations.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual year follows rain seasons, initiation, birth and marriage customs.

- **Short-rain season** (*Mvula ya Masika*) — Mvula ya Masika brings short rains for planting fast-maturing crops.
- **Long-rain season** (*Mvula ya Kilimo*) — Mvula ya Kilimo brings long rains for crops such as rice that need more time and water.
- **Male initiation** (*kuhinywa*) — Kuhinywa is circumcision that marks the passage to adulthood for men.
- **Wedding tobacco vessel** (*perenkera*) — Wedding procedures include presenting a perenkera tobacco vessel.
- **Marriage payment** (*mahari*) — Wedding procedures include payment of mahari after family consultations and gifts.

## Foodways

Rice and fish are central foods, supported by farming, fishing and many banana and maize dishes.

- **Tana catfish** (*mtonzi; mpumi*) — Catfish from the Tana River are called mtonzi, or mpumi when especially large, and are boiled, sun-dried or smoked.
- **Tilapia** (*ntuku*) — Tilapia is called ntuku.
- **Fish and banana chips** (*Matoli*) — Matoli is cooked banana chips mixed with fish.
- **Mashed banana and fish** (*Marika*) — Marika is cooked banana mixed with fish and mashed together.
- **Maize and bean mixture** (*Konole*) — Konole is made from sifted maize cooked with green grams or beans.

## Oral tradition & literature

Pokomo stories and songs teach behavior and preserve memories of spirits, movement and floods.

- **Night-whistling spirit** (*seha*) — A story says that whistling at night brings an encounter with a seha and warns children not to reveal their location.
- **Haunting spirits** (*Maseha*) — Maseha are haunting spirits believed to live at Kitere village.
- **Nkozi migration story** — A story tells how people from Mnazini and Nkozi travelled toward one another while following singing and eventually met.
- **Ndera song** — A song from Ndera begins “mpanzi mpanzi kuniyawa...” and continues “nakwenda Kitere...”.
- **Pokomo lullaby** — The tune of Kenya’s national anthem is based on a Pokomo lullaby.

## Language & religion

Pokomo is a Bantu language linked to Kingozi and Swahili, while religious life differs between Upper and Lower Pokomo.

- **Pokomo language** (*Kipfokomo*) — Pokomo, or Kipfokomo, is spoken mainly along the Tana River, and adult speakers are bilingual in Swahili.
- **Ancestor of Swahili** (*Kingozi*) — Kingozi is described as the ancestor of Swahili, and the Pokomo are said to be its only continuing users.
- **Universal creator** (*Mulungu*) — Upper Pokomo use Mulungu for God and the creator of everything.
- **Universal creator** (*Mungu*) — Lower Pokomo use Mungu for God and the creator of everything.
- **Spiritual council tradition** (*Kijo*) — Traditional religion was guided by spiritual elders among the Kijo, who sought guidance in sacred riverine forests.
