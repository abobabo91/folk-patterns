## Overview

The Mbunda, also called Vambunda, are a Bantu people whose present core lies in south-eastern Angola, from the Lunguevungu river in Moxico to Cuando Province. Mbunda communities also live in western Zambia, including areas around Kalabo, Senanga, Mongu, Kaoma, Lukulu and Kabompo, while others live in Northern Namibia, around Rundu and Nkurenkuru, and in the Caprivi Strip. Their history includes migrations from Angola to Barotseland and later movements connected with Portuguese occupation, the Angolan War of Independence and the Angolan Civil War. The Mbunda speak Mbunda, a Bantu language with several dialects. Their folk culture includes iron and copper working, hunting, fishing, basketry, bark-cloth production, wood-carving, pottery, ritual practices, annual ceremonies and the Mukanda initiation tradition with Makithi artifacts and masquerade.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Vifundo* — Bark cloth made by peeling bark from trees such as *mushovi* and *munyumbe* and hammering it with mallets called *vithano* until it becomes soft. The finished cloth, also called *maina*, can be worn around the waist or used as a blanket.

*Manala* — Mats made from *mateve*, identified as papyrus. Mats are also made from long grass called *n'olokoko*, as well as from *mavoya* and *kambanga* water grass.

*Chiyengele* — A red cloth belt. The term is also the name of a Senior Mbunda Chief in Bulozi, declared by King Mulena Mulambwa of the Aluyi people.

*Kanjengo* — White cloth, recorded as a meaning of a Mbunda name.

*Makayi* — Bracelets, recorded among Mbunda names and meanings.

*Manjolo* — Tubular anklets, likewise recorded in the Mbunda vocabulary.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe bark cloth as the principal specifically named textile garment. *Vifundo* or *maina* can be worn around the waist and used as blankets. A *Chiyengele* is a red cloth belt. The sources also record *Kanjengo* as “white cloth,”. They mention *Makayi* bracelets and *Manjolo* tubular anklets as forms of body adornment. Women create baskets from *makenge* roots, while men and women have different craft responsibilities in basket-making and bark-cloth production. No source describes footwear, head coverings, ordinary complete outfits, or a distinct ceremonial costume in sufficient detail.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Mbunda people are described as skilled workers in iron, called *vutale*, and copper, called *vunegu*. They make pots and jars from baked clay. Clay is collected from plains or river banks, placed in a container called *liwati*, wetted, pounded and mixed with burnt clay powder called *vunga vwa vitambi*. Men carve wood into pounding sticks, mortars, spear and fish-spear shafts, knife handles, walking sticks, axe and hoe handles, poles, canoes, oars, stools, bowls, pounding troughs and other utensils. They also make musical instruments, including *vithandthzi*, a type of harp, and *vinkuvu*, drums.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources name *Makayi* bracelets and *Manjolo* tubular anklets. They also record *Mathumba* or *Masumba* as crescent-shaped medallions and *Mundthzimba* as a word in the Mbunda vocabulary. *Kathonda* or *Kasonda* is described as a kind of coiffure.

## Music & performance

Mbunda wood-carving includes the production of *vithandthzi*, identified as a type of harp, and *vinkuvu*, identified as drums. They do state that the Mbunda annual ceremonies showcase the *makishi* masquerade, wood carvings, traditional foods and dances.

The Mukanda initiation tradition includes dancing connected with the exhibition of Makithi artifacts. During the initiation period, a Makithi masquerade consisting of not less than fifty exclusive Mbunda artifacts is exhibited, and dances involving the artifacts’ pantomime-like artistry are performed as cultural entertainment for the community. The sources describe this performance as part of a boys’ initiation institution rather than as a separate music genre.

The Mbunda annual ceremonies include *Lithathe Lya Miondo Ya Mbunda* in Angola, *Mbunda Mbalango Lyenya* in Zambia, *Mbunda Liyoyelo* in Zambia and *Mbunda Lukwakwa* in Zambia. These ceremonies are occasions for Mbunda people from Angola, Congo, Namibia and Zambia to praise their Creator, the Almighty God, and to mark blessings associated with the harvest.

## Dance & theatre

The sources describe the *makishi* masquerade and dances associated with Makithi artifacts. In the Mukanda initiation period, boys remain for three to six months at a bush camp away from their villages, where they learn practical survival skills, knowledge about nature, religion, social practices and values. During this time, a masquerade with not less than fifty exclusive Mbunda artifacts is exhibited, and dances using pantomime-like artistry are performed for the community.

The sources also state that all four annual Mbunda ceremonies showcase the Mbunda *makishi* masquerade and dances.

## Festivals & rituals

The Mbunda have four annual ceremonies. In Angola, *Lithathe Lya Miondo Ya Mbunda* is celebrated every second week of August, with the weekend as its climax, at the palace of King Mbandu III Mbandu Lifuti in Lumbala Nguimbo, Moxico. In Zambia, *Mbunda Mbalango Lyenya* is celebrated every August at Chief Muundu’s palace in Liumba, Kalabo District, Western Zambia. *Mbunda Liyoyelo* is celebrated every September at Chief Chiyengele Chingumbe II’s palace at Kayombo in Kabompo District, North Western Province of Zambia. *Mbunda Lukwakwa* takes place during the first week of October, with the weekend as its climax, at Senior Chief Sikufele’s palace at Manyinga in Kabompo District.

Mukanda is an initiation ritual for boys. After circumcision, boys usually live for three to six months at a bush camp away from their villages. At the end of the initiation period, they are reintegrated into the community. The sources also describe *Litungu* or *Bwali* as a coming-of-age ritual for girls.

Mbunda people maintain practices of respecting ancestors. At the beginning of the planting season, after a hunter has failed to kill animals, during sickness or death when evil spirits are suspected, after bad dreams, or when summoning rain during drought, villagers gather at fetish poles called *vimbundi*. These poles are marked or coloured with red and white clay. Offerings can involve the sacrifice of an animal, including a chicken, goat, cow, pig or sheep, but not a human being. The animal’s blood is rubbed against the poles as respect, praise and honour to God and the spirits.

## Foodways

The Mbunda cultivate tropical forest environments and grow maize, millet, sorghum, beans, sweet potatoes and cassava, with cassava described as a staple food. They also grow rice and keep domestic stock. Their food sources include cattle, goats, wild meat, fish, chicken and cassava. Both men and women fish: men use nets and other implements, while women use special baskets.

Women make small bowl baskets called *vingalo* for food and large bowl-shaped baskets called *mendeko* for keeping mealie meal and other things. These baskets are made from the roots of *mijalu* trees and small roots called *tujalu*. Men make fishing baskets called *matambi* from reeds called *manenga*.

The Mbunda also make salt called *mukele*. It is produced from *mulele*, maize and millet stalks, *mateve* or papyrus, and cassava stalks. Plant and animal oil-making is mainly women’s work with the help of men. Oil is made from wild fruits that bear fat, some of which are edible.

## Oral tradition & literature

According to the oral tradition of the Vambunda, the first monarch of the Mbunda Kingdom was King Mwene Nkuungu. The sources preserve historical narratives about migrations, royal succession, wars with the Chokwe and Luvale, movement to Barotseland, and resistance to Portuguese occupation. They identify King Mwene Mbandu I Lyondthzi Kapova as a ruler who led armed resistance against the Luvale and later against Portuguese forces.

The sources also record a tradition explaining the origin of Mukanda among the Mbunda. Prince Consort Mukwetunga Kamenga, sent by the tenth Mbunda monarch King Mwene Katete ka Lweembe in pursuit of elephants, encountered a Mukanda circumcision camp for the Mbwela people near the present-day Angola and Democratic Republic of the Congo border in Lubaland. After undergoing circumcision, he returned and introduced the ritual to the Mbunda.

The Mbunda Bible translation is described as an important written-language project. The book of John was printed in 1919; Matthew and Mark were translated in 1925, Luke in 1927, and another edition appeared in 1928. The Mbunda Bible was printed and launched on 17 August 2008 in Angola and 31 January 2009 in Zambia.

## Language & religion

Mbunda is a Bantu language spoken in Angola and Zambia. Its dialects include Katavola, Yauma, Nkangala, Mbalango, Sango, Ciyengele or “Shamuka,” and Ndundu. Mbunda was one of six languages selected by the Instituto de Línguas Nacionais in 1980 for an initial phase of establishing spelling rules for teaching in schools and promoting its use. The language is closely related to Luchazi but differs in several consonants. In Zambia, the Chiyengele variety spoken mainly around Mongu is called “Shamuka” and is heavily influenced by Lozi.

The Mbunda have traditional religious practices alongside modern religious practices and beliefs combining traditional and Christian influences. Most Vambunda follow Christianity, with roughly equal shares belonging to the Catholic Church and Protestant denominations, especially the Igreja Evangélica Congregacional de Angola. Some retain African traditional religious beliefs, including belief in Njambi as the supreme creator. Ancestors’ spirits mediate religious relations with Njambi.
