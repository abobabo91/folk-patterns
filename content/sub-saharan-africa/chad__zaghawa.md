## Overview

The Zaghawa live across Libya, Chad, and Sudan. Their culture centres on pastoralism, livestock, nomadic clans, pottery, basketry, and artisan work. Most Zaghawa accepted Maliki Sunni Islam, while some older rites remain. Their language is called Beria by its speakers.

## Material culture

Zaghawa material culture includes pottery, baskets, clay-working tools, livestock-based pastoral life, and inherited artisan occupations.

### Architecture

- **Grass enclosure** — Museum photographs from Kebkebiya show a grass enclosure.
- **Dry river bank** — Museum photographs also show the bank of a dry river in North Darfur.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery and other craft work are linked to inherited artisan occupations, especially among the Hadaheed or Hadahid.

- **Pottery** — Pottery is an inherited occupation of the Hadaheed or Hadahid artisan castes.
- **Clay vessel** — Museum records show women making and working with clay vessels in Kebkebiya.
- **Gourd scraper** — One woman is shown scraping the inside of a clay vessel with a piece of gourd.
- **Reika basket** (*reika*) — The records show a vegetable-fibre basket called reika.
- **Ironwork** — Ironwork was an inherited artisan occupation traditionally viewed as dirty and low-status.

## Music & performance

Music was an inherited artisan occupation, and Zaghawa musicians included drummers.

- **Drumming** — Drummers were among the musicians of the Zaghawa artisan castes.

## Festivals & rituals

Zaghawa religious life combines Maliki Sunni Islam with some retained pre-Islamic rites.

- **Animal sacrifice to ward off evil** (*karama*) — Karama is a ritual sacrifice of animals intended to ward off evil spirits.
- **Conversion to Islam** — Zaghawa leadership converted after Tijani Muslim missionaries travelled through the area on the Hajj route.
- **Mass turn to Islam** — In the 1940s, the Zaghawa began turning to Islam from their traditional religion in large numbers.

## Foodways

Zaghawa foodways are tied to pastoralism, wild grains, and later farming.

- **Cattle keeping** — Cattle are part of traditional Zaghawa pastoral livelihoods.
- **Camel keeping** — Camels are part of traditional Zaghawa pastoral livelihoods.
- **Sheep keeping** — Sheep are part of traditional Zaghawa pastoral livelihoods.
- **Wild-grain harvesting** — The sources describe harvesting wild grains.
- **Millet and sorghum farming** — Contemporary Zaghawa grow millet and sorghum along with sesame, melons, pumpkins, peanuts, and okra.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources name published Zaghawa tales and memoirs about Darfur.

- **Zaghawa Tales of Chad** (*Contes Zaghawa du Tchad*) — This collection contains thirty-seven tales and two legends.
- **The Translator** — Daoud Hari wrote this memoir about Darfur.
- **Tears of the Desert** — Halima Bashir co-authored this book with Damien Lewis about atrocities in Darfur.

## Language & religion

Beria is the Zaghawa language, with several named clan or dialect groupings and both Latin- and Arabic-script writing systems.

- **Beria language** (*Beria*) — Zaghawa speakers call their language Beria, from Beri and a, meaning “mouth.”
- **Beria clan grouping** (*Beria*) — Beria is one of the named Zaghawa clan or dialect groupings.
- **Tuba grouping** (*Tuba*) — Tuba is a named grouping with forms including Biria and Brogat.
- **Kobe grouping** (*Kobe*) — Kobe is a named grouping with forms including Dirong, Guruf, Kobe, and Kapka.
- **Camel alphabet** — Adam Tajir created a Zaghawa Latin alphabet in the 1950s from clan identification marks.
