## Overview

The Alur are a Nilotic ethnic group belonging to the larger Luo group. They live in northwestern Uganda and northeastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, especially in Mahagi territory, mostly north of Lake Albert, and in the southern West Nile region and northeastern Ituri Province. In the Democratic Republic of the Congo, they are densely concentrated in 5,216 square kilometres of Mahagi. The source records two million Alur living in the Democratic Republic of the Congo in 2009 and around 800,000 in Uganda in 2014. Most speak Alur or another Luo language, while some also speak Lendu, Kebu, or Lugbara. Their folk-cultural profile includes hierarchical chiefdoms, royal ritual, farming and herding, fishing, hunting, grass-thatched homesteads, the royal Agwara dance, and a material culture of iron tools, weapons, bows, quivers, fish-hooks, and trumpets.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources record gendered domestic and economic responsibilities: men herded animals, grew crops, built huts, hunted, fished, and participated in political life, while women kept house, reared children, and cooked. The sources also state that men and women traditionally lived and slept separately, with husbands and wives occupying separate huts and eating separately. No clothing materials, dress forms, or adornment practices are described.

### Architecture

Traditionally, the Alur live in grass-thatched huts. Homesteads in Alur clans are located in the central part of their territory, a settlement pattern described as helping keep the territory under clan control. The sources state that men built the huts.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum catalogue records show a material culture containing numerous iron implements and weapons. These include knives with iron blades, wooden handles, and ferrules wrapped in bands of iron or copper wire; a reaping knife whose grip is decorated with strips of copper and brass; and a chopper with a wide iron blade, a projection on one side, and a wooden handle bound with iron wire bands, some beaded. Other recorded tools are an iron blade for an adze or axe with a split hollow socket and an iron fish-hook made with string of vegetable fibre. The catalogue does not document Alur ceramics or named household vessels.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The museum records mention decoration on the grip of a reaping knife with copper and brass strips, and a trumpet ornamented with cowrie shells, leather strips, brass rings, and bells.

## Music & performance

The sources document one musical or sound-producing object in the museum catalogue: a long wooden elephant trumpet bound with the skin of a monitor lizard or python and ornamented with cowrie shells. Leather strips form a handle at one end and terminate in brass rings and bells on plaited strips at the other end. The catalogue does not supply a vernacular name, performance genre, musical scale, repertoire, or occasion for this trumpet.

The Alur social and political sources describe community rituals and festivals as settings for the Agwara dance. The royal spear head bearing tradition is associated with the crowning of Alur kings.

The recorded weapons also indicate a material context connected with hunting and warfare: quivers made of hide covered with long black hair have hide carrying straps, while their arrows have cane or wooden shafts, fibre bindings, and iron heads. Wrapped bows are recorded in forms made from wood, hide, and reptile skin, or from cane, hide, and cord of fibre. These catalogue descriptions do not establish how the objects were used in performance.

## Dance & theatre

**Agwara dance** is described as a royal dance performed only in the presence of the king during community rituals or festivals. Both men and women perform it.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe the Agwara dance as part of community rituals or festivals and state that it is performed in the presence of the king. The current king is referred to as the Rwoth, while the title Ubimu distinguishes the king from other chiefs in the kingdom. Alur kings are crowned according to the royal spear head bearing tradition.

The Alur Kingdom currently has 64 chiefdoms, eight in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and 56 in Uganda. The sources describe a ritual undertaken by the Atyak-Winam and PaNduru chiefs in October 2012 to officially lay to rest a conflict that had continued between the clans after a leadership conflict and subsequent war for territory. Reconciliation efforts had begun under Rwoth Ubimu Valente Keruyoma but halted after his death in 2000.

The sources state that the king has organized cultural sports tournaments in recent years, in which both men and women participate.

## Foodways

The Alur were farmer-herders and grew millet, cassava, maize, sweet potatoes, spinach, and pumpkins. They herded cattle, goats, and chickens. Goat and chicken were important sources of meat. Salt, forest resources, and wild animals were also important, while fishing became important during the drought season. Large herds of animals were typically hunted as secondary sources of meat so that the Alur would not exhaust their own goat and chicken numbers.

The sources describe food as part of the social hierarchy. High-ranking men had first choice of food, especially prestige food such as meat and beer. Cattle were also important because bride price was paid in cattle.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources do, however, preserve accounts of clan and chiefly history, including traditions concerning the Atyak Kingdom, the foundation of the Ukuru Chiefdom, and the formation of Paidha and PaNduru. These accounts connect political identity with descent, migration, rivalry, territorial control, and royal succession.

Alur patrilineages maintain family trees, and the sources describe them as important social realities rather than merely fictional genealogies. The Parombo family in the Ukuru clan, for example, included 2,000 men in 1949.

## Language & religion

Alur is a Western Nilotic language spoken in the southern West Nile region of Uganda and the northeastern Ituri Province of the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Its subdialects are Jokot, Jonam or Lo-Naam, Mambisa, and Wanyoro. Highland Alur, also called Okoro, speak a somewhat different dialect from lowland Alur, also called Jonam, and mutual understanding may be difficult. Alur has nine vowels and 23 consonants.

The language has significant influence from neighbouring Central Sudanic languages. There is extensive bilingualism between Alur and Lendu, and Alur has also been influenced by Okebo and Madi. The language has no officially accepted orthography, although informal conventions appear in written materials and road signs.
