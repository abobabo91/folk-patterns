## Overview

The Kalabari are a sub-group of the Ijaw people living in the eastern Niger Delta region of Nigeria. They are Ijaw-speaking settlers whose lineage is traced to a man called Mein Owei; they were originally known as the Awome. The name Kalabari is derived from their ancestor Perebo Kalabari, who was a son of Meinowei. The people were originally fishermen, and their settlements have historically been close to a river because they believed their powers came from deities in the water. Elem Kalabari became a large kingdom with about 35 settlements, including Bakana, Abonnema, Buguma and Tombia.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe marriage rites. Kalabari people have about three types of marriage: *Iya*, *Igwa* and *Waribiobesime*. The cheapest form of legal marriage recognized in Kalabari is called *Ari Ibara emi*, meaning “she is with me”; the phrase indicates that the woman’s parents should not look for her elsewhere. *Iya* is described as the highest and most expensive form of marriage in Kalabari culture. Its ceremony includes **BIBIFE**, or “buying the mouth.” Until this rite is completed, the potential wife cannot eat food; after her mouth has been bought, she is able to eat in her husband’s house. The rite signifies the man’s responsibility and willingness to care for and feed his wife.

## Foodways

The sources identify three popular traditional Kalabari dishes. *Onunu* consists of pounded yam, ripe plantain and palm oil. *Tominafulo* is made with fresh fish, prawns, periwinkle, oyster and other local ingredients. *Odo’fulo*, also called Native Soup, is made with fresh seafood and other local ingredients. The sources describe the people as originally fishermen.

## Oral tradition & literature

They provide a lineage account in which the Kalabari are Ijaw-speaking settlers connected to Mein Owei, and explain the name Kalabari through the ancestor Perebo Kalabari, a son of Meinowei.

## Language & religion

The Kalabari are described as Ijaw-speaking settlers and as a sub-group of the Ijaw people. Historically, their settlements were close to a river because they believed their powers came from deities in the water. The article also notes that some Ijaw consider the Kalabari a different ethnic group, while others do not.
