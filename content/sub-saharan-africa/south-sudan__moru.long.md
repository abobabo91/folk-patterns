## Overview

The Moru are a people of South Sudan, living in the southern part of Mundri County in Western Equatoria, in the Central Western part of the Equatoria Region, between 4°N and 6°N and 29°E and 31°E. Their population possibly does not exceed 200,000. They speak Moru, a Central Sudanic language, and belong to the wider grouping that includes the Madi, Lugbara, Avukaya and Logo of Uganda and the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Five main divisions are recognised — *Miza*, *Ägyi*, *Moroändri*, *Kediro* and *'Bari'ba* — each occupying a distinct area and each with its own dialectal differences; other partial divisions exist as well. Moru Miza served as the vernacular of education and church throughout the district. In folk-culture terms the Moru are notable for a drumming and dance system explicitly keyed to the agricultural year, and for a literature built in the church.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here — the Wikipedia articles on the Moru people and the Moru language, and the British Museum catalogue records of the objects shown — describe no woven or dyed textile tradition for the Moru. The nearest thing they document to a patterned worn object is a **waist girdle** of leather thong hung with bunches of nuts strung on iron rings, with a few iron pendants among them.

### Clothing & dress

The sources say almost nothing about Moru dress. The British Museum records show one **girdle**, a waist girdle made of a leather thong from which hang bunches of nuts strung on iron rings, with a few iron pendants among them, and a **ring or guard** made of leather — a protecting ring of the kind associated with archery, consistent with the bows and arrows in the same collection. A **carrying-sling** or baby-carrier of antelope skin is also recorded; it is a worn object rather than a garment, used to carry an infant. The catalogue gives no vernacular names for any of these, and the Wikipedia articles do not describe everyday or ceremonial clothing, head coverings, footwear, or any distinction between men's and women's dress.

### Architecture

The sources treat settlement pattern rather than building form. Moru settlement is in extended family groups surrounded by their gardens, and these families are linked together into clans under headmen and sub-chiefs. There are few physical concentrations of villages: the word "village" is generally used to describe the people who relate to one centre, not a compact built cluster. The bush is named as the source of building materials, alongside fuel and fibres. Under the medical mission of Dr Kenneth Grant Fraser from 1920, schools, churches and a hospital at Lui were established along the main roads of Moruland, and those buildings also operated as dispensaries — an introduced rather than a vernacular building programme. Two British Museum photographic prints record **megalithic stone grave markers**, with horns attached to the top; one shows a carved stone or wood marker with horns at the centre of the grave marker, and a shelter in the background. The sources describe no house form, roof type or architectural decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Iron dominates the recorded object set. The British Museum holds Moru **hoe blade currency** — wide, flat iron hoe blades with a handle, serving at once as agricultural tool and as money — in two examples. Weapons are the other concentration: a **bow** of wood bound with lizard skin and strung with gut; **arrows** with long wooden shafts and iron points, one of them a poisoned iron point; and a **knife** with a two-edged, leaf-shaped iron blade set in a wooden handle. Hunting and trapping equipment includes a **wheel trap** of wood, its many spokes bound to two rims with palm leaf. The sources record no ceramics.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Two ornaments are documented in the British Museum collection: an iron **bracelet** ornamented with a double row of short projecting barbs, now worn down, and a brass **finger-ring** with a serrated design. The leather waist girdle hung with nut bunches strung on iron rings and a few iron pendants belongs here as much as to dress. The sources describe no tattooing, scarification, hair practice or cosmetic tradition, and give no vernacular names for any ornament type.

## Music & performance

Moru drumming, dancing and singing are bound to the three seasons, and the correlation between them, the seasonal periods, and the general physical and economic welfare of the people is one of the most striking features of Moru society. The drum ensemble changes with the season and the dance. The largest drum is the *Dugye*, also called *Lari Endre*, "the mother drum"; the medium drum is the *Ruma*; and the small high-pitched drums are the *Lari Ngwa*, "children of the drum". A fifth drum, the *Diri* — also known as *Biri* — enters in the harvest season ensemble.

Two harps play important roles in Moru society: the *Kudi* and the *Lekyembe*. They serve as recreation and as a realization of cultural values, play a particular part in engagement between males and females, and are also used as important instruments for covering long distances and for entertainment. Women play a rattle, the *gara*, in church or at dances. It is made from a small dried gourd with a spherical body and a narrow tapering neck whose rounded end serves as the handle; medium-sized seeds, normally okra, are inserted through a circular hole cut into the centre of the top, which is then resealed.

Church singing is a living context for the language: editions of the hymn book *Buku Loŋgo ro* have been published over the years, and a Moru school song book, *Longo Moru ro Madrasa ya*, was produced by Scopas Mägyä Elias in 1996.

## Dance & theatre

The Moru spend a great deal of time on cultural pursuits of non-economic value between and during their economic activities, and dancing and singing are predominant among them. Three named dances divide the year.

**RUMA** is danced from January to April, when food is abundant, people are healthy, physical labour is light and there is a great deal of idle time. Four drums are used: the *Dugye*, the *Ruma* from which the dance takes its name, and two *Lari Ngwa*.

**DEGO** runs from May to August, the period of heavy agricultural work. Three drums are used — one *Dugye* and two small *lari ngwa*. With people's strength sapped by heavy labour and by the assumed or real scarcity of food, the dance begins slowly, in keeping with the slow rhythm of the songs, and then progresses to *Ngala*, when the movements and steps become considerably quicker and faster. DEGO dances are infrequent and do not last a complete night.

**YELU** is the harvest season dance, performed from September to December with five drums: one *Dugye*, one *Diri* (*Biri*) and three *Lari Ngwa*. Its movements reflect the great health and strength the people have just acquired from eating freshly harvested crops.

The sources record no theatrical or masked tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not describe a Moru festival calendar or life-cycle rites in any detail. What they do document is a seasonal rhythm expressed through the three dances above, and burial practice attested only in photographs: the British Museum holds two gelatin silver prints of megalithic stone grave markers with horns attached to the top, one of them a carved stone or wood marker standing at the centre of the grave with a shelter behind it. Christianity is the setting for much contemporary ritual life — most Moru are members of the Episcopal Church of the South Sudan, and the Book of Common Prayer in Moru, *Buku Mätu Ro*, has gone through a number of editions. E. E. Evans-Pritchard published a note on the rain makers of the Moro clan in 1938, and Kenneth Fraser wrote on witchcraft and healing in the Moru tribe in 1926; the mission is credited with the reform of some harmful indigenous practices and customs, which the sources do not name.

## Foodways

Most Moru families are subsistence farmers dependent on rain-fed agriculture, selling or bartering any surplus for goods that cannot be produced locally. The staple crop is **sorghum**, grown in various field types in association with sesame, cowpeas, bulrush millet and finger millet. Other important crops are groundnuts, maize, cassava, sweet potatoes, okra, pumpkins and various green vegetables. Livestock are kept mainly on the northern and eastern sides of the district, most commonly sheep and goats; poultry are kept by most households, and cattle numbers have risen over the last two decades.

Contact with the wild bush is important to the diet, and many relishes come from wild sources. Hunting and fishing are significant economic and social activities — the bow bound with lizard skin, the poisoned arrow and the spoked wheel trap in the British Museum belong to this economy. Other wild foods include vegetables, wild fruit, fungi and insects, especially termites.

Cultivation itself is organised socially: close relatives traditionally cultivate together in one area, a unit called a *Kätiri*, made of segments divided around a central marker such as a large tree or an ant hill. The sources name no prepared dishes and describe no tea or coffee tradition.

## Oral tradition & literature

A collection of local folk tales was published in Moru Kediro in 1981 as two booklets, *Kitoŋwa Tauniʼba yi* and *Kitoŋwa ʼdi Odoʼba yi*, one of which is available online as a PDF. Scopas M. Elias published *Taoye Opi Moru Ro Kyeno Kayi Ro* ("Behaviour of the Past Moru Chiefs") through the Moru Literature Committee in Juba in 1987, and later wrote *Canon Ezra: My best friend in Sudan* (1996). The Moru Literacy Programme issued a Moru–English Dictionary in 2007. Issa Suraya has written *Odragba's legend: A tale from the Republic of South Sudan* and the poetry collection *When God left*, both 2012, followed by several later books. The clearest communal historical narratives concern attacks by the Azande — particularly those that drove the Moru onto Odo hill near Lui — and raids by slavers from the north.

## Language & religion

Moru is a Central Sudanic language; the Wikipedia article on the people calls it Central South Sudanic. Its dialects are Agi, Andri, ʼBaliʼba, Kadiro — also known as Lakamaʼdi — Miza and Moruwaʼdi. The Jur cluster of northern Mundri County (Beli, Sopi, Mödö, Nyamusa, Wira, Biti, Morokodo) is often erroneously classified as Moru but forms a distinct language group more closely related to Bongo and Baka. The language has been maintained through church use: the New Testament and Psalms, *Taobaro Toʼdi*, appeared in 1951, and the full Bible, *Baibolo Alokado*, was published by the Bible Society in Sudan in 1999. The school primer *Kito Lusi ro Luka be* was first published in 1953 and often reprinted; an adult literacy primer, *Buzevosite*, followed in the 1970s from New Day Publishers in Juba, with a Christian theme. Most Moru are Christians, most of them in the Episcopal Church of the South Sudan.
