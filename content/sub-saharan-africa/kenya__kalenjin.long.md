## Overview

The Kalenjin are a Southern Nilotic ethnic group native to East Africa, consisting of over 20 Kalenjin-speaking tribes and sub-groups. They primarily inhabit Kenya’s Rift Valley, especially the former Rift Valley Province and the counties of Kericho, Nandi, Bomet, Baringo, Uasin Gishu, Elgeyo-Marakwet, West Pokot, Trans Nzoia, Nakuru, and Narok. Related populations live in Uganda, particularly on the slopes of Mount Elgon and in the Karamoja sub-region, and in Tanzania, including the Manyara and Singida regions and areas around Lake Eyasi. The group numbered 6,358,113 in the 2019 census. Kalenjin languages belong to the Southern Nilotic branch of the Nilotic family. Folk-cultural features documented in the sources include age-set organization, initiation, pastoral cuisine, beadwork, dances, music, sacred places, oral traditions, and a strong association with long-distance running.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Kalenjin beadwork** — Kalenjin visual arts are traditionally strongly influenced by applique beadwork. The sources state that beadwork from different regions displays distinctive styles, but do not describe particular patterns, motifs, colors, or techniques.

**Sirikwa holes and related structures** — The Sirikwa archaeological tradition is associated with fortified homesteads, internal livestock pens, and structured gate systems. Sirikwa holes, tumuli, and megaliths are described as enduring material remains of this tradition.

The sources used do not document further named Kalenjin textile traditions, including Nandi beadwork, Pokot beadwork, or Sabaot beadwork, in sufficient detail for separate entries.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe limited clothing information. The Kalenjin culture article states that women of the Chemwal community were associated with a distinctive cowrie shell adornment, through which the Karamojong referred to them as Siger. The museum records document leather back pieces for men from Amudat and the West Suk, identified with the Suk, Pokot, and Kalenjin. One record describes a leather back piece for a man measuring 75 cm; another records the same length and notes partial loss of the fur through an earlier pest attack. A British Museum photographic record shows adult males dancing in a row, wearing leaf headdresses, body paint, and American shorts. The sources do not describe ordinary footwear, belts, women’s garments, or a complete ceremonial dress system.

### Architecture

The Sirikwa archaeological tradition is described as occupying the Kenyan highlands from the Mau and Chepalungu forests to Mount Elgon and the Cherangany Hills. Its settlements contained fortified homesteads, internal livestock pens, and structured gate systems, suggesting concern with security and herd management. The sources also mention enduring tumuli, megaliths, and Sirikwa holes. In religious practice, the Kalenjin traditionally did not build a permanent structure for worship because this was thought to restrict the power of the deity to a particular building. Instead, they used hilltops, family altars or prayer trees, and crossroads as sacred places. The sources do not provide a detailed description of ordinary house forms, wall materials, roof types, or decorative architecture.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum catalogue records bows and arrows collected at Amudat in Uganda during the 1953 expedition to Karamoja. The bows measure 160 cm and 150 cm. The arrows have iron points and measure 93 cm, 87 cm, 83 cm, 85 cm, 83 cm, 86 cm, and 78 cm. The records identify the objects with the West Suk, Suk, Pokot, and Kalenjin. A British Museum record lists a snuff-flask made of horn, leather, and iron wire, with a chain and lid. The sources do not identify its Kalenjin maker, locality, or use, and do not describe Kalenjin ceramics or other household production.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention cowrie shell adornment associated with the Chemwal women. This distinctive adornment contributed to the name Siger used for the community by the Karamojong. The British Museum photographic record also describes leaf headdresses and body paint worn by adult males during a dance, possibly at a funeral. No named Kalenjin jewelry types, tattoo practices, hair arrangements, or ritual body-marking systems are documented in the supplied sources.

## Music & performance

Kalenjin music includes folk traditions and contemporary popular music. Traditional music played on the *sukutit* drum and on various stringed lyres is described as rare and largely restricted to cultural events and venues. Contemporary Kalenjin music derives from the benga sound, whose defining feature is guitar playing principally by plucking rather than strumming. The sources connect this development to the transition from traditional African stringed instruments to Western stringed instruments and identify Kericho as a recording center in the mid-Rift Valley region. The music producer and retailer A.P. Chandarana established studios and a shop in Kericho, where musicians from western Kenya recorded work on spool tapes.

The sources do not provide named Kalenjin song genres, musical scales, performance texts, or detailed descriptions of instrumental construction. They also do not document a specific music tradition connected with the museum photograph, although that record shows adult males dancing in a row, possibly at a funeral.

## Dance & theatre

Traditional Kalenjin dance includes *kambakta*, described as a warriors’ dance performed during the event bearing the same name, and *sondoiyo*, described as an old people’s dance. Contemporary dance styles influenced by Kenyan, African, and American trends are now common, while older forms are largely confined to weddings, cultural performances, the Kenya Music Festivals, and cultural venues such as Bomas of Kenya.

The British Museum catalogue includes a photographic print of adult males dancing in a row, possibly at a funeral. The men wear leaf headdresses and body paint. The sources do not describe Kalenjin theatre, masked drama, puppetry, or other dramatic traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

Peaceful relations, especially between relatives, are highly valued. This relationship is called *Tiliet*, and the wider circle of personal relationships is called *Tilionutik*. Hospitality is also important: guests may be offered tea, snacks, and entertainment, including unexpected visitors.

Male initiation is a central component of Kalenjin identity. Circumcision is called *yatitaet*, while initiation is called *tumdo*. Together they mark the transition from boyhood to manhood. The traditional initiation season was September/October, although in some orthodox, urban, and Christian settings the date has moved to December to coincide with the Kenyan school calendar. The sources state that *ibinwek*, associated with the learning process, is declining in some contemporary contexts, and that circumcision may also take place in hospital.

Kalenjin marriage ceremonies include the proposal or show-up, *kaayaaet'ap koito*; the formal engagement, *koito*; and the wedding, *tunisiet*. The *kaayaaet'ap koito* involves the young man informing his parents, who consult relatives and approach the prospective bride’s family. During *koito*, the families meet, introductions and dowry negotiations take place, and older relatives advise the couple. *Tunisiet* is a large ceremony for relatives, neighbors, friends, and business partners and often follows the pattern of a Western wedding.

The sources do not provide a calendar of annual Kalenjin festivals or a detailed funeral ritual.

## Foodways

Pastoral life has influenced Kalenjin cuisine. *Mursik*, a fermented milk delicacy, is described as emblematic of Kalenjin culture and remains appreciated in contemporary life. Roast meat, especially beef and goat and less often mutton, is known in Kenya as *nyama choma* and has long been associated with celebrations and gatherings. The *Kipsundet* roast is described as an older celebratory practice involving four different and specific types of wood as fuel.

The Kalenjin generally grow and prepare grains, principally maize, which functions as the main starch. Accompaniments are served onto individual plates from a central serving dish and usually include vegetable relishes; meat stews and roasts are also eaten. Since the colonial period, eating while seated on a chair at a table has become customary. People eat with their hands, or use a knife, fork, and spoon according to the meal and setting. Sharing food among family members is described as a common practice.

Hospitality includes offering tea, snacks, and entertainment to guests. The sources do not document a wider dietary code, brewing tradition, named alcoholic drinks, or a festival food calendar.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kalenjin oral traditions connect ancestral origins with *Misiri*, often interpreted in the supplied material as ancient Egypt, and with the Nile Valley and Ethiopian highlands. The sources describe narratives concerning migration, including a southward movement toward the Rift Valley. Kipsigis traditions are also said to link their origins with the Pharaoh’s army, although the account is presented as a traditional narrative.

The Kalenjin creation story recorded from the Nandi section concerns the relationship between the sky and earth, the sun and moon, *Ilet*, an elephant, and an *Okiek*. In the story, the elephant is killed by a man and asks Ilet to take him to heaven, but Ilet refuses. The sources also preserve religious accounts concerning *Tororut*, *Asis*, *Ilat*, *Ilet ne-mie*, and *Ilet ne-ya*.

The sources mention Kalenjin oral traditions, folklore, and oral literature but do not identify a named epic, poetry genre, proverb collection, riddle tradition, or contemporary literary preservation project.

## Language & religion

Kalenjin languages form the Southern Nilotic branch of the Nilotic family. The Kalenjin language is also called Elgeyo or Kalenjin proper and is described as a dialect cluster. The Kenyan conception of Kalenjin includes several dialects spoken in the northern Rift region. The language has five vowels, contrastive vowel length, advanced tongue-root harmony, lexical and grammatical tone, and predominantly Verb–Subject–Object word order. Its verbal system distinguishes past and non-past tense, several degrees of past, and perfective and imperfective aspect.

The name *Kalenjin* gained prominence in the late 1940s and early 1950s. In 1944, fourteen students at Alliance High School, led by Taaitta Arap Toweett, proposed and adopted it. The name is explained as a Nandi expression meaning “I tell you.” The Kalenjin Union was founded in Eldoret in 1948.

Kalenjin religious traditions include older beliefs centered on the sky and a later Asisian religion. *Asis* is described as a supreme God who lives in the sky, while *Ilat/Ilet* is associated with thunder and rain. Traditional sacred places include *Kaapkoros*, *Mabwaita* or *Korosyoot*, and *Sach ooraan/Kimwocho*. The sources describe decline during the British colonial period and renewed interest in Kalenjin religious traditions.
