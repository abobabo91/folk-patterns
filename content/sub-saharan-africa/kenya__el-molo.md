## Overview

The El Molo, also known as Elmolo, Dehes, Fura-Pawa and Ldes, are an ethnic group mainly inhabiting northern Kenya. They are concentrated in Marsabit District on the southeast shore of Lake Turkana, between El Molo bay and Mount Kulal. The El Molo are associated with lakeside fishing in an arid environment and historically spoke the El Molo language, an Afro-Asiatic language of the Cushitic branch. Most El Molo now speak Samburu, and the language is moribund or extinct, with only semispeakers left. The 2019 Kenya census recorded 1,104 El Molo residents, while another account describes the ethnic population as about 1,100. The sources present the group as important to folk-culture research because fishing, language shift, assimilation and the preservation of older linguistic knowledge all form central parts of their documented history.

### Architecture

The El Molo historically erected tomb structures in which they placed their dead. A 1962 archaeological survey in the Northern Frontier District, led by Susan Brodribb Pughe, observed hieroglyphics on a number of these constructions. The structures were mainly found near springs or wells of water.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document traditional religious practice centered on the worship of Waaq/Wakh. In the related Oromo culture, Waaq denotes the single God of an early pre-Abrahamic, monotheistic faith believed to have been adhered to by Cushitic groups. Some El Molo have also adopted Christianity.

The documented treatment of the dead concerns tomb structures rather than a fully described funeral ceremony. The El Molo historically placed their dead in these structures, which were mainly located near springs or wells of water.

## Foodways

The El Molo historically entered an arid environment and are held to have abandoned agricultural activities in favor of lakeside fishing. In the population account, they are described as fishermen concentrated in two villages in Marsabit District on the southeast shore of Lake Turkana. Unlike surrounding neighbors, they do not depend on livestock for their livelihood. Fish is their main diet, while crocodile, turtle and hippos are eaten occasionally.

No information is provided about coffee, tea, alcohol, fasting or food associated with life-cycle ceremonies.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral tradition sees the El Molo people as an offshoot of the Arbore people in South Ethiopia. The language source connects this tradition with the linguistic proximity of El Molo to the Arbore language.

The sources do document language-preservation efforts. In 1995, the “Elmolo Development Group” was established to promote self-reliance among the Elmolo people and to attempt revitalization. An Elmolo language revival program had begun, and Michael Basili, founder and chairman of the Gura Pau, later attempted to reinstate El Molo as the language of the community through school teaching. Basil and his colleagues collected further linguistic and anthropological data. Efforts were dropped in 2012 because it was difficult to implement and extend Cushitic lexical material, which was limited or unevenly distributed among the community.

## Language & religion

El Molo belongs to the Cushitic branch of the Afro-Asiatic language family. It was spoken on the southeastern shore of Lake Turkana in northern Kenya and has no known dialects. The language is similar to Daasanach; the language source identifies Arbore as its closest relative, followed by Dhaasanac. The name El Molo is a Samburu name referring to people who do not use livestock as their source of income. It is formed from the Samburu definite article l-, el- and the El Molo word molu, meaning “this person.”

Native transmission of El Molo largely ceased during the first half of the twentieth century. Most El Molo now speak Samburu, described as a Maasai language or dialect of Kenya. The last “good” speaker, Kaayo, reportedly died in 1999, although the language is still spoken. The original Cushitic El Molo vocabulary includes body parts, numerals, names of plants and animals, and kinship terms. Present-day El Molo follows the phonological and morphological rules of Samburu.

Many El Molo practice a traditional religion centered on Waaq/Wakh. Some have adopted Christianity.
