## Overview

The Mambila (Mambilla in Nigerian usage, Mambila in Cameroon) live on the Mambilla Plateau in the Sardauna Local Government Area of Taraba State, Nigeria — a high, river-dissected grassland at the northern continuation of the Bamenda grassfields, cut by the River Donga and crowned by Gang Peak, Nigeria's highest landform. A smaller population descends the escarpment into the Ndòm (northern Tikar) Plain in Cameroon, settled at Mbèrr (Sonkolong), Taga (Atta) and Ndiba (Somié) from roughly A.D. 1700 onward. They call themselves *Norr* ("the people") in the Nigerian dialects and *Ba* in Cameroon. Their languages form the Mambiloid cluster within Benue-Congo (Niger-Congo), split by SIL into MCU and MZK across the international border. In folk-culture terms the Mambila matter for one thing above all: a sculptural tradition in raffia pith and soft wood that has no close parallel anywhere in Africa, tied to the masquerade-and-oath complex known as *Suu* / *Suaga*.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Mambila cloth-making is modest compared with the plateau's sculpture; the pattern-bearing surfaces that carry identity are fibre costume, bark cloth, and painted wood rather than loom weaving.

**Raffia fibre mask costume** — the full-body dress worn beneath *Suu* helmet masks, built from shredded raffia palm frond stripped into long hanks and knotted onto a netted or twined yoke. The Mambila version is shaggier and less finely plaited than the Bamileke and Tikar netted-cloth costumes to the south, and the visual effect depends on bulk and motion rather than embroidered surface.

**Bark-fibre cloth** — beaten bark cloth from fig species, worked with grooved wooden mallets and used for wrappers, sleeping covers and ritual coverings before cotton trade cloth reached the plateau. Surface interest comes from the mallet grooving itself and from pigment applied afterward.

**Tikar-style beadwork** — netted glass-bead cladding over carved wood and calabash, reaching the Mambila through the lowland Tikar and the Bamenda grassfields. Mambila use of it is sparing and concentrated on prestige objects rather than the dense all-over beading of Bamum thrones.

**Cowrie applique** — cowrie shells stitched in rows and rosettes onto hide, netting and costume, marking wealth and ritual office; cowries also served as currency across the plateau before colonial coinage.

**The red-white-black scheme** — the most important "pattern tradition" the Mambila own is not a textile at all but the annual repainting of *tadep* figures, *kike* pith figures and *Suu* masks in red (camwood/ochre), white (kaolin) and black (charcoal or soot). The colours are renewed on working objects as an act of maintenance and reactivation, not decoration, and the banded, zoned application is the group's signature surface language.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Heart-shaped face* (the concave facial plane framed by a raised ridge — the defining Mambila form); *chevron incising* (zig-zag bands cut into calabash and wood); *cylindrical projecting eyes*; *bird beak* (the outward-thrusting jaw of many *Suu* masks, read as avian); *buffalo horn*; *tooth-row* (the carved dentition of helmet masks); *concentric ring* (banded painted zones on limbs and torsos); *cowrie rosette*; *mallet groove* (the ribbed texture of beaten bark cloth).

### Clothing & dress

Daily dress on the plateau is now largely northern Nigerian and generic: the men's *riga*-style gown and embroidered cap, wrappers and blouses for women, with heavier layering than lowland Nigeria because the plateau is the coldest inhabited ground in the country and evening temperatures fall well below what Taraba's valleys see. Historically, bark cloth and skin wrappers preceded imported cotton. Ceremonial dress is where Mambila specificity survives: the masquerader is entirely concealed in the raffia costume, so that no part of the wearer's body is visible, and the helmet mask *Suuburr* (also *Sua Burr*) — a mask of the first rank — rests on the shoulders and encloses the head completely. Second-rank masks such as *Suu Dua* sit on top of the head rather than over it, and appear in numbers around a single first-rank mask. Women are excluded from seeing these masks and their associated figures, an exclusion that remains in force and that limits what outsiders have been able to record.

### Architecture

Mambila villages sit both on hilltops and in valley bottoms, separated by steep river-cut terrain that becomes impassable to vehicles during the August–September rains, which has kept village styles and dialects distinct from one another. The compound is the unit: circular huts of wattle-and-daub or **cane hut** construction with a **conical thatch roof** of grass, arranged around a swept yard and often enclosed. **Bamboo-mat wall panelling** — split cane or bamboo woven into panels — screens sleeping areas and encloses verandas. The **compound granary** is the architecturally loaded structure: a raised, capped storage hut whose interior held *kike* pith figures out of general sight, while other figures stood outside it. The granary is therefore both a barn and a shrine housing, and the sacred enclosure attached to a compound is the structure whose access is restricted by sex. Ndòm Plain settlements at some 700 m sit in a different ecology — oil palm and gallery forest — and build in correspondingly different vegetal materials.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

**Sorghum-beer pottery** anchors the domestic repertoire: coil-built, open-fired jars for brewing and serving the grain beer that accompanies every festival and work party, with burnished shoulders and roughened bodies for grip and heat. Calabash is the other universal container, decorated with **chevron incised** bands cut and darkened with soot. In metal, the **iron hoe currency** — hoe-blade-shaped iron bars circulating as bridewealth and store of value alongside cowries — and the **Mambila brass anklet**, a heavy cast ring worn in pairs, are the documented forms. The **Mambila stool**, a low single-block seat carved from one piece, belongs to the same soft-wood carving tradition as the figures. Carving is men's work but not specialist work: a carver forges or shapes his own chisels and straight and curved knives as the job requires, and the resulting surfaces are deliberately faceted rather than polished smooth.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Brass and iron anklets and armlets, cowrie strings, and imported glass beadwork from the grassfields trade constitute the documented body ornament. Cowries worn on the body doubled as portable wealth. Beyond this, Mambila adornment practice is thinly recorded in the published ethnography, and the norms that keep *Suu* material from outsiders have limited what has been documented about ritual body marking.

## Music & performance

Mambila performance is organised around the *Suu* (also *Sua*, *Shua*, *Shuaga*; phonetically [ʃwaɣa] in the Somié/Ndeba dialect) association, which conjoins masquerade with oath-taking — a single institution that in most descriptions of the region would be split into "religion," "law" and "art." Masks appear at the **Mbà Sùu** festival, and the sound of the masquerade is itself restricted: the voice of the *Suuburr* is produced by a disguiser, and both its sound and its sight are withheld from women. The instrumental accompaniment across plateau festivals is drums and struck idiophones supporting massed song, with men travelling between villages to perform — the inter-village circuit is the point of the music as much as the music itself. David Zeitlyn's *Sua in Somie: Aspects of Mambila Traditional Religion* (1994) is the fullest account of the performance complex in a single community, and deliberately withholds material that local norms exclude. Recordings from the Mambila Plateau are scarce in commercial catalogues; the Virtual Institute of Mambila Studies is the standing repository for field material, including Bruce Connell's linguistic survey work across the dialect chain.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is embedded in the festival circuit rather than staged separately. Men travel village to village for festivities combining **dance, sport and friendship-making**, and these visits are the mechanism by which inter-village relations are maintained across a landscape where rivers isolate settlements for months at a year. The **Suu masquerade** is the theatrical form: a first-rank helmet mask attended by several second-rank masks, moving in full raffia concealment, its appearance restricted to initiated men. Because the audience is bounded by initiation and sex, Mambila masquerade is not spectacle in the sense that Yoruba *egúngún* or Igbo *mmanwu* performances are — it is a closed proceeding, and the published descriptions of it come from outside collectors rather than from participants, who withhold detail by norm.

## Festivals & rituals

The Mambila year runs on a named festival calendar that structures agricultural and social time on the plateau:

- **Bol** (November) — known alternatively as *Lùm* on the Ndòm Plain and at the eastern plateau villages of Nyo and Mvua.
- **Kàtìi** (December)
- **Toshin** (January–February)
- **Literr** (February)
- **Mbà Sùu** (April–June) — the masquerade festival, when *Suu* masks appear.
- **Tírrìm** (September), alongside **Gùbírr**, also in September, which begins at Dieb (Dembe).

Names shift between the plateau and the Ndòm lowlands, so the same festival carries different labels within the same ethnic group — a direct reflection of the dialect fragmentation. The *Suu* association combines masquerade with oath-taking, which makes it the instrument of dispute settlement and truth-testing as well as of ritual: to swear on *Suu* is a legal act. *Tadep* and *kike* figures are sacra of associations concerned with illness and healing, and the pith figures serve as embodiments for visiting ancestral spirits, who guard family treasures held in the shrines. The male *tadep* figure is carved with a small opening in the abdomen that receives food offerings; the female figure's abdominal opening is closed.

## Foodways

Agriculture concentrates in the valley bottoms, with the highlands given over to grazing since the 1940s, when Fulani cattle graziers arrived toward the end of British administration. The staples are guinea corn (sorghum) and maize, with cocoyam, yam and beans; the plateau's cool, reliably wet climate also supports Irish potato and, since the colonial period, tea at Kakara and Nguroje, and dairy from the Fulani herds — the Mambilla Plateau is one of the few parts of Nigeria where fresh milk and cheese-like *wara* enter everyday diet from local production. Grain is pounded and cooked as stiff porridge eaten with leaf and groundnut sauces. Sorghum beer is the ritual drink and the reason for the specialised brewing pottery: it is brewed for festivals, work parties and the events of the *Suu* association, and hospitality between visiting villages during the festival circuit runs on it. The conflicts over grazing land that erupted from late 2000 and again in 2017 and 2018 are, at bottom, a dispute between these two food systems — valley-bottom farming and highland cattle — on the same mountain.

## Oral tradition & literature

Mambila oral tradition centres on migration and settlement history: the accounts of the descent from the Mambilla Plateau onto the Ndòm Plain from about A.D. 1700, and of the displacement of the Twumwu, a pre-Tikar population who held the plain before the Mambila arrived (Zeitlyn & Connell, 2003). Toponymy itself carries the record — the Ndòm of the plain is a transplanted name from the old Ndòm at Nyö Heights, and the lowland Mbèrr takes its name from the Mbèrr of the Gwalì area. Kinship terminology is unusually complex and has been the subject of a book-length study (Zeitlyn, *Words and Processes in Mambila Kinship*, 2005). Written Mambila is a twentieth-century development: the Common Mambilla (Tungbo) dialect is the literary standard in Nigeria and carries the New Testament known as *Li Fa* plus a body of language-study texts, while a separate New Testament in *Ju Ba*, based on the Songkolong dialect, serves Cameroonian speakers. A French-language local history of Somié was written by Nicodème Mial and Charles Mbe with David Zeitlyn (2000).

## Language & religion

Mambila is not one language but a congeries of dialects and closely related languages within the Mambiloid group of Benue-Congo. Ethnologue divides it at the border (MCU for Cameroon, MZK for Nigeria); Glottolog rejects that division and treats it as a single "Mambila" unit — a live classificatory disagreement. Common Mambilla (Tungbo) is the most widely understood variety in Nigeria and its literary standard; Songkolong serves the same function on the Cameroon side. Orthography work by Mona Perrin (1969, 2005) established the writing system. Religiously the plateau is Christian and Muslim, with Fulani Islam arriving with the twentieth-century cattle migration, layered over the *Suu* complex, which persists as the framework for oath-taking, healing associations and ancestral practice. The *kike* pith figures as spirit embodiments and the feeding of the male *tadep* through its abdominal aperture are the practices at the centre of it.
