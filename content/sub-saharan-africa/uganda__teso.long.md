## Overview

The Iteso — "the people of Teso" — are a Nilotic people of eastern Uganda and western Kenya. *Teso* names their homeland and *Ateso* their language, an Eastern Nilotic tongue of the Teso–Turkana cluster closely related to Turkana and Karamojong. In Uganda they live mainly in the Teso sub-region, across the districts of Amuria, Bukedea, Butebo, Kaberamaido, Kapelebyong, Katakwi, Kumi, Ngora, Serere and Soroti, and also in Bugiri, Pallisa, Tororo and Busia; the 2014 Ugandan Bureau of Statistics report put them at about 2.36 million, 7.0% of the national population. Roughly 670,000 more live in Kenya, chiefly in Busia County south of Mt. Elgon, concentrated in the Teso North and Teso South sub-counties. In folk-culture terms they matter as a cattle-keeping people who became prolific farmers within a single century, and whose surviving material record is dominated by iron, horn, hide and gourd.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here — Wikipedia's articles on the Teso people and the Ateso language, and the catalogue records of the museum objects shown alongside this profile — describe no Iteso weaving, dyeing or embroidery tradition, and name no textile types. The one decorated surface they do record is not cloth but a gourd: a subspherical vessel whose skin is incised with human and animal figures.

### Clothing & dress

The sources do not describe Iteso everyday or ceremonial clothing, and name no garment, belt or footwear types. What they do document is worn on the head and the arms, and is treated under Jewelry & body adornment below.

### Architecture

The sources used do not cover Iteso house form, building materials, roofing or architectural decoration. The only built structure they mention is a modern one: the war memorial raised near Soroti, the Iteso capital, after the 1992 settlement that ended the Teso insurgency.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Iron work dominates the collected record. The **finger-knife**, also catalogued as a "knuckle" knife or finger-hook, is a small iron blade carried on a loop handle that fits over the finger; the British Museum holds a matched pair as well as single examples. Defence is represented by a rectangular **hide shield**, its top edge decorated with an applied feather "pom-pom". Domestic objects run to a three-legged **wooden stool**, its two legs joined to the third by a horizontal bar beneath a rectangular seat, and to the incised **gourd vessel** noted above, carrying human and animal figures cut into its surface. The wider economy behind these objects was reshaped by one imported tool: the Iteso were noted for their quick adoption of the ox plough in the early 1900s.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Two **head-dresses** in the British Museum are built on an iron frame carrying two horns. In one, the horns are iron and their tips sheathed in a strip of leather held by a thong; in the other, hide horns are tipped with brown hair and set with glass beads threaded on iron rings at their bases, with leather thongs for tying the piece on. A separate iron **frontlet** is worn across the forehead. Ivory supplies the arm ornaments: a boat-shaped **arm ornament** with a pronounced ridge running round its lower and side edges, and a plain ivory **armlet**. The sources record no tattooing, scarification or hair practice.

## Music & performance

Two wind instruments survive in the collection, both made from horn. One **whistle** is hung with fur tassels; the second is a horn whistle sheathed in snakeskin and finished with brass mounts, catalogued explicitly as a musical instrument. Song is the performance form the written sources dwell on: the Iteso traditionally sang and danced educative folksongs suited to different occasions, and three are named. **Ataikatiaka Kitiso** treats the unity of the Teso people. **Akidai Imojong** enjoins respect and care for elders, and **Iyalama Imojong** offers them appreciation. The didactic purpose is what the sources stress — these are songs that teach a social obligation rather than accompany one. No instrument ensemble, professional singer class or court performance context is described. The language itself carries a musical constraint that shapes any sung Ateso text: syllable pitch is phonemic, so that identically spelled words differ in meaning by pitch alone — *élípì* "I am praying", *elìpǐ* "I was praying", *elipí* "he or she was praying" — while stress falls roughly evenly across syllables and does not lengthen the vowel it falls on.

## Dance & theatre

The sources record that dancing accompanied the educative folksongs described above, performed on different occasions, but name no individual dance, no mask or puppet tradition and no dramatic form.

## Festivals & rituals

The Iteso hold a cultural event at **Kakapel mountain** on 26 December each year, the occasion at which cultural practices are performed. The life cycle is marked mainly by cattle and by drink. A father's gift of cattle to his son signals the son's maturity — that he is able to manage his own estate and start his own family — and cattle are central to negotiating bride-price and other important social occasions. After a birth, custom keeps the mother confined to the home for three days; afterwards *ajon*, the millet brew, is placed in the mouth of the infant. Death opens the longest ritual sequence. Iteso traditional religion holds that at death the body separates from its spirit, the *eparait*, which departs to live in the bush; ideally it moves further and further out, but a discontented spirit returns to trouble the living and demand offerings of food and drink. Bodies are therefore exhumed from the bush after some years in order to "cool" them and make them more amenable to the living — a practice oral tradition credits to the legendary Oduk. Many Iteso are reluctant or afraid to be buried in coffins, holding that a body in a coffin cannot be cooled and that the dead are thereby suffocated. Missionary influence has since associated the spirits of the dead with *ajokin*, small creatures of the bush, and both in turn with the devil.

## Foodways

The staples are finger millet, *akima*, and sorghum, *imomwa*. Cassava was introduced during the colonial period by the authorities as a dietary supplement. Alongside the grains the Iteso eat pumpkins, wild berries, peas, groundnuts and beans, with the meat of domestic and wild animals, milk, butter and fish. The cultural drink is *ajon*, a fermented brew made from dried finger millet, usually of the *emiroiti* variety, and drunk at local ceremonies, social gatherings and important events. It is taken either from a calabash or from a communal pot, the drinkers sipping through long tubes; the same brew is placed in a newborn's mouth at the end of the mother's three-day confinement. Some Christian Iteso criticise the customs that surround it. Production has since become commercialised, and making and selling *ajon* is now an important source of income for the families who specialise in it. Cotton is the main cash crop rather than a food one, grown by men and women in separate plots during the short rains, with tobacco as a second; when Kenyan cooperative ginneries failed to pay for delivered cotton in the 1980s, growers turned to tobacco on loans from large agricultural companies, and the cotton industry was only partially revived from the 1990s.

## Oral tradition & literature

Iteso oral tradition centres on the hero **Oduk** and his wife **Among'in**, credited with leading the people in the migration that brought them to what is now Busia County around 1500 AD. Oduk is remembered as the man who organised the Iteso militarily and enabled them to defeat their rivals, and the same tradition makes him the origin of the practice of exhuming bodies from the bush. Written Ateso literature begins with the orthography agreed by the Ateso Orthography Committee in 1947, under which most of the first published Ateso books were printed; the 1957 prayer book *Ekitabo Loka Ailip Naka Ituŋa Kere* survives in digitised form. Preservation work continues through the Atɛsɔ Local Language Board, which issued *A guide to the standardised orthography of Atɛsɔ* at Kampala in 2014, and through lexicography such as Apuda Ignatius Loyola's *English-Ateso Pocket Dictionary*. The sources name no epic, proverb collection or riddling tradition.

## Language & religion

Ateso is Eastern Nilotic, part of the Teso–Turkana cluster, ISO 639-3 code *teo*; it is also recorded as Bakedi, Bakidi, Elgumi, Etossio, Ikumama, Iteso and Wamia. Its 22-letter alphabet omits f, h, q, v, x and z except in loanwords and adds *ŋ* and the digraph *ny*; every noun is masculine, feminine or neuter, marked by an initial e-/o-, a- or i-, and counting is built on the numbers one to five, so that six is "five and one". The two dialect poles reflect conquest and contact: northern Ateso is markedly influenced by Luganda following the 19th-century Ganda conquest, southern Ateso by Turkana. Before European missionary work the Iteso worshipped an omnipotent god, **Akuj**, and **Edeke**, a god of calamity; both names carried into Christian usage, as the first sentence of the Ateso Bible shows — *Ageunet, abu Edeke Kosub akwap keda akuj*, "in the beginning God made the earth and the heavens", literally "the down and the up". The Baháʼí Faith is also represented: Enoch Olinga, a Knight of Baháʼu'lláh and the youngest person appointed a Hand of the Cause, was Iteso.
