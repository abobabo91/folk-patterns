## Overview

The Bassa, also spelled Basa or Basaa and sometimes known as Bassa-Bakongo, are a Bantu ethnic group in Cameroon numbering approximately 800,000 individuals. They historically lived along the Atlantic coast of what is now Cameroon and are associated with the Sanaga and Wouri river regions, including the lower Sanaga valley and the banks of the Nkam River. They also live around Édéa, Éséka and Douala, and oral traditions describe earlier movements through the Sahara Desert, Kanem Bornu, the Logone Valley, the Mandara Mountains and Ngog Lituba. The Bassa speak the Basaa language, a Bantu language whose oral traditions, music, dance, foodways, carving and ritual practices form the principal folk-culture subjects documented in the sources.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The Wikipedia article mentions colorful costumes in connection with dance, but gives no garment names, materials or construction details.

### Architecture

They state that the Bassa lived in villages and that their historical movements reached the Atlantic coast, the lower Sanaga valley, the Nkam River and other forest and river areas, but provide no architectural description.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do describe wooden carvings, masks and sculptures as important forms of Bassa artistry, with aesthetic, cultural and spiritual significance. These objects are used in ceremonies including initiation rites and funerals.

## Music & performance

Music and dance are described as important forms of Bassa social expression, associated with joy, grief and celebration. Traditional instruments named in the source are drums, xylophones and flutes. The source distinguishes two broad categories of music: urban styles and rural styles. The urban styles named are *Assiko*, *N'gola* and *Ma-Kune*. No further information is supplied about their musical structures, instruments, performance settings, histories or regional distribution.

Bassa music is also connected with dance, where drums, xylophones and flutes accompany rhythmic movements and colorful costumes. They likewise do not provide musical notation, performance terminology or details of how urban and rural styles differ.

## Dance & theatre

The sources describe Bassa dances as having rhythmic movements and colorful costumes. Dance is presented as a means of expressing joy, grief and celebration, and is accompanied by drums, xylophones and flutes. Wooden masks and sculptures are used in various ceremonies, including initiation rites and funerals.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe Bassa traditional religion as involving ancestral spirits, a spiritual world coexisting with the physical realm, ancestor worship, animism and traditional rituals. Nature is also revered, and the source connects spiritual life with agricultural and hunting customs. Wooden carvings, masks and sculptures have cultural and spiritual meanings and are used in initiation rites and funerals.

The historical account states that the Bassa designate their oldest history as *Mbog kôba ni kwân*, translated as “legends and myths.” Griots divide Bassa history into three periods: *Kwan*, from the beginning until the 19th century BC; *Kôba*, from the 19th century BC to the 15th century; and *Len*, from the 15th century to the present day.

## Foodways

The Bassa historically lived from subsistence farming and fishing, and agriculture, fishing and hunting remain central activities in the descriptions of their traditional way of life. The source states that products introduced since 1472, including rum and salt, became part of daily Bassa consumption. It also names several crops introduced through Portuguese influence from Sao Tome and Fernando Po: avocado, papaya, cocoa and sugar cane.

Two well-known Cameroonian dishes are identified as originating from Bassa country: *Bongo'o sauce* and *Mintoumba*. *Mintoumba* is a cassava bread made from kneaded cassava mixed with palm oil, salt, spices and chili pepper. The mixture is rolled and tied in a banana leaf.

## Oral tradition & literature

Bassa oral tradition places the origins of the people in Ancient Egypt or Ancient Nubia, near the Nile on the border of Egypt and Sudan. The account describes movements after invasions and natural disasters, followed by migration across the Sahara Desert toward Kanem Bornu, the Logone Valley, Guelingdeng, the Mandara Mountains and later the river systems of central and southern Cameroon.

Ngog Lituba is presented as a central place in Bassa founding traditions. The expression *Bon ba ngok lituba*, translated as “the children of the pierced rock,” identifies the Bassa as descendants of those who inhabited Ngog Lituba. The source also relates an origin story for the ethnonym Bassa in which an argument among the sons of a common ancestor called Mban concerned the sharing of a snake after a hunt.

Griots preserve historical knowledge through oral narratives, and elders pass down cultural heritage, wisdom and history through storytelling. They do identify contemporary figures including Bassek Ba Kobhio, a writer and filmmaker, and Werewere Liking, a writer, playwright and performer.

## Language & religion

The Bassa people primarily speak the Bassa language, also called Basaa. It belongs to the Bantu language family and is spoken by around 800,000 people around Édéa, Éséka and Douala. The source describes four periods in the language’s evolution: Bassa spoken before 1472, between 1472 and 1919, between 1919 and 1945, and Bassa spoken today.

Basaa has noun classes, an implosive “b” and a tone system consisting of high tone, low tone, low-high tone, high-low tone and medium tone. It is written using an adapted Latin alphabet with consonants, vowels and accents specific to Bantu languages. The language preserves cultural heritage, oral traditions and storytelling, while elders play an important role in transmitting historical knowledge.

The sources describe ancestor worship, animism, traditional rituals and belief in ancestral spirits.
