## Overview

The Soninke, also called Sarakolleh, are a West African Mande-speaking ethnic group found in Mali, southern Mauritania, eastern Senegal, The Gambia, and Guinea, especially Fouta Djallon. They are concentrated in the valley of the upper Senegal River and along the Mali–Senegal–Mauritania border between Nara and Nioro du Sahel. Their contemporary population is estimated at over 2 million. The Soninke language is a Mande language, also known as Serakhulle or Azer. Soninke communities and culture were spread through migration, trade networks, French colonial labor migration, and Wangara mercantile confederations. The Soninke are associated historically with the ancient Ghana or Wagadu Empire and maintain cultural practices involving Islam, social stratification, marriage, circumcision, agriculture, trade, foodways, textiles, and body adornment.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Indigo stitch-resist-dyed cotton cloth** — British Museum catalogue records describe skirts made from five or six wide strips of plain-weave cotton, hand sewn together. The cloth is dyed dark indigo and decorated with alternating bar, dot, geometric, linear, triple-dot, and semi-circle patterns, with plain indigo borders and unworked warp-thread fringes.

**Indigo tie-dyed and stitch-resist-dyed shawls** — The catalogued women’s shawls are made from cotton woven in six or seven wide strips and hand sewn together. They use alternating indigo-dyed and black warp stripes, dot and line patterns, deep indigo borders, and loose warp-thread fringes.

**Indigo checkerboard shawl** — One catalogue record describes a cotton shawl woven in ten wide strips, with indigo-dyed hand-spun cotton warp and alternating indigo-dyed and natural-coloured cotton bands aligned as a checkerboard. A narrow red-dyed cotton weft stripe runs through each weft band.

**Indigo herringbone-patterned skirt** — One skirt is made from eight narrow machine-spun cotton strips, hand sewn together. Plain-weave strips are resist-dyed in indigo to create lighter herringbone patterns, with zig-zag patterns along one short end.

**Indigo-dyed embroidered gown** — A man’s gown, identified as a boubou or *tobe*, is made from four sheets of European cotton sheeting dyed indigo in Africa and sewn into a rectangular garment with square sleeves. It has a pocket and extensive hand-sewn embroidery in white, green, peach, red, light blue, dark blue, and yellow cotton thread.

**Motif vocabulary.** Triple dots, linear patterns, dotted semi-circles, bars, dots, geometric patterns, herringbone patterns, zig-zags, checkerboard bands, knives, stars, and spirals.

### Clothing & dress

The museum records show women’s skirts and shawls made from indigo-dyed cotton, together with a man’s rectangular gown identified as a boubou or *tobe*. The gown has square sleeves, a pocket, and embroidered decoration on the front and back. The catalogue records also document photographs of Soninke women, men, children, older women, and boys from the village of Garawol in The Gambia. These records mention women’s head coverings and garments. One photograph shows a woman wearing large, heavy gold earrings; another records gold rings attached to a head covering at the forehead.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources document gold earrings and gold rings in museum photographs, and mention pottery workers within the hereditary caste category called the tage or numu.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Museum catalogue records from The Gambia describe Soninke women from the village of Garawol wearing gold earrings and gold rings attached to head coverings. One record states that large, heavy gold earrings were a sign that a woman was married, while unmarried women wore considerably smaller gold earrings. Another records small rings attached to the head covering, at the forehead, or at the sides when women married. A related photograph identifies the earrings and the ring at the forehead as gold. The sources record boys with shaved heads during a circumcision ceremony.

## Music & performance

The sources describe music and performance in connection with circumcision and with hereditary social categories. During the circumcision period, boys circumcised the previous year organize *tam-tams* for the new boys in order to prepare them psychologically. During the ceremony, the boys sit around a drum called the *daïné*. Other teenagers, young girls, women, men, and slaves form a circle around them, while the boys are surrounded by scarves called *disa sing*.

The social hierarchy includes the *fune*, described as a bard, and the *gesere* or *jeli*, described as griots and singers. The sources also mention the *karikompe*, a wedding event accompanying the marriage contract.

## Dance & theatre

The circumcision account describes a circle formed around the boys and the use of *tam-tams*.

## Festivals & rituals

The Soninke practice Islamic rites of marriage and circumcision. Marriage follows Islamic practices, and cousin marriage is common and preferred. When both families agree, the couple becomes engaged, described as *i na tamma laga*, in a mosque. Each month after the engagement, the man pays the woman’s family a bridewealth dower called *nakhafa*. The marriage, called *futtu*, is completed through a marriage contract mentioning the dower and a wedding event called *karikompe*. The couple has advisers: the man’s is called *khoussoumanta-yougo* and the woman’s *khoussoumanta-yakhare*. After one week of celebration, women meet to show gifts received by the couple from their parents, especially from the woman’s mother.

Circumcision is called *birou*. Boys circumcised the previous year organize *tam-tams* for the new boys, who sit around the drum called *daïné*. The boys are surrounded by scarves called *disa sing*. The sources also state that female genital mutilation has long been practiced among the Soninke as a ritual of social acceptance and that it continues in Mauritania and Senegal despite being illegal in contemporary law.

## Foodways

Soninke breakfast foods include *fonde*, a porridge made of millet, sugar, milk, and salt, and *sombi*, a porridge made of rice, millet, or corn. Lunch foods include *demba tere* and *takhaya*, both containing rice and peanuts. *Dere* is a stew made from millet and beans. The sources identify rice, millet, peanuts, corn, sugar, milk, and salt as ingredients in these foods. The Soninke traditionally engage in agriculture, and during the rainy season men and women cultivate crops, while women usually remain at home to cook and care for children.

## Oral tradition & literature

Soninke oral tradition identifies Dinga as the ancestor of the Soninke. His sons included Dyabe Sisse, described as the founder of the Wagadu kingdom with its capital at Kumbi. Another Soninke tradition indicates migration from Aswan, Egypt. The sources also record a traditional proverb: “Cousins are made for each other.” The historical tradition of the Soninke includes their role as founders of the ancient Ghana Empire, also called the Wagadu Empire.

The sources mention the tradition of epic storytelling among Soninke blacksmiths in the bibliography through Badoua Siguine’s *La tradition épique des forgerons soninké*. They also list a work on the supernatural in Soninke tales, *Le surnaturel dans les contes soninké*. No specific epic title, riddle tradition, contemporary literary revival, or preservation project is described in the supplied material.

## Language & religion

Soninke is a Mande language spoken by the Soninke people of West Africa. It is also known as Serakhulle, Azer, or Maraka, and the language is called *Sooninkanxanne* in Soninke. The language has an estimated 2.3 million speakers, primarily in Mali and Mauritania, and is also spoken in Senegal, Ivory Coast, The Gambia, Guinea-Bissau, and Guinea. It is a national language of Mauritania, Mali, Senegal, and The Gambia. Dialects include the Berber-inflected Azer dialect. Long vowels are written double: aa, ee, ii, oo, uu.

Most Soninke are Muslims. The rulers and people of the Ghana Empire converted to Islam in the 11th century, and Soninke people have been Muslim ever since. They typically adhere to the Maliki school of Sunni Islam. Islamic practices appear in marriage, circumcision, and other rites of passage.
