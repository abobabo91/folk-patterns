## Overview

The Haya, also called Bahaya or Wahaya in Swahili, are a Bantu ethnic group living in Kagera Region in northwestern Tanzania, on the western side of Lake Victoria. Their population is estimated at over one million, approximately 4% of Tanzania’s population. Haya belongs to the Bantu language family and is spoken along the southern and southwestern coast of Lake Victoria. Haya society historically organized political authority through kingdoms ruled by kings known as *bakama*. Agriculture, particularly banana cultivation, remains central to economic life. The Haya are also associated with early iron smelting and the independent production of carbon steel through pre-heating techniques, documented as dating back approximately 2000 years.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Bark cloth** — Museum records document a painted bark-cloth fragment from Buhaya. It measures approximately 80 × 48 cm and depicts a four-legged predator; a tear has been repaired with white thread.

**Vegetable-fibre dress** — British Museum records include vegetable-fibre dresses and garments with leaf fringes attached to plaited leaf girdles. The records identify men’s, women’s, and boys’ garments, but provide no further information about their construction or social use.

**Plaited leaf girdles** — The museum records describe girdles made from plaited leaves, sometimes supporting a fringe of leaves. The sources do not identify a Haya vernacular name for this garment form.

**Grass and leaf basketry** — Catalogue records include coiled cylindrical baskets made of grass or leaf, with circular forms, flat bases, lids, and woven black and pale stripes. Another record describes a grass basket with a conical lid and faded purple decoration.

**Olushansha** — An *olushansha* is recorded as a woven case for coffee beans. The case is made partly in twill technique, patterned in red, and formed from two sections joined with green cord.

**Oluhungo** — An *oluhungo* is a woven plate or winnowing tray used to separate coffee beans from chaff. The catalogue describes twill basketry made from the stems of a reed-like grass, with a supporting rim formed from the central veins of a palm leaf.

**Mushonge materials** — The traditional house called a *mushonge* uses flexible reeds, wooden poles, banana fibre, and grass. The sources do not describe a separate named textile pattern tradition associated with these materials.

**Motif vocabulary.** Painted four-legged predator on bark cloth; black and pale woven stripes; faded purple pattern; red patterning.

### Clothing & dress

The museum records identify vegetable-fibre dresses and garments with leaf fringes attached to plaited leaf girdles. They include men’s garments, a small girl’s skirt, a boy’s garment, and vegetable-fibre dresses. The sources do not describe everyday and ceremonial distinctions, head coverings, footwear, hairstyles, or additional Haya clothing terms.

### Architecture

The traditional Haya house is called a *mushonge*. It is circular, has a conical peak, and is built from the top down using flexible reeds, wooden poles, banana fibre, and grass. Construction involves rituals and the consumption of alcohol during and after completion. Wooden poles divide the interior into areas for men and women, animal corrals, and shrines honoring ancestors. The size of a mushonge reflected village social hierarchy: the chief’s house was largest, followed by those of religious authorities, clan leaders, sub-clan leaders, and common people. Haya kings lived in a similar structure called a *nyaruju*, or palace. The mushonge later gave way to mud-walled *kiteti* and mudbrick *banda* houses; most Haya now live in square houses with corrugated iron-sheet roofs.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Haya ironworking is documented through archaeology, oral tradition, and experimental reconstruction. Excavation beneath the sacred shrine tree *Kaiija*, described as “the place of the forge,” revealed an iron furnace carbon-dated to the 1st millennium BC. Experimental smelting in the 1970s and 1980s used iron ore, charcoal, clay tuyeres, and grasses. The preheating hypothesis proposed that furnace preheating enabled temperatures of 1300 to 1400 °C and was confirmed during a 1976 experimental smelt.

Museum records include iron knives, a finger knife, arrows with iron heads, spears with iron points, wooden bows, shields, carved wooden spoons or ladles, combs, and iron bill-hooks. One shield measures 104 × 35 cm and has a flat wooden core surrounded by a plaited edge.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used do not document Haya jewelry, tattooing, scarification, henna, hair practices, or named forms of body adornment.

## Music & performance

Museum catalogues record several Haya-associated instruments from Buhaya. A *bågharpa* is a bow harp with a calabash resonator, measuring 131 cm. A *trågharpa* has seven nylon strings and measures 55 cm. A *Hornfela* is described as a monochord: a skin is stretched over a cut, circular horn, with a string running across the skin to a tuning peg on the upper part of the neck. It measures 42 cm. The catalogue also records an oil gourd with a reed straw, measuring 60 cm in height for the calabash and 77 cm for the straw.

The sources do not name Haya song genres or provide descriptions of musical performance contexts. They do record dancing and singing within *omuteko*, an age-set training system for boys between the ages of 10 and 12. During their training at the king’s court, sports, games, dancing, and singing were competitive activities involving different sub-counties. The sources do not identify named dances, praise-poetry forms, or professional musician categories.

## Dance & theatre

The sources do not document named Haya dances, theatre forms, masked performances, or dramatic traditions. Dancing and singing are mentioned as competitive activities within *omuteko*, the royal training system for boys, alongside sports and games.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do not provide an annual Haya festival calendar. They describe monthly new moon ceremonies conducted by the king and overseen by religious specialists. These ceremonies involved feasting, alcohol consumption, offerings to gods and goddesses, and the use of a deceased king’s regalia as ritual catalysts. Kings also visited sacred centres throughout their kingdoms to make offerings to ancient Bacwezi gods and goddesses associated with prosperity, fertility, famine, and disease.

Haya family shrines called *ekikalo* were dedicated to ancestors. Offerings included banana beer, unripe bananas, and dried coffee beans. Bananas also played a role in the ritual called *koshobekera*, believed to assist a child’s growth. The death of a king required a mourning period during which all labour stopped until a new king was installed. Royal drums and the jawbone of the deceased king were important objects in securing the succession, while the jawbone received special burial at the royal burial estate, or *gashani*, within the royal compound, or *kikale*. The former king’s regalia and ritual paraphernalia were curated in the *buchwankwanzi* house.

## Foodways

Haya agriculture is organized around three land types: the banana grove, or *kibanja*, surrounding the homestead; family plots called *musiri*; and communal grasslands called *rweya*. Family plots provide maize, cassava, sweet potatoes, and other staple foods, while communal grasslands provide grazing, grass for mulching, thatching, and flooring. Forested areas supply wood and charcoal.

Bananas are central to Haya foodways. Kagera Region is described as producing nearly half of Tanzania’s bananas, with cooking bananas known as *matoke* forming most of the yield. Matoke is prepared with beans and either beef or fish. Other bananas are used to make beer called *orubisi*, spirits called *konyagi*, and sweet non-cooking varieties. Bananas also serve as offerings to ancestors.

Haya coffee traditions include sun-drying and chewing coffee beans rather than roasting and brewing them for drinking. Coffee beans are offered to visitors and traditionally placed at ancestral shrines. The museum record for *olushansha* describes a coffee-bean case used for beans chewed as a stimulant and also as an affirmative response sent to a suitor. The *oluhungo* was used to separate coffee beans from chaff. The sources do not describe a broader dietary code.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral history records the founding of Haya kingdoms beginning around the 16th century, including the Bankango clan’s association with Kyamtwara and Kiziba. Oral accounts also describe the entry of the Bahinda royal clan into Kyamtwara during the 17th century and their rule under King Rugomora Mahe. Traditions concerning ironworking state that ancestors smelted iron beneath the sacred shrine tree Kaiija.

The sources describe *buchwankwanzi* as a divining house where kings and advisers made decisions about war and raids according to what diviners foretold. The term is translated as “spitting pearls,” referring to words of wisdom spoken by those prophesying inside. The sources do not document named epics, folktale cycles, proverbs, riddles, or contemporary literary preservation projects.

## Language & religion

Haya, also called Oruhaya, is a Bantu language spoken by the Haya people of Tanzania. In 1991, the population of Haya speakers was estimated at 1,200,000. Haya is closely related to Nyambo and to languages of western Uganda such as Nyoro-Tooro and Nkore-Kiga, which together form the Rutara group. It has two tones, high and low, and nine tenses.

Traditional Haya society is patrilineal and organized through clans, or *oluganda*. Pre-dynastic religion included Bachwezi beliefs and religious specialists such as spirit mediums, diviners, priests, and priestesses who communicated with or channelled ancient Bacwezi gods and goddesses. Family ancestor shrines and royal rituals remained important features of religious life. The sources also describe the arrival of Christianity and the adoption of European-style formal education.
