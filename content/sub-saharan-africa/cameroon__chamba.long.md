## Overview

The Chamba occupy the broken country where the Benue lowlands rise into the Alantika Mountains, a frontier zone divided since the colonial partition and the 1961 plebiscites between Adamawa State in Nigeria and the Faro and Mayo-Banyo divisions of Cameroon. They are one of the most-discussed anomalies of West African ethnography because a single people, sharing chiefship, cults, clanship and material culture, speaks two entirely unrelated languages: Chamba Daka, of the small Dakoid group whose deeper affiliation is still debated, and Chamba Leko, an Adamawa language of the Leko–Nimbari cluster; "Chamba" itself is an exonym of Hausa and Fulfulde currency, while people commonly call themselves *Sama* or *Samba*. Chamba society rests on double descent, each person belonging simultaneously to a patriclan and a matriclan, with cults, shrine objects and ritual offices distributed along those crossing lines. The nineteenth century was decisive: pressure from the Fulani jihad and the consolidation of the Adamawa emirate at Yola scattered Chamba war bands southward, and those raiding diasporas — the best known led by the war-leader commonly named as Gawolbe, killed in battle in the Grassfields in the 1830s — carried Chamba chiefship, dress and regalia into western Cameroon, where their descendants founded the five Bali chiefdoms (Bali Nyonga, Bali Kumbat, Bali Gham, Bali Gashu, Bali Gangsin). Bali Nyonga's alliance with Eugen Zintgraff in 1889 and its later role as a Basel Mission centre made the Chamba-derived Bali disproportionately visible in Cameroonian history. Materially, the Chamba are known above all for two things: a family of horizontal helmet masks fusing buffalo and antelope features, among the most abstract sculptural forms of the Benue valley, and an iron-working tradition that produced multi-bladed throwing knives, currency bars and staffs traded far beyond Chamba country — alongside janus and post-like wooden figures, incised and pyro-engraved gourds, indigo strip cloth, heavy cast brass anklets and the great globular pots in which sorghum beer, the medium of every Chamba ritual, is brewed and served.

## Material culture

Chamba material culture is organised around three poles: the forge, which supplies weapons, currency and ritual iron; the shrine, which houses masks and figures belonging to named cults; and the beer pot, around which labour, funerals and hospitality are arranged.

### Masks & figural sculpture

Chamba sculpture is dominated by helmet masks danced at the funerals of elders and at cult occasions, and by tubular standing figures kept in shrines rather than displayed.

- **Buffalo helmet mask** (*vara*) — Horizontal helmet mask worn over the crown of the head, combining a buffalo's swept-back horns, a gaping crocodile-like jaw and an antelope's muzzle into a single abstract form, blackened and picked out with red and white earth; the name *vara* is the term most often recorded for both the mask and its associated cult.
- **Vara masquerade costume** — The mask is never seen alone: it is danced with a full body covering of dyed fibre, netting or cloth strips, so that the performer's identity is wholly concealed, and it commonly appears in male and female pairs distinguished by colour and horn profile.
- **Chamba janus figure** — Double-faced figure, carved in wood or occasionally cast, in which two heads share one body or neck; janus forms are widely associated with cult officials and with the idea of seeing in two directions at once.
- **Wunu figure** (*wunu*) — Standing figure with a columnar body, everted or flexed arms and a summarily carved head, of a type kept in shrines and brought out for cult occasions; museum collections generally record these as guardian or cult figures rather than portraits.
- **Jup shrine figure** (*jup*) — *Jup* is the ordinary Chamba word for a cult or medicine, and "jup figure" covers the varied assemblage of carved figures, iron objects, pots and horns held in a cult's shrine; the figure's power lies in its cult membership, not in its form.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Chamba weaving follows the West African narrow-strip pattern, dyed in indigo pits, while the strongest surviving decorative idiom is the burnt and incised ornament applied to gourds.

- **Chamba indigo cloth** — Narrow bands woven by men on the horizontal treadle loom, dyed in fermented indigo pits and sewn edge-to-edge into wrappers and gowns; banded blue-and-white and blue-on-blue schemes predominate, with the deepest, most heavily dyed cloth carrying the highest value.
- **Chamba calabash pyrography** — Gourd bowls, dippers and bottles ornamented by scorching and incising the skin with a heated blade or point, producing dark geometric registers of hatching, chevrons, lozenges and concentric bands; the technique is shared with neighbouring Adamawa peoples and is generally women's work.
- **Resist and stitched patterning** — Tie-dyed and stitch-resisted cloth circulates widely through regional markets, and Chamba dress often combines locally woven strip cloth with such traded pieces.
- **Fibre and leaf work** — Plaited fibre skirts, fringes and mask costumes constitute a parallel "textile" tradition, and in ritual contexts fibre outranks cloth as the proper covering.

### Clothing & dress

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century accounts describe minimal everyday dress, later displaced by the gown-and-cap style of the Muslim Adamawa towns, with ornament rather than tailoring carrying the social information.

- **Riverain gown** (*babban riga* in Hausa) — Wide embroidered gown adopted from Hausa-Fulani neighbours, worn with drawstring trousers and an embroidered cap; it became the mark of chiefs, traders and Muslim converts.
- **Waist cloth and apron** — Men's loin coverings of skin or strip cloth and women's short cloth or fibre aprons formed the older everyday dress, with beaded or cord waist strings worn beneath.
- **Leaf bunch** — Leaf or fibre bunches worn front and back by women in some communities into the twentieth century, retained longest in the Alantika hill settlements.
- **Bali regalia** — In the Chamba-derived Grassfields chiefdoms, chiefly dress follows Grassfields conventions of indigo resist-dyed *ndop* cloth, beaded prestige items and richly worked caps, a visible measure of how far the diaspora assimilated.
- **Warrior dress** — Hide or quilted protection, iron arm rings and the throwing knife worn slung at the shoulder made up the older war costume, now seen only in dance.

### Architecture

Chamba build in mud and thatch, arranging round houses and granaries into walled compounds; there is no monumental tradition, and the grandest Chamba-associated buildings are the Grassfields palaces of the Bali.

- **Compound** — Cluster of round mud-walled, conical-thatched houses linked by low walls into a single enclosure with one controlled entrance, containing separate sleeping huts for wives, kitchen, byre and shrine.
- **Chamba granary** — Round mud or coiled-clay storage bin raised on stones or a plinth against damp and termites, closed with a removable conical thatch cap and filled from the top; granaries dominate the compound visually and their number is a direct statement of a household's standing.
- **Hill settlement** — Dispersed, defensible settlements on the slopes and saddles of the Alantika range, dating from the era of slave raiding, contrasted with the open plains villages of the Ganye lowlands.
- **Shrine hut** — Small, low structure or fenced enclosure housing a cult's objects, sited at the compound edge or in bush groves and entered only by initiated members.
- **Bali palace** — In the Chamba-founded Grassfields chiefdoms, the palace complex follows Grassfields form: rectangular pillared buildings, carved doorframes and posts, and an assembly plaza used for *lela*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Iron is the prestige medium and pottery the essential one: smiths made currency, weapons and ritual staffs, while women potters supplied the vessels of daily life and of the shrine.

- **Chamba throwing knife** — Multi-bladed iron weapon with a leaf or hook-shaped head and lateral cutting arms set on a wrapped grip, thrown or carried as a prestige and ritual object; it belongs to a Benue–Chad family of throwing knives and became a standard regional trade item.
- **Chamba iron currency** — Bars, rods and hoe- or spatula-shaped iron pieces used as stores of value and in bridewealth and ritual payment, kept in bundles and passed between households rather than consumed.
- **Gbana staff** (*gbana*) — Forged iron staff, often with a flattened or figurated terminal, planted in shrines or carried as an emblem of office; iron staffs stand for the permanence and danger of the forge.
- **Chamba blacksmith forge** — Workshop of bellows, tuyère, stone anvil and hammers, historically supplied by local smelting in shaft furnaces; smiths commonly marry within their own group, act in funerals and cult work, and are regarded as both indispensable and set apart.
- **Chamba beer pot** — Large hand-built globular vessel with a short neck and roulette- or cord-impressed decoration, used to brew, carry and serve sorghum beer; specific pots are dedicated to shrines and never used for ordinary drinking.
- **Chamba stool** — Low stool carved from a single block, generally a circular seat over an openwork or solid support, kept as personal property and offered to visitors as the basic gesture of hospitality.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment is dominated by cast copper alloy worn on the limbs, supplemented by iron rings, beads, cowries and — in older practice — extensive scarification.

- **Chamba brass anklet** — Heavy cast copper-alloy anklet, sometimes weighing several kilograms, worn in pairs by women; the weight enforces a measured walk and the anklets were accumulated as household wealth and transferred at marriage.
- **Arm and leg rings** — Iron and brass bracelets, spirals and cuffs worn in graded numbers by men and women, with certain forms restricted to cult members and titleholders.
- **Bead and cowrie ornament** — Waist strings, neck strands and hair ornaments of glass beads, cowries and seeds, formerly also indicating a girl's stage of maturity.
- **Scarification** — Patterned cicatrisation of face, torso or abdomen once marked clan and maturity; it has largely lapsed under Islam, Christianity and schooling.
- **Hairdressing** — Shaved, crested and plaited coiffures, dressed with oil and ochre, distinguished age and marital status, and are still revived for festivals.

## Music & performance

Chamba music is functional and ensemble-based rather than virtuosic and solo: it exists to summon, to mourn, to work and to honour. The core instruments are drums — the **Chamba drum** being the single-headed or double-headed cylindrical drum played in pairs and threes with hands or stick — joined by iron clapperless bells and gongs struck with a rod, calabash rattles and iron leg-jingles, and side-blown horns of antelope or of wood that announce a chief's presence and address him with a fixed motif. Above these sit the voices of the cults: whistles, flutes, friction instruments and bull-roarers, which are not music for an audience but the sound of the *jup* itself, produced out of sight so that the uninitiated hear the cult without seeing it. Funerals of elders are the great musical occasions, with drumming that continues for days, mourning songs led by senior women, and gun salutes where firearms are available. In the Bali chiefdoms the distinctive ensemble is the set of end-blown bamboo pipes played at *lela*, whose overlapping pitches make a dense single texture and which is the most-recorded Chamba-derived music in Cameroon. Praise-singing and horn-calling for chiefs, work songs at hoeing and threshing parties fuelled by beer, and children's game songs complete a repertoire in which almost nothing is performed for its own sake.

## Dance & theatre

There is no Chamba theatre separate from masquerade, and masquerade is theatre of a particular kind: the mask does not represent a character to be interpreted but presents a presence to be received. The *vara* masker enters the plaza covered head to foot in fibre, with the buffalo-antelope helmet held forward at head height, and moves in charged bursts — stamping, wheeling, sudden rushes at the crowd that scatter onlookers, especially children and the uninitiated — punctuated by stillness while the drums reset. Appearances cluster at the funerals of senior men and women, where masks are said to escort the dead and to affirm that the cult outlives its members, and at cult initiations, where the same figure is fearful to the outside and explicable to the inside. Alongside masking there are line and circle dances for the community at large, in which women dance in a close arc with small steps and shoulder work while men move outside them; warrior dances in which throwing knives and spears are handled in mock combat; and, in the Grassfields chiefdoms, the processional dancing of *lela*, where horsemen, flag-bearers and ranked societies move between palace, plaza and stream over four days. Satirical and mimetic performance exists mostly in the licence given to particular masks and to funeral clowning, which can mock the living with impunity for the duration of the rite.

## Festivals & rituals

The Chamba ritual year is anchored to sorghum: rites clearing the way for planting, rain-calling at shrines controlled by particular clans and specialists, and first-fruits observances at which the new crop and the new beer are offered before anyone may consume them freely. The greatest ceremonies, however, are mortuary rather than calendrical. The funeral of an elder is staged in two movements — burial, then a later, much larger commemorative gathering once the harvest and the brewing allow — and it is here that masks appear, cattle and goats are killed, inherited iron and brass are displayed, and rights are transferred along both the patriline and the matriline that double descent requires. Initiation into a *jup* cult, graded and paid for in beer, iron and livestock, structures a man's public life; parallel women's cults and the rites surrounding first childbirth and widowhood structure women's. Installation of a chief involves seclusion, ritual instruction and the handing over of iron and pottery regalia. In the Chamba-derived Bali chiefdoms the annual *lela* festival — four days in the dry season of flag-raising, pipe music, mounted parade, gun salutes, palace audience and a procession to water — is the signal public event, and it is explicitly narrated as a Chamba inheritance carried south in the nineteenth century. Among Muslim Chamba of the Faro plains, Ramadan and the two Eids, with mounted salutes to the chief at *Id el Kabir*, have largely replaced the older cycle, and the refusal of beer marks the sharpest fault line in Chamba ritual life. No Chamba tradition is at present inscribed on the UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage lists; the nearest regional inscriptions are Nigeria's *Kwagh-hir* theatrical performance of the Tiv (2019) and the multinational Gelede oral heritage, neither Chamba.

## Foodways

Sorghum and bulrush millet are the old staples, with maize, cassava, yams, groundnuts, bambara nuts, cowpeas and rice added in varying measure across the two countries. The daily meal is a stiff grain porridge, broken by hand and dipped into a sauce whose character comes from the condiments rather than the meat: fermented locust-bean cakes from *Parkia biglobosa* seeds, dried and pounded baobab or bitter leaf, okra, hot pepper, and oil from shea or groundnut. Meat is occasional — goat, sheep, guinea fowl and chicken at rites, bush game where hunting survives, fish from the Faro and Benue systems — and cattle circulate chiefly as wealth and bridewealth, with milk and butter reaching Chamba markets through Fulani herders. The centre of Chamba foodways is not a dish but a drink: sorghum beer, malted, mashed and briefly fermented by women in the great globular pots, cloudy, sour and low in alcohol, consumed fresh because it does not keep. Beer pays for work parties, opens negotiations, is poured for shrines and the dead, and measures a household's capacity to be generous; a funeral is reckoned by how much was brewed. Sweetened drinks of ginger, tamarind and baobab, kola nuts obtained by trade, and the smoked and dried preserved foods of the long dry season complete the diet. Muslim households substitute non-alcoholic millet gruels and sweet drinks in every context where beer would otherwise be served, which reshapes not only diet but the conduct of funerals and labour exchange.

## Oral tradition & literature

Chamba oral tradition is preoccupied with movement and with origins. Clan and chiefdom histories recount arrival from the east or north, settlement on named hills, the splitting of groups after disputes, and the nineteenth-century dispersals under the pressure of Fulani raiding — narratives that reach their fullest development in the Grassfields, where the Bali chiefdoms recite the migration of Gawolbe, his death in battle, and the fivefold division of his following as a charter of chiefly legitimacy and of relations with their non-Chamba subjects. Because descent is double, genealogical knowledge is itself literature: a person's standing is argued through patriclan and matriclan names, the cults attached to each, and the precedents of past transfers. Alongside history run the ordinary genres — animal trickster tales in which a small clever creature outwits the large and powerful, etiological stories explaining the separation of God and humanity or the origin of death, riddles exchanged competitively at night, and a dense proverbial repertoire used in dispute settlement, where the apt proverb functions as an argument rather than an ornament. Praise names and horn formulae for chiefs, dirges improvised by senior women at funerals, and songs of insult and correction aimed at the lazy, the mean and the adulterous carry social sanction. The Chamba are unusually well served by scholarship: Richard Fardon's long series of studies, above all *Raiders and Refugees* on political development and *Between God, the Dead and the Wild* on religion and ritual, together with his work on *lela* in Bali, constitute the standard written record against which oral versions can now be read.

## Language & religion

The Chamba speak two mutually unintelligible Niger-Congo languages whose speakers nonetheless regard themselves as one people: Chamba Daka, the Dakoid language predominant around Ganye and in the Nigerian lowlands, whose classification within Benue-Congo or Adamawa remains contested; and Chamba Leko, an Adamawa language of the Leko–Nimbari group spoken on the Cameroonian side and in the Alantika area, from which the important Leko-based literature on Chamba religion derives. Fulfulde serves as the regional lingua franca throughout Adamawa, with Hausa, Nigerian English and Cameroonian French and Pidgin layered on according to the border's side; in the Bali chiefdoms the Chamba speech of the migrants was lost within generations in favour of Mungaka, which the Basel Mission then made a written church language. Indigenous religion recognises a high God associated with the sky and with rain — commonly rendered *Su* in Leko-based accounts — who is distant and rarely addressed directly, while practical religious attention goes to the ancestral dead, who remain interested in their descendants' conduct, and to the *jup*, the cults and medicines whose objects, sounds and masks mediate power from outside the settled human world. Fardon's formulation of Chamba life as lived "between God, the dead and the wild" captures a system in which the bush is the source of both danger and efficacy, and the smith and the cult official are the licensed handlers of it. Islam arrived with Fulani political expansion in the nineteenth century and is now dominant in the Cameroonian Faro plains and among market and official families; Lutheran missions in Nigerian Adamawa and Basel Mission Presbyterianism in the Grassfields produced large Christian populations elsewhere. Neither conversion has emptied the older frame: funerals, chiefship, rain, and the moral weight of beer and of clanship remain the terms in which Chamba communities argue about what they owe each other.
