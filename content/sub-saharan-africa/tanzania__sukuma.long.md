## Overview

The Sukuma (*Basukuma*, sing. *Nsukuma* or *Musukuma*) are Tanzania's largest ethnic group, roughly 10 million people — about 16 percent of the national population — spread across the flat savannah tableland south and east of Lake Victoria, in the Mwanza, Shinyanga and Simiyu regions, with the northern edge of their territory running into the Serengeti Plain. The land sits between 910 and 1,220 metres elevation, receives 51–102 cm of rain between November and March, and is farmed in small, ridged plots interspersed with baobabs and thornbush steppe. They call their homeland *Busukuma* or Sukumaland. *Kisukuma* is a Bantu language, closely tied to Nyamwezi — the two form what Abrahams called the Nyamwezi–Sukuma complex, with *Sukuma* literally meaning "north" and *Dakama* "south." In folk-culture terms the Sukuma matter above all for their dance societies: a competitive, guild-structured performance system unmatched in East Africa.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Sukuma are not a weaving people. Their visual culture is carried by hide, beadwork, gourd, iron and carved wood rather than by loom cloth, and the pattern-bearing objects in museum collections are overwhelmingly dance regalia, medicine containers and chiefly insignia. What follows are the documented pattern-bearing traditions, several of which belong to dance rather than dress.

**Ngoma regalia** — the assembled costume of a dance society, built from cowhide skirts, goatskin capes, cowrie-studded bands and feather headpieces. Pattern here is accretive: a dancer's standing is read off the density of shells, bells and charms rather than off a woven design.

***Busumba*** — beaded and cowrie-worked belt and waist ornament worn by dancers and by young men at courtship gatherings. Cowries, which travelled inland along the Tabora caravan route, cluster in bands and lozenges against hide.

***Ntemi* regalia** — the chiefly insignia of the *ntemi* (chief), including beaded and iron-mounted stools, staffs, and the conus-shell disc worn at the chest. These are the closest Sukuma equivalent to a court textile: status objects whose ornament is fixed by office, not fashion.

***Bunuunguli*** — the porcupine society's paraphernalia, named for *nunguli*, the porcupine. Quills are mounted into headdresses and into the bundles carried in the *mbina ya bunuunguli*, giving a radiating, spined silhouette found nowhere in the neighbouring Haya or Maasai repertoire.

***Lubango*** snake charm — the medicine bundle of the snake-handling *bagika* dancers, wrapped in hide and cord and hung with horn, root and shell. The wrapping itself is the pattern: helical binding over a gourd or horn core.

***Ndono*** figure — carved wooden figures used by dance societies and healers, their surfaces scarified with incised chevrons and dot fields that echo human body marking.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Cowrie band* (wealth, caravan trade), *chevron incision* (on gourds and *ndono* figures), *porcupine quill radiance* (*bunuunguli* headdress), *conus disc* (chiefly chest ornament), *concentric ring* (pyro-engraved gourd), *lizard and snake* (associated with *bagika* medicine), *bovine horn curve* (cattle wealth), *hoe blade* (agricultural prosperity, tied to the Sukumaland iron trade).

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress since the mid-twentieth century is East African coastal: the *kanga* and *kitenge* wrapper for women, worn doubled — one at the waist, one over the shoulder or head — and shirt with trousers or a wrapper for men. Older Sukuma dress, still worn for dance and initiation, was hide-based: a tanned cowhide or goatskin skirt for women, softened and oiled, sometimes edged with cowries or beadwork; a shoulder cape of goat or civet skin for men. Dance dress is the surviving ceremonial register and is deliberately loaded — *busumba* belts hung with iron bells and cowries, ankle rattles, feather and quill headdresses, whitened or ochred body paint, and shoulder-slung medicine bundles. *Bagalu* and *bagika* society dancers dress in competition against each other, so costume is a claim of superiority as much as an aesthetic choice. Elders at *wigashe* seated-song gatherings dress soberly by contrast: wrapper, blanket over the shoulder, staff, and a hide-covered stool.

### Architecture

The Sukuma homestead is the *kaya*: a walled or fenced family compound holding the household's huts, granaries and a cattle enclosure, set among its own fields rather than in a nucleated village — the settlement pattern the Wikipedia description of "small farm plots and sparse vegetation" reflects. The core house type is the *nzengo*, a rectangular or square flat-roofed earth house, built on a timber frame with mud-and-wattle walls, the roof carried on beams and loaded with packed earth over a layer of grass and branches. This flat earth roof distinguishes Sukuma building from the conical thatched round houses of many neighbouring Bantu groups and gives the compound its low, block-like profile on the plain. Round thatched huts occur as well, usually for grain storage, kitchens and unmarried sons. The cattle *boma* — a thorn or pole kraal at the compound's centre or edge — is the organising feature, reflecting a mixed-farming economy in which herds survived the 1890s rinderpest and tsetse collapse better among the Sukuma than almost anywhere else in German East Africa.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Sukumaland's iron deposits supported a substantial smithing industry: the region re-exported some 150,000 iron hoes to Tabora in the nineteenth century, and the *mashona* hoe blade is both the central agricultural tool and a standard of value, appearing in bridewealth and in the ridged-field cultivation the Nyamwezi–Sukuma practised on arid soils. Smiths also produced spearheads, bells, bracelets and the iron staff-heads mounted in chiefly regalia. Gourd (*kibuyu*) work is the other everyday craft: calabashes cut for milk, beer and medicine storage, their surfaces pyro-engraved with concentric rings and dot fields and fitted with hide-and-cord slings. Pottery is hand-coiled, open-fired and largely undecorated — cooking pots, water jars and beer vessels. Animal skins are used for house decoration and for bags, as well as for stool seats and drum heads.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Beadwork dominates: strung waist, neck and ankle bands in the trade beads that arrived along the caravan roads from Bagamoyo and Tabora, worked with cowries into the *busumba*. Iron and brass bracelets and anklets, made by local smiths, are worn in stacked coils. The conus-shell disc is the marked status ornament, associated with *ntemi* chieftaincy. Dancers add bells at the ankle and waist so that movement is audible, and medicine charms — small horn, root and hide bundles — are worn on cords at the neck or arm, including the *lubango* carried by snake handlers. Body marking includes ochre and white clay painting for dance and initiation, and filed or extracted teeth among older generations. Women's hair is plaited close in ridged rows.

## Music & performance

Sukuma music is organised around dance competition, and its instruments serve that structure. The *ngoma* drums — a graded set played with hands and sticks, hide heads pegged or laced over carved log bodies — carry the dance; the *hiyo* drum is named among the Sukuma dance drums in the Utamaduni archive recordings. Alongside them: the *ndono*, a one-string bowed tube-fiddle whose name the carved figures share; gourd rattles and seed-pod leg rattles worn by dancers; iron bells; and the *ilimba* lamellophone, the plucked-tongue instrument of the wider Nyamwezi–Sukuma zone whose Gogo cousin gave jazz the "limba" name.

The two great song-and-dance genres divide by posture. *Wigashe* is the seated song — from *kwigasha*, "to sit" — performed by elders and by women's groups in a ring, a sung, call-and-response commentary form in which lyrics carry praise, complaint, dispute and social criticism; it is the Sukuma satirical and news-bearing genre. *Mabina* is the standing, danced repertoire. Both are staged within the rivalry of the *bagalu* and *bagika* moieties, the two hereditary dance federations whose founding figures — *Ngika* for the *bagika*, and the herbalist *Ng'wana Malundi* associated with the *bagalu* line — are invoked in song. Competitions run in the dry season after harvest, in a cleared arena, and are judged by the crowd: whichever society draws and holds the larger audience wins. Because audience is the prize, societies invest in medicines (*samba*), charms and spectacle, and performances can run through the night.

## Dance & theatre

The named Sukuma dances are the clearest expression of the competitive system. ***Bugobogobo*** is the hoe dance: dancers perform with *mashona* iron hoes, striking, swinging and balancing them in a choreography drawn from ridged-field cultivation, and it doubles as work — societies hoe a member's field in formation, turning collective labour into performance. ***Bugalu*** is the repertoire of the *bagalu* federation. ***Bunuunguli*** — *mbina ya bunuunguli* — is the porcupine dance, in which quills, live porcupines and porcupine medicine feature, historically performed by the society that hunted and handled them. ***Bagika*** performances centre on snake handling, with pythons and venomous snakes carried, draped and danced with, protected by *lubango* charms — the medicine men's capture of snakes and porcupines "to be used as entertainment" is the documented origin of both. *Isamva* and *samba ya mzee* belong to the medicine-and-charm complex that underwrites these acts. There is no masked or scripted theatre tradition; the dramatic register is the competitive arena itself, where *wigashe* lyrics stage public argument.

## Festivals & rituals

The festival calendar follows the agricultural year rather than a fixed-date cycle. The dance season opens after the harvest, from roughly June through the dry months, when labour demand falls and *bagalu*–*bagika* competitions are mounted across Sukumaland; this is when the largest *bugobogobo* and *bunuunguli* performances occur. Rain-calling and first-fruits observances precede planting at the start of the November rains, and involve sacrifice at ancestral shrines — Sukuma tradition holds that late-nineteenth-century famine followed religious innovation, and conservative opinion expected regular sacrifices to household or chiefdom ancestors. Ancestor veneration is the ritual spine: offerings of beer, flour and livestock at the homestead shrine, made by the senior man of the *kaya* on behalf of the lineage.

Life-cycle rites: birth is followed by seclusion and a naming that often records circumstance or day. Initiation into a dance society, rather than a universal age-set ceremony, is the major transition for young men, involving apprenticeship to a society leader, payment, and the transmission of *samba* medicines. Marriage is by bridewealth in cattle — the herds that survived the 1892 rinderpest made the Sukuma unusual in retaining cattle-based marriage economics through the colonial period — negotiated between lineages, with the transfer staged over years. Funerals bring burial within or beside the homestead, followed by a mourning period and a later commemorative gathering with beer and dance. Divination and healing sit alongside all of these: Sukuma cosmology treats the world as interconnected across living, natural and supernatural, and possession by spirits is an accepted diagnosis, treated by healers whose *dawa* draws on both plants and animals — pangolin scales sold to protect a harvest, faunal medicines classified against a matched list of diseases and symptoms.

## Foodways

The staple is *ugali*, stiff maize porridge cooked from *sembe* flour, eaten by hand in lumps dipped into a relish; sorghum and millet preceded maize and remain in use, particularly for beer. The relish (*mboga*) is most often *mlenda* — the mucilaginous leaf stews of jute mallow, hibiscus or *Corchorus* that are the signature Sukuma vegetable dish, slippery in texture and eaten with ugali — alongside beans, cowpea leaves, pumpkin leaves and groundnut sauces. Sweet potato, cassava and pumpkin fill the hungry season. Milk and soured milk come from the household herd, but cattle are slaughtered mainly for ceremony: the Sukuma historically supplied meat to Unyanyembe, the Nyamwezi kingdom centred on Tabora, rather than eating heavily of their own stock. Freshwater fish from Lake Victoria — tilapia, Nile perch, and the small dried *dagaa* — are a staple protein across Mwanza and Magu. Chicken and goat mark visits and feasts. The ceremonial drink is *pombe*, home-brewed millet or maize beer, fermented in gourds and drunk communally from a shared pot; it is poured for ancestors, paid to work parties, and consumed through dance competitions. Rice, and coastal dishes like *pilau*, arrived with the caravan trade and are now wedding food.

## Oral tradition & literature

The principal Sukuma verbal art is sung rather than recited: *wigashe* lyrics function as the community's running commentary, praising, shaming and adjudicating, and a society's composer is a figure of real influence. Prose genres are the folktale (*shihaanja*), often animal-trickster narratives featuring the hare and hyena common across the Bantu-speaking Great Lakes, told in the evening at the homestead; proverbs (*nhungo*), used densely in dispute settlement and in song lyric; and riddles exchanged as a children's pastime. Praise naming attaches to persons, cattle and dance societies alike. Written preservation of the repertoire has been substantial: the Sukuma Museum (Bujora, near Mwanza), founded by the Catholic missionary-ethnographer David Clement, holds dance-society collections, a chief's pavilion and a royal drum house; the Daraja la Utamaduni association in Denmark maintains an archive of Sukuma dance video. Per Brandström's fieldwork at Uppsala and R. G. Abrahams's work on Greater Unyamwezi remain the anchoring ethnographies.

## Language & religion

*Kisukuma* is a Bantu language of the Niger–Congo family, in the Northeast Bantu group (Guthrie F.21), mutually intelligible in large part with Nyamwezi and internally divided into dialect clusters — Kimunasukuma, Kimunakiiya, Kimunadakama and Kimunangiiya — roughly corresponding to the north, east, south and west of Sukumaland. It has no indigenous script; Roman orthography was introduced by missionaries, and Swahili is the language of school, church and market. The religious landscape is majority Christian, Catholic and Protestant, with a Muslim minority along the lake and trade routes, laid over a persistent substrate of ancestor veneration and spirit possession: the *ntemi* chiefship carried ritual as well as political authority, and healers (*bafumu*) continue to diagnose possession, prepare *dawa* from plant and animal materials, and supply the *samba* medicines that dance societies depend on.
