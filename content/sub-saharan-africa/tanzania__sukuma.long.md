## Overview

The Sukuma are Tanzania's most numerous people, an agro-pastoral Bantu-speaking population occupying the dry savanna and granite-strewn plains that sweep south and east from Lake Victoria — a territory they call Sukumaland, centred on Mwanza and extending through Shinyanga, Simiyu and Geita. Their language, Kisukuma, is so close to Nyamwezi that the two are often treated as a single dialect continuum, and the ethnonym itself derives from a directional term meaning "north," a name given from the vantage of the Nyamwezi to the south; this hints at what historians and anthropologists have long argued, that "Sukuma" names a broad, absorptive and relatively recent identity rather than an ancient tribe, assembled over several centuries from Bantu farmers moving into the lake basin and from pastoral peoples drifting in from the drier east. Before colonial rule the region was divided among dozens of small chiefdoms, each headed by an *ntemi* whose authority rested less on armed force than on ritual guardianship of rain, land and the fertility of herds; these chiefdoms were formalised and then dissolved by the Tanzanian state in 1963, leaving the ritual and aesthetic apparatus of chiefship to survive mainly as heritage and performance. Sukuma livelihood has long rested on a double foundation of cattle and cultivation — sorghum and millet historically, then maize, cassava, rice and above all cotton, which made the Mwanza hinterland one of East Africa's great cash-crop zones — and this labouring life is the wellspring of their most distinctive cultural contribution: an extraordinarily developed complex of competitive dance associations, above all the rival societies *Bagalu* and *Bagika*, whose contests structure village life, absorb enormous expenditure on medicine, costume and carved display objects, and have made Sukuma performance among the best documented in East Africa. Their material signature lies less in loom-woven textiles than in sculpture, ironwork and the theatrical assemblage: carved figures, ornamented hoes, hide drums, beaded and feathered dance dress, and the porcupine, snake and other animal-handling spectacles that accompany them.

## Material culture

Sukuma material culture is organised around three poles: the homestead and its herds, the agricultural tool, and the dance arena. Objects are typically made for use — hoe, pot, granary, drum — and it is in ceremonial and competitive display that they become elaborate.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Sukuma are not a weaving people in the West African or Central Asian sense; there is no indigenous loom tradition of note, and pattern is carried instead on beadwork, basketry, hide and body decoration, with imported factory cloth adopted early and enthusiastically.

- **Bark cloth and hide** — Before trade cloth became general, clothing and bedding commonly used dressed cattle and goat hides and, in some communities, beaten bark cloth; hides remained in ritual and dance use long after cotton replaced them for daily wear.
- **Trade cloth** (*kanga*, *kitenge*) — Printed cotton wrappers bought at market are the everyday textile; the region's own cotton is a cash crop grown for export and spinning mills rather than for local handweaving.
- **Beaded panels and belts** — Geometric beadwork in dense red, white, blue and black bands decorates dance belts, headbands, bandoliers and gourd containers, and is the principal vehicle of Sukuma surface pattern.
- **Coiled basketry and winnowing trays** — Coiled and plaited grass baskets, granary linings and winnowing trays carry banded and spiralling patterns, made by women for household use.
- **Dance costume assemblage** (*mabina* dress) — Competition dress combines cloth, hide, beadwork, feathers, bells, animal skins and fibre skirts; the aesthetic is additive and startling rather than woven.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress today is Tanzanian national and Swahili-coastal in character; distinctively Sukuma dress survives in the costume of dance societies, healers and the vestigial regalia of chiefship.

- **Hide wrapper and skin cloak** — Historic garments of dressed skin worn wrapped or knotted at the shoulder, retained by elders and ritual specialists into the twentieth century.
- **Chiefly regalia** (*ntemi* regalia) — The *ntemi*'s insignia commonly included a stool, staff or spear, leopard or other cat skin, copper and iron ornaments, and above all custody of chiefdom drums; regalia was less costume than a portable claim to ritual legitimacy.
- **Elder's dress** (*samba ya mzee*) — Sober wrapper, staff and fly-whisk marking the seniority of a household head at gatherings and adjudications.
- **Dance headdress** — Feathered, horned or fur headpieces, often with colobus or goat hair, distinguish dance groups and their specialisms in the arena.
- **Ankle bells and rattles** — Iron or tin bells and seed rattles bound at the ankle and waist make the dancer's body part of the percussion ensemble.

### Architecture

Sukuma building is an architecture of the homestead rather than the monument: dispersed family compounds set among granite outcrops, combining dwellings, granaries and a cattle enclosure, with the herd's security as the organising principle.

- **Homestead** (*kaya*) — The family compound, comprising one or more dwellings for the household head and each wife, grain stores and a stock enclosure, usually sited near grazing and a water source.
- **Round thatched house** — Circular wattle-and-daub or mud-and-pole walls under a conical grass roof is the widespread historic form; rectangular houses with mud-brick walls and metal sheeting have largely replaced it.
- **Cattle enclosure** — A thorn-brush or stake kraal at the heart or edge of the compound, into which the herd is driven at night; its size is a direct statement of household standing.
- **Granary** — Raised or plinth-set grain store with a removable thatch cap, protecting sorghum, millet and maize from damp and vermin.
- **Communal building party** (*nzengo*) — Houses, granaries and field work are commonly raised by reciprocal work parties fed with beer and accompanied by song; the same institution that builds also dances.
- **Bujora Cultural Centre** — The mid-twentieth-century complex near Mwanza, developed with the Catholic mission, where a royal pavilion, dance-society pavilions and a church incorporating Sukuma motifs and a drum-shaped altar constitute a deliberate Sukuma architectural statement.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Iron and clay are the classic Sukuma crafts. The hoe in particular is not merely a tool but an emblem — of labour, of marriage payment, of the dance — and smithing carried a corresponding prestige and ritual guardedness.

- **Iron hoe** (*mashona* type hoe) — Broad-bladed iron hoe with a tanged fitting into a wooden haft, used for the heavy tillage that underwrites Sukuma subsistence and historically circulating as wealth and bridewealth.
- **Smith's work** — Blades, spearheads, arrowheads, bells, wire and ornaments worked at village forges; smiths were commonly surrounded by avoidance rules and mystique.
- **Women's pottery** — Hand-built, coil-raised earthenware fired in open bonfires: cooking pots, water jars and wide-mouthed beer pots, often burnished and decorated with incised or roulette bands below the rim.
- **Gourd vessels** — Calabashes for milk, beer and medicine, sometimes beaded, pyro-engraved or fitted with hide collars; milk gourds are treated with particular care in pastoral households.
- **Carved figures** (*ndono*) — Wooden figure sculpture, including tall figures displayed or carried in dance competitions, whose ownership and revelation formed part of a group's competitive arsenal.
- **Stools, mortars and headrests** — Solid-carved low stools, grain mortars and pestles, and small headrests, plain in form and valued for surface finish rather than ornament.
- **Medicine containers and charms** (*bugota*) — Horn, gourd and hide containers for compounded medicines, including the reputed *lubango* substances credited with attracting audiences and controlling snakes.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ornament is mostly of beads, iron, copper, brass wire, cowrie and animal material, worn in quantity for ceremonies and dance and sparingly in daily life.

- **Beaded collars and bandoliers** — Multi-strand beaded neckpieces and crossed chest bands worn by dancers and by women at weddings and initiations.
- **Wire coils and bangles** — Coiled brass, copper and iron worn on wrists, upper arms and ankles; heavy sets denote wealth and were often given at marriage.
- **Cowrie ornament** — Cowrie shells sewn to headbands, belts and figures, retaining older Indian Ocean trade prestige.
- **Ivory and horn armlets** — Carved armlets and pendants of ivory, hippo tooth or horn, associated with senior men and chiefly households before colonial restriction of the ivory trade.
- **Scarification and tooth modification** — Patterned cicatrisation on the torso and, in some communities, filing or removal of lower incisors were historic beauty and identity marks, now largely abandoned.

## Music & performance

Sukuma music is inseparable from labour and from competition. Its two great arenas are the cultivated field and the dance ground, and its characteristic sound is a lead voice answered by a dense chorus over a battery of hide drums. The *ngoma* — a word that means drum, dance and occasion at once — is the organising unit: a named repertoire owned by a group, with its own songs, steps, medicines and paraphernalia. Drums range from the deep single-headed vessel drums to smaller high-pitched instruments, among them the type named *hiyo*, and are struck with hand and stick in interlocking patterns; singers add rattles, ankle bells, whistles, and clapped and stamped accents, while one-string and multi-string plucked and bowed lutes of the Sukuma–Nyamwezi type provide accompaniment for solo praise and narrative singing. The most celebrated genre is *bugobogobo*, the hoe dance of the farming work parties, in which cultivators hoe in ranks to song and then carry the hoes into choreographed display, brandishing and balancing them; Frank Gunderson's study of Sukuma labour song documents how such groups competed to finish fields fastest and most beautifully, so that agricultural productivity and aesthetic reputation were the same currency. Alongside the danced repertoires sits *wigashe*, a seated song contest in which rival choirs, singing without leaving their benches, trade extended topical, boasting and satirical verses — a genre prized for verbal invention, and a reminder that Sukuma competition is at least as much about words and medicine as about legs.

## Dance & theatre

The defining institution is the pair of rival dance societies, *Bagalu* and *Bagika*, whose membership cuts across chiefdoms and whose traditions of origin attribute them to founding medicine-men — *Bagalu* to the figure remembered as *Ng'wana Malundi*, *Bagika* to a counterpart founder — and whose rivalry has structured Sukuma performance for well over a century. Almost every named dance belongs to one side or the other, so that a village festival becomes a contest of reputation: *bugalu* for one society's repertoire, and a spread of specialist *mabina* including animal-handling displays, of which the snake dance is the most notorious. Snake dancers, commonly associated with the specialist *bayeye* tradition, handle pythons and venomous snakes before the crowd, their confidence credited to *lubango* medicine; other groups keep porcupines — the *bunuunguli* complex and its *mbina ya bunuunguli* repertoire — or display carved figures, drugged animals and other secret attractions unveiled at the climax of a performance. The competitive logic is explicit: the winner is the group that draws and holds the crowd, and much of the expense goes not on choreography but on medicines, charms and objects believed to pull spectators from a rival's arena. Young men's associations (*busumba*) were the traditional organising frame for this, combining labour, mutual aid, courtship and performance for the unmarried; theatre in a scripted sense is modern and urban, but Sukuma dance has always carried strong theatrical elements of masking, mimicry, comic interlude and topical satire directed at neighbours, chiefs and, in the colonial period, at authority.

## Festivals & rituals

The Sukuma ceremonial year has no single dominant festival comparable to a harvest new year; instead, ritual clusters around rain, the agricultural calendar, the life cycle and the ancestors. Rainmaking was the central public duty of the *ntemi*, whose failure could end his tenure, and rain rites — offerings at chiefdom shrines, at ancestral graves and at conspicuous rocks and trees — remain remembered even where the office does not. The dead are addressed as *masamva*, ancestral spirits whose neglect brings illness, barren cattle and misfortune, and who are appeased with libations of beer and milk, small offerings of flour and the slaughter of a beast at the homestead. Divination and healing form a dense professional field: the diviner-healer (*mfumu*, pl. *bafumu*) diagnoses through chicken entrails, thrown objects, dreams and possession, prescribes *bugota* medicine, and is often also a dance-society specialist, since the same knowledge that cures also wins competitions. Birth, naming, puberty, marriage and death each carry their own observances; marriage is negotiated between lineages with bridewealth paid principally in cattle, and funerals for senior people bring large gatherings, extended mourning and eventual commemorative feasting with the slaughter of stock. Dance competitions themselves function as festival, staged at the close of the cultivation season, at weddings and at commemorations, and since the mid-twentieth century concentrated in annual gatherings at Bujora and in national culture festivals. No Sukuma element is currently inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, although Sukuma dance and labour song are regularly cited in Tanzanian heritage inventories as candidates.

## Foodways

The Sukuma table rests on stiff grain porridge eaten with a relish, a pattern shared across eastern Africa but inflected by the region's cattle and by the lake. The staple is *bugali*, a firm porridge of maize meal — historically sorghum or bulrush millet, and still these in drier districts — dipped into a sauce of leafy greens, beans, groundnuts, tomato and onion, and on better days into meat or fish. Cattle are wealth rather than everyday food: they supply milk, soured milk and ghee, they discharge bridewealth and fines, and they are slaughtered chiefly for funerals, commemorations and major hospitality, when the meat is roasted or stewed and distributed by seniority. Proximity to Lake Victoria has made fish central in the northern districts, particularly tilapia, Nile perch and the small dried sardine *dagaa*, which travels far inland as a cheap protein. Cassava, sweet potato, groundnuts, bambara nuts, pumpkins, sesame and, in the irrigated lowlands, rice fill out the field repertoire, with wild greens, mushrooms, honey and seasonal winged termites gathered as relishes. Grain beer — sorghum, millet or maize, commonly called *pombe* in Swahili — is the essential social substance: it pays the work party, opens the negotiation, honours the ancestor and fuels the dance, and its brewing is women's work and women's income.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kisukuma oral literature is rich in genres that reward verbal competition: narrative folktales with animal tricksters and moral reversals, riddles exchanged in the evening, proverbs deployed in argument and adjudication, praise and genealogical recitation for chiefly houses, and above all song texts, which carry the sharpest commentary. Because the dance societies compete partly through words, *wigashe* and labour-song repertoires preserve a running poetic record of grievance, gossip and history — colonial taxation and cattle-culling campaigns, cotton prices, migration to the mines, the abolition of chiefship, later national politics — composed by named song-leaders whose reputations rest on invention and cutting allusion. Traditions of origin, including the accounts of *Ng'wana Malundi* and the founding of the two societies, circulate as narrative and as claim, since a society's charter is also its argument for precedence. Written Kisukuma owes much to mission work, which produced orthographies, catechetical translations and the first substantial printed collections; the mid-century ethnographic and folkloric record assembled around Bujora, together with the colonial sociologist Hans Cory's documentation of Sukuma law, custom and dance, means the tradition is unusually well archived for an East African interior people, and Sukuma-language radio, gospel and dance recordings continue it in new media.

## Language & religion

Kisukuma is a Bantu language of the Sukuma–Nyamwezi group spoken across Sukumaland with substantial internal dialect variation, mutually intelligible to a large degree with Nyamwezi and written in Latin script; it remains a vigorous household and local-market language, though Swahili dominates schooling, administration, trade and inter-ethnic life, and urban Sukuma in Mwanza are commonly Swahili-first. Religiously, most Sukuma today identify as Christian — Catholicism is especially strong around Mwanza, a consequence of long Missionaries of Africa activity, with Anglican, Lutheran and Pentecostal communities also present — while a Muslim minority reflects older caravan-route contact and coastal influence. Neither confession has displaced the older cosmology so much as layered over it: a distant creator associated with the sky and the ordering of the world, and the immediate, demanding presence of the ancestral *masamva*, whose consultation through diviners and propitiation with beer, milk and stock continues in many households alongside baptism and church membership. Witchcraft accusation remains socially consequential and has at times had violent outcomes, including the well-documented persecution of elderly women and of people with albinism in the lake regions — a grim modern face of an old concern with hidden causation, which is also what makes the healer, the medicine and the charm so central to Sukuma life and to Sukuma art.
