## Overview

The Turkana live in a semi-arid region of northwest Kenya. They rely mainly on herding goats, camels, donkeys and zebu. Their material culture includes woven baskets, leather clothing, milk vessels, weapons and beadwork. Ritual specialists, elders and life-cycle ceremonies remain important in community life.

## Material culture

Turkana material culture uses wood, hide, leather, fibre, gourds, beads, bone and metal for clothing, homes, vessels, weapons and adornment.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Turkana textile traditions use woven fibre, hides, leather and beads for clothing, baskets and containers.

- **Rectangular woven wraps** — Men and women traditionally wear rectangular woven wraps, with men arranging them like tunics over the right shoulder.
- **Leather wraps with ostrich eggshell beads** — Women traditionally wore leather undergarments covered with ostrich eggshell beads, though these are now uncommon in many areas.
- **Hide aprons** — Hide aprons include beaded aprons, belts and a girl’s pubic apron.
- **Woven fibre baskets** — Turkana baskets are woven from light-coloured fibre and may have flat bases, straight sides and shallow lids.
- **Leather and hide containers** — Leather and hide containers hold fat or ghee and may include carrying thongs, cords or bead decoration.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional dress combines cloth, animal skins, beads and practical objects, although many people in towns now wear western-style clothing.

- **Men’s shoulder wrap** — Men wear a wrap arranged like a tunic and secured over the right shoulder.
- **Women’s two-piece dress** — Women wear one piece of cloth around the waist and another over the upper body.
- **Men’s shaved hair and cap** — Men shave their hair and wear a cap whether they are Muslims or not.
- **Ekicholong stool** (*ekicholong*) — Men carry small wooden stools called ekicholong.
- **Wrist knife** — Men carry wrist knives made of steel and goat hide.

### Architecture

Turkana houses are domed structures made from saplings, palm fronds, hides or skins, and lashings.

- **Domed family house** — A Turkana house is built over a framework of domed saplings and is large enough for a family of six.
- **Wet-season house** — During the wet season, houses are usually elongated and covered with cow dung.
- **Brushwood animal pen** — Animals are kept in a pen made from brushwood.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Everyday objects are made from wood, gourds, hide, leather, fibre and iron, with many linked to milk, herding and fighting.

- **Milk-pail** — Milk-pails are made from wood and hide.
- **Milk-bowl** — Milk-bowls are made from hollowed wood or gourds.
- **Pokerwork gourd container** — A small gourd container has pokerwork decoration, glass beads around its neck and an iron spoon attached by a thong.
- **Crocodile-skin shield** — Museum records document shields made from crocodile skin.
- **Throwing stick** — Throwing sticks are among the recorded Turkana weapons and household objects.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Turkana adornment centres on necklaces, bone and glass beads, decorated hair, head-dresses and lip or nose ornaments.

- **Ostrich-eggshell necklace** — Women customarily wear necklaces made from ostrich eggshell beads.
- **Bone and blue-glass belt** — Some belts are made from rock hyrax digit bones linked with blue glass beads.
- **Ancestor’s-hair head-dress** — Some head-dresses combine woven ancestor’s hair, feathers and coiled brass wire.
- **Pangolin-scale amulet** (*dawa*) — A pangolin-scale amulet called dawa is worn around the leg as medicine and protection.
- **Male lip ornament** — A male lip ornament may be worn on a wooden pin through the lower lip.

## Music & performance

The sources name few musical traditions but record instruments, bells, horns, flutes and performance objects.

- **Six-string trough zither** — A composite trough zither has six strings and a wooden handle.
- **End-blown flute** — A wooden flute is end-blown, has four finger holes and is bound with fibre cord.
- **Dance horn** — A horn made from sable antelope or oryx horn is used during dances.
- **Dance-stick** — A wooden dance-stick has a leather thong securing lengths of animal hair.
- **Goat bell** — A goat bell is made of iron and hide.

## Dance & theatre

Turkana dance is documented through photographs and objects, but the sources do not describe its movements or theatre traditions.

- **Turkana dance** (*Turkana-ngoma*) — A 1922 catalogue identifies a photograph of Turkana dance as Turkana-ngoma.
- **Dance whisk** — A dance whisk is worn tightly around the forearm between the wrist and elbow.
- **Dance horn** — A horn is used during dances.
- **Hair-decorated dance-stick** — A dance-stick is decorated with animal hair and leather thongs.

## Festivals & rituals

Turkana rituals mark birth, initiation, marriage, blessing and death, and are guided by elders and religious specialists.

- **Birth ritual** (*aikido*) — Birth rituals are called aikido.
- **Male initiation** (*aspen*) — Male initiation rituals are called aspen and do not include circumcision.
- **Female initiation** (*akinyonyo*) — Female initiation rituals are called akinyonyo and do not include circumcision.
- **Marriage ritual** (*Akuuta*) — Marriage rituals are called Akuuta.
- **Annual blessing sacrifice** (*Apiaret an awi*) — Annual blessing sacrifices are called Apiaret an awi.

## Foodways

Milk and meat from herds form the centre of Turkana foodways, supported by porridge, gathered fruits, honey and trade.

- **Maize porridge with milk** — People commonly eat maize porridge with milk in the morning.
- **Nang’aria** — Plain maize porridge called nang’aria is commonly eaten with stew at lunch and dinner.
- **Milk tea** — The Turkana buy tea from towns and make milk tea.
- **Roasted goat** — Goat is often roasted over a fire and is eaten more frequently than zebu.
- **Festival zebu** — Zebu is eaten during festivals.

## Oral tradition & literature

Turkana traditions connect the people’s identity and power with the grey bull, zebu cattle and diviners.

- **People of the grey bull** — Turkana oral traditions call the people the “people of the grey bull,” referring to the zebu.
- **Diviners** (*ngimurok*) — The ngimurok are religious specialists who receive revelations, interpret problems and communicate with ancestors.
- **Diviners of God** — The most respected diviners are said to receive revelations directly from Akuj, normally through dreams.
- **Lokerio** — Lokerio is named as a famous Turkana ngimurok.
- **Lokorijem** — Lokorijem is named as a famous Turkana ngimurok.

## Language & religion

Turkana is an Eastern Nilotic language, and traditional religion centres on Akuj, ancestors, sacrifice and blessings.

- **Turkana language** — Turkana is spoken mainly in northwestern Kenya and is closely related to several Ateker languages.
- **Ateker** — Ateker is the collective name for the related language cluster that includes Turkana.
- **Akuj** — Akuj is the Supreme Being who created the world and controls the blessings of life.
- **Ancestors** (*ngipean*) — Ancestors called ngipean or ngikaram are believed to require animal sacrifices when angry.
- **Ekipe** — Ekipe is an ancestor believed to act in everyday life through negative effects.
