## Overview

The Aka, also called Biaka, Bayaka and Babenzele, are a nomadic Mbenga pygmy people living in south-western Central African Republic and northern Republic of the Congo. They are traditionally hunter-gatherers whose society is characterised by cooperation, extensive food sharing and relatively little formal hierarchy. The Aka speak the Aka language and also commonly speak one or more Bantu languages used by neighbouring farming populations. Their cultural importance lies especially in their oral traditions, rainforest knowledge, communal foodways and polyphonic singing. Aka communities have maintained long-standing exchange relationships with neighbouring Bantu-speaking farming communities, while many Aka today combine hunting, fishing and gathering with wage labour, ecotourism and conservation work.

## Music & performance

Music is an important part of Aka life. Polyphonic singing accompanies communal celebrations and ceremonies associated with forest spirits, and remains an important part of community life. It consists of several interlocking vocal lines that are continuously adapted through improvisation. The Aka are particularly known for dense contrapuntal communal improvisation. Mbenga–Mbuti Pygmy music can consist of up to four parts and is described as an “ostinato with variations,” based on repeated periods of equal length that singers divide through different rhythmic figures.

Aka music is transmitted through participation and everyday life. The sources state that Pygmies learn the music while growing up rather than through a theoretical framework. Traditional songs, stories, ritual practices and rainforest knowledge are transmitted primarily through oral tradition.

Named instruments associated with Pygmy music include the *ieta*, a bow harp; the *ngombi*, a harp zither; and the *limbindi*, a string bow. **Liquindi** is water drumming typically practised by Pygmy women and girls, who stand in water and strike its surface with their hands. **Hindewhu** is a singing and whistle-playing style of the BaBenzélé pygmies of the Central African Republic. A performer alternates pitched syllables with blowing into a single-pitch papaya-stem whistle. Hindewhu announces the return from a hunt and may be performed solo, in a duo or in groups.

In 2003, the polyphonic singing of the Aka was proclaimed by UNESCO as a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity. It was later incorporated into UNESCO’s Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity and was relisted there in 2008. Simha Arom produced early systematic studies of Aka polyphony, Michelle Kisliuk examined its social and performative context, and Louis Sarno recorded more than 1,400 hours of music, oral traditions and rainforest soundscapes.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that polyphonic singing accompanies communal celebrations and ceremonies associated with forest spirits. The sources also describe ritual practices as part of the knowledge transmitted through oral tradition.

Aka social life includes shared childcare, food preparation, hunting and many other daily activities. Camps usually consist of several related families who share food, childcare and daily activities, while decisions affecting the group are generally reached through discussion and consensus.

## Foodways

The Aka are traditionally hunter-gatherers with a varied diet that includes at least 63 plant species, 28 species of game and 20 species of insects, as well as nuts, fruit, honey, mushrooms and roots. Meat, fish, honey and other forest resources are commonly distributed among families within the camp. Food sharing reinforces social ties and helps reduce material inequalities.

Some Aka cultivate small seasonal crops, although most agricultural products are obtained through exchange with neighbouring Bantu-speaking farming communities, collectively known as the Ngandu. Through this exchange, the Aka obtain manioc, plantains, yams, taro, maize, cucumbers, squash, okra, papaya, mangoes, pineapples, palm oil and rice in return for bushmeat, honey and other forest products. The sources describe these relationships as long-standing and economically important.

Hunting, butchering and preparation of bushmeat involve frequent contact with wild animals.

## Oral tradition & literature

Aka oral traditions were proclaimed by UNESCO in 2003 as a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity. The Aka language is transmitted primarily through oral tradition. Traditional songs, stories, ritual practices and knowledge of the rainforest pass from one generation to the next through everyday life rather than written records.

The sources identify *likanos* stories as one form within Pygmy music. They also describe oral traditions as part of Louis Sarno’s recordings, which include music, oral traditions and rainforest soundscapes. The Aka have maintained a distinct cultural identity while living in long-standing exchange relationships with neighbouring Bantu-speaking farming communities.

## Language & religion

Aka is described as a Bantu language spoken in the Central African Republic and Republic of Congo along the Ubangi River, while the supplied account of Aka society describes the Aka language as an Ubangian language. The sources therefore present differing classifications. Aka is also known as Yaka or Beka. The people call themselves *Mraka* in the singular and *Beka* in the plural. The western Aka are known as the Benzele and the eastern Aka as the Sese.

The Aka retain their own language while maintaining relationships with neighbouring Bantu-speaking farming communities. Most Aka are multilingual, and long-standing contact has produced vocabulary exchange, particularly concerning agriculture, trade and material culture. The Aka language has 26 consonants and seven vowels.

The sources associate Aka ceremonies with forest spirits.
