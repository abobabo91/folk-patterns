## Overview

The Nara are an ethnic group inhabiting southwestern Eritrea, primarily along the border with Sudan and in the western parts of the Barka Plains. They live north of the Kunama and near the area just to the north of Barentu in the Gash-Barka Region. The Nara number between 73,000 and 108,000 individuals and constitute around 1.5–1.9% of Eritrea’s population. They are mostly subsistence farmers and are typically agrarian. Their society is divided into four subtribes: Higir, Mogareb, Koyta and Santora. The Nara speak a language described either as a language isolate or as Northern Eastern Sudanic, and many also speak Tigre and/or Arabic. Their folk-cultural record in the supplied sources is represented especially by patterned bamboo, gourd, cane and fibre objects held in the British Museum.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The supplied sources do not document named Nara textile traditions or vernacular pattern systems.

**Cane belt** — A British Museum belt consists of numerous finely plaited lengths of dark brown cane cord secured at each end with barkcloth. Further plaited cane, wrapped in barkcloth on one side, and lightly beaten bark ribbons form ties at either side.

**Plant-fibre netbag** — A rounded rectangular bilum made from unstained plant fibre has a single handle. Its front carries alternating red and black horizontal bands intersected by vertical bands of repeated short diagonal lines, with repeated rectangles and darker circular patches near the base.

**Pyroincised gourd decoration** — Several lime-containers are elongated or globular gourds decorated by pyroincision. Their surfaces include serrated-edged oval or circular motifs containing inter-linked spirals, plain circles, vertical linear bands, triangles and geometric borders.

**Pyroincised bamboo decoration** — Bamboo tobacco-pipes and cigar-holders carry incised serrated lines arranged as concentric squares, diamonds, zigzags, chevrons, rectangles, triangles and vertical bands.

**Dwarf-coconut charm** — A charm carved from whole dwarf coconut has five incised panels, traces of red pigment, concentric zigzags, concentric triangles, chevrons, concentric diamonds and a raised horizontal band of rounded zigzags.

**Model bullroarer** — A flattish wooden model bullroarer has an upper section incised with a diamond pattern and a perforation threaded with twisted fibre cord.

**Motif vocabulary.** inter-linked spirals, serrated edging, concentric squares, concentric diamonds, zigzag bands, chevrons, opposing triangles, vertical linear bands, repeated rectangles, diamond patterns.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The supplied catalogue records do not document Nara ceramics or metalwork. They do document bamboo tobacco-pipes, a bamboo cigar holder or container, pyroincised gourd lime-containers, a plant-fibre netbag, a cane belt, a wooden model bullroarer and a dwarf-coconut charm. The tobacco-pipes have circular perforations, bamboo nodes, twisted plant-fibre cords and incised serrated geometric decoration. The lime-containers contain, in some cases, traces of lime powder and are decorated with spirals, triangles, circles and serrated lines. The cigar-holder is made from pyroincised bamboo and has vertical chevron-filled bands and in-filled triangle motifs.

## Oral tradition & literature

The supplied sources do not document Nara folktales, epics, proverbs, riddles or storytelling contexts. The Nara language was also called Nara-Bana, meaning “Nara-Talk.” The Nara participate in cultural and linguistic preservation efforts through Eritrea’s Ministry of Information.

## Language & religion

The Nara language is spoken by the Nara in an area just to the north of Barentu in the Gash-Barka Region of western Eritrea. It is often confused with Kunama, which the source describes as at best only distantly related. Nara has been classified as Northern Eastern Sudanic by Rilly, while Glottolog classifies it as an isolate. The language is also known as Nara-Bana, meaning “Nara-Talk.” According to the supplied source, its endangerment status is unclear.

Four dialects are listed: Higir, the standard literary dialect spoken just to the north of Barentu; Mogoreeb, spoken from the outskirts of Haykota to Bisha village; Saantoorta, spoken to the west of Barentu; and Koyta, spoken to the northeast of Barentu. The Nara traditionally had no writing system. In 1985, the Eritrean People’s Liberation Front decided to use the Latin script for all non-Semitic languages in Eritrea, including Nara.

The Nara traditionally adhered to animist beliefs. By the 15th century they were introduced to Islam, and after the Egyptian occupation in the 19th century, most Nara adopted Islam.
