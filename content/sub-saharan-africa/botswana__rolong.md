## Overview

The Rolong, also known as the Barolong, are a Batswana people and principal tribe native to Botswana, Namibia, Lesotho, and South Africa. Their name is associated with their first *kgosi*, Morolong, who lived around 1270–1280; the ancient word *rola*, meaning metal forging and hammering, is believed to have contributed to his name. The Rolong were first recorded in 1150, were located in the southwestern Transvaal in 1300, and moved south between the Molopo and Vaal Rivers in 1400. A branch that travelled northwest into Botswana in 1450 became known as the Bakaa, while the Bakgwatheng established themselves in Molepolole by 1500. The 2022 Botswana census recorded 58,855 Rolong.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Pottery** — Pottery discovered in southeast Botswana between the years 500 and 600 has been attributed to the Rolong and Bakgalagadi. Many pieces feature a herringbone pattern around the neck and thick rims.

**Motif vocabulary.** Herringbone pattern around the neck of pottery.

### Clothing & dress

They state that beads, cloth, and seashells were sought in trade with non-neighbouring societies.

### Architecture

Rolong huts were observed in the 1880s to be significantly better in quality than Batlaping huts. They were made from strong mudwalls and sun-dried bricks covered in red mortar.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery attributed to the Rolong and Bakgalagadi was discovered in southeast Botswana between the years 500 and 600. Its described characteristics include a herringbone pattern around the neck and thick rims. The ancient word *rola* is associated with metal forging and hammering, while iron and copper tools, iron ore, and other goods such as hematite, furs, tobacco, dogs, and ivory were recorded as trade items.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They mention beads and seashells as goods sought through trade with non-neighbouring societies, without describing how the Rolong used or wore them.

They state that initiation regiments were expected to carry out tasks including hunting, raiding cattle, clearing land, and entertaining, but provide no further account of the entertainment practices.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document life-cycle and initiation practices. When Sotho-Tswana adolescents reached the age of sixteen, they were required to attend initiation schools known as *bogwera* for boys and *bojale* for girls. These schools were popular among the Rolong before the Union of South Africa in 1910, were held every four to five years, and lasted about five months.

The boys’ *bogwera* was led by the chief’s son and took place in an isolated area. Boys were circumcised and taught the clan’s laws, customs, and history. At the end of the initiation, they were organized into a *mophato*, or regiment, and given a name. The regiments were expected to carry out assigned tasks, including hunting, raiding cattle, clearing land, and entertaining. The girls’ *bojale* was shorter and less arduous; by its conclusion, girls formed a regiment similar to the boys’ regiment, although their expectations differed. Similar regiments still existed as of 1940, but initiations were no longer common. Men and women who had undergone initiation as children were observed to receive more respect than those who had not.

Marriage involves the individuals’ families and the rest of the clan. Men and women who never marry are called *lefetwa*, “one who has been passed by,” and are not considered fully grown up or to have a physical, mental, or moral defect. *Bogadi*, also known as dowry, is a mandatory payment of cattle expected by the woman’s family and required for marriage validation. The saying “Bogadi ga bo boe” means “Bogadi does not return.”

## Foodways

They state that agriculture was important on Rolong farms and that, in 1960, the Botswana government encouraged the construction and use of a grinding mill, resulting in increased crop production. The sources also mention agricultural work as part of everyday life and describe girls’ absence from missionary schools during ploughing and harvesting seasons because they needed to work on farms.

Hunting was conducted through familial and solo hunts as well as organized hunts initiated by the *kgosi*. Organized hunts with specific groups were mandatory and were typically held before an impending battle to increase morale. Their goal was to circle and kill large herds of game, after which the *kgosi* was often responsible for distributing the animals. The sources also state that such hunts could be used by the *kgosi* and *khuduthamaga*, or secret advisers, to lure and kill people whom they wanted to execute.

## Oral tradition & literature

They state that boys attending *bogwera* were taught the clan’s laws, customs, and history, but do not preserve the form in which this knowledge was transmitted. The sources also record the phrase “Mosimane, nyala mmago, o itsalêlê bo-monnawo,” translated as “Son, marry your mother and beget yourself younger brothers and sisters.” It concerns the relationship between a man and one of his father’s widows and the understanding that children born from such a marriage would be regarded as the deceased father’s children.

## Language & religion

The Serolong dialect is spoken by four Rolong clan divisions: the Tshidi, the Seleka, the Rapulana, and the Ratlou. Missionary education contributed to written language, and early schools taught Setswana alongside reading, writing, scripture, arithmetic, history, geography, and home economics.

Many Rolong religious practices were diminished by evangelical missionaries. In the early 1800s, European and American evangelicals organized missionary societies to spread the Bible and oppose customs that conflicted with their religious beliefs, including the payment of dowries, polygamy, rain-making rituals, and adolescent initiation schools. The London Missionary Society, formed in 1795, and Robert Moffat had a significant impact on Rolong education, trade and economy, and written language. Clan chiefs in Botswana did not embrace Christianity because changing values and beliefs caused conflict in political discussions. Churches founded by Rolong people were rarely attended because most people preferred those run by the London Missionary Society.
