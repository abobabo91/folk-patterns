## Overview

The Limba are an ethnic group in Sierra Leone, representing 12.4% of the country’s total population and forming its third largest ethnic group. They are based mainly in the Northern Province, especially in Bombali District, Koinadugu, Kambia District, Karene District and Tonkolili District, with smaller numbers in Guinea. Limba communities are also found in Freetown and the surrounding Western Area, following migration during the colonial era. The Limba consider themselves a mountain people and associate their history with the Wara Wara mountains. They speak Limba, also called *Hulimba*, a Niger–Congo language that is not closely related to other languages and appears to form its own branch of the Atlantic–Congo family. Their documented livelihood includes rice farming, trading, hunting, palm-wine brewing and stone building.

## Material culture

### Architecture

 They state that the Limba are mainly stone builders and that they live in the savannah-woodland region of the Northern Province of Sierra Leone.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention agricultural knowledge, rice farming, stone building, charms, shrines and an ancient wooden figure discovered in a cave at The Kakoya Village. The figure is now in the British Museum and may have represented an ancestor or deity.

## Music & performance

 They state that the Limba’s primary sport of interest is soccer and that Limba music recordings are listed among external materials associated with the group.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do document practices associated with the Bondo secret society, which aims to establish attitudes related to adulthood in girls and includes discussions of fertility, morality and proper sexual comportment. The society also maintains an interest in the well-being of its members throughout their lives.

A minority of Limbo practice a traditional religion, mainly in more remote villages. Traditional religious practices include worship of a god called *Kanu Masala* together with lesser deities, and the creation of shrines, charms and rituals. The Limba also have a spiritual home called The Kakoya Village in Wara-Wara Bafodia Chiefdom. They believe that all Limbas return to the mountain through the town beyond a “door” through the rock.

## Foodways

The Limba are mainly rice farmers, and rice farming is central to the livelihood information given in the sources. The sources also describe them as palm-wine brewers, traders and hunters. The Limba language article gives *kameci* as a Limba word meaning “late, brown rice,”.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources document Limba folklore concerning political leadership and spiritual beings. According to the folklore described, Limbas make excellent political leaders because they are descendants of the original rulers of Sierra Leone. The sources also mention folklore about spirits called *Krifi*, while noting that information about this is limited.

## Language & religion

Limba, also called *Hulimba*, is a Niger–Congo language of Sierra Leone and Guinea. It is not closely related to other languages and appears to form its own branch of the Atlantic–Congo family. Dialects listed in the sources are *Tonko*, *Sela*, *Kamuke* or *Ke*, *Wara-wara*, *Keleng*, *Biriwa* and *Safroko*. East Limba is spoken in Ouré-Kaba, Guinea, while West-Central Limba is spoken in northern Sierra Leone, including the Little Scarcies River area in east Bombali District and northeast Kambia District, as well as north of Makeni. The sources describe most Limba as Muslim, with Christianity influencing Limba in the southern province and a minority practising traditional religion in remote villages. Traditional beliefs include Kanu Masala, lesser deities, shrines, charms and rituals. Muslim Limba also practise aspects of their traditional religion.
