## Overview

The Venḓa, also called VhaVenḓa or Vhangona, are a Bantu people native to Southern Africa, living mostly near the South African-Zimbabwean border. In South Africa they are concentrated in the northern part of Limpopo province, especially in Makhado, Thulamela, Musina and Mutale local municipalities, while Venda speakers also live in Gauteng province and elsewhere in the country. The Venda language arose through interactions with Sotho-Tswana and Kalanga groups from 1400 and is related to Shona, including Karanga and Kalanga. According to the 2011 census, the Vhembe district had 844,000 Venda speakers, with 1.2 million in South Africa overall. Venda history is associated with Mapungubwe, Great Zimbabwe, the Soutpansberg and the Limpopo River, while museum collections document metalwork, pottery, wooden objects, musical instruments, adornment and domestic life.

### Architecture

Venda historical settlement is associated with the Soutpansberg and with royal kraals. The first Venda settlement in the Soutpansberg is described as that of the legendary chief Thoho-ya-Ndou, whose royal kraal was called D’zata. The remains of D’zata have been declared a National Monument. A museum photograph shows two women and two children within a decorated walled compound beside a thatched building at Tshakhuma in Limpopo Province. Another photograph shows a woman and two children standing by a low circular wall containing a palm tree. The sources do not describe the construction, plan or decoration of Venda houses in general.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Mapungubwe people mined and smelted copper, iron and gold, made glass and ceramics, spun cotton, grew millet and sorghum, and tended cattle, goats and sheep. The museum catalogue records earthenware bowls ornamented with red and burnished graphite, a gourd porridge bowl, a gourd vessel decorated with figures outlined in poker work and marked “MAKILIEBIN,” and a partially repaired gourd porridge bowl. It also records iron tongs, wooden spoons, a ladle, wooden sticks and staffs, copper ingots, a cylindrical copper currency bar called “lerale,” composite axes and composite spears.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The catalogue records a bronze wire and animal hair bracelet, a composite wristlet made from animal hairs with two groups of beads attached, and iron charms on a fibre string of glass beads. It also lists a head ornament consisting of a single row of ostrich-eggshell beads on a fibre string. The sources do not explain the occasions, symbolism or ritual functions of these objects. They also do not document tattoos, henna, hair practices or named forms of ceremonial adornment.

## Music & performance

The museum catalogue records several Venda musical instruments. These include a composite drum with a large wooden body tapering to a narrow foot and a hide membrane; a wooden drum with a hide membrane and a handle; a wood and hide drum with a hemispherical resonator carved from solid wood, four handles, a hole in the base and a rattle inside; and another composite drum with a wooden body, hide and hair membrane, vegetable fibre and a loop handle. The catalogue also records a composite xylophone made of wooden slats joined by strings and suspended on gourd resonators.

Photographs from Sibasa show two young girls playing drums held between their legs. The sources do not name the musical genres, songs, occasions or performance rules associated with these instruments. Mapungubwe is described as having sophisticated knowledge of the stars, with astronomy playing a major role in tradition, culture and day-to-day life, but the sources do not connect this statement to a named musical practice.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not describe an annual Venda festival calendar, seasonal celebrations, birth rites, weddings or funerals. They do document musangwe, a traditional Venda bare-knuckle fist-fighting practice. Musangwe developed for entertainment and also as a means of earning respect among peers. It provided a structured outlet for resolving conflicts in a culture that generally discouraged violence and fighting. When challenged, participants were expected to accept the fight or face consequences such as fines or physical punishment administered by community elders.

Matches have no predetermined time limit and end when one combatant concedes defeat. Village elders supervise the contests and enforce rules prohibiting actions such as biting or kicking. No medical personnel are present, gambling is forbidden, and participants compete for the honour of representing their village or family rather than for material gain. Victors receive prizes determined by the Khosi, meaning chief, or Vhamusanda, meaning headman.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral tradition holds King Shiriyadenga to have been the first king of Venda and Mapungubwe. The Venda are closely associated with the 13th century Kingdom of Mapungubwe, whose center had about 5,000 people. Mapungubwe lasted as a trade center between 1220 and 1300 AD and later declined around 1300, after which the population scattered as power moved north to the Great Zimbabwe Kingdom. The sources also identify Thoho-ya-Ndou as a legendary chief and translate his name as “Head of the Elephant.”

The sources do not provide Venda folktales, epics, proverbs, riddles, literary genres or contemporary preservation projects.

## Language & religion

Venḓa, or Tshivenḓa, is a Bantu language and an official language of South Africa and Zimbabwe. It is mainly spoken by the Venda people, or Vhavenḓa, in the northern part of South Africa’s Limpopo province and by some Lemba people in South Africa. The language is related to Shona, including Karanga and Kalanga. It uses the Latin alphabet with five additional accented letters: ḓ, ḽ, ṋ, ṱ and ṅ. The sources also state that Venda uses the sintu writing system Isibheqe Sohlamvu/Ditema tsa Dinoko, known technically in Venda as Luṱhofunḓeraru lwa Mibvumo.

The sources do not describe the current religious landscape, religious denominations, syncretism or named spiritual practices.
