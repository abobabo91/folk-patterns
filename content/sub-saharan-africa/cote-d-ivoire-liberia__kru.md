## Overview

The Kru are not a single tribe but a cluster of communities speaking related languages, settled along the Liberian and Ivorian coast from the eighteenth century onward. They based their livelihood on forest farming and hunting, but became widely known through seafaring: from the 1790s, Kru men crewed European merchant ships across West Africa, making them one of the most photographed coastal peoples of the nineteenth century. Their society was organized around small independent commonwealths led by hereditary chiefs, with real power held by councils of elders.

## Material culture

The Kru left few records of textiles and pottery, but their canoe paddles, drums, and stringed instruments survive as well-documented objects.

### Textile & pattern traditions

No Kru weaving or cloth-dyeing tradition is recorded in these sources.

### Clothing & dress

Coastal working dress was the loincloth with personal ornaments; formal dress for civic processions combined European jackets with wrapped cloths and sashes.

- **Loincloth** — The basic garment worn by nineteenth-century Kru men on the coast.
- **Iron leg rings** — Insignia of office worn by elders of the older Kru commonwealths to mark their rank.

### Architecture

Kru villages consisted of long rectangular buildings under thatched roofs set in forest clearings.

- **Rectangular thatched houses** — The standard building form documented in photographs of Kru settlements.
- **Little Kroo** — One of the first coastal Kru towns established from the interior in the eighteenth century.
- **Setra kroo** (*Welteh*) — A coastal Kru town whose name in Klao language survives in European records as Setra Kroo.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The canoe paddle is the defining Kru object, consistently carved and painted black for ocean-going trade canoes.

- **Canoe paddle** — A single-piece carved wood paddle with rounded handle and flat blade painted black, used to paddle out to meet merchant ships.
- **Metal knife** — Recorded in collections with a wood-and-hide sheath.
- **Wooden bell** — A hollowed rectangular object with braided fibre cord threaded through two carved knobs.
- **Brass currency piece** — A nineteenth-century object cast in brass over a fired terracotta core, held at the Metropolitan Museum.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Kru body marking centered on forehead and nose tattooing with indigo, a deliberate mark of freedom and status.

- **Indigo forehead and nose tattooing** — A mark applied by the Kru to distinguish themselves as freemen and prevent confusion with enslaved people.
- **Iron leg rings** — Metalwork insignia worn by elders to mark their rank and authority.

## Music & performance

Three types of instruments survive in collections: drums, a notched-wood stringed instrument, and harps built on gourd resonators.

- **Hour-glass drum** — Carved wood with skin stretched over each end, held by fibre strings, its waist painted with blue zigzags and orange dots.
- **Notched-wood stringed instrument** — Two lengths of wood with burnt holes mounted on a calabash resonator, lashed together with vegetable fibre and strung with six strings.
- **Gourd harp** — Built on a gourd resonator with wood and vegetable fibre, one example incorporates blue, white and red cloth, another includes metal.

## Dance & theatre

One photograph documents a Kru masquerade performed on a bamboo structure with stilt masqueraders.

- **Stilt masquerade** — A performance tradition with masqueraders on stilts seated on bamboo structures, watched by adults and children.

## Festivals & rituals

Kru political and social life centered on village ties reinforced by ceremonial exchanges, with power held by councils of elders.

- **Deya practitioners** (*deya*) — Specialists found in some Liberian areas who underwent long training to treat medical and spiritual crises with pharmaceutical and spiritual remedies.
- **Elder council governance** — The real authority in Kru commonwealths rested with councils of elders whose president held both religious and political office.
- **Sanctuary for the accused** — The elder president's house served as a sanctuary for an accused offender until guilt was proved.

## Foodways

The Kru lived by forest farming supplemented by hunting, and valued salt and Guinea pepper from their coastal location.

- **Forest farming** — The primary Kru livelihood based on cultivating the forest, with communal land rights that lapsed when a plot was left unfarmmed.
- **Salt trading** — Ancestral migration tradition records that the Kru learned the value of salt when descending the Poor River to the coast.
- **Guinea and melegueta pepper** — Pepper production and export were central to the Pepper Coast economy where the Kru settled.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kru origins are preserved in oral traditions tracing the people from an inland place called Claho, down the Poor River to the coast in the eighteenth century.

- **Claho origin place** (*Claho*) — The inland location from which Kru oral tradition says the people migrated one to one-and-a-half centuries before the mid-nineteenth century.
- **Poor River migration** — The Kru descended this river from the interior, founding Bassa and Little Kroo, joining with other Kru-speaking communities from further inland.
- **Kru proverbs** — A body of proverbs collected in the 1930s from Sie Tagbwe by anthropologist Melville J. Herskovits, preserving Kru wisdom literature.

## Language & religion

Kru languages are spoken from southeastern Liberia to western Ivory Coast with complex tone systems and high dialect counts, while the spiritual universe centers on the creator God Nyesoa alongside territorial and ancestral spirits.

- **Klao** — The Kru people's own name for themselves, Anglicized to 'Kru' in European records, not derived from the word 'crew'.
- **Nyesoa** — The high creator God at the center of Kru spiritual belief, described by missionaries.
- **Territorial and ancestral spirits** — Spirits attached to territories and familial guardians or souls of the departed who remain nearby and can influence events.
- **Kru language subgroups** — Include Kuwaa, Grebo, Bassa, Belle, Belleh, Kwaa, and Neyo, each village historically an independent state with limited intercommunication.

## Glossary

- *Klao* — The Kru people's own name for themselves
- *Nyesoa* — High creator God in Kru belief
- *deya* — Kru practitioners trained in medical and spiritual healing
- *Claho* — Ancestral inland place from which Kru migrated
- *Poor River* — River down which Kru descended to the coast
- *Welteh* — Klao name for the coastal town of Setra Kroo
- *Little Kroo* — One of the first coastal Kru towns established from the interior
- *Pepper Coast* — The coastal region known for Guinea and melegueta pepper production
- *Kuwaa* — One of the Kru language subgroups
- *Grebo* — One of the Kru language subgroups
- *Bassa* — One of the Kru language subgroups and a coastal Kru town
- *Belle* — One of the Kru language subgroups
- *Kwaa* — One of the Kru language subgroups
- *Neyo* — One of the Kru language subgroups
- *Sie Tagbwe* — Kru elder from whom Melville Herskovits collected proverbs in the 1930s
