## At a glance

| Who | The Karamojong, also called Karimojong, are a Nilotic ethnic group. |
| Where | They live mainly in north-east Uganda, in southern Karamoja. |
| How many | About 475,000 people. |
| Language | Ngakarimojong, a Nilotic language in the Nilo-Saharan family. |
| Religion | Traditional beliefs are reflected in the language; the current religious landscape is not described. |
| Known for | Livestock herding · Pastoral transhumance · Wrestling linked with marriage · Traditional dance · The social importance of cattle |

## Overview

The Karamojong are agro-pastoral herders whose main livelihood is keeping livestock. Crop growing is secondary and possible only in suitable areas. Their culture includes age-based social organization, seasonal movement with animals, wrestling linked to marriage, and traditional dance. Cattle have strong social and cultural importance.

## Material culture

Karamojong material culture includes hide clothing, beadwork, metal tools, weapons, gourds, wooden vessels, and food-storage containers.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The profile records beadwork, hide work, cowrie shells, and fibre decoration, but no broad named textile tradition.

- **Beaded women’s aprons** — These aprons use glass beads arranged in patterns on hide backed with hair.
- **Cowrie-shell girdles** — Women’s girdles were made with cowrie shells, hide, ostrich eggshell discs, and glass beads.
- **Married woman’s tail-piece** — This tail is made of vegetable fibre and decorated with pink, white, and blue glass beads.
- **Hide arm ornaments** — These ornaments use five plaited hide strips, tassels, and sometimes coils of aluminium wire.

### Clothing & dress

Women’s dress includes hide front pieces, beaded aprons, girdles, tail-pieces, and open sandals, while headgear is recorded for both men and women.

- **Married woman’s front piece** — This front piece is made of hide and was worn as part of a married woman’s dress.
- **Unmarried woman’s front piece** — This smaller front piece could be decorated with cowrie shells and beads.
- **Buffalo-hide sandals** — Open sandals were made from buffalo hide.
- **Composite feather headdress** — This headdress uses black ostrich feathers mounted on cow hide with a fibre chin strap.
- **Hair-and-clay chignon** (*Chignon*) — This head-dress is made of human hair and clay and painted brown, blue, and grey.

### Architecture

The sources do not describe Karamojong houses, settlements, fences, roofs, or other architecture.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Everyday objects include metal tools and weapons, clay pots, gourds, wooden vessels, and containers for storing food.

- **Club** (*rungu*) — Clubs are called rungu.
- **Finger-knife** — A finger-knife has a leather guard.
- **Leaf-headed arrows** — These arrows have leaf-shaped iron heads.
- **Clay pots** — Clay pots without ears were recorded from Kangole.
- **Food-storage vessels** — Cylindrical wooden or other vessels, called drums in some records, were used to store food.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Body adornment uses hide, cowrie shells, beads, metal, hair, clay, and stone.

- **White-stone lip ornament** — A man’s lip ornament was made from white stone.
- **Spiral child’s bracelet** — A child’s bracelet consists of aluminium and copper spirals on a metal core.
- **Iron neck ornament** — A woman’s neck ornament is made of iron with rolled ends.
- **Black-bead waist ornament** — This waist ornament consists of five loops made mostly from black glass beads.
- **Painted hair ornament** — A frontal head ornament was made from felted human hair and clay and then painted.

## Music & performance

The sources do not name Karamojong musical genres or instruments, though they record a dance bell and objects called drums that were used for food storage.

- **Dance bell** — A girl wore a bell below the knee during dance.

## Dance & theatre

Traditional Karamojong dance is described as remarkable and enduring, but no particular dance or theatre form is named.

- **Traditional dance** — The sources describe Karamojong traditional dance as remarkable and lasting over time.

## Festivals & rituals

The profile describes seasonal livestock movement, marriage wrestling, and an offering against illness and accidents, but no annual festival calendar.

- **Pastoral transhumance** — For three to four months each year, people move their animals to nearby districts for water and pasture.
- **Marriage wrestling** — A young man must wrestle the woman he wants to marry, and winning allows him to marry her and begin dowry talks.
- **Marriage wrestling for outsiders** — A non-Karamojong man who wants to marry a Karamojong woman must also undergo the wrestling ceremony.
- **Illness-and-accident offering** — A goat-hide strap was worn within a family during an offering against illness and accidents.

## Foodways

Foodways center on cattle, milk, water, and stored food, while crop growing is secondary.

- **Milk vessel** — A milk vessel from Nabilutuk is recorded in a museum collection.
- **Calabash bowl** — Calabash bowls were among the recorded household vessels.
- **Gourd water vessel** — Water vessels were made from gourds and carried with leather straps.
- **Stored-food vessel** — Several wooden or other vessels were used to store food.
- **Water** (*ŋakipi*) — The Ngakarimojong word for water is ŋakipi.

## Oral tradition & literature

Migration stories explain Karamojong origins and the meaning of their name, while the sources also record greetings and proverbs.

- **Abyssinian migration tradition** — One tradition says the group migrated from Abyssinia between 1600 and 1700 AD before splitting into several peoples.
- **Ethiopian migration tradition** — Another account places the wider migration from present-day Ethiopia around 1600.
- **Name-origin phrase** (*ekar ngimojong*) — The name Karamojong is linked to ekar ngimojong, explained as “the old men can walk no farther.”
- **Greeting** (*Ejoka?*) — Ejoka? is a recorded greeting.
- **Greeting response** (*Ejok-nooi*) — Ejok-nooi is the recorded response to the greeting.

## Language & religion

Ngakarimojong is a written Nilotic language with several tones, related languages, and vocabulary that reflects traditional beliefs.

- **Ngakarimojong** — Ngakarimojong is spoken by the Karamojong in north-east Uganda.
- **Alternative spelling** (*ŋaKarimojoŋ*) — ŋaKarimojoŋ is one Karamojong spelling of the language name.
- **God** (*Akuj*) — Akuj is the word for God, and its root also means “north” and “up.”
- **Roman alphabet** — Written Ngakarimojong uses the Roman alphabet.
- **Language tones** — The language has three basic tones and three contour tones.
