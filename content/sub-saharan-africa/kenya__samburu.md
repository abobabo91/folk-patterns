## Overview

The Samburu traditionally herd cattle, sheep, goats, and camels. They speak a dialect of Maa closely related to Camus and South Maasai. Their culture is known for beadwork, distinctive clothing, age grades, divination, and livestock-based life. Museum collections include weapons, milk containers, ornaments, clothing, tools, and game equipment.

## Material culture

Samburu material culture combines cloth, hide, beads, wood, leather, metal, fibre, shells, feathers, and animal hair.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Samburu clothing uses wrapped cloth, hide, beads, and strong animal or floral patterns.

- **Beadwork** — Coloured beads are sewn onto clothing and used for necklaces, bracelets, headbands, aprons, and other ornaments.
- **Hide apron** — A hide apron is trimmed with coloured beads and decorated with cowry shells, a plastic button, and small groups of beads.
- **Wrapped cloth** (*kikoi*) — Men wear cloth around the waist, while women wear cloth around the waist and over the chest; contemporary examples include red tartan and dark green or blue plaid called kikoi.
- **Moran attire** (*moran*) — Warriors may wear cloth with floral or pastel designs and traditionally wear their hair in long braids.
- **Animal and floral patterns** — Animal and floral patterns appear on contemporary women’s cloths.

### Clothing & dress

Samburu dress is marked by wrapped cloth, bead ornaments, shaved or braided hair, and red ochre.

- **Men’s waist cloth** — Men traditionally wear a cloth around the waist, often pink or black.
- **Women’s wrapped cloths** — Women traditionally wear one blue or purple cloth around the waist and another over the chest.
- **Long warrior braids** (*moran*) — Many moran wear long braids and shave them off when they become elders.
- **Red ochre body decoration** — Hair and bodies may be coloured or decorated with red ochre.
- **Plaid skirts** — Plaid skirts have become common in women’s clothing since the 2000s.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Samburu collections include weapons, tools, a game board, and containers made from wood, hide, leather, metal, fibre, and feathers.

- **Painted quiver** — A wooden quiver is painted pale pink and has a leather strap and leather lid.
- **Wooden spear** — A three-piece wooden spear has a metal fitting and a leaf-shaped spearhead.
- **Arrows** — Wooden arrows have carved notches, sewn feathers, fibre bindings, and metal arrowheads.
- **Mancala board** — A long wooden mancala board has two rows of raised cups and comes with sixty-nine pebbles for playing.
- **Knives and tools** — Knives and a small tool have wooden handles and iron blades.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Samburu adornment uses beads, hide, elephant hair, brass, wire, shells, bones, and other materials.

- **Fertility necklace** — A woman’s fertility necklace is made from beads, hide, and elephant hair.
- **Elephant-hair headband** — A headband uses differently coloured beads threaded on elephant hair and fastened with hide and small bones.
- **Elephant-toenail bracelet** — A bracelet is made from an elephant toenail with a wire fastener.
- **Brass earrings** — Some earrings are made from coiled brass and suspended by vegetable fibre.
- **Decorated wooden doll** — A female wooden doll is decorated with hide, glass beads, copper, and elephant hair.

## Music & performance

The profile does not document Samburu music but records commercial and popular-culture portrayals.

- **MasterCard commercial** — A MasterCard commercial shows Samburu people dancing.
- **Nike commercial phrase** — A Nike commercial incorrectly translated a Samburu man’s words as “Just Do It”; the source gives the words as “Mayieu kuna. Ijooki inamuk sapukin,” meaning “I don’t want these. Give me big shoes.”
- **Film portrayals** — A 1953 film used Samburu as background for its stars, and later films used Samburu extras to portray Maasai.

## Dance & theatre

No named Samburu dance or theatre form is described, and popular portrayals may confuse Samburu with Maasai.

- **Commercial dancing** — Dancing Samburu appeared in a MasterCard commercial.
- **Popular portrayals** — Samburu have appeared in films, television commercials, journalism, and a reality television series.

## Festivals & rituals

Samburu social life is shaped by elders, age grades, circumcision, marriage authority, and ritual responses to Nkai.

- **Elder authority** — Samburu society is described as a gerontocracy in which elders arrange marriages and may take further wives.
- **Warrior age grade** (*moran*) — Younger unmarried men can remain socially suspended until about age thirty, while moran are identified as warriors.
- **Teenage circumcision** — Boys are circumcised during their teenage years.
- **Beading** — Girls who have not undergone female circumcision are described as at risk of rape through a practice called beading and as not being allowed to have children.
- **Elder’s curse and blessing** (*Nkai*) — A person facing misfortune after disrespecting an older man should offer reparation to the senior person in return for a blessing connected with Nkai.

## Foodways

Samburu foodways center on livestock, especially milk, blood, and small-stock meat, alongside purchased maize meal, tea, and sugar.

- **Fresh or fermented milk** — Milk may be drunk fresh or fermented, and ripened milk is often considered better.
- **Ceremonial beef** — Cattle meat is eaten mainly during ceremonies or when a cow dies.
- **Small-stock meat** — Goat and sheep meat is eaten more often than cattle meat, though not regularly.
- **Maize porridge** — Purchased maize meal is commonly made into porridge.
- **Blood meals** — Blood is taken from living or slaughtered animals, and the source says it can be prepared in at least thirteen ways and form a whole meal.

## Oral tradition & literature

The profile gives no named Samburu literary genres but records a Nandi story linked to cultural transfer.

- **Kipkenyo tradition** (*Kipkenyo*) — A Nandi tradition says Kipkenyo was the first man to practise circumcision in Nandi and came from a country called Do, or To in other versions.
- **Circumcision origin story** (*Do*) — The story says Kipkenyo circumcised his children after their siblings died at puberty, and the Nandi followed his example.

## Language & religion

Samburu speak a Maa dialect and traditionally follow Nkai, while many are now Christian.

- **Samburu dialect** (*Maa*) — Samburu is a Maa dialect spoken by pastoralists in northern Kenya and closely related to Camus and South Maasai dialects.
- **Samburu self-name** (*Lokop*) — Samburu refer to themselves as Lokop or Loikop, though the meaning is interpreted in different ways.
- **Leather-bag name** (*saamburr*) — The word Samburu may come from the Maa word saamburr, described as a leather bag used by the Samburu.
- **Nkai** — Nkai is a multi-faceted divinity regarded as active in contemporary life and as a source of protection.
- **Diviners** (*loibonok*) — Ritual diviners or shamans called loibonok identify causes of illness and misfortune and guide warriors.

## Glossary

- *Lokop* — A Samburu self-name
- *Loikop* — Another Samburu self-name
- *Maa* — The language family and dialect group
- *Camus* — A closely related Maa dialect
- *kikoi* — Dark green or blue plaid cloth
- *moran* — A warrior age grade
- *Nkai* — The traditional divinity
- *loibonok* — Ritual diviners or shamans
- *saamburr* — A leather bag; possible source of Samburu
- *Kipkenyo* — A figure in a Nandi circumcision tradition
- *Do* — The country in the Kipkenyo story
- *To* — Another name for that country
