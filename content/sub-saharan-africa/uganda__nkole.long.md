## Overview

The Nkole, also called the Banyankole, are a Bantu people of south-western Uganda, native to the Ankole region that was historically the Ankole Kingdom. Their language, Nkore — also written Nkole, Nyankore, Orunyankore, Runyankore and Runyankole — is spoken across the Mbarara, Bushenyi, Ntungamo, Kiruhura, Ibanda, Isingiro, Rukungiri, Buhweju, Mitooma, Rwampara, Sheema and Rubirizi districts and parts of Kitagwenda, as well as by speakers in Tanzania, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Rwanda and Burundi. They are closely related to the Nyoro, Kiga, Toro and Hema. The sources used here describe the Banyankole above all as cattle-keepers, and the objects this atlas shows for them — milk vessels, coiled basketry, quivers and bows — follow directly from that. The sources give no population figure.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources describe no woven-cloth tradition for the Nkole. What they do document at length is coiled basketry, which is where the group's surface pattern lives, so the entries below are basketry rather than textile.

**Coiled grass basketry** — Baskets, bowls, plates, trays and covers are coiled from grass or leaf. A circular basket with a flat base carries black patterns woven into the body; a lidded bowl is buff-coloured with black zig-zags; a tray is coiled "like a beehive". The colour scheme running through the group is a pale ground with dark figures worked in.

**Conical coiled covers** — Several covers and plates in the collection are conical coiled work in grass or leaf, patterned in black against pale. The same coiled technique produces the very fine lid that caps a wooden milk vessel, decorated with two "christmas tree" patterns in blue and black, and the coiled basketry stopper of a beaded gourd bottle.

**Coloured banded coil-work** — A bottle and its lid are coiled grass-work coloured red, black and yellow in horizontal bands, showing that the coiling was also dyed and organised into registers rather than being left in the natural colour.

**Plaited grass matting** — A composite mat is made of grass strands joined by transverse bands of black grass, given a pile effect by being plaited over locally made string. A second mat, red and yellow with bands of cane embroidered decoration in dark grey, has three fibre cords at the top so it can be hung or tied on.

**Motif vocabulary.** Zig-zags; concentric circles (on a gourd container, in black); incised geometric patterns (on bamboo quivers); low-relief serrated bands (carved on a bamboo quiver); linear designs separated by groups of dots (around the neck of a milk vessel); "christmas tree" figures (on a basketry lid).

### Clothing & dress

The sources say almost nothing about Nkole dress. The one item in the collection that touches it is a mat catalogued jointly as a head-dress: red and yellow, with bands of cane embroidered decoration in dark grey and three fibre cords at the top for attachment. A second composite grass mat is catalogued the same way, as mat and head-dress together. Beyond these, the museum records and the Wikipedia articles used here describe no everyday or ceremonial garments, no distinction between men's and women's dress, and no footwear or belts, so nothing further can be said without going outside the sources.

### Architecture

Neither the Wikipedia articles nor the museum catalogue records used here describe Nkole house forms, building materials, roofing or architectural decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Ironworking and woodworking dominate the object set. Weapons include a socketed iron spear blade on a hard wooden shaft with a short iron butt, a wooden bow strung with sinew cord, a dagger with an iron blade and wooden handle in its own sheath, and a knife with a curved iron blade. A bill-hook or sickle, again curved iron on a wooden handle, is the agricultural counterpart. Arrows are carried in quivers of bamboo, wood or hide, closed with hide or removable wooden caps; the wooden ones are carved and painted in black. Milk vessels are the signature form: one is conical, with a broad circular base, tapering neck and narrow aperture; another is a composite wooden milkpot with a groove below the neck and iron and copper symbols stapled onto it. Smoking pipes pair a pottery bowl with a wooden stem. A cylindrical needle-case of soft pale wood, engraved and blackened, holds two iron needles. Gourds serve as bottles and containers.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Glass beadwork and cowrie shells are the adornment materials the sources record, and both appear on objects rather than on the body. A fly-whisk of fibre and hair has its fibre handle covered in bands of turquoise-blue, red, pink, white and black glass beads, with cowries at the top, and a string of ten cowries, a metal bell and two wooden charms at the base. A gourd bottle is entirely covered in a geometric design of dark blue, pink and white beads. One amulet in the collection is made of horn. The sources describe no tattooing, no hair practices and no personal jewelry types.

## Music & performance

The only instrument in the sources is a musical horn: pale green, spiral, hollow, with an oval hole near the tip. A metal bell hangs from the handle of the beaded fly-whisk, though the record does not call it an instrument. The sources name no song genres, no ensembles and no performance occasions, and Uganda has no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions to draw on. The Wikipedia article on the Nkore language does point to *Ebyevugo*, named there as a Runyankore poetry form, but says nothing about how it is performed.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used here document no Nkole dances or dramatic traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used here describe no annual festivals and no birth, initiation, marriage or funeral rites. The nearest they come to ritual material is the fly-whisk, whose handle carries cowries, a metal bell and two wooden charms, and the single amulet made of horn — objects of a kind associated with ritual use, though the catalogue records do not say how either was used.

## Foodways

Cattle-keeping is the practice the sources most consistently attach to the Banyankole, and the object set supports it: two vessels in the collection are specifically for milk, one conical in wood with a narrow aperture and a finely coiled lid, the other a composite wooden milkpot with iron and copper symbols stapled to it. The Runyankore vocabulary recorded on Wikipedia gives the everyday foods by name: *ebitookye*, bananas or matooke; *ebihimba*, beans; *enyama*, meat; *oburo*, millet bread; *ebitakuri*, potatoes; and *ebicoori*, maize. Maize meal or corn bread is also listed. The sources give no recipes, no cooking methods, no ceremonial dishes and no drink traditions.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Nkore language article names *Ebyevugo* as a Runyankore poetry form, but gives no description of it, no named works and no performance context. The greeting formulas recorded for the language are the one verbal convention the sources set out in full: *Agandi*, literally "other news!" and used for "How are you?", answered with *Ni marungi*, "good news!"; and the fuller *Oraire ota?* and *Osiibire ota?*, "How was your night?" and "How was your day?", with *Oraare gye* for "good night" and *Osiibe gye* for "good day". Address terms include *Nyabo* for a woman and *Sebo* for a man. Beyond these the sources record no folktales, epics, proverbs or riddles.

## Language & religion

Nkore is a Bantu language, written variously as Nkole, Nyankore, Nyankole, Orunyankore, Orunyankole, Runyankore and Runyankole. It has a five-vowel system, with /i, u/ heard as [ɪ, ʊ] when short or lax. It is close enough to Kiga — 84 to 94 percent lexical similarity — that some treat the two as dialects of one language, which Charles V. Taylor called Nkore-Kiga; they share an alphabet with minor orthographic differences. Taylor wrote a brief description and teaching guide in the 1950s, and an adequate dictionary is in print. Most speakers in the region also use English, one of Uganda's two official languages and the language of schooling. The Banyankole are predominantly Christian: the 2002 Census of Uganda records 52.6% Anglican, 34.8% Roman Catholic, 5.2% Muslim and 4.6% Pentecostal.
