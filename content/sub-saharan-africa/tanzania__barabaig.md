## Overview

The Barabaig, also called Wabarabaig in Swahili, are a nomadic tribe of the Datooga people living in the northern volcanic highlands near Mount Hanang, in Manyara Region of Tanzania. They speak the eponymous dialect of the Datooga language, one of the Nilotic languages. Their population is reported as about 50,000, although the number is difficult to establish because the Tanzania census does not record ethnicity. Barabaig life is organized around cattle, grazing rotation, clan membership, councils, ritual authority, and oral judgement. Their pastoral culture is also connected with farming, hunting, burial ceremonies, animal sacrifice, and the use of sticks as weapons and percussion instruments at dances.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

Cattle provide skins for clothing, while cattle horns are used as drinking vessels. In the burial ritual called *bung'ed*, an esteemed elder is buried naked, seated and facing east, beneath a mound built over the corpse.

### Architecture

They state only that cattle dung is used for building and that Barabaig homesteads have gardens where vegetables are grown.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They state that sticks are important as weapons and percussion instruments, that cattle horns serve as drinking vessels, and that donkeys are used as beasts of burden. Cattle dung is used for building and urine as a cleanser.

## Music & performance

Sticks have an important place in Barabaig culture as both weapons and percussion instruments at dances. The name Barabaig is explained in the source as meaning “Beaters of Sticks,” from *bar*, “to beat,” and *baig*, “sticks.”

Performance is connected with social recognition and displays of authority. A man who hunts a *halot*, or enemy, using only a spear can be considered a *ghadyirochand*, or hero, and may be rewarded with cattle, women, and prestige. The burial ceremony called *bung'ed* lasts nine months and is associated with a mound and ritual observances for an esteemed elder.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention dances in which sticks function as percussion instruments. The sources also do not distinguish ceremonial and recreational dance forms.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe several important rituals and institutions.

The Barabaig distinguish esteemed elders through *bung'ed*, the name both for a burial mound and for the nine-month ceremony accompanying it. Before the ceremony is granted, the elder’s clan discusses whether the person lived a moral life, had many wives and children, possessed many cattle, commanded authority through oratory, performed brave deeds, and showed wise judgement. The elder is then buried naked, seated, and facing east. The mound becomes sacred, carries the deceased’s name, and is maintained by the clan in perpetuity.

Sheep have an important role as sacrifices in rituals. Cattle also function in inheritance, gifts, loans, payments, fines, and sacrifice.

Social regulation is conducted through councils and jural moots. *Gitabaraku* is the public assembly of all Barabaig and deals with community-wide issues. *Girgwageda Dosht* handles clan matters, *Girgwageda Gisjeuda* addresses neighbourhood issues, and *Girgwageda Gademg* allows women to adjudicate offences by men against women. Serious offences are handled in camera, with sanctions imposed by a *Makchamed* made up of selected senior elders.

## Foodways

Cattle are central to Barabaig subsistence. They provide milk, meat, and occasionally blood for food. Goats are traded and slaughtered for eating, while sheep are important in ritual sacrifice. The sources state that Barabaig keep chickens but do not eat eggs.

Barabaig traditionally did not grow crops, but they now cultivate plots of maize, sorghum, and beans, as well as vegetables in gardens near their homesteads. Most of what is produced is consumed by the household that grows it. Cattle are generally kept for milk rather than meat, and male stock is sold to obtain other necessities. Female cattle are retained because selling them would reduce the breeding herd and threaten milk production. Cattle are also traded through sale or barter to obtain other goods needed by the household.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do state that authority in the burial qualification process may be demonstrated through oratory and that clan affairs are conducted through councils.

The sources also preserve several names and explanations connected with Barabaig history and social organization. The group name is associated with beating sticks, and the people are divided into groups called *emojiga*. Clans are called *dosht*; spiritual clans are *daremng'ajega*, while secular clans are *homatk*. These terms are presented in the source as part of social organization rather than as a documented literary tradition.

## Language & religion

The Barabaig speak the eponymous dialect of the Datooga language. The Barabaig are described as one of the Nilotic peoples. The source states that the Datooga-speaking population forms the largest group among the Tatoga-speaking people.

Barabaig religion is described as traditional animist beliefs and practices, as with all Datooga. Sheep are used as sacrifices in rituals, and burial mounds for esteemed elders become sacred places maintained by the clan. The sources also state that fundamentalist U.S. evangelicals have listed the Barabaig and Datooga as “unreached peoples.”
