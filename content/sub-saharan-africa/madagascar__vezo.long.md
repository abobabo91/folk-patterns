## Overview

The Vezo are the semi-nomadic coastal people of southwestern Madagascar who define themselves by living from sea fishing rather than by descent. They occupy most of the littoral zone along Madagascar's west coast between Toliara and Mahajanga, with a coastal belt running from Intampolo in the south to Morondava in the north; Andavadoaka, a village of some 1,200 people, is one of their settlements. Their population is hard to count because of their marine migrations, and has been estimated by counting their dugout canoes, the *pirogues* (*lakana* in Malagasy). They speak a dialect of Malagasy, a Malayo-Polynesian language derived from the Barito languages of southern Borneo. In folk-culture terms they matter because identity here is a learned bodily skill — fishing, swimming, and the calluses these produce — not a shared origin, and because their funerary sculpture is internationally known.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used do not describe Vezo weaving or patterned textile traditions. What they do record of worked fibre is utilitarian and tied to the sea and the household: a **hat** of raffia, a **carrying basket** of basketry, and a canoe **sail** assembled from rope, cotton cloth, raffia and polythene — the last a plain illustration of new materials entering an old form.

### Clothing & dress

The sources used do not cover Vezo dress in any detail. The only garments and body-worn items they record are museum pieces: a raffia hat, and a sandal made from zebu hide and goat skin fastened with nails.

### Architecture

The sources used describe Vezo building only indirectly. A British Museum piece is a wooden **model of an ancestor's house**, which points to the house as a form worth reproducing in miniature and to the continuing presence of the dead in Vezo settlement life. Houses themselves figure in the sources mainly as occasions for addressing the dead: moving into a newly built house is one of the events at which the senior head of a family must call the ancestors and inform them, alongside launching a new canoe or leaving for a temporary fishing camp. Fishermen use mangroves for timber and wood. Away from the villages, families who fish far offshore set up camp on sandbars, sometimes for six to nine months of the year, bringing their families and all their possessions. Village public building is represented by the one school building at Andavadoaka, with two designated teachers.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The recorded object set is dominated by the working equipment of an artisanal fishery. The canoe is the central artefact: the *lakana* or *pirogue* is made by hollowing out a large log and is driven by sail and paddle, and the museum holds a model outrigger canoe of wood with nylon thread and rubber, a wooden **paddle**, and a wooden **bailer**. Three **fish spears** are made of wood and iron. Men fish predominantly with line, nets and spears. Household goods include a clay **water vessel** and a pottery **pot**. One further carved piece is a **heron figure** made of zebu horn.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ornament as recorded is chiefly metal and worn on the arm: five **bracelets**, three of copper — identified variously as a woman's, a man's and a probable man's — and two of aluminium. Alongside these is a **necklace** strung on cotton thread from the seeds of the *harea* tree. The sources also note a form of adornment that is not worn at all but grown: the calluses produced by fishing and swimming, which the Vezo treat as the embodied evidence of who they are. No tattooing, henna or hairdressing practice appears in the sources used.

## Music & performance

The sources used do not document Vezo instruments, song genres or musical performance contexts. What they record of collective performance falls under ceremony: the Vezo hold official cultural ceremonies called *Fomba*, comprising **Bilo**, **Tromba**, **Savatse**, **Takasy** and **Soro**, all of which except Takasy are also practised, with variations, by the neighbouring inland Masikoro. The sources name these ceremonies without describing their music.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used contain no account of Vezo dance or dramatic traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

Ritual life is organised around the five *Fomba* ceremonies — **Bilo**, **Tromba**, **Savatse**, **Takasy** and **Soro** — and around keeping the dead informed. The circumcision ceremony runs from 4 am until 9 am. The parents ask a wise elder to suggest the best date and time and identify a nurse or doctor who can perform the cut; family members are invited, and one of the child's uncles holds him during the ceremony. Drinking follows the cut, and afterwards the child is called *savatse*.

Funerary practice is emphatic. Vezo cemeteries lie in the forest far from the villages, so well concealed by vegetation that they are considered invisible to the eye; they are hidden because the sight of tombs makes people sad, and the living approach a cemetery only to carry a corpse or to "work" for the dead, digging graves and building tombs. Sakalava and Vezo funerary sculpture is internationally renowned for erotic wooden figures, often shown in copulation with oversized phalluses and breasts; why the sculpture takes this form is unknown, though it may relate to tourist demand.

The dead must be told promptly of events in a family's productive, reproductive and social life: moving to a temporary fishing place, entering a new house, launching a new canoe, a difficult birth, a newborn's first outing, a school exam, angry words, the arrival or departure of a visiting anthropologist. The senior head of the family calls and speaks to them, asking protection or forgiveness — because whenever the dead have cause to be surprised, they ask questions and cause trouble for the living.

Life is also governed by numerous *fady* (taboos). Men risk *hanimboky*, a disease affecting only men whose name means "swollen/full with food" and which is treated as a male pregnancy: the sufferer is constipated and his stomach swells until it resembles a pregnant woman's. It is caused by a man eating food accepted from a sexually active female kinswoman, because that food is assumed to have been bought with *tangy*, the presents her lover gives her for sex — so accepting it places the man in the position of a woman before his sister's or daughter's lover.

## Foodways

The staple food is rice, which women buy with the money they earn selling the catch the men bring them, along with other essential foodstuffs and a variety of luxury items. The sea supplies the rest: men fish with line, nets and spears, mostly 5 km or less from shore, while women glean the reef flats for invertebrates including octopus and sea cucumbers. Sharks and sea cucumbers are taken on long offshore trips, both in high demand in Asian seafood markets. Turtles have long been exploited for subsistence with associated cultural traditions, hunted for meat and carapaces and their nests raided; they have been protected by local law under Decree 24 of 1923, but the law has seldom been enforced. Trade supplies what the sea does not: the Vezo traditionally traded with the agro-forester Masikoro, and exchange fish with the Mikea for honey and tubers, though increased trade has shifted the economy from barter to cash. Alcohol is drunk after a circumcision. The sources used name no prepared dish.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used record no folktales, epic or proverb collection. They do describe a standing channel of communication with the ancestors that functions as speech: the senior family head calls the dead aloud and talks to them, and the dead answer through dreams. At death the breathing stops and the body stiffens, cools and decomposes, while the *fanahy* — the spirit as it is known up to the moment of death — departs permanently. In its disembodied form it is called *angatse*, is invisible, and moves about like wind; to be seen it enters the dreams of the living, where it appears with its original uncorrupted body as it was in life.

## Language & religion

The Vezo speak a dialect of the Malagasy language, a branch of the Malayo-Polynesian group derived from the Barito languages of southern Borneo. Like other Malagasy peoples their origins lie in a mix of Austronesian settlers from Asia and Bantu migrants from mainland East Africa, with Arab-Persian and Indian ancestry added since the Middle Ages. They do not identify with any particular Malagasy ethnic group but with a way of life, and state emphatically that they need have no common origin or shared essence with one another. The sources used name no religious affiliation; the practices they record are ancestral — the *Fomba* ceremonies, the obligation to inform and address the dead, and the *fady* that regulate daily conduct.
