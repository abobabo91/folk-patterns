## Overview

The Mumuye are a people of Taraba State, Nigeria, also found in many parts of neighboring Adamawa State. They are associated with the Benue River Valley and predominate in the local government areas of Zing, Yorro, Jalingo, Ardo-Kola, Lau, Gassol, Bali and Gashaka. The sources do not provide a population number. Mumuye is the most populous of the Adamawa languages and belongs to the Leko–Nimbari branch of Savanna languages. Mumuye communities are divided into smaller, independent groups that speak related dialects and share cultural traditions. Their folk-cultural importance in the sources centers on figurative sculpture, masquerades, scarification, rainmaking, divination and the social organization of village groups called *dola*.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Langtang* — The museum catalogue records a textile made of handspun cotton. It has narrow warp-faced borders along both long sides, alternating lines of natural-coloured and brown-dyed cotton, a loosely woven net-like central section, warp and weft stripes, and long twisted cotton tassels at both ends.

The sources used do not document additional Mumuye textile traditions or establish distinctive differences from neighboring cultures.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name warp and weft stripes, alternating lines, borders and tassels as textile features, but do not identify a broader Mumuye motif vocabulary.

### Clothing & dress

The museum catalogue records men’s aprons made of hide, including an apron with fringe. It also records a handspun cotton textile identified as *langtang*. The sources do not describe everyday clothing, women’s dress, footwear, belts, head coverings or a complete ceremonial costume. They do state that Mumuye women undergo scarification before marriage and that scarification appears in Mumuye sculpture. A British Museum record also describes a wooden male figure wearing a crested hat and a necklace of beads, but the catalogue does not identify this as a form of ordinary or ceremonial dress.

### Architecture

The sources do not describe Mumuye house forms, villages’ construction materials, roofs, compounds, workshops or architectural decoration. They state that people currently live in small villages consisting of extended families, and that these social groups are called *dola*. The sources also state that Iagalagana figures are kept in a separate hut, but provide no further description of that building.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum catalogue records clubs made of wood and leather or wood and vegetable fibre, including clubs with elaborate or curved heads. It also records a composite rattle made from four sticks with gourds, joined with string or fur and fitted with a bast cord for tying on the leg. Other objects include a boar’s tusk ornament cased in leather and wooden rods bound with copper, iron and aluminium wire. The sources describe Iagalagana figures as carved by blacksmiths or weavers, but do not document pottery or named ceramic forms.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The museum catalogue records a boar’s tusk ornament with a leather casing and attachment loop, and a composite ornament of two wooden rods bound with copper, iron and aluminium wire, worn by men. It also records a braided fibre necklace and a necklace of beads on wooden figures. Mumuye sculpture is associated with large ear lobes, linked in the sources to the tradition of inserting discs into the ears. The sources state that Mumuye women undergo scarification before marriage; other scarification consists of three horizontal rows of cuts dividing the face into thirds. The sources do not describe hair practices, tattoos, henna or additional named jewelry types.

## Music & performance

The sources do not document Mumuye musical instruments, song genres or musical repertories. They do record a composite rattle made of four sticks with gourds, with a bast cord for tying it on the leg, but do not state how it was performed or in which musical context. Performance is discussed mainly through masquerades and ritual objects. The Vabon masquerade is a funeral masquerade that encompasses the whole community. The Mumuye also have the Ushavuko ceremony, which occurs twice a year. Within the secret society called the Vabong, initiation includes an explanation of masks and other objects used for divination. Sukwava masks were traditionally worn during ceremonies before war and are currently used for healing and rainmaking. Sukuru masks are sometimes worn for rainmaking rites. The sources do not identify dances, songs, instrumental ensembles or performance venues.

## Dance & theatre

The sources do not name Mumuye dances or dramatic genres. They describe masquerade performance, including the Vabon funeral masquerade, which encompasses the whole community. They also describe two types of masks: the Sukwava mask, with a long neck and large ears, and a face mask used for initiation, with hollowed-out eyes, animalistic features and a skeletal quality. The sources do not specify choreography, music, audience arrangements or distinctions between entertainment and ceremony beyond the ritual contexts named above.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do not provide an annual festival calendar or dates for festivals. They name the Ushavuko, a ceremony that occurs twice a year, and the Vabon, a funeral masquerade encompassing the whole community. Mumuye women undergo scarification before they are wed, and scarification is also represented in sculpture. The Vabong is a secret society with seven levels. Initiation into it is intense and often involves beating, as well as an explanation of masks and other objects used for divination. Rainmakers protect the community and are considered powerful in ensuring that the community thrives. Rainmaking rods are shaped like the letter Z and are associated with lightning, snakes, fertility of the land and fertility of the people. Sukuru masks may be worn during rainmaking rites, while Sukwava masks are used for healing and rainmaking. The Iagalagana is used for divination, bestowing good luck, warding off evil and making rain.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used do not document Mumuye folktales, epic poetry, proverbs, riddles, literary works or storytelling contexts. They state that Mumuye subgroups share cultural traditions despite their isolation, but do not describe those traditions as oral genres.

## Language & religion

Mumuye is spoken in northeastern Nigeria and is classified in the Leko–Nimbari branch of Savanna languages; Adamawa is no longer considered a valid family. The sources list dialects including Zinna, Rang, Dong, Yoro, Lankaviri, Gola, Gongla, Kasaa, Saawa, Jalingo, Nyaaja, Jeng, Gnoore, Yaa, Sagbee, Shaari, Kugong, Mang, Kwaji, Meeka and Yakoko. They also describe Zing, Monkin and Kpugbong groups, with further named dialects and settlements. Historically, the Mumuye did not have a central government; villages are governed by a council of elders and an elected leader. Spirituality is governed by the Vabong secret society. The Benue River Valley is described as having a spiritual attachment to the land. Iagalagana figures are used for divination, good luck, protection from evil and rainmaking, and are kept in a separate hut by someone thought to have magical powers.
