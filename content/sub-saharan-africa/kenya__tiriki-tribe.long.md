## Overview

The supplied sources identify the Tiriki as a group associated with Kenya. The museum records refer repeatedly to Tiriki people, including wood carvers, adult men, adult women, and children. The records also associate Tiriki objects with the Kisumu area of East Africa and with the Swedish Mount-Elgon-expedition of 1920. The sources used do not provide a population estimate, a specific settlement pattern, a river valley, city, oasis, or mountain range where the Tiriki live. They do not identify a language family, language, political history, or detailed account of Tiriki social organization. The general Wikipedia discussion describes “tribe” as a contested category in anthropology and says that tribal membership can be associated with kinship, ethnicity, language, dwelling place, political grouping, religious beliefs, oral tradition, or cultural practices.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used do not document Tiriki textile traditions, named patterns, weaving techniques, or vernacular textile terms.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources used do not name Tiriki textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

The museum photographs record Tiriki people wearing several forms of clothing and adornment. One adult male wood carver is described as wearing a blanket, beard, and an American shirt and shorts. Another adult male is described as wearing an American waist-coat with shorts. A further wood-carver record mentions a cloth, an ear-ornament, a beard, and a smoking pipe. In another photograph, standing adults wear cloths and children wear loin-cloths. Two adult women are described as wearing loin-cloths, personal ornaments, scarification on the torso, and elaborate hairstyles; a child is described as wearing personal ornaments and a patterned hairstyle. The sources do not name garments in a Tiriki language and do not document head coverings, footwear, or a distinct ceremonial dress.

### Architecture

The sources used do not document Tiriki house forms, building materials, roofs, settlement layouts, named building types, or architectural decoration. One museum photograph shows a pot on a hearth in a Tiriki dwelling, with a partition at the left and a basket at the right, but the catalogue record does not describe the dwelling itself.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum catalogue records pottery, wood, iron, leather, horn, cane, banana fibre, cow skin, animal skin, hide, glass beads, and reed among the materials of objects associated with the collection. A double pottery vessel was used for filtering salt. Pottery bowls were used for cooking vegetables and meat, and another pottery bowl used for cooking meat was made by women. Wooden objects include a man’s stool, food bowls, a milking jug, clubs or staffs, and a gourd vessel. A hoe with an iron blade and wooden handle was used for digging but not cultivating. An adze with an iron blade and wooden handle was used by male woodworkers to carve bowls. Other objects include a smith’s bellows, a fat container made from a cow horn, a snuff-box made from a horn, and a food basket made of cane.

## Jewelry & body adornment

The records document necklaces, armrings, a foot ring, a calf necklace, ear-ornaments, personal ornaments, scarification, hairstyles, and decorative materials. British Museum records describe an iron necklace with rings and a model knife; an iron-wire necklace with two small rings, one bearing a bell and the other beads; an iron necklace with pendants; and a necklace for a calf, hung from its neck as a charm against leanness. A cow bell is described as hung with charms on a leather strap. Museum of Ethnography records identify jewellery and armrings from Kenya and the Kisumu area, associated in the catalogue with Tiriki. One foot ring is described as iron wire and ornamented. The sources do not explain the meanings of Tiriki scarification, hairstyles, jewellery, or body adornment beyond the catalogue descriptions.

## Music & performance

The museum records document a Tiriki musical instrument collection that includes a lyre made of wood, hide, wire, and string, another lyre made of wood, hide, and string, a wooden drum with a cow-hide membrane fixed by hide thongs, a trumpet made from antelope and cow horn joined with cow skin, reed flutes, rattles, a boy’s musical bow made of wood and string, and an iron cow bell with a leather strap. One reed flute has five holes, one covered by a leather binding; another has three round holes and is described as unused. A man’s leg rattle is made from folded iron containing two iron balls, while another record describes iron leg rattles strung on leather.

A museum photograph shows an adult male Tiriki wood carver making a wooden drum with an adze. Another depicts an apprentice hitting wooden pegs through drum skin with a stone to fasten it to the ground to dry. The sources do not name Tiriki song genres, musical scales, performance contexts, dances accompanied by these instruments, or occasions such as weddings, funerals, court performances, or festivals.

## Dance & theatre

The catalogue records describe clubs or staffs made of wood with a round knob at the end and re-used as dancing staffs. The sources do not name a Tiriki dance, theatre form, dramatic tradition, mask dance, or performance sequence. They also do not explain whether the dancing staffs belong to ceremonial, social, initiatory, or entertainment contexts.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not document an annual Tiriki festival calendar, seasonal celebrations, dates, weddings, funerals, birth rites, coming-of-age ceremonies, or religious festivals. They do record two circumcision masks woven from string. One has two circular eyelet holes and a large piece of animal fur between them. The catalogue records do not state the circumstances in which these masks were used, the meaning of circumcision among the Tiriki, or whether the masks were worn during a named ceremony. The sources also record a calf necklace described as a charm against leanness, but provide no further account of ritual practice.

## Foodways

The museum records document vessels and utensils connected with food and drink. A double pottery vessel was used for filtering salt. Pottery bowls were used for cooking vegetables and meat, and one bowl used for cooking meat was made by women. A food bowl with a short handle was made from wood. Gourd vessels were used for carrying water or milk, while a gourd with a cow-skin lid was used for carrying milk; one such vessel was decorated with glass beads. A wooden milking jug had an iron ring. A brush made from a cow’s tail was used to clean a beer-pot or as a fly-whisk. A container made from a cow horn held fat and was worn tied to a man’s belt. The sources do not name staple grains, dishes, beverages, dietary rules, ceremonial foods, or cooking traditions beyond these catalogue descriptions.
