## Overview

The Zande, also known as Azande, Azandeh, Azende and Bazende, are an ethnic group in Central Africa who speak Zande languages. In the Democratic Republic of the Congo, they live in Bas-Uélé, Haut-Uélé and Tshopo provinces along the Uele River, including Isiro, Dungu, Kisangani and Duruma. They also live in south-eastern Central African Republic and in parts of South Sudan. Estimates cited for Azande speakers give 730,000 in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, 62,000 in the Central African Republic and 350,000 in South Sudan. Zande is the largest of the Zande languages and is called *Pazande* in Zande and *Kizande* in Lingala. Zande material and intangible culture documented in the sources includes metalworking, throwing knives, sculpture, drums, thumb pianos, oral literature, religious traditions, and agricultural and household practices.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

### Architecture

Zande houses were made from mud and grass framed around wooden poles and thatched with grass. Each household was organized around a courtyard where people could gather and converse. Kitchen gardens stood beside the courtyards and were used for plants that did not require large-scale farming, including pineapples and mangos. During the Zande Scheme, the British established settlements centered around cotton ginneries and resettled 60,000 Azande people into settlements of 50 families called *gbarias*. Families received 25–40 acres of land and answered to a gbaria chief. The British also constructed agricultural training facilities and experimental farms in Yambio and promoted urbanization schemes.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Azande were considered skilled metalworkers in pre-colonial Sudan. Their most famous metal objects are throwing knives called the *shongo*, whose curved and multi-bladed features display the skill of Zande metalworkers. Zande visual art also includes sculptures made from wood or clay. Some sculptures represent important animals or ancestors.

## Music & performance

Zande musical instruments include drums and thumb pianos called *sansa*. The sources state that these instruments sometimes had forms resembling people, animals, and abstract figures. They were used at celebrations such as marriages and community dances. No museum catalogue records were supplied for the instruments or for other Zande musical objects.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention community dances as occasions at which drums and *sansa* were used, and identify marriages and community celebrations as performance contexts.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do describe marriages and community dances as occasions for musical performance. Traditional religious practice involved belief in Mbori, an omnipotent god, together with magic, oracles, and witchcraft. The Azande used several kinds of oracle for different purposes. The *benge* poison oracle was regarded as the most powerful, was used solely by men, and was treated as an important legal authority. The termite oracle could be consulted by women, men, and children, while the rubbing-board oracle was less expensive but less reliable.

Witchcraft, called *mangu*, was understood as an inherited black fluid in the belly. Protection from and cancellation of witchcraft involved rituals that could be performed almost daily. When an unfortunate or unusual event occurred, it could be attributed to witchcraft.

## Foodways

The Azande are mainly small-scale farmers. Crops named in the sources include maize, rice, groundnuts or peanuts, sesame, cassava, sweet potatoes, millet, sorghum, corn, and sugar cane. Fruits include mangos, oranges, bananas, pineapples, and sugar cane is also grown in the region. Zandeland is described as having palm oil and sesame. Major cash crops include cassava and peanuts.

During the rainy season, women and men both help obtain food from the river. Women fish in dammed streams and shallow pools, collecting fish, snakes, and crustaceans. Men make and set traps in the river. The Azande also collect and eat termites, which the source describes as favorites. The sources mention no named dishes, cooking vessels, cooking methods, tea or coffee tradition, dietary rule, or ceremonial food.

## Oral tradition & literature

Recorded Zande literature is mostly oral. Some of it was published by missionaries in the early twentieth century, and some was translated in the 1960s. Folk stories about tricksters helped establish social conduct and explain the consequences of violating social expectations. The Zande trickster *Ture* represents poor decisions and rash actions and serves as a deterrent against similar conduct in the community.

The poison oracle was reserved exclusively for men and helped keep oral traditions and stories alive.

## Language & religion

Zande is the largest of the Zande languages. It is spoken primarily in the north-eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo and western South Sudan, and also in the eastern part of the Central African Republic. In Zande it is called *Pazande*, while in Lingala it is called *Kizande*. Related languages named in the sources include Adio, Barambu, Apambia, Geme, Kpatiri and Nzakara. Estimates cited for Zande speakers range between 700,000 and one million, while another estimate gives the language approximately 1.1 million speakers.

Zande spelling rules were established at the 1928 Rejaf Language Conference. Archibald Norman Tucker published a proposed alphabet during the Bangenzi Conference of 1941, and SIL International published a Zande alphabet in 2014. Most Azande formerly practiced a traditional African religion, but Christianity has largely supplanted it. The traditional religion involved belief in Mbori, magic, oracles, and witchcraft. The source states that 85 percent of Azande consider themselves Christian and 15 percent follow their traditional religion; more than half identify as Catholic.
