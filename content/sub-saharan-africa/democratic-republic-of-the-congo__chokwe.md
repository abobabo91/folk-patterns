## Overview

The Chokwe are a Bantu people of the upper Kasai and Kwango watersheds who began as small hunter-gatherer chieftaincies and gradually absorbed Lunda court culture before expanding aggressively northward in the nineteenth century. They are one of Central Africa's great sculptural traditions, with instantly recognizable masks, ancestor figures and divination baskets that rank among Africa's most important artworks. Their culture centers on the mukanda initiation cycle and the sona system of continuous-line drawings that encode proverbs and cosmological knowledge. Today most Chokwe are nominal Christians who continue to participate in traditional rituals, divination and mask dances.

## Material culture

Chokwe material culture is dominated by wood-carving and beadwork, with a distinctive graphic vocabulary of lattice patterns that appears across textiles, masks, scarification and divination baskets.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Chokwe textiles carry the graphic vocabulary of sona ideographic sand-drawings, a continuous-line lattice grammar that migrates onto raffia mats, beaded belts, incised gourds and mask cheeks.

- **Sona drawings** (*sona*) — Ideographic sand-drawings executed on smoothed earth by elders as mnemonics for proverbs, cosmological narratives and initiation lore, with a visual language of continuous interlocking lattice patterns.
- **Interlaced cross motif** (*cingelyengelye*) — The interlaced cross of the Portuguese Order of Christ, absorbed into Chokwe royal iconography and worked into beadwork and textiles.
- **Concentric-diamond motif** (*mikonda*) — A recurring textile and beadwork pattern representing matrilineal continuity and worn as waistbeads and belt ornaments.
- **Kasai velvet** — A cut-pile raffia cloth technique used to decorate traditional textiles alongside embroidery and painting with red camwood powder and kaolin.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional Chokwe dress centers on raffia or hide wraps secured by beaded belts, with elaborate coiffures, scarification and body adornment marking gender, status and life-stage.

- **Male wrap-skirt** (*mahamba*) — A raffia wrap-skirt or hide loincloth secured by a wide beaded belt, the basic male garment augmented on ceremony with a leopard skin and fibre crown.
- **Leopard skin** (*ngo*) — A prestige garment draped over one shoulder, the exclusive prerogative of the mwanangana chief.
- **Beaded loin-cord** (*mikonda*) — Heavy beadwork worn by women as waistbeads and aprons, worked in the same lattice patterns as sona and marking marital status.
- **Female scarification** (*mikonda ya kucisa*) — Raised keloid patterns on the abdomen, temples and back arranged in lattice grammar, historically a compulsory mark of a woman's initiation.
- **Initiates' dance skirt** (*mahezu*) — A knotted-string body-net and raffia-fibre skirt worn by male dancers of the mukanda circumcision cycle that shakes percussively with the drum pulse.

### Architecture

Chokwe vernacular architecture is modest and dispersed, centered on rectangular pole-and-dagga houses arranged in matrilineal hamlets around a carved men's shelter called the cota.

- **Men's shelter** (*cota*) — An open-sided pavilion with forked posts and a conical or hipped grass roof, its lintels and central king-post often carved with figurative relief, serving as courthouse, guesthouse and place of male sociability.
- **Hamlet** (*cifuci*) — A dispersed matrilineal settlement arranged around a central plaza holding the men's shelter, ancestor shrine and, seasonally, the mukanda initiation enclosure.
- **Mukanda enclosure** (*mukanda*) — A temporary fenced bush camp at distance from the village containing sleeping shelters and a lodge for masked spirit-guardians, ritually burned at the close of the initiation cycle.
- **Ficus palisade** (*mulemba*) — A fence of ficus posts surrounding a chief's compound, distinguishing it from ordinary dwellings.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Chokwe pottery is modest women's work, but blacksmithing and especially wood-carving are the group's signature media, producing prestigious objects charged with ritual and narrative significance.

- **Water jar** (*mulondo*) — A hand-coiled pottery water jar with combed or roulette-impressed shoulders, blackened by post-firing smudging in leaves.
- **Beer pot** (*cizanga*) — A large hand-coiled ceramic vessel for storing and serving millet or maize beer.
- **Chokwe chair** (*ngundja*) — A European-derived four-legged high-backed form utterly transformed into a narrative object, with stretchers and panels densely carved with miniature figures depicting mukanda initiation, hunting, drumming and daily life.
- **Divination basket** (*ngombo ya cisuka*) — A lidded basket packed with sixty-plus miniature figurines, bones, seeds and iron tuponya objects cast and read by the tahi diviner to diagnose illness, death, infertility or theft.
- **Ancestor figure** (*hamba*) — A standing carved wooden figure often representing the hunter-prince Cibinda Ilunga, with massive coiffure, half-closed eyes, filed teeth and geometrically abstracted torso, receiving offerings of beer, tobacco and blood.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Chokwe personal adornment is dense with imported Venetian glass beads arranged in lattice patterns, brass and copper metalwork marking rank, and distinctive keloid scarification treated as jewelry in itself.

- **Multi-strand bead collar** — Heavy layered necklaces of imported Venetian glass beads worked in the same lattice patterning as sona drawings, worn by women as markers of status.
- **Waistbeads** (*mikonda*) — Beaded cords and aprons worn by young initiates and adult women, arranged in lattice patterns and marking marital and initiation status.
- **Brass anklets and armlets** — Often heavy spiralled forms of brass and copper worn to mark rank and marital status.
- **Ivory diviner's amulet** (*mikisi mihake*) — An ivory pendant carved as a miniature pwo face and worn by hunters and diviners as a protective charm.
- **Female keloid scarification** (*cingelyengelye*) — Raised decorative scars over the sternum, radiating around the navel and on the temples, faithfully reproduced on mwana pwo masks.

## Music & performance

Chokwe music is polyrhythmic and drum-driven, with interlocking drums joined by lamellophone, bow-lute and friction drums to accompany praise-singing, topical song and the arrival of masked spirits.

- **Large single-headed drum** (*cinguvu*) — The tall bass drum that leads Chokwe music and announces all mukanda processions with a deep boom answered by higher hand-drums.
- **Friction drum** (*pwita*) — A smaller drum used to create distinctive textures in the polyrhythmic ensemble.
- **Lamellophone** (*cisanji*) — A thumb piano with a characteristic gourd resonator that carries the melodic voice in solo praise-singing and narrative accompaniment.
- **Pluriarc bow-lute** (*cihumba*) — A five-string lute, each string with its own neck, used for solo praise-singing and accompanying sona narratives.
- **Praise poem** (*misambo*) — Elaborate melismatic praise songs to chiefs and ancestors performed as a solo song genre.

## Dance & theatre

Chokwe masquerade is the theatre of the makishi, masked embodiments of ancestral and bush spirits who emerge from mukanda initiation and dance for the community with a sophisticated vocabulary of pelvic isolations, tremors and stylised mimicry.

- **Male ancestor mask** (*chihongo*) — A broad disc-faced form with radiating raffia beard and swept-back crest representing wealth, chiefly power and the male ancestor, historically the prerogative of the mwanangana.
- **Female ancestress mask** (*mwana pwo*) — A serene finely modelled wooden face with almond eyes, filed teeth, keloid scarification and elaborate fibre coiffure, danced by men and embodying the archetypal young woman and bringer of fertility.
- **Initiates' patron mask** (*chikunza*) — A towering conical mask representing the patron of initiates and of hunting.
- **Rain and passage mask** (*kalelwa*) — A fringed sun-disc form associated with rain and rites of passage.
- **Chief's mask** (*cikungu*) — A massive net-and-resin mask of the paramount chief brought out only for the most senior royal occasions.

## Festivals & rituals

The mukanda boys' circumcision and initiation cycle is the central ritual complex, lasting months to over a year and involving circumcision, esoteric teaching, masking and a final public dance and burning of the camp.

- **Boys' initiation cycle** (*mukanda*) — A multi-month or multi-year seclusion of boys aged eight to fifteen in a bush camp where they are circumcised, taught mask lore, sona drawings, hunting technique and genealogy, given new adult names, and progressively introduced to the makishi masks.
- **Girls' initiation** (*mwali*) — A shorter parallel female initiation marking first menstruation with seclusion, instruction in domestic and sexual life, scarification and a coming-out dance in pwo-like adornment.
- **Alternate girls' initiation** (*ukule*) — Another name for the female initiation rite.
- **Ancestor veneration** (*hamba*) — Household and lineage rituals addressing carved ancestor figures, which receive offerings of millet beer, tobacco and blood.
- **Divination session** (*ngombo*) — A consultation with the tahi basket diviner to diagnose illness, sudden death, infertility or theft, widely consulted even in nominally Christian communities.

## Foodways

Chokwe subsistence is based on cassava porridge supplemented by maize, millet and peanuts, with hunting contributing game and palm wine and beer lubricating all social occasions.

- **Cassava staple** (*makamba*) — Cassava root cultivated by shifting cultivation as the primary carbohydrate staple.
- **Stiff porridge** (*funji*) — The daily staple made from cassava flour and taken with sauce of pounded leaves, dried fish, smoked bushmeat or beans.
- **Alternate porridge name** (*nsima*) — Another name for the cassava flour porridge.
- **Leafy peanut sauce** (*matapa*) — Cassava leaves pounded together with peanut, a common sauce served with stiff porridge.
- **Master-hunter** (*cibinda*) — An ideologically central hunting figure, with hunting contributing duiker, bushpig, cane rat and — for chiefs — leopard and elephant to the diet.

## Oral tradition & literature

Chokwe oral literature is dominated by long historical narratives recounting the Lunda migration and the exploits of the hunter-prince Cibinda Ilunga, complemented by genealogical praise-poems, animal tales, riddles and the great didactic tradition of sona sand-drawings.

- **Long historical narrative** (*yishima*) — Extended narratives recounting the migration from Musumba, the exploits of Cibinda Ilunga and the nineteenth-century wars of expansion.
- **Genealogical praise-poem** (*jinjimbo*) — Praise-poems recited by court bards tracing the lineage from the Lunda princess Lueji through Cibinda Ilunga.
- **Animal tale** (*yishimo*) — Cautionary tales featuring the trickster tortoise and hare.
- **Riddling contest** (*jinongonongo*) — Riddle exchanges performed around the evening fire as a form of oral art.
- **Ideographic drawing tradition** (*sona*) — Continuous-line sand-drawings accompanied by the singing or reciting of a fixed narrative, so that the graphic form and verbal text function as a single mnemonic system for proverbs and cosmological knowledge.

## Language & religion

The Cokwe language is a tonal Bantu language written in Latin orthography, and traditional religion recognizes a distant creator, ancestral spirits who intervene actively in daily life, and nature-spirits, with a widespread Christian overlay that coexists with divination and initiation rites.

- **Cokwe language** (*Cokwe*) — A Bantu language of Guthrie zone K.11, mutually intelligible with Lwena, Luchazi and Mbunda, tonal and agglutinative with classic Bantu noun-class morphology, written in Latin orthography developed by missions.
- **Creator** (*Kalunga*) — The distant creator deity recognized in traditional Chokwe cosmology.
- **Alternate creator name** (*Nzambi*) — Another name for the creator deity.
- **Ancestral spirits** (*mahamba*) — Spirits of the dead who intervene actively in daily life through dreams, illness and divinatory signs.
- **Basket diviner** (*tahi*) — A ritual specialist who casts and reads the ngombo divination basket to diagnose illness and misfortune.

- *cingelyengelye* — interlaced cross motif, also female keloid scarification pattern
- *mikonda* — concentric-diamond textile motif; also waistbeads and marital-status beadwork
- *mahamba* — ancestor figures; also ancestral spirits
- *makishi* — masked ancestral and bush spirits (singular: likishi)
- *chihongo* — broad disc-faced male ancestor mask with radiating raffia beard
- *mwana pwo* — serene female mask embodying young woman and fertility
- *mukanda* — boys' circumcision and initiation cycle; also the bush camp enclosure
- *ngombo ya cisuka* — divination basket containing miniature figures and objects
- *tahi* — basket diviner and ritual specialist
- *ngundja* — carved narrative chair or caryatid stool
- *cota* — open-sided men's shelter serving as courthouse and gathering place
- *cifuci* — matrilineal hamlet with central plaza
- *cisanji* — lamellophone or thumb piano with gourd resonator
- *cihumba* — five-string bow-lute used for praise-singing
- *cinguvu* — large single-headed bass drum that leads all processions
- *mwali* — girls' initiation rite marking first menstruation
- *cibinda* — master-hunter; also the culture-hero Cibinda Ilunga
- *hamba* — standing carved ancestor figure
- *funji* — stiff cassava flour porridge, the daily staple
- *matapa* — cassava leaves pounded with peanut, a common sauce
- *yishima* — long historical narratives recounting migration and wars
- *jinjimbo* — genealogical praise-poems recited by court bards
- *mwanangana* — land-holding chief who mediates between ancestors and living
