## Overview

The Chokwe are a Bantu people living across three countries in the upper Kasai and Zambezi watershed, where the rainy season shapes their yearly cycle of field-clearing, building, and ritual. Their art — court sculpture, masks, and divination apparatus — is among the most studied in Central Africa. They speak Chokwe alongside colonial languages and centre their religious and social life on ancestors, initiation, and the spirits called mahamba.

## Material culture

Chokwe craftsmanship emphasizes carved wood, fibre construction, and surface pattern over weaving, with professional carvers (songi) producing commissioned ritual objects while laymen handle everyday work.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Chokwe pattern work appears on masks, carved wood, and basketry rather than looms, with a fixed vocabulary of geometric designs shared across objects and skin.

- **Makishi mask fabric** (*makishi*) — Mask bodies are built from plaited vegetable fibre over a wooden or resin armature, then covered with barkcloth, trade cloth, and paper, and painted in black, red, and white.
- **Pwo and mwana pwo masks** (*pwo, mwana pwo*) — Near-portrait masks of a mature woman and girl whose surfaces carry the same incised linear designs (cijingo) that are scarified onto an initiate's body at the close of ukule.
- **Cikungu mask** (*cikungu*) — The largest Chokwe mask, property of the chief alone, with lateral wings referencing the black stork and bands of triangles read as Gaboon viper markings.
- **Ngombo divination basket** (*ngombo ya cisuka*) — A coiled lidded basket holding miniature figures, bones, horns, seeds, and fragments that a diviner shakes and reads to interpret the configuration that surfaces.
- **Basketry and matting** — Coiled and twilled utility baskets, grain containers, and sleeping mats in savanna grasses with banded geometric registers that recur on carved stool rims.

### Clothing & dress

Daily dress is cloth wrappers, but ceremonial dress is sharply codified and varies by role — chief, masquerader, diviner, or initiate.

- **Chief's masquerade skirt** (*cikungu*) — A floor-length skirt worn by the cikungu masquerader, who is the mask's owner and the chief himself, and who carries a mukwale sword or rifle.
- **Cikunza costume** (*cikunza*) — The grasshopper-antelope masquerader wears a tall conical fibre-and-resin headdress and carries a mukwale sword in one hand and a citete branch in the other.
- **Diviner's dress** (*cimbanda*) — Costumes trimmed with fur, feathers, claws, beaks, and bone; a diviner may carry genet fur for its association with night movement and invisibility.
- **Girl's initiation body-dress** (*ukule*) — At the end of ukule, a girl's body itself is the costume, painted with linear designs for procession through the village where she dances and receives gifts.
- **Chiefly regalia** (*mwanangana*) — An ensemble of combs, jewellery, whistles, horns, spears, scepters, and staffs, all carved, rather than a single garment.

### Architecture

Villages are rings of square mud-brick houses around a central courtyard, anchored by a men's house and bounded by ritual enclosures for initiation.

- **Village layout** (*chota*) — A Chokwe village is occupied by a headman's relatives and their families in square huts roofed with grass, with kitchens and storage rooms adjacent.
- **Men's house and court** (*chota*) — A cone-shaped shelter of freestanding wooden poles under grass roof in the courtyard's middle, where visitors are received, men convene, and ritual sacrifices are hung.
- **Circumcision compound** (*mukanda*) — A screened enclosure in remote bush away from the village where boys are secluded, circumcised, and instructed for months or a year.
- **Girl's initiation hut** (*ukule*) — A cone-shaped grass structure on the village edge, built in the village's architectural idiom, where individual girls are tutored before marriage.
- **Ancestral shrines** (*kachipango*) — Structures holding sculpture and objects through which ancestors and the high god are called.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Carved wood is the signature medium, with thrones and ritual objects commissioned from professional carvers, while household inventory includes baskets, containers, and mats.

- **Chief's throne or stool** (*ngunja*) — A carved chair or square stool belonging to a chief, headman, or senior elder, with uprights, stretchers, and rims carrying figural scenes of initiation, drumming, divination, and daily life.
- **Snuff and tobacco mortars** — Carved vessels with concentric lozenges on surfaces, marked by the professional carver.
- **Ceremonial axes, whistles, and horns** — Carved ritual objects owned by chiefs and used at ceremonies and hunts.
- **Combs, pipes, and lidded containers** — Carved personal and domestic vessels, some marked with the professional carver's work and rank-signalling function.
- **Coiled baskets and grain containers** — Utilitarian woven baskets and mats woven in savanna grasses for everyday storage and sleeping.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Body scarification is the most consequential body art, surgical and gendered, with the same linear pattern vocabulary appearing on masks and as chiefly ornament.

- **Female initiation scarification** (*ukule*) — An elder scars an initiate's abdomen and lower back by cutting and flaring skin and rubbing ashes in, understood as preparing the body for childbirth.
- **Linear body designs** (*cijingo*) — Geometric paint patterns applied at the end of ukule for procession, and reproduced as raised keloid lines on mask cheeks, temples, and foreheads.
- **Carved combs and jewellery** (*mwanangana*) — Chiefly adornment that counts as royal art, alongside staffs and horns, carved by professional carvers.
- **Diviner's worn ornament** (*cimbanda*) — Claws, beaks, and genet fur worn on the person as both adornment and functional divination gear.
- **Hairline treatments** — Raised keloid lines and geometric fields on foreheads rendered in fibre or incision, mirroring mask and body patterns.

## Music & performance

Drums organize all masked performance, with two registers of masquerade — private entertainment masks and initiation-cycle masks — anchoring the repertoire.

- **Cihongo dance mask** (*cihongo*) — The wealth spirit, danced only by the chief, his son, or nephew, and once able to accuse a spectator of a capital crime; now mainly an entertainment mask.
- **Pwo fertility masquerade** (*pwo*) — A male masquerader dances a female portrait mask whose performance transmits fertility to spectators; when the dancer dies, the mask is buried as a person.
- **Exorcism sound ritual** (*cimbanda, hamba*) — A diviner induces possession in a patient and the sequence is complete only when a final scream signals the mahamba spirit leaving through the mouth.
- **Drum ensemble** — The organizing instrument for all masked dancing at mukanda graduation, chiefly appearances, and hamba cult events, with song led by the assembled village.

## Dance & theatre

The makishi masquerade is Chokwe theatre, with strictly maintained categories of ancestral, wealth, and female portrait masks, each with a defined social function.

- **Cikungu ancestral mask** (*cikungu*) — Enormous and winged, performed by the chief alone only on special occasions, embodying the collective ancestors.
- **Cikunza grasshopper-antelope** (*cikunza*) — A mukanda mask whose dramatic function is to seek out circumcision-age boys, lead them into the bush, and guard them through seclusion.
- **Cihongo wealth spirit** (*cihongo*) — A dance mask and hamba simultaneously, which punishes neglect with infertility in women, illness in men, or failed hunting.
- **Pwo and mwana pwo female portraits** (*pwo, mwana pwo*) — Female portrait masks danced by male masqueraders in initiation and entertainment contexts.
- **Pre-masking ritual** — Before putting on a mask, a masquerader spits into it as purification, offering, petition, and request for protection against hostile possession.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual year is keyed to dry and rainy seasons, with two initiations (mukanda and ukule) as load-bearing rites and continuous mahamba spirit cult activity.

- **Mukanda circumcision** (*mukanda*) — A collective male initiation in screened bush seclusion, lasting months to a year, with circumcision, instruction in religion and tradition, and rebirth upon graduation.
- **Ukule girl initiation** (*ukule*) — An individual female initiation aimed at marriage, closing with scarification, body painting, procession, dance, and gift-giving from kin and neighbours.
- **Mahamba spirit cult** (*mahamba*) — Continuous prayers, sacrifices, and offerings to ancestral spirits and parasitic spirits embodied in trees, termite mounds, or masks.
- **Hamba possession and exorcism** (*hamba*) — Illness from parasitic spirits triggers induced possession, white clay rubbing as medicine and innocence-statement, a terminal scream, purification, and cult initiation.
- **Wedding feasts** — The primary occasion for slaughtering cattle, goats, pigs, or chickens, making meat a marker of ceremony rather than everyday diet.

## Foodways

Staples are corn, cassava, and millet from dry-season field agriculture, supplemented by hunting and fishing, with meat reserved for ceremonies and social occasions.

- **Field agriculture** — Dry-season cleared savanna cultivation of corn, cassava, and millet, with sweet potatoes, pumpkins, tomatoes, okra, rice, beans, and peanuts in particular districts.
- **Hunting and fishing** — Seasonal supplementary food practices that punctuate the agricultural calendar and are commemorated in carved chair scenes.
- **Meat at ceremony** — Cattle, goats, pigs, and chickens are kept but slaughtered only for special occasions like wedding feasts, making meat a ceremony marker.
- **Palm-wine tapping** — A recurrent subject on carved chair stretchers, placing it firmly in the social repertoire and suggesting ritual or festive significance.
- **Ritual substances** — The goat or rooster sacrificed with the mukwale sword at cikungu rites and hung from the chota pole; white clay applied as curative medicine in hamba exorcism.

## Oral tradition & literature

Chokwe narrative centres on Chibinda Ilunga, the hunter-hero whose marriage and power-taking explains the people's descent from and eventual overthrow of the Lunda Empire.

- **Chibinda Ilunga** — The single most frequently sculpted hunter-hero figure, whose story explains Chokwe descent from one of twelve Lunda clans and their subsequent independence.
- **Lunda genealogy** — Chokwe, Lunda, Luvale, Luchazi, Ovimbundu, and Mbunda chiefly lines all trace to sixteenth-century Lunda migrations, transmitted orally across the region.
- **Animal narrative symbolism** — Pangolin is nkaka (grandfather); aardvark and pangolin contact underworld ancestors; dogs' howling warns of invisible beings; baboons are favoured for their family structure.
- **Divination objects as mnemonic** (*ngombo*) — The ngombo basket's miniature figures form a fixed lexicon read aloud during divination, functioning as stored oral knowledge.
- **Published Chokwe narrative** — Contes tshokwé d'Angola (1992) is a published corpus; the Mofeko project maintains the largest online Chokwe-language resource.

## Language & religion

Chokwe is a Benue-Congo language spoken with colonial languages; religion is ancestor-centred with a high god called Kalunga, syncretic with Christianity, and administered through shrines and village courts.

- **Chokwe language** (*Kichokwe, Tshokwe*) — A Benue-Congo language of the Niger-Congo family, mutually intelligible in part with neighbouring Lwena, Luchazi, and other groups.
- **Multilingualism** — Speakers are additionally fluent in Portuguese (Angola), French (DRC), or English (Zambia) depending on the state.
- **High god** (*Kalunga, Nzambi*) — The supreme deity, represented on earth by the chief (mwanangana), called the overseer of the land.
- **Ancestral shrines** (*kachipango*) — Structures where carved objects and handicrafts serve as contact points between the living, ancestors, and Kalunga.
- **Syncretic Christianity** — Mass conversion during colonialism produced syncretism rather than replacement; spirit ritual, mahamba cults, and initiation rites all continue alongside Christian practice.

## Glossary

- *makishi* — Masks; the masquerade theatre
- *pwo* — Mature woman mask; also the dancing spirit
- *mwana pwo* — Girl mask; young woman
- *cikungu* — Largest ancestral mask, performed only by the chief
- *cikunza* — Grasshopper-antelope mask of circumcision initiation
- *cihongo* — Wealth spirit mask; dance mask and hamba
- *ngombo ya cisuka* — Divination basket holding miniature figures and objects
- *cijingo* — Linear scarification and painting designs
- *mukanda* — Male circumcision initiation; the compound and rites
- *ukule* — Female initiation rite and tuition preceding marriage
- *mahamba* — Ancestral and parasitic spirits
- *hamba* — Spirit; especially a parasitic or possessing spirit
- *cimbanda* — Diviner; spirit doctor
- *kachipango* — Ancestral shrine holding sculpture and ritual objects
- *mwanangana* — Chief; overseer of the land; representative of Kalunga
- *chota* — Cone-shaped men's house and court in village centre
- *songi* — Professional carver
- *ngunja* — Chief's carved throne or stool
- *mukwale* — Sword carried in ritual masquerade
- *citete* — Branch carried in cikunza masquerade
- *nkaka* — Pangolin; also grandfather
- *Kichokwe* — Chokwe language
- *Kalunga* — High god; supreme deity
