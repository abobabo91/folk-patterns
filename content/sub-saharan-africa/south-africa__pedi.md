## Overview

The Pedi are a Sotho-Tswana people of South Africa. Their homeland lies between the Olifants and Steelpoort rivers, near the Drakensberg and Leolo mountains. Pedi culture includes beadwork, pottery, house building, music, dance, initiation and communal work. Ancestors play an important role in religious life.

## Material culture

Pedi material culture includes beadwork, pottery, woodwork, metalwork, baskets, house building and drums.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Pedi textile traditions are not named in the sources, but beadwork and grass work are well documented.

- **Beaded animal-fur apron** — This fur apron has glass beads in white, red, black, dark blue and orange, including discs and a central diamond design.
- **Beaded fur apron with waistband** — This apron has a fur waistband, red-earth-coated cotton strings, triangular bead patterns and more beaded fur.
- **Grass belt** — This belt is made from grass attached to a fibre string.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe fur aprons and a grass belt but give little information about everyday clothing.

- **Fur apron** — Fur aprons were decorated with glass beads and worn with ties or a waistband.
- **Women’s kneeling dance dress** — The sources describe women dancing on their knees.

### Architecture

Older Pedi settlements centered on family groups, huts, cattle areas and shared open spaces.

- **Family-group village** (*kgoro*) — A kgoro was a group centered on an agnatic family cluster.
- **Central village area** — The central area served as a meeting place, cattle byre, graveyard and ancestral shrine.
- **Round thatched hut** — Each wife in a polygynous marriage had her own round thatched hut.
- **Open-air enclosure** (*lapa*) — A lapa connected the huts and was surrounded by mud walls.
- **Grid settlement** — Later settlements often had rectangular houses with flat tin roofs arranged in grids.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pedi everyday objects include pottery, wooden tools, baskets, dish covers and meat trays.

- **Incised red ceramic bowl** — This bowl has markings around the rim and black-painted designs on its body.
- **Wooden spoon** — Wooden spoons were among the documented household objects.
- **Carved wooden meat tray** — Carved wooden meat trays were used with household food preparation.
- **Woven food basket** — Food baskets were woven from tree creeper, sometimes with dyed sections or dark triangular motifs.
- **Grass-and-cane dish cover** — Some dish covers were made from coiled grass and cane.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources do not name Pedi jewelry or body-decoration systems, but they record elaborate beadwork on fur aprons.

- **Glass-bead apron decoration** — Glass beads formed borders, discs, triangles and a central diamond on animal-fur aprons.

## Music & performance

Pedi music uses distinctive instruments, six-note melodies and group singing.

- **Six-note music** (*mmino wa Setšo*) — Mmino wa Setšo traditionally uses a six-note scale and was played on reeds, jaw harps or autoharps.
- **Pipe ensemble** (*Dinaka/Kiba*) — Dinaka/Kiba uses aluminum end-blown pipes of different pitches to make a descending melody.
- **End-blown pipe** (*naka*) — A naka is an aluminum pipe used in Dinaka music; the plural is dinaka.
- **Knee-dance music** (*koša ya dikhuru*) — Koša ya dikhuru is led by a singer and supported by a chorus and drums.
- **Work songs** — People also sang together while doing communal work.

## Dance & theatre

Women’s dance centers on kneeling, vigorous upper-body movement, singing and drums.

- **Kneeling dance** (*koša ya dikhuru*) — Women dance on their knees while shaking their upper bodies and moving with drums and singers.
- **Combined pipe dance** (*Dinaka/Kiba*) — Dinaka/Kiba combines music and dance in a tradition performed by men and also women.

## Festivals & rituals

Pedi ritual life includes initiation, marriage customs and communication with ancestors.

- **Initiation school** (*koma*) — Girls and boys attended koma, where circumcision and initiation were held about every five years.
- **Initiation regiment** (*mephato*) — Boys entered mephato, cohorts whose members kept lifelong loyalty and often traveled together for work.
- **Girls’ regiment** (*ditswa-bothuku*) — Girls entered their own regiments, usually about two years after the boys.
- **Bridewealth** (*Magadi*) — Magadi was bridewealth linked to marriage between close or classificatory cousins in ruling and chiefly families.
- **Ancestor communication** (*go phasa*) — Go phasa involves burning incense, making an offering and speaking to ancestors.

## Foodways

Pedi foodways combined cattle keeping with hoe farming and communal labor.

- **Sorghum farming** — Sorghum was one of the main crops grown by women.
- **Pumpkin and legume farming** — Women also grew pumpkins and legumes on fields allocated to them after marriage.
- **Communal work party** (*matsema*) — Matsema were work parties that completed major tasks together.
- **Triple meat tray** — A documented triple circular meat tray had four short legs and a stub handle.
- **Double meat tray** — Another documented meat tray or bowl had a short footed base.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources name no Pedi folktale or epic tradition, but they mention work songs and a manhood song.

- **Lion-hunting song** — One song tells of killing a lion in order to become a man.
- **Work songs** — Songs were performed collectively during work.

## Language & religion

Pedi religious life centers on ancestors, while language labels remain debated.

- **Northern Sotho** — Northern Sotho is a standardized Bantu language of the Sotho-Tswana group, written in the Latin alphabet and using š.
- **Creator or God** (*Modimo/Mmopi*) — Modimo/Mmopi is the Creator or God in Pedi religious practice.
- **Ancestor ritual** (*go phasa*) — Go phasa is the practice of addressing ancestors through incense, offerings and speech.
- **Ritual elder** (*kgadi*) — The kgadi, usually the father’s elder sister, held an important place in family ritual.
- **Diviner** (*ngaka*) — A ngaka is a diviner who may become one through inherited spiritual illness or spirit possession.
