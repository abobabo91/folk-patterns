## Overview

The Lozi live around the Barotse Floodplain and move between low and high ground each year. Their society is organised around the Litunga, the Paramount King. Their material culture includes pottery, baskets, carved wood, musical instruments, masks and ceremonial clothing. Lozi culture also includes royal dances, flood-season festivals and stories about Nyambe.

## Material culture

Lozi material culture uses clay, wood, basketry, metal, fibre, horn, ivory, beads and calabashes for household objects, clothing, instruments and ceremonial items.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources do not describe a distinct Lozi textile tradition, but they record many geometric patterns.

- **Geometric decoration** — Lozi objects use geometric decoration, including zigzags, bands, rectangles, dots, rings, squares and stripes.
- **Cross-line patterns** — Some masks have brown and white cross lines and rows of dots.

### Clothing & dress

Traditional Lozi dress includes separate garments for men and women.

- **Men’s shirt and skirt** (*siziba*) — The siziba is a men’s outfit made of a shirt and a knee-length skirt.
- **Women’s dress** (*musisi*) — The musisi is a women’s garment that may have been influenced by missionary-era crinolines.
- **Red beret** (*mashushu*) — The mashushu is a red beret sometimes worn with the siziba.
- **Makishi dance hat** (*Makishi*) — Makishi dance hats are round and made from bark, willow and grass.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Lozi household objects include pottery, carved wood, baskets and iron tools.

- **Water or beer jar** — A yellowish-brown pottery jar with grooves, red ornament and ochre decoration was used for water or beer.
- **Handled water pot** — A pottery water pot has a handle and painted decoration.
- **Wooden milk pot** — A carved wooden milk pot is among the recorded household objects.
- **Iron axe** — Iron axes are among the Lozi-associated metal tools.
- **Double gong** — An iron double gong is among the recorded Lozi objects.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Recorded Lozi adornments use wire, beads, calabash, fibre and natural materials.

- **Coiled neck ornament** — A neck ornament is made from six lengths of coiled wire and has a calabash pendant.
- **Beaded armlet** — An armlet uses glass, ostrich eggshell and wooden beads on a plant-fibre cord.
- **Charms** — Recorded charms use materials such as bone, wood, gum, abrus seeds, fibre, hair, horn, feathers, calabash and glass.

## Music & performance

Lozi musical instruments include xylophones, drums, horns, sansas and gongs.

- **Twelve-key xylophone** (*sirimba*) — The sirimba is a wooden xylophone with twelve keys and twelve calabash resonators.
- **Drums** — Lozi drums have wooden bodies, hide membranes and cords, or reptile-skin heads fixed with metal tacks.
- **Hunting horn** — An antelope-horn hunting horn or trumpet is among the recorded instruments.
- **War trumpet** — An ivory horn war-trumpet is among the recorded instruments.
- **Board sansa** (*sansa*) — The sansa has a wooden board, metal keys and a gourd resonator.

## Dance & theatre

Lozi dances include royal dances, evening dances, initiation dances and masked Makishi performances.

- **War dance** (*Ngomalume*) — Ngomalume is a men’s war dance with belly movement and no singing, performed at royal events.
- **Women’s royal dance** (*Liwale*) — Liwale is a women’s ceremonial dance performed at royal events without singing.
- **Evening dance** (*Sipelu*) — In Sipelu, men and women stand in opposite lines while one or two pairs dance at a time.
- **Initiation and festival dance** (*Siyemboka*) — Siyemboka is performed at beer parties, festivals, ceremonies and girls’ initiations.
- **Makishi masked dance** (*Makishi*) — Makishi dances use masks and hats, including bark masks with painted faces and geometric patterns.

## Festivals & rituals

The flood cycle shapes Lozi movement, royal ceremonies and annual festivals.

- **Flood-season ceremony** (*Kuomboka*) — During Kuomboka, the Litunga moves from Lealui on the floodplain to Limulunga on higher ground.
- **Annual move to high ground** — At the start of the wet season, Lozi people move from the floodplain to higher ground.
- **Royal events** — Ngomalume and Liwale are performed at royal events.
- **Girls’ initiations** — Siyemboka is performed during girls’ initiations.

## Foodways

The profile records only a few Lozi food and drink vessels and one mythic account of cooking meat.

- **Water or beer jar** — A pottery jar was used to hold water or beer.
- **Milk pot** — A wooden milk pot is among the recorded household objects.
- **Food-vessel lid** — A carved wooden lid was made for a food vessel.
- **Meat cooking** — In Lozi mythology, Kamunu killed animals and cooked their meat for food.

## Oral tradition & literature

Lozi myths tell of Nyambe, the first human Kamunu and the origins of Lozi sovereignty.

- **Creator god** (*Nyambe*) — Nyambe created the world, taught Kamunu many skills and later moved from Earth into heaven.
- **First human** (*Kamunu*) — Kamunu was the first human and learned fire-making, metal forging, pottery, carving, farming and animal keeping.
- **Heaven** (*Litooma*) — Litooma is the heaven to which Nyambe moved in Lozi mythology.
- **First Luyi sovereign** (*Mwambwa*) — Mwambwa is described in some versions as the first Luyi sovereign and founder of the nation.
- **Origin woman of the kingdom** (*Njemakati*) — Njemakati is a title for Mwambwa meaning “a woman from whom the kingdom originates.”

## Language & religion

Silozi grew from Luyana and Kololo languages and belongs to the Bantu language family.

- **Lozi language** (*Silozi*) — Silozi is the name used by native speakers for the Lozi language.
- **Lozi homeland** (*Bulozi*) — Bulozi is a name for the Lozi land, also called Barotseland.
- **Luyana kingdom** (*Barotseland*) — Barotseland was the kingdom established by the Luyana on the upper Zambezi floodplains.
- **Luyana** — The Luyana settled on the upper Zambezi floodplains and formed the earlier kingdom.
- **Kololo** — The Kololo conquered the Luyana in the 1830s and imposed their rule and language.
