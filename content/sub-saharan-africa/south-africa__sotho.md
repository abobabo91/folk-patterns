## Overview

The Basotho are a Sotho-Tswana people unified as a nation in the 1800s by Moshoeshoe I from his mountain stronghold. They live in Lesotho and parts of South Africa, speaking Sesotho. They are known worldwide for their distinctive blankets and conical hats, and for the geometric wall paintings women create on their homestead walls.

## Material culture

Basotho material culture centers on geometric pattern-making in textiles, murals, and everyday objects, with blankets and wall painting as the core traditions.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Patterned blankets and geometric wall painting form the heart of Basotho visual tradition.

- **Basotho blanket** (*seanamarena*) — A heavy wool blanket worn pinned at the shoulder or wrapped at the waist, industrially woven with named patterns like maize cob and shield, replaced the traditional hide karosse in the 1800s.
- **Mokorotlo hat** (*mokorotlo*) — A conical grass hat plaited from natural fibers, its shape taken from a mountain peak, now Lesotho's national emblem appearing on the flag.
- **Wall painting** (*litema*) — Geometric relief designs combed and painted by women into the wet mud plaster of homestead walls, built on square grids and worked in ochre, white, blue-black, and grey.
- **Cotton cloth** (*seshoeshoe*) — Indigo or brown printed cotton cut into tiered dresses with ribbon trim, named after Moshoeshoe and made up locally.
- **Grass mats** (*moseme*) — Coiled and plaited reed and grass mats made by women for sleeping, sitting, threshing, and storage, with patterns created through the plaiting itself.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress layers blankets over Western clothing, while initiation and formal wear show distinct ceremonial forms.

- **Herding outfit** — Herd boys wear a blanket pinned at the shoulder with gumboots and a woollen balaclava, a mountain dress now cited as fashion.
- **Girls' initiation dress** (*lebollo la basadi*) — Girls wear the thethana apron, grey blankets, and goatskin skirts during seclusion and emergence.
- **Boys' initiation dress** (*lebollo la banna*) — Boys wear the tshea loincloth with brightly coloured blankets, often combined with sunglasses and modern items.
- **Healer's costume** (*bandolier*) — Crossed strips and strings of leather and beadwork worn over the chest, hung with medicine pouches, adopted by San from Basotho.

### Architecture

The homestead is a circular stone or mud-walled rondavel under a conical thatch roof, decorated with litema painting.

- **Homestead unit** (*rondavel*) — A circular stone or mud wall under a conical thatch roof with a central pole, entered through a low doorway and floored with smoothed dung-and-earth.
- **Courtyard screen wall** (*lelapa*) — A wall enclosing the homestead courtyard, decorated with combed and painted litema designs.
- **Mountain fortress settlement** — Flat-topped hills with restricted passes, like Butha-Buthe and Thaba Bosiu, built as defensive capitals by Moshoeshoe.
- **Field shelter** — Small corbelled stone huts built by mountain communities for use during herding and agricultural work.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Basotho pottery is hand-coiled and burnished, while carved wooden objects and beadwork are equally refined.

- **Beer pot** — A large clay vessel used for brewing and serving sorghum beer, central to feasts and ancestral offerings.
- **Fighting stick** (*molamu*) — A carved wooden stick worn by men at public gatherings and worn during the mohobelo dance.
- **Snuff container** (*nkgo or lekhetho*) — A wooden or horn container for snuff, often plugged with a stopper and worn on a thong.
- **Iron tools** — Hoes, spear and axe blades produced by iron-working established by the sixteenth century.
- **Winnowing tray** — Grass and reed work items including grain baskets, beer strainers, and winnowing trays of refined technique.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Beadwork, wire coils, and amulets mark age and status, while ochre and clay are applied during initiation.

- **Waist apron** (*thethana*) — A beaded or twisted-fiber apron of short hanging strands worn by unmarried women and girls in initiation.
- **Healer's bandolier beadwork** — Beaded strips worn on the bandolier crossed over the chest, with white beads associated with healing.
- **Medicinal amulet** (*litšoantšo*) — Small hide or cloth pouches containing medicinal roots prepared by a healer, worn on neck cords or sewn to clothing.
- **Wire coils** — Brass and copper wire coils worn at the wrist, neck, and ankle, obtained through trade.

## Music & performance

Basotho music centers on massed vocal part-singing and pastoral instruments like the lesiba.

- **Men's processional song** (*mokorotlo*) — A deep, slow, declamatory unison sung on the way to war, court, and at chiefs' gatherings and national occasions.
- **Women's dance-song** (*mokhibo*) — Sung by women, accompanies a kneeling dance driven by handclapping and sharp call-and-response.
- **Reed flute** (*lekolulo*) — A notched end-blown flute played by herd boys to cattle in the mountains.
- **Stick-and-string aerophone** (*lesiba*) — Lesotho's signature instrument: a player blows across a quill that sets a string vibrating, producing a hoarse tone to call and calm cattle.
- **Choral singing tradition** — Four-part choral singing became a defining Basotho practice from the nineteenth century through mission schools.

## Dance & theatre

Basotho dance is characterized by distinct male and female forms centered on the upper body and high-stepping movement.

- **Women's kneeling dance** (*mokhibo*) — Dancers kneel and drive the shoulders and upper torso in rhythmic pulses while seated on their heels, blankets at the waist.
- **Men's striding dance** (*mohobelo*) — A high-stepping dance of long gliding paces, leaps, and stamped landings performed in blankets and hats with sticks.
- **Women's pelvic dance** (*litolobonya*) — A women's dance of pelvic and buttock articulation performed away from men.
- **Borrowed high-kick dance** (*ndlamo*) — A dance using the high kick and heavy stamp, borrowed from Nguni neighbours.

## Festivals & rituals

Agricultural feasts and initiation ceremonies mark the Basotho year, with marriage and funeral rites anchored in cattle exchange and beer.

- **Boys' initiation** (*lebollo or lebollo la banna*) — Boys withdraw to a mountain lodge for months of instruction, endurance, and circumcision, returning with new names and blankets.
- **Girls' initiation** (*lebollo la basadi*) — Girls are secluded and emerge in thethana, grey blankets, and goatskin skirts.
- **Bride-price** (*bohali*) — Cattle transferred from the groom's family to the bride's, negotiated by intermediaries.
- **Postpartum ritual** (*pitiki*) — A ritual for a woman after childbirth, followed by the repainting of the homestead in litema.
- **Rain-making rite** (*mophato*) — A drought ritual addressed through chiefs and diviners to bring rain.

## Foodways

Maize and sorghum porridge form the base of daily meals, with beer central to ritual and feast.

- **Stiff maize porridge** (*papa*) — Maize or sorghum pounded and boiled into a stiff porridge, the base of nearly every meal, eaten by hand with relish.
- **Wild and cultivated greens** (*moroho*) — Boiled greens, pumpkin leaves, amaranth, and spinach dressed with onion, the standard meal accompaniment.
- **Fermented soft porridge** (*motoho*) — Sorghum fermented and served warm at breakfast, sour and thin.
- **Sorghum beer** (*joala*) — Brewed by women over several days in a clay pot and served with a woven strainer, the ritual drink for all major occasions.
- **Highland tuber** (*khabu*) — A wild tuber gathered in the mountains as food during lean seasons.

## Oral tradition & literature

The praise poem is the prestige oral genre, while folktales and proverbs carry pedagogical and historical weight.

- **Praise poetry** (*thoko or lithoko*) — Dense, allusive, metaphor-heavy verse recited at high speed to declare a person's lineage, cattle, battles, and character.
- **Chief's praises** (*lithoko tsa marena*) — The praises of the chiefs, above all Moshoeshoe I, forming a historical archive in verse collected from the 1800s.
- **Folktale** (*litšomo*) — Night stories told by women featuring the ogre Koqoloko, cannibal tales, and the hero Sankatana.
- **Proverb** (*maele*) — Short sayings used pedagogically in initiation instruction and forensically in chiefly courts.
- **African-language novel** — Thomas Mofolo's Chaka (1925) is among the earliest African-language novels, translated into English, German, and French.

## Language & religion

Sesotho is a Southern Bantu language; religion blends Christianity with ancestral veneration and divination.

- **Supreme being** (*Modimo*) — The remote supreme being unapproachable by the living, mediated through the ancestors.
- **Ancestors** (*balimo*) — The ancestral spirits who intercede between the living and Modimo, addressed through beer libation and slaughter.
- **Diviner-healer** (*ngaka*) — A healer-diviner who divines with bones and dispenses plant medicine for protection and healing.
- **Divination bones** (*ditaola*) — Bones cast by the ngaka to divine causes of illness and misfortune.
