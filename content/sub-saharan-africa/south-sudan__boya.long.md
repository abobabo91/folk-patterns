## Overview

The Boya — who call themselves Longarim or Laarim and whose language is catalogued as Narim — are a small agro-pastoral people of the granite hills that rise abruptly out of the dry plain between Chukudum and Kapoeta in Eastern Equatoria, South Sudan. Linguistically they belong not to the Nilotic groups that dominate the region's reputation but to the Surmic branch of Nilo-Saharan, in the South-West Surmic cluster shared with the neighbouring Didinga and, more distantly, with the Murle of Jonglei and the Suri and Mursi across the Ethiopian border; the Didinga and Boya are close enough that older colonial reports treated them as a single "Didinga-Longarim" complex, and Boya oral history commonly describes a separation from Didinga stock followed by a retreat into the hills. That retreat is the central fact of Boya culture. The hills are defensible, better watered than the surrounding plain, and cultivable by terracing, and they placed the Boya between two very different worlds: the sorghum-farming Surmic highlanders to the west and the cattle-centred Ateker (Karamojong-cluster) peoples to the east, above all the Toposa, with whom the Boya have exchanged raids, wives, cattle and vocabulary for generations. Much of the material vocabulary recorded around Kapoeta and reproduced below — *lokwoto*, *leketio*, *ekicholong*, *emong'o*, *emuron* — is Ateker in form, and in a borderland where markets, cattle camps and marriages cross language lines, objects and their names travel together; where a term is likely shared with or borrowed from Toposa rather than specifically Narim, that is noted. Boya livelihood is a double economy of hillside sorghum, maize, groundnuts and tobacco worked mainly by women, and cattle, goats and sheep herded by young men from seasonal camps on the plain. Their signature contributions to the wider ethnographic record are not made of cloth at all: they are the elaborately beaded and cowrie-worked leather dress of women, one of the densest scarification traditions surviving in East Africa, a refined culture of red ochre and butterfat applied to hair and skin, and the small carved wooden headrest-stool that every man carries — a single object that serves as seat, pillow and personal emblem. Decades of Sudanese civil war, displacement into Kenyan and Ugandan camps, and the insecurity that followed independence in 2011 have thinned and altered these practices without extinguishing them.

## Material culture

Boya material culture is built from hide, bead, gourd, iron, horn and wood rather than from cloth or clay sculpture. Its organising principle is the decorated body: the most technically ambitious objects a Boya household produces are the ones worn, and the most complex "surface pattern" is worked directly into skin.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Boya have no loom-weaving tradition, and this absence is characteristic rather than accidental. Across the Surmic and Ateker borderlands, cotton cultivation, spinning and narrow-strip weaving of the kind that defines West African textile culture never took hold; the region's own cloth was skin, and imported trade cotton arrived only in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries through Arab, colonial and later missionary and relief channels. Pattern therefore lives on three other substrates: dressed hide, which is cut, fringed, smoked and oiled; bead and cowrie work, sewn onto that hide in registers of colour that read as a grammar of age and marital status; and the skin itself, incised in raised keloid rows. The only true textile crafts of local manufacture are basketry and matting — coiled and plaited grass trays, sleeping mats and granary linings — which carry geometric banding produced by alternating light and dark fibre.

- **Beaded corset** (*lokwoto*) — Close-fitting torso garment of hide or fibre densely sewn with imported glass beads in horizontal colour bands, worn by girls and young women and typically renewed or elaborated at each change of status.
- **Beaded skirt** (*dolulu*) — Front-and-back leather skirt or apron, the lower register weighted with beadwork, cowries and metal, cut and fringed so that it swings and rattles in movement.
- **Leather belt** (*leketio*) — Broad hide belt, often beaded and studded, worn at the waist to carry the skirt; the term is shared with neighbouring Ateker-speaking groups.
- **Coiled basketry and matting** (*nyakolo*, for the winnowing tray) — Plaited and coiled grass trays, mats and granary liners, with banding produced by alternating fibre colour; the winnowing tray is the form seen daily at every threshing floor.
- **Cowrie work** (*akiring*, a cowrie headband) — Cowrie shells, reaching the hills through long-distance trade from the Indian Ocean coast, sewn in dense rows onto bands and skirt hems as both ornament and stored wealth.

### Clothing & dress

Dress is graded sharply by sex and age. Married women wear heavy beaded leather; girls wear lighter beadwork and more ochre; young men in the cattle camps go largely unclothed but heavily ornamented, with a cloak carried rather than worn, and older men adopt draped cloth.

- **Goat-skin cloak** (*abalang*) — Dressed and oiled goat or sheep hide worn over one shoulder and knotted, doubling as blanket at night and as ground cover by day.
- **Women's leather skirt** — Two-piece hide skirt, the back panel longer than the front, scraped and softened with fat and ochre until it hangs supple; beadwork and cowries concentrate along the hem.
- **Ochre hair pack** (*emuron*) — Mixture of red ochre, animal fat and sometimes ash worked into the hair and scalp to form a shaped, sun-hardened cap that is periodically rebuilt; the same mixture is rubbed over the body for shine, insect protection and beauty. The term is also widely used in neighbouring Ateker languages for a diviner, and the two senses should not be conflated.
- **Ivory bracelet** (*ekachil*) — Arm or wrist ring cut from elephant ivory, historically a mark of a hunter or of a wealthy household; increasingly replaced by aluminium, plastic and horn.
- **Cattle-horn ornament** (*emong'o*) — Worked horn ornaments and pendants associated with cattle wealth and with individual named oxen; the root is the Ateker word for ox.

### Architecture

Boya settlement is the most visibly distinctive thing about them from a distance: villages sit high on the hill flanks, terraced into the slope with dry-stone retaining walls that hold both house platforms and sorghum plots. The compound is a cluster of round single-room houses with low mud-and-wattle walls and a steep conical thatched roof, encircled by a thorn or stone perimeter, with granaries, a threshing floor and a livestock enclosure within. There is no monumental architecture, no masonry tradition beyond terracing, and no specialised religious building: shrines are trees, stones and cattle byres rather than structures.

- **Round thatched house** (*lomangoli*, the thatch itself) — Mud-and-wattle cylinder with a steep grass-thatched conical roof tied to a timber frame; thatch is renewed communally and the term refers to the grass covering.
- **Grass granary** (*ebolo*) — Raised woven or daubed basket-granary set on stones or a timber platform above ground, capped with its own small thatch lid against rain and rodents.
- **Stone terracing** — Dry-stone retaining walls cut into the hillside to hold soil for sorghum and to level house platforms, the defining engineering of Boya hill settlement.
- **Cattle enclosure** — Thorn-fenced kraal within or beside the compound, and seasonal camps on the plain where young men live with the herds for months at a time.
- **Headrest stool** (*ekicholong*) — Carved from a single block of hardwood, this small stool is the closest thing to portable architecture: stool by day, pillow by night, protecting the ochred hair pack, and a personal object closely identified with its owner.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery is made locally in modest quantities — undecorated or lightly combed hand-built cooking and brewing vessels, fired in open bonfires — but the gourd rather than the pot is the signature container, and iron comes chiefly through smiths and trade rather than from a large indigenous smelting industry.

- **Gourd vessel** (*achok*) — Calabash cut, dried and sometimes pyro-engraved or bound with beaded netting, used for milk, water, sorghum beer and butterfat, and given as a marriage gift.
- **Iron spearhead** (*lokoro*) — Leaf-bladed socketed or tanged spearhead mounted on a hardwood shaft, the principal weapon for herding, hunting and defence, and a standard item of bridewealth-adjacent exchange.
- **Hide shield** (*abwo*) — Oval or elongated shield of thick hide over a wooden spine, carried in dance and display as much as in fighting.
- **Clay pipe** (*ariau*) — Short-stemmed pipe of fired clay, often with a wooden or reed stem, used by men and older women for locally grown tobacco.
- **Grinding stone and winnowing tray** (*nyakolo*) — The paired tools of the sorghum economy: a saddle-shaped lower stone with hand-held rubber, and a coiled grass tray used to toss and separate grain from chaff.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Scarification is the central Boya art. Raised keloid rows are cut and rubbed with ash or plant matter so they heal proudly, applied in patterned registers across the belly, chest, back and arms — most extensively on women, where the sequence of cuttings tracks puberty, marriage and childbearing, and on men in narrower marks that in some communities have historically recorded achievement in hunting or raiding. Beadwork, brass and ostrich-shell beads complete the composition.

- **Patterned scarification** (*ngaroto*) — Registers of raised cicatrices worked in rows, arcs and chevrons across the torso, cut in stages across a woman's life rather than in a single sitting.
- **Body cicatrice** (*emuya*) — Individual raised keloid mark; the accumulated field of these is read locally as a record of endurance, status and beauty.
- **Coiled brass neck ring** (*lopeta*) — Heavy coiled brass or copper-alloy ring worn stacked at the neck, the metal acquired through trade and treated as portable wealth.
- **Ostrich eggshell disc beads** (*lokwalimoe*) — Small white discs ground and drilled from ostrich eggshell and strung in bands, one of the oldest bead technologies in East Africa and still valued against glass imports.
- **Lip and ear ornament** — Piercing of the lower lip and of the earlobe and helix rim for plugs, rings and short pins is reported in the region, with plate-sized lip discs of the kind famous among the Ethiopian Surma and Mursi not characteristic of the Boya.

## Music & performance

Boya music is overwhelmingly vocal, communal and unaccompanied by melody instruments. There is no well-documented local chordophone and no harp or lyre tradition of the kind found further north among the Nilotic peoples of the Nile valley; the instrumental palette is percussive and aerophonic, and its function is to organise bodies in motion. Songs cluster around cattle — praise songs composed for a favourite named ox and sung by its owner, herding songs from the camps — and around work, with women's grinding and threshing songs in call-and-response. Large single-headed drums beaten with hands or sticks provide the ground; hand-clapping, stamping, and leg and waist rattles supply the rest; antelope or cattle-horn trumpets sound at gatherings and alarms. Composition is collective and topical, and a new song commemorating a raid, a drought, a marriage or a disliked official can spread between villages within weeks. Christian hymnody, sung in Narim and in Juba Arabic, has become a major second repertoire since the mid-twentieth century and is now for many households the most frequently performed music of all.

- **Ox praise song** — Solo or small-group song composed by a young man for his favourite ox, naming its colour pattern and horn shape; a principal genre of personal poetry.
- **Cattle-camp song** — Herding and night-watch songs sung antiphonally by young men in the seasonal camps on the plain.
- **Women's work song** — Call-and-response songs timed to grinding, threshing and winnowing, with the rhythm of the stone or tray as the pulse.
- **Drum** — Large single-headed hide drum, hand- or stick-beaten, that anchors dance gatherings; in most settings it is the only constructed instrument present.
- **Horn trumpet** — End- or side-blown antelope or cattle horn used for signalling, for summoning to dance, and as a punctuating voice within it.

## Dance & theatre

Dance among the Boya is participatory rather than theatrical. There is no masking complex, no puppet tradition, and no class of professional performers of the kind found in West and Central Africa; performance happens in a ring of participants rather than before an audience, and the distinction between dancer and spectator dissolves as people enter and leave the circle. The dominant vocabulary is vertical: dancers form facing lines or a ring, and individuals or pairs leap straight upward from flat feet with the torso held erect, a form broadly shared across the cattle-keeping peoples of the South Sudan–Uganda–Kenya borderlands. Beadwork and metal are integral rather than incidental, since the sound of a beaded skirt and stacked neck rings on landing is part of the music.

- **Jumping dance** — Ring or facing-line dance in which participants leap vertically in turn, the height and control of the jump serving as display.
- **Courtship dance** — Evening gatherings, often at the cattle camps or after harvest, where young men and women assess one another; ochre, beadwork and fresh scarification are prepared for these occasions.
- **Spear-and-shield display** — Men's dance with *abwo* shield and *lokoro* spear, combining stamping advance and retreat with rhythmic striking of shield against shaft.
- **Wedding dance** — Multi-day dancing at the transfer of bridewealth and the bride's move to her husband's compound, involving both families' villages.
- **Mourning dance** — Slower, lower-bodied collective movement with wailing and drum at funerals, marking the passage of a senior person.

## Festivals & rituals

The Boya ritual year turns on rain and on sorghum. The decisive ceremonies are those that open the rains and those that release the harvest for eating, and authority in both is vested less in a political chief than in rainmaking lineages — the pattern Simon Simonse documented across this borderland, in which a rain-giving figure is credited with the seasons and held accountable, sometimes violently, when they fail. Life-cycle ritual is organised by age and by cattle. Boys move through graded sets whose promotion involves seclusion, instruction and the slaughter of oxen; girls' passage is marked by successive stages of scarification and beadwork rather than by genital cutting, which is not characteristic of the group. Marriage is a protracted transfer of cattle and goats between lineages, negotiated by elders over months or years, and the bride's beaded dress is assembled for it. Divination and healing employ chicken and goat entrails, the reading of misfortune back to an offended ancestor or a neighbour's ill will, and sacrifice at a kraal or a named tree. No Boya practice appears on the UNESCO Representative List or the List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in Need of Urgent Safeguarding; South Sudan has no inscribed elements at all, so the region's living traditions are documented mainly through anthropology, mission records and photography rather than through international instruments.

- **Rain ceremony** — Sacrifice and appeal conducted by a rainmaking lineage at the opening of the season, with the rainmaker credited for good years and pressured in bad ones.
- **First-fruits rite** — Ritual release of the new sorghum for general consumption, preceded by offering and by a prohibition on early eating.
- **Age-set promotion** — Graded initiation of young men through seclusion, instruction by elders and the slaughter of cattle, conferring the right to marry and to speak in assembly.
- **Scarification rite** (*ngaroto*) — Staged cutting of a girl's or woman's torso at puberty, betrothal and after childbirth, performed by experienced female specialists.
- **Bridewealth transfer** — Negotiated handing over of cattle and small stock from groom's to bride's lineage, the central economic and political event linking families.

## Foodways

The Boya eat from two larders that rarely mix on the same plate. From the hill fields come sorghum above all, with maize, millet, groundnuts, cowpeas, pumpkins, sesame and tobacco; from the herds come milk, soured milk, butterfat and, at ceremonies, meat. Sorghum is ground on a saddle stone into flour and boiled to a stiff porridge eaten with a relish of wild greens, pounded groundnut, sesame paste or, when available, goat. Cattle are rarely killed for food outside ritual, so animal protein arrives chiefly as milk and as blood in some households, and meat is a marker of occasion rather than a staple. Sorghum beer is the pivot of social life — brewed by women in clay pots and gourds, drunk collectively from a shared vessel through reeds or dipped bowls, and offered at work parties, negotiations and funerals, so that a household's capacity to brew is a direct measure of its ability to mobilise labour and goodwill. Wild resources matter more than a bare crop list suggests: honey collected from log and rock hives, shea and other oil-bearing fruits, tamarind, wild greens gathered by women, and small game and fish taken opportunistically. Gourds (*achok*) serve as churn, cup, store and measure; the clay pipe (*ariau*) closes a meal. The lean season before the rains has historically been survived on gathered foods, stored sorghum in the *ebolo* granaries and the exchange of goats for grain, and in recent decades on relief distributions during conflict and drought.

## Oral tradition & literature

Narim was unwritten until the twentieth century, and Boya verbal art is accordingly oral, performed and functional. Its genres include clan origin narratives that explain the move into the hills and the relationship with the Didinga; migration and raid histories that anchor land claims and grazing rights; animal trickster tales told to children in the evening, in which the hare and the hyena commonly occupy the clever and the greedy roles familiar across the region; proverbs deployed in dispute settlement, where the elder who can produce the apt saying often carries the argument; riddles as children's competitive play; and the personal ox-praise poem, which is the closest thing to lyric authorship in the culture, composed by a named individual about a named animal and sung by him alone. Genealogy is itself a literary form, recited to establish standing in bridewealth negotiations and land disputes. Literacy has arrived through two channels, both recent: Christian missions, which produced the first orthography, primers and scriptural translation work in Narim in collaboration with linguistic organisations, and displacement, which sent a generation through English- and Arabic-medium schooling in Kenyan and Ugandan camps and in Juba. Written Narim literature remains very thin, and the language's documentation is still largely the work of comparative Surmic linguistics rather than of local authorship.

## Language & religion

Narim, also called Longarim or Boya, belongs to the South-West Surmic group within the Surmic branch of Nilo-Saharan, alongside Didinga, Murle, Tennet and Baale; the branch as a whole extends east into Ethiopia to include Suri, Mursi and Me'en. Speakers of Narim and Didinga are close, and mutual intelligibility with Didinga is high enough that the two are often treated as a dialect pair, with Didinga the larger and better documented partner. The language is tonal, has the complex verbal morphology typical of Surmic, and is spoken by a population commonly estimated in the tens of thousands, with Juba Arabic serving as the regional lingua franca, English as the language of school and state since independence, and Toposa widely understood along the eastern edge. Religion in practice is layered rather than divided. Older strata centre on a sky-and-rain divinity, on ancestral shades who remain implicated in the fortunes of their descendants, and on specialists — rainmakers, diviners and herbalists — who diagnose misfortune and prescribe sacrifice, most often of a goat or an ox at a kraal or named tree. Christianity has spread substantially since the mid-twentieth century, with Catholic missionary work in the Chukudum and Kapoeta area the most significant single influence and Protestant congregations also established; Islam is marginal in the hills. For most Boya households the two systems coexist: church attendance and baptismal names sit alongside bridewealth in cattle, ancestral sacrifice at a death, and consultation of a diviner when a child sickens or the rain does not come.
