## Overview

The Boya — also spelled Buya, and known to their Didinga neighbours as *Larim* or *Langorim* — are a Surmic-speaking people of 20,000 to 25,000 living in Budi County in the Greater Kapoeta region of Eastern Equatoria, South Sudan. They occupy the south and west flanks of the Boya Hills, concentrated around Mt. Kosodek and Mt. Lobuli, with the market town of Kimatong sitting at the foot of the range. Their language, Narim, belongs to the Southwest Surmic branch of Nilo-Saharan and is closely related to Didinga, Tenet and Murle. They are agro-pastoralists: sorghum, maize and beans on hillside plots, cattle and small stock on the plains, supplemented by hunting and fishing. In folk-culture terms the Boya matter as one of the best-photographed of the small Surmic groups — the Pitt Rivers Museum holds a dedicated Larim (Boya) photographic and manuscript collection — and as bearers of a beadwork and scarification aesthetic shared with the Toposa, Didinga and Lotuko but locally distinct in colour and cut.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Boya did not weave cloth. Their pattern-bearing surfaces are hide, bead, brass and skin — the body itself is the principal ground, and the "textile" tradition is a tradition of beaded and worked leather.

**Lokwoto beaded corset** — the *lokwoto* is a stiff, close-fitting torso piece built up from horizontal courses of glass seed beads stitched to a leather or fibre foundation, worn by young unmarried women. Its distinguishing feature against Toposa equivalents is the tightly banded, near-solid coverage of the ribcage in alternating colour registers rather than open lattice work.

**Leketio leather belt** — a broad hide waist belt, *leketio*, worked with rows of cowrie or bead insets and metal studs; the belt carries the beaded skirt and is itself a statement of a woman's marital and reproductive standing.

**Dolulu beaded skirt** — the *dolulu* is a short apron-skirt of hide with dense bead embroidery on the front panel and a fringed or tabbed lower edge, hung from the *leketio*. Colour blocks encode age-grade rather than clan.

**Abalang goat-skin cloak** — the *abalang* is a supple, oiled and ochred goat- or calfskin cape worn over one shoulder, doubling as a sitting mat and a child-carrier; the surface is dressed with fat and red ochre rather than dyed, so the "pattern" is a gradient of wear and re-greasing.

**Akiring cowrie headband** — a narrow fillet, *akiring*, of cowrie shells sewn in one or two ranks onto a leather strap, worn across the brow at dances and betrothals.

**Ngaroto scarification and emuya body cicatrice** — *ngaroto* denotes the raised keloid scarring worked in arcs and chevrons across the forehead, temples and cheeks; *emuya* the larger field of cicatrice patterning laid over a woman's belly, back and upper arms in dotted rows and lozenges. These are the Boya's most elaborate designed surfaces and are cut in stages tied to puberty, first marriage and childbearing.

**Motif vocabulary.** Horizontal colour registers (age-grade banding on the *lokwoto*); chevron arcs (forehead *ngaroto*); dotted rows (belly *emuya*); lozenge and diamond fields (back cicatrice); cowrie ranks (fertility and wealth); alternating red-white-blue bead courses; solid black bead ground; fringed tab edging on hide skirts; concentric brass coil (the *lopeta* neck ring); spot-and-line ochre painting applied to the torso for dances.

### Clothing & dress

Women's dress is the Boya's visual signature: the beaded *dolulu* skirt over the broad *leketio* belt, the *lokwoto* corset for unmarried girls, multiple brass and iron neck coils (*lopeta*), stacked wire armlets, and the *abalang* skin cloak thrown over the shoulder in cool weather. Married women's heads are commonly shaved or close-cropped and dressed with *emuron* ochre hair packs — a paste of red ochre and animal fat worked into the remaining hair and scalp, which both colours and protects. The *akiring* cowrie band is added for dances. Men traditionally went largely unclothed for herding, wearing a hide or cotton wrap over one shoulder, a hide belt carrying a knife and snuff container, and sandals cut from hide; adult men carry the *lokoro* iron-bladed spear and the *abwo* hide shield as dress items as much as weapons. The *ekachil* ivory or bone upper-arm bracelet marks a man who has killed large game or an enemy. Today, imported cotton shirts, wraps and plastic sandals are everyday wear, with beadwork and ochre reserved for dances, weddings and funerals.

### Architecture

Boya settlements are hillside hamlets of round *lomangoli* huts — the term covers the thatched tukul form generally: a ring of timber uprights and withy wall plates, mud-and-dung plastered, carrying a steep conical roof of grass thatch tied to a light rafter cone. The steep pitch sheds the heavy Equatoria rains; the eaves oversail far enough to shade the wall and keep the plaster dry. Compounds are fenced with thorn or stockade and hold a sleeping hut, a cooking hut or open hearth, and one or more *ebolo* grass granaries — raised basketry silos on stone or timber footings, woven of grass and withies and capped with a removable thatch lid, lifted clear of the ground against damp and rodents. Cattle are held in a separate thorn-fenced kraal, often at the hamlet edge or on the plain below. Because the Boya live on and around rocky hills, terracing and dry-stone retaining of cultivation plots is part of the built landscape, as is the use of rock overhangs and boulder clusters as defensive refuges — a legacy of a long history of cattle raiding with neighbouring groups.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Household gear is dominated by gourd, wood and hide rather than fired clay. The *achok* gourd vessel — a calabash cut, cured and sometimes bound with hide or fibre — serves for milk, water, beer and grain, and is the standard drinking and pouring container. Wooden forms include the *ekicholong* headrest stool, a single-piece carved billet with a curved upper saddle and one or two struts, doubling as a stool by day and a neck-rest by night that keeps an ochred hairdress off the ground; it is carried slung from the wrist or belt. Basketry supplies the *nyakolo* winnowing tray, a shallow coiled or plaited grass fan used to toss threshed sorghum. Ironwork is smith-made and functional: the *lokoro* socketed spearhead, knife blades, arrowheads and bracelet stock. The *ariau* clay pipe — a short-bowled smoking pipe with a reed or wood stem — is one of the few fired-clay forms in regular use.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment is cumulative and biographical. Women stack the *lopeta* coiled brass neck ring in graduated tiers, add wire and bead armlets and anklets, and pierce the lower lip and ears for plugs and pendants; *lokwalimoe* ostrich-eggshell disc beads, ground and drilled to flat discs and strung in ranks, are the oldest bead form in the region and predate the glass trade beads that now dominate. Men wear the *ekachil* ivory arm ring, twisted iron wire bracelets, and the *emong'o* cattle-horn ornament — worked horn tips mounted as arm or head ornaments referencing a favourite ox. Hair is dressed with *emuron* ochre-and-fat packs, and the torso is painted in spot-and-line ochre and ash for dances. *Ngaroto* facial scarification and the broader *emuya* cicatrice fields are permanent adornment and the clearest external marker of Boya identity, cut by specialist women with a blade and irritant to raise the keloid.

## Music & performance

Documentation of Boya music specifically is thin; what is recorded belongs to the shared Surmic and Karimojong-cluster idiom of Eastern Equatoria. Performance is overwhelmingly vocal and percussive rather than instrumental. The core forms are unaccompanied group song in call-and-response, led by a soloist and answered by a chorus of the same age-set, driven by handclapping, foot-stamping and the rattle of the women's brass and bead ornaments — the *lopeta* coils and beaded *dolulu* are themselves sound-producing, and a line of dancing women is an audible as well as visual mass. Skin-headed drums, struck with hand or stick, mark the beat at dances and funerals. Ox songs — praise songs composed by a young man for his favourite ox, naming its colour pattern, horn shape and lineage — are the central lyric genre across the region and the vehicle for a man's poetic reputation; the *emong'o* horn ornament is worn as its material counterpart. Whistles and horn calls are used in herding and hunting and carry into dance as punctuation. Lament singing by women is the dominant funeral genre. No court or professional musician class exists; competence is expected of every adult within their age-set.

## Dance & theatre

The Boya have no theatrical or masked tradition. Dance is communal, age-graded and held in an open clearing or the cattle kraal: lines or facing ranks of women, fully ornamented, advance and retire with a stamping step while men leap vertically from a standing position — the high jump-dance common to the pastoral peoples of the Karamoja–Kapoeta belt, performed individually within a circle and read as a display of physical condition and marriageability. Dances cluster around the harvest, rain-making, the return of successful raiding or hunting parties, betrothals and funerals; courtship dance is the principal formal occasion on which unmarried men and women meet and assess each other, and the *lokwoto* corset and *akiring* headband are worn precisely for it. Wrestling and stick-play between young men of neighbouring hamlets occupy the same festive slot as dance and follow similar age-set rules.

## Festivals & rituals

The Boya calendar is agricultural and pastoral rather than religious-astronomical: the two poles are the sowing of sorghum at the start of the long rains and the harvest and threshing at their close, both marked by dancing, ox slaughter and sorghum beer. Rain-making is the most important collective rite, conducted by hereditary rain specialists whose authority — as among the neighbouring Didinga and Lotuko — rests on control of rain stones and ancestral shrines, and whose failure carries real political cost. Life-cycle rites structure a person's visible appearance: the first cutting of *ngaroto* facial scars at puberty, further *emuya* cicatrice at marriage and after a first child, and the assumption of the *leketio* belt and *dolulu* skirt as a woman moves out of girlhood. Marriage is by bridewealth in cattle, negotiated between lineages over an extended period, with the transfer of stock and a feast of slaughtered animals as the public act; a young woman's beadwork and brass is substantially bridewealth-financed and is itself the record of the transaction. Funerals bring burial within or beside the homestead compound, women's lament singing, the slaughter of cattle for the mourners, and a later second gathering closing the mourning period. Age-set initiation binds men of a cohort for life and governs who dances, herds, raids and speaks together.

## Foodways

Sorghum is the staple, ground on a saddle stone and cooked as a stiff porridge eaten with the hand and dipped into a relish of boiled beans, wild greens, groundnut or — on a feast day — meat. Maize is the second grain, eaten roasted on the cob in season and otherwise ground with the sorghum. Threshed grain is winnowed on the *nyakolo* tray and stored in the *ebolo* granary against the dry months. Milk is drunk fresh or soured in *achok* gourds cured with smoke and ash, which both sterilises the vessel and flavours the contents; blood taken from a living beast and mixed with milk is a strengthening food eaten in the cattle camps, as across the pastoral belt. Meat is eaten at slaughters tied to marriage, funeral, rain rites and the settlement of disputes, roasted directly on coals or boiled; goat and sheep are the everyday animals, cattle reserved for occasions of consequence. Hunted game, honey, wild fruits and fish from the seasonal streams round out the diet. Sorghum beer, brewed by women from malted and fermented grain and drunk warm and thick from communal gourds through the hosting of work parties, is the medium of hospitality and of collective labour: a field is weeded or a roof thatched in exchange for beer. Tobacco is smoked in the *ariau* clay pipe and taken as snuff from small hide or horn containers worn at the belt.

## Oral tradition & literature

Narim is an oral language with no historical literary tradition; the SIL and church-led orthography and translation work of the later twentieth century is its first substantial written corpus. The oral repertoire is that of the Surmic groups generally: origin and migration narratives tracing the split of the Boya from the Didinga and the settlement of the Boya Hills, genealogies of lineage and rain-priest succession, cattle-raiding narratives naming specific engagements with Toposa and Didinga, animal trickster tales, riddles used as evening entertainment for children, and the improvised ox-praise poetry that doubles as the main lyric literature. Proverb use is dense in dispute settlement and elders' speech. Formal transmission is by grandparents and by age-set seniors at the evening fire and in the cattle camps. Documentation is scattered: the Pitt Rivers Museum's Larim (Boya) photograph and manuscript collection is the single most concentrated archive, and missionary and SIL linguistic fieldwork on Narim supplies most of what is recorded of the language itself.

## Language & religion

Narim (also Larim, Boya) is a Southwest Surmic language of the Eastern Sudanic branch of Nilo-Saharan, in the same cluster as Didinga, Tenet (Tennet) and Murle; Didinga and Narim are mutually intelligible to a substantial degree, and the two groups' relationship — kin, neighbour and occasional adversary — is the axis of Boya political life. There is no indigenous script. The older religious system centres on a high creator deity, ancestral spirits addressed at homestead and lineage shrines, and specialists: rain-makers controlling rain stones, and diviners consulted over sickness, theft and the success of raids. Christianity, chiefly Catholic through the Kimatong and wider Torit mission field, is now widely professed and coexists with rain rites, ancestral offerings and divination rather than displacing them.
