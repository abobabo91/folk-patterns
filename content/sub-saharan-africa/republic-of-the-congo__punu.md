## Overview

The Punu are a Bantu meta-ethnicity of Gabon and the Republic of Congo. The sources describe migration into the Republic of Congo in the 16th century and into southern Gabon in the 18th century. Their language is Yipunu, spoken by several thousand people. Punu material culture is especially represented by carved masks, figures, and other wooden objects, while communal performance centers on masked dancers.

## Material culture

Punu material culture is especially represented by carved masks, figures, and other wooden objects.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources do not document a Punu textile tradition in sufficient detail, but they describe diamond-shaped scarification marks.

- **Diamond-shaped forehead and temple marks** — A Punu girl had diamond-shaped scarification marks on her forehead and temples.
- **Eye punctures** — Small punctures surrounded the eyes of one museum mask.

### Clothing & dress

The sources do not give a general account of Punu dress, but museum records show several clothing details.

- **Woven fibre skirt** — A black-pigmented male figure wears a woven fibre skirt held by a belt of printed cotton.
- **Pith style helmet and jacket** — Another figure wears a colonial pith style helmet and jacket with a pocket, and carries guns.

### Architecture

The sources do not cover Punu houses, settlements, roofs, or architectural decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The catalogue records document carved wooden bellows, masks, and figures, but not Punu ceramics or metalworking.

- **Female figure bellows** — One carved wooden bellows takes the form of a female figure with a wide flat torso and two round breast cavities covered with skin.
- **Wooden figures** — The figures are carved from wood and may have integral circular bases.
- **White-painted face** — One bellows has a face painted white.
- **Pigmented carved surfaces** (*Ricinodendron africanum?*) — The wooden figures may be identified as Ricinodendron africanum? and can have white kaolin, black, red, or reddish-brown pigment.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Scarification and coiffure are documented more clearly than jewelry.

- **Diamond scarification** (*Mukudj*) — Intricate diamond-like scarification marks on the forehead and temples are associated with Mukudj, female ancestor masks.
- **Elaborate coiffure** — Museum figures and masks show elaborate hairstyles, crests, braids, and coiffures.
- **Glass bead necklace** — One figure wears a necklace of glass beads.
- **Animal claw pendant** — The same figure wears an animal claw on fibre cord.

## Music & performance

The sources do not identify Punu musical forms, but the Yipunu lexicon records terms for instruments, singing, dancing, and performance.

- **Musical bow** (*Mungongu*) — Mungongu is glossed as “musical bow.”
- **Harp** (*Ngomfi*) — Ngomfi is glossed as “harp.”
- **Singing** (*Wimbile*) — Wimbile means “to sing.”
- **Song of exchange with ancestors** (*Ndele*) — Ndele is a song signifying an exchange between the living and the ancestors.
- **Performer** (*Ikambi*) — Ikambi means “performer.”

## Dance & theatre

Masked dancing is the best-documented Punu performance tradition.

- **White mask dance** — Life-size white masks cover a dancer’s face during major community events in southern Gabon.
- **Nine-clan forehead dots** — White masks have nine dots on the forehead, symbolizing the nine Punu clans.
- **Black training mask** — Black-mask dancers perform first to announce the arrival of the more experienced white-mask dancer.
- **Night dancing** — Black masks are danced with only in the dark at night.
- **Blackened white mask** — When misfortune affects a group, white masks may be painted black.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe major community events, secret-society dances, and rituals concerning illness and death.

- **Secret-society dance** — A dance performed for a secret society is one major community event involving masked dancers.
- **Death investigation operation** — The Punu formerly carried out surgical operations after someone’s death to discover the cause.
- **Family-circle wizard** (*mulosi*) — If the cause of death could not be found, a family-circle wizard called mulosi was held responsible.
- **Spirit-driving healing ritual** — Healing required a special ritual intended to drive away an evil spirit’s work.

## Foodways

The sources do not document Punu foods or cooking traditions, but the Yipunu lexicon includes several food words.

- **Cooking** (*Ikuku*) — Ikuku means “cooking.”
- **Palm wine** (*Dingibe*) — Dingibe means “palm wine.”
- **Food** — The Yipunu lexicon includes a word glossed as “food.”
- **Honey** — The Yipunu lexicon includes a word glossed as “honey.”
- **Meat** — The Yipunu lexicon includes a word glossed as “meat.”

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources do not provide specific Punu narratives, but the Yipunu lexicon records several literary and storytelling terms.

- **Tale or fable** (*Dusavu*) — Dusavu means “tale, fable.”
- **Proverb or parable** (*Nongu*) — Nongu means “proverb, parable.”
- **Short story** (*Ilombi*) — Ilombi means “short story.”
- **Short story** (*Musamu*) — Musamu is also glossed as “short story.”
- **Telling a dream** (*Walule*) — Walule means “tell a dream.”

## Language & religion

Yipunu is a Bantu language, and Punu religious life centers on Nyambye, evil spirits, ritual healing, and the mulosi.

- **Yipunu language** (*Yipunu*) — Yipunu is spoken in Gabon and the Republic of Congo by about 120 thousand native speakers, including 8 thousand in southern French Congo.
- **Yisira name** (*Yisira*) — Yisira is another name for Yipunu.
- **Punu god** (*Nyambye*) — The Punu believe in Nyambye, their god.
- **Evil spirits** — The Punu fear evil spirits and associate illness with their action.
- **Wizard blamed for unexplained death** (*mulosi*) — A mulosi was held responsible when the cause of a death could not be determined.
