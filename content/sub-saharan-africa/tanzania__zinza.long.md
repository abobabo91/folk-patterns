## Overview

The Zinza — *Wazinza* in Swahili — are a Bantu ethnolinguistic group of northwestern Tanzania, living on the southwestern islands of Lake Victoria and along the adjacent lakeshore. The sources place them in the Chato District and Geita District of Geita Region and in Mwanza Region; the language survey literature describes their speech area as the southern shore of the lake. In 1987 the Zinza population was estimated at 138,000. Their language, Zinza — also recorded as *Dzinda* and referred to in its own lexicon as *Luzinza* — is Bantu, and has been documented in a trilingual Zinza–Swahili–English lexicon and in grammatical and tonological studies. For this atlas the Zinza matter chiefly through a small group of British Museum objects registered in 1950: plaited grass containers and head cushions worked in black and white, a decorated calabash, a soapstone-bowled pipe and a spotted snuff bottle.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources contain no record of Zinza weaving, dyeing or bark-cloth production. What they do document is a plaited-grass tradition, and its patterning is the group's clearest surviving design vocabulary in this collection.

**Black-and-white plaited grass basketry** — Baskets plaited from grass and patterned in black and white, together with a matching plaited lid, registered at the British Museum in 1950. The colour scheme is carried in the plaiting itself rather than applied afterwards.

**Twilled head cushion** — A circular plaited head-ring, also in black and white, but worked in twilled plait so that the two colours resolve into a diagonally striped effect. The twill is what separates this piece from the plain-plaited baskets beside it: the same two-colour palette, a different structural rhythm.

**Incised calabash ornament** — A yellow calabash bearing fine incised ornament that has been burnt into the gourd wall, a decorative technique entirely distinct from the plaited work.

**Motif vocabulary.** The catalogue records name no motifs by name. The decorative effects it does describe are black-and-white plaited patterning, the diagonal stripe produced by twilled plait, fine burnt incision on calabash, and spots on a snuff bottle.

### Clothing & dress

The sources used here — the Wikipedia articles on the Zinza people and the Zinza language, and the British Museum catalogue records for the objects shown — say nothing about Zinza dress, either everyday or ceremonial. No garment names, head coverings, belts or footwear appear in them. The indexed search terms include several Great Lakes garment and cloth names, but none of them is attested for the Zinza in any source available for this profile, so none is repeated here as fact.

### Architecture

The sources do not describe Zinza houses, building materials, roof forms or settlement layout. They locate the group geographically — on the southwestern islands of Lake Victoria and on the shores of Chato and Geita Districts in Geita Region, and in Mwanza Region — but say nothing of the built environment there. One of the language studies is a lexical-change case study of Kome Island in Sengerema District, which confirms an island settlement as a Zinza-speaking place; it is cited for language rather than architecture.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum objects shown for the Zinza are containers, a headload cushion and smoking equipment, and they are the fullest material record the sources provide.

**Plaited grass containers** — Two grass baskets and a plaited lid, all patterned in black and white, registered in 1950.

**Head-ring** — A circular grass head cushion in twilled plait, the pad placed on the head to carry loads.

**Calabash vessel** — A yellow gourd vessel carrying fine incised, burnt ornament.

**Tobacco pipe** — A smoking pipe combining a wooden stem with a bowl of black soapstone; the bowl's socket is bound with copper wire, the one metal element recorded in the group.

**Snuff bottle** — A snuff bottle ornamented with spots, showing that snuff and smoked tobacco were both in use.

### Jewelry & body adornment

No jewelry, beadwork, tattooing, scarification or hairdressing practice is described in the sources. The only personal-use objects recorded are the tobacco pipe and the snuff bottle listed above, and the head-ring, which is a carrying pad rather than an ornament.

## Music & performance

The sources used for this profile contain no information on Zinza instruments, song genres or performance occasions, and Tanzania has no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions that could be consulted here. The linguistic literature does touch on one sound-related subject, though not a musical one: David Odden's study of the phrasal tonology of Zinza, published in the *Journal of African Languages and Linguistics* in 2000, analyses the tone system of the spoken language.

## Dance & theatre

The sources say nothing about Zinza dance, masquerade or dramatic performance. No named dance, ceremonial or recreational, appears in the Wikipedia articles or in the museum catalogue records for the objects shown.

## Festivals & rituals

Neither the Wikipedia articles nor the museum records describe a Zinza festival calendar or any life-cycle rite — birth, initiation, marriage or funeral. There are no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage elements listed for Tanzania to supply seasonal or ceremonial material. The objects in this collection are domestic and personal rather than explicitly ceremonial: baskets and a lid, a load-carrying head cushion, a calabash, a pipe and a snuff bottle. The catalogue records do not assign any of them a ritual function, and none is claimed here.

## Foodways

The sources describe no Zinza staples, dishes, cooking methods or drinking customs. Three of the recorded objects are nonetheless food- and consumption-related in form: the two plaited grass baskets with their plaited lid, which are storage or carrying containers, and the yellow calabash vessel, a gourd container with burnt incised ornament. Tobacco appears twice — as a pipe with a wooden stem and black soapstone bowl, and as a spotted snuff bottle — but the records say nothing about when or by whom either was used.

## Oral tradition & literature

No folktale, epic, proverb or riddle tradition is recorded for the Zinza in the sources consulted. What does exist is a documentary and lexicographic literature in and about the language: Yunus Ismail Rubanza's *Luzinza* lexicon and his Zinza–Swahili–English and English–Zinza–Swahili dictionary, both published in 2008 in the Languages of Tanzania series by the Department of Foreign Languages and Linguistics at the University of Dar es Salaam; Shannon Ronit Odom's *The Grammar Basics of Zinza*, issued by SIL International in 2016; and Bahati Kulwa's 2016 University of Dodoma MA thesis assessing lexical change in Zinza on Kome Island, Sengerema District. Together these constitute the group's principal modern preservation effort in print.

## Language & religion

Zinza, also written *Dzinda* and called *Luzinza* in its own lexicon, is a Bantu language of Tanzania spoken on the southern shore of Lake Victoria. Swahili renders the people's name *Wazinza*. The language has been described grammatically by SIL International and analysed tonally by David Odden, who treated its phrasal tonology; a case study of Kome Island in Sengerema District examined lexical change in it, which implies contact pressure on the vocabulary. The lexicographic work pairs Zinza with both Swahili and English, the two languages of wider communication in the region. The sources record no dialect divisions, no historical script, and nothing at all about Zinza religion, denomination or spiritual practice.
