## Overview

The Sukuma are Tanzania's largest ethnic group, occupying the dry plains and granite-strewn savanna south and east of Lake Victoria. Historians argue that Sukuma identity, assembled over centuries from Bantu farmers and pastoral peoples, is relatively recent rather than ancient. Before colonial rule the region was divided among dozens of small chiefdoms headed by an ntemi whose authority rested on ritual guardianship of rain, land and herds. Today the Sukuma are known above all for their extraordinarily developed complex of competitive dance societies, especially the rival Bagalu and Bagika, whose contests structure village life and absorb enormous expenditure on medicine, costume and carved objects.

## Material culture

Sukuma material culture centers on the homestead and herds, the agricultural tool, and the dance arena, with objects typically made for use but becoming elaborate in ceremonial display.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Sukuma are not a weaving people; pattern is carried on beadwork, basketry, hide and body decoration rather than on woven cloth.

- **Trade cloth** (*kanga, kitenge*) — Printed cotton wrappers bought at market are the everyday textile; the region's own cotton is grown for export mills rather than local handweaving.
- **Beaded panels and belts** — Geometric beadwork in red, white, blue and black bands decorates dance belts, headbands, bandoliers and gourd containers.
- **Coiled basketry** — Coiled and plaited grass baskets, granary linings and winnowing trays carry banded and spiralling patterns made by women.
- **Dance costume assemblage** (*mabina*) — Competition dress combines cloth, hide, beadwork, feathers, bells, animal skins and fibre skirts in an additive, startling aesthetic.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress today is Tanzanian national and Swahili-coastal; distinctively Sukuma dress survives in dance society costume, healer dress and chiefship regalia.

- **Chiefly regalia** (*ntemi regalia*) — The ntemi's insignia commonly included a stool, staff or spear, leopard or cat skin, copper and iron ornaments, and custody of chiefdom drums.
- **Elder's dress** (*samba ya mzee*) — A sober wrapper, staff and fly-whisk mark the seniority of a household head at gatherings and adjudications.
- **Dance headdress** — Feathered, horned or fur headpieces, often with colobus or goat hair, distinguish dance groups and their specialisms.
- **Ankle bells and rattles** — Iron or tin bells and seed rattles bound at the ankle and waist make the dancer's body part of the percussion ensemble.

### Architecture

Sukuma building is an architecture of the homestead rather than the monument, with dispersed family compounds organizing around herds and grazing.

- **Homestead** (*kaya*) — The family compound comprises dwellings for the household head and each wife, grain stores and a stock enclosure, usually sited near grazing and water.
- **Cattle enclosure** — A thorn-brush or stake kraal at the heart or edge of the compound into which the herd is driven at night; its size is a direct statement of household standing.
- **Granary** — A raised or plinth-set grain store with a removable thatch cap, protecting sorghum, millet and maize from damp and vermin.
- **Communal building party** (*nzengo*) — Houses, granaries and field work are commonly raised by reciprocal work parties fed with beer and accompanied by song.
- **Bujora Cultural Centre** — The mid-twentieth-century complex near Mwanza developed with the Catholic mission, incorporating royal and dance-society pavilions and a church with Sukuma motifs and a drum-shaped altar.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Iron and clay are the classic Sukuma crafts, with the hoe functioning as an emblem of labour, marriage payment and dance.

- **Iron hoe** (*mashona*) — A broad-bladed iron hoe with tanged fitting into a wooden haft, used for heavy tillage and historically circulating as wealth and bridewealth.
- **Women's pottery** — Hand-built, coil-raised earthenware fired in open bonfires: cooking pots, water jars and beer pots, often burnished and decorated with incised or roulette bands.
- **Carved figures** (*ndono*) — Wooden figure sculpture including tall figures displayed or carried in dance competitions, whose ownership formed part of a group's competitive arsenal.
- **Gourd vessels** — Calabashes for milk, beer and medicine, sometimes beaded, pyro-engraved or fitted with hide collars; milk gourds are treated with particular care in pastoral households.
- **Medicine containers and charms** (*bugota*) — Horn, gourd and hide containers for compounded medicines, including the reputed lubango substances credited with attracting audiences and controlling snakes.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ornament is mostly of beads, iron, copper, brass wire, cowrie and animal material, worn in quantity for ceremonies and dance.

- **Beaded collars and bandoliers** — Multi-strand beaded neckpieces and crossed chest bands worn by dancers and by women at weddings and initiations.
- **Wire coils and bangles** — Coiled brass, copper and iron worn on wrists, upper arms and ankles; heavy sets denote wealth and were often given at marriage.
- **Cowrie ornament** — Cowrie shells sewn to headbands, belts and figures, retaining older Indian Ocean trade prestige.

## Music & performance

Sukuma music is inseparable from labour and competition, with the ngoma — a named repertoire with its own songs, steps, medicines and paraphernalia — as the organizing unit.

- **Hoe dance** (*bugobogobo*) — Cultivators hoe in ranks to song and then carry hoes into choreographed display, brandishing and balancing them; groups competed to finish fields fastest and most beautifully.
- **Seated song contest** (*wigashe*) — Rival choirs, singing without leaving their benches, trade extended topical, boasting and satirical verses; a genre prized for verbal invention and commentary on history and politics.
- **Hide drums** (*ngoma, hiyo*) — Range from deep single-headed vessel drums to smaller high-pitched instruments, struck with hand and stick in interlocking patterns.
- **Plucked and bowed lutes** — One-string and multi-string instruments of the Sukuma–Nyamwezi type provide accompaniment for solo praise and narrative singing.

## Dance & theatre

The defining institution is the pair of rival dance societies, Bagalu and Bagika, whose membership cuts across chiefdoms and whose rivalry has structured Sukuma performance for over a century.

- **Bagalu** — One of the two rival dance societies, attributed to a founding medicine-man remembered as Ng'wana Malundi, whose repertoire and medicines compete for spectators.
- **Bagika** — The counterpart rival dance society, competing with Bagalu through repertoire, medicines, charms and objects believed to pull spectators from a rival's arena.
- **Snake dance** (*bayeye*) — Dance specialists handle pythons and venomous snakes before the crowd, their confidence credited to lubango medicine; one of the most notorious attractions.
- **Porcupine complex** (*bunuunguli, mbina ya bunuunguli*) — Some groups keep porcupines or display carved figures, drugged animals and other secret attractions unveiled at the climax of performance.
- **Young men's associations** (*busumba*) — Traditional organizing frame for dance and performance, combining labour, mutual aid, courtship and competitive display for the unmarried.

## Festivals & rituals

The Sukuma ceremonial year has no single dominant festival; ritual clusters around rain, the agricultural calendar, life cycle and the ancestors.

- **Rain rites** — Offerings at chiefdom shrines, ancestral graves and conspicuous rocks and trees; rainmaking was the central public duty of the ntemi.
- **Ancestral veneration** (*masamva*) — The dead are addressed as masamva, ancestral spirits whose neglect brings illness and misfortune, and who are appeased with libations of beer and milk and offerings of flour.
- **Divination and healing** (*mfumu, bafumu*) — Diviner-healers diagnose through chicken entrails, thrown objects, dreams and possession, prescribe bugota medicine, and are often dance-society specialists.
- **Marriage and bridewealth** — Marriage is negotiated between lineages with bridewealth paid principally in cattle; funerals for senior people bring large gatherings, extended mourning and commemorative feasting.

## Foodways

The Sukuma table rests on stiff grain porridge eaten with a relish, a pattern shared across eastern Africa but inflected by cattle and lake resources.

- **Maize porridge** (*bugali*) — A firm porridge of maize meal dipped into sauce of leafy greens, beans, groundnuts, tomato and onion, or on better days into meat or fish; historically sorghum or millet.
- **Lake fish** (*dagaa*) — Tilapia, Nile perch and small dried sardines are central in northern districts, with dagaa traveling far inland as a cheap protein.
- **Milk and dairy** — Cattle supply milk, soured milk and ghee; meat is slaughtered chiefly for funerals, commemorations and major hospitality, distributed by seniority.
- **Grain beer** (*pombe*) — Essential social substance brewed by women from sorghum, millet or maize; pays the work party, opens negotiations, honours the ancestor and fuels the dance.

## Oral tradition & literature

Kisukuma oral literature is rich in genres that reward verbal competition: folktales, riddles, proverbs, praise recitation and above all song texts.

- **Dance society song** (*wigashe, bugobogobo*) — Song-leaders compose repertoires that carry a running poetic record of grievance, gossip and history — taxation, cattle-culling, cotton prices, migration, politics — with reputations resting on invention and cutting allusion.
- **Folktales and riddles** — Narrative folktales with animal tricksters and moral reversals, and riddles exchanged in the evening, are traditional genres still performed.
- **Traditions of origin** — Accounts of Ng'wana Malundi and the founding of the two societies circulate as narrative and as claim, since a society's charter is also its argument for precedence.

## Language & religion

Kisukuma is a vigorous Bantu language of the Sukuma–Nyamwezi group, though Swahili dominates schooling and administration; Christian and Muslim identities layer over older cosmology centered on the ancestral masamva.

- **Kisukuma language** — A Bantu language of the Sukuma–Nyamwezi group spoken across Sukumaland with substantial internal dialect variation, mutually intelligible with Nyamwezi and written in Latin script.
- **Catholicism** — Especially strong around Mwanza as a consequence of long Missionaries of Africa activity; other Christian confessions include Anglican, Lutheran and Pentecostal communities.
- **Ancestral cosmology** (*masamva*) — A distant creator associated with the sky, and the immediate, demanding presence of ancestral masamva, whose consultation through diviners and propitiation continues alongside baptism and church membership.
- **Witchcraft and healing** — Witchcraft accusation remains socially consequential; healers, medicines and charms are central to life and art, addressing hidden causation and misfortune.

## Glossary

- *Bagalu* — One of two rival dance societies; attributed to founding medicine-man Ng'wana Malundi
- *Bagika* — Rival dance society competing with Bagalu for spectators and prestige
- *bugobogobo* — The hoe dance of farming work parties, with choreographed display of cultivating tools
- *bugali* — Stiff maize meal porridge, the staple grain dish
- *bugota* — Compounded medicines prescribed by healers, stored in horn, gourd and hide containers
- *bunuunguli* — Porcupine complex; animals displayed as secret attractions in dance competitions
- *busumba* — Young men's associations organizing labour, mutual aid, courtship and performance
- *dagaa* — Small dried sardines from Lake Victoria, cheap protein traveled far inland
- *kaya* — Family homestead compound with dwellings, granaries and stock enclosure
- *lubango* — Reputed medicine credited with attracting spectators and controlling snakes
- *mabina* — Dance competition costume combining cloth, hide, beads, feathers, bells and animal skins
- *masamva* — Ancestral spirits whose neglect brings illness and misfortune; addressed with libations and offerings
- *mfumu* — Diviner-healer who diagnoses through entrails, dreams and possession and prescribes medicines
- *ndono* — Carved wooden figures displayed or carried in dance competitions as part of competitive arsenal
- *ngoma* — Hide drum; also a named repertoire owned by a dance group with its own songs, steps and medicines
- *ntemi* — Chiefdom leader whose authority rested on ritual guardianship of rain, land and herds
- *nzengo* — Communal building party for houses, granaries and field work, fed with beer and accompanied by song
- *pombe* — Grain beer brewed from sorghum, millet or maize; essential social substance and women's income
- *samba ya mzee* — Elder's dress: wrapper, staff and fly-whisk marking seniority of a household head
- *wigashe* — Seated song contest in which rival choirs trade topical, boasting and satirical verses
