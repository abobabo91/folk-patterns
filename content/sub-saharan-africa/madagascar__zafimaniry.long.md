## Overview

The Zafimaniry are a sub-group of the Betsileo of Madagascar, living in the forested mountains of the southern central highlands southeast of Ambositra, between the Betsileo to the west and the Tanala. Their country sits at 1,000–1,800 metres, a highland rainforest that is cold, damp and cloudy, receiving 2,000–3,000 mm of rain a year and only around 100 rain-free days. Roughly 25,000 people live in about 100 villages, of which 17 are larger; the biggest is Antoetra, with a population of around 1,000 and the only one reachable by car. They speak a dialect of Malagasy — a Malayo-Polynesian language descended from the Barito languages of southern Borneo — nearly identical to Betsileo. They matter in folk-culture terms because their woodcarving, inscribed by UNESCO in 2003, preserves a decorative idiom once common across Madagascar and now largely extinct elsewhere.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources describe Zafimaniry pattern almost entirely as carved wood rather than cloth; the textile record here is the woven burial cloth held in the British Museum, plus a note that traditional clothing was made of beaten bark cloth.

**Lambamena** — a bast burial cloth woven of raffia: a rectangle of brown raffia with dark brown and beige stripes running down the centre and a fringe at each short side. It is the only Zafimaniry textile in the catalogue recorded under a vernacular name.

**Burial shawls** — further burial cloths woven of vegetable fibre, some a single rectangle, some two rectangles sewn together lengthwise. Their patterning is striped along the length: two sets of green and yellow stripes on brown; beige, green and burgundy; or beige with two sets of dark brown stripes. Each carries a fringe at both short ends.

**Bark cloth** — traditional clothing was made of beaten bark cloth. The sources give no further detail on its preparation or decoration.

**Plaited grass and fibre** — the woven, non-cloth end of the same skill: a grass mat carrying an inscription, a winnowing tray of vegetable fibre and a hat of vegetable fibre are all in the collection.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no individual motifs. They record only that houses and wooden household items are decorated with symbolic geometric patterns blending Arab and Austronesian influences.

### Clothing & dress

The sources say very little about Zafimaniry dress. Traditional clothing was made of beaten bark cloth, and the British Museum holds a hat of vegetable fibre and a comb of wood. Nothing in the sources distinguishes men's from women's dress, names garment types, or describes belts, footwear or a ceremonial costume separate from daily wear. The one clothing-like object the sources treat at length belongs to death rather than life: the raffia and vegetable-fibre burial cloths and shawls described above, several of them catalogued as shawls, striped along their length and fringed at both ends.

### Architecture

Zafimaniry houses are built entirely of wood, without nails, and this is where the group's reputation rests. Unlike most other Malagasy ethnic groups, the Zafimaniry invest durability and decoration in the house rather than the tomb. The structural members are themselves the carved surface: the British Museum holds house poles and house-posts, including a central house post from a Zafimaniry house in two sections, carved with designs on its front face, and carved wooden windows, one of them recorded under the vernacular name *varankoaka*. Bird figures carved in wood are catalogued as fittings for a house pole. The decoration is geometric and symbolic, blending Arab and Austronesian influences, and the value the Zafimaniry place on monogamous marriage and family stability is expressed symbolically in how the house is built and ornamented. The woodworkers draw on over 20 species of tree, each with its own properties and uses. Deforestation has cut the supply of wood and with it the viability of the craft; this way of building was once widespread across Madagascar and now survives mainly here.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The catalogue is overwhelmingly wooden, and almost every domestic function is met in carved wood: bowls, a mortar, a stool, a folding chair made as a separate seat and back, a square box with its lid, and a comb. Several honey pots survive, some as a vessel alone and some as vessel with lid, matching the gathering of wild honey from the surrounding forest. Fire-lighting equipment is made of wood and fibre; a smoking pipe combines wood and horn; a winnowing tray is plaited from vegetable fibre. Weapons and their fittings follow the same material logic — a sword of wood and iron, a wooden sheath, and a dart of wood and fibre. Fire is applied to harden the wood used for blowpipes and arrows. The sources record no Zafimaniry ceramics.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources used do not cover Zafimaniry jewelry, tattooing or hair practice; the only personal object in the catalogue is a wooden comb.

## Music & performance

One instrument is named: the *lokanga*, much favoured among the Zafimaniry, whose shape is symbolic of a coffin. That single detail carries the group's characteristic linking of everyday objects to death and ancestry — the same logic by which a boy about to be circumcised is ritually measured for his coffin. The sources name no song genres, no named repertoire and no professional performers, and describe no performance context other than the funeral. Madagascar has no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions in the material supplied here; the Zafimaniry element on the Representative List is their woodworking knowledge, not a musical tradition.

## Dance & theatre

The one performance tradition the sources record belongs to the funeral: ritualized wrestling matches take place at Zafimaniry funeral ceremonies, and these often resemble a form of dance. The sources name no other dances, no mask or puppet traditions and no theatrical forms, and draw no distinction between ceremonial and entertainment performance.

## Festivals & rituals

Two life-cycle rites are documented. Circumcision is bound to death: the child to be circumcised is ritually measured for his coffin before the operation, an association the sources treat as an old Malagasy practice retained here after dying out elsewhere on the island. Funerals entomb the dead in wooden tombs placed in the forest, and the ceremonies include the wrestling matches described above. The Zafimaniry also practise the ancient tradition of erecting tall standing stones to commemorate an ancestor or an event in that ancestor's life.

Marriage and family are the axis of ritual life rather than a fixed calendar. Monogamous marriage is seen as central to social stability, and the house is built and decorated to say so. Women marry much younger than men; young men pass through an extended adolescence of hunting and long-distance trading. Pairings among the young are fluid and even a formal marriage can dissolve without complication — it is the birth of children that fixes a relationship, and parents are judged more successful the more children they raise. Elders, *raiamandreny*, lead by example, speaking calmly and politely with reference to proverbs and to the respect owed the ancestors. The agricultural year has one fixed act: fields are burned each year at the start of the planting season. The sources name no annual festival.

## Foodways

Maize is the staple, adopted around 200 years ago, grown with beans under *tavy*, slash-and-burn cultivation. The average Zafimaniry man spends 22 days a year clearing land by *tavy*, leaving the rest of his time for carving or seasonal migrant work. A field is burned at the start of the planting season and cultivated four to ten years until its fertility fails, then left fallow about five years; as it weakens, maize and beans give way to potatoes and sweet potatoes, with cassava the last crop before fallow. During fallow the plot grazes cattle and grows medicinal plants and fibre and construction species. After about seventy years of this cycle the land is retired to permanent fallow pasture. Taro joins maize and beans, and wild forest products supplement them — honey above all, which the carved honey pots in the collection were made to hold, and crayfish. Rice, the Malagasy staple elsewhere, has only recently been taken up: land exhaustion forced the attempt, and warming has made the cold highlands more workable, but the terracing and irrigation needed on this terrain are beyond local knowledge, so Betsileo specialists are hired to build the paddies. Most cash income goes into those terraces. The sources name no prepared dish and no drink tradition.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral form appears in the sources as the manner of elders rather than as a named repertoire: *raiamandreny*, the older family members, are respected leaders who model harmonious communal living by speaking calmly and politely with reference to proverbs and to the respect due to ancestors. The sources name no epic, no folktale cycle, no riddle tradition and no storytelling occasion, and record no contemporary literary revival. What preservation effort the sources do document is directed at the woodworking: its inscription on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, and the value historians place on the carving as evidence for the applied arts of Madagascar's past.

## Language & religion

The Zafimaniry speak Malagasy, a Malayo-Polynesian language within the Barito group of southern Borneo; their dialect is nearly identical to that of the Betsileo who border them to the west. The sources record no script. Zafimaniry culture originally had no well-defined religion: belief centred on respect for ancestors and on *Zanahary*, rendered "God" by European missionaries but in fact an indifferent, ever-present force closer to fate, manifested through the forces of nature. From the late 1800s the London Missionary Society converted free Zafimaniry villages to Protestantism; Jesuit missionaries worked heavily among them in the mid-1930s and drew their converts especially from the recently freed slave class, who became the main Catholic population. That division still shapes the economy: Catholic ex-slaves organised the mass production of carved handicrafts for sale and dominated the trade until the 1970s.
