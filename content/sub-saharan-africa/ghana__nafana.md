## Overview

The Nafana are a Senufo people living in the central north-west of Ghana and the north-east of Côte d’Ivoire, in the area east of Bondoukou. They are mainly farmers and speak Nafaanra, a Senufo language. Their communities include Sampa, Kokoa, Duadaso No 1, Duadaso No 2, Jamera or Jaamala, Debibi, Kabile, Brodi, and Banda Ahenkro. The Nafana say that they came from Kakala in Côte d’Ivoire, and their oral history holds that some of their people remain there. Their cultural history is associated in the sources with the Banda area, the Songhei Festival, and Jamera, described as a town where their history and traditions are based.

## Festivals & rituals

The Nafana people’s major festival is the Songhei Festival, celebrated annually and associated mainly with Jamala or Jamera. The sources describe Jamera as the main cultural heritage town of the Nafana, where their history and traditions are based. The sources also mention Tolee Sahfufuo, described as one of the first kings and as having fought for the liberation of the Nafana people in the area against the Gyaman kings.

## Oral tradition & literature

Nafana oral history says that the people came from a village called Kakala in Côte d’Ivoire. It also says that some of their people remain there and would not be allowed to leave again if they returned. The sources connect the Nafana arrival in the Banda area with the earlier arrival of the Ligbi people, who came from Begho, also called Bigu or Bighu, in the early 17th century. The sources further associate Nafana history with the Songhai empire and with Tolee Sahfufuo, who is described as having fought against the Gyaman kings. Several books of Nafana folk tales have been published by the Summer Institute of Linguistics.

## Language & religion

Nafaanra, also written Nafaara and known as Nafanan or Nafana, is a Senufo language spoken in north-west Ghana along the border with Côte d’Ivoire, east of Bondoukou. Its speakers call themselves Nafana, while others call them Banda or Mfantera. The language has two dialectal variants: Pantera of Banda and Fantera of Sampa; the Banda dialect is considered central. The sources describe Nafaanra as tonal, with three distinct tones, and as having a subject–object–verb word order. It has a noun-class system with five genders, and its grammar marks tense and aspect through particles and verb forms.
