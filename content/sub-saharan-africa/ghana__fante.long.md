## People and place

Fante communities are concentrated along Ghana's coast and its nearby hinterland. The museum sources place objects in Ghana and identify their makers as Fante. A label such as ?Akan? alone is broader: it does not establish that a specific object was made by a Fante artist. That distinction matters here, because Akan, Asante, and Fante objects are often displayed together.

## Asafo companies and their flags

The Smithsonian describes *asafo* as a long-standing Akan institution especially developed among coastal Fante communities. Companies have names, leaders, regalia, and shrines. They participate in civic and political life as well as ceremonies. Their flags, sometimes called *frankaa*, are made by cutting, sewing, and appliqu?ing colored cloth; some have embroidery or fringe. A flag could identify a company, show a proverb, or make a claim about its standing among rivals.

The British Museum's flag Af1978,22.715 uses an elephant, a palm, other animals, and a person. Its catalogue connects the elephant and palm to a proverb about strength. The many flags in this collection differ in imagery and cloth, and several include a small Union Jack. Such a canton helps date a flag to before Ghana's 1957 independence, as the Smithsonian explains, but it does not mean that the cloth was made in Britain. The Brooklyn Museum identifies its own Asafo Company Flag as Fante and reads its dramatic creature as a warning to opponents. Interpretations of other flag scenes should be tied to the particular company and museum record, not guessed from the image alone.

## Carving and clay

The selected wooden *akua'ba* figures have elongated bodies and tall, roughly rectangular heads. The British Museum notes that one was once registered under an Asante label but is now attributed to Fante makers; it also records an older comparison between Fante and Asante figure types. This is a reminder that a familiar Akan form is insufficient evidence for a narrower maker identity. These are museum-identified Fante examples, not a template for every carved figure in the region.

Several terracotta heads and vessels came from the Agona Swedru area. The British Museum describes part of that 1933 acquisition as excavated funerary equipment and attributes it to Fante production. A head, jug, bowl, or pedestal vessel should still be understood through its own record. The excavated context does not by itself explain how every vessel was used, and the objects should not be treated as typical household ware without further evidence.

## Sound, regalia, and portraits

The British Museum attributes the selected carved drums to Fante makers. One has a wooden body, a hide membrane, and painted decoration. A long staff and a painted ceremonial paddle show further forms of public display, though the short catalogue descriptions leave their original occasions of use uncertain. Two historical photographs explicitly identify their sitters as Fante or Fanti; they document particular people and dress rather than a timeless costume.

## Reading this collection

Most objects here are historical museum holdings, and flags, ceremonial objects, and excavated material are overrepresented. Some catalogue records preserve the maker attribution but little about who commissioned an object or how it was used. The selection excludes items the museum assigns to Assin or Asante makers, records attributed to several peoples, and a drum described as Fante *or* Asante. Its purpose is to show identifiable Fante work without turning a broader Ghanaian or Akan tradition into a specific maker claim.

## Glossary

- *Asafo* ? A company with civic, political, and ceremonial roles.
- *Frankaa* ? An *asafo* company flag.
- *Akua'ba* ? A carved figure; selected Fante examples have tall, rectangular heads.
- *Appliqu?* ? Cut cloth shapes sewn onto a fabric ground.
