## Overview

The Pedi, also called Bapedi, are a Sotho-Tswana ethnic group native to South Africa. They are primarily situated in Limpopo and Gauteng, with the present-day Pedi area, Sekhukhuneland, located between the Olifants River (Lepelle) and the Steelpoort River (Tubatse), bordered by the Drakensberg range and crossed by the Leolo mountains. The Pedi belong to the Bantu language group and speak Sepedi, while Northern Sotho is an umbrella term for related dialects. The 2022 South African Census recorded approximately 6.2 million first-language speakers of Northern Sotho. Pedi culture includes beadwork, pottery, metalworking, woodworking, house building and painting, music, dance, initiation institutions and communal agricultural practices.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Beaded animal-fur apron** — British Museum records describe an apron made of animal fur, with a wide central band, narrow side strips and ties. Its exterior is decorated with predominantly white glass beads, together with red, black, dark blue and orange beads, including beaded discs and a central diamond-shaped decoration.

**Beaded fur apron with waistband** — Another apron has a doubled-over fur waistband, twisted cotton strings coated with red earth, alternating white and dark blue triangular bead arrangements, and additional fur decorated with white, mid-blue and red glass beads.

**Grass belt** — A belt in the museum records is made of grass on a fibre string.

Important documented crafts included beadwork, pottery, house building and painting, woodworking, metalsmithing and drum making.

**Motif vocabulary.** Diamond-shaped beaded decoration; triangular bead arrangement; circular beaded discs.

### Clothing & dress

The sources provide limited information about everyday and ceremonial dress. Museum records document aprons made of animal fur, including one with a wide central band, narrow side strips and ties, and another with a fur waistband, twisted cotton strings, red earth coating and glass-bead decoration. The records also identify a grass belt on a fibre string. The sources do, however, describe women’s traditional dance, in which women dance on their knees and perform vigorous movement of the upper torso; they do not specify a separate dress associated with this performance.

### Architecture

Before conquest, people lived on elevated sites in relatively large villages divided into *kgoro*, groups centered on agnatic family clusters. A settlement included households arranged around a central area used as a meeting place, cattle byre, graveyard and ancestral shrine. Houses were huts, and each wife in a polygynous marriage had her own round thatched hut. Open-air enclosures, or *lapa*, connected the huts and were surrounded by mud walls. Older boys and girls lived in separate huts. Later, many families replaced round huts with rectangular houses with flat tin roofs. Newer settlements and the outskirts of older settlements often consisted of houses arranged in grid formation.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pedi crafts included pottery, metalsmithing, woodworking and drum making. The British Museum records include bowls made of pottery, including a red ceramic bowl with incised markings around the rim and black-painted designs on the body. Other objects include wooden spoons, carved wooden meat trays and dishes, coiled-grass-and-cane dish covers, and food baskets woven from tree creeper. One basket has reddish-brown sections dyed with an extract from the root of another creeper, while another has small dark-brown triangular motifs. The records also include a double meat tray and a triple circular meat tray with short legs.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources document extensive glass-bead decoration on animal-fur aprons, including white, red, black, dark blue, orange and mid-blue beads arranged in borders, discs, triangular patterns and a central diamond-shaped design.

## Music & performance

Pedi music, *mmino wa Setšo*, traditionally uses a six-note scale. It was traditionally played on reeds and is now also played on a jaw harp or autoharp. Migrants influenced by Kibala music play aluminum pipes of different heights to reproduce vocal harmonies.

The principal musical expression described in the sources is **Dinaka/Kiba**. In its men’s version, an ensemble of players uses aluminum end-blown pipes called *naka*, plural *dinaka*. Each pipe has a different pitch, and together the pipes produce a descending melody that imitates traditional vocal songs with harmonized qualities. The women’s version includes songs called *koša ya dikhuru*, or knee-dance music, led by a singer and accompanied by a chorus and drums called *meropa*. The drums were formerly wooden and are now also made from oil drums and milk urns.

The sources list several types of cultural music: *Mpepetlwane*, played by young girls; *Mmatšhidi*, played by older men and women; *Kiba* or *Dinaka*, played by men and boys and now also by women; *Dipela*, played by everyone; and *Makgakgasa*, played by older women. Songs were also sung collectively during work. One song concerned killing a lion in order to become a man.

## Dance & theatre

Traditional women’s dance is performed on the knees and is accompanied by drums, backing vocals and a lead singer. The women’s *koša ya dikhuru* repertoire is associated with kneeling dance and vigorous shaking movements of the upper torso. The sources describe these dances as occurring at drinking parties and celebrations such as weddings. They also describe **Dinaka/Kiba** as a combined musical and dance tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe life-cycle and social rituals. Both girls and boys underwent initiation. Boys, called *bašemane* and later *mašoboro*, spent their youth looking after cattle at remote outposts with peers and older youths. Circumcision and initiation at *koma*, an initiation school, were held about once every five years. Initiates entered cohorts or regiments called *mephato*, whose members maintained lifelong loyalty and often traveled together for work.

Girls attended their own *koma* and entered their own regiments, called *ditswa-bothuku*, usually two years after the boys. Initiation continues to provide income to chiefs who license it and to private entrepreneurs who establish schools outside chiefly jurisdiction.

Marriage was patrilocal. Polygamy was practiced especially by people of higher or chiefly status, although polygyny is now rare. Marriage between close or classificatory cousins was associated with ruling and chiefly families and with the circulation of *Magadi*, or bridewealth. Ancestors were addressed through burning incense, making offerings and speaking to them in *go phasa*. Animal sacrifice or beer could be presented when necessary.

## Foodways

The pre-conquest economy combined cattle-keeping with hoe cultivation. Principal crops were sorghum, pumpkins and legumes, grown by women on fields allocated to them when they married. Women ground grain, cooked, brewed and collected water and wood. Men herded cattle, hunted and worked in wood and metal. Major tasks were often completed communally by *matsema*, or work parties.

Museum records document household food-related objects, including woven food baskets, carved wooden meat trays and dishes, wooden spoons, and dish covers made of coiled grass and cane. One record describes a triple circular meat tray with four short legs and a stub handle; another describes a double meat tray or bowl with a short footed base.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources mention songs performed during work and a song about killing a lion to become a man. Pedi music is also discussed as a field in which scholars emphasize studying indigenous African music from the perspective and language of practitioners.

## Language & religion

Northern Sotho is a Bantu language belonging specifically to the Sotho-Tswana group. It is spoken mainly in Limpopo Province and also in Gauteng, Mpumalanga and North West. Northern Sotho is written in the Latin alphabet and uses the letter š. It is a standardized variety that amalgamates several distinct dialects, including Highveld-Sotho/Pedi and Lowveld-Sotho varieties. The sources describe continuing debate over the use of Sepedi, Northern Sotho and Sesotho sa Leboa as labels for the language community.

Pedi religious practice centers on ancestors, who are viewed as intermediaries between humans and the Creator or God, called *Modimo/Mmopi*. Communication with ancestors involves *go phasa*: burning incense, making an offering and speaking to them. The *kgadi*, usually the father’s elder sister, held an important place in family ritual. A *ngaka*, or diviner, may become one through inherited spiritual illness or possession by spirits called *malopo*.
