## Overview

The Shirazi, also known as Mbwera, are an ethnic group associated with the Swahili coast and nearby Indian Ocean islands, particularly Zanzibar, Pemba, and Comoros. Their history is connected with the spread of Islam on the Swahili Coast, the establishment of southern Swahili sultanates such as Mozambique and Angoche, commercial activity, and the development of the Swahili language. Shirazi origins remain controversial: local traditions emphasize Persian ancestral heritage, while other traditions describe Arab origins. The archaeological record rejects mass migration or colonization but documents extensive trade relations with Persia. Shirazi communities were also connected through marriage and mercantile networks with Comoros. In contemporary Tanzania, Shirazi identity has been associated with Zanzibar and with both Persian ancestry and Afro-Shirazi political identity.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Crafts associated with Shiraz.** The separate article on Shiraz culture mentions carpet-weaving, triangular inlaid mosaic work, and rugs called *gilim* or Shiraz Kilim. It also mentions blankets called *Jajim* found in villages and among tribes.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name triangular design in relation to inlaid mosaic work, but provide no documented Shirazi motif vocabulary.

### Architecture

The sources describe the Shirazi as historically connected with coastal towns, sultanates, trade, and prestigious stone houses. The upper-strata Waungwana, also called Swahili-Arabs, had the exclusive right to build prestigious stone houses. Waungwana women were confined to certain premises within these houses, called *Ndani*, in connection with the maintenance of ritual and sexual purity.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The separate article on Shiraz culture mentions silver-ware as a craft of Shiraz.

## Music & performance

The separate article on Shiraz culture states that Fars Symphony Orchestra is based in Shiraz and that Shiraz hosts concerts by famous Iranian artists. These statements concern the city of Shiraz and are not identified as traditions of the Shirazi people of Tanzania.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that most people along the Swahili coast follow the Shafi'i madhhab of Sunni Islam.

The separate article on Shiraz culture describes Iranian festivals in Shiraz. *Nowruz* is an ancient Iranian tradition celebrated annually on the vernal equinox and was registered on UNESCO’s list of Masterpieces of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2009. *Chaharshanbe Suri* is held on the eve of the last Wednesday of the preceding year and includes jumping over bonfires, firecrackers, and fireworks. *Sizdah Be-dar* occurs on the thirteenth day of the Iranian year, Farvardin 13, usually coinciding with 1 or 2 April. *Mehregan* is described as a Zoroastrian and Persian festival preserved in Shiraz for centuries.

## Foodways

They do state that Shirazi commercial activity historically included trade in spices and produce from clove, coconut, and other plantations run with slave labor.

The separate article on Shiraz culture describes *Shirazi salad*, a relatively modern Iranian dish named after Shiraz. Its primary ingredients are cucumber, tomato, onion, olive oil, herbal spices, and verjuice, with lime juice sometimes used. It also describes *Shirazi faloodeh*, or *Paloodeh*, as a special version of Faloodeh from Shiraz. These dishes are not identified as foodways of the Shirazi people of Tanzania.

## Oral tradition & literature

The origins of the Shirazi are connected in the sources with oral traditions and contested genealogies. The late nineteenth-century *Book of the Zanj*, or *Kitab al-Zanuj*, is described as a likely compilation of mythical oral traditions and memories of settled traders on the Swahili coast. It attributes the establishment of the Shirazi dynasty to Madagan and Halawani Arab merchants, although their identity and roots are unclear. A critical assessment cited in the source describes much of the document as deliberate falsification intended to invalidate established oral traditions of local Bantu groups.

Recorded nineteenth-century clan and town traditions instead emphasize that early Shirazi settlers had Persian ancestral heritage. Swahili elites also developed fictive or real genealogies linking themselves to early Muslims in Arabia or Persia.

## Language & religion

The Shirazi speak local dialects of Swahili as a mother tongue. Swahili belongs to the Bantu branch of the Niger-Congo family. The source describes Swahili dialects as syncretic, blending Sabaki Bantu, Comoro, Pokomo, Persian, English, Arabic, and Indian words and structure. Comorian is divided into western and eastern language groups. The western group consists of Shingazidja and Shimwali; the eastern group consists of Shindzwani and Shimaore. Shingazidja is spoken on Ngazidja, Shindzwani on Ndzwani, Shimaore on Mayotte, and Shimwali on Mwali. Comorian languages use a modified version of the Arabic script.

Most people along the Swahili coast follow the Shafi'i madhhab of Sunni Islam. Islam influenced Shirazi identity and social structures, including the category *Sawahil*, described as “free but landless” people who had adopted Islam. The sources also describe Bantu influences and continuing distinctions connected with race, class, economic status, and region of origin.
