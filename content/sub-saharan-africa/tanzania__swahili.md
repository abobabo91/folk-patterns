## Overview

The Swahili are coastal traders and town-dwellers whose civilization emerged between the 8th and 13th centuries from the fusion of Bantu farmers with Arab, Persian, Indian and Portuguese merchants. They built distinctive stone towns, developed a rich manuscript and musical culture, and speak Kiswahili, now the national language of Tanzania and Kenya. Their culture reflects centuries of Indian Ocean trade in ivory, cloves, and other goods.

## Material culture

Swahili material culture reflects Indian Ocean trade: imported Chinese and Persian ceramics displayed in homes, locally-made earthenware for daily use, and distinctive carved wooden objects.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Swahili cloth traditions center on the kanga and kikoi, each with distinctive patterns and social meanings.

- **Kanga** — A printed rectangular cotton cloth sold in identical pairs, worn wrapped around the waist and over the shoulders, with a Swahili proverb or prayer printed along the border.
- **Kitenge** — A heavier waxed cotton fabric tailored into dresses, headwraps and men's shirts, with East African motif patterns.
- **Kikoi** — A woven striped cotton wrap with knotted fringe, traditionally a man's waistcloth worn under a kanzu, now also used as a shawl.

### Clothing & dress

Swahili dress blends Arab, Indian and local traditions, with men wearing the kanzu robe and women layering kanga cloths and buibui.

- **Kanzu** — An ankle-length white or cream cotton robe of Omani origin worn by men, paired with an embroidered stiff-topped cap called a kofia.
- **Kofia** — A stiff flat-topped embroidered cap with openwork whitework panels hand-embroidered in Lamu and Zanzibar, signaling a man's wealth by the density of embroidery.
- **Buibui** — A full-length black overgarment worn by women in public or in observant households, sometimes with a face veil.
- **Henna** (*hina*) — Fine black line-work applied to a bride's hands and feet with tamarind and lime, or simpler red henna for festival days.

### Architecture

Swahili architecture centers on coral-stone townhouses with characteristic narrow rooms, flat roofs, and the famous studded wooden door.

- **Swahili door** (*mlango wa Kiswahili*) — A heavy double-leaf hardwood door set in a carved frame with rosettes and chain designs, studded with brass bosses, and framing the home's entrance.
- **Stone Town** (*mji wa mawe*) — Coral-rag townhouses with lime-plastered walls, flat roofs on mangrove poles, and a central open court surrounded by long rooms.
- **Makuti house** (*nyumba ya makuti*) — A timber-framed vernacular dwelling with wattle-and-daub walls and a steep hipped roof thatched in split palm leaf.
- **Coral mihrab** (*mihrab*) — A pierced carved coral niche in mosque walls indicating the direction of prayer, exemplified by the Great Mosque of Kilwa.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Swahili households display imported fine ceramics as status goods while using simple local earthenware for cooking and daily life.

- **Zanzibar chest** (*sanduku*) — A hardwood dowry chest of teak or mahogany with a flat or domed lid, studded and strapped in brass, often made in Zanzibar in the 19th century.
- **Siwa horn** (*siwa*) — A ceremonial side-blown horn of ivory or cast brass used to summon the town and to sound at weddings and inaugurations.
- **Mkeka mat** (*mkeka*) — A woven mat of dyed palm strips used for sitting, praying, and wrapping the dead for burial.
- **Water jar** (*mtungi*) — A low-fired red earthenware vessel used for storing water in everyday households.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Swahili jewelry draws on shared Indian Ocean traditions of filigree and granulation, with women wearing anklets, bangles, and amulet cases.

- **Amulet case** (*hirizi*) — A cylindrical or rectangular silver case worn on a chain and holding a folded Qur'anic verse for protection.
- **Ankle bracelets** (*bangili za miguu*) — Heavy silver or gold ankle bracelets worn by coastal women as markers of wealth and status.
- **Nose stud** (*kipini*) — A small metal nose stud worn by women as part of coastal dress.
- **Turmeric paste** (*singo*) — A yellow paste of turmeric, sandalwood and rose water rubbed onto a bride's skin during seclusion.

## Music & performance

Taarab is the signature Swahili musical form, a seated orchestral genre fusing Arab, Indian and Swahili traditions.

- **Taarab** — A sung-poetry orchestral genre of the late 19th century combining Egyptian instruments (oud, qanun, nay, violin), Indian harmonium and tabla, and Swahili verse.
- **Maulidi** — Sung praise of the Prophet in Arabic and Swahili, performed with frame drums, most famously the Maulidi ya Homu and Maulidi ya Rama of Lamu.
- **Beni** — A colonial-era brass-band dance mimicking European military parade that swept the coast between the 1890s and 1930s.
- **Zither** (*zeze*) — A trough zither used in coastal music alongside other traditional instruments.

## Dance & theatre

Swahili dance is largely social and ceremonial, centered on wedding celebrations and life-cycle rituals.

- **Chakacha** — A women's wedding dance from Mombasa with rapid isolated hip movements over a 6/8 drum pattern.
- **Msondo** — A drum dance accompanying girls' initiation seclusion, teaching sexual and domestic instruction through gesture.
- **Goma** — A slow, dignified men's stick or sword dance performed in kanzu and kofia at weddings and Islamic feasts.
- **Razha** — An Omani-derived men's dance related to goma, performed at celebrations.

## Festivals & rituals

The Swahili ritual year follows the Islamic hijri calendar, with major festivals including Ramadan, Idd celebrations, and the Maulidi.

- **Lamu Maulidi** (*Maulidi*) — A week-long gathering at the Prophet's birthday drawing pilgrims from across the Indian Ocean, with sung qasidas, dhow races, and Sufi dhikr.
- **Mwaka Kogwa** — The Zanzibari Persian New Year festival at Makunduchi, opening with a ritualized stick-fight and ending with women singing bawdy songs in the fields.
- **Unyago** — Girls' initiation seclusion and instruction under a female teacher (somo), with instruction in sexual and domestic duties.
- **Kesha** — The henna night before a wedding, when elaborate henna is applied to the bride's hands and feet.

## Foodways

Swahili cuisine is an Indian Ocean creole built on Bantu staples enriched with coconut, spice and rice from monsoon trade.

- **Rice in coconut milk** (*wali wa nazi*) — Rice cooked in coconut milk, the festive staple dish of Swahili meals.
- **Pilau** — A rice dish with Persian and Indian lineage but Swahili spicing of cardamom, clove, cinnamon and black pepper.
- **Urojo** — A Zanzibar street soup of tamarind and turmeric with cassava crisps, potato bhajia and boiled egg.
- **Spiced coffee** (*kahawa chungu*) — Strong coffee spiced and served in tiny handleless cups from a long-spouted brass pot.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Swahili possess one of sub-Saharan Africa's oldest written literatures, recorded in Arabic script since the 17th century.

- **Utenzi** — A long narrative poem in quatrains of eight-syllable lines with refrain, used for religious epic, prophecy, history and modern political verse.
- **Shairi** — A shorter four-line stanza poem used in coastal poetic duels (malumbano) and printed in newspaper poetry columns.
- **Town chronicle** (*kitab*) — Prose records of town history such as the Kitab al-Sulwa of Kilwa and the Habari za Lamu and Habari za Pate.
- **Proverb** (*methali*) — A vast body of Swahili proverbs partly preserved on kanga borders, reflecting wisdom and social commentary.

## Language & religion

Kiswahili is a Bantu language with 20-30 percent loanwords from Arabic and other Indian Ocean languages; religion is Sunni Islam with strong Sufi tradition.

- **Kiswahili** — A Bantu language of the Sabaki subgroup with loanwords from Arabic, Persian, Portuguese, Hindi and English, spoken by over 100 million as a lingua franca.
- **Qadiriyya order** (*Qadiriyya*) — A Sufi Islamic order whose gatherings (hadra) and saint pilgrimages (ziyara) remain central to Swahili religious practice.
- **Shadhiliyya order** (*Shadhiliyya*) — A Sufi Islamic order active in Swahili communities alongside the Qadiriyya and Alawiyya.
- **Spirit** (*majini*) — Pre-Islamic spirits recognized in Swahili belief and propitiated through coastal healing seances (ngoma ya pepo).
