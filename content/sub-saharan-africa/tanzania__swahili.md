## Overview

The Swahili are Muslim coastal traders whose material culture blends Bantu Africa, Indian Ocean monsoon trade, and Islamic practice into a single idiom. Their towns of coral-rag stone, their printed and woven cloths, their carved wooden objects, and their oral literature in Arabic script represent one of Africa's oldest urban and literary traditions. Life follows the Islamic calendar marked by Ramadan, Maulidi celebrations, and life-cycle rites that weave Islamic and older African practice together.

## Material culture

Swahili material culture fuses local resources (coral, coconut, mangrove wood) with imported goods and vocabularies to make objects that are read and worn as statements of faith, status, and belonging.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The kanga is the signature Swahili cloth — a printed cotton sheet worn in pairs by women, unique for carrying a Swahili proverb that makes it read as much as worn.

- **Kanga** — Rectangular machine-printed cotton sheet worn in pairs by women, with a patterned border, central field, and printed Swahili proverb along one edge, used as a message in courtship, marriage, and quarrel.
- **Kikoi** — Woven men's waistcloth of striped cotton with knotted fringe, made on the Kenyan and Tanzanian coast and distinguished by its banded stripe layout.
- **Mkeka** — Plaited floor and prayer mats of split doum or coconut palm leaf, dyed in bands of red, black and natural straw, on which Swahili people sit, eat, pray, and are washed for burial.
- **Embroidered kofia cap** (*kofia*) — Cylindrical white cotton men's cap worked in dense white-on-white or coloured counted-thread embroidery, with openwork panels graded by fineness and distinguished by stitch density.
- **Kitenge** — Heavier waxed or roller-printed cotton sold by the length without the kanga's proverb, used for tailored dresses, wraps, and baby-carrying slings, shared with the wider East African interior.

### Clothing & dress

Men wear the long white kanzu robe with embroidered kofia cap and kikoi waistcloth; women wear paired kanga at home and the black buibui overgarment in public, with blouse and wrap beneath.

- **Kanzu robe** (*kanzu*) — Long white ankle-length cotton robe worn by men over kikoi or trousers, topped by the embroidered kofia, with tailored jacket or Omani waistcoat added for mosque and festival.
- **Buibui** — Black full-length overgarment with face veil or headscarf worn by women in public, becoming standard in Zanzibar and Mombasa in the early twentieth century.
- **Kisutu bridal cloth** (*kisutu*) — Heavy red-and-black patterned cloth worn by brides with heavy gold or gilt jewellery and veil for the procession.

### Architecture

The Swahili stone town is built inward-facing from coral rag and lime mortar, with narrow galleried rooms dictated by mangrove ceiling joists, ornament concentrated on elaborately carved doors and mosque niches.

- **Swahili stone house (nyumba ya mawe)** (*nyumba ya mawe*) — Inward-facing coral-rag house with raised street bench, entrance vestibule, open courtyard, and progressively private galleries ending in the innermost ndani room for childbirth and laying out the dead.
- **Swahili carved door** (*Swahili door*) — Thick-jambed and lintel door carved in lotus, rosette, chain and Qur'anic bands with brass studs and central boss, with Lamu type showing deeper foliate relief and Zanzibar type showing Gujarati square frames.
- **Pillar tombs** — Distinctively coastal funerary form marking graves and commemorating the dead.
- **Wattle-and-daub house (nyumba ya udongo)** (*nyumba ya udongo*) — Rural and poorer-quarter dwelling of wattle-and-daub on a mangrove frame with makuti thatch of plaited coconut leaf.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Swahili households bought fine imported ceramics for display, while local potters made unglazed earthenware for cooking and incense burning; prestige woodwork includes brass-studded chests and carved ebony stools.

- **Zanzibar chest** (*sanduku*) — Brass-studded teak or mahogany dowry box with iron or brass strapwork, secret drawers, and Lamu variants, a prestige woodcraft object.
- **Ebony stool** (*kiti cha mpingo*) — Carved ebony stool made by prestige woodworkers.
- **High-backed woven chair (kiti cha enzi)** (*kiti cha enzi*) — High-backed chair of woven cord and turned finials signifying office and authority.
- **Siwa horn** (*siwa*) — Great side-blown horn of ivory or cast brass, the regalia instrument of Pate and Lamu, sounded at weddings and installations.
- **Sewn-plank ship (mtepe)** (*mtepe*) — Hull planks stitched with coir and no iron nails, built by coastal shipwrights.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Gold and silver filigree jewelry, strung beads, henna patterns, and protective amulets holding Qur'anic verses form the body adornment of Swahili people, especially at marriage and ceremonial times.

- **Henna patterning (piko)** (*hina*) — Fine floral and lattice henna patterns worked on a bride's hands and feet over several nights as central body art.
- **Silver amulet case (hirizi)** (*hirizi*) — Silver amulet case holding folded Qur'anic verses, worn by men and children against jini and the evil eye.
- **Gold chain and pendant (mkufu)** (*mkufu*) — Heavy gold chain and pendant worn as part of bridal jewelry made by coastal goldsmiths using filigree and granulation.
- **Hinged silver anklet and bangle (bangili)** (*bangili*) — Hinged silver anklets and bangles worn as bridal jewelry.
- **Beads (shanga)** (*shanga*) — Coral, amber, carnelian and imported Venetian glass beads strung for waist strings and necklaces.

## Music & performance

Taarab is the signature Swahili genre — sung poetry orchestra of Zanzibar and Mombasa — with Swahili verse set over oud, qanun, violin, accordion and drums, consolidated in clubs from the 1900s and carried worldwide.

- **Taarab** — Sung poetry orchestra of Zanzibar and Mombasa setting Swahili verse in strict metre over oud, qanun, violin, accordion, ney, double bass and dumbak, consolidated in clubs from 1905.
- **Kidumbak** — Rural Zanzibari cousin to taarab using small clay-pot drums, tin-box bass, violin and chorus, sharper-tongued in style.
- **Beni brass-band music** (*Beni*) — Brass-band music genre filling the life-cycle calendar.
- **Gungu drumming** (*gungu*) — Drumming for weddings and other celebrations.
- **Maulidi recitation** (*maulidi*) — Sufi ritual recitation in praise of the Prophet with rhythmic body sway, performed at gatherings and celebrations.

## Dance & theatre

Ngoma covers the spectrum from stately formal processional dances to raucous hip-driven wedding dances, with initiation dances, stick-fighting displays, and spirit-possession ceremonies used for therapy.

- **Lelemama** — Women's formal processional dance of Mombasa and Zanzibar town associations danced in ranks with restrained upper-body movement.
- **Chakacha** — Hip-driven wedding dance performed by women for women in closed courtyards, considered too explicit for mixed audiences.
- **Unyago initiation dances** (*unyago*) — Dances instructing girls at initiation, led by the ritual teacher (kungwi).
- **Goma la mkwaju** — Men's sword-and-stick dance form.
- **Spirit-possession ceremony (ngoma ya sheitani)** (*ngoma ya sheitani*) — Ceremony using drumming and dance as therapy directed by a mganga healer, rather than entertainment.

## Festivals & rituals

The Islamic calendar governs the year, with Ramadan, Idd el-Fitr, Idd el-Hajj, and the Prophet's birthday (Maulidi) as major observances, while life-cycle rites blend Islamic practice with older African custom.

- **Ramadan and Idd el-Fitr** (*Ramadan*) — Month of fasting closing in Idd el-Fitr (Sikukuu) with new clothes, visiting, and ngoma in the streets.
- **Lamu Maulidi** — The coast's greatest gathering at the tomb of Habib Swaleh on the Prophet's birthday, with recitation, processions, donkey and dhow races.
- **Mwaka Kogwa** — Shirazi solar New Year (Nauruz) observed at Makunduchi in southern Zanzibar in late July with ritual banana-stalk stick fights, hut burning, and communal feasting — the clearest surviving Persian-derived rite on the coast.
- **Girls' seclusion and instruction (unyago)** (*unyago*) — Life-cycle rite of girls' seclusion and instruction under a kungwi ritual teacher.
- **Boys' circumcision (jando)** (*jando*) — Life-cycle rite of boys' circumcision ceremony.

## Oral tradition & literature

Swahili has one of Africa's oldest written vernacular literatures, in Arabic script from at least the seventeenth century, anchored in the utenzi narrative poem form and fed by animal tales, proverbs, and riddles.

- **Utenzi narrative poem** (*utenzi*) — Long narrative poem in four-line stanzas, the great form of Swahili literature including the Utendi wa Tambuka (1728) on Byzantine wars and the Utendi wa Mwana Kupona (1858) on marriage and piety.
- **Al-Inkishafi** — Meditation on the ruin of Pate by Sayyid Abdalla bin Ali bin Nasir, a major utenzi-form poem.
- **Animal tales (hadithi)** (*hadithi*) — Traditional stories built around the trickster hare (Sungura) against hyena and elephant.
- **Proverbs (methali)** (*methali*) — Traditional proverbs, the same corpus printed on kangas.
- **Riddles (vitendawili)** (*vitendawili*) — Riddles exchanged in call-and-response.

## Language & religion

Kiswahili is Bantu with heavy Arabic borrowing plus Persian, Hindi, Portuguese and English layers; overwhelmingly Sunni Muslim with Shafi'i school and Sufi orders central, coexisting with beliefs in jini spirits and protective amulets.

- **Kiswahili language** (*Kiswahili*) — Bantu language (Niger-Congo) with heavy Arabic lexical borrowing, plus Persian, Hindi, Portuguese, German and English layers; Modern Standard Swahili derives from Zanzibar's Kiunguja.
- **Qadiriyya Sufi order** (*Qadiriyya*) — Sufi order historically central to town religious life on the Swahili coast.
- **Shadhiliyya Sufi order** (*Shadhiliyya*) — Sufi order historically central to town religious life on the Swahili coast.
- **Spirit possession (pepo)** (*pepo*) — Belief in spirit possession treated through ngoma ya sheitani ceremonies directed by a mganga healer.
- **Shrine visits (ziara)** (*ziara*) — Visits to pillar tombs and saints' graves as part of Islamic practice coexisting with older spirit beliefs.
