## Overview

The Pondo, also called the Mpondo people or Ama-Mpondo, belong to a kingdom in what is now the Eastern Cape. The kingdom includes the towns of Lusikisiki, Siphaqeni, Mbizana, Ntabankulu, Port St. Johns, Libode and Ngqeleni. Its great house is Ndimakude Great Palace at Flagstaff, while Nyandeni Great Place is situated at Libode. The Mpondo people are related to other Aba-Mbo kingdoms and chiefdoms in South Africa and share a common lineage with Ama-Mpondomise, Ama-Xesibe, Abakwa-Mkhize, Ama-Bomvu and Ama-Bomvana. The sources describe oral traditions, royal history, resistance to colonial systems, music, dance, festivals and a lunar calendar.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources identify royal residences rather than describing a general vernacular house form. The great house of Mpondo is called Ndimakude Great Palace and is situated in Flagstaff, Eastern Cape. The right-hand house is called Nyandeni Great Place and is situated in Libode, Eastern Cape. The sources also identify Lwandlolubomvu Great Place in Ntabankulu as the location of the Mpondo Culture and Heritage Festival and describe Ntabankulu as the mountainous part of the Mpondo Kingdom, surrounded by the great Mzimvubu River. No construction materials, roof forms or domestic architectural features are given.

## Music & performance

The Mpondo people are described as one of the major tribes that produce and consume Maskandi music. They do, however, connect Maskandi with *ukusina*, described as a Nguni dance, and with the traditional dance *imfene*, glossed as a “baboon dance.” *Imfene* is performed by young people and adults of both sexes to the sounds of Maskandi music.

Music and performance also appear in the annual Mpondo Culture and Heritage Festival, which celebrates Mpondo culture and heritage. The festival is held in September at Lwandlolubomvu Great Place, Ntabankulu, the palace of the customary head Jongilanga Sigcau. It is preceded by the Annual Mpondo Reed Dance and is attended by approximately over 20,000 people according to the source. The event is presented as a setting for sharing Mpondo culture and heritage with other cultures from South Africa, the broader African continent and beyond the oceans.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name two performance traditions. *Ukusina* is described as a Nguni dance. The Mpondo also perform *imfene*, or “baboon dance,” to Maskandi music. It is performed by young people and adults of both sexes.

The Annual Mpondo Reed Dance precedes the Mpondo Culture and Heritage Festival, but the supplied material gives no further description of the dance.

## Festivals & rituals

The Mpondo Culture and Heritage Festival is celebrated annually to mark Mpondo culture and heritage. It is held in September at Lwandlolubomvu Great Place in Ntabankulu, at the palace of the customary head Jongilanga Sigcau. The event is preceded by the Annual Mpondo Reed Dance and is attended by approximately over 20,000 people. The festival also seeks to promote cultural diversity through the sharing of Mpondo culture and heritage with other cultures from South Africa, the broader African continent and beyond the oceans.

September is important in the account of Mpondo history because it was originally the Mpondo new year in the ancient Mpondo calendars. The source also states that King Mqikela and King Sigcau were born in this month, and that the festival commemorates figures including Faku, Mqikela, Sigcawu and Marhelane.

According to the ancient Aba-Mbo people, including Mpondo, September is the first month of the year. Some Mpondo people recognise the appearance of the Pleiades, called *isilimela*, as signalling the beginning of the year, although the source says this interpretation was most likely adopted from the sotho people. The calendar begins with *u-Mphanda* in September, followed by *u-Zibandlela* in October, *u-Lwezi* in November, *u-Ntsinga* in December, *u-Ntlolanja* in January, *u-Ndazosela* in February, *u-m'Basa* in March, *u-Mgudlula* in April, *u-Ntlangula* in May, *u-Ntulikazi* in June, *u-Ncwabakazi* in July and *u-Mfumfu* in August.

## Foodways

They mention only that, during the 1824 invasion of Mpondoland, surviving Zulu forces were forced to forage for wild watermelons called *amabhece* in Zulu or *ujodo* in IsiMpondo.

## Oral tradition & literature

The origins of ema-Mpondweni are explained through oral tradition. The story personifies and symbolises a nation whose lands were shaped like a horn, including the lands of ema-Mpondomiseni, and also made *Iintsomi*, meaning educative stories, easier to tell. In this account, Mpondo is taken as the son because it was associated with defending, while Mpondomise is taken as the daughter because it was associated with attacking and negotiation.

According to the Mpondo oral tradition, the people are legendary descendants of King Mpondo, the grandson of King Sibiside, who led the once-powerful Embo nation, also called Aba-Mbo or Ma-Mbo. Mpondo and Mpondomise were twins, and the sources state that there is an ongoing argument over which twin was eldest, although the most commonly held view is that Mpondo was the senior twin. The story says that while hunting, Mpondo killed a lion and refused to give its skin to Mpondomise according to the custom that the senior was entitled to the skins of certain animals. Mpondo and his followers then left and settled away from their father’s land.

The sources also preserve genealogical traditions, including the offspring of King Sibiside: Mavovo, Gubhela, Nomafu and Njanya. They state that Mpondo people share a common lineage with several other named groups. No epic poetry, proverb collection, riddle tradition, literary revival or documentation project is described.

## Language & religion

The sources mention IsiMpondo and refer to the Mpondo language in connection with the word *ujodo*. They also state that Ntabankulu means “Big Mountains” in the Mpondo language.

The Mpondo material does mention traditional healers and magical African powers in its account of the wars against the Zulu forces.
