| Who | Communities identified as Dogon, with distinct local histories and practices. |
| Where | The Bandiagara plateau, escarpment, and nearby plains of central Mali; some communities extend into Burkina Faso. |
| Language | Several related Dogon languages are spoken across the region. |
| Religion | Religious practice varies by community and includes local traditions, Islam, and Christianity. |
| Known for | Earthen architecture · Carved doors and granary fittings · Indigo textiles · Wooden sculpture · Masquerade traditions |

## Overview

Dogon communities live across the plateau, cliff, and plain around Bandiagara. Farming settlements, granaries, meeting shelters, and ritual spaces form a varied built landscape. The area is shared with other peoples and contains remains from earlier periods, so an object found at Bandiagara is not automatically Dogon. Languages and ceremonies also differ among Dogon communities. The collection therefore follows each museum's specific cultural attribution rather than treating every object from the escarpment as Dogon.

## Material culture

The objects gathered here show cloth making, carved wood, pottery, metal adornment, and the equipment of domestic and ceremonial life. Many museum records identify a Dogon maker or community; dates and original uses are less certain for some older pieces.

### Textile & pattern traditions

- **Indigo cotton** — Narrow woven strips are sewn into wrappers and blankets. Resist dyeing, white linear marks, and alternating indigo bands make several of the selected cloths distinctive.
- **Woven geometry** — Stripes, checkerboards, chevrons, and repeated small motifs appear in the museum's Dogon textiles. Their meanings should not be assumed from appearance alone.
- **Masquerade fibers** — Fiber, cloth, and shell ornaments can be part of a mask's costume, not simply decoration on the carved wooden face.

### Clothing & dress

- **Cotton garments** — The collection includes a white strip woven smock, tunics, wrappers, a child's indigo hat, and headwear with cowrie shells.
- **Dress for performance** — Some mask ensembles combine wooden faces with woven skirts, fiber, hair, beads, and cowries. The complete costume matters to the performance.

### Architecture

- **Earth and stone settlements** — UNESCO describes houses, granaries, altars, sanctuaries, and meeting places across the Bandiagara landscape.
- **Toguna** — Low, thatched meeting shelters stand on carved wooden posts. Museum collections preserve several posts and describe their place in village discussion.
- **Granary doors and locks** — Carved human, animal, and geometric forms appear on doors and wooden locks. These are working parts of buildings as well as sculptural objects.

### Ceramics, woodwork & everyday objects

- **Pottery** — A footed terracotta vessel in the collection shows one form of Dogon ceramic work.
- **Wooden furnishings and tools** — Headrests, stools, heddle pulleys, and carved troughs connect sculpture with domestic work and weaving.
- **Carved figures** — Museums document figures associated with ancestry and ritual, but the meaning of an individual piece cannot be read reliably from its shape alone.

### Jewelry & body adornment

- **Metalwork and beads** — The selected objects include armlets, a bracelet of stone and glass beads, a ring, and small pendants. Their forms show variety without proving a single Dogon style or a universal social meaning.

## Music & performance

Drums, bells, rattles, a harp, and figurative instruments appear among the selected objects. The photographs and masks also document performance, while the museum records do not establish a single repertoire for all Dogon communities.

## Dance & theatre

Dogon masquerades can bring carved masks, full body costume, music, and movement together. The **Awa** mask society is associated with some of these traditions. Kanaga, satimbe, and other named mask types occur in accounts of funerary performances, although their use and interpretation vary locally.

## Festivals & rituals

- **Dama** — Funerary masquerades honor the dead and involve masks and dance in communities that practice them.
- **Sigui** — A long cycle, often described as recurring roughly every sixty years, links ceremonies across communities. A museum object's connection to this cycle should be stated only when its record supports it.

## Foodways

Millet and other grains are central to farming in the Bandiagara region. Granaries protect stored harvests; the photographed onion gardens at Sanga show another part of local cultivation. Crops and diets differ across the plateau and plains.

## Oral tradition & literature

Stories and ritual knowledge are transmitted in different Dogon languages and settings. Published accounts by Marcel Griaule and later writers made selected narratives widely known. The collection presents documented objects and practices without assigning one cosmology to all makers.

## Language & religion

"Dogon" covers a family of languages rather than a single uniform speech community. Local religious traditions, Islam, and Christianity coexist in the region. A museum figure may refer to an ancestor or other spiritual being; interpretations require the object's own documentation and community context.

## Glossary

- *Awa* — Mask society associated with some Dogon masquerades.
- *dama* — Funerary ceremony or masquerade.
- *kanaga* — A named type of carved mask used in masquerade.
- *satimbe* — A named mask type.
- *Sigui* — Long cycle of ceremonies described in Dogon ethnography.
- *toguna* — Low meeting shelter supported by posts.
- *Tellem* — Earlier inhabitants of the Bandiagara escarpment; their objects should not be filed as Dogon without a specific attribution.
