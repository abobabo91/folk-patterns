## Overview

The Beja are a Cushitic-speaking people of the Eastern Desert, the arid belt between the Nile and the Red Sea, living from southeastern Egypt through eastern Sudan into northwestern Eritrea. They descend from peoples who have inhabited the area since 4000 BC or earlier, and ancient texts know them as the *Medjay* and, in Roman times, the *Blemmyes*. They number between 1.9 and 3 million. Most Sudanese Beja live in the states of Red Sea (around Port Sudan), River Nile, Al Qadarif and Kassala; in Eritrea they are found in the Northern Red Sea, Gash-Barka and Anseba regions; in Egypt in the Red Sea, Aswan, Luxor and Qena governorates, with a large population of Qamhat Bisharin in the Kharga Oasis of the Western Desert, displaced there by the Aswan High Dam. They are nomadic pastoralists, and the objects shown here — camel pack-saddles, hide shields, daggers, leather cushion-covers — are the equipment of that life. Their language, Beja, is the sole member of the Northern Cushitic branch of Afroasiatic.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here — the Wikipedia articles on the Beja people and the Beja language, and the catalogue records of the museum objects shown — do not describe a named weaving or textile-patterning tradition. What they do document is decorated leather and plaited plant fibre, which carry the ornament in the objects the atlas holds.

**Plaited palm-leaf and coiled grass work** — Conical and trumpet-shaped ornamental headpieces in the British Museum collection are built from coiled plaited palm-stem leaf, or from coiled grass, and then covered with leather; one pair is further covered with cowrie shells. The same plaiting appears structurally rather than decoratively in camel pack-saddles, where woven palm-leaf matting forms the padded seat and plaited vegetable-fibre rope secures it to the wooden frame.

**Stained and incised leatherwork** — A rectangular leather cushion-cover is worked with geometric and linear motifs, the incised areas filled in with green, red or yellow stain. Its two slightly flared end flaps are cut in openwork and set with strips of green plastic fixed by metal studs, a recent material used within an established decorative scheme.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Geometric and linear motifs* — the incised and stained ornament of the leather cushion-cover; *incised linear motifs* — the decoration of the dagger's wooden hilt, flaring quillons and pommel. The sources give no vernacular names for these.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe Beja dress mainly through photographs rather than through named garments. British Museum photographic prints made in Egypt show groups of Beja men and children wearing gowns, with at least one man in a turban and carrying a walking-stick; a print of a desert camp-site in Ethiopia shows an adult in a robe and head-gear, and a child wearing a cloth together with a neck-ornament and arm-ornaments. Beyond this, the historical record in the sources is one of dress as a marker of cultural change rather than of specific cut or fabric: the Arabization that followed the migration of the Juhaynah, Mudar, Rabi'a and other Arab tribes into Beja territory led the Beja to adopt Arab clothing along with the Arabic language and Arab kinship organization, a process that by the 15th century had also brought them into Islam. The sources used do not describe ceremonial dress as distinct from everyday wear, nor women's dress specifically.

### Architecture

Beja building, as the sources show it, is the portable and semi-portable architecture of nomads in the Eastern Desert rather than a fixed village form. Two British Museum photographs of camp-sites in Ethiopia, identified as possibly Beja, show a tent made of textiles stretched over a frame of branches, standing in open desert with mountains behind; pots and a bowl sit on the ground outside, and a separate structure made of branches stands in front of one tent. Photographs taken in Egypt show a different form: shelters made of poles and mats, with a wooden platform, possibly a bed, standing in the open in front of them. Both are light frameworks skinned with what the household already carries — cloth in one case, matting in the other — and both are pitched directly on desert ground with no enclosure or foundation. The sources used do not describe permanent house types, roofing systems, applied architectural decoration, or tomb or mosque architecture.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The objects the atlas holds are weapons, riding gear and household furnishings. The **dagger** has a curved iron blade and a wooden hilt with flaring quillons and a pommel, both embellished with incised linear motifs. Two **shields** survive: one circular, with a conical central boss formed in a mould; the other of hide, with a conical boss on the face and a single handle on the reverse, bound with wire and fitted with a leather carrying strap. The **camel pack-saddle** is the most elaborate object of the group — a carved wooden frame with high pommels lashed together with leather thongs, its padded seat built up from hessian sacking and woven palm-leaf matting and tied to the frame with plaited vegetable-fibre rope. Two examples of this saddle are held. Pots and a bowl appear on the ground in the camp photographs, but the sources do not describe a Beja ceramic tradition.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources document adornment chiefly through the British Museum's ornaments and photographs. Two pairs of tall, trumpet-shaped **hats** are made of plaited coiled palm-stem leaf covered with leather; a further pair of conical ornaments is built of coiled grass, covered first with leather and then with cowrie shells, so that the shell surface is the finished face of the object. In the photograph of a Beja camp-site in Ethiopia, a child wears a neck-ornament and arm-ornaments together with a plain cloth. The sources used do not describe tattooing, henna, hairdressing practice, or metal jewellery, and give no vernacular names for any of these ornament types.

## Music & performance

The sources record no Beja instruments, song genres or performance occasions. What they do establish is that verbal art, rather than instrumental music, is where the sources place Beja cultural prestige: Beja poetry is still highly praised, and the standing of the language rests in part on that (see *Oral tradition & literature* below). No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage element is inscribed for Sudan, so none covers Beja musical practice.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used do not cover Beja dance or dramatic traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not describe a Beja festival calendar or life-cycle rites. They establish only the religious frame within which such observances would fall: the tenth-century geographer Abu Nasr Mutahhar al-Maqdisi recorded that the Beja were Christians at that time, and by the 15th century the Beja had been Islamized, following the migration of Arab tribes into their territory and centuries of intermarriage. Al-Yaqubi documented five Muslim Beja kingdoms in the 9th century. On geography rather than calendar, one place is recorded as sacred: Jebel Uweinat is revered by the Qamhat Bisharin, the group now largely resident in the Kharga Oasis. The ethnographic literature listed in the sources indicates where this material has been studied — Frode Jacobsen's work on theories of sickness and misfortune among the Hadandowa Beja, Amal Fadlalla's on fertility and regeneration in eastern Sudan, and John Morton's on *sakanab*, the greeting-and-information exchange of the northern Beja — but the sources given here do not summarize their findings.

## Foodways

The sources used do not cover Beja foodways: no staple grains, dishes, cooking methods, coffee or tea practice, or ceremonial food are described. The only material traces in the record are the pots and bowl standing on the ground outside tents in the British Museum's camp-site photographs, and the camel that the pack-saddles were built for.

## Oral tradition & literature

Beja has an extensive oral tradition with multiple poetic genres, and the claims over Beja land are only valid when expressed in Beja — poetry and formal speech are load-bearing, not ornamental. The best-known epic is the story of the hero **Mhamuud Oofaash**, portions of which have appeared in publications by Klaus Wedekind; an edition appears in Mahmud Mohammed Ahmed's *Oomraay*, published in Asmara. In the 1960s and 1970s the Beja intellectual Muhammed Adarob Ohaj recorded poetic and narrative material now held in the University of Khartoum Institute of African and Asian Studies Sound Archives; Didier Morin and Mohamed-Tahir Hamid Ahmed have drawn on these, alongside their own collections, for academic publications in French on Beja poetics. Morin also published a study of tales across the Cushitic area, *"Des paroles douces comme la soie"* (1995). Contemporary preservation is active: between 2010 and 2013 Red Sea University and the NGO Uhaashoon worked with oral story-tellers to produce 41 short readers and a longer collection of three short stories in Beja.

## Language & religion

The Beja language — *Bidhaawyeet* when indefinite, *Tubdhaawi* when definite — is the only member of the Northern Cushitic branch of the Afroasiatic family, an identification made by Enrico Cerulli between 1925 and 1951 and standard since. Ethnologue counted 2,550,000 speakers in Sudan in 2022 and 121,000 in Eritrea, with an estimated 88,000 in Egypt as of 2023. Much vocabulary is borrowed from Arabic, and in Eritrea and Sudan some terms are Tigre loanwords. Not all Beja speak it: the Beni Amer speak a variety of Tigre, and the Ababda, an Arabized Beja subtribe, now speak Arabic. Both Roman and Arabic scripts have been used to write Beja — three Arabic orthographies have seen limited use — but no system has gained wide support, and only the Latin script has been used in publications by more than one writer. The Beja are Muslim, Islamized by the 15th century; in the tenth century al-Maqdisi described them as Christians.
