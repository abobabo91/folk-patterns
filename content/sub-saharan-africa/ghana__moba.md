## Overview

The Moba, also known as Bimoba, Bimawba, B’Moba, or Moab, are an ethnic group primarily located in northern Ghana and northern Togo, including the Northeasten Region of Ghana and the Dapaong region in Northern Togo. The sources describe an estimated 200,00 people who identify themselves as Bimoba and about 481,500 people in northern Togo. Moba belongs to the Niger-Congo language family and the Gur language group, within the Gurma or Mabia subgroup. Moba culture is organized around clans, family heads, ancestors, agriculture, livestock rearing, initiation, shrines, pottery, woodcarving, and Tchitcheri figures. The group’s material culture includes circular mud houses, dry-vegetation roofs, pottery, and anthropomorphic wooden shrine sculptures.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** *Wari* — a term for details on a surface made with lines or engraved lines, visible on surfaces and usable objects.

### Clothing & dress

 During Kondi initiation, participants wear special dress, metal jewelry, red clothes, and cowrie shells. The sources state that male initiation takes place in the woodland and female initiation near the home. Participants have shaved heads and are marked with a scar to symbolize rebirth. The initiation rules also include dietary limits, a secret language, and symbolic ornaments connected with protection, past familial relationships, and purity when returning to the village.

### Architecture

Moba settlements are found in hilly and elevated areas, which historically provided strategic advantages for observing potential threats. The sources locate settlements across the Northeasten Region of Ghana, from Nakpanduri (Nanmanbour) to Bunkpurugu (Bunkperu), and in the Dapaong region in Northern Togo. Nakpanduri historically means a place for eating meat, while Bunkpurugu means an old river in Moar.

Moba homes are circular structures with mud walls and roofs made from dry vegetation. Their size changes according to the number of people in the household; the birth of a baby can result in another room being added. The sources describe this expansion as connected with the idea that having children makes a complete home. Kitchens contain distinct zones for storing food, preparing meals, and cooking. A typical compound also contains a clay altar called a *patir*, plural *pataa*, placed in an enclosed hut called a *nakouk* or *nakuuk*.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Women make pottery by hand without a potter’s wheel, using the convex mold method. Their pottery is described as more decorated and colorful, and women fill the kiln while older women supervise the loading and hardening of the pots. These vessels are used for beer and provide income for families. Men use the concave mold method and can make about twenty to thirty pots in a day. Male potters make beer pots, producing them for extra money during only two months of the year.

Moba carvers, called *Tikpierroa*, learn woodcarving within the family and use adzes. Their work includes figures with little detail, extended human features, and an anthropomorphic style. These figures are called **Tchitcheri figures**.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 Kondi initiation includes metal jewelry, cowrie shells, red clothes, shaved heads, scars, and symbolic ornaments. These objects and bodily marks form part of the ritual passage from childhood to adulthood and the participants’ return to the village.

## Music & performance

 Moba is described as a tonal language with four tones, and tone patterns form the core of a whistled language based on Moba.

## Dance & theatre

 The sources describe ceremonies, initiation rituals, shrine practices, and communal meals.

## Festivals & rituals

Moba society is organized around clans and family heads and emphasizes relationships with ancestors. The Bimoba worship personal deities collectively referred to as *Yennu*, translated as “God,” and communicate with ancestors through shrines. A compound altar is called a *patir*, with *pataa* as the plural. It is located in an enclosed hut called a *nakouk* or *nakuuk*, where sacrifices are made to invoke the ancestors. Women are allowed into the *nakuuk*. Individuals may construct a smaller altar called a *mier*, while a community shrine is called *tingban*. The *tingban* is visited during problems affecting the entire community, including drought or a disease outbreak.

The sources describe an annual blood sacrifice involving children and women. The ceremony addresses conflicts concerning land, leadership, and relationships. An opening is made in the ground, animal blood is poured into it, and a weapon and symbolic objects are added. The act represents an agreement between foes, the removal of past bitterness, and the transformation of aggression into calmness. After the sacrifice, the community shares food and makes a hereditary pledge for continuing peace.

Kondi initiation symbolizes transformation and social growth, taking children from childhood into adulthood and individual social identity. Boys undergo initiation in the woodland, while girls undergo it near the home. The *Kondaak*, translated in the source as “Kondi market,” is a return ritual in which participants stop at homes and receive gifts.

The sources also describe several forms of marriage: *Puokpedu*, *Puokuul*, *Puopaab*, *Puotugnu*, *Petulant Fallu*, and *Puoton/Taalon*. These include exchange obligations, work on the bride’s family farmland, agreement by a family head, abduction as described in the source, remarriage into a deceased husband’s family, and a “woman-debt” owed by the groom’s lineage.

## Foodways

The sources identify agriculture and livestock rearing as central to Moba subsistence. Crops include millet, maize, groundnut, sorghum, and yams. Livestock include cattle, goats, pigs, fowls, and sheep.

Food is present in ritual contexts. After the annual blood sacrifice, the community shares food while making a pledge for continuing peace. Pottery is also connected with foodways and household income: women make pots used for beer, while men make beer pots using the concave mold method.

## Oral tradition & literature

 They state that initiation rituals use a secret language.

## Language & religion

Moba or Bimoba is a Niger-Congo language spoken by the Moba people of Togo and Ghana, with speakers also in Burkina Faso. The language has two dialects: Moba in Togo and Burkina Faso, and Bimoba in Ghana. The language cluster is also called Moba–Bimoba. Moba is a Gur language within the proposed Niger-Congo family and belongs with Bassari and Konkomba/Likpakpaln to the Gurma, or Mabia, subgroup of Gur languages. Moba has twenty-two consonants and six vowels, oral and nasal vowels, and a distinction between long and short vowels. It is an SVO language with noun-class and verb-class systems.

The Bimoba practice predominantly ethnic religions. They identify with *Yennu*, and ancestors act as contacts between people and Yennu. Shrines and altars are central to religious life. The *bawoong* household shrine is associated with family health, prosperity, livestock fertility, and a yearly harvest. Its accompanying **bawoong tchitcheri** generally represent recent ancestors, including parents and grandparents. **Yendu tchitcheri** are tiny shrine figures connected directly with a person’s relationship to God. **Tchitcherii sakab** are larger shrine sculptures for the whole village and depict Bimoba descendants.
