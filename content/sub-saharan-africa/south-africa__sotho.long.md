## Overview

The Sotho (*Basotho*, singular *Mosotho*) are a Bantu-speaking people of southern Africa whose heartland straddles the mountain kingdom of Lesotho and the adjacent highveld of South Africa's Free State, with substantial diaspora communities across Gauteng, the Eastern Cape and the Vaal industrial belt. Their language, Sesotho (also written Southern Sotho to distinguish it from the related Tswana and Northern Sotho/Pedi tongues of the broader Sotho–Tswana cluster), belongs to the Bantu branch of Niger–Congo and is one of the eleven official languages of South Africa as well as the co-official language of Lesotho alongside English. Numerically the Southern Sotho are estimated at around six to seven million speakers — roughly two million in Lesotho itself and four to five million in South Africa — with the broader Sotho–Tswana continuum reaching well over ten million. Their emergence as a coherent nation is comparatively recent and directly traceable: in the early nineteenth century, during the *difaqane* upheavals set off by the expansion of the Zulu polity, the chief Moshoeshoe I gathered the shattered clans of the Caledon Valley on the flat-topped fortress mountain of Thaba Bosiu and by diplomacy, cattle raiding, and negotiated alliance with French Protestant missionaries and the British Cape welded them into the Basotho nation, whose successor state is the modern kingdom of Lesotho. In folk-culture terms the Basotho are known internationally for a small and unusually coherent cluster of signature objects — the conical straw *mokorotlo* hat modelled on the peak of Mount Qiloane, the striped and heraldic Basotho blanket (*seana marena* and its cousins), the incised earthen wall-painting tradition of *litema*, and the tall painted grain baskets and beer pots of the highveld homestead.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Unlike the great weaving traditions of West Africa, the Basotho are not a loom-weaving people; textile identity here is expressed almost entirely through the imported wool blanket, which since the second half of the nineteenth century has been absorbed so completely into ceremonial and everyday life that it now reads as fully indigenous. The **Basotho blanket** (*kobo*) is a heavy wool blanket, typically about 155 by 164 cm, woven originally in the mills of Witney in Oxfordshire and today produced in South Africa by Aranda Textile Mills under trade names such as *Seana Marena* ("to swear by the king"), *Victoria England*, *Sefate* ("tree"), *Malakabaneng* ("in the flames"), *Lingoetsi* and *Morena* ("chief"). Each design carries a distinct combination of striped border, central heraldic panel (crowns, mielie cobs, playing-card motifs, cabbage leaves, aeroplanes, chevrons) and a woven "pin stripe" (*melamu*) which is always worn vertically, since to wear it horizontally is held to stunt growth or bring bad luck. The heraldic *Seana Marena* is the most senior grade, given at weddings, chieftainship investitures and to honoured guests; the *Victoria England* commemorates Queen Victoria's granting of Basotho protectorate status in 1868. Beyond blankets, the second great pattern tradition is **litema** (see Architecture below), a mural grammar of incised and painted geometry which functions as the female counterpart to the male blanket in the visual economy of the homestead. Beadwork, while present, is more restrained than in neighbouring Zulu, Ndebele or Xhosa traditions and is worked mainly into initiation regalia, waistbands (*thethana*) and small amulets rather than large panels.

### Clothing & dress

The signature of Basotho dress is the wool blanket worn as a cloak, pinned at the right shoulder with a large safety pin or brooch so that the right (spear or greeting) arm is left free. Men combine the *kobo* with dark trousers, a collared shirt, gumboots or leather riding boots, and the conical **mokorotlo** hat of woven *mosea* grass, whose silhouette — a tall cone terminating in a topknot and a flared brim — is said to reproduce the profile of Mount Qiloane near Thaba Bosiu and which appears at the centre of the Lesotho national flag. Women wear the blanket wrapped horizontally around the body over a long pleated skirt (*mose*) and a headscarf (*tuku*) tied in a distinctive high knot, often with a shawl or a second blanket over the shoulders; married women commonly cover their heads at all times in public. Herd boys (*balisana*), whose long apprenticeship in the mountains is a stage of male life in its own right, wear a lighter grey or brown blanket, a woven grass or plastic-strip *molianyeoe* balaclava-style face cover against wind and cold, and carry a fighting stick (*molamu*) and knobkerrie. Initiates emerging from the male circumcision school (*lebollo la banna*) appear in white blankets and white-clay body paint; female initiates wear grass skirts and cover the upper body with red ochre. Everyday urban dress is of course indistinguishable from that of any other South African community, but the blanket, hat and *tuku* remain compulsory for weddings, funerals, church confirmations and audiences with a chief.

### Architecture

The traditional Basotho homestead (*motse*) is a cluster of round thatched houses (*rondavel*, Sesotho *mokhoro*) arranged around a central cattle byre (*lesaka*), with a separate cooking hut, grain storage, and a small ancestral shrine area. Walls are built of stone or of sun-dried earth blocks plastered with a mixture of clay, cow dung and ash which cures to a hard, waterproof, subtly polished surface. Roofs are conical thatch of highveld grass bound to a timber armature, finished with a decorative topknot (*sefika*) that echoes the *mokorotlo*. It is on the plastered exterior walls, and on the interior of the reception room, that the Basotho practise **litema** — a mural art performed exclusively by women, in which wet clay plaster is combed, incised, moulded in low relief and later painted with earth pigments (ochres, kaolin, charcoal) and, in the twentieth century, with washing blue, oxide reds and commercial enamels. Litema designs are strictly geometric — chevrons, lozenges, sun rosettes, plaited borders, stylised mielie plants and aloe leaves — laid out in tessellating square units so that the whole wall reads as a single quilt. The compositions are renewed annually before major ceremonies and after the summer rains, and are considered a form of prayer and welcome; a well-decorated homestead announces both the industry of its women and the readiness of the household to receive guests and ancestors. In the high mountains, drystone kraals and shepherds' shelters (*motebo*) built without mortar are a distinct sub-tradition, as are the *metsoalle* cave shelters used seasonally by transhumant herders.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Basotho pottery is a women's craft, hand-built by coiling from local clays, burnished with a stone, and open-fired in a shallow pit fuelled with dung and grass. The characteristic forms are the wide-shouldered beer pot (*nkho*) used to brew and serve sorghum beer (*joala*), the smaller drinking pot (*sekotlolo*), and the water-carrying pot (*pitsa*) with a narrower neck. Decoration is restrained — incised bands, applied bosses, and a deep even blackening achieved by post-firing reduction in wet grass. Basketry and grass-work are proportionally more important than ceramics: the tall lidded grain basket (*seroto*), the flat winnowing tray (*leselo*), sleeping mats and floor mats of woven reed (*moseme*), and beer strainers of finely coiled grass all belong to a shared southern African tradition but are made in distinctive Basotho proportions. The **Sotho snuff container** (*nkgo ea koae*) — carved from horn, wood, or a small dried gourd, sometimes plugged with a beaded stopper and hung from a neck cord — is a personal object of some elaboration, since tobacco snuff is offered to elders and to the ancestors and its container therefore participates in ritual exchange. Metalwork was historically the province of specialist smiths (*litsheka*) who forged iron hoes, assegai blades, and the long fighting sticks tipped with an iron ferrule, though most heavy metal goods have been trade items since the mid-nineteenth century. The wooden headrest (*mutsago*), the milking pail (*kgamelo*), and carved wooden spoons round out the domestic inventory.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Basotho personal adornment is comparatively modest by regional standards. Beadwork in white, blue, red, green and black glass seed beads is worked into initiation belts, ankle bands, chest ornaments (*thethana*) for girls, and small pouches; colour combinations carry age-grade and marital-status information within the community rather than the elaborate courtship-letter grammar of Zulu beadwork. Brass and copper wire is coiled into arm and leg bangles. Small conus-shell disks and, in the twentieth century, mother-of-pearl buttons are sewn onto ceremonial dress. The **thebe** — the small oval hide shield carried by men in dance and ceremony — is not strictly jewellery but functions as a body ornament in performance. Red ochre (*letsoku*) and white kaolin are applied to the body during initiation, mourning, and healing rituals. Facial scarification is not a Basotho practice; ear-piercing is universal for women and near-universal for men, and small brass or beaded ear-plugs are worn. Hair is traditionally cropped short for both sexes, with elaborate braided or beaded styles reserved for specific ritual moments such as the emergence of a female initiate.

## Music & performance

Basotho music divides into several clearly named genres. *Mokhibo* is the seated women's dance-song, in which performers kneel and move the shoulders, chest, and head in rippling patterns while a chorus claps and sings in call-and-response. *Mohobelo* is its male counterpart, a striding, stamping dance performed standing, with high leg kicks and shouted praises. *Litolobonya* is a more raucous women's dance associated with weddings. The signature solo instrument is the *lesiba*, a mouth-resonated aerophone unique to the Basotho and recognised as the national instrument of Lesotho: a single string is stretched along a light stick and a flattened quill is bound at one end; the player places the quill between the lips and inhales and exhales sharply so that the quill vibrates as a free reed while the player's mouth cavity shapes the overtones into a plaintive, speech-like melody traditionally played by herd boys to their cattle. Other instruments include the *setolo-tolo* mouth-bow played by men, the *thomo* calabash-resonated bowed lute played by women, the *moropa* drum, and the ubiquitous ankle rattles (*matlapa*) of dried cocoons or tin cans. In the twentieth century the Basotho migrant-labour experience on the South African gold mines gave rise to *famo* — an accordion-and-vocal genre with a heavy bass drum, sung in Sesotho over a rolling triple-metre groove, historically associated with the shebeens of the mine compounds and with a rough reputation, but now a major commercial genre with stars such as Puseletso Seema, Tšepo Tšola and, more recently, a large and sometimes violent famo-gang subculture. Church choral singing in the Sesotho hymn tradition established by the Paris Evangelical Missionary Society is another central register of Basotho vocal life.

## Dance & theatre

Beyond *mokhibo* and *mohobelo*, the ceremonial dance repertoire includes the *ndlamu*-style stamping dances performed by initiates on their return from the mountain school, the men's stick-fighting displays (*mokallo*) which combine martial technique with rhythmic drumming, and the horseback processions in which mounted men in blanket, hat and riding boots parade at weddings and royal occasions — Lesotho being one of the few African cultures with a fully developed equestrian folk tradition, built on the hardy Basotho pony. There is no indigenous masked-theatre tradition of the West or Central African kind; dramatic performance is carried instead by praise poetry (see below) and by the choreographed pageantry of initiation. Twentieth-century Sesotho-language theatre, radio drama, and film have grown from these oral roots rather than from a mask tradition.

## Festivals & rituals

The Basotho ritual year is anchored less in a fixed calendar than in life-cycle events and agricultural moments. **Initiation** — *lebollo la banna* for boys and *lebollo la basadi* for girls — remains widely practised, particularly in Lesotho and the rural Free State: cohorts spend weeks to months in a secluded mountain school (*mophato*) where they are circumcised (boys), instructed in adult conduct, praise poetry, sexual knowledge and the secret vocabulary of the initiated, and emerge in white blankets and clay paint to be reincorporated into the village with feasting and dance. Weddings (*lenyalo*) involve extended negotiation of bridewealth in cattle (*bohali*), the ceremonial delivery of the bride, the slaughter of an ox, and the presentation of a *Seana Marena* blanket to the bride by her in-laws. Funerals are large communal affairs with all-night singing and a ritual washing of hands on returning from the graveside. Ancestral veneration (*balimo*) is expressed through household beer offerings, the slaughter of a goat or ox in the cattle byre, and consultation of diviner-healers (*lingaka*, singular *ngaka*) who throw bone-and-shell divination sets (*litaola*) to diagnose illness and social rupture. The Morija Arts and Cultural Festival, held annually in the historic mission town of Morija in Lesotho, is the main modern showcase of Basotho music, dance, poetry, and craft, and Lesotho's Independence Day (4 October) and King's Birthday are the largest secular gatherings.

## Foodways

Basotho cuisine is the cuisine of a highland maize- and sorghum-farming, cattle- and sheep-keeping people. The staple is *papa* (also *bohobe* or, in the softer porridge form, *motoho*) — a stiff porridge of white maize meal eaten by hand and dipped into a relish (*seleche*) of stewed greens, beans, or meat. *Moroho* — wild or cultivated leafy greens (pumpkin leaves, amaranth, wild spinach) stewed with onion and sometimes peanut — is the everyday relish. *Motoho oa mabele* is a lightly fermented sorghum porridge drunk as a breakfast and refreshment, sour and slightly effervescent. Meat, historically reserved for ceremonial occasions, is now more common: *nama* is grilled or stewed beef or mutton, and the whole slaughtered sheep at a wedding or funeral is butchered on the spot and distributed by strict protocol among kin. *Joala* — sorghum beer, brewed by women in the large *nkho* pot over several days of malting and fermentation — is the ritual drink of ancestral offerings, weddings and beer-parties (*letsema*), and its lighter cousin *hopose* is the everyday brew. Commercial maize beer (*Chibuku*) has partly displaced home-brewed *joala* in towns. Wild peaches, prickly pear, and the highland *moroetsana* berry are seasonal treats.

## Oral tradition & literature

The senior oral genre is **praise poetry** — *lithoko* (singular *thoko*) for men and *liboko* for lineages — declaimed in a rapid, high-register, densely metaphorical Sesotho by a trained praise-singer (*seroki*) at chiefly installations, weddings, and initiation graduations. The great *lithoko* of Moshoeshoe I, composed in the nineteenth century and preserved through both oral transmission and early missionary transcription, are the founding literary monument of the Basotho nation. Folktales (*litšomo*) told by grandmothers around the evening fire feature the trickster hare (*mmutla*), the greedy hyena, the ogress *Koko*, and cycles about the culture hero Senkatana. The nineteenth-century arrival of the Paris Evangelical Missionary Society at Morija in 1833 produced one of the earliest and most complete written literatures in any African language: the Bible in Sesotho (1878), Azariele Sekese's ethnographic compendium *Mekhoa le Maele a Basotho* (1893), and above all the novels of Thomas Mofolo, whose *Chaka* (written 1910, published 1925) is a foundational work of African-language literature and has been translated worldwide. Modern Sesotho poetry, drama, and prose remain vigorous, with the Morija Museum and Archives holding the principal manuscript collection.

## Language & religion

Sesotho is a fully standardised Bantu language of the Sotho–Tswana group, written since the 1830s in a Latin orthography developed by the Morija missionaries Casalis and Arbousset (Lesotho continues to use this original orthography, while South Africa adopted a slightly modified spelling in the twentieth century). It is characterised by an elaborate noun-class system, agglutinative verb morphology, three phonemic tones, and a rich lexicon of praise, kinship, and cattle vocabulary. Religiously the great majority of Basotho today are Christian — historically split between the Lesotho Evangelical Church (heir to the Paris Mission), the Roman Catholic Church (which is numerically dominant in Lesotho), the Anglicans, and a large and growing sector of African Independent Churches such as the Zion Christian Church and various Apostolic bodies whose white-and-blue-robed congregations are a common sight on the highveld. Beneath and interwoven with Christian practice, the ancestral religion of *balimo* veneration, diviner-healers (*lingaka*), the throwing of the *litaola* divination bones, and the seasonal offerings in the cattle byre remains a living substrate, and most Basotho move without contradiction between Sunday church, ancestral beer, and the diviner's consulting hut.
