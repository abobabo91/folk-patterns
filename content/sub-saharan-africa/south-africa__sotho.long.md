## Overview

The Basotho (singular *Mosotho*) are a Sotho-Tswana people of the southern African highveld and the Maloti-Drakensberg mountains, concentrated in Lesotho — where Sesotho is a first language for some 1.5 million people, about 85% of the population — and in South Africa's Free State (64.2% Sesotho-speaking) and Gauteng (13.1%), with smaller communities in Botswana, Eswatini and the Caprivi Strip of Namibia. Close to four million South Africans speak Sesotho as a first language, a Southern Bantu (Sotho-Tswana) language distinguished from Northern Sotho (Sepedi). The Basotho nation is a nineteenth-century political creation and an older cultural stratum at once: Bantu-speaking clans, organized by kraal and chiefdom, that absorbed San neighbours and were welded into a state by Moshoeshoe I from his 1822 mountain stronghold at Butha-Buthe. In folk-culture terms they are known above all for two things: *litema*, the geometric mural relief women cut and paint on house walls, and the *mokorotlo* hat and Basotho blanket, an invented-tradition dress code now read internationally as national costume.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Basotho blanket (*seanamarena*)** — The heavy wool blanket, worn pinned at the shoulder or wrapped at the waist, that replaced the karosse of hide during the nineteenth century and became the Basotho garment. Industrially woven in Britain and later South Africa to designs commissioned for the Lesotho market, the *seanamarena* ("chiefs' blanket") range carries named patterns — the maize cob, the shield, the crown — printed in dense repeats on a coloured ground with a contrasting border stripe. The stripe, the *pitiki*, is worn vertically; wearing it horizontally is a visible error.

**Mokorotlo** — The conical grass hat with a looped, knotted finial at the apex, plaited from *mosea* and *lehlaka* grasses, its silhouette taken from the peak of Qiloane near Thaba Bosiu. It is the national emblem of Lesotho, appearing on the flag and on vehicle registration plates, and is worn and set differently by men and women.

**Litema** — Not a textile but the group's core pattern system, and the one our gallery reads against the blankets: geometric relief designs combed, incised and painted by women into the wet mud plaster of homestead walls, then repainted seasonally. Designs are built on a square grid and worked in fourfold rotation or mirror symmetry, in ochre, white lime, blue-black and cow-dung greys.

**Grass matting and basketry (*moseme*)** — Coiled and plaited reed and grass mats for sleeping, sitting, threshing and grain storage, made by women from river reed and *loli* grass; the plaiting sequence itself produces the banded and chevroned surface, without added dye.

**Thethana** — A beaded or twisted-fibre waist apron of short hanging strands worn by girls and unmarried women, and a central garment of the *lebollo la basadi* girls' initiation, where it is worn with grey blankets and goatskin skirts.

**Seshoeshoe (*seshweshwe*)** — The indigo or brown discharge-printed cotton, named for Moshoeshoe, cut into tiered dresses with ribbon trim bordering each layer. Made up locally in a silhouette closer to West and East African dress than to the German-print originals from which the cloth derives.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Seanamarena* maize cob (fertility and staple food), shield and spear (chieftaincy), crown (allegiance to the monarchy), *pitiki* stripe (the blanket's orientation marker); in *litema*, chevron and zigzag fields, lozenge grids, fern- and leaf-derived combed relief, the fourfold rotated square, and the *balaclava*-fine comb-tooth hatching that gives the plaster its texture.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday rural dress layers the blanket over Western clothing rather than replacing it. Herd boys in the Lesotho highlands and the eastern Free State wear a blanket pinned at the shoulder, heavy gumboots against wet mountain terrain, and a woollen balaclava or knitted cap worn year-round against cold and dust — a herding uniform recognizable at a distance and now a fashion citation in its own right. Men wear the blanket with the *mokorotlo*; a *molamu* (fighting stick) completes the set at public gatherings. Women wear long bright printed skirts and dresses, with a blanket or a print cloth wrapped at the waist as a second garment or as a sling for carrying an infant on the back; *seshoeshoe* dresses are for weddings and formal occasions. Initiation dress is distinct: girls at *lebollo la basadi* wear the *thethana* apron, grey blankets and goatskin skirts; boys at *lebollo la banna* wear the *tshea* loincloth with brightly coloured blankets, often combined with sunglasses and other modern items. Traditional healers wear the *bandolier*, crossed strips and strings of leather, sinew or beadwork over the chest, hung with pouches of medicines — an attire the San adopted from Basotho during a period of trade and intermarriage, and which appears in San rock paintings from the 1700s.

### Architecture

The homestead unit is the *mohlongoa-fatše* or, in its best-known round form, the *rondavel* — a circular wall of stone or moulded mud coursing under a conical thatch roof tied to a central pole, the thatch bound in tiers and finished with a knotted crown. Stone construction is characteristic of the Lesotho highlands, where dressed sandstone blocks are laid dry or in mud mortar and then plastered; on the lowveld and in the Free State the wall is pure earth. Entrances are low, floors are of smoothed dung-and-earth (*lithoto*), and interior benches, hearth surrounds and storage bins are modelled from the same plaster. The decorative system is *litema*: the outer wall, the courtyard screen wall (*lelapa*) and the doorway surround are combed and painted by the women of the household, conventionally before weddings, at the end of the agricultural year and for the return of initiates. Mountain settlements are terraced along contours, with stone-walled cattle kraals and small corbelled stone huts used as field shelters. Moshoeshoe's mountain capitals, Butha-Buthe and then Thaba Bosiu, show the defensive logic that shaped highland siting — flat-topped hills with restricted passes.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Basotho pottery is hand-built by coiling, burnished with a pebble and open-fired to a red-brown or blackened surface; the repertoire runs to the large beer pot, the narrow-necked water jar and the small drinking bowl, decorated with incised bands, punctate rows and applied bosses near the shoulder rather than painted. Beer pots for *joala* are central at feasts and ancestral offerings. Wooden objects include the *molamu* stick, carved headrests, meat platters, milk pails and the wooden or horn snuff container (*nkgo* / *lekhetho*), often plugged with a stopper and worn on a thong — snuff-taking is bound up with greeting and with addressing the ancestors. Iron-working was established in Basotho communities by the sixteenth century, producing hoes, spear and axe blades; brass and copper wire, obtained through trade, was wound onto sticks and bangles. Grass and reed work — mats, grain baskets, beer strainers, winnowing trays — carries as much technical refinement as the ceramics.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Beadwork is worked in glass seed beads on fibre and leather grounds: the *thethana* waist apron, beaded headbands and collars, beaded strips on the healer's bandolier, and wide beaded bands for arms and ankles. Colour and bead density mark age grade and marital status, with white and pale beads associated with initiates and with the ancestral and healing domains. Brass and copper wire coils are worn at the wrist, neck and ankle. Amulets — small hide or cloth pouches containing medicinal roots (*litšoantšo*) prepared by a *ngaka* — are worn on neck cords or sewn to the bandolier for protection. Hair practices run from close-cropping for initiates and mourners to elaborate plaited and ochre-dressed styles; ochre and clay are applied to the body during *lebollo*, and white clay marks the initiate's liminal state.

## Music & performance

Basotho music is overwhelmingly vocal and pitched around massed part-singing. The best-known secular genre is *mokorotlo*, a men's processional song of deep, slow, declamatory unison and overlapping shouts, sung on the way to war, to court, at chiefs' gatherings and now at national occasions; *mokhibo*, sung by women, accompanies the kneeling dance of the same name, driven by handclapping and a sharply articulated call-and-response. *Litolobonya* is women's song-dance performed among women only. Herd boys are the principal instrumentalists: the *lekolulo*, a notched end-blown reed flute, is the pastoral instrument, played to cattle in the mountains; the *thomo* is a woman's braced calabash-resonated bow, and the *setolo-tolo* a mouth-resonated bow played by men, both producing a thin melodic line over a drone. The *lesiba*, Lesotho's signature instrument, is a stick-and-string aerophone with a quill lashed at one end: the player blows across the quill, which sets the string vibrating, producing a hoarse buzzing tone used to call and calm cattle and to imitate speech. Drums (*moropa*) belong chiefly to initiation and to diviners' ceremonies. Since the nineteenth-century mission schools, four-part choral singing has been a defining Basotho practice; Joshua Pulumo Mohapeloa and Michael Mosoeu Moerane composed the core Sesotho choral repertoire, and the jazz band Sankomota, with Tsepo Tshola, carried the idiom into popular music.

## Dance & theatre

**Mokhibo** is the emblematic women's dance: dancers kneel and drive the shoulders and upper torso in rhythmic pulses while seated on their heels, blankets worn at the waist, the movement carried almost entirely above the hips and cued by clapping and song. **Mohobelo** is its male counterpart — a striding, high-stepping dance of long gliding paces, leaps and stamped landings, performed in blankets and hats with sticks held out, and read as a display of a man's carriage. **Ndlamo** (borrowed from Nguni neighbours) uses the high kick and heavy stamp. **Litolobonya** is a women's dance of pelvic and buttock articulation performed away from men. Initiation carries its own choreography and songs, restricted to initiates and their guardians; diviners' ceremonies involve possession dancing with drum and clapping. There is no indigenous masked or puppet theatre; dramatic performance runs instead through praise recitation (see Oral tradition), through mission and school concert traditions, and since the twentieth century through Sesotho-language radio and television drama on SABC and the Lesotho National Broadcasting Service.

## Festivals & rituals

The Sotho year is marked by a lunar calendar of thirteen named months tied to agricultural and animal signs, and the major collective occasions are agricultural rather than calendrical: first-fruits and harvest feasts, communal work parties (*matsema*) ended with beer, and the rain-making rites (*mophato*) addressed through chiefs and diviners in drought. The dominant life-cycle institution is initiation, *lebollo*: boys withdraw to a mountain lodge (*mophato*) for months of instruction, endurance and circumcision, returning with new names, new blankets and a public song; girls' *lebollo la basadi* similarly secludes initiates who emerge in *thethana*, grey blankets and goatskin. Marriage proceeds through *bohali*, cattle transferred from the groom's family to the bride's, negotiated by intermediaries, and is followed by the *pitiki*, the ritual for a woman after childbirth, and by the repainting of the homestead in *litema*. Funerals are large and slow, with night vigils, massed hymn singing, and the ritual washing of hands and shaving of heads by the bereaved; the deceased joins the *balimo*, ancestors who mediate with *Modimo*, the remote supreme being. Feast days in the Christian calendar — Lesotho's population is majority Catholic — fold ancestral offerings of beer and slaughtered beast into Christian observance. National days in Lesotho (Moshoeshoe's Day, 11 March; Independence Day, 4 October) are the occasions on which *mokorotlo*, blankets and *mohobelo* appear in mass performance.

## Foodways

Maize and sorghum are the staples, pounded or milled and boiled into *papa* (stiff maize porridge), the base of nearly every meal, eaten by hand with a relish. *Moroho* — wild and cultivated greens, pumpkin leaves, *thepe* (amaranth), spinach, boiled and often dressed with onion — is the standard accompaniment; *nama* (meat, chiefly mutton and beef) marks feasts rather than ordinary days. *Likhobe* is boiled whole grain, often cooked with beans; *ditlhobolo* / samp-and-bean dishes take the same form. *Motoho* is the fermented sorghum soft porridge drunk warm at breakfast, sour and thin. Sour milk (*mafi*), thickened in a gourd or clay vessel, is eaten with maize meal. *Joala*, sorghum beer brewed by women over several days in a clay pot and served from it with a woven strainer, is the ritual drink: no ancestral offering, initiation return, wedding or funeral is complete without it, and a flag flown outside a homestead advertises fresh brew. Wild foods matter in the highlands — *khabu* and other tubers, wild spinach, prickly pear fruit. Ceremonial slaughter of an ox or sheep, with the distribution of specific cuts by seniority and the beast's portion set aside for the ancestors, is the central food act of the ritual cycle.

## Oral tradition & literature

The praise poem, *thoko* (praises of a person or chief) and *lithoko* (the heroic corpus), is the prestige oral genre: dense, allusive, metaphor-heavy verse recited at high speed by a praiser to declare a man's lineage, cattle, battles and character. *Lithoko tsa marena*, the praises of the chiefs — above all of Moshoeshoe I — form a historical archive in verse, collected from the nineteenth century by the French missionaries Eugène Casalis and Thomas Arbousset, who between 1837 and 1855 also fixed Sesotho orthography and printed the first Sesotho materials; the Sesotho Bible followed in 1878. Folktales (*litšomo*) are told by women at night and feature the ogre *Koqoloko*, the cannibal (*ledimo*) tales, and the hero-child Sankatana who kills the swallowing monster Kholumolumo. Proverbs (*maele*) and riddles (*dilotho*) are used pedagogically and forensically, in initiation instruction and in chiefly courts. Written Sesotho literature begins with Thomas Mofolo, whose *Chaka* (1925) is among the earliest African-language novels and has been translated into English, German and French; Lesedi FM and the Sesotho press in Lesotho and the Free State carry the language's contemporary oral and literary life.

## Language & religion

Sesotho (Southern Sotho, *Sesotho sa borwa*) belongs to the Sotho-Tswana branch of Southern Bantu, separated from Setswana by roughly the fourteenth century and closely related to Silozi, spoken by 60,000 people in Zambia — a legacy of the Kololo migration of 1823 across the Zambezi and their conquest of Barotseland in 1845. It is co-official with English in Lesotho and one of South Africa's eleven official languages. Missionary orthography, Latin-based, was settled by Casalis and Arbousset, with Lesotho and South African spelling conventions diverging. The religious landscape is overwhelmingly Christian, with a Catholic majority in Lesotho, layered over an older cosmology: *Modimo*, the supreme being, is unapproachable by the living, and the *balimo* (ancestors) intercede, addressed through beer libation, slaughter and the diviner-healer *ngaka*, who divines with bones (*ditaola*) and dispenses plant medicine.
