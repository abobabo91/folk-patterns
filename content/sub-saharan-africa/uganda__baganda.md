## Overview

The Baganda are a Bantu people mainly from Buganda in Uganda. Every Muganda belongs to a clan, and the Kabaka has a central role in social and political life. Their culture includes barkcloth, courtly music, named marriage and death rites, and stories about Kintu and Nnambi. Baganda communities also live in several countries outside Uganda.

## Material culture

Barkcloth, pottery, baskets, shields, tools, musical instruments, and beadwork are important parts of Baganda material culture.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Barkcloth, especially Lubugo, was the most common traditional cloth in Buganda.

- **Barkcloth** (*Lubugo*) — Lubugo was made from the bark of many trees, especially the Mutuba, and was used for clothing, bedding, and wrapping goods.
- **Fine rusty-brown barkcloth** (*Kimote*) — Kimote was the finest quality of barkcloth and had a rusty-brown color.
- **Barkcloth beater** — Wooden barkcloth beaters had grooved or serrated heads, and some had carved pointed patterns.
- **Decorated barkcloth** — Some barkcloth had black geometric patterns, bands, seams, repairs, and stains.
- **Barkcloth patterns** — Recorded motifs include geometric patterns, pointed patterns, bands, and chequer plaiting.

### Clothing & dress

The Gomesi and Kanzu are the traditional clothes of Baganda women and men.

- **Women’s dress** (*Gomesi*) — The Gomesi is the traditional dress of Baganda women.
- **Men’s dress** (*Kanzu*) — The Kanzu is the traditional dress of Baganda men.
- **Bark cloak** (*Lubugo*) — Bark cloaks could look like yellow corduroy and were carefully crimped and stiffened.
- **Goat-skin garment** — Some recorded garments were made from several pieces of goat skin sewn together and pierced around the edges.
- **Gazelle-skin robe** — Museum records include a robe made from white gazelle skin.

### Architecture

The sources do not describe Baganda houses or named building types.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Baganda everyday objects included pottery, baskets, snuff containers, shields, knives, and grain-pounding tools.

- **Red pottery milk vessel** — Some pottery bottles were blackened with plumbago and had long necks, cup-shaped lips, globular bodies, and red-and-white bands.
- **Wooden pot with cap** — A recorded wooden pot had a plaited cap.
- **Beer strainer** — A basketry beer strainer is among the recorded household objects.
- **Cartridge-case snuff container** — One snuff container was made from two brass cartridge cases soldered together.
- **Shield** — Recorded shields were made from wood, cane wicker-work, or basket-work and could have bosses or ornamentation.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Baganda adornment used hair, beads, iron, ivory, silver, fibre, leaf, and cowrie shells.

- **Elephant-tail necklace** — Some necklaces were made from elephant-tail hair and red glass beads.
- **Ivory anklet** — An ivory child’s anklet is among the recorded ornaments.
- **Silver armlet** — A recorded silver armlet had a hinged construction.
- **Dance anklet** — A dance anklet or rattle was made from an iron bar with nine iron beads.
- **Beaded waist ornament** — One waist ornament had 102 strands of plaited fibre covered with white, red, and blue glass beads.

## Music & performance

Baganda music uses xylophones, harps, lyres, drums, and lamellophones in a pentatonic musical system.

- **Log xylophone** (*Amadinda*) — The amadinda has 12 wooden bars placed on two fresh banana stems and is played by three musicians.
- **Court xylophone** (*Akadinda*) — The akadinda was used in the courtly music of the Kabaka.
- **Harp** (*Ennanga*) — The ennanga was used in courtly music and could have scales fixed to its strings to create buzzing sounds.
- **Lyre** (*Etongoli*) — The etongoli lyre was used in Baganda music and courtly music.
- **Lamellophone** (*Kadongo*) — The kadongo entered Baganda music in the early twentieth century and is also called budongo in the plural.

## Dance & theatre

The sources do not describe named dances or theatre traditions, but dancing is part of the twin-naming ceremony.

- **Twin-naming dance** (*Okuzina abalongo*) — Dancing forms part of okuzina abalongo, the naming and confirmation ceremony for twins.

## Festivals & rituals

Baganda rites mark naming, twins, marriage, death, and the end of mourning.

- **Child naming rite** (*Okwalula abaana*) — In okwalula abaana, a child was named and confirmed as a member of the clan using a saved piece of the umbilical cord.
- **Twin-naming ceremony** (*Okuzina abalongo*) — Okuzina abalongo confirms twins and includes drinking, eating, obscene words, promiscuity, and dancing.
- **Bride’s introduction ceremony** (*Okwanjula*) — Okwanjula is the traditional introduction ceremony for the bride-to-be.
- **Wedding reception** (*Embaga*) — Embaga is the wedding reception in traditional marriage.
- **End of mourning rite** (*Okwabya Olumbe*) — Okwabya Olumbe marks the end of mourning days or months after burial.

## Foodways

The sources give little detail about Baganda food, but mention eating, drinking, and pounding grain.

- **Grain pounding** — A recorded photograph shows a Ganda man pounding grain with a wooden pestle and mortar.
- **Eating and drinking at twin rites** — Eating and drinking are parts of the twin-naming ceremony.

## Oral tradition & literature

Baganda origin stories trace the people to Kintu and Nnambi and explain death through Walumbe.

- **First man** (*Kintu*) — Kintu is described as the first man on earth and in Buganda.
- **Mother of the Baganda** (*Nnambi*) — Nnambi was the daughter of Ggulu, wife of Kintu, and the Baganda are called her children.
- **Cause of death** (*Walumbe*) — Walumbe is Nnambi’s jealous brother and is blamed in the myth for disease, sickness, and death.
- **Protector and pursuer** (*Kayikuuzi*) — Kayikuuzi tried to protect humans and continues trying to capture Walumbe underground.
- **Proverb** — A recorded Ganda proverb says, “He who makes you shed tears, you make him shed blood.”

## Language & religion

Luganda is a tonal language, and Baganda religious life includes Abrahamic faiths, traditional beliefs, clans, and totems.

- **Traditional language** (*Luganda*) — Luganda is the traditional tonal language of the Baganda, and its tones influence song melodies.
- **People of the clan** (*Muganda*) — A single Baganda person is called a Muganda.
- **The Baganda people** (*Abaganda*) — Abaganda is another name for the Baganda.
- **Traditional gods and spirits** (*Lubaales*) — The Lubaales are among the traditional religious references of the Baganda.
- **Clan totem** — Each clan has a totem animal, plant, or thing that members must not eat or harm.
