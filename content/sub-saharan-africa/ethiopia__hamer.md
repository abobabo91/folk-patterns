## Overview

The Hamar, also spelled Hamer, are a rural agropastoral community living in the mountainous and lowland areas of the Lower Omo Valley in Southern Ethiopia. They live on the eastern side of the Omo River, in Hamar Woreda of the South Omo Zone, whose administrative center is Dimeka. The Hamar district has a total population of 64,925, including 61,227 people living in rural areas. Hamar is a South Omotic language within the Afroasiatic language family and is spoken primarily by the Hamar people, Banna people, and speakers of Karo. Hamar culture combines livestock keeping, cultivation, beekeeping, hunting and gathering, ritual leadership, age-set organization, and ceremonies connected with adulthood, marriage, social identity, and spiritual purity.

### Architecture

 They state that smaller livestock herds remain near the village, while older boys and men may care for cattle in faraway camps, and that development projects introduced schools, roads, and markets during the Derg regime.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention cattle, goats, sheep, camels, farming, beekeeping, and hunting and gathering as parts of the economy.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 They do state that women’s scars from whipping during the bull-jumping ceremony are worn with pride and symbolize a woman’s devotion to men.

## Music & performance

The sources describe performance primarily through ceremonies and dance rather than through named musical instruments or song genres. **Evangadi**, translated as “night dance,” is a nighttime dance and play performed by adolescent and unmarried males and females. It is also described as a setting in which participants choose future marriage partners.

The **bull-jumping ceremony** is the most significant ceremony among the Hamar. It reflects masculinity and social cohesion and marks a man’s passage from boyhood to manhood. After completing it, a man can own cattle and marry. Unmarried men who have successfully completed the ceremony are known as *maza* and are responsible for whipping women during the associated practice. A *maza* enters into an agreement with each woman he has whipped, promising to support her if the need arises.

## Dance & theatre

The sources identify **Evangadi** as a Hamar nighttime dance and play involving adolescent and unmarried males and females. Its social function includes providing a ground for choosing future marriage partners.

The bull-jumping ceremony is a rite of passage from boyhood to manhood rather than an entertainment form. Its associated whipping of women is encouraged by the community but is identified by human rights advocates and local authorities as a harmful traditional practice because it can cause physical injury, excessive bleeding, and wound infections.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do document several important Hamar rituals and cultural events.

The **bull-jumping ceremony** marks a man’s transition from boyhood to manhood. Completion permits him to own cattle and marry. The ceremony is connected with the social position of *maza*, unmarried men who have completed the rite and who whip women. Women may compete over who will be whipped first, and scars from the whipping are described as signs of devotion to men. Human rights advocates and local advocacy groups contest the practice as harmful.

**Evangadi** is a nighttime dance and play involving adolescent and unmarried males and females. It is associated with the selection of future marriage partners.

*Mingi* is a condition of ritual impurity or spiritual pollution. The sources state that impurity may be associated with a spiritual leader carrying a gun or killing a person, sexual relations with a kinsman, the premature eruption of upper teeth, or an unnatural birth. Mingi children are believed to bring harm to their families. Historically, a person considered *mingi* could be killed through forced permanent separation from the group, by being left alone in the jungle or drowned in the river.

The Hamar social structure includes two ritual leaders, known as *bitta*, one from the Gatta clan and one from the Worla clan. They oversee spiritual matters but do not possess political authority. Hamar society is also organized into twenty-four exogamous clans grouped into two moieties.

## Foodways

The Hamar economy combines livestock keeping, cultivation, beekeeping, and hunting and gathering. Livestock include cattle, goats, sheep, and, more recently, camels acquired through government and non-governmental organizations. Cultivated foods include sorghum, maize, and varieties of beans.

The sources mention a beverage made from coffee sheath, locally called *shoforo*. A woman may prepare tea made from coffee sheath as part of her daily household work. The sources also describe women collecting firewood and small leaves and branches for small and weak livestock, while girls may cook meals in the farms and boys may care for cattle, goats, and sheep.

## Oral tradition & literature

Written sources on Hamar origins and history before the conquest by Emperor Menelik II toward the end of the nineteenth century are scarce. The sources describe Hamar oral history as central to accounts of migration and intergroup relations. The Hamar trace their origins to migration into the Lower Omo Valley and acknowledge the presence of the mythical Boa people, believed to have been cattle herders who inhabited the area before them.

Oral tradition recounts that a Hamar ritual leader used magical powers to drive the Borana eastward, clearing the way for Hamar settlement. Hamar histories also recall encounters with the Arbore, Dasanech, Murle, Kara, and Kwegu. Relations with the Dasanech were sometimes peaceful, including periods when the Hamar sought refuge among them during a severe drought and after defeat by Menelik’s troops.

Ivo Strecker wrote the important paper on Hamar origin myth identified by the source.

## Language & religion

Hamar or Hamer-Banna is a language of the South Omotic branch of the Afroasiatic language family. It is spoken primarily in southern Ethiopia by the Hamar people, Banna people, and speakers of Karo. A pidginized variety is in local use. Hamar has no official writing system, although several romanization schemes and a Gə'əz orthography have been proposed. There is no movement for official recognition of these systems.

The language uses a base-ten numeral system for numbers from one to nineteen and a base-twenty system for numbers greater than nineteen. The noun *gur* represents “zero,” and the verb *paidá* describes counting. Younger speakers may use the Amharic term *qutʼər* for “number.”

Hamar religion includes concepts of ritual purity and impurity, including *mingi*. The two *bitta* ritual leaders oversee spiritual matters.
