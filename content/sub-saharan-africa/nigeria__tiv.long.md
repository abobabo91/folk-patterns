## Overview

The Tiv are a Bantu-speaking people of the middle Benue Valley in central Nigeria, numbering over eight million and making up roughly 3.5% of Nigeria's population. Their heartland is Benue State, with substantial populations in Taraba, Nasarawa, Plateau and northern Cross River, and a border community in the Akwaya sub-division of Cameroon's Manyu division (87,252 counted in the 2005 Cameroonian census). The Tiv language belongs to the Benue–Congo branch of Niger–Congo. Tiv oral tradition describes a migration — the "coming down" — from the Bantu homeland via the Congo River and the Cameroon highlands to Swem, then a dispersal into the Benue Valley between roughly 1600 and 1700 CE. In folk-culture terms they matter for three things: the black-and-white striped *a'nger* cloth, the *kwagh-hir* puppet theatre, and a wooden signalling-instrument repertoire (*indyer*, *ilyu*, *gbande*) that functioned as a full communications system in a society with no kings.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**A'nger** — the defining Tiv cloth: narrow warp-striped strips in black (or deep indigo) and white, sewn selvedge-to-selvedge into a large panel or robe. Tiv tradition explains the design as a deliberate memorial: on reaching the Benue Valley the migrants found no zebra, whose hide had supplied ceremonial dress, and on acquiring the loom they wove the zebra's stripes into cloth instead. Unlike the multicoloured strip-weaves of the Yoruba (*aso oke*) or Asante *kente*, *a'nger* is rigorously bichrome, and its identity rests on stripe rhythm rather than colour.

**Gberyum** — a striped cloth in the *a'nger* family whose bands run in a distinct proportion from the standard stripe, marking a different grade of cloth within the same visual logic.

**Godo** — a heavier, broader-banded strip-weave used as a wrapper and a body cloth; its wide bands read at a distance and it is the type most often worn draped from the shoulder.

**Lishi** — a finer-striped variant, the narrow-line end of the Tiv range, where the black and white bands are reduced to close pinstripes.

**Tugudu** — historically the most economically loaded Tiv cloth: a large plain-weave panel, often pale or undyed, that circulated as a unit of value and prestige exchange. Paul and Laura Bohannan's fieldwork in the 1950s documented *tugudu* cloth as one of the goods that moved in the Tiv "spheres of exchange," alongside brass rods and livestock, rather than in ordinary market barter.

**Ivav** — cloth used in wrapping and bundling contexts, including the enclosure of ritually charged objects and the presentation of goods at marriage.

**Motif vocabulary.** The Tiv visual repertoire is fundamentally linear and countable rather than pictorial: warp stripe (the zebra reference underlying all *a'nger*); paired black-white alternation; broad band (*godo* proportion); pinstripe (*lishi* proportion); concentric and nested circles, carried onto carved surfaces; chevron and zigzag; the sun-and-ray figure; the lizard and the tortoise, both drawn schematically; and the scarification lines of *avan* — chest, abdomen and facial patterning — which share their geometry with cloth and with carved wood.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress in the twentieth century converged on Nigerian norms, but *a'nger* remains the marker of Tiv identity and is worn at every public occasion of consequence. The original form was a single length of striped cloth draped around the torso and over one shoulder; it is now tailored into long robes, caps, wrappers and shirts. Rank is legible in the elaboration: the *Tor Tiv* — the paramount office created under colonial administration in 1946 — and the elders below him wear the fullest robes, worn with black-and-white beaded necklaces chosen deliberately to match the cloth. Women wear *a'nger* as wrapper and blouse with a matching head-tie. At a *kwase kuhan*, the marriage reception, both bride and groom appear in *a'nger*, and the outfit is considered incomplete without accessories: headpieces, bracelets and necklaces, most commonly in black-and-white beads. Ear-piercing was general to both sexes; young men historically also filed their teeth.

### Architecture

The Tiv built compound (*tar*) is the physical expression of the lineage: the members of an *ipaven*, the smallest lineage unit, live adjacent to one another, so the settlement is a genealogical diagram on the ground. Houses are round, with walls of mud over a stick armature and steeply pitched conical roofs thatched in grass, arranged in a ring around a swept central yard. The compound's focal structure is the reception hut — an open-sided, roofed circular shelter on posts, without walls, where the senior man of the compound, the *orya*, receives visitors, hears disputes and sits with other elders through the day; it is the architectural counterpart of a courthouse in a society that had neither chiefs nor councils. Granaries for yam and millet stand raised on stones or timber within the compound. Farming settlements are large by regional standards, ranging from about a thousand to tens of thousands of people.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The signature Tiv objects are carved in wood. The **indyer** is a slit drum hollowed from a mahogany trunk through an opening so small that tradition holds the carver turns into a worm to eat out the interior — a story the carvers themselves do not contradict. **Agbande** are the large drums played by young men at festivals; the **ilyu** is a light wooden idiophone. Domestic carving covers mortars and pestles for pounding yam, stools, and lidded bowls. In iron, the *ityogh* pickaxe served as the circumcision tool before razor blades (*atsem*) displaced it in the twentieth century, and hoes and blades for yam cultivation came from local smiths. Brass rods, obtained through trade, were a prestige and exchange good rather than a Tiv product.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Black-and-white beadwork is the Tiv rule, matched to *a'nger*: strands of necklace beads for rulers and elders, and bracelets, waistbeads and headpieces for brides. Body marking is the more distinctive tradition. The Tiv carried no marks during their period of cohabitation with the Fulani; after separating from them they adopted body patterning specifically to distinguish themselves, as the Fulani had. Young men cut patterns on the face, pierced the ears and sometimes sharpened the teeth; young women cut patterns across the abdomen — raised keloid work whose geometry, in concentric and radiating lines, matches the carved and woven repertoire. The practice has largely lapsed since the mid-twentieth century, surviving in museum photography and in the motif stock of Tiv design.

## Music & performance

Tiv instruments were a communication system before they were entertainment, and the distinction between message and music is not sharp. The **kakaki**, the long metal royal trumpet shared with Hausa, Nupe and other Sahelian courts, announces royal events — a birth in the ruler's household, a naming, a coronation, a royal marriage — and sounds the alarm at an attack. The **indyer**, the great mahogany slit drum, is held to carry magico-spiritual potency and is not played for secular purposes without the elders' sanction; its voice announces the death of an important person or a call to war. The **ilyu** summons people to the elders' meeting or the market square, and is today the standard instrument for the marriage reception. The **adiguve** is a bowed one-string fiddle, played with *agbande* for dance, and played slowly and mournfully at funerals and at the death of an elder. **Akya** joins *agbande* and *ageda* at festivals to call out the display of culture. The **kolugh ku bua**, a cow-horn trumpet comparable to a shofar, is blown by farmers' work associations to raise their members for a day's ridging. A human link completes the system: the *ortindin* (or *ortyom*), the messenger chosen by the elders to carry word to neighbouring family heads. Tiv communities in Benue still use all of these.

## Dance & theatre

The Tiv's major performance form is **kwagh-hir** — literally a "thing of magic" — a night theatre combining carved masks, articulated rod and string puppets, narration, drumming and dance. Troupes stage sequences of tableaux in which puppets and masked figures enact legend, current events and political commentary; the performers take the part of the *adzov*, the bush spirits, and keep their identities concealed until the end of the performance, when each is recognised by his individual style. *Kwagh-hir* emerged in its modern competitive form in the mid-twentieth century and became the principal vehicle of Tiv public satire. Alongside it stands a written Tiv-language drama in which the oral repertoire is reworked for the stage: **Adan Wade** by Suemo Chia, **Sons of Akpe** by Boniface Leva, and **A Close Shave** by Chris Kyoive. Social dancing to *agbande* and *adiguve* — vigorous, waist-driven, performed in lines and rings by age-sets — accompanies weddings, festivals and the installation of officeholders.

## Festivals & rituals

The Tiv ritual year is not keyed to a single named calendar festival but to the yam cycle and the demands of the lineage. The harvest of yams, cocoyam and beni-seed brings the largest gatherings, with *agbande* drumming, *kwagh-hir* performances and the display of *a'nger*; since the 1990s these have been consolidated into state-level Tiv Day celebrations centred on the *Tor Tiv*'s palace at Gboko. The older ritual system turns on **akombo** — the material emblems and rites governing fertility, illness, farming and social order, whose "repair" by a qualified elder restores what has been disturbed — and on **tsav**, the inner power attributed to certain individuals, which can protect the lineage or be turned against it. The ancestral hill of **Swem** is the ultimate oath-place: an oath sworn on Swem earth is believed to kill a liar, and the swearing of *swem* remained the strongest Tiv sanction into the colonial period. Life-cycle rites: circumcision, once performed on teenagers with the *ityogh* pickaxe, is now done in hospital days after birth, but retains its genealogical weight, since all Tiv descend from either Ichongo ("circumcised") or Ipusu ("uncircumcised"). Marriage has moved through distinct forms — *yamshe*, the exchange of sisters between two men seeking wives; *kwase tsuen*, marriage by capture; *iye*, courtship and elopement; and *kwase kemen*, bride-price marriage, which became the norm after the colonial administration abolished the Tiv exchange-marriage system in 1927. Funerals of elders bring the *adiguve* played in mourning and, for a major figure, the *indyer*.

## Foodways

Benue is called the food basket of Nigeria, and the Tiv diet is built on roots and pounded starches. The staples are yam (*iyou*), cocoyam (*mondu*), cassava (*alogo*), beans (*alev*) and maize (*kyuleke*). The central dish is *ruam kumen*, pounded yam, with *ruam nahan* (fufu) beside it; also *pete*, yam pottage; *akuto*, sweet-potato pottage; *akpukpa* (okpa), a steamed bambara-bean pudding; and *ibyer*, a fermented cereal pudding that is both food and drink. Roasted and fried yams, *ahuma*, *kuese* bean cakes and jollof rice fill out the everyday table. The soup repertoire is unusually long and is where Tiv cooking is most distinctive: *ichegh*, *pocho*, *ager*, *ive*, *genger*, *atyever*, *tur*, *vambe*, *igyo*, *agbende ashwe*, *atuur*, *aninge*, *adenger*, *gbungu*, *mngishim* (garden-egg soup) and *ijôv*, mushroom soup. Sesame — which the British named beni-seed after the Benue, and which became the valley's main export crop — is eaten as a snack seed and ground into pastes; *n'gyata* is peanut paste. Other snacks are *asondo* (dried sweet potato), *igbough ahi* (roasted or boiled bambara nuts), *mzembe* (roasted pears) and *huu*, roasted termites. Drinks are palm and grain-based: *tyo*, *burukutu*, *atemba a suwanbin* and *ibyer*.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Tiv had no script; their history is oral, and genealogy is its principal literary form. The recitation of descent from Tiv — son of Takuruku and Aliwe, husband of Ayaaya, father of Ichongo and Ipusu — and downward through major and minor branches to the *ipaven* is both a narrative and the operative charter of the segmentary lineage system, which the Tiv exemplify in West Africa as the Nuer do in East Africa. Versions differ: the Anyamazenga tradition recorded by Cletus A. Lanshima adds Gbe and Anadendem as further children of Tiv and Ayaaya, where shorter accounts give only the two sons. Beyond genealogy the repertoire runs to animal trickster tales, riddles, praise and abuse songs, and the narrated episodes that supply *kwagh-hir* its material — *kwagh-hir* is best understood as oral storytelling given puppets. Documentation began with Sigismund Koelle's 1854 record of the language from freed slaves in Sierra Leone (*Polyglotta Africana*); systematic work followed with R. C. Abraham and Rev. W. A. Malherbe in 1933 and the Bohannans' ethnography in the 1950s.

## Language & religion

Tiv (*Tiiv*) is a Benue–Congo language of the Niger–Congo phylum, spoken by over eight million people. Its affiliation was long disputed: Harry Johnston (1919) and P. Amaury Talbot (1926) classed it as Semi-Bantu; Abraham and Malherbe's 1933 study classified it as Bantu, Abraham noting its lexical closeness to the Nyanza group of East Africa. There was no pre-colonial script, and the modern orthography is Latin. The Tiv are predominantly Christian: Dutch Reformed Church missionaries from South Africa reached the Tiv village of Sai in Katsina-Ala in 1911 and founded the church now known as the NKST (*Nongo u Kristu u i Ser u sha Tar*), the dominant denomination in Tivland. Catholic and Pentecostal churches are also strong. *Akombo* rites, *tsav*, and oaths on *swem* earth persist alongside Christianity rather than under it, and the fear of *tsav* continues to shape social behaviour.
