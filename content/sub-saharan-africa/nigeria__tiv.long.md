## Overview

The Tiv are the largest ethnic group of Nigeria's Middle Belt, occupying the valleys of the Benue and Katsina-Ala rivers with Makurdi and Gboko as their principal centres, and are commonly estimated today at between five and seven million people. Linguistically they stand somewhat apart from their neighbours: Tiv is a Southern Bantoid language within Benue–Congo, more closely related to the Bantu languages of the far south than to the Jukun, Idoma or Hausa tongues that surround them, and its speakers' own traditions of origin trace a migration northwards and westwards from the Cameroon highlands, probably arriving in the Benue trough within the last several centuries. They organised themselves without chiefs for most of their recorded history, in a vast segmentary lineage system in which every Tiv could in principle trace descent from a single ancestor named Tiv through a branching genealogy of *ityo* (agnatic lineage segments), a structure made famous in anthropology by Paul and Laura Bohannan's fieldwork of the 1940s and 1950s and by their analysis of the Tiv "spheres of exchange". Colonial administration imposed a paramount office, the *Tor Tiv*, in 1946, which remains the group's central symbolic institution. Materially the Tiv are best known for three things: the boldly striped indigo-and-white cloth called *a'nger*, now worn as a national-level Nigerian marker of Tiv identity; the *kwagh-hir*, a composite theatre of carved and articulated puppets, masks, song and narration that emerged in its present form in the mid-twentieth century; and an agricultural reputation as the great yam farmers of the Benue, whose labour discipline and land hunger shaped the demography of the whole Middle Belt.

## Material culture

Tiv material culture is agrarian, portable and relatively unmonumental: it concentrates on woven cloth, carved wood, worked iron, raffia and clay, with the most elaborate artistry reserved for cloth, for the puppet-and-mask complex of *kwagh-hir*, and formerly for the surface of the human body itself.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Tiv weaving is built on narrow strips of hand-spun cotton sewn edge to edge, with the pattern carried almost entirely by the arrangement of warp stripes rather than by figurative or supplementary-weft ornament; the resulting aesthetic is severe, rhythmic and immediately recognisable.

- **Striped cloth** (*a'nger*) — The signature Tiv textile: narrow strips woven in alternating black or deep indigo and white warp stripes, sewn together into a large wrapper. The stripe intervals are read as a deliberate visual rhythm, and the cloth has become the standard emblem of Tiv identity at Nigerian national occasions.
- **Anger** (*anger*) — The general Tiv term for woven cloth and for the strip-woven wrapper, from which the specific named patterns are distinguished; in use the word covers both the medium and the finished garment.
- **Gberyum** (*gberyum*) — A named Tiv cloth pattern distinguished by its particular stripe sequence and width; such pattern names function much as tartan names do, marking an identifiable design rather than a technique.
- **Godo** (*godo*) — Another named strip-cloth pattern in the Tiv repertoire, commonly cited alongside *a'nger* and *gberyum* among the recognised designs woven on the narrow horizontal loom.
- **Lishi** (*lishi*) — A further named Tiv cloth design; the multiplicity of such names indicates a weaving tradition in which small variations of stripe proportion were socially legible.
- **Tugudu** (*tugudu*) — A white or undyed cloth type, historically important beyond dress: cloth of this kind circulated in the Tiv prestige-exchange sphere described by the Bohannans, alongside brass rods and cattle, rather than in the ordinary market for subsistence goods.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday Tiv dress is the strip-woven wrapper, worn draped over one shoulder or tied at the waist, with the striped cloth reserved for public and ceremonial visibility; modern Tiv formal dress has standardised the *a'nger* into tailored shirts, caps and gowns.

- **Striped wrapper** (*a'nger*) — Worn by men draped over the left shoulder and gathered at the waist, and by women as a wrapped skirt with a matching upper cloth; the black-and-white field reads at a distance as a single identifying sign.
- **Tugudu wrapper** (*tugudu*) — Plain white cloth used for wrapping, for gifts and in ritual contexts where the bold stripe would be inappropriate, including some *akombo* observances and mortuary use.
- **Cap and tailored shirt** — Contemporary Tiv formal dress pairs a stitched cap in striped cloth with a tailored shirt or gown of the same material, a twentieth-century standardisation that now functions as the group's public costume.
- **Ivav** (*ivav*) — A Tiv-named category of dress or adornment item recorded in the tradition; in some communities such terms designate specific garment forms or their decorative treatment.
- **Ishagba** (*ishagba*) — A named Tiv material form associated with dress or bodily equipment; details vary by district and the term is used with some local flexibility.

### Architecture

The Tiv had no centralised state and therefore no monumental architecture; their building tradition is instead a domestic one of great coherence — the round thatched hut grouped into a compound around a swept central space, with the whole settlement periodically shifted as farmland was exhausted.

- **Compound** (*ya*) — The basic Tiv settlement unit: round mud-walled, conical-thatched huts arranged in a ring around a swept open yard, housing an elder, his wives and their married sons, with the arrangement of huts mapping the lineage relations of their occupants.
- **Reception hut** (*ate*) — The open-sided round shelter with a conical thatched roof standing at or near the centre of the compound, where men receive visitors, hold discussions, settle disputes and sit through the heat of the day; it is the social and political heart of the homestead and commonly the place where *kwagh-hir* narration and drumming are staged.
- **Granary** — Raised cylindrical mud or wickerwork stores with their own small thatched caps, holding yam, millet and guinea corn, and positioned within the compound ring as visible evidence of a household's farming success.
- **Shifting settlement** — Because Tiv agriculture historically involved moving compounds as soils were exhausted, buildings were designed to be renewable rather than permanent, which is the principal reason the tradition produced no masonry monuments.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Tiv craft production is dominated by carved wood and worked iron, with pottery made largely by women for domestic use; the most elaborate objects are the ritual emblems and the carved figures and puppets used in performance.

- **Swem** (*swem*) — The most charged of Tiv ritual objects: a potsherd or shallow vessel filled with ash, earth and other substances from the Swem hill, used in oath-taking and truth-telling. To swear falsely on *swem* was believed to bring wasting illness, and the emblem now appears on the paraphernalia of the *Tor Tiv* and in official Tiv iconography.
- **Slit drum** (*indyer*) — A very large hollowed wooden slit-drum, sometimes several metres long, kept in the open and struck to carry announcements, alarms and ritual signals across long distances; it is both instrument and public institution.
- **Akombo emblems** (*akombo*) — The material apparatus of the Tiv ritual complex of the same name: pots, stones, iron pieces, plant matter and carved objects assembled and manipulated by those holding the relevant ritual knowledge, used in affliction, fertility, farming and protection.
- **Brass rods** (*tsar*) — Brass or copper rods that formed the prestige currency of the pre-colonial Tiv exchange sphere, convertible into cattle, slaves, *tugudu* cloth and rights in women, and famous in economic anthropology as the classic case of non-market spheres of exchange.
- **Carved figures and puppets** — The articulated wooden figures of *kwagh-hir*, worked with movable limbs and heads operated by concealed handlers, together with carved masks and headpieces; these are the most technically ambitious Tiv sculptural works.
- **Household pottery** — Hand-built, pit- or bonfire-fired earthenware for cooking, water storage and beer, generally undecorated or bearing simple incised and roulette-impressed bands.

### Jewelry & body adornment

For the Tiv the decorated surface of the body was historically the primary field of visual art, far more than cloth or carving; scarification in particular carried an elaborate and changing repertoire of designs, and its decline in the twentieth century removed the group's richest ornamental tradition from living practice.

- **Scarification** (*Tiv body scarification*) — Raised keloid patterns cut into the abdomen, chest, back, face and arms, arranged in concentric, chevron and radiating fields. Designs were fashionable rather than fixed, changing across generations, and were understood as marks of courage, beauty and full social adulthood; the practice is now largely historical, surviving on elderly bodies and in museum photography.
- **Avan hair pattern** (*avan*) — A named Tiv coiffure pattern in which the hair is parted, ridged and dressed into a recognised design; Tiv hairdressing followed the same logic as scarification, with named styles that came into and out of fashion.
- **Brass and iron ornament** — Coiled brass and iron anklets, armlets and neck rings, historically linked to the same metal economy as the *tsar* rods, worn in quantity by women on ceremonial occasions.
- **Beadwork** — Strung glass and seed beads at neck, waist and wrist, with waist-beads worn by women under clothing; imported glass beads entered Tiv adornment through the Benue river trade.
- **Adiga** (*adiga*) — A named Tiv item of adornment or personal equipment; as with several such terms its precise reference varies between districts.

## Music & performance

Tiv musical life is overwhelmingly vocal and percussive, organised around composer-singers who make new songs for specific occasions and around drums whose roles are as much communicative as musical; the Tiv are widely described as a people who sing their commentary on public affairs.

- **Slit drum** (*indyer*) — Struck with heavy beaters to send signals and to underpin large gatherings; its sound is understood as the voice of the community rather than of an individual performer.
- **Gbande** (*gbande*) — A Tiv drum type used in ensemble for dance and song accompaniment, providing the rhythmic frame over which singers and chorus work.
- **Ilyu** (*ilyu*) — A Tiv term for song, and by extension for the composed song-genre in which topical, praise and satirical texts are set; individual composers are named and their songs travel.
- **Composer-singers** — Named song-makers who compose for weddings, funerals, political campaigns and public controversies, with a call-and-response chorus answering; their texts are a principal medium of Tiv public opinion.
- **Flutes and horns** — Side-blown animal-horn and reed instruments used for signalling and for melodic interjection in dance ensembles.

## Dance & theatre

The Tiv contribution to Nigerian performance is *kwagh-hir*, a composite theatre of puppets, masks, song and narration that grew out of older storytelling practice in the mid-twentieth century and is now the group's most celebrated art form; alongside it stand vigorous social dances performed in striped cloth.

- **Kwagh-hir** (*kwagh-hir*) — Literally something like "a magical thing" or "wonder tale": a night-time travelling theatre in which carved, articulated puppets and large masked constructions are manipulated by concealed operators while a narrator tells episodes and a chorus and drums answer. Sequences run from domestic comedy to political satire to displays of apparent wonder. It emerged in its present form around the 1960s and has been extensively documented as Nigeria's most developed puppet theatre.
- **Puppet operation** — Figures with movable heads, arms and jaws are worked from below or behind by handlers under cloth, producing motions timed to the narrator's lines; the technical skill of the carvers and operators is itself part of the spectacle.
- **Masked constructions** — Large composite masks and body constructions, sometimes multi-figured, appear alongside the puppets and cross the boundary between puppet and masquerade.
- **Social dance** — Vigorous group dances with waist and shoulder articulation, performed in *a'nger* wrappers to *gbande* drumming at weddings, funerals and festivals, with lines or circles of dancers answering the lead singer.
- **Storytelling origins** — *Kwagh-hir* is generally understood to have grown out of the older practice of evening tale-telling in the *ate*, absorbing carving and masking traditions into a staged form.

## Festivals & rituals

Tiv ceremonial life is anchored less in a calendar of festivals than in life-cycle rites, in the agricultural year of yam, and in the ritual complex of *akombo*; the modern era has added institutional celebrations centred on the *Tor Tiv*.

- **Akombo rites** (*akombo*) — A large family of ritual observances, each with its own emblems, prohibitions and specialists, addressing illness, infertility, crop failure, theft and social breach; a person's misfortune is diagnosed to a particular *akombo* and repaired by the appropriate rite.
- **Swem oath** (*swem*) — The supreme Tiv truth ordeal, in which disputants swear on the ash-filled emblem; its authority underwrote dispute settlement in a society without chiefs and it remains a potent symbol.
- **Yam harvest observances** — Seasonal rites and feasting around the yam crop, the staple and prestige food, with the first harvest marked by household and lineage-level celebration.
- **Funerals** — Extended mortuary observances with drumming, composed songs, dancing and large-scale hospitality, commonly the largest gatherings in Tiv social life and the principal occasion for *kwagh-hir* performance.
- **Tor Tiv occasions** — Installation and anniversary ceremonies of the paramount ruler, a twentieth-century institution now central to Tiv public identity, at which striped cloth and *swem* iconography are displayed.

## Foodways

Tiv cuisine is built on yam and guinea corn, pounded and boiled into stiff staples eaten with soups thickened by seeds and leaves, and it is closely tied to the group's identity as farmers of the Benue floodplain.

Yam is the central crop and the prestige food, boiled, roasted or pounded; alongside it stand guinea corn, millet, maize, cassava, sweet potato, cocoyam, beniseed (sesame) and a wide range of leafy greens and legumes. The characteristic Tiv meal is a stiff pounded or stirred starch — from yam, or from cereal flour — eaten by hand with a soup, and among the most distinctive of these soups are those thickened with beniseed, which the Tiv grow extensively and which gives Benue cooking a nutty, slightly bitter register uncommon elsewhere in Nigeria. Bush meat, river fish from the Benue and Katsina-Ala, and goat and chicken from the compound supply protein; palm oil, locust bean condiment and hot pepper are the standard seasonings. Fermented cereal beer brewed from guinea corn or millet is drunk at funerals, work parties and festivals, and palm wine is available where oil palms grow. Communal labour parties, in which a farmer feeds and beers his neighbours in exchange for a day's work on his fields, tie food directly to the Tiv agricultural system.

## Oral tradition & literature

Tiv oral literature is unusually well documented and unusually central to the group's own sense of itself: genealogy, tale and song together did the work that written law and history do elsewhere.

The genealogical recitation is the foundational form. Because Tiv society was segmentary and acephalous, the ability to place oneself and one's opponent in a branching descent from the ancestor Tiv determined rights in land, obligations of support and the jurisdiction in which a dispute would be heard; competence in this recitation was therefore both an art and a practical legal skill. Alongside it stand the evening tales told in the *ate*, from which *kwagh-hir* is generally held to have developed, including trickster narratives, tales of transformation and moral fables. The composed song, *ilyu*, is the living literary genre: named composers produce texts of praise, complaint, satire and political commentary that circulate widely and have repeatedly entered Nigerian public argument. Proverb and riddle are used densely in ordinary speech and in judicial argument before elders. Written Tiv literature dates from the missionary orthography of the early twentieth century, and Tiv scholars including Akiga Sai — whose *Akiga's Story*, prepared with Rupert East in the 1930s, is one of the earliest African-authored ethnographies of an African people — have produced an unusual body of insider documentation.

## Language & religion

Tiv (*Tiv*, also written *Tivi*, with *Tiv* used for both people and language) is a Southern Bantoid language of the Benue–Congo group, and thus a distant relative of the Bantu languages rather than of the neighbouring Jukunoid, Idomoid and Chadic tongues. It is a tone language with a noun-class system reduced from the fuller Bantu pattern, and it is spoken with relatively little dialect fragmentation across a large territory — a linguistic uniformity consistent with a comparatively recent expansion. It has been written since the early twentieth century, has a Bible translation, and is used in broadcasting, primary education and a considerable printed literature; most Tiv are additionally fluent in Nigerian English or Hausa, or both.

Religiously, the great majority of Tiv today are Christian, with the Nongo u Kristu u i Ser u sha Tar (the church founded out of Dutch Reformed and later Sudan United Mission work) and Roman Catholic communities both strongly represented; Islam is present but has never been dominant in the Tiv heartland. Underlying this is an older cosmology centred on *Aondo*, the sky-associated high god, distant and not directly propitiated, together with the ritual complex of *akombo* and beliefs concerning *tsav* — an inherent personal force or substance associated with the ability to affect others, historically understood to grow with age and to underlie both leadership and witchcraft accusation. These categories remain operative in ordinary explanation of illness and misfortune in many communities, coexisting with Christian practice rather than having been displaced by it, and *swem* retains its force as an oath even among the devout.
