## Overview

The Gola or Gula are a West African ethnic group living primarily in western or northwestern Liberia and eastern Sierra Leone. In Liberia, they are found in Lofa, Bomi, Montserrado and Grand Cape Mount counties. As of 2015, the Gola language was spoken by about 278,000 people. Gola is an isolate within the Niger–Congo language family and appears to form its own branch. The Gola are significant in the cultural history of the region through the Sande and Poro institutions, which function as rites of passage and have spread to neighboring ethnic groups. Their traditions include masked performances, oral history, spiritual specialists, and systems of initiation associated with gender, social order, training, and community authority.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

 They do record ceremonial clothing associated with the Mazo, the High Priestess of the Sande Society and of the Gola people. She is known as “the lady in white” because she usually dresses in white cloth. The Zogbenya are described as always wearing men’s shoes to show their dominance in society. They are also decorated with nets, cowrie shells, and water deer horns, and are dyed black. These details belong to the appearance of the Zogbenya during Sande-related performance and ritual rather than to a general account of daily dress.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention objects used by the Zogbenya in performance: a cane, knife, stick, or spear, each described as carrying heavy medicines and talismans. The sources also state that many Zogbe masks are found in mountains, creeks, rivers, and streams and are regarded as gifts from the other side.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 In the ceremonial appearance of the Zogbenya, the sources name cowrie shells, nets, and water deer horns as decorations. The Zogbenya are also dyed black and are associated with the depths of water and the universe.

## Music & performance

 They do identify singing as part of Sande activity: Vai Sande women sing Sande songs in Mende, Gola, or Dei languages.

Performance is central to the Sande and Poro institutions. Zogbenya manifest through black masks that are danced and used for Sande sessions, dances, and rituals. The Zogbenya are described as nature spirits associated with the Gola ancestors and as trainers for Gola women. During a performance, the Zogbe stares at men, while men and uninitiated people remain at a distance. Only the Dazo is allowed to dance or remain around the Zogbe in the specified manner. The Zogbenya may carry a cane, knife, stick, or spear bearing medicines and talismans.

The Poro masks Gbetu, Nafai, Yafi, Jobai, Nyaa, and Kɔkpɔ are described as “play things” rather than spiritually important masks. They are used for entertainment, meetings, and celebrations. The Poro spirit Dadewe does not use a mask; the sources state that villagers hear it when it approaches the town.

## Dance & theatre

Dance and masking are documented primarily through Sande and Poro. Zogbe masks are danced during Sande sessions, dances, and rituals. The Zogbenya are associated with water, medicines, justice, and order in Gola society, and their performances are connected with the protection and training of women. When Sande is in session in a clan or chiefdom, the Zogbenya go from town to town “cleansing” the land.

Poro masks including Gbetu, Nafai, Yafi, Jobai, Nyaa, and Kɔkpɔ are used in entertainment, meetings, and celebrations. They are distinguished from the Zogbenya because the sources state that they have no comparable spiritual importance. The sources also describe Dadewe, a forest monster or demon with a deep voice and furious teeth, but state that Dadewe has no mask.

## Festivals & rituals

 They do document Sande and Poro as rites of passage of the Gola people. Sande seasons last two to three years before the influence of western school. During a Sande season, the Zogbenya go from town to town to cleanse the land. If war occurs during Sande season, the sources state that Sande must be paused and boys must go to Poro to be trained.

According to Gola oral history, Sande existed for as long as the Gola people had existed and came before Bohn, or Poro. The sources describe an earlier matriarchal order in which women ruled the societies through Sande and water guardian spirits. The women were called Kings, or Kandanya, and were said to have received the Mandate of Heaven from DAYA, identified as God.

The sources describe the Zogbenya as having made a covenant with the Gola ancestors to train girls into women. They also describe Dadewe as having made a covenant to train all Gola boys or descendants and make them men. Boys were said to be brought to Dadewe to be “swallowed” and reborn as men. The Poro later became a trans-ethnic institution among the Vai, Mende, Dei, Bassa, Kpelle, Kisi, and others.

## Foodways

 They state only that most Gola people are Muslim and that Islam was adopted through the influence of Mandinka merchants from the 16th century onwards.

## Oral tradition & literature

Gola oral tradition is presented in the sources under the name Kabandé. It preserves accounts of the origin and development of Sande and Poro, including the claim that Gola society was once matriarchal and that women ruled through Sande and water guardian spirits. The tradition also explains the emergence of Dadewe. According to the account, warfare against the Gola led the men to enter the deep forest, where they cast a furious forest monster or demon. Dadewe was then brought to “swallow” boys and rebirth them as men so that they could be trained for war.

The sources describe covenants between the Gola ancestors, the Zogbenya, and Dadewe. They state that Dadewe would train Gola boys and descendants, and that it would be sent to a nearby forest during Sande because its deep, loud voice terrorized the women.

## Language & religion

Gola is a language of Liberia and Sierra Leone. It is not closely related to other languages and appears to form its own branch of the Niger–Congo language family. It was traditionally classified as an Atlantic language, but this classification is no longer accepted in more recent studies. Dialects named in the sources are Deng, also called Todii, Kongba, and Senje. Gola is spoken in Gbarpolu County, Grand Cape Mount County, and Lofa County between the Mano River and Saint Paul River, as well as in inland areas of Bomi County and Montserrado County. The language has at least high and low tones; middle, falling, and rising tones are also reported. Until recently, the Vai script was used across Liberia and Sierra Leone to transcribe Gola.

Most Gola people are Muslim. The sources state that Islam was adopted through the influence of Mandinka merchants from the 16th century onwards. Gola spiritual traditions include Sande, Poro, the Zogbenya, Dadewe, water guardian spirits, ancestors, medicines, and talismans.
