## Overview

The Fon objects in this gallery come mainly from southern Benin and from museum records linked to the former kingdom of Dahomey. Abomey court commissions are especially visible, but court objects are only one part of Fon material culture. The selection also includes vessels, small ornaments, musical instruments, and figures. Museum records describe particular works and collection moments; they do not establish a complete inventory of Fon practice across places and generations.

## Ancestor altars and ritual vessels

The Metropolitan Museum of Art describes an *asen* as a portable metal altar planted in a family or royal shrine house. Its selected royal *asen*, made by Hountondji guild artists for the Abomey court, has a bird at its summit. The museum explains that living relatives could meet and address the dead at such staffs, bringing offerings at commemorations or important moments in family life. A second selected *asen* was commissioned in Ouidah to remember a prominent person. Its metal tableau uses a seat, birds, a calabash, and other forms to refer to that person's life. The Met's interpretation belongs to these documented altars; the motif on another figure should not automatically be given the same meaning.

Pottery in the British Museum's 1951 Fon group was purchased at the Abomey market. The register says a red vessel with a long ridged neck was for a royal ancestor cult. Another vessel carries a two-headed serpent in relief and was recorded for the cult of Dã. Other selected pots and lids show painted bands, raised forms, piercing, and joined chambers. The catalogue links some to specific ritual purposes, but a market purchase does not identify the individual potter or prove the exact place of manufacture.

## Court signs, cloth, and metalwork

A *récade* is a staff or axe-shaped sign of royal authority. The selected British Museum ceremonial axe has a wooden shaft and a curved, incised iron blade. A separately reviewed Toulouse museum photograph shows a bird-headed Fon *récade* of wood and brass; its catalogue calls the form a sign carried with a royal message. These records support its political role more directly than the object's shape alone.

The British Museum's appliqué banner illustrates a Dahomean military victory during the reign of King Glélé. Its curator says this example was made in the early twentieth century by the Yémadjé family, traditional makers in the Abomey palace, as a replica for presentation or sale. The cloth therefore documents a continuing court craft and historical imagery, not the physical banner used in the nineteenth-century event it depicts.

Several metal figures show bowls, human figures, birds, and small animals assembled into complex forms. Their individual catalogue descriptions support what is visible, but do not assign every figure a named rite. The collection also includes bead-and-shell necklaces or anklets, a drum with woven construction, two different iron gongs, and a carved rattle staff. The British Museum register specifically says its paired gongs were used in ceremonies and dances.

## What the source review withheld

The contact-sheet review removed weak or repeated pot, doll, axe, anklet, and rattle images. Two British Museum catalogue entries were photographs of the same object rather than separate surviving objects. A Europeana white shirt was removed because its description concerned Yoruba culture and mentioned Fon only for comparison.

Three more objects were withheld after reading older museum notes. A stool now indexed as Fon was originally registered as Ashanti and later relabeled Dahomey without a specific maker identification. A cloth was attributed to Fon only as a probability. The register groups a doll with both Fon and Yoruba forms without identifying that individual piece. Those uncertainties matter more than an unqualified modern maker field. A Spanish museum's iron ritual hat had a specific Fon explanation, but the available photograph did not show the object clearly enough for this gallery.

The four approved Commons photos show objects with specific Fon attributions: a *récade*, a decorated apron, a Vodun figure, and a Brooklyn Museum royal or noble costume. A scene captioned only as a Vodun shrine near Abomey was held back because the pictured people and objects were not specifically identified as Fon. A historical image of a Dahomean woman soldier was a printed outsider depiction, so it was not used as a direct record of Fon dress.
