## Overview

The Harari people, also called *Gēy Usuach* (“People of the City”), are a Semitic-speaking ethnic group traditionally associated with the walled city of Harar in the Harari Region of eastern Ethiopia. Harar is called *Gēy* (“the City”) in Harari. The Harari language belongs to the South Ethiopic grouping within the Semitic subfamily of the Afroasiatic languages. Hararis have also established communities in Addis Ababa, Dire Dawa, Jijiga, Djibouti, Saudi Arabia, Somalia, Yemen, North America, Europe and Australia. Their material culture is especially documented through basketry, decorated houses and the *Mesob*, while their historical identity is closely connected with Harar, the Sultanate of Adal and the Emirate of Harar.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Basketry** — Harari baskets are decorated with complex geometric patterns and are known for their quality and beauty. They are used especially for house decoration and on occasions including weddings and mournings.

**Wall baskets** — Harari women make handcrafted wall baskets with very intricate designs. These baskets form part of the decoration of Harari traditional houses.

### Architecture

Harari traditional houses are richly decorated. Handcrafted wall baskets made by Harari women are displayed alongside enameled plates with floral patterns. The plates were imported to Ethiopia after the Second World War by Japanese traders.

The ground floor is dedicated to the kitchen and living room. The main room contains large steps covered with carpets and pillows, called *Nedeba*. Family members and guests take their seats there according to rank. The floors are devoted to bedrooms. In the wealthiest families, four black clay pots called *Aflala* are placed on a stone shelf carved into the wall. The pots were used to store valuable goods, including gold, coins, medicine and coffee beans.

## Music & performance

The sources mention the Zeila songs, which are considered to use Old Harari and are described as originating in the thirteenth century. They are popular in Somaliland. The sources also state that the language spoken by the imams and sultans of Adal would have closely resembled contemporary Harari.

## Language & religion

Harari is an Ethio-Semitic language spoken by the Harari people of Ethiopia. It is closely related to the Eastern Gurage languages, Zay and Siltʼe, which are linked to the now-extinct Semitic Harla language. Harari is also described as belonging to the South Ethiopic grouping within the Semitic subfamily of the Afroasiatic languages. Locals or natives of Harar call the language *Gēy Sinan*, “language of the City.”

Harari was historically written using the Arabic script and a Harari secret script of unknown origin. The Ethiopic script was adopted in the 1990s, and a Latin version is used by the Harari diaspora. Harari Arabic script consists of 36 letters, including the original 28 Arabic letters and eight additional letters. The majority of Harari are Sunni Muslim. The community’s earliest kabir or Islamic teacher was Aw Sofi Yahya, and the first Qur’anic school was built approximately 10 kilometres (6.2 mi) south of Harar’s city center.
