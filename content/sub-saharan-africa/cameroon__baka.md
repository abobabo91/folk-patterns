## Overview

The Baka are an ethnic group of hunter-gatherers living in the Central African rain forest, especially in southeastern Cameroon, northern Gabon, the northern Republic of the Congo, and southwestern Central African Republic. In the Congo they are also known as Bayaka, Bebayaka, Bebayaga, or Bibaya. The Baka community of Cameroon represents roughly 30 000 individuals, while the Baka language article gives a figure of 25,000 in Cameroon. Baka people live semi-nomadically and have an average height of 1.52 meters. Their cultural life is closely connected with forest subsistence, fishing, hunting, gathering, music, dance, ritual, and communal decision-making. The sources describe them as maintaining the Baka language despite pressures from deforestation, settlement, and neighboring Bantu farming communities.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no Baka textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

They do describe the appearance of the figure associated with the *Ejengi* ceremony: a creature covered from top to bottom in strips of russet-hued raffia. The description presents this as ceremonial material rather than ordinary dress. The sources also mention a historical reference to “dwarfish people” wearing clothing made from the palm tree, but this passage concerns historical references to “pygmy” peoples generally and does not document a Baka clothing tradition. No source describes gender-specific daily dress or a separate Baka costume tradition in sufficient detail.

### Architecture

Baka groups establish temporary camps containing huts made from bowed branches covered in large leaves. The sources state that more homes are now constructed by other methods, including mud and mud brick. Camps are abandoned when the surrounding area has been hunted out, after which the group settles in another part of the forest. During the dry season, Baka commonly move and set up camp within the forest to facilitate fishing and gathering.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do record that anthropologist Shiho Hattori documented about 100 instruments and utensils used daily for cooking, hunting and gathering, rituals, and related activities. Of these, 40 were made partly or entirely from natural resources found in the forest. Hunting tools include poisoned arrows and spears, while fishing equipment includes fishing rods. The Baka also use plant material to make fishing chemicals and use dogs during hunting excursions.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The only detailed visual description concerns the raffia-covered figure appearing in accounts of *Ejengi*, which is a ritual manifestation rather than a jewelry tradition.

## Music & performance

Baka music is described as mainly vocal and polyphonic. It uses repetitive melody and rhythm, with few variations, and includes improvisation. Music and dance accompany hunting preparation, demonstrations of skill, healing rituals, initiation rituals, traditional stories, group names, entertainment, and daily life. Musical participation helps bring groups together and supports the sharing of survival techniques.

The most common vocal technique is called *Jodel*. It moves from the chest voice to the head voice and is often performed on syllables or sounds without meaning. Baka singers can produce tone colors ranging from tense or raspy to relaxed or breathy. The sources also describe structured improvisation: performers know when and how to add or change a phrase or beat.

Named instruments include the flute, cylindrical drums, the arched harp, the harp-zither, lamellaphones, rattles, water drums, and the musical bow, which is played only by women. The sources state that many instruments used by the Baka come from Bantu peoples. The *molimo* is described as a trumpet-like instrument made from metal pipe, wood, or bamboo. It is sacred because of the sound it makes or imitates rather than because of the physical object alone.

A *molimo* ceremony follows a daily pattern over two months. Food offerings are collected around midday; after dinner, women and children enter huts while men gather around the *molimo* fire. Singing and instrument playing imitate forest-animal sounds. The instrument is stopped at streams so that water can be run through it or it can be “given a drink.” It is later brought into the village, passed through the flames, rubbed with coals, and played again.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is important in Baka social and ritual life. The sources state that every part of the forest has its own dance ritual and that dances are performed for male initiation, healing, funerals, and maintaining community closeness. Skilled dancers usually undergo a specific initiation. Their performances use structured improvisation, with dancers adding or changing phrases and beats.

After a successful hunt, the Baka perform songs of thanksgiving and dance in a ritual called *Luma*. A separate death ritual, *Mbouamboua*, involves dancing around the debris-covered corpse of a deceased person throughout the night. The sources also describe *Makala*, a performance of *Mabo* associated with net-hunting. Villagers form an entourage of novices and expert singers and drummers while moving toward another village.

## Festivals & rituals

They do, however, describe rituals connected with hunting, initiation, healing, death, and the forest spirit *Jengi*, also known as *Djengui* or *Ejengi*.

After a successful hunt, the Baka perform *Luma*, a thanksgiving ritual involving songs and dancing. *Jengi* is believed to appear when harmony reigns among villagers. The spirit is also central to an initiation ceremony called *Jengi*, in which a young man passes from boyhood to manhood. Young Baka men volunteer to be initiated; after initiation, they have the right to live and walk freely within the sacred forest.

Death is considered a misfortune and a sign of spiritual discord. Each tribe that has witnessed the death of one of its members is described as praying to *Jengi* and dancing around the corpse for an entire night. After the *Mbouamboua* dance, the villagers leave the place and move elsewhere to escape the curse.

The sources also describe the *molimo* ceremony, which lasts two months and includes food offerings, singing, dancing or communal gathering around the fire, imitation of forest-animal sounds, and the ritual movement of the instrument through streams and village space. No source gives a fixed calendar date for these ceremonies.

## Foodways

Baka foodways center on hunting, fishing, gathering, plant cultivation, and beekeeping. Men hunt and trap in the surrounding forest, using poisoned arrows and spears, and may be accompanied by dogs. Fishing is particularly important: boys learn to use fishing rods from a young age, while men use chemicals obtained from crushed plant material. The chemicals are dispersed downstream in fast-moving river water, depriving fish of oxygen so that they float to the surface. Women generally perform dam fishing, removing water from a dammed area and collecting fish from the exposed ground. Children and adolescent girls may accompany women and help watch infants while fishing.

Women cultivate plantains, cassavas, and bananas and practice beekeeping. During the dry season, men hunt from dawn until dusk while women gather the fruits called *mabe* and *peke*, which are used to provide juice and nuts. The Baka also monitor bee activity to obtain honey, called *poki*. The group remains in one area until it has been hunted out, then moves to another part of the forest.

During the *molimo* ceremony, food offerings are collected around midday. After the instrument leaves the village, the men eat what was collected earlier that day.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that Baka music is used for traditional stories and that the community knows who wrote particular songs and where entire repertories originated. Named repertories include *Mabo* and *Togo*. Musical knowledge is generally acquired through enculturation, although older groups may teach younger groups and members of groups from different regions may also teach one another. They mention Robert Brisson’s *Mythologie des Pygmées Baka (Sud-Cameroun): mythologie et contes* in the further-reading material, but no individual stories are summarized in the supplied sources. The sources also mention documentation by Mauro Campagnoli and a film concerning the Baka.

## Language & religion

Baka is described in the language source as a dialect cluster of Ubangian languages spoken by Baka Pygmies in Cameroon and Gabon. It is also called Be-bayaga, Be-bayaka, and Bibaya de L’est. The Baka language has no standard orthography, and different writing systems are used. A 2009 recommendation supported by the Ministry of Scientific Research and Innovation uses the General Alphabet of Cameroon Languages. Tones are marked with acute and grave diacritics, while middle tone is not indicated.

The language is spoken in southeastern Cameroon, including parts of the Eastern and Southern regions. The source identifies varieties or possibly related forms called Gundi, Ngundi, Ganzi, and Massa or Limassa, but states that it is unclear whether these are mutually intelligible with Baka proper. Baka vocabulary includes specialized terms connected with edible plants, medicinal plants, and honey collecting.

Baka religion centers on the forest spirit *Jengi*, also called *Djengui* or *Ejengi*, and the supreme being *Komba*. *Jengi* mediates between *Komba* and the Baka and is regarded as a protecting father or guardian. The Baka believe *Jengi* is omnipresent in the forest and can punish transgressors. The sources state that nature is ultimately worshipped because *Komba*, rather than *Jengi*, resides in it.
