## Overview

The Margi are the speakers of Margi — also written Marghi, and distinguished in the linguistic literature as Marghi Central — a Chadic language of the Afroasiatic family spoken in Nigeria, Cameroon and Chad. Margi is perhaps the best described language of the Biu–Mandara branch of Chadic, largely because of a full grammar published in 1963, so the people are better known through their language than through any single documented art tradition. The speech community is internally varied: Madube, Izge, Lassa, Gulak, Damboa, Mulgwai, Uba and Sukur are all named as kinds of Marghi, and the neighbouring Marghi South and Putai are close enough that they are sometimes treated as dialects of the same language. In folk-culture terms the surviving material record shown in this atlas is dominated by ironwork — weapons, edged tools and iron ornament — together with beadwork worn by women. The sources used here give no population figure.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here document no weaving, dyeing or cloth-printing tradition for the Margi, so no textile entries can be listed. What they do document is ornament built on prepared skin and string rather than on woven cloth:

**Beaded aprons** — Women's aprons made of beads, in two constructions: one worked on a leather foundation and hung with iron rattles, the other worked on a foundation of string. These are the group's principal recorded pattern-bearing garments.

**Plaited leather binding** — Throwing-knife handles are covered in plaited leather, one of them in plaited red leather, with a loop worked at the end of the grip; a dagger hilt is sheathed in black leather. The plaiting is the decorative surface as well as the grip.

**Incised sheath panels** — A skin dagger-sheath carries a central groove with a pattern along it, and a sword sheath is partly covered with twisted string, which gives a banded, ridged surface.

The sources name no motifs, so no motif vocabulary can be given.

### Clothing & dress

The only garments in the sources are women's: two beaded aprons, one on a leather foundation with iron rattles attached and one on a string foundation. Nothing in the sources describes men's dress, head coverings, belts, footwear, or any distinction between everyday and ceremonial clothing, so those cannot be set out here.

### Architecture

The sources used here say nothing about Margi house form, building materials, roofing, settlement layout or architectural decoration.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Iron is the material the record is built around, worked in combination with bamboo, wood, hide and leather rather than alone. Arrows are composite: narrow, pointed iron heads with barbs — single-barbed, double-barbed, or shaped as pointed foliate blades — mounted on bamboo shafts, sometimes fletched with feathers, and carried in sets (groups of four, eight, thirteen and eighteen are recorded). Quivers are made either of skin or of wood with skin covering the foot. The bow is wooden, strung with twisted leather. Edged weapons include composite throwing-knives with iron blades and plaited leather grips, an arm-dagger with an iron hilt and iron blade in a skin sheath, and an iron sword in a composite sheath. Defence is a single large triangular hide shield. The sources record no pottery.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment in the sources is iron on leather. Women's rattles are made of iron pieces strung on a leather thong, and iron rattles are also fixed to a beaded apron, so ornament that sounds when the wearer moves is documented twice over. Beads are the other adornment material, mounted on leather or on string. The sources say nothing about tattooing, scarification, hairdressing, or the ritual meaning attached to any of these pieces.

## Music & performance

One instrument is documented: a composite drum with a circular wooden body and skin membranes at both ends, that is, a double-headed drum rather than a single-skin one. The women's iron rattles on a leather thong, and the iron rattles sewn onto a beaded apron, are body-worn sound-makers and belong to the same performance record. Beyond these objects the sources name no song genres, no other instruments, and no performance occasions.

One detail of the language bears on vocal expression: Margi has a sound, a labiodental flap, that is used in mimesis rather than in ordinary lexical words — that is, in sound-imitating speech rather than in the everyday vocabulary.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name no Margi dance or dramatic tradition. The only evidence bearing on dance is material: the iron rattles catalogued as women's dance-rattles, strung on a leather thong, and the iron rattles attached to a beaded apron.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used here contain nothing on the Margi festival calendar or on birth, initiation, marriage and funeral rites.

## Foodways

The sources used here say nothing about Margi staples, cooking methods, dishes, drinks or food rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources used here record no Margi folktales, epics, proverbs or riddles, and no storytelling occasions. What they do record is the documentation of the language itself: a grammar published in 1963 and a 1987 study of the vowel system, which together make Margi the best described language in its branch of Chadic.

## Language & religion

Margi is a Chadic language, and Chadic is a branch of Afroasiatic. Named varieties include Madube, Izge, Lassa, Gulak, Damboa, Mulgwai, Uba and Sukur; Marghi South and Putai are closely related and are sometimes counted as dialects. The language is unusual on both sides of its sound system. Its vowels form a vertical system with only two phonemic vowels in native vocabulary, /ɨ/ and /a/, with /ɛ/ and /o/ distinguished only in loan words. Its consonant inventory is very large: the 1963 grammar lists 84 consonantal phonemes, which would rival the Caucasian language Ubykh for the largest inventory of any language without clicks, though many of those entries are now analysed as consonant clusters, leaving 66 if labialised consonants are counted separately and 54 if they are read as consonant-plus-/w/ sequences. The glottalised consonants have been described as both creaky-voiced and implosive, as in Hausa. The closely related Bura is similar but adds a palatalised lateral series. The sources say nothing about Margi religion.
