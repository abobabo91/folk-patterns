## Overview

The Dagaaba people, singular Dagao and, in northern dialects, Dagara for both singular and plural, inhabit the area around the convergence of Ghana, Burkina Faso and Côte d’Ivoire. Their homeland includes northwestern Ghana, especially the Upper West Region, and southwestern Burkina Faso, including Ioba Province, Poni, Bougouriba, Sissili and Mouhoun provinces. Communities also occur in towns including Wa, Nandom, Lawra, Jirapa, Kaleo, Papu, Nadowli, Daffiama, Wechiau, Hamile, Bogda, Babile, Tuna, Han, Zambo and Nyoli. There are over a million Dagaaba across West Africa. They speak Dagaare, a Niger-Congo language with several dialects. Their folk-culture significance in the sources lies in small-scale agriculture, oral literature, ritual institutions, iron production, musical instruments, local beer making and systems of spiritual custodianship over land and resources.

## Material culture

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Dagaaba were historically self-reliant in iron production.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Cowrie shells are mentioned as currency and as having traditional ornamental and ceremonial uses, but no specific jewelry forms are identified.

## Music & performance

The Dagaaba developed sophisticated musical instruments, including the *gyle*, described in the source as a xylophone. No additional Dagaaba instruments or named song genres are identified.

Music is not presented separately from the wider social and ritual life of the community in the sources. The Dagaaba maintain oral literature as a living vehicle of education and acculturation. Their sacred literature is produced during ritual and religious services, while secular oral literature consists of stories, tales, proverbs and other oral genres. The most important sacred narratives are *bagr* mythical narratives, recitations and orations produced during initiation rituals and other religious services.

## Dance & theatre

They mention ritual services, initiation rituals and sacred oral performances.

## Festivals & rituals

They do describe a ritual and political system organized around the *Yir* subclan or household group and the *Tengan*, an earth-deity shrine area. Several shrine areas form part of a system called the *tendaalun*. Roles within this system are commonly inherited within the same household group.

The head of a shrine-area system, the *tengan sob*, sometimes called *tindana*, serves as community elder and priest. The *tengan dem* is the ritual custodian and maintainer of the ritual center. Other roles include the *suo sob*, who performs ritual animal slaughter to the earth deity; the *zongmogre*, who performs rituals at sacred market centres; and the *gara dana* or *wie sob*, who leads ritual activity among hunting societies.

The sources connect *bagr* narratives, recitations and orations with initiation rituals and other religious services. Until the latter part of the nineteenth century, broader Dagaaba communities functioned through councils of elders. Some communities maintain traditional ceremonial chieftainships.

## Foodways

Dagaaba communities remain primarily small-scale agricultural communities, with family farming plots cultivated by the family. The sources describe mixed-crops farming and identify agriculture as a central economic activity. Fishing communities persist along the Black Volta, which is described as a de facto boundary of Dagaaba lands. Trade has also long been important because the communities lie along historic coast-to-Sahel trade routes, although the source characterizes this trade as largely involving local goods.

The Dagaaba are known for making a local beer called *dāā Dagadāā*, popularly known as *Pito*.

Markets in larger towns are held on Sundays, while other markets operate on a six-day cycle. Cowrie shells have been used in some contemporary northern Ghanaian Dagaaba communities alongside the modern Ghanaian cedi. They serve not only ornamental and ceremonial purposes but also as internal savings and as a medium of trade across national and currency boundaries.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral literature has a long tradition among Dagaaba communities and remains a living vehicle of education and acculturation. The sources divide it broadly into secular and sacred forms. Secular literature includes stories, tales, proverbs and other oral genres. Sacred literature is produced during ritual and religious services.

The most important sacred forms are *bagr* mythical narratives, recitations and orations associated with initiation rituals and other religious services. Oral tradition also informs accounts of Dagaaba history. It describes the Dagaaba as an outgrowth of the Mole-Dagbani group that migrated to the semi-arid Sahel region in the fourteenth century CE and later moved to the lower northern part of the region in the seventeenth century. Oral literature likewise records resistance to attempted conquest by states in the south of modern Ghana and by the Kingdoms of Dagbon, Mamprugu and Gonja.

The sources identify debate over the pre-colonial origin of Dagaaba communities and mention one thesis that the Dagaaba formed as a breakaway faction of Dagbon under Na Nyagse.

## Language & religion

The Dagaaba speak Dagaare, a Niger-Congo language consisting of several dialects. Northern Dagara and Dagaari Dioula are mainly spoken in Burkina Faso, while Southern Dagaare is mainly spoken in Ghana. Dagaare, Dagara, Waale, identified as Southern Dagaare, and Birifor are described as four major dialects. The language shares close similarities with other Gur languages, including Dagbanli, Mooré, Mampruli and Kusaal.

Dagaaba communities have historically practiced traditional religions, Islam and Christianity. Traditional belief recognizes *Naaŋmene* as a supreme god and *Teŋgan* as an earth deity, among other deities. Rivers are understood as abodes of deities and supernatural beings that can interact directly with the living. Rivers, trees, hills and animals, including the cow, awl, dove and mud fish, are described as possessing spiritual essence or power. Blasphemy toward deities, especially *Teŋgan*, is believed to bring warnings, sickness, plague and, in extreme situations, death. Ghanaian Dagaaba have traditionally maintained a Cousinage/Joking relationship with the Frafra, or Gurunsi, people.
