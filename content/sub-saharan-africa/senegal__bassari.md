## Overview

The Bassari are an ethnic group living primarily in Senegal, with diaspora communities in Gambia, Guinea and Guinea-Bissau. Most are concentrated on either side of the Senegal–Guinea border, southwest of Kedougou in the Kédougou Region, within the wider Bassari Country. Their population is estimated at 15,000. On the Senegalese side, the Bassari-inhabited area of Kedougou is known as Salemata; on the Guinean side, villages are collectively called Bokore, a name translated as “Those of Kore.” The Bassari speak o-niyan, a Tenda language, and call themselves *a-liyan*, plural *be-liyan*, translated as “those of the laterite.” They are a matrilineal society organized into age-based social groups. Subsistence farming, metallurgy, initiation societies, masks and performance are central features of the material and intangible culture described by the sources.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name rings of hexagons around a circular center as a motif on Bassari initiation masks. The meaning is secret within the initiation society; the source relates the motif to hierarchy, levels of knowledge, precolonial mathematics, the Bassari calendar and divination rites.

### Clothing & dress

 They state that Bassari migrants to Senegalese and Guinean cities and towns use wage labor earned during the dry season to buy household equipment, clothing and other necessary items. Bassari art includes metalwork used for cosmetic decoration and regalia associated with the initiation society.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Bassari have a longstanding tradition of metallurgy, recorded in European sources since the late 19th century. They obtain iron ore through trade with the Coniagui and copper from the Fula, then process these metals through their own line of blacksmiths. The resulting objects include tools and jewelry. Metalwork is also made for cosmetic decoration and for regalia significant to the initiation society.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources describe Bassari metalwork as including jewelry made by local blacksmiths from traded iron ore and copper. Such metalwork serves cosmetic decoration and can also form part of regalia connected with the initiation society. No specific jewelry types, personal names for ornaments, tattooing, henna practice or hair practices are documented in the sources used.

## Music & performance

The sources describe music and performance mainly in relation to the Festival of the Fonio and the Kore initiation society. The Festival of the Fonio is an annual event in the Kedougou region. It includes political speeches, traditional singing and dances, as well as the presentation of offerings to Adja Aissatou Aya Ndiaye. The festival promotes fonio as a local crop and food and supports its cultural significance.

Bassari men take part in the Kore initiation society. Entrance into the Bassari sect of Kore defines male adulthood. The society has three distinct levels of adulthood, each lasting six years and each involving its own initiation process. Initiates perform a guttural sound associated with *endaw*, a formless being whose only mode of existence is this sound.

Bassari dancers use special rattles known as *Bamboyo*. Each Bamboyo consists of twenty plaited capsules; each capsule is folded into an antiprism using a single palm leaf.

## Dance & theatre

The sources document traditional singing and dances at the annual Festival of the Fonio. Bassari dancers also use Bamboyo rattles, each made from twenty plaited palm-leaf capsules. Performance is especially important within the Kore initiation society, where initiates gain access to the roles of masks and kore. Across the three levels of the society, these roles produce six modes of simulated entities.

Bassari initiation masks commonly feature rings of hexagons around a circular center. The motif’s actual meaning is secret within the initiation society, while the source connects it with hierarchy and the progression through levels of knowledge. The source also states that *endaw* is often represented visually as a leafy mass or a chameleon. No named theatrical genre, puppet tradition or non-ritual entertainment form is documented.

## Festivals & rituals

The Festival of the Fonio is an annual event held in the Kedougou region. It was established to popularize fonio as a local crop and food. Before the festival, fonio was often associated with poor people; the event therefore gives the crop greater cultural legitimacy and presents it as an adaptation to climate change. The festival includes political speeches, traditional singing and dances, and the gifting of offerings to Adja Aissatou Aya Ndiaye, who is called the Queen of the fonio by participants.

Kore initiation is the principal life-cycle and adulthood rite described by the sources. Bassari male adulthood is defined by entering the Bassari sect of the Kore initiation society. The first stage begins when a boy is fifteen. He is temporarily separated from his family and undergoes a symbolic rebirth as an initiate. Kore has three levels of adulthood, each lasting six years.

Kore and other initiation societies were nearly destroyed under the Sekou Toure regime of the 1960's. Toure’s Militia conducted public unmasking ceremonies before women and children, beat the men and burned ritual objects. The Bassari tradition survived.

## Foodways

Bassari are primarily subsistence farmers. Their crops include rice, millet, earth-peas and fonio. Fonio has particular importance both as food and as a crop associated with cultural identity and adaptation to climate change. Around 80% of production within Bassari Country is for self-consumption. The agricultural off-season from August to November is described as harsh.

Bassari households have been documented stocking food for 2–3 years in advance. This practice also creates opportunities for market exchange with neighboring communities. The Festival of the Fonio promotes fonio as a local food and crop, especially because the grain had previously been associated with poverty.

## Oral tradition & literature

The mythology of the Bassari centers on the creation god Unumbotte. The sources also describe *endaw*, a formless being associated with symbolic rebirth during Kore initiation. Endaw exists as a guttural sound performed by initiates and is often represented visually as a leafy mass or a chameleon.

## Language & religion

Bassari, also called Basari or Oniyan, is a Senegambian language of Senegal and Guinea spoken by the Bassari people. It is also known in the sources as Onian, Onëyan, Ayan, Biyan and Wo. The Bassari call their language o-niyan, which is identified as a Tenda language. They call themselves *a-liyan*, plural *be-liyan*, translated as “those of the laterite.” In the Kedougou population, people are also fluent in Wolof, described as one of the vernacular languages between Senegalese villages. French is the official language of Senegal; the source states that only educated men are fluent in it, while some women have learned French through training courses.

Most Bassari are animists, with a significant minority of Catholic and Protestant Christians. Very few are Muslims. The mythology of the group centers on Unumbotte. Male adulthood is linked to Kore initiation, symbolic rebirth and the ritual roles of masks and kore.
