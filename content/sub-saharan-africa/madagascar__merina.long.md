## Overview

The Merina — also called *Imerina*, *Antimerina*, *Borizany* or *Ambaniandro* — are the largest of Madagascar's eighteen official ethnic groups and the "highlander" Malagasy people of the island's central plateau, concentrated in the former Antananarivo Province around the capital. Their origins are mixed: early Austronesian settlers who reached Madagascar from Maritime Southeast Asia before the 6th century, Bantu migrants from the African mainland, and later Neo-Austronesian arrivals, with smaller Arab, Indian and European contributions; oral tradition traces them to an aboriginal group called the *Vazimba*. They speak the Merina dialect of Malagasy, an Austronesian language and Madagascar's national language, spoken fluently by an estimated 7.5 million people in 2011. In folk-culture terms they matter for their engineered highland rice landscape, their silk *lamba* weaving, and a caste order and ancestral tomb cult that still shape social life.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The museum objects for the Merina are dominated by cloth, and the sources name the tradition with enough precision to describe technique.

**Lamba** — the general term for the rectangular Merina shawl, worn by men and women alike and visible in every nineteenth-century market and street photograph in this collection. Museum examples are silk, warp-striped in bands of varying width — purple, blue, red, green, yellow, black; one white example has alternating narrow red stripes and blue pin-stripes. Cloths are typically woven as two or three broad strips sewn together lengthwise, with loose warp-thread or long plaited fringes at both ends.

**Lamba akotofahana** — the figured, brocaded class of silk *lamba*. On top of the warp striping, patterns are built up in supplementary weft floats in as many as ten colours (blue, magenta, yellow, purple, green, orange, pink, pale turquoise, white). One two-panel example in this collection served as a burial cloth.

**Supplementary single-heddle patterning** — the British Museum records identify the technical signature of the finer *lamba*: pattern stripes bearing geometric or rosette designs woven "using a series of supplementary single-heddles." A narrow silk **trial piece** in the collection — three alternating yellow and green pattern stripes flanked by fine lines in red, green, yellow, black and blue — shows the weaver working out such a band before committing it to a full cloth.

**Aloe-fibre and raffia weaving** — a separate, coarser register of striped cloth. A noble's skirt is two rectangles of brown and blue woven aloe fibre sewn lengthwise with a pink cotton fringe; a matching shirt is built from folded rectangles with sewn-on sleeves and a V-neck. A costume identified as a Queen of Madagascar's is two lengths of brown raffia striped in blue, light blue, tan and beige.

**Burial cloth** — silk *lamba* used as shrouds are a distinct object class here. One is three woven lengths sewn lengthwise: a wider centre with a black stripe flanked by beige, carrying multicoloured floral designs on a purple-and-black striped ground, and two narrower outer strips with purple stripes and floral figuring on red.

**Motif vocabulary.** Warp stripes of varying widths; supplementary weft float motifs; diamonds (a peach and blue silk shawl woven with diamond shapes); rosettes in white and yellow; paired floral motifs on side bands; blue chevrons within fine red stripes; unspecified geometric designs in pattern stripes.

### Clothing & dress

Dress in the photographs and objects is organised around the *lamba*. Men wear it over trousers or a tunic, always with head-gear; women wear it over dresses. It appears in the market views, in the group portrait in front of a brick building, and on the men driving carts drawn by *ombi* (humped cattle) — it is ordinary daily wear across age groups, not a costume. Head covering is near-universal for men in these images. One portrait shows a man seated on a palace step in what appears to be a uniform of shirt, trousers and head-gear, and market-goers carry dark umbrellas or shelter under large white ones.

Elite dress is materially distinguished rather than differently cut: a noble's aloe-fibre shirt and skirt, and a raffia costume attributed to a queen, use striped bast and raffia with cotton fringes. The most elaborate garment in the collection is a **cap or hat** of plaited vegetable fibre covered in red felt — a broadly rectangular body with a wooden batten nailed horizontally across the top, and a long tapering red felt tail ending in red and black tassels, embroidered in silk and gold thread and appliquéd with felt floral patterns. Silk *lamba* also clothed the dead as shrouds.

### Architecture

The sources describe the Merina built environment only glancingly. The plateau landscape itself was engineered: early settlers built vast irrigation works that drained the marshes of the highlands, watered arable land and allowed two rice harvests a year, and by the eighteenth century this infrastructure underwrote the Imerina kingdom's wealth. Their capital — Antananarivo — remains the capital of Madagascar, and the Andriana nobility were buried within the sacred hills of Imerina.

Of buildings, the photographs record a raised open thatched structure reached by steps, with a low brick wall at one side and rectangular thatched shelters beside it; brick buildings behind a family portrait; and a palace building in the capital. A market takes place in the open, sellers sitting on the ground around their baskets under large white umbrellas. Family tombs are the architecturally significant form the texts name: they stand in the ancestral village of origin and hold the lineage dead.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Merina material culture in this collection runs to iron farm tools and worked horn and brass. Agricultural implements include an iron-and-wood **spade** and **sickle**, matching the sources' account of rice, cassava and potato cultivation; one photograph shows a plough with a circular blade and two handles standing before a thatched structure. A fishing **basket** of plant fibre and the flat baskets ranged around market sellers represent the plaited-fibre trades.

A **gunpowder-flask** of black cow's horn carries decorated brass mounts at mouth, tip and centre with an iron carrying chain, and its own cast brass cover. Weapons include a **spear** with a rounded brown wooden shaft and a broad leaf-shaped iron head socketed open on one side, and a circular wooden **shield** covered in brown and white ox hide, stitched at the edge with leather thongs, its handle cut from the solid.

Woodwork is represented by a model **loom** with two thick side pieces, uprights and three cross-pieces, still holding an unfinished brown-and-blue striped textile with sticks and a small bobbin in place, and by a carved low-relief **panel** from a nineteenth-century bed showing the Merina army, birds and a formal aristocratic event.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Merina ornament in this collection is silver, gilt metal and beadwork, and much of it is protective. A gilt-metal **waist-ornament** takes the form of a rectangular box-amulet studded with red, blue, green and yellow glass, hung with five gilded ornaments shaped as crocodile teeth; its lid carries five gilded cupolas, and it suspends from a brown silk brocade waistband with cream details. A silver **amulet** or charm is modelled as a zebu — a humped bull with shortened legs and long tapering horns — pierced through the shoulder for suspension, tying personal adornment to the cattle that dominate Merina ritual and diet.

A silver **head-ornament** is a chain of ring and cable links from which shorter vertical chains hang, each ending in a silver coin. A silver **hat-ornament** is made in the form of animal claws. Two **bangles** are cotton cloth covered with multicoloured glass beads.

## Music & performance

The sources used — the Wikipedia article on the Merina people and the catalogue records of the objects shown — do not describe Merina instruments, song genres or performance occasions, and there are no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions for Madagascar to draw on.

## Festivals & rituals

Merina ritual life centres on the ancestors and on cattle. The land is *tanin'drazana*, the land of the ancestors, and reverence takes material form in burial: the dead are laid in family tombs, normally in the ancestral village of origin, and many hold that ancestors intervene in earthly events for good or ill — a belief that shapes conduct widely among Malagasy. The presence of a family tomb was itself a social fact of the first order. In earlier usage the *fotsy*–*mainty* distinction ("white–black") did not turn on physiognomy but on tombs: *fotsy* had a family tomb, *mainty* had none or only a recently established one. Silk shrouds — including figured *lamba akotofahana* — are among the objects here.

Boys are circumcised within their first seven years, in a rite at which relatives ask the ancestors for blessing and protection. Cattle sacrifice is the other named rite: the Merina ritually kill their cattle with unusual violence, then cook and ceremonially consume the beef.

Marriage was governed by caste. The Andriana were subdivided into six sub-strata, each endogamous and holding an inherited occupation, and marriage or any sexual relation between *fotsy* and *mainty* was taboo. A 2012 report by Gulnara Shahinian, the United Nations Special Rapporteur on contemporary forms of slavery, found that descendants of the former slave castes continue to suffer and that cross-caste marriages are socially ostracised. The sources used name no annual or seasonal festival calendar.

## Foodways

Rice governs the Merina table so completely that the ordinary term for eating a meal is simply "to eat rice." Rice is *masina* — holy — and a common Merina belief holds that eating it is the key to moral behaviour; the French who occupied Merina lands were looked down on for eating bread instead. The double harvest that supports this was made possible by the irrigation works the early settlers built on the plateau, which drained marshes and watered arable land well enough to grow rice twice a year. Rice, cassava and potatoes are the staple crops, with onions and other supplements grown alongside.

Beef is the other pillar. Merina oral history credits a servant of King Ralambo with the discovery that cattle were edible; he passed the knowledge to the king, who told the rest of the kingdom. Cattle are ritually killed with unusual violence, and the beef is cooked and eaten ceremoniously. Cattle, pigs and animal husbandry are significant occupations, and humped cattle — *ombi* — appear in the photographs drawing carts on the road, while a silver charm in the collection is cast as a zebu. Markets are where this food economy is visible: sellers sit on the ground among flat baskets, or set up stalls under large white umbrellas, buyers in *lamba* and head-gear moving between them.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral history is the Merina account of their own past, and the Wikipedia article treats it as such. It traces the emergence of a united kingdom in Imerina to the early sixteenth-century king Andriamanelo, records the settlement of the central highlands in the fifteenth century after wars and migrant pressure from the coast, and preserves the story of King Ralambo's servant discovering that cattle could be eaten.

The *Vazimba* dominate Merina oral history and popular imagination. They are held to have been the island's original population, descended from Southeast Asian seafarers, and among some Malagasy they are not reckoned human at all but a kind of supernatural being possessing magical power — *mahery*.

Written literature in Malagasy followed the Latin script introduced by Christian missionaries, and cultural self-assertion took organised form in 1913 with a secret society, *Vy Vato Sakelika* (VVS, "Iron and Stone Network"), founded in Antananarivo under the Malagasy Protestant pastor Ravelojoana and dedicated to affirming Malagasy cultural identity. It was repressed through arrests in 1915 and 1916 and re-emerged in the 1920s. The sources used name no epic poem, proverb collection or riddling tradition.

## Language & religion

Malagasy is an Austronesian language; the Merina dialect, also called Highland Malagasy, became the official language and the basis of the country's other dialects, and is also widely spoken by the Betsileo, Bezanozano, Sihanaka and Vakinankaritra and by south-eastern groups other than the Antesaka. It is written in Latin script, introduced by missionaries, and under the 2010 constitution shares official status with French; the 2007 constitution had also included English. Malagasy is the medium of instruction in public schools through grade five.

King Radama I welcomed Christian missionaries in the 1810s and the Merina nobility were among the first converts; the London Missionary Society founded missions along the coast in the 1820s, sending converts to scholarships in London and apprenticeships in Manchester. Under British influence the Merina upper classes became wholly Protestant by the mid-nineteenth century, following Queen Ranavalona II. The French favoured Catholic interpreters and former Merina slaves converted to Catholicism, so denomination came to track caste and ethnicity — a division still legible in Madagascar's religious demography. Ancestor veneration runs alongside: tombs, ancestral intervention in worldly affairs, and the *mahery* power attributed to the *Vazimba*.
