## Overview

The Damara, also called Damaras, Damaran, Daman, or Damaqua, are an ethnic group who make up 8.5% of Namibia’s population. They speak Khoekhoe, also known as Khoekhoegowab or Damara, a Khoe-family language, and the majority live in northwestern Namibia, although Damara communities are found throughout the country. They are associated with central Namibia, the Erongo region, Windhoek, Damaraland, and numerous river valleys and mountain areas, including the Brandberg, Erongo, Waterberg, Khomas Hochland, and the valleys of the Swakop, Kuiseb, Hoanib, Huab, and Orange rivers. Their folk-culture profile includes pastoralism, agriculture, copper-smithing, hunting rituals, clan-based identity, distinctive clothing, leatherwork, body adornment, and a complex vocabulary of named communities and landscapes.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources describe clothing made from animal hides and later from manufactured fabrics.

**Damarokoes** — The Damarokoes is a Damara dress adopted from missionary wives in the mid-19th century. It has ankle-length construction, long sleeves, and a *ǃkhens* shawl.

**ǃgaes** — The *ǃgaes* is an apron-like loin-cloth worn by all Damara children to cover the genitalia.

**ǁnaweb** — The *ǁnaweb* is a loin-cloth worn by an unmarried man, tucked between the legs.

**ǀgâubes** — The *ǀgâubes* is a rear loincloth worn by unmarried and married women.

**ǀawiǃgaes** — The *ǀawiǃgaes* is a loincloth consisting of strips, worn by married women instead of the regular *ǃgaes*.

**ǃgūb** — The *ǃgūb* is a skirt-like loin-cloth or traditional skirt worn by elderly men and women.

**ǀhapis** and **ǀhapib** — The *ǀhapis* is a tussled apron for females, while the *ǀhapib* is the corresponding form for males; both were worn with an anklet made from moth larvae during performances or dances.

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no Damara textile motifs or pattern vocabulary.

### Clothing & dress

The Damara used animal hides for clothing, especially springbok and goat hides for garments and sheep and jackal hides for blankets. Dress distinguished girls, unmarried and married women, elderly women, boys, unmarried and married men, and men of age. Girls and boys wore the *ǃgaes*. An unmarried man wore a *ǁnaweb*, while an unmarried woman wore a *ǃgaes* and *ǀgâubes*. A married man with children, called an *aob*, wore a *sorab*, a strip of soft leather passed between the legs and tucked beneath a waist thong. He also wore a *danakhōb*, a small animal hide presented by his wife at the wedding and worn on the head during ceremonies and auspicious occasions. Married women wore a *ǀgâubes* and *ǀawiǃgaes*. The *ǁkhaikhōb*, made from the hide of a medium-sized antelope, covered the breast and abdomen and was worn during pregnancy, at ceremonies, and by elder women. Elderly men and women wore the *ǃgūb*. Contemporary Damara men wear shirts, coats, or blazers in blue, white, and green, while women wear the Damarokoes and *ǃkhaib* headgear.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The Damara were copper-smiths known for melting copper and making ornaments and jewellery. Iron was used for knives and spear heads, and also for traditional earrings, arm bangles, and anklets. Women used a *ǀīǃnâs*, a dome-shaped basket, during the perfuming of hides and blankets with buchu. A *ǃūro-ams*, made from hare fur, was used to apply *sâ-i* buchu powder from a *ǃūros*, a tortoise-shell container carried by women.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Women wore *ǃgamdi*, small traditional earrings made from iron or copper, and necklaces of ostrich egg shells called *ǁnûib* in Khoekhoegowab. They also wore *ǃganudi* arm bangles and *ǃgoroǃkhuidi* ornamental anklets, originally made from iron or copper and later replaced by beads or ostrich egg shells. Men wore unadorned and denser arm bangles called *ǃganugu* and anklets called *ǃgoroǃkhuigu*. A strand of beads crossing the chest was called a *karab*. An anklet made from moth larvae, *ǀkhîs*, was worn during performances and dances. Girls underwent *ǂgaeǂnoas*, the making of earring holes, and later began wearing *ǃgamdi*. Women perfumed hides and blankets with buchu, applied red ochre to their faces, and used *sâ-i* buchu powder.

## Music & performance

 They do describe performances and dances in connection with particular forms of adornment. An anklet made from moth larvae, *ǀkhîs*, was worn during performances and dances, together with a tussled apron called a *ǀhapis* for females or *ǀhapib* for males. The sources also state that Damara men underwent an elaborate hunting ritual as an initiation into manhood. The ritual was repeated twice, first for teenagers and later for grown men, after which the initiates were considered clan elders.

## Dance & theatre

The sources mention performances and dances. They state only that *ǀkhîs* moth-larvae anklets and *ǀhapis* or *ǀhapib* tussled aprons were worn during performances and dances.

## Festivals & rituals

 They describe several life-cycle and community rituals. Damara boys were initiated into manhood through an elaborate hunting ritual, performed first for teenagers and repeated for grown men; completion of the second ritual marked entry into the group of clan elders. Damara males were not circumcised. A girl was defined as a female who had not yet undergone menstruation, and she stayed in the house during her first menstrual cycle. A boy was defined as a male who had not yet undergone the first hunting ritual. Girls underwent *ǂgaeǂnoas*, after which they began wearing *ǃgamdi* earrings. Marriage was marked by the presentation of a *danakhōb* by the wife to her husband, who wore the hide on his head during ceremonies and auspicious occasions. The sources also describe beliefs concerning death: the deity ǁGamab directs human fate, and after death the souls of the dead travel to his village in heaven.

## Foodways

The sources state that the Damaran practiced pastoralism with sheep and cattle and were also agriculturalists who planted pumpkins, corn, and tobacco. They were rich in cattle and sheep, and some chiefs possessed up to 8,000 head of horned cattle. The sources describe men as traditionally hunting and herding cattle, while women’s household duties included cooking, cleaning, gardening, milking cows, and nurturing the young. The sources also mention that one Damara clan, the ǀGaiodaman, was associated with consuming the wild cucumber, while the ǃNaranîn were associated with the ǃNara plant.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state that oral tradition holds that the Damaran came to Namibia from ǁKhaus, described as the Equatorial Rainforest, through ǃĀǂkhib. Oral and written historical records describe their settlement between Huriǂnaub, the Kunene River, and ǃGûǁōb, the Kavango River, followed by movement into other parts of Namibia. Oral tradition also preserves accounts of battles, dispersal, clan formation, and the leadership of Damara Gaobs.

## Language & religion

Khoekhoe, also called Khoekhoegowab, Damara, or Nama/Damara, belongs to the Khoe language family and is spoken primarily by Namakhoen, ǂNūkhoen, and Haiǁomkhoen in Namibia, Botswana, and South Africa. It is a national language in Namibia. Modern scholars generally identify the Nama–Damara dialect, including Sesfontein Damara, Haiǁom, and ǂĀkhoe; Eini is described as extinct. Khoekhoe has click consonants, including dental, lateral, alveolar, and palatal clicks, and uses a subject–object–verb word order.

The supreme deity of the Damaran, or ǂNūkhoen, is ǁGamab, also called ǁGammāb, ǁGauna, ǁGaunab, and Haukhoin. He is associated with water, clouds, thunder, lightning, seasonal renewal, game animals, crops, and death. After death, souls travel to ǁGamab’s village in heaven, gather around a ritual fire, and receive a drink of liquid fat.
