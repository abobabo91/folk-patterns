## Overview

The Dinka — *Jiɛ̈ɛ̈ŋ* in their own language, *Muonyjang* in the singular — are a Nilotic people of South Sudan, living along the Nile from Mangalla-Bor north to Renk, across the Bahr el Ghazal and Upper Nile regions and the Ngok Dinka area of Abyei. The 2008 Sudan census put them at roughly 4.5 million, the largest ethnic group in South Sudan. They are agro-pastoralists whose year is divided between rainy-season villages on high ground and dry-season cattle camps in the seasonally flooded *toic* grasslands. Their material world is built around cattle, the spear and the river: parrying shields and leaf-bladed spear-heads, beaded corsets worn by men, ostrich-eggshell belts, ivory armlets, and shrine-mounds raised over the bodies of priests. Along with the Tutsi of Rwanda they are the tallest population measured in Africa.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here — Wikipedia and the museum catalogue records of the objects shown — do not describe Dinka weaving or cloth-patterning as a named tradition. What the collection holds instead are beadwork garments, in which the pattern is built from strung glass and shell rather than woven thread.

**Beaded corset** — a man's collar or bodice built as a corset: a "spinal column" of hide down the back from which strings of predominantly blue glass beads spring to encircle the torso, meeting at the front in a panel of red, yellow, black and white beads finished with six brass tassels.

**Beaded collar** — seventeen strings of faceted blue glass beads, the central front panel made of seventeen pairs of spotted beads flanked by round white beads, with three pendants made from brass cartridge cases; the back is bound with hide into a spine edged by a row of seventeen white beads on either side.

**Ostrich-eggshell breast-ornament** — fifteen strings of ostrich shell beads plus one string of faceted blue glass beads, attached to two woven hide bands, each band decorated with coiled metal and a cowrie shell and carrying hide thongs.

### Clothing & dress

The catalogued garments are almost entirely men's. The beaded corset described above is recorded as a man's bodice, worn against the body with the hide spine at the back. Belts are made of light brown goat skin, of five or eight strands of white ostrich eggshell beads, and — in one case that dates itself — of nickel bullet-cases strung on hide. Two hats are held: a conical hat woven from straw and finished with a plume of brown ostrich feathers, and a hat made of human hair built over a palm-leaf frame with cotton material. Neck-bands are made from thongs of plaited cow hide. Two of the photographic prints in the collection show adult Dinka men unclothed while setting and carrying an animal trap, which is consistent with dress being ornamental and occasional rather than continuous. The sources do not describe women's dress, footwear, or a ceremonial costume distinct from daily wear.

### Architecture

Dinka settlement follows the flood. Rainy-season villages of mud and thatch housing stand on the highlands — ground raised only a few centimetres above the surrounding plain, but enough to sit above flood level. These permanent settlements include cattle byres, called *luak*, and granaries. From about December–January everyone except the aged, the ill and nursing mothers moves to semi-permanent dwellings in the *toic* for grazing, returning around May–June when the rains begin. The museum's photographic prints show rectangular thatched shelters with mud walls, beds set outside with four poles rising from the ground around them, and open thatched shelters in the background.

A second built form is religious. Across Dinka territory stand mounds the Dinka themselves describe as "pyramids", raised in a cone from cattle ashes, cow dung, cotton soil, clay and debris. Each is tied to a prominent priest who ordered its construction as a monument to his name: **Alel** at Makuac in Western Luaic territory, entombing the Eastern Twic priest Kuol Alel; **Yik Ayuong** at Rukcuk, over the priest Ayuong Dit, who was shut into his cattle byre with his wife and eight bulls by his own order; **Luak Deng**, a palisade on high ground by a pool ringed with ardeiba and suba trees, holding the shrine of the deity Deng Dit; and **Pwom Ayuel**, on Zeraf Island, the burial place of the culture hero Ayuel.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The catalogued objects are wood, iron, hide and plant fibre rather than pottery. Weapons dominate: leaf-shaped iron spear-heads with a cylinder at the base for fitting to the shaft; narrow parrying shields carved from a single piece of wood with a central boss, a hollowed-out handle and two tapering ends — one is four inches across at its bulbed middle, narrowing to an inch at the tips, with a deep groove down the back; straight wooden clubs, one knobbed with a pointed butt, one grooved and served with lizard skin at the grip, one of heavy hard wood pierced at the butt for suspension; a staff or club of light brown wood with a hide-covered handle; and a wooden bow strung with fibre and bound with crocodile skin at each end. Household goods include an iron-bladed hoe, a four-legged stool carved from wood in a crescent shape and fixed with iron nails, a beer strainer of plaited vegetable fibre, a swizzle-stick tipped with vertebrae, a bamboo cradle lashed with leather, a tobacco box of bamboo covered in lizard skin with a gourd stopper, and an iron cow-bell with iron ring, clapper and hide thong.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Ivory, ostrich eggshell and imported glass are the materials of Dinka ornament. The collection holds ivory armlets, including one made from two joined pieces; a necklace of small coloured glass beads with an ivory pendant; a man's necklet of three strings of green glass beads carrying a square ivory charm; a necklet of twenty-six opalescent glass beads; a double-spiral nose-ornament of aluminium wire; and a pair of armlets made of cow-hide tassels on leather thongs. The waist belts of ostrich-eggshell beads, in five and eight strands, belong to the same ornamental system. One body-marking practice is documented: young men pass into adulthood through an initiation that includes marking the forehead with a sharp object, and at that ceremony they acquire a second cow-colour name.

## Music & performance

The collection holds two Dinka instruments. The **lyre** has a wooden frame, five strings of vegetable fibre, and a resonator made from a tortoise carapace with a hide cover laced into position. The second is a **pipe** made from reed, with six small holes running its length and a wax seal at each end.

Song is embedded in Dinka religious practice rather than separated from it. The stages of sacrificial prayer run: the leader describes the issue facing the people; leader and congregation acknowledge past sins; praise is offered through hymns of honour or **ox-songs**; and the misfortune is expelled onto the sacrificial animal. Those speaking the prayers hold a fishing spear, chanting short phrases naming the need while thrusting the spear at the animal, with the participants repeating the leader's words. At times of crisis the praying and sacrificing continues for long stretches. Song also carried Christianity into the villages: after 1983, when educated Dinka including ordained clergymen fled the cities for rural areas, songs and praise were the medium used to teach a largely illiterate population the new faith. Francis Mading Deng's *The Dinka and Their Songs* (Clarendon Press, 1973) is the standing published collection.

## Dance & theatre

The sources used for this profile do not describe Dinka dance or dramatic traditions.

## Festivals & rituals

The ritual year is set by the flood and the herd. Movement to the rainy-season settlements begins in May–June with the planting of millet and other grains; harvest follows when the rains are heaviest in June–August; cattle are driven to the *toic* in September and November as rainfall drops off, to graze on crop remnants.

Two named annual observances attach to the shrine-mounds. At the pyramid of Alel, north of the Paliang region in Bahr el-Ghazal, a celebration is held every year in honour of Kuol Alel; local Dinka reckon the mound predates the Egyptian colonial period of 1821, making it more than two centuries old. At Yik Ayuong, District Commissioner Ibrahim Bedri recorded a mound seventy-five paces in circumference and twenty-six paces along the slope; in the harvest season it was cleared of grass, built up with more earth, and smoothed by women who drew stripes down its face with large quantities of durra flour in preparation for the ceremony **yairunka baiet**. At that gathering the people made communal offerings to the spirit of Ayuong Dit and sacrificed eight bulls, which was believed to make women fertile. Luak Deng, called the "Mecca" of the Dinka and Nuer, is tended by a small section of Rut Dinka living there by agreement with the Nuer.

The central rite of Dinka religion is the sacrifice of oxen by the **masters of the fishing spear** (*beny bith*), the at least partly hereditary ritual chiefs of otherwise independent clans. Life-cycle ritual is documented at two points: male initiation, with the forehead marking and the second cow-colour name, and marriage, where grain and cattle serve as bridewealth. Cattle given as bridewealth may be returned if a couple divorces, which is why cattle bought with surplus grain are held in higher honour — that wealth is not returnable, carries no external links, and counts as "pure property" derived from labour.

## Foodways

Sorghum is the most important Dinka crop, grown alongside okra, sesame, pumpkin, cow peas, maize, cassava, groundnuts, several kinds of beans, watermelon, tobacco and millet. Before the Sudanese civil wars each household cultivated about two acres of sorghum around the homestead. Crops supply an estimated 87% of total calories and 76% of protein by weight, against 13% of calories and 24% of protein from livestock — so the Dinka are agro-pastoralists, not pastoralists, and about 83% of available labour goes to agriculture against 17% to livestock husbandry. Both genders cultivate; on large farms the women brew beer and everyone joins in. The beer strainer of plaited vegetable fibre in the collection belongs to this work, as does the wooden swizzle-stick tipped with vertebrae.

During their southward migrations the Dinka introduced **caudatum sorghum** into southern Sudan — drought-resistant, productive with little care, and not grown by the region's existing peoples during the great droughts of the 1300s to 1600s. Milk is prioritised for children when supply is low; children are fed milk from nine to twelve months and start on solid food, porridge, after about a year. From the age of three children eat two meals a day, as do adults. Men and women eat separately. Beer-drinking rituals accompany the sowing and harvesting of sorghum, a practice whose resemblance to Nubian custom was noted by Ibn Selim el-Assouani.

## Oral tradition & literature

Dinka oral history carries the migration itself: accounts of fierce battles in which the Dinka forced the Funj northward, of the displacement and absorption of peoples in their path, and of the culture hero **Ayuel Longar**, who reached the Nile and Sobat and — in the version recounted by the Aliab Dinka Parmena Awerial Aluong — ordered the building of the great monument at Pwom Ayuel himself, some labourers dying in the work and their bodies adding to the rising edifice. Creation narratives centre on the first humans **Garang** and **Abuk**, molded from the clay of Sudan; Garang's protest that permanent death was necessary lest food run short, and Abuk's disobedience in planting more than one grain, after which Nhialic cut the rope connecting Heaven and Earth. The Dinka also name the stars: Orion is *Wathal Jook*, the dogs who are hunters, and the constellation of the scorpion is *Cuur*, a fish that resembles a scorpion.

Recent Dinka literature is dominated by the refugee experience — John Bul Dau's *God Grew Tired of Us*, and *They Poured Fire On Us From The Sky* by Alephonsion Deng, Benson Deng and Benjamin Ajak.

## Language & religion

Dinka — *Thuɔŋjäŋ* — is a Nilotic dialect cluster with main varieties including Padang, Rek, Agaar, Ciec, Malual, Aliab, Bor, Hol, Nyarweng, Twic East and Twic Mayardit, distinct enough to require separate literary standards. It has thirteen contrastive short vowels, four described phonations, three contrastive vowel lengths, and heavy grammatical use of tone. Its vocabulary shows considerable proximity to Nubian from medieval contact with the kingdom of Alodia. Missionaries wrote it in several Latin alphabets from the late 19th century; the current script was made official at the 1928 Rejaf Language Conference, and the New Testament appeared in Dinka in 2006. Traditional religion is polytheistic under one creator, *Nhialic*, god of sky and rain, with *Deng* the sky god of rain and fertility and *Abuk* the patron goddess of gardening and of all women, represented by a snake. Totems (*kuar*) are inherited from both parents and spoken of as relatives, not worshipped. Most Dinka converted to Christianity after 1983.
