## Overview

The Himba (singular *OmuHimba*, plural *OvaHimba*) are a semi-nomadic pastoralist people of roughly 50,000, living in Namibia's Kunene Region — the arid northwest formerly administered as Kaokoland — and across the Kunene River into southern Angola. They speak *OtjiHimba*, a dialect closely related to Herero within the Bantu branch of Niger–Congo, and are culturally distinguishable from the Herero despite that linguistic kinship. Small groups of *OvaTwa*, hunter-gatherers whom the OvaHimba count as part of their own people, live among them. They are commonly described as the last semi-nomadic people of Namibia: base homesteads with rain-fed maize and millet, but movement within the year following rainfall and water. In folk-culture terms they matter because almost the entire material repertoire — dress, hairwork, jewelry, house, cosmetic — is still made, worn and repaired inside the homestead rather than recovered from archives, and because that repertoire is legible as a precise notation of age, marital status and kinship.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Himba material culture is a **skin-and-ochre** tradition, not a woven-cloth one. There is no indigenous loom weaving; the pattern-bearing surfaces are hide, iron, shell, bead and the human body itself, and that is what our gallery indexes.

**Himba leather (skin dress)** — Women's skirts and men's loin garments are cut from calfskin and sheepskin, softened by working and dressed with fat, then rubbed with *otjize* so the hide takes the same red as the wearer's skin. Ornament is applied rather than woven: iron and copper studding, incised edging, appliquéd strips, and pendant tabs cut from the same hide. Neighbouring Herero dress shifted to imported Victorian cottons in the colonial period; Himba hide dress did not, which is precisely why the two are visually distinct despite a shared language.

**Otjize** — The signature Himba surface treatment: butterfat creamed with ground red ochre, often scented with the aromatic resin of the *omuzumba* shrub, applied to skin, hair plaits and leather. It cleanses where water is scarce, screens the semi-arid sun and deters insects, and is above all the Himba aesthetic ideal — the red of the earth and of blood, the essence of life.

**Erembe** — The married woman's headpiece, sculpted from sheepskin and worn from roughly a year into marriage or after the birth of a first child, with braided, otjize-loaded hair streams falling from it. It is the single most diagnostic object of Himba dress and marks a status change rather than an occasion.

**Ekori** — A hide headdress of three lobes, leather-worked and often iron-studded, of the older Herero-Himba stratum. It survives in Himba and Zemba use and in museum collections while the Herero replaced it with the cloth *otjikaiva*.

**Ohumba shell pendant** — A conus shell disc, traded inland from the Atlantic coast, worn as a pectoral on a hide or beaded strap. Scarcity and trade distance make it a wealth object; it recurs in nineteenth- and twentieth-century collected assemblages.

**Himba beadwork and aprons** — Anklets, waistbands, girls' front aprons and the small skin backpack worn by women who have given birth are built from iron and copper beads, glass trade beads, ostrich eggshell discs and leather thong, strung in stacked bands rather than figurative panels.

**Himba doll** — Small hide-and-bead figures made for girls, dressed in miniature skirts, *erembe* and beadwork; they teach the dress code before the girl wears it.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Otjize* red — earth and blood, the base register of all Himba surface; banded stacking — repeated horizontal courses of bead, iron and shell; studding — iron or copper nail-heads driven into hide in rows; three-lobed profile — the *ekori* silhouette; braided-stream fall — hair or thong pendants read as parallel lines; conus disc — the *ohumba* circle; coiled-wire spiral on arm and leg rings; incised hide edging; graded bead diameters ascending the limb. Himba pattern is arithmetic and positional, not pictorial: meaning comes from count and placement on the body.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress is short and skin-based, suited to a hot semi-arid climate. Women wear a pleated hide skirt, the small beaded back-piece of skin if they have borne a child, heavy iron and bead anklets, arm rings, and — once married about a year — the *erembe*. Cowhide sandals are usual for women; men's sandals are cut from old car tyres. Men wear a skirt-like hide wrap and, increasingly, modern textiles bought in Opuwo. Hair is the fuller notation system. From puberty a boy keeps a single plait to the rear of the head; a married man covers his head with a cap or head-wrap over unbraided hair, and a widower removes the covering to expose it. Girls wear many otjize-textured plaits, some deliberately arranged to veil the face, tied back in daily work. Married women's plaits hang from the *erembe*, recoloured and reshaped with *otjize*. Wood ash serves for hair cleansing where water is short. There is no separate ceremonial costume set: status, not occasion, is what dress announces.

### Architecture

The residential unit is the *onganda*, a homestead of one extended family — a circular hamlet of huts and work shelters enclosing two sacred things: the *okuruwo*, the ancestral fire, and the kraal for the sacred cattle. Huts are conical or beehive frames of mopane wood, plastered by women and girls with a mixture of red clay soil and cattle dung as binder, renewed as it weathers. The plan is not decorative but cosmological: the fire sits between the headman's hut and the kraal gate, and the axis linking hut, fire and kraal is the line along which the living address the dead. Fire and livestock together are what allow, in the ethnographic phrase, proper relations between human and ancestor. Because pasture use is heterogeneous — rainy-season and rested dry-season grazing — a family may also hold secondary stock posts, built lighter and abandoned seasonally. Decoration is structural: the stockade of upright mopane, the swept plaza, the kraal fence itself.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The defining vessel is not fired clay but the *calabash*, tended on vines maintained by women specifically to guarantee a supply for souring milk; the soured-milk gourd is the household's central utensil and its contents the central food. Wooden milk pails, stirring sticks, headrests, stools and mortars are carved from mopane and other hardwoods. Metalwork is cold-worked and recycled: iron and copper wire drawn into arm, leg and neck rings, beaten sheet and nail-heads for hide ornament, with imported wire long since replacing smelted stock. Leatherworking tools, awls and hide scrapers are household equipment, and women are the artisans for handicraft, clothing and jewelry alike. Fire-making and the fire-keeper's paraphernalia at the *okuruwo* are ritual objects rather than domestic ones.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment is dense, heavy and permanent-in-wear rather than occasional. Women stack coiled iron and copper arm and leg rings, broad beaded and iron anklets, and multi-strand neck ornaments of glass beads, ostrich eggshell disc and metal; the *ohumba* conus shell hangs at the chest as the prestige pendant. Hair is treated as jewelry: plaits weighted and coloured with *otjize*, mounted from the sheepskin *erembe*, the face-veiling arrangement of a girl's plaits read as an ornament in its own right. There is no tattooing or henna tradition; the whole body surface is instead worked in *otjize*, reapplied daily, which is why Himba people appear in the literature above all as the ochre-red people. Boys are circumcised before puberty as a rite of passage — body modification here is ritual rather than decorative.

## Music & performance

Himba musical life is homestead music, unaccompanied or lightly accompanied: group singing with hand-clapping and stamped rhythm, solo praise and herding songs addressed to individual cattle, and night dancing at the homestead plaza. The documented Himba dance repertoire is visual as much as sonic — filmed **Himba dance** from Omuhonga in Kaokoland shows the clapped circle with soloists entering the ring in turn. Beyond that, the specific instrument names, tuning systems and song-genre terminology of OtjiHimba performance are not reliably documented in accessible sources, and I will not supply Herero or generic southern-Bantu terms in their place. Recorded southern-African pastoralist music in the Smithsonian Folkways and international archives is the practical entry point.

## Dance & theatre

Dancing takes place in the swept centre of the *onganda*, in a clapped circle from which dancers step forward singly, and is tied to homestead sociality and life-cycle occasions rather than to a stage tradition. There is no masking, puppet or dramatic-theatre tradition among the OvaHimba. What exists instead is a strongly developed performance of status through appearance, and — as a modern, contested development — the staging of Himba dance for visitors and for foreign television, which the Epupa chief Kapika publicly condemned in October 2013 after the German RTL programme *Wild Girls* filmed in his region, demanding that broadcasts stop because they mocked Himba culture.

## Festivals & rituals

The Himba ritual year is not a calendar of dated festivals but a rhythm governed by rainfall, pasture and the ancestral fire. The recurring rite is the fire-keeper's approach to the *okuruwo* every seven to eight days to address Mukuru and the ancestors on behalf of the family. Life-cycle rites carry the weight. Boys are circumcised before puberty; a boy is reckoned a man on marriage, a girl a fully-fledged woman only when she bears a child. Marriage is arranged, with girls betrothed by their fathers from the onset of puberty — a practice illegal in Namibia, contested by some OvaHimba, and still widespread. Bridewealth is negotiated in cattle between the groom's family and the bride's father, scaled to the families' means; an ox is the standard offer, and the animals must visibly be of high quality for the bride's family to accept. Marriage is polygynous, the average man husband to two wives. Death opens an evaluative process rather than a single funeral: the community weighs the care owed to those left behind, orphans and widows, and rights to water-points and pastures pass along with cattle — inherited not through the father's *oruzo* patriclan but through the mother's *eanda* matriclan, so a son inherits from his maternal uncle. Misfortune and death are often attributed to *omiti*, malicious medicine, and a diviner-healer may be consulted to find its source.

## Foodways

The Himba count wealth in cattle and eat mostly milk. The staple pairing is soured milk with maize porridge — *oruhere ruomaere* — and in seasons when milk and meat are short, plain stiff porridge alone. Fat-tailed sheep and goats are bred alongside the cattle and supply both milk and meat; cows and goats are milked by women and girls, and the soured-milk calabash is kept in continuous production, which is why the calabash vines are tended as deliberately as a crop. Rain-fed maize and millet are grown at the base homestead and ground for porridge; cornmeal bought in Opuwo now supplements it. The diet is filled out with chicken eggs, wild herbs and honey. Butterfat has a second, non-food use as the carrier for *otjize*. Meat eating is tied to slaughter, which is men's work and largely ritual or occasional rather than routine — livestock are sold for cash only opportunistically. There is no tea or coffee ceremony and no indigenous grain-beer tradition documented as central; what governs consumption instead is the distinction between ordinary animals and the sacred herd kept in the kraal beside the ancestral fire, which is not eaten casually.

## Oral tradition & literature

OtjiHimba is an unwritten vernacular and its verbal art is transmitted in the homestead: genealogical recitation tracing both the *oruzo* and the *eanda*, which is the practical instrument of an unusual bilateral descent system and must be held accurately for inheritance and marriage to work; praise naming of cattle; and narrative about *omiti* and about the droughts and wars that structure recent memory — the 1980s drought that killed 90% of Himba livestock, the flight to Opuwo, the abductions across the Angolan border during the South African Border War. There is no named epic cycle. The best documentation is ethnographic rather than literary: David Crandall's year-long residence among the cattle-herders, and Rina Sherman's seven-year film and photographic record *The Ovahimba Years*, which is the closest thing to an archive of Himba speech and performance.

## Language & religion

*OtjiHimba* is a dialect of Herero (Otjiherero), Bantu within Niger–Congo, spoken across the Kunene on both sides of the Namibia–Angola border and unwritten in daily use. The OvaHimba are monotheists who worship *Mukuru* alongside their clan ancestors: Mukuru only blesses, while the ancestors both bless and curse, and because Mukuru is occupied in a distant realm the ancestors commonly act as his representatives. Each family has its own *okuruwo*, the sacred ancestral fire, kept by a designated fire-keeper who is the ritual intermediary. Belief in *omiti* — rendered variously as witchcraft, black magic or bad medicine — accounts for death and for ordinary illness turning fatal, and may strike a relative rather than the intended victim. Diviner-healers are consulted to identify its source. A linguistic curiosity attaches to Himba colour naming, studied repeatedly: four terms — *zuzu*, *vapa*, *buru*, *dambu* — partition the spectrum along lines that cut across English categories.
