## Overview

The Longuda live mainly around the volcanic Lunguda Plateau and in parts of Adamawa and Gombe States. Their language has several distinct dialects and considerable word diversity. The profile highlights matrilineal social organisation, marriage rites, Guinea Corn farming, and pottery. Christianity and cultural assimilation have changed some marriage practices.

## Material culture

The recorded material culture includes a hide-reinforced sling and many distinctive pottery vessels, while clothing, buildings, adornment, and textiles are mostly undocumented.

- **Bride’s clothing** — In a contemporary marriage rite, the bride takes her clothing from her parents’ house as a sign that she agrees to marry.

- **Traditional ruler’s seat** — The seat of the traditional ruler is in Guyuk, Adamawa State.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

British Museum records describe a sling and many forms of pottery vessels.

- **Hide-reinforced sling** — The sling is made of string and has a pouch reinforced with hide.
- **Reptile-form vessel** — A British Museum record describes a pottery vessel shaped like a reptile.
- **Cock’s-head vessel** — Some coiled pottery vessels have a cock’s head, and one also has wings.
- **Face-neck vessel** — One pottery vessel has a face on its neck.
- **Handled vessel** — One pottery vessel has a neck and a handle.

## Festivals & rituals

The profile describes marriage rites that differ across dialects and have changed through Christianity and cultural assimilation.

- **Traditional bride-taking rite** — A young man and his friends traditionally took a woman to his cottage without her prior knowledge, after which the families treated them as married.
- **Rescue duel** — The young men from the woman’s neighborhood tried to rescue her, leading to a fight that the groom and his companions usually had to win.
- **Contemporary marriage rite** — In a widely used newer version, the woman consents, both groups take her and her clothing from her parents’ house, and she spends the night in the groom’s uncle’s house.
- **Union token** — A token is left where the couple used to sit during courtship to seal the union.

## Foodways

Guinea Corn is the traditional staple and remains the dominant Longuda crop.

- **Guinea Corn** (*Sorghum bicolor*) — Guinea Corn was formerly ground on a stone hand mill and cooked into a thick paste called tuwo.
- **Tuwo** — Tuwo is a thick Guinea Corn paste eaten with vegetable soup.
- **Rice, maize, and millet** — Rice, maize, and millet are also staple foods today.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral tradition says that the Longuda parted from the Kanuri in Borno State.

- **Longuda origin tradition** — According to oral tradition, the Longuda separated from the Kanuri in Borno State.
- **Longuda folk-tale studies** (*Longuda folk tales*) — John F. Newman studied Longuda folk tales, but the profile gives no details about the tales.

## Language & religion

Lunguda has several named dialects and strong word diversity linked partly to word-taboo customs.

- **Lunguda language** (*Lunguda*) — Lunguda, also called Nʋngʋra, is a Niger–Congo language spoken in Nigeria.
- **Nʋngʋra** — Nʋngʋra is another name for the Lunguda language.
- **Guyuk and Wala dialects** (*Guyuk and Wala Lunguda*) — Guyuk and Wala Lunguda are part of the Longuda dialect cluster.
- **Cerii and Banjiram dialects** (*Nʋngʋra(ma)*) — Nʋngʋra(ma) of Cerii and Banjiram is part of the Longuda dialect cluster.
- **Thaarʋ and Koola dialects** (*Longura(ma)*) — Longura(ma) of Thaarʋ and Koola is part of the Longuda dialect cluster.
