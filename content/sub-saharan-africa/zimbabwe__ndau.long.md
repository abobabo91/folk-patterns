## Overview

The Ndau are an ethnic group indigenous to south-eastern Zimbabwe, especially the districts of Chipinge and Chimanimani. They are also found in parts of Bikita, the Zambezi valley, central Mozambique along the coast, and central Malawi. Their presence in these regions was first documented in the 1500s by Portuguese missionary João dos Santos. The Ndau have historical and cultural connections with the Kalanga, Venda, and Shona peoples, including through the Mapungubwe trading network, while Nguni influences became strong in modern times. As of 1997, an estimated 581,000 people spoke Ndau in Mozambique. Ndau is a Bantu language and a Shona language, mutually intelligible with other Shona languages, and received official-language status in Zimbabwe in 2013.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Mpalu** — A textile traded by the Ndau in the Mapungubwe-centered trade networks. It was described as a distinctive red cloth.

**Njeti** — A traded Ndau textile distinguished in the source as a white cloth.

**Vukotlo** — A traded Ndau textile described as a distinctive blue cloth.

These textiles were among the Ndau trade goods exchanged with various groups, including the Khoisan peoples and Arab traders. The sources do not describe their fibers, weaving techniques, looms, production settings, or later use in clothing.

### Clothing & dress

The sources state that the Ndau traded textiles called *Mpalu*, *Njeti*, and *Vukotlo*, described respectively as red, white, and blue cloths. They also traded animal skins and bones. The sources used do not describe everyday clothing, ceremonial dress, gendered garments, head coverings, footwear, or the ways these cloths were worn.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The historical account identifies traditional herbs, spiritual items, animal skins, bones, textiles, and golden beads among Ndau trade goods. The museum catalogue records objects associated with divination equipment: flasks and containers made of gourd, a container made of gourd, skin with hair, cord, and metal, a basket made of cane, a chief’s ornament made of porcelain, and other divination objects. The records do not identify the objects as specifically Ndau or describe their manufacture, use, or place of origin beyond these catalogue descriptions.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources mention golden beads as historical trade goods. The museum catalogue records a chief’s ornament made of porcelain, a head-band made of cowrie shells, leather, and string, a belt with thongs made of glass beads, leather, and cord, a necklet made of glass beads, cloth, and string, and a fly-whisk with a ring made of wood, horse tail, cord, and glass beads. ## Music & performance

The Ndau language article records songs and musical traditions preserved in publications by C. Kamba Simango, Franz Boas, and Natalie Curtis Burlin. In 1922, Simango, described as a Vandau ethnographer, worked with Franz Boas to publish a collection of 20 Ndau fables and 29 Ndau proverbs with English translations in the Journal of American Folklore.

The collection Songs and Tales from the Dark Continent, published in 1920, included Ndau texts and translations supplied by Simango. It contained Ndau lyrics, English translations, and transcriptions of the music. The named ritual songs include *Mate'ka*, identified as “Song of the Rain Ceremony,” and *Manthi'ki*, identified as “Spirit Song.” Other named songs include *Lum'bo Lgo Lu'do*, identified as “Love-Song,” and *Mafu've*, identified as “Dance of Girls.” The same account lists “Children's Songs,” “Laboring Songs,” and “Dance Songs,” together with *Kufa'mba*, a mocking song, and *Chili'lo*, a lament.

The sources also mention songs in the folktale “How the Animals Dug Their Well,” and songs forming part of the “Legend of the Daughter and the Slave” and the “Legend of the Sky-Maiden.” An appendix provided an interlinear word-for-word rendering of Ndau proverbs and song lyrics into English. The sources used do not name musical instruments or describe performance ensembles, venues, or musicians beyond these publications.

## Dance & theatre

The sources name *Mafu've* as a “Dance of Girls” and refer more generally to “Dance Songs.” They also identify *Mate'ka* as a song of the rain ceremony. The sources used do not describe Ndau dance movements, costumes, dramatic forms, theatre, masks, puppetry, or distinctions between ceremonial and entertainment performance.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used do not provide an annual Ndau festival calendar or dates for festivals. They do describe several ritual and spiritual practices. *Mate'ka* is identified as a “Song of the Rain Ceremony.” Ndau n'anga are traditional healers who communicate with spirits and use traditional medicine, called *mushonga*, to heal or harm people. They may be male or female, although the source says they are more often female.

When resolving a case of spirit aggression, an n'anga determines the nature of the spirit and whether it was sent by a witch or another n'anga. Spirits can be captured in a ceremony called *ukufemba*, in which the spirit introduces itself and explains how it entered a family. N'anga are also called upon to manipulate weather during droughts or floods by appealing to the *nzuzu*. They practice divination by inviting *madzvoka* to possess them, enabling them to perceive remote places and events and to determine the perpetrator of a crime, locate lost people or objects, or discover whether someone is lying.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Ndau language article records 20 Ndau fables and 29 Ndau proverbs published in 1922 by C. Kamba Simango and Franz Boas. The proverbs include “Simba lo ngwena lili mumfula,” translated as “The strength of the crocodile is in the water”; “Chipanga achizivi vatendji,” translated as “The knife does not know its owner”; “Hove djinotevela mulambo wadjo,” translated as “Fish follow their river”; and “Manthede a·novengana pakurga, napamfumfu anobesana,” translated as “Baboons quarrel over food, but in danger help one another.”

Simango also supplied Ndau texts and translations for Songs and Tales from the Dark Continent, published by Natalie Curtis Burlin in 1920. The book included Ndau songs, folktale songs, English translations, musical transcriptions, commentary, and an interlinear rendering of the proverbs and lyrics. Named narratives include “How the Animals Dug Their Well,” the “Legend of the Daughter and the Slave,” and the “Legend of the Sky-Maiden.” The sources do not describe a contemporary literary movement beyond the recorded publications and the 2013 rejuvenation of the ChiNdau identity in Zimbabwe.

## Language & religion

Ndau, also called chiNdau, Chindau, Ndzawu, Njao, and Chidanda, is a Bantu language and a Shona language. The language article states that it is spoken by 2,400,000 people and is mutually intelligible with Manyika, Zezuru, and Karanga. In Zimbabwe, Ndau is mainly spoken in Chipinge and Chimanimani districts. In Mozambique, it is spoken in parts of Sofala Province, including Machanga, Chibabava, Machaze, Buzi, Nhamatanda, Dondo, and Beira, and to a lesser extent in Mambone and Mossurize. Ndau also uses L, Q, and X in its orthography, with sounds acquired from neighboring Nguni languages.

Traditional Ndau identify themselves as one people through Musikavanthu/Mlambo, regarded as a common ancestor and as a rainmaker and earthly embodiment of the Creator of the Universe, known as “Mwari Musikavanthu” or “Musikavanhu.” Many Ndau people have also embraced Christianity through missionary activity. The sources used do not describe a historical script.
