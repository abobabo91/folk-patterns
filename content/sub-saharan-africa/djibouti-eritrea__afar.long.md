## Overview

The Afar (*Qafar*, sometimes rendered *ʿAfar*; historically known to outsiders as the *Danakil* and, in medieval Arab geography, as inhabitants of the sultanate of *Adal*) are a Cushitic-speaking pastoralist people of the Horn of Africa, occupying the arid lowland wedge known as the Afar Triangle where the borders of Djibouti, Eritrea and Ethiopia converge on the Danakil Depression. Their language, *Qafar af*, belongs to the Lowland East Cushitic branch of Afroasiatic and is closely related to Saho; total speakers are commonly estimated at three to four million, with the largest community in Ethiopia's Afar Regional State, roughly one third of Djibouti's citizens, and a substantial minority along Eritrea's Denkalya coast. The Afar homeland is one of the harshest inhabited landscapes on earth — salt pans below sea level, lava fields, the shifting volcanic caldera of Erta Ale, and thin acacia savanna along the seasonal *wadis* — and Afar material culture is accordingly a nomad's culture, pared to what a camel can carry: light palm-fibre mats and baskets, wooden milk vessels, plaited leather, silver amulets and, most emblematically, the crescent-bladed dagger (*jile* or *gile*) worn at every adult man's waist. Historically the Afar controlled the caravan routes that carried rock salt (*amole*) mined from Lake Assale up to the Ethiopian highlands and slaves and ivory down to the Red Sea ports of Tadjoura, Obock and Assab; the medieval Muslim polities of Adal and later the sultanates of Aussa, Tadjoura, Rahaita and Goba'ad grew out of this exchange, and remnants of those sultanate structures still shape Afar clan authority today.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Afar textile production is limited by the pastoral, mobile way of life: unlike the settled weaving cultures of the Ethiopian highlands there is no highland-style loom tradition and no cotton cultivation of note in the Afar lowlands. Cloth has historically been acquired by trade — undyed cotton lengths (*abu jedid*) from Harar, Yemen and later the Red Sea ports — rather than woven at home. What Afar craftswomen do produce and pattern intensively is not cloth but plaited fibre and leather. The signature "textile" of an Afar encampment is the *Afar mat* (*bura* or *daboyta*), a large rectangular sleeping and screening mat plaited from split doum-palm (*Hyphaene*) leaf, its natural cream ground worked in geometric bands of red, black and orange dyed strips arranged as lozenges, chevrons and stepped triangles. A related genre of finer, tightly coiled *Afar basketry* — lidded food bowls (*gambo*), milk covers and small presentation baskets — carries the same disciplined geometric vocabulary and is made as part of a young woman's marriage portion. Leather, tanned and softened with butter and ochre, is fringed, punched and beaded to make skirts, saddle-pads and amulet pouches; this *Afar leather* work, more than any woven textile, functions as the group's decorated surface.

### Clothing & dress

The classic Afar man's dress is the *sanafil*, a length of undyed or lightly striped cotton wrapped around the waist and reaching to mid-calf, worn with the upper body bare or draped in a second cloth (*harayto*) thrown over one shoulder in the manner of a Somali *macawiis* and Ethiopian *gabi* combined. A folded cloth or thin cord belt secures the *jile* dagger horizontally across the belly — the single most diagnostic element of adult male dress, presented at circumcision and worn thereafter for life. Hair is characteristically worn as a high, teased *afro* dressed with clarified butter (*subaag*), a style shared with neighbouring Somali and Beja pastoralists and combed out daily with a long-toothed wooden *Afar comb* (*mishaat*) that men carry thrust into the hair itself. Sandals are cut from raw hide. Women wear a longer wrap (*shash* or *sabota*) covering from breast to ankle, often dyed brown or red-ochre with local earths, together with a second cloth thrown over the head; married women add a black head-cloth (*shash madow*) knotted at the nape. Beading, cowries and small silver charms are sewn onto the collar and hem. In some communities the leather skirt (*harayto* in its older sense) survives as a ceremonial garment worn at weddings and at the reception of guests, its lower edge fringed and hung with metal jingles.

### Architecture

Afar architecture is a nomad's architecture: the standard dwelling is the *ari* (also *aris*), a portable domed hut of bent acacia or *Salvadora persica* saplings lashed into a hemispherical frame roughly two to three metres across and covered with layered palm mats — the same *daboyta* mats used for sleeping — over which, in the wet season, hides or salvaged canvas may be added. The frame is disassembled, bundled and loaded onto pack camels when the encampment (*burra*) moves in search of pasture, and reassembled by the women of the household in under an hour. A cluster of *ari* belonging to a lineage forms the *burra*, typically enclosed by a low thorn *zariba* against hyenas and lions. Permanent architecture is confined to the old sultanate and caravan towns — Tadjoura, Obock, Rahaita, Aussa (Asaita) — where flat-roofed coral-rag and coral-lime houses of Red Sea type, with carved wooden doors and small shuttered windows, line narrow lanes around a Friday mosque; the coral-and-lime mosques of Tadjoura, some attributed to the early Islamic centuries, are the oldest standing Afar monuments. Nomadic Afar have, in short, no monumental stone tradition of their own; the durable landmarks of the Afar country are graves — cairns and, for notables, low rectangular tumuli walled in dry stone and sometimes topped with an upright slab.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Afar ceramics are limited and largely utilitarian — low-fired earthenware water jars and coffee pots traded in from settled neighbours rather than produced in quantity within the pastoral camps. Wood and leather do the work that pottery does elsewhere. The characteristic *Afar milk container* is a tall lidded wooden vessel, hollowed from a single block of acacia or *Cordia* wood and bound with plaited leather or fibre, used to store and gently churn camel and goat milk; smaller lidded gourds serve the same purpose on the move. Every household also owns one or more carved wooden *Afar headrests* (*barkuma*), low stools of a single piece of hardwood with a concave top and two splayed legs, used by men to protect the buttered *afro* hairstyle during sleep and doubling as a portable seat — an object type shared across the Horn with Somali, Oromo and Turkana pastoralists but recognisable in the Afar version by its slender proportions and often incised geometric decoration on the underside. Metalwork centres on the smith's forge, historically the work of endogamous specialist lineages sometimes regarded as caste-apart. Their masterpiece is the *jile* (*gile*) — a broad, curved, double-edged dagger some 30–40 cm long, with a pronounced belly and sharp upturned tip, its hilt of horn, wood or ivory and its wooden scabbard sheathed in tooled leather or, for wealthier men, chased silver. Related smith production includes spearheads, the long straight sword (*seif*) of the sultanate elite, and the round hide *Afar shield* (*gaasha*), typically cut from oryx or buffalo hide, sun-hardened, and fitted with a central boss and a leather handgrip on the reverse.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Afar jewellery is silver, coral and amber, showing the imprint of centuries of Red Sea exchange with Yemen and the Hejaz. Women wear heavy silver hoop earrings, torque necklaces of silver beads interspersed with coral and old Maria Theresa thalers, and stacks of thin silver or copper bangles at the wrist and ankle. Nose ornaments — a small silver stud in the left nostril for married women — are common. The most charged category is the *Afar amulet* (*hijaab* in Arabic, *khataba* in some dialects): a small cylindrical or rectangular silver or leather case containing a folded paper with Qur'anic verses or protective *fal* text, worn on a cord at the neck, upper arm or, for infants, tied into the hair; a matched pair of larger amulet boxes flanking a central pendant is a standard bridal form. Henna (*hinna*) is applied to the bride's hands and feet, and, in some communities, in a broad band across the forehead. Facial scarification — a few short vertical cuts on the cheekbone or temple — is a fading practice, more often seen among older women. Infibulation was historically widespread and is the object of active reform campaigns across the Afar Triangle.

## Music & performance

Afar music is overwhelmingly vocal and drum-driven, tied to the rhythms of pastoral labour, courtship and rites of passage. The core song genres include *keeke*, sung by young men driving camels to and from water; *saare* laments; and long praise poems (*ginnili*) performed by respected elders and clan poets, who occupy a role comparable to the Somali *gabayaa*. Women's genres centre on the *malabo* — collective clapping and hand-drumming songs performed at weddings and after childbirth — and lullabies. The chief instrument is the frame drum, joined occasionally by the single-stringed spike fiddle (*masinqo*) borrowed from Amhara and Tigrayan neighbours, and by the shepherd's end-blown flute cut from a length of reed. Ceremonial announcements — the arrival of a sultan's envoy, the opening of a wedding — were historically made on a long straight trumpet and on kettledrums (*nagaarit*) modelled on those of the Ethiopian and Adal courts. Formal poetic contest, in which two *ginnili* answer each other in extempore verse before an audience that judges the wit and metrical mastery of each turn, is the highest prestige performance form and preserves much of the historical memory of the clans.

## Dance & theatre

Afar dance is participatory rather than theatrical; there is no masked or scripted drama in the ritual repertoire. The best-known form is the *malabo* wedding dance, in which two lines — men and women, or two teams of young men — face one another and advance and retreat in a stamping, shoulder-jerking pattern to a driving hand-clapped rhythm, with soloists springing forward in turn to leap, shake the head, and brandish the *jile*. A related warrior dance, performed at the return of a raiding party or at the reception of guests, features the drawn dagger and the hide *gaasha* shield brought together in a mock parry. Women's line dances at weddings involve ululation (*ilili*), synchronised hip movement and the passing of a lit incense burner along the row. Improvised solo dance during evening camp gatherings, accompanied by clapping and a single drum, is a courtship setting in which young men and women measure each other's poise.

## Festivals & rituals

The Afar calendar is Islamic, and the two Eids — *Ciid Wayn* (Eid al-Adha) and *Ciid Yar* (Eid al-Fitr) — together with the Prophet's birthday (*Mawlid*) are the principal collective festivals, marked by congregational prayer, the slaughter of a goat or camel, and reciprocal visits between camps. Life-cycle rites structure the year for the household: naming on the seventh day after birth (*magaqisiya*), male circumcision in late boyhood at which the initiate receives his first *jile*, marriage — negotiated between maternal cousins in the preferred pattern and marked by bride-price in camels — and the forty-day mourning enclosure after a death, during which the widow remains within the *ari* and receives condolence visits. Older, pre-Islamic strata survive in the veneration of certain trees, springs and volcanic sites, and in seasonal rain-calling ceremonies (*rooba cabsiisa*) at which a black bull or ram is sacrificed and its blood poured at the base of a sacred tree. Clan assemblies (*mablo*) convened under a customary law code known as *madqa* handle disputes, blood compensation (*diya*) and the reaffirmation of grazing rights.

## Foodways

Afar diet is a pastoralist's diet built on milk, meat and imported grain. Camel milk (*caano geel*) is the staple, drunk fresh, soured, and lightly churned in the wooden milk container; goat and cow milk supplement it. Meat — goat, sheep, occasionally camel — is eaten grilled over embers on festive occasions and boiled with clarified butter on ordinary ones. The everyday starch is a thin unleavened flatbread of sorghum or wheat flour (*gaambo*), baked on a heated stone or metal plate, eaten with milk or with a thin sauce of onion, chilli and dried meat; where trade permits, the fermented teff pancake *injera* of the Ethiopian highlands has been adopted. Coffee, taken as a decoction of the husks (*qishr*) rather than of the roasted bean in the older Yemeni manner, and sweet spiced black tea are the required offerings to any guest, brewed on a small charcoal brazier at the entrance of the *ari*. Rock salt from the Danakil pans, once the currency of the caravan trade, remains a staple seasoning and a prestige gift.

## Oral tradition & literature

Afar literary tradition is overwhelmingly oral and poetic. The *ginnili* poets carry genealogies (*ablis*), historical narratives of the sultanates of Adal, Aussa and Tadjoura, elegies for fallen warriors, and long didactic poems on the *madqa* customary law. Shorter genres include the *saare* lament, the *keeke* work song, and pointed *hija* invective verse used to shame an opponent in a clan dispute. Prose folktales, told around the evening fire, feature the trickster hyena, the wise camel, and the culture-hero ancestors from whom the major clan confederations — the Adoyimara "white" and Asayimara "red" — trace descent. A modest written literature in Qafar af has developed since the late twentieth century, using an adapted Latin orthography (*Qafar Feera*) devised in the 1970s; Arabic remains the language of religious learning, and Amharic, Tigrinya and French of secular education in Ethiopia, Eritrea and Djibouti respectively.

## Language & religion

*Qafar af* is a Lowland East Cushitic language of the Afroasiatic family, forming with Saho a small sub-branch; it is tonal in a limited sense, uses a rich system of case-marked nouns, and has been written variously in Arabic script, in Ethiopic *fidel*, and, since the 1970s, in the Latin-based *Qafar Feera*. Nearly all Afar are Sunni Muslims of the Shafi'i school, Islamised gradually from the coastal ports between the ninth and thirteenth centuries and consolidated under the sultanate of Adal in the sixteenth. Sufi affiliations — chiefly to the Qadiriyya and, in some districts, the Sammaniyya *tariqa* — remain strong, and the tombs of local saints (*sheekh*) are places of pilgrimage. Pre-Islamic elements survive in the sacred geography of certain trees, springs and volcanic vents, in the diviner (*ginili*, distinct from the poet *ginnili*), and in the calendrical rain and pasture rites of the pastoral year.
