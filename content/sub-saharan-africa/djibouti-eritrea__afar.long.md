## Overview

The Afar (*Qafár*), long known to outsiders as the **Danakil**, **Adali** or **Odali**, are a Cushitic-speaking pastoral people of the Horn of Africa. Their territory is the Afar Triangle: the Danakil Desert and the volcanic Afar Depression of northeastern Ethiopia, the lower Awash River and Lake Abbe, the salt pans around Lake Assal and Lake Karum, northern Djibouti, and the whole southern coast of Eritrea — making them the only Horn people whose traditional lands touch both the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden. Ethiopia's 2007 census counted 2,276,867 Afar; they form over a third of Djibouti's population. They speak Afar, an East Cushitic language of the Afroasiatic family, paired with Saho in the Saho–Afar dialect cluster. In folk-culture terms the Afar matter as makers of a complete portable material world — mat house, milk vessel, headrest, dagger — carried on camelback across one of the hottest inhabited places on earth.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

Afar craft is dominated not by loom weaving but by plaited and coiled plant fibre, above all the leaf of the doum palm (*Hyphaene thebaica*) that grows along the Awash and in the oases.

**Afar mat (*fiddima*-type palm matting)** — Long strips of split doum-palm leaf, some left natural and some dyed red, black and orange, plaited into panels and stitched edge to edge with leather thong. The panels do double duty as the skin of the house and as the floor, bedding and sitting surface inside it; unlike the rigid reed screens of Ethiopian highland houses, Afar matting is made to be rolled, tied and loaded on a camel.

**Afar basketry** — Tight coiled work in palm fibre over a grass bundle core, the coils counted in bands of contrasting dye. The technique is shared across the Horn and the southern Red Sea littoral, but Afar pieces are characteristically squat, thick-walled and made for liquid rather than for grain.

**Afar milk container** — The signature Afar vessel: a coiled or plaited fibre container so densely worked that it holds milk, waterproofed with fat or resin and fumigated inside with smouldering aromatic wood, which both sterilises it and flavours the soured camel milk it carries. Rims and lids are bound in tooled leather and often hung with cowries or metal discs.

**Afar leather** — Hide from goat, sheep, camel and wild ungulates is scraped, sun-cured and worked into water skins, food bags, sandals, belts, dagger sheaths and carrying straps, and frequently cut into fringes and openwork bands. Leather, not cloth, is the structural material of Afar portable goods.

**Afar shield** — A round or slightly conical buckler of thick hide, oryx or giraffe by preference, moulded wet over a form and carried with a spear. A shield and two spears were the diplomatic gift the Danakil ruler sent Emperor Baeda Maryam in the fifteenth century.

**Cotton wrap cloth (*sanafil*)** — Plain-woven cotton lengths, mostly undyed or with a narrow coloured border, traded in from Red Sea ports and the Ethiopian highlands rather than woven locally. Afar distinctiveness lies in how the cloth is worn and knotted, not in its weaving.

**Motif vocabulary.** Banded registers (horizontal colour courses counted in coils); chevron and zigzag; lozenge and diamond chains; stepped triangle; checkerboard alternation of dyed and natural strips; toothed or sawtooth edging; concentric ring (the growth spiral of coiled basketry made visible in dye); cowrie and disc appliqué as accent rather than pattern; incised chevron and hatching on wood and horn.

### Clothing & dress

Men wear the *sanafil*, a cotton cloth wound round the waist and reaching the calf, knotted at the hip, with a second length thrown over the shoulder in the manner of a toga; a leather belt carries the *jile* dagger at the front of the body, where it is meant to be seen. Older men add a shawl for prayer, and Islamic caps and turbans are ordinary in the coastal towns of Tadjoura, Obock, Assab and Beylul. The most commented-on element of male appearance historically was hair: Amda Seyon's chronicler in the fourteenth century described Afar men wearing their hair plaited to the waist, and into the twentieth century warriors dressed an outsized coiffure with clarified butter, kept it in order with a wooden comb worn stuck in the hair, and slept on a carved headrest to preserve it. Women wear a wrapped skirt of the same *sanafil* cloth with a bodice or shawl, and after marriage a head cloth, the *shash*, commonly dark; brides and dancers add heavy silver and bead ornament. Sandals are cut from thick hide.

### Architecture

Afar building is a mat architecture, engineered for transhumance between dry-season river camps and wet-season grazing. The *ari* is the portable house: a dome of bent acacia or other supple branches lashed into a hemispherical frame and sheathed in the plaited palm-leaf panels described above, with a low door and a smoke gap. It belongs to the woman, who builds it, owns it, dismantles it and rebuilds it, and the whole thing travels as a camel load. The *dabooyta* is its sedentary counterpart — larger, rectangular in plan with a ridged or flattened roof over a posted wooden frame, again clad in mats — used at permanent settlements and dry-season bases along the Awash. Camps group several houses loosely by household and clan, ringed or flanked by thorn-branch enclosures for goats, sheep and the calves of the camel herd. Decoration is structural rather than applied: the pattern of the house is the pattern of its mats. In the old sultanate towns of the Red Sea coast, Afar elites also used the Arabian-influenced coral-rag and lime-plastered courtyard house of the Tadjoura and Rahaito ports.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The defining Afar object is the ***jile*** (also *gile*), a broad curved double-edged dagger with a short hilt and a hide-covered sheath, worn horizontally at the waist by every adult man and inseparable from Afar reputation for martial prowess. Blades were forged by specialist smiths or imported from Harar and the Yemeni coast and refitted with local horn and leather. The **Afar headrest** — a small stool-like neck-rest cut from a single block of hard wood, with an incised or chip-carved stem — protected the buttered coiffure in sleep and travelled tied to the belt. **Afar combs** in wood, horn and bone, long-toothed and carved on the handle, belong to the same grooming set. Regalia included a silver baton, the primary emblem of the Sultan of Aussa, credited with magical properties. Fired pottery is marginal in a mobile economy; wood, horn, hide and coiled fibre take its place, alongside imported enamelware and the salt bars (*amole*) cut from Lake Karum that long served as currency across the region.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Afar silverwork runs to heavy necklaces, torques, bangles and anklets, hoop and pendant earrings, and forehead and temple chains worn by brides, much of it recycled from Maria Theresa thalers, the silver trade dollar that circulated throughout the Red Sea basin. The **Afar amulet** is the region's Islamic type: a cylindrical, rectangular or trapezoidal silver or leather case holding a written Qur'anic verse or a healer's text, hung at the neck, on the upper arm, or on a child's cord against the eye — a material expression of the syncretic religious life described below. Beadwork in imported glass, amber, carnelian and cowrie decorates women's head cloths, belts and the straps of milk vessels. Henna is applied to hands and feet for weddings and Eid; scarification in small patterned cuts on cheek or temple is attested but has receded. Men's hairdressing with ghee, comb and headrest is itself a form of adornment.

## Music & performance

Afar performance is overwhelmingly vocal. The sources on the group agree that its most developed genre is the **battle song** — the Afar are credited with an extensive repertoire of them — sung solo or antiphonally by a leader and a responding group, and directed at named clans, named raids and named dead. Around this core sit praise and lineage songs, camel and herding songs sung on the move and at the well, milking and grinding songs belonging to women, work songs for cutting and loading salt, lullabies, and the ululation (*ilillil*-type trilling) that frames every celebration. Composition and recitation are the province of the *ginnili*, a figure who combines warrior, diviner and poet and whose verse carries both prophecy and the record of clan events. Accompaniment is bodily rather than instrumental: handclapping, stamping, thigh-slapping, and the rhythm of the jile struck against a hide shield. Where instruments appear they are drums — single-headed and frame types — and their most consistent context is Sufi rather than secular: the Qadiriyya order is widespread among the Afar, and *dhikr* sessions at saints' tombs and on the Prophet's birthday use drum and chanted litany. Coastal Afar in Djibouti, Assab and Tadjoura share the sung repertoire and wedding music of the Red Sea littoral, including Arabic-influenced melodic style and the lute and tambourine of the ports.

## Dance & theatre

The Afar have no puppet, mask or scripted theatre tradition. Dance is participatory and occasional, performed at weddings, circumcisions, the return of successful raiders or hunters, and religious feasts. The characteristic form is a line or a facing double line, men on one side and women on the other, advancing and retiring to clapped and sung rhythm, with solo breaks in which a man leaps on the spot, brandishing the *jile* or a spear and shield in a mimed display of prowess — the choreographic residue of the battle-song repertoire. Women's dance emphasises shoulder and neck articulation, footwork kept small, and the sound of massed silver anklets and bracelets; ululation cues the transitions. A secular counterpart to dance as a competitive display is ***kwosso***, the Afar ball game played between clans or age-groups with a ball of rolled hide, which functions much as a festival contest does elsewhere — a scheduled, spectated occasion for inter-clan rivalry to be expressed without bloodshed.

## Festivals & rituals

The Afar calendar is the Islamic one: Ramadan and the night prayers that go with it, *Eid al-Fitr* and *Eid al-Adha* with its slaughter of a sheep or goat, *Mawlid* for the Prophet's birthday, and local *ziyara* pilgrimages to the tombs of holy men, which draw Qadiriyya-affiliated congregations for chanted *dhikr*, feeding and blessing. Alongside this runs a pre-Islamic layer that ethnographers record without embarrassment: rain sacrifices performed at fixed sacred locations — a particular tree, hill, spring or grave — when the rains fail; divination; and folk healing carried out by specialists who also write the amulets described above.

Life-cycle rites: a naming ceremony about a week after birth, with the head shaved and an animal slaughtered; boys' circumcision, marked publicly with feasting and dance and formerly treated as a test of courage before marriage; female circumcision, historically near-universal and now the target of sustained campaigns in Ethiopia, Djibouti and Eritrea. Marriage is governed by ***absuma***, the recognised claim a man holds on his maternal uncle's daughter (and its reciprocal), which keeps alliance within a known circle of kin and makes bridewealth in livestock a transfer between related houses rather than strangers. Funerals are Muslim, with burial within the day, cairn-marked graves in the desert, and the mourning and memorial feasts of a pastoral economy. Disputes — over blood, water, grazing and stock — are settled by elders under ***mada'a***, Afar customary law, and its compensation payments.

## Oral tradition & literature

The most distinctive Afar oral institution is ***dagu***, the obligatory exchange of news. When Afar meet on the road or arrive at a camp, they exchange, at length and in a set order, everything they know about rain, grazing, water, movements of people and stock, deaths, marriages and conflicts. The convention requires accuracy: information passed through *dagu* is expected to be true and to be relayed onward, and it works as a desert-wide intelligence system across a territory with few roads. Beyond news, the repertoire includes clan genealogies recited to fix descent and rights, the prophetic and commemorative verse of the *ginnili*, praise poetry, proverbs, riddles told in the evening, and animal and trickster tales that Didier Morin has studied comparatively across the Cushitic area alongside Saho, Somali and Beja material. Written literature is recent: Afar was standardised in Latin script in the 1970s, and since then schoolbooks, newspapers, radio in Ethiopia, Djibouti and Eritrea, dictionaries and collections of oral texts have created a small modern Afar literary output.

## Language & religion

Afar (*Qafár af*) is an East Cushitic language of the Afroasiatic family, forming the Saho–Afar dialect cluster with Saho, its immediate northern neighbour in Eritrea. It is spoken in the Afar Region of Ethiopia, northern Djibouti and southern Eritrea, and — because the speakers are nomadic herders — well beyond those boundaries. It was written occasionally in Arabic script and is now written in a Latin orthography used in all three states. The Afar are overwhelmingly Sunni Muslim of the Shafi'i school, converted largely by the thirteenth century through traders and holy men from the Arabian peninsula, and organised in part through Sufi orders, of which the Qadiriyya is the most widespread. Religious life is frankly syncretic, combining Islamic observance with rain sacrifice at sacred places, divination and folk healing. Society is organised into clan families under elders, and into two classes: the *asaimara* ("reds"), politically dominant, and the *adoimara* ("whites"), a working stratum associated with the Mabla Mountains — a division inherited from the era of the Afar sultanates of Aussa, Tadjoura, Rahaito, Girrifo and Gobaad.
