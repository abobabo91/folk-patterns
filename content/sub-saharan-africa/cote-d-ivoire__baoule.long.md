## Overview

The Baoulé, also called Baule, are an Akan people and one of the largest ethnicities in Ivory Coast. They traditionally farm in the centre of the country, in the French braid-shaped Baoule “V” between the rivers Bandama and N'Zi, around Bouaké and Yamoussoukro. The Baoulé language, whose native name is *Wawle*, is a Kwa language of the Akan branch and is spoken in central and southern Ivory Coast, including the regions of Lacs, Lagunes, Gôh-Djiboua, Sassandra-Marahoué, Vallée du Bandama, Woroba, and Yamoussoukro. The language article gives an approximate population of 5.3 million speakers. Baoulé folk culture includes farming, hand-woven textiles, carved wood, masks, figures, goldwork, music, dance, naming practices, and a religious system connecting human, earthly, and spiritual worlds.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Pagne** — The sources identify traditional *pagne* as also known as Kente cloth, but do not describe its specific structure or patterns.

**Hand-woven textiles** — The Baoulé are described as well known for unique hand-woven textiles. The village of Sakiaré is described as having 95% skilled hand-weavers.

**Woman’s wrapper or shawl** — Museum records describe wrappers or shawls made from twelve or eighteen narrow strips hand sewn together. Their materials include hand-spun or machine-spun cotton, indigo-dyed cotton, white cotton ikat, plain white cotton, orange- or red-dyed stripes, supplementary weft float patterns, twined white cotton threads, tassels, and short warp-thread fringes.

**Ikat** — The museum records specifically describe white cotton ikat and ikat in white and orange on indigo. The records do not give a Baoulé vernacular name for the textiles.

**Loom weaving** — The shuttle places the weft yarn between the warp yarns, while the beater pulls the inserted weft forward to secure the fabric. Museum records also identify heddle-pulleys as working elements of a narrow-band loom.

**Heddle-pulley** — Heddle-pulleys are carved wooden loom objects. Recorded examples bear opposing faces, bearded heads, an antelope head, or a top-knot; one Cleveland record identifies a miniature face mask on a pulley.

**Supplementary weft float patterns** — This technique is recorded on the wrappers and shawls, where coloured cotton produces additional surface decoration.

**Motif vocabulary.** Lizard, tortoise, geometric decoration, triangles with angled lines, plant motif, concentric semi-circles, antelope head, bearded head, human head.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe women’s wrappers or shawls made from sewn strips of cotton decorated with ikat, supplementary weft float patterns, twined threads, tassels, and fringes. A museum record describes a hunter’s cap made of coarse dark brown cotton with a light brown cotton edge, rounded top, triangular flaps, geometric chain-stitch decoration, twisted leather thong decoration, and appliqué. The cap carries 41 pairs of leopard (?) claw charms and five sealed leather pouches described as charms. Museum records also describe a masquerade costume consisting of a polychrome wooden mask, raffia [?] skirt, wrist and ankle bands, and rattles around the ankles. The sources do not describe ordinary footwear, belts, or a separate everyday-versus-ceremonial clothing system.

### Architecture

The sources do not cover Baoulé house forms, village architecture, roofing, building materials, courtyards, or named architectural traditions. They mention a carved wooden door with two panels, the upper panel showing a lizard and the lower panel a tortoise, but provide no architectural context for it.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources do not describe Baoulé ceramics. Wooden everyday and status objects in the museum records include carved doors, stools, cosmetic boxes, fly-whisks, heddle-pulleys, drums, bells, musical instruments, masks, figures, and a hunter’s cap. A stool is carved from one block of wood, with a rectangular curved seat, rectangular base, four circles, and a central pillar. Gold objects include a lost-wax cast pendant and beads; bronze objects include women’s anklets. The sources also mention the machete, used for clearing vegetation and making paddles or canoes from logs, and the snail shell, used for grounding and pounding tobacco and making snuffs.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Museum records describe gold beads, a gold-plated hair-pin, gold-plated spectacles, bronze women’s anklets, and a lost-wax cast gold pendant called *srala/boloa atre*. The pendant is a bi-facial rectangle decorated with alternating triangles, angled lines, and openwork circles. One bead is shaped like a musical horn or leopard tooth; another is elongated and clove-shaped. Recorded figures wear bead necklaces, white bead anklets, bone bead necklaces, and anklets of small bone beads. The sources do not describe tattooing, henna, or hair practices as general customs, although one figure has hair carved into side plaits and another has hair dressed with horns.

## Music & performance

The sources identify drums, a bell, and wooden musical instruments among Baoulé museum objects. One bell is an iron instrument with a separate striker linked by string and a wooden end carved as an antelope’s head. Another wooden musical instrument is shaped as a woman’s head with an elongated neck. A Museum of World Culture record identifies a drum from Toumodi, Côte d’Ivoire, associated with the Baoulé; the record gives a diameter of 21 centimetres and a height of 42 centimetres.

The sources do not provide names for Baoulé song genres, instrumental repertoires, or musical scales. They connect masks with dance types including *goli glin*, *kple kple*, *gbagba* or *mblo*, and *bo nu amuin*. The *gbagba* or *mblo* mask is used in entertainment-based performances consisting of a dance sequence. The *bo nun amuin* mask is danced in times of trouble to protect the village and at important men’s funerals.

## Dance & theatre

Baoulé masks are associated with several named dances. The *goli glin* mask belongs to the goli performance. Its appearance resembles an animal, with horns and a boxy, downward-facing muzzle; vertical bars of varying colours appear around the eyes. At night, women are barred from witnessing the dance and take shelter in their homes, while during the day anyone may watch.

The *kple kple*, also called *kouassi gbe*, is related to the goli masks but is described as mundane. These masks are often worn by children for entertainment and are used in playful and frivolous dancing. They are generally circular or elliptical, with a solid red or black colour, rounding horns, eyes, and a rectangular opening.

The men’s sacred *bo nun amuin* mask is a boxy helmet mask representing a menacing animal with bared teeth. Viewing it is restricted to men; a woman or child who sees it risks serious injury or death. The *gbagba* or *mblo* mask is worn with cloth drapery covering the dancer and is used in entertainment-based performances.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources do not provide an annual Baoulé festival calendar or named seasonal festivals. They describe several ritual contexts. The *bo nun amuin* mask is danced in times of trouble to protect the village and at important men’s funerals. Its dance assists the recent passing of the dead in becoming spirits capable of protecting following generations.

Baoulé religious belief is described through three realities: the Domain of God, *Niamien*; the earthly world of human beings, animals, plants, and supernatural beings living in mountains, rocks, rivers, and forests; and the beyond, *blolo*, where ancestral spirits reside. *Blolo bla*, meaning blolo woman, and *blolo bian*, also called *blolo yasua*, meaning blolo man, are private sculptures representing an individual’s spirit spouse. People make offerings of food and money to these figures because the spirit spouse can influence earthly relationships and personal endeavours.

The sources also describe naming practices. Names can reflect the day of the week or circumstances of birth. The third child in a row is named *I'nsan*, the ninth *N'Goran*, the tenth *Brou*, the eleventh from the same mother *Loukou*, and the twelfth *N'Gbin*.

## Foodways

The staple food of the Ivorian diet is yam. It is boiled, cooled, and mashed. Cassava is also integral to Baoulé cuisine. Yams and maize are stored until needed. Other foods are obtained from local markets, where fish is described as especially important and is wrapped in palm leaves.

The sources state that Ivorians typically eat goat meat, sheep meat, lamb, and chicken, shared by the entire community. Goats are milked and chickens provide eggs. Firewood and yams are transported to local markets, where they are sold with other goods. The sources do not give names for particular Baoulé dishes, cooking vessels, ceremonial foods, tea or coffee traditions, or dietary rules.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources do not describe Baoulé folktales, epic poetry, proverbs, riddles, named storytellers, or contemporary literary preservation projects. They do record the transmission of religious concepts through sculpture, dance, naming, and the interpretation of spiritual beings.

The Baoulé language article records that portions of the Bible translated into Baoulé were first published in 1946, that the full New Testament followed in 1953, and that the complete Bible was first published in 1998 by the Bible Society in Abidjan.

## Language & religion

Baoulé, whose native name is *Wawle*, is an Akan language of the Kwa family and Akan branch. It forms a dialect continuum with Anyin and is closely related to Nzema and Sehwi. The language is spoken in central and southern Ivory Coast, including Lacs, Lagunes, Gôh-Djiboua, Sassandra-Marahoué, Vallée du Bandama, Woroba, and Yamoussoukro. Baoulé has five tones: high, low, mid, rising, and falling. Five vowels may be nasalized.

In school, Baoulé children speak French, while at home they speak Baoulé; French study begins in grade one. The religious world includes *Niamien*, the earthly world, and *blolo*, the realm where ancestral spirits reside. Sculptures can house *zulu*, protective spirits. *Waka sran*, meaning “wooden beings,” provide *zulu* with a bridge between the physical and spirit worlds. *Bo usu* refers to forest spirits and supernatural beings with magical powers; their statues assist hunters in attracting prey.
