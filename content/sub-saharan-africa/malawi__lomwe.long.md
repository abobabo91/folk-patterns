## Overview

The Lomwe are a Bantu-speaking people of Mozambique and Malawi. Their language, Elomwe — also called Lomwe or Lowe, and known as Western Makua — is widely spoken across central Mozambique, where it belongs with Makua in a group of distinctive Bantu languages of the northern part of the country. In Malawi a mutually unintelligible form, Malawi Lomwe, is spoken; it carries elements of the Mozambican language but stands apart from it. The Lomwe have been prominent in Malawian public life: the late former president Bingu wa Mutharika and his brother Peter Mutharika, also a president of the Republic of Malawi, belong to this ethnic group. The sources used here do not give population figures, nor do they describe a specific settlement region within Malawi.

## Material culture

The museum objects in this gallery, all held by the British Museum, are the fullest record of Lomwe material culture available to this profile: edged iron tools, cotton waist-bands, smoking pipes, pipes and flutes of bamboo, seed-pod rattles and necklaces, iron and brass ornaments, a carved wooden powder flask, a wooden lime-box and a sample of barkcloth.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The catalogue records two woven cotton pieces and one non-woven cloth.

**Cotton waist-band** — A long woven band of cotton. One example has a natural cotton warp and weft with brown-dyed warp lines running along both outer sides, and a horizontal border hand-stitched to each end in an alternating brown-dyed and natural check, finished with loose warp-thread fringes. A second is a long strip of off-white cotton banded with black stripes, with a fringe at one short end.

**Barkcloth** — The collection holds a sample of barkcloth. The record says nothing further about how it was beaten, dyed or worn.

**Motif vocabulary.** Brown-dyed warp lines along the cloth edges; alternating brown and natural check in the end borders; bands of black stripes on off-white ground.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe dress only through the objects themselves, and do not distinguish men's from women's wear or everyday from ceremonial clothing. What is documented is the waist-band, woven of cotton in a long fringed strip, in both a check-bordered and a striped version; and barkcloth, present as a sample rather than as a finished garment. Ornaments that would have been worn on the body are better attested: iron and brass bangles, an iron ankle-ring, and several necklaces of seeds and palm seed-pods, one of them long enough to coil about ten times around the neck. Two objects are classed as dance ornaments — rattles of seed pods strung on cord — which indicates dress assembled specifically for dancing, though the catalogue does not describe the rest of a dancer's costume.

### Architecture

The sources used for this profile contain nothing on Lomwe house form, building materials, granaries or any other element of the built environment.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Ironwork dominates the tool record. Two axes each consist of an iron blade passed through a wooden handle. Knives appear with short wooden handles, with bamboo handles and with brass handles. There are also pincers of iron with leaf-shaped blades, and a pair of pincer knives of iron with sharpened blades.

In black earthenware and black pottery the recorded forms are all pipe bowls, described below. Woodwork includes a powder flask carved in the shape of a horn, decorated with carved designs and fitted with a long cord; and a small box of hard dark wood shaped like a water-bottle with a flat bottom, hollowed out through a circular hole in the base that was then plugged with a wooden disc. The box is banded down one side with a broad vertical zig-zag and half a matching band on the other, split at the neck, bound there with thin string, and hung with a string of light blue, pink and red beads. It served as a lime-box, holding lime to be chewed with tobacco.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Neck ornaments are made from plant material: necklaces of small dark brown seeds, of brown and black seeds long enough to form some ten coils around the neck, of brown palm seed-pods, and one of palm seed-pods strung with pink beads and finished at the ends with two buck horns. Metal ornaments are plainer in form but carefully worked. An iron bangle is simply a strip of iron bent nearly into a circle; an ankle-ring is a thin iron bar beaten to a square section and bent almost closed. A brass bangle carries a longitudinal ornament of paired hollowed semi-circles separated by a vertical raised bar, the cutting of which has left the bracelet's outline visibly wavy. Imported glass beads appear as a pink-bead necklace element and as the blue, pink and red bead string on the lime-box. The sources say nothing about tattooing, scarification or hair practice.

## Music & performance

Three classes of instrument are documented, all of bamboo or bamboo-and-seed construction.

**Pan-pipes** are the best represented. One set has five bamboo pipes ranging from 3 to 4¼ inches, lashed with two transverse bands of fibre, irregularly coated with black gum, and inlaid on one side with an irregular patch of red seeds; two fibre tags project from one side. A second five-pipe set of thin bamboo is tied side by side and thickly coated with black gum, the pipes closed at one end and set with a patch of red seeds let into the gum near the open ends, running from 4 to 5¾ inches. A third has eight pipes from 3¼ to 5¾ inches, bound with two fibre bands — the upper gummed on both faces, the lower on one only — with a loop of soft flax cord attached at the middle for suspension. The graded lengths, the black gum body and the red-seed inlay are the consistent signature of the type.

**Flute** — A single complete joint of bamboo closed at both ends, pierced with three holes, two near one end and the third near the other. Its decoration is worked in the bamboo skin itself: four compartments of diagonal zig-zag left in the natural bark against a cut-away brown ground, separated by bands of natural bark incised with herringbone and zig-zag.

**Dance rattles** of seed pods strung on cord supply percussion for dancing. The sources name no song genres, ensembles or performance occasions beyond the fact that these rattles were worn as dance ornaments.

## Dance & theatre

The only evidence in the sources is the two rattles of seed pods on cord, catalogued as dance ornaments — proof that dancers wore sounding ornaments, with no named dance, mask tradition or dramatic form recorded. The sources used here do not cover Lomwe dance or theatre otherwise.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources used for this profile describe no annual festivals and no life-cycle rites. Malawi has no UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscriptions available to this profile.

## Foodways

The sources name no staples, dishes or cooking methods. One consumption practice is documented in detail: tobacco-chewing with lime. The carved wooden lime-box, shaped like a water-bottle and hung with beads, held the lime that was chewed together with tobacco. Smoking is documented by three pipes, each combining a bamboo stem with a bowl of black earthenware or pottery, and each with a perforation for the cord that tethers bowl to stem. One has a slightly bell-shaped bowl rising at a right angle from a socket that expands towards the stem, faceted all round by deep incision into three bands of lozenge design, with transverse bands enclosing three bands of shallow incised herringbone beneath. A second has a double bowl of black pottery, the sides of each bowl ornamented with raised bands of oblique and crossed incised lines rising to a horizontal band of plain incised lines, with flat projecting rims incised along the side edges. The third has a heavy bell-shaped bowl with deeply incised lozenge pattern below the rim, and a long thick socket bearing five raised rounded transverse ridges, all perforated for a cord.

## Oral tradition & literature

The documented literary activity in Lomwe is scriptural translation. Lewis Mataka Bandawe worked with E. D. Bowman on a translation of the New Testament into Lomwe. Bandawe led the Mihecani Mission until 1928, and during that time the four gospels and Acts were published in Lomwe. The sources record no folktales, epic, proverb or riddle tradition.

## Language & religion

Elomwe is a Bantu language of Mozambique, grouped with Makua among the distinctive Bantu languages of the north of the country, and also known as Western Makua. Beyond the regional variation found within Makhuwa proper, Lomwe uses *ch* where Makhuwa orthography has *tt*: Makhuwa *mirette* ("remedy") corresponds to Lomwe *mirecce*, Makhuwa *murrutthu* ("dead body") to Lomwe *miruchu*, and Makhuwa *otthapa* ("joy") to Lomwe *ochapa*. Unusually among Bantu languages, the infinitive takes an *o-* prefix rather than the typical *ku-*: *omala* is "to finish" and *omeeela* "to share out". The Malawian form is mutually unintelligible with the Mozambican one; Maho (2009) separates Ngulu (Mihavane) as a language of its own, close to Malawi Lomwe. On religion the sources record only the mission translation work noted above.
