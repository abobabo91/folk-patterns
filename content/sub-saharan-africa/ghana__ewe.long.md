## Overview

The Ewe are a Gbe-speaking ethnic group located primarily in the coastal regions of West Africa, south and east of the Volta River to around the Mono River at the border of Togo and Benin, and in southwestern Nigeria near the Atlantic Ocean. They are primarily found in Ghana’s Volta Region and Eastern Region and in southern Togo. The largest population is in Ghana, with 6.0 million people, and the second largest is in Togo, with 3.1 million. They speak Ewe, a language of the Gbe family, within Niger-Congo. Their culture includes strip weaving, complex drum-based music, dances associated with war, hunting, funerals and social occasions, and a traditional religion called Vodun. Ewe textile production is distinguished by narrow woven strips, extensive colour and pattern variation, and the autonomy of weavers from centralised royal control.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Strip weaving** — Strip weaving uses very small looms to produce long, narrow lengths of cloth. The strips can be assembled into robes, blankets, rugs and ceremonial decorations, and the portable looms suited nomadic and village families.

**Ewe kente** — Museum records describe men’s wrappers composed of narrow strips hand sewn together. One example has repeating warp stripes in green, red, white and indigo, with supplementary weft-float patterns and multi-coloured pattern bands; another uses indigo-dyed cotton with supplementary red cotton warp threads and geometric and figurative patterns in multi-coloured cotton.

**Keta cloth** — Museum records identify several wrappers as Keta cloth. These are formed from hand-woven cotton strips sewn together along the selvages, often using industrial European thread in colours including white, blue, red, green and yellow. They combine alternating warp patterns with weft-faced blocks and varying weft-float designs.

**Anlo Ewe wrapper** — One museum record identifies a woman’s wrapper as Anlo Ewe. It is made from imported industrial cotton and twelve narrow strips machine-stitched together, with alternating blue and red stripes, a broad orange stripe, variegated weft-faced blocks and turquoise bands.

**Woman’s wrapper** — Museum examples include wrappers made from six wide strips or sixteen narrow strips, with hand-sewn construction, indigo-dyed cotton, red, white and yellow yarn, supplementary weft motifs, soumak braiding and loose warp fringes.

**Man’s wrapper** — Men’s wrappers in the museum records use alternating warp- and weft-faced strips, indigo-dyed cotton, red-dyed cotton, white, yellow and blue stripes, and hand-sewn hems. One man’s cloth was formed from imported European yarn and altered into a sleeveless tunic by folding the cloth and sewing its sides.

**Ewe cloth** — A recorded man’s wrap-around garment is made from twenty-one narrow strips of cotton hand sewn together in black cotton. Its strips include black, blue, white, red, green, yellow and cream-dyed cotton, with tweed-effect stripes produced by twisting yellow and blue threads.

**Motif vocabulary.** Hands, animals, fish and combs; geometric and figurative patterns; supplementary weft-float motifs; stripes, blocks and bands.

### Clothing & dress

The sources describe wrappers, cloths and wrap-around garments for men and women. Women’s wrappers include narrow strips of imported industrial cotton, hand-woven or machine-stitched strips, and weft-faced blocks in colours such as orange, yellow, turquoise, indigo, red and white. Men’s wrappers include Ewe kente and other strip-woven cloths with hand-sewn hems, supplementary weft patterns and fringes. A recorded man’s cloth was folded and sewn at the sides to form a crude sleeveless tunic, leaving openings for the head and arms. A photographic print shows an adult male wearing a cloth, shirt and wrist-ornament while holding a staff. The sources do not describe Ewe head coverings, belts, footwear, or a separate system of ceremonial dress.

## Music & performance

Ewe music is associated with Ghana, Togo and Benin and is primarily percussive, with considerable metrical complexity. It includes dance music performed by a drum orchestra, as well as work, play and other songs. The Ewe musical tradition is closely integrated with traditional religion, and Ewes believe that a good drummer has inherited the spirit of an ancestor who was a good drummer.

Cross-rhythm is central to Ewe drumming. The most fundamental cross-rhythm is three-against-two, or 3:2, also described as six-against-four, 6:4, or a vertical hemiola. Another documented pattern is 3:8, used in the Ewe rhythm *kadodo*. Ewe musical descriptions also emphasise repeated structures, call and response, additive melodies, fixed time-backgrounds and rests that form part of the musical structure.

The *atoke*, *gankogui* and *axatse* provide the rhythmic foundation. The atoke is a high-pitched gong played with an iron rod. The gankogui is a clapperless double bell played with a stick, and its most common key pattern is the twelve-pulse basic Ewe or standard pattern. The axatse is a beaded gourd rattle struck against the knee and with the free hand. The drum ensemble includes the *atsimevu*, *sogo*, *kidi* and *kagan*. The kagan is the “child” or “baby brother” drum, the kidi is the “mother” drum, the sogo is the “father” drum, and the atsimevu is the “grandfather” drum and leader of the ensemble. Other musical elements include voice, hands, flutes and drums.

Named genres include *Agbekor*, associated with songs and music around war, and *Tro-u*, ancestral drum music performed at sacred occasions at a shrine. *Sowu* is one of seven styles of drumming associated with the cult of Yewe and adapted for the stage.

## Dance & theatre

Ewe dances vary according to geographical region and other factors. *Adevu* is a professional hunting dance intended to make animals easier to hunt and to give an animal a ritual funeral so that its spirit will not return to harm the hunter.

*Agbadza* was traditionally a war dance but is now used in social and recreational contexts to celebrate peace. War dances could also serve as military training, with signals from the lead drum directing warriors to move ahead, to the right or down. *Atsiagbekor* is a contemporary version of the Ewe war dance *Atamga*, whose name refers to oaths taken before battle. It is performed in platoon formation and is now used for entertainment at social gatherings and cultural presentations.

The *Atsia* dance is performed mostly by women. Its stylistic movements are directed by the lead drummer, and each movement has a prescribed rhythmic pattern. The name means “style or display” in Ewe. *Bobobo*, originally called *Akpese*, developed from Wusuta and Highlife music. It became nationally recognised in the 1950s and 1960s and is performed at funerals and other social occasions. The men generally sing and dance in the centre while women dance in a ring around them.

*Agahu* is both a dance and a secular music association. In its performance, two circles are formed, with the men stationary and the women progressing around the circle. *Gbedzimido* is a war dance associated with Mafi-Gborkofe and Amegakope and is performed at the Asafotu festival and at funerals of highly placed people. *Gota*, now used for social entertainment, is known for synchronised stops by drummers and dancers.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources describe dances and music connected with war, hunting, funerals, shrines, social gatherings and cultural presentations. *Gbedzimido* is performed at the Asafotu festival, which is celebrated annually by the Tongu people around December. It is also performed at funerals of highly placed people, mostly men.

*Tro-u* is ancestral drum music used to invite ancestors to special sacred occasions at a shrine. A priest or priestess may be present, and a religious leader can call fast or slow rhythms to facilitate communication with the spirit world. In the northern region, the bell rhythm is played on a boat-shaped bell, while the southern region uses a double bell.

The traditional Ewe religion is called Vodun. It holds Mawu as the creator God, who created lesser deities called *trɔwo*. The Ewe concept of *Si* describes a “spiritual marriage” between a deity and a faithful person. Ancestral spirits are important in the traditional religion and are shared by a clan. The cult of Yewe is associated with sacred music, and Yewe is described as the God of Thunder and lightning among Ewe-speaking people of Togo, Benin and the south-eastern parts of the Volta Region.

The sources describe funerals, naming ceremonies and religious occasions, but do not provide a complete annual festival calendar or detailed accounts of coming-of-age or wedding rites. The Ewe language source states that a final name is given at a naming ceremony seven days after birth.

## Foodways

The Ewe people flourished historically in cotton and rice farming, palm oil and copra production and exports. The sources therefore document rice, palm oil and copra as economic products, but they do not describe named dishes, cooking methods, staple-food combinations, ceremonial foods, beverages, dietary rules, or table customs.

## Oral tradition & literature

Ewe oral tradition recalls migration from Ketu or Amedzofe, identified in the source as a town now in the republic of Benin near the border between Benin and Nigeria. It also describes the brutal king Agɔ Akɔli, or Agor Akorli, of Notsie, whose rule is associated with the escape and dispersal of the Ewe.

Ewe proverbs transmit generational wisdom and express the importance of lifelong education. One traditional proverb states: “knowledge is like the Baobab Tree and no one can wrap the hands fully around it.” Storytellers use a dialect of Aŋlɔ spoken in Seva. The sources identify *The Tragedy of Agbezuge* (*Amegbetɔa alo Agbezuge ƒe Ŋutinya*) by Sam J. Obianim as the first novel written in Ewe. *The Fifth landing stage* by Ferdinand Kwasi Fiawoo is described as a notable play depicting part of the history of the Ewe people.

## Language & religion

Ewe, also written Evhe or Eʋe, is a Gbe language spoken mainly in Ghana and Togo. It belongs to the Gbe family of Niger-Congo. The language has coastal or southern dialects, including Aŋlɔ, Tɔŋu, Avenor, Dzodze and Watsyi, and inland varieties characterised as Ewedomegbe, including Ho, Kpando, Peki, Danyi and Kpele. Dialects vary between nearby villages, while speakers from different localities understand one another. Ewe is tonal and uses the African Reference Alphabet in writing. It is a national language in Togo and a government-backed regional language in Ghana.

The traditional religion is Vodun, in which Mawu is the creator God and lesser deities influence human destiny. Christianity arrived with colonial merchants and missionaries; major missions were established after 1840, German Lutheran missionaries arrived in 1847, and about 89% of the Ewe population has converted to Christianity. The sources state that many continue to practise traditional rites and rituals.
