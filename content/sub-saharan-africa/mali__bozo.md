## Overview

The Bozo are a Mande ethnic group located predominantly along the Niger River in Mali, especially in the Inner Niger Delta. They are famous for fishing and are occasionally called the “masters of the river.” The Bozo founded the Malian cities of Djenné and Mopti and took possession of the Niger’s banks under the 10th century Ghana Empire. A 2000 census counted 132,100 Bozo in Mali. Their language belongs to the northwestern branch of the Mande languages and is considered a dialect cluster with four recognized languages. The Bozo are predominantly Muslim, having begun to convert around the 14th century, while preserving a number of animist traditions. Their animal totem is the bull: its body represents the Niger and its horns represent Bozo fishing pirogues.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The name Bozo is thought to derive from the Bambara expression *bɔ-so*, meaning “bamboo house,” or, in another rendering, “house of straw.”

## Festivals & rituals

The Bozo are predominantly Muslim and began converting to Islam around the 14th century; all were Muslim by the 19th century according to the source. They nevertheless preserve a number of animist traditions. The bull functions as their animal totem: its body represents the Niger and its horns represent Bozo fishing pirogues.

## Language & religion

Bozo is a Mande language spoken by the Bozo people of the Inner Niger Delta in Mali. It belongs to the northwestern branch of the Mande languages, and its closest linguistic relative is Soninke. The Bozo cluster is divided into Hainyaxo, Tiɛma Cɛwɛ, Tiéyaxo, and Sorogaama. Hainyaxo is spoken by the Hain, while Tiéyaxo is also spoken in Burkina Faso and Sorogaama in Mali, Nigeria, and Ivory Coast. The Bozo often speak regional languages such as Bambara, Fula, or Western Songhay. Bozo is tonal, with three lexical tones, and is written with conventions for long vowels and nasalization. The Bozo are predominantly Muslim, but animist traditions remain part of their cultural life.
