## Overview

The Wolof are a Senegambian people of the West Atlantic branch of the Niger–Congo family, concentrated in northwestern Senegal between the Senegal River and the Gambia River — the old kingdom lands of Waalo, Kajoor, Bawol, Jolof, Saloum and Dimar — plus the Cap-Vert peninsula around Dakar, northwestern Gambia (where they dominate Banjul), and coastal Mauritania. They number over six million across the three countries and form Senegal's largest group at roughly 39.7 percent; Wolof, unusually for sub-Saharan Africa, is not tonal and functions as Senegal's lingua franca far beyond the ethnic group itself. In folk-culture terms the Wolof matter twice over: as heirs to the Jolof Empire's caste-based craft economy (c. 1350–1890), in which smithing, weaving, leatherwork and gold jewelry were hereditary occupations of the *ñeeño* artisan castes, and as the creators of Dakar's twentieth-century urban idioms — *sabar* drumming, *sous-verre* glass painting, and the couture *boubou* — which now stand for Senegalese culture at large.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Pagne tissé (*sër*, *ndëkët*)** — Narrow-strip cloth woven on the double-heddle horizontal treadle loom by men of the weaver caste (*rabb*), historically the domain of slave-descended and *ñeeño* lineages. Strips 10–20 cm wide are cut and edge-sewn into wrappers and blankets; the Wolof preference is for bold weft-face bands in indigo, white and red rather than the dense supplementary-weft figuring of Mande *kente*-adjacent traditions to the south.

**Indigo-dyed cloth (*gara*)** — Dyeing is women's work, using fermented indigo vats and stitch- or raffia-resist to produce circular and lozenge reserves. The distinguishing Senegalese finish is *gara* beating: the dried cloth is pounded with wooden mallets and often dressed with gum arabic to a hard, near-metallic sheen, producing a lustre absent from Yoruba *adire* or Malian *bogolan*.

**Batik and wax-resist pagnes** — A twentieth-century Dakar and Saint-Louis workshop industry layering hot-wax resist and chemical dyes on factory cotton, sold as two-metre *pagnes*. Wolof workshop batik favours large-scale calligraphic and figurative panels — marabout portraits, fish, hands — over the small repeat-field of Javanese practice from which the technique was borrowed.

**Boubou (*mbubb*, women's *ndoket*)** — The great flowing robe, cut from strip-cloth or from imported damask (*basin riche*), is itself a pattern-bearing object: the neck opening, chest panel and hems carry dense machine or hand embroidery (*bërëndal*) in silk floss, worked in interlace and arabesque registers by male embroiderers.

**Sous-verre (glass painting)** — Reverse painting on window glass, a Wolof urban art since the 1930s, descended from imported Mecca pilgrimage prints. Subjects are Mouride and Tijānī marabouts (above all Cheikh Amadou Bamba), market women, wrestlers and taxis, the figures outlined first and the ground filled last.

**Wolof beadwork (*bin-bin*, *féer*)** — Strung waist-beads worn under clothing by women, in glass, amber and coral, ordered in colour sequences that read as erotic and marital signals; also beaded amulet sheaths and hair ornaments.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Tiir* — narrow warp stripe bands; lozenge/diamond reserves from stitch-resist indigo; checkerboard strip alternation; arabesque interlace of *bërëndal* neck embroidery; Arabic calligraphic panels and *jawhar* squares from talismanic practice; concentric circle resist (tie-dye "eye"); cowrie form; comb and fishbone tooth rows; crescent and star; stylised hand (protective).

### Clothing & dress

Men wear the *mbubb* or *grand boubou*, a wide sleeveless-looking robe over drawstring trousers (*tubéy*) and a long undershirt (*caaya*), the ensemble known as a *xaftan* when tailored close. Headgear runs from the embroidered cap (*kufi*, *mbaxana*) to the tall wound turban (*kala*) of marabouts and Baye Fall, whose patchwork robes are a deliberate badge of mendicant discipline. Women wrap the *sër* (pagne) at the waist over a chemise, with a matching bodice (*taille-basse*) and headwrap (*musoor*), tied in high architectural forms in Dakar and Saint-Louis; over these goes the *ndoket*, the vast embroidered boubou of *basin* damask, starched and beaten to a gloss for ceremonies. Under the wrapper sit the *bin-bin* waist-beads and a perfumed cloth belt (*pëtaw*, *thiouraye*-scented). Leather slippers (*tëgg*-made sandals, embroidered *babouches*) are worn by both sexes; gold is loaded on at naming ceremonies and weddings. The *signare* costume of nineteenth-century Saint-Louis — layered skirts and elaborate headtie — survives in festival dress.

### Architecture

The Wolof are still roughly three-quarters rural, living in villages (*dëkk*) built as clusters of compounds (*kër*), sometimes scattered without a centre, more often ringed around a plaza with the mosque at its middle. A compound holds several huts (*neeg*) — round or square — with walls of mud and millet-stalk armature, and conical thatched roofs of millet straw or grass laid over a timber ring-beam; the whole compound is fenced with a hedge or palisade of reeds and millet stalks, and one joint kitchen serves the polygynous household until internal disputes split it into separate hearths. The senior man's hut faces the entrance; granaries (raised millet stores) stand within the yard. In the old river towns — Saint-Louis, Gorée, Rufisque — a creole urban type prevails: two-storey coral-stone and lime houses with wooden galleries, shutters and interior courtyards, built for the *signare* households and now the core of Saint-Louis's listed quarter. Mouride Touba is the modern architectural statement: the Great Mosque with its minaret *Lamp Fall* organises a purpose-built holy city.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery is made by women of the potter caste, hand-built by coiling and pinching without a wheel and open-fired: water jars (*ndaa*) whose porous walls cool by evaporation, cooking pots, incense burners for *thiouraye*, and the couscous steamer. Smiths (*tëgg*) of the *ñeeño* castes forge hoes, the long-handled *iler* weeding blade, adzes, knives and the iron staffs of ritual practitioners; a separate branch of the same caste works precious metal. Woodworkers turn mortars and pestles for millet, the great pounding mortar, low stools, combs, and the carved *sabar* and *tama* drum shells. Leatherworkers (*uude*) — ranked lowest of the *ñeeño* because hides were held polluting — produce scabbards, cushions, sandals, horse trappings and above all the stitched envelopes of *gris-gris* amulets. Basketry and coiled-grass food covers, calabash bowls and ladles complete the household inventory.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Wolof goldsmithing (*orfèvrerie*) is the signature craft: filigree (*filigrane*) worked in drawn gold wire and granulation into large hollow bicone and globular beads, crescent pendants, and the massive hoop and pendant earrings whose weight is carried by a cord over the head. Ceremonial suites combine gold, imported amber beads, coral and Venetian glass; silver is worked for anklets and rings. The *gris-gris* or *téere* — a Qur'anic verse or *jawhar* magic square folded into leather or silver — is worn on the upper arm, waist or neck by men, women and children alike, prescribed by a marabout and the most widespread item of Wolof adornment. Women wear *bin-bin* waist-beads, hennaed hands and feet (*fudden*) for weddings, and elaborate threaded and braided coiffures with added fibre and gold ornament; teeth-blackening and lip tattooing (*xas*) were practised as beauty marks.

## Music & performance

Wolof music is drum music first. The *sabar* is both an instrument and an ensemble: a set of carved, single-headed goatskin drums — *nder* (the tall lead), *mbëng-mbëng*, *col*, *talmbat*, *tungune*, *lamb* — played with one bare hand and one thin stick (*galan*), producing the dense interlocking bàkk phrases that are the core of Senegalese rhythm. Drummers are *gëwël* (griots), the hereditary caste that also supplies praise-singers and genealogists. The *tama* is a small tension-laced talking drum tucked under the arm, its pitch bent to imitate speech; the *junjung* or *jung-jung* drums belong to royal and warrior contexts. Melodic instruments include the *xalam* (plucked lute, ancestor of the banjo) and the *riti* single-string bowed fiddle. Griot vocal art — *woy* (song), *taasu* (rhythmic sung-spoken praise and insult, a direct ancestor of rap) — is performed at naming ceremonies (*ngénte*), weddings, and the wrestling arena. *Bàkk* are named rhythmic phrases, each encoding a verbal formula. Religious repertoire is equally central: Mouride *xassaïda*, the chanted Arabic poems of Cheikh Amadou Bamba, and Tijānī *zikr*. The urban *mbalax* style of Youssou N'Dour and Thione Seck put the sabar ensemble under electric bands from the 1970s.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is inseparable from *sabar*: at a neighbourhood *tànnebéer* — a women's dance gathering held in a street circle — dancers enter one at a time to answer a drummer's *bàkk* with short, explosive solos of leaps, thrown legs and rapid pelvic articulation. Named forms include *ventilateur*, *ceebu jën* and *farta*, choreographies that circulate and date like popular songs. *Lëmbël* is the slow, close-hipped dance performed for women at weddings. The **lamb** — Senegalese wrestling — is a full performance genre in its own right, where the wrestler's pre-bout *bàkk*, amulet-laden costume and circling display are as watched as the fall itself. Griot performance blends song, mime and satire rather than staged drama; masking is not a Wolof institution as it is among the Bassari, Diola or neighbouring Mande, and objects catalogued as "Wolof masks" generally belong to those traditions or to a modern art market. Contemporary theatre in Wolof — Daniel Sorano company plays, and the *kotéba*-influenced popular troupes broadcast on television — carries the satirical griot voice into a scripted form.

## Festivals & rituals

The calendar is Islamic. *Korité* (Eid al-Fitr) and *Tabaski* (Eid al-Adha, with the ram slaughter that dominates household spending) are the year's great feasts; *Tamxarit* (Islamic New Year, ʿĀshūrāʾ) is marked by a night of couscous with meat, cross-dressing and children's door-to-door singing (*tajaboon*). *Gàmmu* (Mawlid, at Tivaouane for the Tijāniyya) and the **Màggal of Touba** — the Mouride pilgrimage commemorating Cheikh Amadou Bamba's 1895 exile, drawing millions to Touba on 18 Safar — are the two largest gatherings in Senegal and the defining ritual events of Wolof religious life. Life-cycle rites: the *ngénte* naming ceremony on the eighth day after birth, when the child's name is whispered at the ear, a ram or goat is killed, the baby's head shaved, and griots are paid to sing lineage; male circumcision (*njuli*), historically a seclusion of initiates in a bush camp with its own songs and a *njuli* return procession; marriage (*takk*) contracted between families with brideprice (*warugar*), the bride escorted at night to her husband's compound, and preferred bilateral cross-cousin matches; and funerals, where burial follows within a day and the *dénkaané* and later memorial feasts (*xéwal*) redistribute food and cloth. Marabout consultation, the prescribing of *téere*, and libation-like offerings to *rab* and *jinné* spirits — in particular the *ndëpp* healing ceremony borrowed from Lebu practice, with drumming and animal sacrifice to appease a possessing spirit — persist inside a Sunni framework.

## Foodways

Millet (*dugub*) is the historic staple, pounded in wooden mortars and cooked as *cere* (steamed couscous) or as porridges: *laax* (millet porridge with soured milk and sweet baobab or peanut sauce), *ruy*, *fondé*. Rice took over in the twentieth century, above all as *ceebu jën* — "rice and fish," Senegal's national dish, a one-pot of broken rice cooked in a tomato-and-fish stock with stuffed *thiof* (white grouper), cassava, carrot, cabbage and bitter tomato (*ñoxoos*), served red (*ceebu jën bu xonk*) or white. Other mainstays: *yassa* (onion-and-lemon marinated chicken or fish), *maafe* (peanut-butter stew, the *tigadèguèna* family), *thiou* (tomato stew), *soupe kandia* (okra and palm oil), *ndambe* (black-eyed pea stew), and *caldou* in the river region. *Bissap* (hibiscus), *bouye* (baobab fruit), *ditax* and ginger juices accompany meals; *attaya*, the three-round ceremony of gunpowder green tea poured foaming into small glasses with heavy sugar and mint, structures male sociability. Eating is communal from one bowl, right hand only. Beef is rare and ceremonial; the Tabaski ram and the *ngénte* goat are the ritual meats; pork and alcohol are avoided under *halal* rules, and Wolof farmers' chickens, goats, and dried or smoked fish (*guedj*, *yeet* — fermented sea snail — and *tambadiang*) supply the umami base of most sauces.

## Oral tradition & literature

Wolof oral literature is carried by the *gëwël* caste, whose repertoire runs from royal genealogy and the epic of the Jolof kings — the Ndiadiane Ndiaye foundation legend, in which a stranger rises from a lake to settle a dispute between two villages and becomes the first *Buur-ba Jolof* — through the praise-epics of Lat Dior Ngoné Latyr Diop and the queens Ndaté Yalla Mbodj and Njembot Mbodj of Waalo. The folktale corpus (*léeb*) turns on the trickster hare *Leuk-le-lièvre*, the hyena *Bouki* as greedy dupe, and *Kumba am ndeey* / *Kumba amul ndeey* — the girl with a mother and the orphan girl — a step-mother tale collected in dozens of versions. Sung-spoken *taasu*, proverbs (*léebu*) and riddles (*cax*) are performed at *ngénte* and *tànnebéer*. Birago Diop's *Les Contes d'Amadou Koumba* (1947) fixed the corpus in French; Cheikh Aliou Ndao, Boubacar Boris Diop (*Doomi Golo*, 2003) and Mame Younousse Dieng write novels in Wolof itself, and the *Ajami* Wolofal tradition — Wolof written in Arabic script, used heavily for Mouride devotional verse — is the group's own literate register.

## Language & religion

Wolof belongs to the Senegambian branch of Niger–Congo, alongside Serer and Fula, and is atypically non-tonal; it has noun-class concord marked by consonant-initial determiners. Dialects divide between Waalo, Kajoor, Bawol, Jolof, Lebu and Gambian varieties, with "Dakar-Wolof" an urban register saturated with French and Arabic loans. It is written both in a Latin orthography (official since 1971) and in Arabic script as *Wolofal* / *Ajami*, the vehicle of Mouride devotional poetry; Assane Faye's and Cheikh Anta Diop-era script experiments never displaced these. Virtually all Wolof are Sunni Muslims of the Mālikī school, organised into Sufi brotherhoods — the **Mouridiyya** founded by Cheikh Amadou Bamba with its centre at Touba and its Baye Fall working disciples, and the **Tijāniyya** centred on Tivaouane and Kaolack — whose marabouts hold decisive religious, economic and electoral weight. Pre-Islamic belief is undocumented in written or oral form, but survives absorbed into practice: *jinné* and *rab* spirits, the *ndëpp* possession-healing rite, *téere* amulets, and divination.
