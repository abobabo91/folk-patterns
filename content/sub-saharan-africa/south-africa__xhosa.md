## Overview

The Xhosa are the second largest ethnic group in South Africa, with over ten million speakers concentrated in the Eastern Cape. Their culture centers on cattle, ancestors, and initiation rites that survive in modern urban life. Beadwork, ochred blanket dress, and a ritual vocabulary of cattle and ancestors define their folk culture. Archaeological evidence places Xhosa-speakers in the Eastern Cape since at least the seventh century.

## Material culture

Xhosa material culture is built on four named crafts: pottery, woodwork, weaving and beadwork, with beads serving as the primary information system encoding age, gender, status, and spiritual state.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The defining Xhosa cloth is umbhaco, a heavy white or cream cotton skirt-and-wrap material ochred and built up in horizontal registers of black braid, buttons and beaded panels.

- **Ochred cloth and skirt** (*umbhaco / isikhakha*) — Heavy white or cream cotton material ochred to varying intensity and built up in horizontal registers of black braid, buttons and beaded panels, with pattern sewn on rather than woven in.
- **White and black men's blanket** (*ingcawa*) — White-and-black blanket worn draped over the shoulder by men, embellished through beading worked directly onto the cloth.
- **Breast-cloth** (*incebetha*) — Small blanket worn as a breast-cloth, pinned in place and finished with beads.
- **Headcloth** (*iqhiya*) — Fitted to the head and covered with beadwork, worn by every woman as a mark of respect to the head of the household.
- **Cloth doll** (*Xhosa doll*) — Cloth-and-bead figures dressed in miniature umbhaco, headcloth and collar, made as children's objects and as fertility and teaching pieces.

### Clothing & dress

Dress encodes social standing and stage of life, with unmarried women wearing wraps tied at the shoulder and married women wearing the full ensemble of isikhakha, breast-cloth, headcloth and waist blanket.

- **Goatskin tobacco bag** (*goatskin bag*) — Skinned off in one piece, cured with the hair on and turned inside out, carries tobacco and knife.
- **Short collar necklace** (*isichebe*) — Short collar worn by men as graded neckwear.
- **Long multicoloured necklace** (*isidanga*) — Long multicoloured strand worn by men as graded neckwear.
- **Beaded pubic apron** (*inkciyo*) — Mpondo beaded apron worn at virginity-testing ceremonies, turquoise and white.
- **Long-stemmed pipe** (*umbheka phesheya*) — Pipe meaning 'look across the sea', beaded in the same technique as other dress elements.

### Architecture

The Xhosa homestead is organised around the cattle byre, the physical and jural centre where marriage negotiators are received.

- **Homestead** (*umzi*) — The Xhosa homestead organised around the cattle byre as the physical and jural centre.
- **Cattle byre** (*inkundla*) — The physical and jural centre of the household where marriage negotiators are received.
- **Main bedroom** (*indlu enkulu*) — Emptied of furniture at a death so that extended family can be seated on the floor to pay last respects.
- **Thatched house** (*rondavel*) — Cylindrical dwelling with conical thatch and wattle-and-daub walls, with interiors lime-washed and painted in broad bands around door and window openings.
- **Grass floor mat** (*ukhukho*) — Mat used to cover the floor and for mourners to sit on during death rituals.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The four named Xhosa crafts are pottery, woodwork, weaving and beadwork, producing unglazed vessels, milk pails, baskets and beaded items.

- **Beer pot** (*beer pot for umqombothi*) — Unglazed burnished pottery vessel that is a household ritual object since beer is poured on the ground of the bride's homestead.
- **War club and status stick** (*iwisa*) — Knobkerrie carried as weapon and status stick.
- **Spear** (*assegai*) — Spear brought south by the migrating Nguni.
- **Snuff spoon** — Bone, horn or metal spoon, often worn in the hair or on a beaded cord.
- **Beer strainer basket** (*grass and palm coiling*) — Lidded baskets and beer strainers made from grass and palm coiling.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Little is recorded.

- **Ostrich-eggshell beads** — Discs chipped, bored, polished and strung on sinew, obtained from the San by trade before glass beads arrived.
- **Beaded veil for trance** (*amageza*) — Beaded veil swayed before a person's eyes to induce trance, part of diviner regalia.
- **Adolescence whistle** (*impempe*) — Whistle strung on a necklace marking entry into adolescence.
- **Beaded horn** — Part of diviner regalia, used in ritual practice.
- **Wire-wound arm and leg rings** (*wire coils*) — Brass and wire rings worn on arms and legs as metalwork adornment.

## Music & performance

Xhosa music centres on group singing with handclapping and call-and-response entries, with women as principal composers and players of mouth-resonated musical bows.

- **Gourd bow** (*uhadi*) — Mouth-resonated musical bow struck with a light stick.
- **Mouth bow** (*umrhubhe*) — Mouth bow bowed and played with the lips selecting harmonics above a fundamental.
- **Wedding song about the knocking beetle** (*Qongqothwane*) — Best-known Xhosa song carried worldwide by Miriam Makeba as 'Click Song #1'.
- **National anthem hymn** (*Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika*) — Song written in isiXhosa by Enoch Sontonga in 1897 and now part of South Africa's national anthem.
- **Choral movement** (*makwaya*) — Choral singing movement founded by Western four-part hymn absorption, remains a mass participatory tradition.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is embedded in ritual rather than staged, with diviner ceremonies turning on possession dancing where the ancestor is made to speak.

- **Diviner possession dance** (*intlombe*) — Diviner initiation ceremonies combining drumming, clapping and dance over long night-long sessions.
- **IsiXhosa opera film** (*U-Carmen eKhayelitsha*) — Shot entirely in isiXhosa in the Cape Town township of Khayelitsha, reworks Bizet's 1875 opera with African music.

## Festivals & rituals

The Xhosa ritual calendar is organised by the life cycle and by the ancestors, centred on male initiation, marriage, and death rites.

- **Male initiation** (*ulwaluko*) — The decisive rite in which the initiate is secluded, circumcised, smeared with white clay and emerges ochred and dressed in new blanket and beadwork.
- **Girl's seclusion** (*intonjane*) — Girls' rite including seclusion and virginity-testing ceremonies at which the beaded inkciyo apron is worn.
- **Marriage** (*umtshato*) — Proceeds through named stages including declaration of intent, checking of clan names, lobola negotiation, and the bride's instruction and naming.
- **Spirit escort ritual** (*umkhapho*) — Performed by the male clan leader to escort the spirit to the ancestors with slaughter of a cow for a family head.
- **Post-burial cleansing** (*ukuxukuxa*) — Follows on the day of burial with a further slaughter, and the day after, women wash the deceased's blankets at the river and the family shaves its hair.

## Foodways

The staple is maize in a family of preparations distinguished by texture, paired with fermented sour milk and ritual beers made from maize and sorghum.

- **White maize with beans** (*umngqusho*) — White maize cooked with sugar beans, the Xhosa staple dish proper.
- **Crumbly dry pap** (*umphokoqo*) — Crumbly dry pap, eaten as umvubo when mixed with fermented sour milk.
- **Fermented sour milk** (*amasi*) — Fermented sour milk, the other pillar of the diet alongside maize.
- **Ritual beer** (*umqombothi*) — Beer fermented from maize and sorghum, both everyday drink and the medium of ancestral offering.
- **Wild sorrel-like spinach** (*rhabe*) — Gathered wild spinach used in food preparation.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral performance centres on praise poets who deliver improvised praise poems naming a chief's lineage and deeds, and on folktales told by older women with sung refrains.

- **Praise poem** (*izibongo*) — Praise poems naming a chief's lineage, deeds and clan, improvised at high speed and in an archaic register.
- **Clan praise recitation** (*iziduko*) — Personal identity statement reciting clan praises, and also the name by which one states whom one may not marry.
- **Folktale genre** (*iintsomi*) — Folktales told by older women to children in the evening, built on sung refrains the audience joins.
- **Praise poet** (*imbongi*) — Poet who delivers praise poems, licensed to criticise as well as to praise.

## Language & religion

IsiXhosa is an agglutinative tonal Bantu language notable for three click series absorbed through Khoisan contact, spoken by about 18% of South Africans.

- **Dental click** (*c*) — One of three click series in isiXhosa absorbed through long Khoisan contact.
- **Lateral click** (*x*) — One of three click series in isiXhosa absorbed through long Khoisan contact.
- **Palatal click** (*q*) — One of three click series in isiXhosa absorbed through long Khoisan contact.
- **Ancestors** (*izinyanya*) — Addressed through slaughter, beer and the umkhapho rite regardless of church membership.
- **Diviner** (*igqirha*) — Called to the vocation by ancestral affliction and trained through the intlombe apprenticeship.
