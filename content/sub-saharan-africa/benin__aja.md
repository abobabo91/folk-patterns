## Overview

The Aja, also called Adja, are an ethnic group native to south-western Benin and south-eastern Togo. Approximately 2,000,000 Ajas live in an area straddling the Benin–Togo border, described as 50 kilometres long and 30 km wide. Significant Aja populations also live in coastal Benin and Togo, southern Nigeria, Gabon, and the urban centres of Cotonou, Lome, Lagos, and Libreville. The Aja speak Aja-Gbe, also called Aja, which belongs to the Gbe dialect continuum. Their history is associated with the Kingdom of Tado, the dispersal of Aja communities, and the formation of the Fon and Ewe peoples. The sources identify Aja influence across southern Benin and Togo but provide little information about distinctive material or performing arts.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The sources describe the historical territory of the Kingdom of Tado and mention defensive walls constructed around Azamé, now called Tado.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The sources provide no catalogue records for Aja museum objects. They mention ancient ironworking among the Alu, including ironworking slag, a mound of industrial waste at the western edge of Tado village, and a blast furnace discovered in 1970 in Tado’s Domé quarter. The Alu are described as skilled ironworkers in antiquity.

## Music & performance

They state that, according to one source, vodún originated from the Aja, but provide no musical information connected with that practice.

## Festivals & rituals

They record several historical and ritual traditions. The Aja are associated in one source with the origin of vodún. In a Porto-Novo tradition, a Yoruba man with magical powers saved Tado from a smallpox epidemic; after marrying the daughter of Aholõhu, chief of the Azanu tribe, he underwent rituals to purify the foreign blood in his veins before becoming king. The sources also state that an outbreak of smallpox once decimated the Adja population. No detailed birth, coming-of-age, wedding, or funeral rites are described.

## Foodways

They state that early Alu communities supplemented ironworking with hunting, fishing, and subsistence agriculture.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral tradition is central to the sources’ account of Aja history. One tradition says that the Aja migrated to southern Benin in the 12th or 13th century from Tado on the Mono River. Around c. 1600, three brothers—Kokpon, Do-Aklin, and Te-Agbanlin—divided rule over the region: Kokpon took Great Ardra and ruled the Allada kingdom, Do-Aklin founded Abomey, and Te-Agbanlin founded Little Ardra, later called Porto Novo by Portuguese traders. Other traditions place Aja origins in the Nile Valley, Oyo, Kétou, or biblical locations such as Babel, while the article also presents migration from the area around the western banks of the Niger River as more likely.

Aja oral traditions also describe the origins of the Alu. Their elders say that their ancestor Eyru descended from heaven carrying a hammer and anvil, symbolizing ironworking. The sources further record traditions concerning the Azanu clan, Kumasi, Tado, and the five-legged stool as a symbol of royal authority. No Aja epic, proverb collection, riddle tradition, contemporary literary revival, or preservation project is described.

## Language & religion

Aja-Gbe, or simply Aja, is a Gbe language spoken in Benin and Togo. The sources name three dialects: Tàgóbé, spoken in Togo only; Dògóbè, spoken in Benin only; and Hwègbè, spoken in both countries. Only 1–5% of Aja are literate in their native tongue. Many are trilingual, speaking French and Fongbe, the lingua franca of southern Benin; Ewe is spoken as a second language by Aja living in Togo and Ghana. The Aja language is described as part of the Gbe dialect continuum. The sources associate the Aja with vodún and state that most Gbe-speaking people, especially the Ewe and Fon, trace their origins to Aja-Tado and regard Aja as their “mother tribe.”
