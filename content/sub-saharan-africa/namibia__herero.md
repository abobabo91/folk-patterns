## Overview

The Herero are traditionally pastoralists whose lives have long been closely tied to cattle. Their culture includes distinctive women’s dress, horn-shaped headdresses, metal and leather ornaments, wooden tools and weapons, and other pastoral objects. They live mainly in Namibia, with significant populations in Botswana and Angola. Their history includes German colonial rule, the 1904–1907 rebellion and the genocide committed by the German Empire between 1904 and 1908.

## Material culture

Herero material culture is strongly shaped by cattle, pastoral life, women’s dress, metal and leather ornaments, and wooden tools and weapons.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Herero clothing uses vivid fabrics, patchwork and patterns linked to cattle horns.

- **Ohorokova dresses** (*Ohorokova*) — These floor-length dresses may use up to ten metres of vivid fabric and several petticoats.
- **Horned headdress** (*Otjikaiva*) — The otjikaiva is made to match or coordinate with women’s dresses and may be formed from rolled-up newspaper covered in fabric.
- **Traditional horn headdress** (*Ekori*) — The Ekori symbolized cow horns, but missionaries rejected its shape as a symbol of the devil.
- **Patchworked dresses** — Everyday dresses may be made from small fabric pieces, including cloth saved from older garments.
- **Pattern motifs** — Named motifs include cow horns, vertical zig-zags and serrated edges.

### Clothing & dress

Herero women are known for full, floor-length dresses and horn-shaped headdresses, while men wear military-style clothes for ceremonies.

- **Women’s dress** (*Ohorokova*) — The ohorokova has a high neck, long sleeves, a full gathered skirt and multiple petticoats.
- **Horn-shaped headdress** (*Otjikaiva*) — Women wear the horizontal otjikaiva as a tribute to cows that historically sustained the Herero.
- **Ceremonial military clothing** — Men wear peaked caps, berets, epaulettes, aiguillettes and gaiters in a military style.
- **Women’s neckerchief** — Coordinating neckerchiefs are knotted around the neck.
- **Decorative dress pins** — Brooches and pins may be attached to the centre front of the otjikaiva.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Herero-associated museum objects include weapons, wooden tools, a smoking pipe and a small meat container.

- **Iron-bladed knife** — The knife has an iron blade and a wooden handle.
- **Wooden-sheathed dagger** — The dagger has a wooden sheath.
- **Straight-bladed sword** — The sword has a straight iron blade and a hard brown wooden hilt and sheath.
- **Wooden crossbow** — A wooden crossbow is recorded among the objects.
- **Smoking pipe** — The pipe has a horn mouthpiece, a steatite bowl and a white-metal cover joined to the stem by a chain.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Herero ornaments use iron, copper, steel, leather, fibre, ostrich eggshell and thongs.

- **Iron-bead necklet** — One necklet has four strings of cylindrical iron beads and a leather front plate decorated with smaller beads.
- **Copper-bead necklace** — A necklace of cylindrical copper beads has similar iron beads at intervals and is threaded on a thong.
- **Serrated iron bangle** — One bangle or armlet is made of iron with serrated edges.
- **Leather leglet** — A woman’s leglet has seven leather loops threaded with iron rings and joined by a thong.
- **Copper ankle rings** — A pair of copper ankle rings is recorded as women’s marriage rings.

## Music & performance

The supplied sources do not identify named Herero musical traditions, but missionaries translated songs into Herero.

- **Translated Herero songs** — Father Peter Heinrich Brincker translated songs into Herero, but the sources give no titles or performance details.

## Festivals & rituals

Herero religious life includes the holy fire Okuruuo and ceremonies involving cattle.

- **Holy fire** (*Okuruuo*) — The holy fire at Okahandja links the people with ancestors, who speak to Ndjambi on their behalf.
- **Other name for the holy fire** (*OtjikaTjamuaha*) — OtjikaTjamuaha is another name for Okuruuo.
- **Relighting the fire** — During immigration, the holy fire was doused and quickly relit.
- **Marriage cattle feast** — Cattle are normally eaten during marriage celebrations.
- **Animal sacrifice** — Cows or other animals may be sacrificed in religious or ancestral-veneration ceremonies.

## Foodways

Cattle are central to Herero foodways, work and social life.

- **Sour milk** (*Omaere*) — Women prepared sour milk called Omaere in pre-colonial times.
- **Milk** — The worth of an OmuHerero woman was measured by milk.
- **Cattle meat** — Cattle are normally eaten at marriages.
- **Goat and sheep meat** — Goats and sheep are kept for meat.
- **Goat dung medicine** — Goat dung is considered medicinal and is normally used to treat chickenpox.

## Oral tradition & literature

The supplied sources do not describe Herero oral literature, but they record strong historical memory and fiction about the genocide.

- **Ceremonial historical memory** — Men’s military-style clothing honours fallen ancestors and keeps their memories alive.
- **Morenga** — Uwe Timm’s novel Morenga is a literary work about the Herero genocide.
- **Mama Namibia** — Mari Serebrov’s Mama Namibia is a literary work about the Herero genocide.
- **We Are Proud to Present a Presentation About the Herero of Namibia** — Jackie Sibblies Drury’s 2012 play is a literary representation of the Herero genocide.
- **The Eagles of Malice** — Anthony Scholefield’s 1968 work is a literary representation of the Herero genocide.

## Language & religion

Otjiherero is widely used in education and broadcasting, while Herero religion combines Christian practice with belief in the holy fire.

- **Herero language** (*Otjiherero*) — Otjiherero is spoken by Herero and Mbanderu people in Namibia and Botswana and by small communities in southwestern Angola.
- **Holy fire** (*Okuruuo*) — Okuruuo is believed to link people with ancestors and Ndjambi.
- **Supreme being** (*Ndjambi*) — Ancestors are understood to speak to Ndjambi on behalf of the people.
- **Bible translation** — Missionary Gottlieb Viehe translated the Bible into Herero and helped establish writing in a Latin-based alphabet.
- **Theological translations** — Father Peter Heinrich Brincker translated theological works and songs into Herero.
