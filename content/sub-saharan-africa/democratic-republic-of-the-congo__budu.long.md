## Overview

The Budu people, also called *Babudu*, are a Bantu people living in the Wamba Territory in the Orientale Province of the Democratic Republic of the Congo. They live on both sides of the Nepoko River, where different dialects are spoken, although the people of the Ibambi side and those of the Wamba side consider themselves one people. Their territory lies within tropical rain forest and is isolated, with roads inaccessible to vehicles other than bicycles. They speak the Budu language, also called Ɨbʉdhʉ. The sources offer evidence of a subsistence economy, bark clothing, palm-leaf-thatched houses, patriarchal family organization, elder consensus, forest exchange with the Mbuti people, and a material culture including iron weapons, a war whistle, a necklace of human teeth, and an ivory armlet.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

*Budu bark loin cloth* — The sources state that people wear loin cloths made from beaten bark.

**Second-hand western clothes** — The sources also report the use of second-hand western clothes, without describing their construction, decoration, or patterns.

### Clothing & dress

Budu clothing described in the sources consists of loin cloths made from beaten bark and second-hand western clothes.

### Architecture

The Budu live in square huts with mud walls and roofs thatched with palm leaves.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The museum catalogue records several iron objects associated with the material culture shown in this atlas. These include an arm-dagger with an iron blade, wooden handle, and sheath; a sword with an iron blade and wooden handle; spears with iron spear-heads and barbs; and several knives. The knives include parade or ceremonial knives with sickle-shaped iron blades, handles covered with iron wire, and plain wooden handles; knives with long iron blades curved at the point; knives with wooden handles bound with iron or iron wire; and a knife whose butt end is wrapped with hide. One catalogue record describes a sickle-shaped ceremonial knife with a copper blade and wooden handle.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The museum catalogue records a necklace made of human teeth strung on twisted fibre cord and a circular armlet made of ivory, now brown with oil. No tattoos, henna, or hair practices are described.

## Music & performance

The museum catalogue records a war whistle made of a long conical piece of hollow wood, bound at intervals with iron and copper strips, with a plaited fibre band added for suspension.

## Festivals & rituals

The sources state that important decisions are made by a consensus of elders and that both the Catholic church and, later, WEC International, or Worldwide Evangelisation for Christ, have been active with the Budu. Charles Studd, the founder of WEC, died in Budu territory and is buried in Ibambi.

## Foodways

The Budu mainly live by subsistence agriculture. Their crops include cassava, yams, corn, rice, peanuts, plantains, and pulses. Palm oil is the main cash crop, giving an income per capita of about $120 a year. Animal husbandry is rare and is limited to poultry and some goats. The Budu obtain meat and other forest resources through trade with the Mbuti people, identified in the source as Pygmies, who share their language.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources state only that the Budu speak the Budu language and that a variety of it called Matta is spoken locally both north and south of Maboma.

## Language & religion

Ɨbʉdhʉ, also called Budu, is a Bantu language spoken by the Budu people in the Wamba Territory in the Orientale Province of the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Its orthography uses the special characters ɨ, ʉ, ɛ, and ɔ, as well as the modifier letters colon ꞉ and equal sign ꞊ for grammatical tone, marking past and future tense respectively. A variety called Matta is spoken locally both north and south of Maboma. The Budu live on both sides of the Nepoko River and speak different dialects; [z] occurs only in the Koya dialect, while /h/ can be heard as voiced [ɦ] or voiceless [h], and /ɗ/ can be heard as [l] or a tap [ɾ] in free variation. Both the Catholic church and WEC International have been active with the Budu.
