## Overview

The Amhara are a Semitic-speaking people of the northwestern Ethiopian highlands, numbering 19,867,817 in the 2007 national census — 26.9% of Ethiopia's population. Their historic heartland runs across the provinces of Begemder, Gojjam, Semien, Lasta, Wollo (medieval *Bete Amhara*, "House of Amhara"), Dembiya, Wegera, Angot and northern Shewa: a plateau of basalt escarpments and barley terraces between the Abay (Blue Nile) gorge, Lake Tana and the Afar Depression, with Gondar and Lalibela as its cultural capitals. They speak Amharic, a South Ethio-Semitic language of the Afroasiatic family, written in the *fidal* abugida derived from Ge'ez. Overwhelmingly Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Christian, the Amhara produced the Solomonic dynasty from 1270 and, with it, the manuscript painting, processional metalwork and handwoven cotton dress that define highland Ethiopian material culture.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Shemma** — the foundation cloth: handspun, handwoven plain-weave cotton produced on a pit treadle loom by the *shemane* weaver, traditionally a hereditary occupational specialist within the Amhara caste structure. Its fineness is graded by thread count, and the whitest, most gauzy grades are reserved for church attendance.

**Tibeb** — the woven decorative border that converts plain *shemma* into a status garment. Worked as a supplementary-weft band in coloured silk or rayon along hems, cuffs, necklines and the vertical opening of a dress, *tibeb* patterning is regionally legible: Gondar, Gojjam, Shewa and Wollo each carry recognizable border repertoires, and a woman's provincial origin can be read from her hem.

**Habesha kemis** — the woman's long white cotton dress, ankle-length, cut from *shemma* and edged with *tibeb*. It is the primary attire of Amhara women and the garment most closely bound to their cultural identity; the version worn for the coffee ceremony and for saints' days differs from neighbouring Tigray and Gurage dress chiefly in the proportion and placement of its coloured borders.

**Netela** — a light two-layer cotton shawl worn by women over the *kemis*, draped differently according to occasion: pulled over the head and across the mouth in church, folded back over the shoulders in the street, reversed with the *tibeb* band outward for mourning.

**Gabi** — a thick shawl of four layers of *shemma* sewn together, worn by men as an all-purpose cloak, blanket and highland cold-weather garment; the plain white *gabi* over a white tunic is the standard image of the Amhara peasant.

**Kuta** — the men's lighter counterpart to the *gabi*, a single- or double-layer white cotton wrap with a narrow coloured selvedge, thrown over the shoulder for daily wear and worn to church.

**Magic scroll / kitab** — not a textile but a pattern-bearing object of the same visual family: a strip of parchment cut to the client's height, painted by a *däbtära* (unordained church scholar) with talismanic geometry, protective angel faces with enormous eyes, and Ge'ez prayers against demons and illness, then rolled into a leather cylinder worn on the body.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Meskel* (the cross, in dozens of interlace variants), Latin and Greek cross forms with lobed terminals, the Gondarine interlace lattice (*harag*), the eight-pointed star, the diamond lozenge chain of *tibeb* borders, the guilloche band, the *däbtära* talismanic "net" of squares filled with Ge'ez letters, the frontal wide-eyed face of scroll and icon painting, and the checkerboard and zigzag registers of woven hems.

### Clothing & dress

Women wear the *habesha kemis* with a *netela* and, in cold highland weather, a heavier *kuta*. Hair is braided in *shuruba* — tight rows running back from the forehead, opening into loose or plaited lengths at the nape — and the whole often dressed with butter. Men wear white cotton jodhpur-style trousers narrow at the calf, a long tunic, and a *gabi* wrapped over the shoulder; older and rural men carry a *dula*, a long walking staff, which doubles as a dance prop. Clergy dress is a separate and far more elaborate register: the Ethiopian Orthodox priest appears in a *kaba* robe, a turban, and for major feasts brocaded and umbrella-shaded processional vestments in red, gold and green, carrying a hand cross and a *maqwamiya* prayer staff. Warriors and nobility of the imperial period wore the lion's-mane headdress and the *lemd*, a velvet cape stiff with silver and gold embroidery, over the plain white *shemma* of everyone else. Bare feet or simple leather sandals were the highland norm well into the twentieth century.

### Architecture

The Amhara rural house is the *tukul*, a cylindrical single-room dwelling with walls of wattle-and-daub — eucalyptus or juniper poles interwoven with withies and plastered with mud and straw — carrying a tall conical thatch roof on a central post, its apex often capped with an inverted pot or an iron finial. A low doorway, no windows, and a central hearth define the interior; livestock share the floor in the coldest districts. The Gondarine tradition is the counterweight: from the 1630s, emperors built the stone castles of the *Fasil Ghebbi* compound at Gondar in dressed basalt with lime mortar, round corner towers, crenellated parapets and arched openings, plus bath complexes and bridges. Church architecture divides into three families: the round thatched church, with a square inner sanctuary (*maqdas*) at its centre holding the *tabot*, surrounded by concentric ambulatories and a ring of painted canvas or mural panels; the Gondarine rectangular basilica; and the rock-hewn churches of Lalibela in Lasta, eleven monolithic structures cut down into red tuff in the Zagwe period, complete with cruciform plan, carved windows and a trench-and-tunnel network. Monasteries on the islands of Lake Tana preserve the round-church form at its most painted.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Amhara metalwork is dominated by the cross. The **processional cross** (*mäsqäl*) — cast in brass or silver by the lost-wax method, mounted on a wooden shaft, its openwork interlace radiating from a central cruciform core and often fitted with cloth streamers — and the **hand cross** (*mäsqäl yä'əj*), a smaller version with a rectangular openwork base carried by every priest, are the signature objects; Lalibela and Gondar workshops produced distinct silhouettes. The *sistrum* (*ts'änats'əl*), a metal rattle with loose discs on cross-bars, and the *kebero*, a double-headed conical drum, are liturgical instruments treated as sacred metal- and leatherwork. Domestic ware includes the black burnished clay *jebena* coffee pot with its bulbous body, long spout and straw lid-finial; the large clay *mitad* griddle for baking *injera*; and the *mesob*, a conical basketry table of coiled grass in red, black and natural bands, from which a household eats communally. Ge'ez manuscripts on goatskin parchment, sewn in wooden boards and carried in leather satchels, are the other great craft object.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Amhara goldsmiths and silversmiths — an endogamous occupational group in the traditional stratification — worked in filigree and granulation. The dominant form is the neck cross: the *mateb*, a blue-black silk cord tied at baptism and never removed, from which hangs a small silver cross, and the larger pendant crosses of Gondar, Lalibela, Axum and Shewa types, each with its own openwork geometry. Filigree earrings, coiled silver bangles, heavy anklets and beaded necklaces of amber and glass complete the female ensemble; married women add silver headbands. Facial and neck tattooing is widespread among Amhara women: a cross or a line of dots at the throat, crosses on the forehead and the backs of the hands, applied with soot and thorn — permanent markers of Christian identity that could not be erased by capture. Kohl around the eyes and butter-dressed *shuruba* braids complete the dress for weddings and feast days.

## Music & performance

Amhara music divides cleanly between church and countryside. The liturgical tradition is *zema*, the chant system of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church attributed to the sixth-century Saint Yared, notated with *melekket* signs and performed by *däbtära* with the *sistrum* (*ts'änats'əl*), the *kebero* drum and the *maqwamiya* prayer staff. The *begena*, a large ten-string lyre with a deep buzzing tone, is reserved for solitary devotional singing, especially in Lent.

Secular folk music is *qañat*, carried by the *azmari* — itinerant village minstrels who improvise praise, satire and news to order. Their instruments are the *masenqo*, a one-string bowed spike lute with a diamond-shaped soundbox, and the *krar*, a six-string lyre; the *washint*, an end-blown bamboo flute, belongs to herders, while the *meleket* trumpet and the *negarit* kettledrum were instruments of royal and military announcement. The modal framework is the *qignit* system, whose named scales — *tizita* (nostalgia), *bati*, *ambassel*, *anchihoye* — give their names to song genres as well. *Shellela* is the war chant, and *fukera* the boast declaimed before battle; Getatchew Mekurya turned *shellela* into a saxophone idiom in the 1950s. From that fusion came Ethio-jazz, built by Mulatu Astatke on *tizita* played through Western instruments, and the Golden Age of the 1960s–70s, with Asnaketch Worku, Bahru Kegne, Kassa Tessema and Mary Armede as masters of the older instruments. Under the Derg (1974–1991) and the EPRDF, *qene* — poetry in the *sam-enna warq* ("wax and gold") double-entendre mode — returned as the vehicle of coded political dissent, a practice continued by Teddy Afro and Nhatty Man.

## Dance & theatre

**Eskista** is the definitive Amhara dance: a standing dance of the shoulders, chest and neck, in which the shoulder blades are rolled, jolted and shuddered in rhythmic isolation while the feet stay largely planted — a technique unique in the region and performed at weddings, saints' days and *azmari* houses (*azmari bet*), where dancers respond to the *masenqo*'s phrasing. Men's dance adds the *dula* staff, leaping, and the shouted *fukera* boast; regional styles differ, with Gondar and Gojjam recognizably distinct in tempo and shoulder attack.

Liturgical dance is separate and strictly ordered: at Timkat, Meskel and other great feasts, ranks of *däbtära* perform the slow processional chant-dance of the *aquaquam*, swaying in unison, striking the ground with prayer staffs and shaking sistra while the *kebero* keeps time — a form traced to King David dancing before the Ark and performed in full brocade vestments.

Scripted theatre is a twentieth-century Amharic invention: Tekle Hawariat Tekle Mariyam's *Fabula: Yawreoch Commedia*, Africa's first scripted play, established a tradition of Amharic-language drama that still fills the National Theatre in Addis Ababa.

## Festivals & rituals

The Amhara year follows the Ethiopian calendar of thirteen months, beginning with **Enkutatash** (New Year, 11 September), when girls carry yellow *adey abeba* daisies door to door singing. **Meskel** (27 September) marks the Finding of the True Cross with the *Demera*, a great conical bonfire of branches topped with daisies, lit after a procession of clergy in full vestments; the direction in which the charred cross-pole falls is read as an omen. **Genna** (Christmas, 7 January) is followed by **Timkat** (Epiphany, 19 January), the most spectacular feast: the *tabot* — the altar tablet that makes a church a church — is carried out under an embroidered umbrella, processed to water, and used to bless the crowd at dawn, after which participants are sprinkled or immersed. **Fasika** (Easter) closes the fifty-five-day Lenten fast and is, with Timkat, the great celebration of the year. **Hidar Tsion** and the monthly saints' days — Kidane Mehret, Abune Tekle Haymanot, Saint Michael, Saint Mary — punctuate the calendar with local church feasts.

Life-cycle rites are church-anchored. A priest visits to bless a newborn; mother and child remain indoors for forty days, and baptism follows at forty days for boys and eighty for girls, when the *mateb* cord is tied. Marriage takes three forms: *semanya* ("eighty bond", also *kal kidan*), the common civil contract permitting divorce; *qurban*, solemnized at the altar and indissoluble, usually observed by priests; and *damoz*, a temporary marriage for wages whose child could claim a share of the father's property. Each family hosts its own wedding feast. Funerals are followed by the *tezkar*, a memorial feast on the fortieth day, and mourners belong to *iddir*, mutual-aid burial societies that finance the rites.

## Foodways

Teff, a highland grain grown almost nowhere else, is the staple. Fermented three days into a batter and poured in a spiral onto a hot clay *mitad*, it becomes *injera*, a pale sour flatbread 30–45 cm across that serves simultaneously as plate, utensil and food. Over it are ladled *wat*, thick stews built on *berbere* — the chilli-and-spice blend of Ethiopian cooking — and *niter kibbeh*, butter clarified with *korarima*, ajwain and fenugreek. *Doro wat*, chicken with hard-boiled eggs, is the festival dish and the standard of a cook's reputation; *key wat* and *siga wat* are its beef versions, *alicha* the mild turmeric-based alternative. *Kitfo*, minced raw beef with *mitmita* and spiced butter, and *tere siga*, raw beef cut at the table, mark celebration. Fasting practice shapes the menu more than anything else: Orthodox Amhara abstain from meat, eggs, butter, milk and cheese every Wednesday and Friday and through Lent, producing the *bayeynetu* platter of vegan stews — *misir wat* (red lentil), *kik alicha* (split pea), *shiro* (ground chickpea), *gomen* (collard greens), *atkilt* — and neither pork nor shellfish is eaten at any time.

Coffee (*buna*) is native and is served through the *buna maflat* ceremony: green beans roasted over coals and passed for their smoke to be inhaled, ground in a mortar, brewed in a clay *jebena* and poured in three successive rounds — *abol*, *tona*, *baraka* — over grass strewn on the floor and incense, hosted by the woman of the household in a *kemis*. *Tella*, a barley-and-*gesho* beer, and *tej*, honey wine fermented with *gesho* and drunk from a flask-shaped *berele*, are the festival drinks.

## Oral tradition & literature

The characteristic Amhara verbal art is *qene*, extemporised religious verse composed in Ge'ez in the church schools and built on *sam-enna warq* — "wax and gold" — in which a surface meaning (the wax model) conceals an intended one (the gold cast inside it). Mastery of *qene* was the summit of traditional education, and the technique migrated into secular song, courtship speech, political satire and dissent under the Derg. Alongside it run *tarik* folk narratives, the animal fables and trickster tales, a dense proverb literature (*mesale*), and riddles exchanged as evening entertainment. The hagiographic *gadl* (Acts of the Saints) and the fourteenth-century *Kebra Nagast*, which grounds the Solomonic dynasty in the union of Solomon and the Queen of Sheba, are the foundational written narratives; *Mälkə'* poems praise saints limb by limb. Written Amharic literature begins with the fourteenth-century royal songs, reaches Europe through Abba Gorgoryos's collaboration with Hiob Ludolf in 1652, and turns modern with Afäwarq Gäbrä Iyäsus's *Ləbb Wälläd Tarik* (Rome, 1908), the first Amharic novel.

## Language & religion

Amharic is a South Ethio-Semitic language of the Afroasiatic family, developed with a Cushitic (Agaw) substratum under a Semitic superstratum and heavily lexified from Ge'ez after the Christianization of the proto-Amhara. It has over 32 million native and 25 million second-language speakers, is a federal working language of Ethiopia, and is written in *fidal*, the abugida adapted from the Ge'ez script. Ge'ez itself survives as the liturgical language of the church. In the Amhara Region, 82.5% are Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo, 17.2% Muslim, 0.5% Beta Israel and 0.2% Protestant. Folk religious practice runs alongside orthodoxy: the *däbtära*'s healing scrolls and talismans, exorcism of the *buda* (evil eye, associated with hereditary craft groups), pilgrimage to Lalibela and the Lake Tana monasteries, and the *zar* spirit-possession cult, treated by the church as illicit but widely practised.
