## Overview

The Songye occupy the wooded savanna between the Lomami and Lualaba rivers in south-central Congo, speaking a Bantu language closely related to Luba and claiming shared ancestry with them. They organised themselves as dozens of autonomous chiefdoms, each led by a chief whose power was balanced by the bwadi bwa kifwebe, a powerful masking society that enforced norms and conducted rituals. The Songye sat astride copper and salt trade routes and became renowned as skilled smiths and aggressive traders. Their art—especially nkisi figures and kifwebe masks—was shaped by the tension between chiefly authority and the regulatory power of secret societies.

## Material culture

Songye material culture emphasises objects of power and office rather than domestic decoration, with surface pattern rendered in incision and metal rather than woven or painted.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Songye worked raffia cloth before colonialism, but much patterned cloth came by trade from neighbours; a shared geometric vocabulary of chevrons and triangles appears across multiple media.

- **Raffia cloth** (*mbuki*) — Palm-fibre cloth woven by men on a single-heddle loom, pounded soft, and used for wrappers, burial shrouds and tribute payment.
- **Geometric surface vocabulary** — Chevrons, lozenges, triangles and parallel bands recur across raffia, gourds, blades and scarified skin, so one design appears in several media at once.
- **Beaded and cowrie-worked panels** (*lukasa-style beadwork*) — Bead and cowrie work applied to caps, belts and ornaments, shared with the Luba and among the Songye serving chiefly regalia rather than memory boards.
- **Trade cloth** — Imported cottons and factory prints absorbed into chiefly dress from the late nineteenth century quickly outranked local raffia in ceremonial display.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday dress was a wrapped skirt with the upper body bare, so ornament, coiffure and scarification carried the burden of signification; ceremonial dress piled skins, feathers, beads, copper and iron onto the body.

- **Chief's regalia** (*bwalelo*) — The assembled insignia of office—cap, leopard or civet skin, beaded sash, copper ornaments, staff, ceremonial adze and stool—worn together at installation and public judgement.
- **Animal-skin mantle** — Leopard, genet or civet pelts reserved to chiefs and senior titleholders, the leopard in particular signifying legitimate lethal authority.
- **Feather headdress** — Feather crests and plumes worn by dancers and titleholders, often combined with fibre neck ruffs.
- **Masking costume** — Full-body raffia suits with long fibre neck ruffs and sleeves worn with kifwebe masks, designed to conceal the dancer's body entirely.

### Architecture

The Songye built no monumental structures but arranged rectangular earth-walled, thatch-roofed houses along village streets and plazas where ritual and governance occurred.

- **Village house** — Rectangular pole-frame house with wattle-and-daub walls and pitched grass or palm-thatch roof, usually single-roomed with a shaded veranda and adjacent cooking shelter.
- **Village plaza** — Central open space along which compounds were ranged, serving as the setting for masquerade, judgement and dance.
- **Men's shelter** — Open-sided shelter where men gathered to hear cases, work and receive visitors, the practical centre of village public life.
- **Chief's enclosure** — Larger compound containing the chief's house, stool, regalia and the space where communal nkisi were kept and fed.
- **Initiation camp** (*mukanda*) — Temporary bush encampment built for boys' initiation and burned or abandoned at its conclusion, so the most ritually charged building was by design impermanent.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Metal is where Songye craft reaches its height, with smiths holding near-ritual status and iron and copper objects functioning as tools, weapons, currency, insignia and sculpture; pottery is a women's craft, largely undecorated.

- **Power figure** (*nkisi*) — Carved male figure, from small personal examples to communal statues over a metre high, sheathed in copper or brass, studded with nails, and packed with medicine in a hollowed head or abdomen.
- **Horn charge** (*nkishi horn*) — Antelope or goat horn set into the crown or belly of a figure and packed with bishimba—composite medicine that makes the object efficacious.
- **Ceremonial axe and adze** (*nsapo*) — Forged blades socketed into carved wooden hafts, often surmounted by a human head, carried as emblems of office rather than used for work.
- **Prestige blade** (*mpungu*) — Forged iron knives, short swords and throwing blades with openwork, pierced or chevron-cut bodies, made to be seen and inherited.
- **Copper cross ingot** (*handa*) — X-shaped copper ingots cast in open sand moulds and circulating widely as currency, bridewealth and store of value, later reworked into ornament.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Songye adornment was worked on and into the body as much as hung from it, with scarification and coiffure the primary signs of identity, status and beauty.

- **Scarification** (*kilonda*) — Raised keloid patterns cut on forehead, torso and abdomen in rows of dots, chevrons and lozenges, marking lineage, initiation grade and beauty.
- **Coiffure** (*shankadi coiffure*) — Elaborate cantilevered and cruciform hairstyles built over a fibre or cane armature and oiled, rendered in sculpture as a high stepped or crested crown.
- **Copper ornament** — Arm rings, anklets, neck rings and coiled wire from Katangan copper, worn in quantity as both adornment and portable wealth.
- **Beaded collar and belt** — Glass-bead collars, belts and bandoliers made from imported beads entering the region through nineteenth-century trade.
- **Amulets** — Small charged bundles, horns, teeth and miniature figures worn at the neck or waist for protection and success in hunting or litigation.

## Music & performance

Songye music is percussion-led and functional, convening court, escorting the dead and announcing masks; singing is typically antiphonal with a soloist answered by chorus.

- **Membrane drums** — Single-headed drums of graded size played in sets with hands and sticks, the largest belonging to the chief and sounded to convene assembly.
- **Slit drum** — Hollowed hardwood log struck to carry messages between villages and accompany masquerade, its two pitches able to encode speech tone.
- **Lamellophone** — Plucked-lamella instrument played solo or in small ensembles for entertainment and praise song.
- **Whistles, horns and flutes** — Side-blown antelope horns and small whistles used for signalling in hunt and masquerade and to produce non-human vocal effects.
- **Responsorial song** — Solo praise and narrative lines answered by chorus and handclapping, the vehicle for lineage praise, work song and mourning.

## Dance & theatre

Songye performance is masquerade rather than staged theatre, centred on bwadi bwa kifwebe, the society that owned striated masks and policed behaviour as an act of governance performed as spectacle.

- **Mask society** (*bwadi bwa kifwebe*) — Initiated men's association whose masked appearances enforced norms, levied contributions and framed the installation and death of chiefs.
- **Striated mask** (*kifwebe*) — Mask with a domed forehead, projecting box mouth and surface covered in fine parallel incised striations, worn with a full raffia suit.
- **Male mask** (*kilume*) — Aggressive type commonly distinguished by a high sagittal crest and bolder striation, associated with disruptive force and public discipline.
- **Female mask** (*kikashi*) — Flatter, white-dominated, low- or no-crested type associated with benevolence, order and reproductive continuity, danced with restrained movement.
- **Danced possession and grotesque play** — The masks move between menace and comedy with lurching, stamping and sudden charges at spectators, a controlled release of violence the society alone was licensed to perform.

## Festivals & rituals

Songye ritual life keyed to office, initiation and death rather than an agricultural calendar, with most important events—investiture, funeral, initiation closing, figure consultation—convened rather than annual.

- **Chiefly investiture** — Multi-day installation at which the chief is seated on the kabila stool, invested with bwalelo regalia and presented to the masks and assembled lineages.
- **Boys' initiation** (*mukanda*) — Seclusion in a bush camp with circumcision, instruction and ordeal, closed by a public return that confers adult status.
- **Funerary rite** — Extended mourning with drumming, dancing and head-shaving, followed by burial with grave goods; the death of a chief required masked appearance and regalia transfer.
- **Feeding the communal figure** — Periodic offerings, libations and sacrifices made to a village nkisi by its keeper, with consultation in epidemic, infertility, drought or war.
- **Divination and affliction cure** (*nganga consultation*) — Diagnosis of misfortune by a specialist and the making, charging or reactivation of a charm to answer it, the ordinary ritual work around which the spectacular objects cluster.

## Foodways

Songye subsistence combined shifting cultivation with hunting, fishing and small livestock; the modern staple is a stiff cassava flour porridge eaten with sauce.

- **Cassava porridge** (*bidia or fufu*) — Stiff porridge made from cassava flour, often blended with maize, eaten by hand with a sauce.
- **Cassava leaf relish** (*pondu or sombe*) — Pounded cassava leaves long-simmered into a dark green relish served with the staple.
- **Palm wine** — Tapped from oil and raffia palms and poured in libation to ancestors before being drunk by the living at assembly and rite.
- **Incised gourd cups** — Calabashes and wooden cups for palm wine worked with triangle-and-chevron vocabulary found on masks and blades.
- **Ritual meat** — Goats and chickens kept less for daily eating than for sacrifice, bridewealth and hospitality, so meat is frequently a ritual food.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Songye transmitted historical and juridical memory through praise, genealogy and narrative performed aloud, with chiefly genealogies functioning as political charters and proverbs used as precedent in dispute settlement.

- **Chiefly genealogy** — Recitation at investiture establishing who may hold office and on what grounds, entangling Songye and Luba traditions to affirm shared Shaba origin.
- **Proverbs and aphorisms** (*nkindi*) — Condensed, figurative sayings used with real weight in dispute settlement, where a well-placed proverb can function as precedent.
- **Folk narrative** — Trickster and animal tales, etiological stories explaining the origin of death and chieftaincy, and hunter's tales transmitted orally.
- **Riddling contests** — Children's and young people's genre that trains in the figurative language adults later use in court.
- **Initiation knowledge** — Restricted verbal knowledge including mask songs, society formulae and the names and properties of medicines, deliberately not public.

## Language & religion

Kisonge (Songe) is a Bantu language closely related to Kiluba; religiously, the Songye are predominantly Christian but practise ancestral veneration and consult specialists to diagnose and remedy misfortune caused by spirits or witchcraft.

- **Kisonge language** (*Songe*) — A Bantu language of the Luba group, closely related to Kiluba and Tshiluba; most Songye today also use Swahili or Tshiluba as lingua franca.
- **Creator god** (*Efile Mukulu*) — A distant god not approached directly, in contrast to ancestral and nature spirits who must be kept in good relation.
- **Specialist practitioner** (*nganga*) — Expert who performs diagnosis and remedy, using nkisi figures as instruments to address misfortune caused by witchcraft or offended spirits.
- **Christianity** — Catholic and various Protestant churches received onto an existing framework of ancestral veneration rather than replacing it; independent and Pentecostal congregations growing.
- **Misfortune diagnosis** — Illness, infertility, crop failure and death traced to witchcraft, broken obligation or offended spirit, answered by diagnosis and remedy rather than resignation.

## Glossary

- *nkisi* — Power figure, a carved male statue charged with medicine and activated by ritual specialist
- *kifwebe* — Striated mask with parallel incised lines, worn by masked society members
- *bwadi bwa kifwebe* — Mask society of initiated men that enforced norms and appeared at major rituals
- *kilume* — Male type of kifwebe mask, aggressive, associated with discipline
- *kikashi* — Female type of kifwebe mask, benevolent, white-dominated
- *bwalelo* — Chief's regalia and assembled insignia of office
- *kabila* — Chief's stool, proxy for the chief's person and office continuity
- *mukanda* — Boys' initiation camp and the initiation ritual itself
- *nganga* — Ritual specialist who diagnoses misfortune and makes charms
- *bishimba* — Composite medicine made of mineral, vegetable and animal matter
- *mpungu* — Prestige blade, forged iron knife or short sword
- *handa* — Copper cross ingot used as currency and store of wealth
- *kilonda* — Scarification, raised keloid patterns marking lineage and status
- *shankadi* — Elaborate cantilevered coiffure, shared with the Luba
- *mbuki* — Raffia cloth woven on a single-heddle loom
- *lukasa* — Luba memory board; style of beaded and cowrie-worked regalia panels
- *nkindi* — Proverbs and aphorisms used in dispute settlement
- *bidia* — Cassava flour porridge, the main staple
- *pondu* — Cassava leaf relish, dark green and simmered
