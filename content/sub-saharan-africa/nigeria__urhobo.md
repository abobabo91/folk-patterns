## Overview

Urhobo communities live in the western Niger Delta. The Smithsonian describes autonomous villages and village groups with shared ancestry. Historical museum collections show carved figures, elaborate head-dresses and masks, musical instruments, and a few household objects. Contemporary Commons photographs add dance and marriage scenes. These are selected records, not a complete account of Urhobo life.

## Material culture

The British Museum holds several nineteenth-century wooden head-dresses with two faces, animal forms, carved figures, and wicker bases that fit over a performer's head. Other masks have bird-like crests or painted faces. Their differing forms should not be folded into a single ritual. A historical photograph of an Urhobo woman carrying produce adds a different kind of evidence about daily work and adornment.

Sculpture is a second strength of the gallery. The British Museum describes an *ivwri* figure with a gaping mouth and multiple human and animal forms as associated with protection and aggressive power. The Smithsonian distinguishes Urhobo figures representing spirits from those representing ancestors, while warning that the difference can be hard to establish without the original context. The selected figures have different postures, clothing, and attachments; a top hat on one figure records contact and change rather than a timeless dress code.

Drums, cane-and-gourd rattles, and an iron gong document musical forms. A carved wooden ladle with a bird at the end of its handle and an iron knife broaden the material picture. Contemporary photographs specifically caption Urhobo dances and a wedding.
