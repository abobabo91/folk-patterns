## Overview

The Nyamwezi are Tanzania's second-largest ethnic group, settled on the western plateau since the seventeenth century as farmers and cattle herders. From around 1800 they became the primary porters and traders of the central caravan route to the coast. Their society was organized around chiefdoms whose rulers held both political and religious authority, and their economy supported part-time crafts in iron, wood and beads that were traded widely across the region. Despite centuries of contact with foreign merchants, Islam and Christian missionaries, their overseas colonies remained culturally distinct.

## Material culture

Nyamwezi craft production focused on iron, wood and glass beads, with objects made for both local use and regional trade.

### Textile & pattern traditions

A once-thriving cloth industry collapsed in the nineteenth century under competition from Indian imports, leaving beadwork and basketry as the main patterned work.

- **Beaded waist-band** — A glass-bead band with plaited tying strings and fringe, worked in white, red and black on pale blue ground in squares and rectangles.
- **Coiled bead-set basketry** — A round-bottomed basket of spirally coiled vegetable fibre with nearly vertical sides, studded with blue glass beads on the outer wall.
- **Reed matting** — Mats made of reed, used in daily life.

### Clothing & dress

Little is documented about garments, but beaded body ornament was an important element of dress, and Islam influenced fashion in the nineteenth century.

- **Beaded waist-band** — A glass-bead band with plaited ties and fringe worn around the waist.
- **Glass-bead necklaces** — Necklaces of plain glass beads strung on fibre, sometimes combining black beads with black strips.
- **Glass-bead bracelets** — Bracelets made of small beads separated by larger ones.

### Architecture

Nineteenth-century Nyamwezi settlements were large, compact and fortified, organized around the residential unit of the tembe.

- **Tembe** — The residential unit of Nyamwezi society, where spouses came from outside and sons commonly moved away from their father's homestead.
- **Fortified settlement** — Large compact towns protected by strong wooden stockades, often built on high rocky sites for defence.
- **Itetemia fortress** — The royal fortress built by ruler Isike around the royal courts, with thick stone and mud walls reinforced by fire, pierced at the top for marksmen to defend the base.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Craft production was part-time and not hereditary, with iron, drums and wooden goods distributed widely through trade networks.

- **Iron hoes** — Ritual-made hoes that served as bridewealth, a central economic and social object.
- **Drums** — Wooden drums made and traded across the region as part of the craft economy.
- **Snuff-boxes** — Boxes made from seed pods or horn, traded across regional networks.
- **Wooden ladle** — A ladle stained by burning and finished with punch marks, used in daily life and trade.
- **Wooden mask** — A carved mask with an open mouth and teeth apparently of shell, held in the collection but with no recorded performance context.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Glass beads strung on fibre formed the bulk of ornament, while metal and animal materials carried more emphatic pieces linked to hunting and prestige.

- **Biconical copper-bead necklace** — A necklace of copper beads graded in size, a more emphatic ornament than simple glass beads.
- **Ivory necklace** — A necklace of eleven rounded triangular plaques of ivory or rhinoceros bone, connecting ornament to the elephant hunters' guild.
- **Ivory armlet** — An armlet of ivory, marking membership in the elephant hunters' guild, the most prestigious occupation.
- **Elephant-tail bracelet** — A bracelet made from an elephant's tail, directly linking ornament to prestige hunting.

## Music & performance

The only documented musical genre is greeting and installation songs for chiefs, sung during central political rituals rather than for entertainment.

- **Greeting and installation songs for chiefs** — Songs recorded in the Tabora district and held by the Smithsonian, performed when a new chief was installed as part of political ritual.

## Dance & theatre

Dance and dramatic performance are not covered in the sources.

## Festivals & rituals

Ritual life centers on ancestor veneration, divination, and political ceremonies, with marriage as the main life-cycle institution and the chief's death the most striking political rite.

- **Ancestor offerings** — Offerings of sheep or goats made to ancestors with the help of the high god Likube invoked beforehand.
- **Baswezi society** (*Baswezi*) — A society that recruits people possessed by the Swezi spirit for religious and healing purposes.
- **Marriage and bridewealth** — The central life-cycle institution where bridewealth was paid in ritually-made hoes, every adult ideally married with the wife maintaining her own household.
- **Chief's death ritual** — Rulers were strangled when seriously ill because the state's wellbeing was identified with the chief.
- **Widow inheritance** — A widow was customarily inherited by her former husband's younger brother or sister's son, though not against her will.

## Foodways

The Nyamwezi are subsistence farmers and cattle herders with ugali as the staple, living in domestic groups centered on husband, wife or wives, and resident children.

- **Ugali** — A porridge made from hominy and served with meat and vegetables, the staple food.
- **Fermented grain beer** — Beer brewed from fermented corn, sorghum or millet, commonly consumed.

## Oral tradition & literature

Oral tradition records that the Nyamwezi settled west-central Tanzania in the seventeenth century, with no documented folktale cycles, epics or proverb collections.

- **Settlement tradition** — Oral tradition holds that the Nyamwezi arrived on the western plateau in the seventeenth century, confirmed by the Galahansa in the late seventeenth century.

## Language & religion

Kinyamwezi is a major Bantu language with three tones, while most Nyamwezi follow traditional religion centered on the high god Likube and ancestor worship despite nineteenth-century missionary conversion.

- **Likube** — The high god of traditional Nyamwezi religion, also called Limatunda (Creator), Limi (the Sun) and Liwelolo (the Universe).
- **Mfumu** — Diviners and witchdoctors who serve as counsellors and medical practitioners in daily religious life.
- **Bulogi** — Witchcraft, described as a powerful force in Nyamwezi religious and social life.

## Glossary

- *Unyamwezi* — The homeland of the Nyamwezi, the western plateau region of Tanzania
- *Kinyamwezi* — The Bantu language spoken by the Nyamwezi, with three tones
- *tembe* — The residential unit of Nyamwezi society, a household compound
- *ugali* — A staple porridge made from hominy
- *Likube* — The high god of traditional Nyamwezi religion
- *Limatunda* — The Creator, another name for the high god
- *Limi* — The Sun, another name for the high god
- *Liwelolo* — The Universe, another name for the high god
- *mfumu* — A diviner and witchdoctor, counsellor and medical practitioner
- *bulogi* — Witchcraft, a powerful force in Nyamwezi religious life
- *Baswezi* — A spirit-possession society for those possessed by the Swezi spirit
- *Swezi spirit* — A possessing spirit whose holders are recruited into the Baswezi society
