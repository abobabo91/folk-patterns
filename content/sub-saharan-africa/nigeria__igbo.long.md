## Overview

The Igbo (*Ńdị́ Ìgbò*) are one of Africa's largest ethnic groups, concentrated in southeastern Nigeria — Abia, Anambra, Ebonyi, Enugu and Imo States — in the forested country straddling the lower Niger River, east and south of the Edoid and Idomoid peoples and west of the Cross River (Ibibioid) cluster. Igbo communities also live in the Niger Delta, along the Cross River, and as residents in Cameroon, Gabon and Equatorial Guinea. The Igbo language belongs to the Igboid cluster of the Volta–Niger phylum within Niger-Congo, closest to Yoruboid and Edoid. In folk-culture terms the Igbo matter for three reasons: the 9th–10th century **Igbo-Ukwu** bronzes, among the earliest lost-wax castings in West Africa; the **mbari** houses of the Owerri region, an architecture built to be abandoned; and a masking complex — *agbogho mmuo*, *ekpe*, *mmwo* — as dense as any in the Guinea forest belt.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Akwete** — a broad, heavy woman's weave from the town of Akwete in Abia State, produced on a vertical single-heddle loom by women, in wide panels (often over a metre) that need no seaming into a cloth of two strips the way most West African narrow-strip weaving does. Supplementary-weft float motifs are laid in by hand, and weavers historically held proprietary rights over particular designs.

**Uli** — the body-and-wall painting idiom of Igbo women, drawn in dye from the *uli* seed pod (which oxidises from pale to blue-black over hours) with a knife-blade or stylus. Its vocabulary of spirals, arcs and interlaces is the single most recognisable Igbo surface-pattern system, and was carried from skin onto compound walls, carved doors, and in the twentieth century onto canvas by the Nsukka school of painters.

**Nsibidi** — an indigenous ideographic sign-set used as a ceremonial script by the *Ekpe* society and the Okonko fraternity, its origin generally attributed to the neighbouring Ejagham, though in the 1900s J. K. Macgregor recorded an Igbo tradition attributing it to the Uguakima or Uyanga. It existed among the Igbo before the sixteenth century but contracted into secrecy once the societies adopted it. Nsibidi signs appear on cloth, on *ukara* indigo resist-dyed wrappers, on skin and on masquerade costume.

**Ukara** — the indigo resist-dyed cloth of Ekpe title-holders, stitch-resisted in a grid of compartments each carrying a nsibidi sign or a figurative emblem (leopard, hand, manilla), worn as a wrapper or hung as a society backdrop. Production is a cross-border Igbo–Ejagham undertaking, the dyeing historically done by specialists outside the Igbo heartland.

**Ogodo and george wrappers** — the two-piece women's wrapper set, the lower and upper cloths, which in the Delta and Onitsha trading towns absorbed imported Indian and Manchester textiles into an entirely Igbo grammar of how cloth is tied, layered and displayed.

**Motif vocabulary.** *Uli* repertoire and its relatives: the coiled spiral, concentric arcs, the crescent moon, the python (*eke*) whose killing was forbidden under Nri law, the lizard, kola-nut lobes, the four-day market week rendered as a cross or lozenge, interlaced ropework, the leopard (*agu*) of Ekpe rank, the manilla currency ring, and the *ofo* staff form.

### Clothing & dress

Everyday Igbo dress is built around the wrapper. Women wear a two-piece set — a lower *ogodo* and a shorter upper cloth — over a fitted blouse, with a stiff head-tie (*ichafu*) knotted high; the more prestigious the occasion, the taller and more architectural the tie. Coral and glass-bead necklaces mark status. Men wear a single wrapper knotted at the waist with a shirt or, since the colonial century, the *isiagu* — a velvet or damask tunic printed with a repeating lion's or leopard's head, worn with a red cap. The red cap (*okpu agu*) is not decorative: it signals title, and among many communities may be worn only by those who have taken one. *Ozo* title-holders carry additional insignia — an ivory or brass anklet, a horsetail whisk, an eagle feather in the cap, and the *ofo* staff of ritual authority. Maiden's dress for dances of the *agbogho mmuo* season was minimal, the body itself carrying *uli* drawing and camwood (*ufie*) rubbed red, with beaded waistbands at the hips.

### Architecture

The Igbo build in mud, clay, wood, bamboo, thatch and palm frond, all locally sourced and suited to a high-rainfall forest climate. The unit is not the house but the compound (*obi* / *ezi*): several dwellings for the members of an extended patrilineage arranged around a central courtyard, modular and expandable as the family grows. Roofs are steeply pitched thatch of palm frond or grass; verandas and raised platforms give shaded outdoor working space; shrines and ancestral altars are built into the compound rather than set apart from it. Doors were carved, and interior and exterior walls could carry *uli* designs painted by women.

Two building types are distinctively Igbo. The **mbari** houses of the Owerri area are large open-sided square shelters packed with life-sized painted mud figures — *Ala* the earth goddess and the thunder and water deities, alongside officials, craftsmen, Europeans, animals, legendary creatures and ancestors. Their construction is a sacred, years-long process, and when a new one is built the old is deliberately left to decay. The **Nsude pyramids**, in modern Enugu State, were ten step-pyramidal stacks of clay and mud — a base 60 ft in circumference and 3 ft high, the next stack 45 ft, and so on to a stick at the summit marking the residence of the god *Ala/Uto* — laid out in parallel groups of five and requiring periodic rebuilding. Wooden signal towers, *ogene* or *isiokwe*, were raised for communication at gatherings.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The **Igbo-Ukwu** finds in Anambra State — over 600 prestige objects from the 9th–10th centuries — are the benchmark: complex cast copper-alloy vessels, pendants, a roped pot and fly-whisk handles of a filigree density unmatched in the region, plus more than 165,000 glass and carnelian beads, the commonest a plant-ash soda-lime glass from Mesopotamia and the eastern Mediterranean, the next a high-lime high-alumina glass from Ife. Iron smelting in the Nsukka region is far older, excavated at Opi to 750 BC and at Lejja to 2,000 BC, and Awka became the byword for Igbo blacksmithing and itinerant smiths. **Igbo pottery** is women's work — burnished water pots, ritual vessels and the *udu*, a clay jug played as a drum. In wood: the *okwa* kola-nut bowl, carved doors and house posts, and the *ikenga*, a horned personal shrine figure embodying a man's right hand, his strength and achievement.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Title and season, not wealth alone, govern Igbo adornment. Coral bead strands — necklaces, wrist and ankle ropes — mark *ozo* men and senior women, alongside heavy brass or ivory anklets that in some communities were cast onto the leg and worn for life. Glass and carnelian beads have a deep local history at Igbo-Ukwu. Body art is the more distinctive register: *uli* drawn on the torso, face, arms and legs in seed dye, fading over a week or two and redrawn for each occasion, and *ufie* camwood paste rubbed over the body for brides and maiden dancers. *Ichi* facial scarification — parallel ridged lines across the forehead and temples — marked Nri-influenced title initiation and appears carved onto *ikenga* figures and *agbogho mmuo* masks, freezing the practice in wood long after it ceased.

## Music & performance

Igbo music is percussion-led and speech-inflected, because the language is tonal and instruments imitate it. The core ensemble draws on the *udu*, a clay pot vessel-drum played by slapping the mouth-hole; the *ekwe*, a slit drum hollowed from a log; the *ogene*, a forged-iron hand bell (single or double) that carries the talking line; the *igba*, a membrane drum; and the *ichaka* rattle. The *opi*, a wind instrument comparable to a flute, and ivory and horn trumpets sound for title-holders. Masking is bound tightly to this music — a masquerade is announced by its own drum pattern before it is seen — and no major mask appears in silence.

The dominant modern genre is **highlife**, the West African fusion of jazz horn arrangement with local rhythm and language, which the Igbo made their own after the civil war: Chief Osita Osadebe, Oliver De Coque, Dr Sir Warrior of the Oriental Brothers, and Bright Chimezie were the leading twentieth-century voices, singing proverb-dense Igbo over guitar lines that translate *ogene* patterns onto strings. Alongside it sit women's *egwu* songs for weddings and childbirth, funeral dirges, and the praise-chanting of title ceremonies, where a soloist names a man's lineage and achievements and a chorus answers.

## Dance & theatre

Igbo performance is largely masquerade theatre. **Agbogho mmuo** — "maiden spirit" — is the best known: young men wear white-faced, delicately featured helmet masks bearing elaborate crested coiffures and *ichi* marks, with appliquéd body-suits, and dance a light, mincing, deliberately feminine step that contrasts with the heavy stamping of the male spirit masks that share the arena. **Mmwo** masks more broadly embody the returning dead, and appear at funerals of title-holders and at dry-season festivals. **Ekpe** (the leopard society, shared with the Cross River peoples) performs in raffia and knitted body-suits, with nsibidi signs drawn on the ground before the dancer as coded instruction. Mask materials run from carved wood to fabric, iron and vegetation.

Masquerade function ranges from religious ritual and secret-society initiation to outright social satire, where a mask names and mocks a living person with an impunity no unmasked speaker would have. Since Christianisation, the masquerade season has attached itself to the Christmas and New Year holidays, when urban Igbo return to their home towns and the age-grade groups that own the masks reassemble.

## Festivals & rituals

The Igbo calendar is its own: a four-day week (Eke, Orie, Afọ, Nkwọ — each a market day), seven weeks to a month, thirteen months to a year with an extra day added in the last, and it still governs market cycles in village and town. The new year opens with *Ọ́nwạ́ M̀bụ́*, "First Moon", in the third week of February, though many communities treat the true start of the year as *Ọ́nwạ́ Ágwụ́*, around June.

The central annual event is the new-yam festival — *Iri Ji* / **Iwa Ji** — held at the end of the rainy season, typically August, when the eldest man or the priest eats the first yam and no one may eat the new crop before him; masquerades, wrestling and the display of the largest tubers follow. Individual towns hold their own *ọmabe*, *ogbagu* and mask festivals in the dry season.

Life-cycle rites are equally structured. Birth is followed by the naming ceremony and the presentation of kola. Marriage proceeds through *iku aka* (enquiry), *igba nkwu* (the wine-carrying, where the bride finds her groom in the crowd and kneels to give him palm wine), and the negotiation of bride-price between the *umunna* — the patrilineage, the load-bearing pillar of Igbo society. Death for a titled elder brings a second burial months later, with mask appearances, cannon fire and the dispersal of his *ofo*. Title-taking — *ozo* and its grades — is the pivotal adult ritual, conferring the red cap, the anklet and the right to speak in assembly, in a society whose politics was famously republican and consultative rather than monarchical, the spiritual authority of the Eze Nri excepted.

## Foodways

Yam is the crop with moral weight — *ji*, the "king of crops", men's farming, and the subject of its own festival — but cassava, cocoyam and rice carry the daily load. The dominant form is a starchy swallow with soup: pounded yam (*utara ji*), cassava *akpu* (fufu), or *garri*, eaten by hand with **ọfe onugbu** (bitterleaf soup, thickened with cocoyam paste and *ogiri* fermented oil-bean condiment), **ọfe egusi** (melon-seed soup), **ọfe oha** (oha leaf), and **ọfe nsala** (white pepper soup, without palm oil). Palm oil colours most of the rest. **Abacha**, shredded cassava dressed with palm-oil paste, *ugba* fermented oil-bean slivers, garden egg and stockfish, is the archetypal Igbo cold dish; **ji mmiri ọkụ** (yam pepper soup) and **ọkpa** — a Bambara-nut pudding steamed in leaves, an Enugu specialty eaten for breakfast — are both strongly regional.

Ritual food is simpler and more fixed. Kola nut (*ọjị*) opens every serious gathering: it is presented, blessed, broken and shared, and the saying *onye wetara ọjị wetara ndụ* — he who brings kola brings life — is the formula. Alligator pepper and *nzu* chalk accompany it. Palm wine (*mmanya ngwọ* from the raffia palm, *mmanya nkwụ* from the oil palm), tapped fresh and drunk the same day, is the drink of every ceremony, above all the *igba nkwu* marriage rite that is named after it. The Nri ritual code forbade eating python, and the abomination of twin births belonged to the same body of law.

## Oral tradition & literature

Igbo verbal art runs on proverbs — *ilu* — which Chinua Achebe rendered as "the palm-oil with which words are eaten", and an elder's authority in the assembly is measured by his command of them. Storytelling (*akụkọ ifo*) is a dry-season, evening, moonlight-square genre, with call-and-response song interludes the children answer; the tortoise *mbe* is the trickster protagonist, greedy, clever and usually punished. Riddles (*agwụgwa*) and praise-names (*aha otuto*) fill out the repertoire, and the *Okwe* strategic betting game carries its own patter.

The written record begins with two eighteenth-century books: C. G. A. Oldendorp's 1777 mission history of St Thomas, St Croix and St John, the first publication to carry any Igbo material, and *The Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah Equiano* (London, 1789), which contains 79 Igbo words and describes daily life in Equiano's home town of Essaka. The modern canon opens with Achebe's *Things Fall Apart* (1959), set in Iguedo among nine villages on the lower Niger, which remains the most widely read account of Igbo life under the arrival of missionaries and colonial administration. A standard written dialect, Central Igbo, was proposed following Ida C. Ward's 1939 dialect survey, drawing on the Owerri and Umuahia varieties including Ohuhu. George Basden's silent black-and-white films of Igbo life from the 1920s and 1930s survive in the British Empire and Commonwealth Collection at Bristol Archives (Ref. 2006/070).

## Language & religion

Igbo is a tonal language of the Igboid cluster within Volta–Niger (Niger-Congo), with hundreds of dialects and related Igboid languages such as Ikwerre and Ekpeye; the deepest split in the group is between Ekpeye and everything else. It is written in Roman script, and formerly in the *Nsibidi* ideograms retained by Ekpe and Okonko. Igbo phonology was influential enough that John Goldsmith used it to argue against the linear model of *The Sound Pattern of English*.

The religious landscape is overwhelmingly Christian following intensive twentieth-century missionary evangelism, which the Igbo embraced alongside Western-style education, with a Muslim minority. The indigenous religion — *Ọdịnala* / *Omenala* — persists in practice and in idiom: *Chukwu* the high god, *Chi* the personal destiny-spirit, *Ala* the earth goddess who owns morality and the harvest, the *alusi* deities served at compound shrines, the *ikenga* of personal achievement, the *ofo* staff of truth, and the ancestors addressed at every kola-breaking. The Nri religio-polity codified this into seven classes of prohibition — human, animal, object, temporal, behavioural, speech and place — binding on all followers of the faith regardless of which local government they lived under.
