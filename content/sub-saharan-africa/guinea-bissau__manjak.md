## Overview

The Mandyak, also called the Manjak or Manjaco, are a West African ethnic group primarily living in Guinea-Bissau, with smaller communities in The Gambia and Senegal. Within Guinea-Bissau, their principal areas are Bassarel and Babok in the northern coastal Cacheu Region. They constitute about 14% of the population of Guinea-Bissau. Their language belongs to the Bak branch of Niger–Congo. The Mandyak are especially documented through their rice-growing landscapes, political organization around wet fields, migration between Guinea-Bissau, Senegal and The Gambia, ancestor shrines, scarification practices and community institutions. Their culture also includes distinctive agricultural engineering in mangrove swamps, social ceremonies such as the Kambatch, and continuing negotiation between Catholicism, Islam, older spirit practices and changing village life.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

They do describe female scarification, which involved intricately scarring women’s torsos. Patterns were drawn by other members of society and tended to focus on women’s breasts. Research conducted during 1936 and a subsequent study in 1960 recorded the practice, while Portuguese observers interpreted it as bodily decoration with little or no further meaning. The practice was reported as more prevalent among older generations of women and had largely declined before the 1960s.

### Architecture

They do document villages organized under local leaders and describe the destruction of many farms and houses during the Portuguese conquest and the following year. Rice cultivation shaped the built environment: the Mandyak and other groups developed *bolanhas*, a system of dikes, drainage canals and rice paddies within mangrove swamps. The sources describe these agricultural systems as complex and efficient.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do describe carved wooden ancestor posts known as *pitchap*. These posts appear to represent individual people but are understood in the source as collective representations of ancestors in general. Mandyak households in the traditional homeland may have an ancestor shrine made from these carved wooden posts.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The principal body-adornment practice documented in the sources is female scarification. Before the 1960s, women’s torsos were intricately scarred, with patterns often concentrated on the breasts. An Austrian photographer, Hugo Bernatzik, documented the practice through photographs of women in the region.

## Music & performance

They do mention Catholic missionaries who encouraged young people to perform cultural skits. These activities contributed to the development of the Culture Development Club in the Bassarel region during the 1980s. The club also emerged from earlier *banju* practices, described as spaces where young unmarried men lived and discussed village life.

## Dance & theatre

Catholic missionaries are described as having encouraged young people to perform cultural skits, and the Culture Development Club later incorporated cultural activity into its efforts to make village life attractive to young men. No specific plays, dances, characters, costumes or ceremonial distinctions are identified.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document the Kambatch, a tradition among Mandyak men that occurs every quarter century. During the ceremony, men are initiated into the group and conduct a government-style discussion about customs and traditions that should be revisited, changed or abolished.

At the Kambatch of 1986, the men decided to change the practice of groom service and the existence of a cult of divination. Groom service involved young boys or men working in the fields of their future bride’s family, usually for several years before marriage. The Kambatch established consent from both parties as the criterion for marriage. The men also rejected the convictions made by women associated with the divination cult, who claimed to serve as human vessels for evil spirits and to explain infertility or infanticide.

Ancestor rites are another documented part of Mandyak religious life. Offerings begin with water and palm wine poured on the *pitchap* shrines. A woman cannot initiate the conversation with the ancestors; a man must begin it before she can speak. Men generally ask for peace over the household, while women discuss daily events and update the ancestors about village and family life. When Mandyak die outside their homeland or community, they are described as being reborn at home as ancestors in a shrine.

## Foodways

Rice is the central documented Mandyak staple and also the basis of historical political and economic organization. Most people participated in rice production, while local landowners and higher-ranking leaders received portions of the harvest. The *bolanhas* system used dikes, drainage canals and rice paddies in mangrove swamps to cultivate rice.

The sources also associate the region’s economy with palm-oil forests and peanut farms. Palm wine is documented in a ritual context: it is poured onto ancestor shrines together with water before questions and conversations are addressed to the ancestors.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do describe forms of community discussion. In *banju* spaces, young unmarried men lived together and discussed village life. The Kambatch likewise involved a formal discussion among men about customs and traditions.

The sources also record contemporary community activity through the Culture Development Club, founded in the 1980s in the Bassarel region. Its members sought to preserve selected elements of Mandyak culture while eliminating practices they considered backward and contributing to the departure of young men from village life. The club supported elderly farmers, helped mothers cultivate crops, purchased school supplies and organized club events. No specific literary revival or documentation project is named.

## Language & religion

Mandyak, also called Manjak, Manjack or Njak, is a Bak language of Guinea-Bissau and Senegal, also known as Kanyop. In 2006, the total number of speakers was estimated at 315,300, including 184,000 in Guinea-Bissau, 105,000 in Senegal and 26,300 in The Gambia. The dialects listed in the sources include Bok, Likes-Utsia, Cur, Lund, Yu and Unhate. Other listed varieties are Canchungo, Baboque, Churo, Pecixe and Calequisse. The official spelling system established by the Senegalese government is regulated by Decree No. 2005-983 of 21 October 2005.

The majority of Mandyak practice Christianity, specifically Catholicism, while a small minority follow Islam. The sources also describe animist beliefs and the prominent role of spirits, although many young people have become skeptical of them. Ancestor shrines, offerings of water and palm wine, spirit possession and divination are documented elements of Mandyak religious life.
