## Overview

The Somali are a Cushitic-speaking people native to the Somali Peninsula — the arid thornbush plateau and coastal plain stretching from the Gulf of Aden south to the Jubba and Shabelle river valleys, and inland across the Haud grazing plateau and the Ogaden. They number roughly 17.6 million in Somalia, 5.7 million in Somaliland, 4.6 million in Ethiopia, 2.8 million in Kenya and 586,000 in Djibouti, occupying one of the most expansive single-ethnicity landmasses in Africa. Their language belongs to the Lowland East Cushitic branch of Afroasiatic, closest to Afar and Saho. In folk-culture terms the Somali matter for two reasons that pull against each other: a camel-pastoral material world engineered for portability — the collapsible *aqal*, coiled fibre vessels, woven mats — and a set of coastal urban crafts at Mogadishu, Merca, Barawa, Zeila and Berbera shaped by a millennium of Indian Ocean trade in frankincense, myrrh, ivory and cloth.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Benadiri weaving** — the loom tradition of the Benadir coast (Mogadishu, Merca, Barawa), worked on narrow treadle looms by hereditary weaver communities in the old stone towns. Cotton was locally grown along the Shabelle; the cloth fed both domestic use and export, and Mogadishu textiles were traded across the western Indian Ocean. It is the one Somali textile tradition that is urban, guild-like and settled rather than pastoral.

**Alindi** — the best-known Benadiri cloth: a cotton wrapper patterned in warp-direction bands of stripes, produced in Merca and Mogadishu and worn as a woman's body-wrap. Its identity lies in the striping — fields of contrasting narrow and broad bands rather than the figural or tie-dye work of Swahili *kanga* further south.

**Guntiino** — the older Somali woman's garment: a long rectangle of cloth wound around the torso and knotted over one shoulder, leaving the other bare. In the interior it was made of plain or striped cotton (*alindi* when the wearer could afford Benadiri work); it predates the imported *dirac* and survives in rural and ceremonial use.

**Shuqqad** — the men's wrapped cloth of the peninsula, a length of cotton wound at the waist and worn in the pastoral interior and coastal towns alike, plain white or narrow-striped.

**Somali basketry** — the dominant fibre art, and effectively the pattern gallery of the nomadic camp. Coiled and twined vessels and mats are built from split palm leaf (*caws*, *doum* palm) and grass over a grass bundle core, dyed in bands of red, black, ochre and natural straw. Forms include the lidded conical milk and grain container, the tightly coiled bowl whose weave is dense enough to hold liquid, and the large woven mats that clad the *aqal* frame. Unlike the figurative coiled work of the Great Lakes, Somali patterning is strictly geometric and organised in horizontal registers.

**Motif vocabulary.** Banded horizontal registers; triangles and stepped triangles; lozenges and diamond chains; zigzag and chevron; the diagonal cross; checkerboard blocks; toothed or sawtooth edges; concentric lozenge fields; alternating dyed and undyed bands. Metalwork and leather carry the same vocabulary: incised and punched rosettes, dotted grounds, and, on *kitab* cases and amulets, the Arabic inscription used as ornament.

### Clothing & dress

Women's older dress is the *guntiino*, a wrapped and shoulder-knotted cloth; the *dirac* — a long, light voile or polyester shift worn over a full slip (*gorgorad*) — has become the standard festive garment. Married women cover the hair with the *shash*, a headscarf tied at the nape, and add the *garbasaar* shawl over the shoulders; the veiling styles *hijab* and *jilbaab* are widespread. Men wear the *macawis* (the *shuqqad* sarong-wrap) with a shirt, or the long *khamiis*; on the head the embroidered skullcap *koofiyad*, and among elders a turban (*duub*) of white cloth. Pastoralists carry the *billaawe*, a leaf-bladed dagger, sheathed at the waist on a leather belt — historically both tool and sidearm, its hilt of horn, wood or silver-mounted. Sandals are cut from camel or cow hide. Ceremonial difference is largely a matter of quality and quantity: imported cloth, silver, and for a bride the heaviest load of jewellery and henna she will ever wear.

### Architecture

The nomadic house is the *aqal* (*aqal soomaali*), a domed, portable frame dwelling built and owned by women. Bent saplings are lashed into a hemispherical armature and clad with woven mats (*dhesig*, *harar*) and hides; the whole structure disassembles into camel loads and is re-erected by the women of the household in a few hours. Settlement is the *reer* — a household camp ringed by a thorn-branch enclosure (*odaa*) that folds the livestock in at night. In the agricultural riverine south, *mundul* houses of wattle-and-daub with conical thatched roofs are the norm, alongside rectangular flat-roofed *arish*.

The coast is a different world. Mogadishu's Hamar Weyne and Shangani quarters, Merca, Barawa and Zeila are stone towns built of coral rag and lime plaster, with carved wooden doors, plastered niches, roof terraces and internal courtyards — the Benadiri variant of the wider Swahili-coast stone architecture. Zeila's two-mihrab **Masjid al-Qiblatayn** dates to the 7th century and is among the oldest mosques in Africa. Inland, the Adal and Ajuran periods left dressed-stone tombs, tumuli and fortified enclosures, and the Ajuran Sultanate is remembered for hydraulic works — wells, cisterns and stone-lined channels along the Shabelle.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

The signature object is the *dabqaad* (also *haaqad* or *girgire*) — the incense burner cut from soft white or grey meerschaum-like stone quarried near Mogadishu, a square-footed cup carved with incised geometric bands, used to burn frankincense (*lubaan*) and *uunsi* in every household. **Somali woodcarving** covers milk vessels, spoons, camel saddle parts, ladles and the *barkin*, the hardwood headrest with an incised arched foot that lets a pastoralist sleep without disturbing a dressed coiffure. Camels carry the *koor*, the wooden camel bell, its clapper cut from hard wood and its tone distinguishing one herd from another. **Somali leatherwork** produces water vessels, sandals, sheaths and amulet cases. *Billaawe* daggers, spearheads and knives come from smith lineages working imported iron; the *Somali kitab* case — a tooled leather or silver container for a Qur'anic text — is the most ornamented small object in the repertoire.

### Jewelry & body adornment

**Somali silver** is worked into filigree and granulated necklaces, heavy hollow bangles, anklets, ear ornaments and the *xirsi* or *tumaar* — an amulet case, cylindrical or box-shaped, containing a written Qur'anic verse and worn on a cord at the neck or upper arm. Coral, amber, carnelian and imported trade beads are strung with silver spacers along the coast. The *xagaa*-season and wedding repertoire adds forehead ornaments and multiple bangles worn in sets. Bridal preparation centres on *cadaan* henna (*xinne*): dense black or red patterning of hands and feet, applied over several evenings in a women's gathering before the wedding. Hair is braided in close-set rows or coiled and covered with the *shash* after marriage; the wooden comb with a long tapering handle and an incised or pierced back is both a grooming tool and a carved keepsake.

## Music & performance

Somali music is built on voice and rhythm rather than large ensembles. The core instrument is the *kaban* (oud), adopted from Arabia and central to twentieth-century urban song; the lyre *shareero* is the older indigenous chordophone, and the flute *sumaari* and reed pipe *durbaan* survive in pastoral use. Drums include the *durbaan* frame and barrel drums and the *jabuu*. Handclapping and stamped rhythm carry much of the pulse.

The defining genre is sung poetry. Classical alliterative forms — *gabay*, *jiifto*, *geeraar*, *buraanbur* — are chanted or half-sung rather than accompanied, and *buraanbur* is specifically the women's metre, performed at weddings and namings with drum and clapping. The work-song repertoire is enormous and occupational: *hees-hawleed* for watering camels at the well, for churning milk, for weaving mats, for lulling a camel into accepting a calf. From the 1940s onward the short lyric *heello* and *hees* forms, sung to oud, drum and later full band, became the vehicle of the nationalist and independence period, and the state radio ensembles of Mogadishu and Hargeisa — Waaberi foremost among them — carried Somali song to a mass audience. Sufi *dhikr* chanting, particularly Qadiriyya, is the main religious performance context.

## Dance & theatre

Somali dance is communal, line- or circle-formed, and tied to specific occasions rather than to a professional performer class. **Dhaanto** is the best-known: a Ogaden-associated dance in which paired lines advance and retreat with a bouncing step whose rhythm imitates a camel's gait, sung antiphonally between men's and women's lines; it became the signature dance of the Dervish period and remains the national dance in practice. **Niiko** is a women's dance built on rapid hip and shoulder movement, performed at weddings in women's-only gatherings. **Ciyaar soomaali** covers the broader repertoire of clan and regional line dances, including the *jaandheer* of the northwest with its long striding step and *shirib*. Wedding celebration is the principal secular performance context, with *buraanbur* poetry, drum and dance running over several nights.

Staged theatre is a twentieth-century urban form, and a strong one: the musical play (*riwaayad*) developed in Mogadishu and Hargeisa from the 1950s, combining spoken drama with sung *heello*, and became the main vehicle for political satire and social commentary.

## Festivals & rituals

The calendar is Islamic. *Eid al-Fitr* (*Ciid*) closes Ramadan and *Eid al-Adha* (*Ciid-Adha*) brings the sacrifice of a sheep, goat or camel and the distribution of meat; both are marked by new clothes, henna, and *xalwo* sweets. *Mawlid* — the Prophet's birthday — is celebrated with Sufi *dhikr*, recitation and communal meals at shrines, and individual saints' commemorations (*siyaaro*) draw pilgrimage to the tombs of Qadiriyya and Salihiyya shaykhs.

Alongside these runs an older solar reckoning. *Dabshid* (the "fire-lighting", also called *Neeroosh* in Mogadishu) marks the Somali solar new year with bonfires, the leaping of the flame, and in coastal towns a mock stick-fight; its timing follows the pre-Islamic Cushitic solar calendar rather than the lunar one. The seasonal cycle itself — *gu* (spring rains), *xagaa* (dry), *dayr* (short rains), *jiilaal* (harsh dry) — governs migration, marriage timing and the great gatherings at wells.

Life-cycle rites: a naming ceremony (*magac-bixin*) on the seventh day after birth with animal sacrifice (*caqiiqo*) and the burning of incense; a mother's forty-day seclusion (*afartanbax*), ended with a celebration, new clothes and *malmal* smoke-bathing. Weddings run several days — *meher* (contract), the bride's henna night, the *aroos* feast, and the *xeedho*, a Darod-associated riddle-object: a tightly bound, hide-wrapped vessel of dates and ghee that the groom's party must unwrap by solving its knot, accompanied by poetic contest. Funerals follow Islamic burial within a day, with three days of condolence (*tacsi*).

## Foodways

The pastoral base is milk and meat. Camel milk (*caano geel*) is drunk fresh and soured; *garoor* is soured camel milk, and ghee (*subag*) is rendered and stored in woven or leather vessels. Meat is grilled or stewed; *hilib ari* (goat) and camel meat are festival food, and *muqmad* (*oodkac*) is meat cut into cubes, dried and preserved in ghee for travel — the classic pastoral provision. Grain comes in as sorghum (*hadhuudh*) and maize in the riverine south, and imported rice and pasta on the coast.

The everyday breads are *canjeero* (also *lahoh*), a fermented sorghum or flour pancake close to Ethiopian *injera* but smaller and thinner, eaten at breakfast with ghee and sugar or with stew; *sabaayad*, a layered flatbread of Indian *paratha* descent; and *muufo*, a sorghum bread baked on the wall of a clay oven. The signature rice dish is *bariis iskukaris* — rice cooked with meat stock, onion, and the spice blend *xawaash* (cumin, coriander, cardamom, cinnamon, clove, turmeric), garnished with fried banana on the side, a Benadiri habit. Italian colonial rule left *baasto* (pasta) as a genuinely Somali lunch dish, sauced and eaten with banana. *Suqaar* is diced meat sautéed with vegetables. Sweets: *xalwo* (a dense ghee-and-sugar confection flavoured with cardamom and studded with nuts, the obligatory celebration food) and *shushumow*.

Tea is *shaah* — black tea boiled with cardamom, cinnamon, clove and ginger, milked and heavily sugared. Coffee carries a distinct northern form, *bun*, in which whole beans are roasted and eaten in ghee, and *qaxwo*, brewed from husk and bean. Food is halal; pork and alcohol are excluded. Frankincense is burned in the *dabqaad* after eating.

## Oral tradition & literature

Somalia's standing epithet is "a nation of poets", and the claim is structural rather than sentimental: until 1972 Somali had no official script, and poetry carried law, genealogy, news, feud and diplomacy. The prestige forms are alliterative and metrically strict — *gabay* (the longest and most serious, used for political argument and lament), *geeraar* (war and horsemanship), *jiifto*, and *buraanbur* (the women's metre). Every line of a poem alliterates on a single consonant, sustained for hundreds of lines and memorised verbatim by reciters. Poems travelled by chain of memorisers across the peninsula and were answered in kind, producing sustained public debates in verse known as *silsilad* — the *Guba* chain of the mid-twentieth century is the most famous. **Sayyid Mohammed Abdullah Hassan**, who led the twenty-one-year Dervish resistance against the British, is remembered as much for his poetry as for his campaigns.

Prose narrative includes *sheeko xariiro* — folktales told to children, with a repertoire of trickster hyena and fox tales — plus riddles (*halxiraale*), proverbs (*maahmaah*) and genealogical recitation (*abtirsiinyo*), in which a person names their ancestors back twenty or more generations. Written scholarly tradition is Arabic-language and religious: *manaqib* hagiographies of Sufi shaykhs and *qasida* devotional verse. The Latin-script orthography adopted in 1972, and the mass literacy campaign that followed, moved this corpus into print for the first time; Nuruddin Farah's novels and the archival work of Hargeisa and diaspora collectors continue it.

## Language & religion

Somali (*Af-Soomaali*) is a Lowland East Cushitic language of the Afroasiatic family, closest to Afar and Saho. Its dialects divide into Northern/Common Somali (the basis of the standard), Benadiri (the coastal urban variety) and Maay, spoken by Rahanweyn groups in the interriverine south and divergent enough that mutual intelligibility with the standard is limited. Before 1972 Somali was written variously in Arabic script (*wadaad* writing), in the indigenous *Osmanya* alphabet devised by Osman Yusuf Kenadid around 1920, and in Borama and Kaddare scripts; the 1972 reform fixed a Latin orthography.

Somalis are overwhelmingly Sunni Muslim of the Shafi'i school, and were among the earliest non-Arab converts — Zeila's Masjid al-Qiblatayn dates to the 7th century. Sufi orders shape popular religion: the Qadiriyya, Ahmadiyya and Salihiyya, with *dhikr* assemblies, shrine visitation (*siyaaro*) to saints' tombs, and the religious specialist (*wadaad*) who writes amulets, teaches Qur'an and adjudicates alongside the secular elder (*oday*). Customary law, *xeer*, operates in parallel with *sharia* and governs compensation (*diya*), grazing rights and water access.
