## Overview

The Tallensi farm millet and sorghum as staples and keep cattle, sheep and goats. The ordinary domestic unit is the polygamous joint family, with descent and inheritance patrilineal. Their ritual life centres on the tension between a man and his first-born son, expressed through strict avoidances from age five or six. Clan ancestors are incarnate in sacred crocodiles in particular pools, which no Tallensi would kill or injure.

## Material culture

Tallensi material culture shows skilled work in raffia and straw weaving, leather ornamentation, carved wooden weapons, and sculptural pottery pipes.

### Textile & pattern traditions

Woven textiles in raffia and natural fibres carry geometric motifs in red, white, black and yellow.

- **Raffia band weaving** — A hand-woven weft-faced plain-weave band with a fringe in undyed raffia with dark brown and pink, bordered by alternating check and banding, with a row of triangles running down the centre.
- **Bast-fibre dress** — A garment of vegetable fibre in red, white and black built as a central woven portion with a loose fringe hanging on either side.
- **Straw-twine belting** — Belts twisted from straw twine made of separate red and black strings wrapped at intervals with yellow.

### Clothing & dress

Two garment classes appear in the record: fringed bast-fibre dresses and wrapped straw-twine belts, with ritual dress including the cap and tunic.

- **Cap** — A first-born son may not wear his father's cap from age five or six, but puts it on for the first time at his father's funeral.
- **Tunic** — A first-born son may not wear his father's tunic from age five or six, but puts it on for the first time at his father's funeral.

### Architecture

Households live in walled compounds where the entrance and granary are ritually charged places.

- **Compound entrance** — Once a first-born son reaches adolescence he may not meet his father in the compound entrance.
- **Granary** — A son may not look inside his father's granary during the father's lifetime, and at the funeral an elder shows him the inside for the first time.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery smoking pipes are sculptural and numerous, modelled as human heads and figures; weapons include carved throwing clubs and arrows with barbed iron heads.

- **Pottery smoking pipes** — Bowls modelled as a human head or face, one as a seated female figure with cicatrices, and plainer bowls carrying girth grooves, hatched diamonds, or notches.
- **Throwing clubs** — Carved from wood with a three-knobbed crooked head, a pineapple head, or as a flat L-shaped piece notched with serrations at the bend.
- **Quiver and arrows** — A wood quiver covered in leather, snake skin and panels of red-dyed woollen cloth holds more than twenty arrows with cane shafts, barbed iron heads and bindings of fibre and reptile skin.
- **Terracotta standing female figure** — A standing female figure with hands on the hips.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Metal ornaments include cast brass leg-bands and finger-rings with geometric patterning; scarification marks appear on objects as cicatrices and painted chest marks.

- **Brass leg-band** — A cast brass leg-band in the form of an oval ring bent upwards at both ends, with a conical projection at one end and chevron patterning around its upper and lower edges.
- **Brass finger-ring** — A brass finger-ring with an upright hollow bezel, terminating in a button bound with brass wire and flanked by applied and serrated bands.

## Festivals & rituals

Ritual life centres on the ambivalence between a man and his first-born, expressed through avoidances that begin at age five or six and culminate at the father's funeral.

- **First-born avoidances** — A first-born son may not eat from his father's dish, wear his cap or tunic, carry his quiver, use his bow or look into his granary; in adolescence he may not meet his father in the compound entrance.
- **Daughter's avoidance** — A first-born daughter is barred from looking into her mother's storage pot.
- **Father's funeral** — The first-born son and daughter lead the rites; the son dons the cap and tunic, and an elder carrying the dead man's bow shows him the granary's interior.
- **Sacred crocodiles** (*ni-saal*) — Particular crocodiles in particular pools are held to be persons and incarnations of important clan ancestors; no Tallensi would kill or injure one.

## Foodways

Millet and sorghum are staples, supplemented by cattle, sheep and goats; crocodiles from dry-season fishing rivers are eaten.

- **Staple crops** — Millet and sorghum are the staple crops.
- **Livestock** — Cattle, sheep and goats are kept on a small scale.
- **First-born food prohibition** — A first-born son may not eat from the same dish as his father from the age of five or six.

## Language & religion

Religious life is ancestral: fulfilment lies in being venerated as an ancestor, and the recently dead father mediates between the living and older ancestors.

- **Ancestor veneration** — A man's fulfilment lies in being venerated as an ancestor, and sacrifice to the ancestors is the duty of the ritually adult first-born son.
- **Tongnaab** — A fertility deity associated with the Tallensi.

## Glossary

- *ni-saal* — Sacred crocodile; a person and clan ancestor incarnate
- *Talni* — Tallensi language; a dialect of Farefare in the Gur branch of Niger-Congo
- *Tongnaab* — Fertility deity associated with the Tallensi
- *Gur* — Language branch of Niger-Congo family to which Tallensi belongs
- *Niger-Congo* — Major language family to which Tallensi language belongs
- *Cicatrices* — Scarification marks on face and body
- *Patrilineal* — Descent and inheritance traced through the father's line
- *First-born* — Child born first, subject to ritual avoidances and responsibilities
- *Raffia* — Natural fibre from palm used for band weaving
- *Bast-fibre* — Vegetable fibre used to make dress garments
- *Straw-twine* — Twisted straw used to make belts
- *Barbed iron heads* — Arrow points made of iron with sharp projections
- *Chevron* — V-shaped band pattern used in ornamentation
- *Weft-faced* — Weaving technique where horizontal threads show on the surface
- *Plain-weave* — Basic weaving technique alternating over and under threads
- *Girth grooves* — Parallel grooves carved around the body of pottery pipes
- *Hatched diamonds* — Diamond shapes with parallel lines used as decoration
- *Quiver* — Container for holding and carrying arrows
