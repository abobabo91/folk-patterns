## Overview

The Gumuz, also spelled Gumaz and Gumz, are an ethnic group speaking a Nilo-Saharan language. They inhabit the Benishangul-Gumuz Region in western Ethiopia and the Fazogli region of Sudan. Most Ethiopian speakers live in Kamashi Zone and Metekel Zone, while Sudanese speakers live east of Er Roseires, around Famaka and Fazoglo on the Blue Nile, extending north along the border. In 2025, the total Gumuz population was around 250,000, including 159,418 people in Ethiopia and around 88,000 in Sudan. Gumuz culture is documented through shifting cultivation, sorghum foodways, hunting and gathering, clan organization, conflict-resolution institutions, traditional religious practices, scarification, earthenware production, and oral and linguistic traditions.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

**Motif vocabulary.** The sources name no textile motifs.

### Clothing & dress

They do state that the Gumuz traditionally adorned their bodies with scarifications, but this custom is disappearing through government pressure and education.

### Architecture

Gumuz cereal crops are stored in granaries decorated with clay lumps imitating female breasts. Spirits called *mus'a* are thought to dwell in houses, granaries, fields, trees, and mountains.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Gumuz cooking and brewing are carried out in earthen pots made by women. Sorghum is used to prepare porridge called *nga* and to brew beer called *kea*. They mention bows and arrows as hunting equipment and state that this custom survives today.

### Jewelry & body adornment

They state that the Gumuz traditionally adorned their bodies with scarifications, a practice that is disappearing through government pressure and education.

## Music & performance

They mention field recordings from 1980 of traditional music of the Gumuz ethnic group in Sudan’s Blue Nile State, but provide no further description of the music or the instruments used.

## Festivals & rituals

They state that most Gumuz maintain traditional religious practices, while those living near the Sudanese borderland converted to Islam and a few are Christians. Spirits called *mus'a* are believed to dwell in houses, granaries, fields, trees, and mountains, and ritual specialists are called *gafea*. All Gumuz are organized in clans, and feuds between clans are commonly resolved through an institution called *mangema* or *michu*, depending on the region. Marriage is through sister exchange, as it used to be among the Sudanese Uduk.

## Foodways

The Gumuz practice shifting cultivation, and sorghum is their staple food. Sorghum is used to prepare porridge called *nga* and to brew beer called *kea*. All cooking and brewing are carried out in earthen pots made by women. Gumuz people also hunt wild animals, including duikers and warthogs, and gather honey, wild fruits, roots, and seeds.

## Oral tradition & literature

According to Gumuz traditions, in earlier times the people inhabited the western parts of the province of Gojjam before being progressively banished to the area of the Blue Nile and its tributaries by more powerful neighbors, the Amhara and Agaw. The Gumuz have traditionally been grouped with other Nilotic peoples living along the Sudanese-Ethiopian border under the collective name *Shanqella*; as *Shanquella*, they are mentioned by Scottish explorer James Bruce in *Travels to Discover the Source of the Nile*, published in 1790.

## Language & religion

Gumuz is a dialect cluster spoken along the border of Ethiopia and Sudan and has been tentatively classified within the Nilo-Saharan family. Varieties are not all mutually intelligible, and grammatical forms differ between northern and southern Gumuz. The sources list Guba, Wenbera, Sirba, Agalo, Yaso, Mandura, Dibate, and Metemma as Gumuz dialects, with Mandura, Dibate, and Metemma forming a dialect cluster. Daats'iin, discovered in 2013, is described as a distinct language, while the poorly attested Sudanese varieties are likely a distinct language called Kadallu. An early record is a wordlist from the Mount Guba area compiled in February 1883 by Juan Maria Schuver. Gumuz has ejective consonants, implosives, palatal consonants, and high and low tones with downstep. Most Gumuz maintain traditional religious practices; some communities converted to Islam, and a few are Christians. Their spirits are called *mus'a*, and ritual specialists are called *gafea*.
