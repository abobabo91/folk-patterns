## Overview

The Murle are a Surmic ethnic group inhabiting Pibor County and the Boma area in Greater Pibor Administrative Area, South Sudan, as well as parts of southwestern Ethiopia. Murle oral traditions describe a mythical origin at *Jen*, located beyond Maji on the Boma plateau, followed by migration southward along the Omo River to Lake Rudolf, now Lake Turkana. From there, traditions recall movement through Kolobaadh in Taposa territory, over Mount Kathiangor, also called Kather, and into the Maruwa Hills, with some groups continuing to Boma. The Murle speak the Murle language, a member of the Surmic language family. Their pastoral way of life, oral traditions, cattle-based marriage system, and material culture are documented unevenly in the supplied sources.

## Music & performance

Murle oral traditions are preserved in songs and myths. One song recalls the creation of Murle humanity at *Jen*, while another version connects the first union between spirit and woman with the birth of Murimaan. The sources also state that a song about creation was still performed at the time of the account. Murle songs and myths describe *Jen* as a mythical Eden and associate it with the arrival of the ancestors, black cattle, and sweet tobacco.

The supplied sources do not name Murle instruments, musical genres, dances performed with music, or performance institutions. They do record that the rat has a ceremonial association with Murle funerary rites, particularly the deaths of Kelenya chiefs and their senior wives. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription concerns this ethnic group.

## Festivals & rituals

The supplied sources do not identify an annual Murle festival calendar or provide dates for seasonal celebrations. They describe several ritual and religious associations. In Murle cosmology, *Jen* is venerated as a sacred point of origin and symbolizes the East, the source of life, rainfall, and ancestral memory. The rat has an enduring ceremonial association with funerary rites involving Kelenya chiefs and their senior wives.

Marriage is connected to bride wealth: men can marry only after paying several dozens of cows. The sources describe this system as a major factor in Murle social and economic life. They also state that Murle practice a blend of animism and Christianity, and that elders and witches often function as trouble fixers. The supplied sources do not describe initiation ceremonies, birth rites, wedding procedures, annual observances, or named funerals in detail.

## Oral tradition & literature

Murle oral traditions, recorded by Bazzet Lewis, describe the community’s origins and migrations. *Jen*, beyond Maji on the Boma plateau, is presented as a sacred place of origin. One creation account describes women descending from the heavens to Jen, where a woman encountered a spirit hidden among grass. A second woman later gave birth to Murimaan, who became the founding ancestor of the Murle. Another version names Abei as the grandmother of all mankind and Murimaan as the progenitor of Murle chiefs.

The traditions also refer to Jok, a great spirit, and Tammu, a term denoting God, rain, and the sky. Other narratives concern Manidherbo, the Pleiades, and Etiwur stories about the discovery of animals. The sources state that these episodes are more fragmented and contain internal contradictions. Murle songs and myths also preserve memories of migration along the Omo River, through Kolobaadh, over Mount Kathiangor, and into the Maruwa Hills.

## Language & religion

Murle, also called Ajibba, Beir, Merule, Mourle, or Murule, is a Surmic language spoken by the Murle people in southeastern South Sudan near the Ethiopian border. A very small number of Murle live across the border in southwestern Ethiopia. Murle clauses have basic verb–subject–object order, and the morphology of the verb agrees with the person and number of the subject and can also indicate those of the object. Number marking on nouns is complex, and vowel length is distinctive. The New Testament has been translated into Murle.

The Murle are described as practicing a blend of animism and Christianity. Their cosmology includes Jok and Tammu, who are sometimes described as distinct but are often used interchangeably by Murle speakers. Jok is described as masculine and resident in heaven, while Tammu is more frequently invoked in general discourse. Elders and witches often function as trouble fixers.
