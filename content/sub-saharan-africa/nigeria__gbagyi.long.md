## Overview

The Gbagyi (also *Gbari*; plural *Agbagyi*/*Agbari*) are a Middle Belt Nigerian people of the granite-strewn savanna between the Kaduna and Gurara rivers, occupying western Abuja and the Federal Capital Territory, southern Niger State, Chikun LGA in Kaduna State, and parts of Nasarawa State. Their towns include Minna, Suleja, Kwali, Ushafa, Bwari, Paiko, Gwada and Abaji, and Zuma Rock stands among their ancestral spiritual landmarks. Population estimates run to about 12 million across four states and thirty local government areas. Two dialects — Gbari (*Gwari yamma*) and Gbagyi — belong to the Nupoid group within Niger-Congo, classed by Kay Williamson in Benue-Congo. Their ethnonym was rendered *Gwari* by Hausa, Fulani and European outsiders; the people prefer Gbagyi/Gbari. They matter to folk-culture studies above all for hand-built pottery: Ladi Kwali, the most internationally known African potter of the twentieth century, was Gbagyi.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Gbagyi textile record is narrower and better attested in dress than in loom technique; the group's decorative genius runs through clay, gourd and grass. Pattern-bearing traditions:

**Indigo resist cloth** — Old dark blue cloth (*yan bula* in regional trade parlance; simply "the blue cloth" to Gbagyi) dyed in sunken indigo pits of the kind worked across the Hausa-Nupe-Gbagyi belt, worn by many generations and originally wrapped about the loins. In the post-colonial period the same blue cloth was cut into trousers and skirts and paired with white shirts.

**Mizhin Gbagyizanu cloth** — The modern black-and-white ethnic-identity print, factory-woven and roller-printed, carrying images of women bearing loads *on their shoulders* and the legend *Mizhin Gbagyizanu*, "we are Gbagyi people." It is worn at cultural days, Gbagyi association meetings and funerals, and is the clearest case anywhere in Nigeria of a printed wrapper functioning as an ethnographic statement about posture.

**Zana grass screen** — Panels of tall guinea grass stems bound in parallel rows with cord, used as compound fencing, bathing enclosures and granary skirts; the binding rhythm produces a banded surface pattern that is the architectural counterpart of pot roulette work.

**Raffia and grass basketry** — Coiled and plaited trays, lidded storage baskets and beer-pot covers, worked in raffia and sorghum stalk, with contrast achieved by dyed strips rather than by float weaving.

**Calabash pyrography** — Gourd bowls, beer dippers and covers decorated by incision and hot-iron burning, the blackened lines standing against the pale gourd wall. Calabash is also ritually governed: a broken calabash may not be used to fetch potting clay.

**Motif vocabulary.** Twisted-cord roulette bands (rocked and rolled impressions in wet clay), incised chevron chains, combed parallel scoring, raised clay nodes and bosses, lizard and serpent relief figures (Ladi Kwali's signature), fish and scorpion silhouettes, concentric neck grooves, burnished-versus-matte zoning, the shoulder-carried-pot figure on printed cloth.

### Clothing & dress

Daily dress historically centred on the indigo loincloth for both sexes, with women wrapping a longer length at the waist; the length worn to the ankle marks seniority. Contemporary everyday dress follows the northern Nigerian register — men in *babbar riga* gown or the shorter embroidered tunic over drawstring trousers, with an embroidered cap; Muslim Gbagyi men adopt the *hula* skullcap, while Christian men, who form the majority, wear the same tunic without it. Women wear the *zani* wrapper with a matching blouse and head-tie, the head-tie tied high for ceremonies. The distinctive Gbagyi marker is not a garment but a carrying posture: loads are borne on the shoulder, never the head, because the head is held to be "the king of the entire body" and must not be burdened — and that posture became the emblem printed on the black-and-white cultural cloth. Ceremonial dance dress, the *knunu* regalia, adds brass anklets, bells, cowrie-strung waistbands and fibre or grass skirting over the wrapper.

### Architecture

The Gbagyi compound (*gida* in regional usage) is a cluster of round mud-walled huts under conical thatch, ringed by a *zana* grass or coursed-earth perimeter and entered through a single gatehouse hut where the compound head receives visitors. Walls are built of puddled laterite laid in courses and smoothed by hand, the plinth thickened against rain splash; roofs are conical frameworks of guinea-corn stalk and bamboo rafters lashed to a ring beam and thatched in bundled grass, finished with a pot or inverted calabash finial. Granaries — raised mud cylinders with detachable thatch caps, set on stone or potsherd footings to break the path of termites and rodents — stand inside the yard and are the compound's most visible statement of a household's harvest. Kitchens hold sunken hearths with three stones, and enormous *kasko* grain pots are built into corners. Settlement scale tracks land use: where farming dominates, hamlets stay small and dispersed so that enough farmland remains within walking distance. Displacement from Abuja after its designation as the federal capital broke many of these compounds up, moving families into transit and resettlement camps and into government housing.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery is the Gbagyi art form of record, transmitted matrilineally as a woman's craft. Clay is dug with hoes from governed claypits — women who are pregnant or menstruating may not go, and no broken calabash may carry the clay — then hand-sorted, kneaded, and stiffened with dried clay or fine sand. No wheel is used: vessels are pinched and coil-built, shaped with wooden ribs and worn knife blades, decorated with roulette and incision, and open-fired. Three named forms carry the tradition. **Randa** — the largest, 20–30 gallons, wide-mouthed with a short neck and narrow rim, for drinking water and, at ceremonies, for *giya* (beer); the *Gwarin Yamma* are its specialists. **Tulu** — long-necked, fat-bellied, narrow-mouthed with a wide flat rim, reserved strictly for *giya*, whose contents are held safe from spirits overnight and are poured as offerings. **Kasko** — the open, variable utility pot for water, oil, meat, fish, salt, cooking and serving, and in giant form for grain. Blacksmiths forge hoe blades, adzes and knife tangs from scrap bloom at bellows hearths.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Dance and marriage adornment turns on metal and shell: cast brass or bronze anklets worn in pairs, which sound with the step and are the audible half of *knunu* dance regalia; cowrie-strung waistbands and cross-body straps; brass and iron bangles; strung glass and stone beads. Familiarity with lapis lazuli among the Gbagyi has been read, in some accounts, as a trace of northeastern connection — a claim tangled with the recognised affinity between the Gbagyi and Nupe languages and with Koro traditions linking them to Wukari and the Kwararafa empire (Cadman, 1913). Facial cicatrization — short cheek and temple cuts marking lineage and locality — was general before the mid-twentieth century and survives on elders. Women shave and plait the hair in ridged rows for ceremonies.

## Music & performance

Gbagyi musical life is drum- and fiddle-led and tied to agricultural labour, chieftaincy and mourning. The **kundu**, a single-headed hourglass or goblet drum played with the hands and carried on a shoulder strap, is the core instrument, played in graded sets with a lead voice and answering drums. The bowed lute **goge** — a single- or two-string fiddle with a gourd body and lizard- or reptile-skin membrane, bowed with a horsehair arc and accompanied by the *kwarya* inverted-calabash percussion — is shared with Hausa and Nupe neighbours and used for praise singing at the *Osu*'s court, at weddings and at spirit-appeasement performances. Iron clapperless bells, gourd rattles and end-blown horns fill out ensembles. Song genres divide between praise-naming of the *Osu* and lineage heads, call-and-response farm songs sung over communal hoeing, funeral dirges for the death of an elder, and children's game songs that carry the folktale repertoire. Christian expansion under the Sudan Interior Mission (later ECWA) and Yoruba Baptist missionaries produced a substantial Gbagyi-language hymn and gospel-chorus repertoire, sung to *kundu* accompaniment, that now outweighs court music in everyday hearing. The musician Bez, of Gbagyi parentage, is the best-known contemporary figure to have carried the identity into Nigerian popular music.

## Dance & theatre

The **knunu** dance is the signature Gbagyi performance: a line and circle dance for women and men in fibre skirting, brass anklets and cowrie belts, driven by *kundu* drums, in which the stamping foot and the ankle bells supply the counter-rhythm. Dances are performed at chieftaincy installations, at the reception of visiting dignitaries, at marriages, and at the second burial of an elder, where dancing is celebratory rather than mournful. A distinct genre of harvest and new-yam dancing accompanies the close of the farming year, with mimed hoeing and load-carrying figures — including the shoulder-borne pot — worked into the choreography. Masquerade appearances tied to the appeasement of deities such as *Maigiro* belong to the traditional-religion minority and are now rare and largely closed to outsiders. Formal theatre in Gbagyi is a modern development, carried by cultural-day pageants staged by Gbagyi associations in Abuja, Minna and Suleja, which dramatise migration, the Fulani jihad displacements, and the loss of land to the federal capital.

## Festivals & rituals

There is no single pan-Gbagyi annual festival; the calendar is local and agricultural, keyed to the single rainy season. Communal hoeing parties at the first rains and harvest thanksgiving at the end of the guinea-corn and yam cycle are the fixed points, the latter now largely absorbed into church harvest services with auction of produce. Chieftaincy installation of an *Osu*, with the assembly of the council of elders who are charged with keeping peace in the land, is the largest political ritual. Since the 1990s, Gbagyi Day cultural festivals — organised by Gbagyi development associations in Abuja, Minna and Kwali — have become the main public occasion, combining *knunu* dancing, pottery display, and explicit political assertion of indigenous ownership of the Federal Capital Territory. Life-cycle rites: naming on the seventh or eighth day, with the child presented to the compound's elders; marriage negotiated between lineages with bridewealth in grain, goats and money, and, historically, farm service by the groom to his in-laws; funerals distinguishing immediate burial from a later second-burial celebration with drumming, dancing and *giya* poured from *tulu* pots. Belief in reincarnation is widespread, and children are read for the return of a named ancestor.

## Foodways

The staples are guinea corn (sorghum), millet, maize, yam, cassava and cocoyam, with cowpea, bambara nut, groundnut, okra and *ugu* leaf. The daily form is a stiff porridge of pounded grain or cassava flour — *tuwo* in the regional Hausa term — turned in a pot and eaten with a soup of okra, baobab leaf (*kuka*), groundnut or bitterleaf, seasoned with locust-bean condiment (*dawadawa*) and palm oil. A thin fermented gruel of sorghum or millet is the morning food. Bush meat, guinea fowl, goat and river fish enter the pot at ceremonies. The defining ceremonial consumption is *giya*, sorghum or millet beer brewed by women, kept in *tulu* pots, drunk from calabash dippers at marriages, second burials and installations, and poured as libation to the deities; its storage vessel is never used for anything else. Roasted yam with palm oil and pepper is the field meal. Islam's spread among a minority of Gbagyi from the nineteenth century onwards, and the Christian majority's own temperance movements, have made beer a contested rather than universal marker.

## Oral tradition & literature

Gbagyi folktale telling is an evening genre performed in the compound yard after the meal, with an adult or senior child narrating and the audience singing the story's refrain — the songs are the structural hinges of the tale, not ornament. The repertoire runs to trickster animal cycles, tales explaining kinship obligation and hospitality, and moral tales about the consequences of refusing a guest, consonant with the reputation the people carry in the Hausa saying *muyi shi Gwari Gwari*, "let's do it the Gbagyi way." Joseph Shekwo's Northwestern University thesis, *Understanding Gbagyi Folktales* (1984), is the foundational study and was framed explicitly around using the tales as the basis for Gbagyi-language mass-media programming — an early argument for broadcast as a vehicle of oral-tradition survival. Proverb use is dense in elders' speech and in dispute settlement before the compound head and the *Osu*'s council. Language documentation includes Elias Rosendall's *Aspects of Gbari Grammar* (1998). Abu Bakr Sadiq, a Gbagyi poet from Minna, represents the contemporary literary turn.

## Language & religion

Gbagyi/Gbari belongs to Niger-Congo, placed by Williamson in Benue-Congo and conventionally in the Nupoid group, with two principal dialects, Gbari (*Gwari yamma*) and Gbagyi, and a recognised affinity to Nupe. It has no indigenous script; Roman orthography arrived with Sudan Interior Mission translation work, which produced the hymnal and scripture texts that anchor written Gbagyi today. Most Gbagyi are Christian — the result of SIM/ECWA and Yoruba Baptist missions — with a Muslim minority whose conversion came mainly in the colonial and post-colonial period rather than at the time of the nineteenth-century Fula jihads, and a smaller body of traditional practitioners. Traditional belief centres on *Shekwoyi*, "one who was there before our ancestors," approached above lesser deities such as *Maigiro*, who receive appeasement offerings of beer and fowl; reincarnation is widely held, and Zuma Rock remains a spiritual landmark.
