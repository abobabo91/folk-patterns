## Overview

The Gbagyi are among the oldest continuously settled peoples of the Nigerian Middle Belt, occupying the granite-strewn plateaus and river valleys between the Niger and the Kaduna in what is today Niger State, Kaduna State, Nasarawa State and the Federal Capital Territory. Their language, Gbagyi — with its close relative Gbari, sometimes treated as a dialect and sometimes as a separate tongue — belongs to the Nupoid cluster of Benue–Congo, making them linguistic cousins of the Nupe, Ebira and Gade rather than of the Hausa who came to dominate the region politically. Population estimates commonly run between three and five million, though the figure is unusually hard to fix: Gbagyi communities are dispersed across five federal jurisdictions, census categories have historically folded them into Hausa or "Gwari" rubrics, and the creation of Abuja in the 1970s and 1980s displaced tens of thousands from the very hills their identity was anchored to. That displacement is the central modern fact of Gbagyi history, and it follows a longer pattern: the nineteenth-century Sokoto jihad and the slave-raiding emirates of Zaria and Bida pressed Gbagyi settlements repeatedly, driving communities into defensible inselberg strongholds and producing the distinctive hilltop compound architecture that survived into the colonial period. The exonym *Gwari* carries this history and is widely rejected; *Gbagyi* is the self-designation, and the people are proverbially characterised — sometimes admiringly, sometimes condescendingly by neighbours — as patient, accommodating and slow to quarrel, a reputation crystallised in the image of the Gbagyi woman carrying her load on the shoulder rather than the head. Their signature material contributions are ceramic and architectural: the pottery villages of Kwali, Ushafa and Dagiri produce hand-built, roulette-decorated water jars and storage vessels that became, through the mid-century work of Ladi Kwali, the most internationally visible Nigerian ceramic tradition of the twentieth century, while the conical thatched compound and the raised mud granary define the visual grammar of the Middle Belt countryside.

## Material culture

Gbagyi material culture is grounded in clay, grass and iron rather than in woven cloth or cast metal. The dominant crafts are women's hand-built pottery, men's blacksmithing, and a broad domestic repertoire of grass, raffia and calabash work; textile production was historically modest and largely supplied by trade with Nupe and Hausa weaving centres.

### Textile & pattern traditions

The Gbagyi were not a major weaving people, and woven cloth in Gbagyi communities was commonly acquired through exchange with Nupe, Hausa and Yoruba producers rather than made locally on any scale. What patterning tradition exists is chiefly a dyeing and surface-decoration one, and the true Gbagyi ornamental vocabulary — concentric bands, herringbone, chevrons, twisted-cord roulette textures — lives on pot surfaces and grass screens rather than on cloth.

- **Indigo resist cloth** — Cotton cloth patterned by tying, stitching or clamping before immersion in fermented indigo, produced in some Gbagyi communities and widely obtained from the pit-dyeing centres of the Hausa north.
- **Grass screen** (*zana*) — Panels of split guinea-corn or elephant-grass stalks bound in rows with cord, whose regular vertical striping and woven bindings form the most ubiquitous patterned surface in a Gbagyi compound, used as courtyard walls, gates and bathing enclosures.
- **Raffia basketry** — Coiled and plaited baskets, mats, winnowing trays and lidded containers, commonly worked in alternating natural and dyed strands to produce banded and spiral patterning.
- **Roulette impression** — The carved or twisted-cord roller rolled across damp clay, giving continuous textured bands; the single most characteristic Gbagyi ornamental technique, transferred from pottery onto other decorated surfaces.
- **Calabash pyrography** — Gourd vessels incised and scorched with heated iron points into geometric registers, a decorative idiom shared across the Middle Belt and executed with particular fineness in Gbagyi and neighbouring Nupe workshops.

### Clothing & dress

Gbagyi dress today is largely continuous with the broader northern Nigerian idiom of gown, cap and wrapper, the result of long Hausa and Nupe contact and of Islamic and Christian influence alike. Older forms, recorded in early colonial photography, were considerably scantier, relying on leather aprons, waist cords and body ornament rather than tailored cloth.

- **Embroidered gown** — Wide-sleeved men's gown worn over trousers, adopted from the northern sartorial repertoire and now standard for elders, titleholders and festival dress.
- **Wrapper and headtie** — Two-piece women's dress of waist wrapper and tied head cloth, commonly in indigo or printed cotton, with a shoulder cloth added for market and ceremonial occasions.
- **Leather waist apron** — Fringed hide or plaited-fibre apron worn at the waist, documented in pre-colonial and early colonial Gbagyi dress before the general adoption of woven cloth.
- **Dance regalia** (*knunu*) — Costume for the *knunu* masked dance, commonly combining grass and raffia fringing, cloth strips, ankle rattles and a concealing headpiece so that the performer's body is entirely disguised.
- **Waist beads** — Strings of beads or cowries worn beneath or above the wrapper by women, marking maturity and, in some communities, marital or fertility status.

### Architecture

Gbagyi architecture is among the most legible in central Nigeria: a walled family compound of round mud-walled rooms under steep conical thatch, linked by *zana* screens and punctuated by raised granaries. Historically these compounds were sited on and around granite inselbergs for defence against slave raiding, with the rock itself incorporated as wall and threshing floor.

- **Conical thatch compound** — Cluster of circular puddled-mud or coursed-earth rooms, each roofed with a steep conical frame of poles bound with rope and thatched in guinea grass, arranged around a swept central courtyard for one extended patrilineal family.
- **Mud granary** — Large bottle-shaped or cylindrical earthen store raised on stones or a timber platform to defer damp and rodents, capped with a removable thatch cone and filled from the top; the visual index of a household's standing.
- **Grass screen enclosure** (*zana*) — Woven grass-stalk panels forming compound walls, entrance baffles and bathing enclosures, replaced seasonally and far cheaper to renew than mud walling.
- **Hilltop settlement** — Defensive siting on and among granite inselbergs, with narrow approach paths, rock-shelter storage and water cisterns in natural hollows, a pattern abandoned only under colonial pacification.
- **Entrance hut** — Roofed gateway chamber at the compound threshold where the household head receives visitors, screening the interior courtyard from the street.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Pottery is the Gbagyi craft of international consequence, made by women without the wheel, built up by coiling and pinching over a mould, decorated with roulette and burnished slip, and fired in open bonfires. The villages of Kwali, Ushafa and Dagiri are the recognised centres, and Ladi Kwali of Kwali — later of the Abuja Pottery Training Centre founded by Michael Cardew — became the best-known Nigerian potter of the twentieth century, her work exhibited internationally and her portrait later placed on Nigerian currency. Ironworking, by contrast, is a male hereditary specialism supplying the agricultural tools on which the farming economy rests.

- **Kwali pottery** (*Kwali*) — Hand-built water jars and bowls from Kwali, characteristically large, thin-walled and decorated with incised and roulette-impressed registers, sometimes with modelled lizard, fish or scorpion motifs in relief.
- **Ushafa pottery** (*Ushafa*) — Ware from Ushafa near Bwari, a long-established potting village whose burnished domestic vessels and open-fired production remain in continuous manufacture.
- **Dagiri pottery** (*Dagiri*) — Pottery from Dagiri in the Gwagwalada area, a third of the named Gbagyi potting communities supplying regional markets with domestic water and cooking vessels.
- **Storage jar** (*randa*) — Very large wide-mouthed earthen jar used to hold water, grain or fermenting beer inside the compound, too heavy to move once filled and often set permanently into a floor.
- **Water pot** (*tulu*) — Narrow-necked round-bodied carrying pot for drawing and transporting water, the form most associated with Gbagyi ceramics and with the shoulder-carrying posture.
- **Shoulder-borne pot carriage** — The distinctive Gbagyi practice of carrying the water pot or load on one shoulder rather than balanced on the head, so widely recognised that it functions as an ethnic emblem and a metaphor for Gbagyi forbearance.
- **Blacksmith hoe forging** — Hereditary smithing of short-handled hoes, axes, adzes and knives at bellows-and-anvil forges, the smith commonly also serving ritual functions connected with iron and the earth.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Gbagyi adornment relies on brass, iron, cowries and beads rather than on gold or silver, and historically on permanent body marking. Facial and body cicatrization was the most consequential form, serving as lineage and community identification, and is now largely discontinued among younger generations under Christian, Islamic and legal pressure against the practice.

- **Brass anklet** — Heavy cast or coiled brass anklet worn by women, sometimes in weighty pairs that alter the gait audibly and visibly, associated with marriage and with dance.
- **Cowrie waistband** — Band of cowrie shells strung or sewn at the waist, cowries carrying both ornamental and older monetary and fertility associations.
- **Facial cicatrization** — Sets of incised cheek, temple and forehead scars cut in childhood and rubbed with pigment or ash to raise them, identifying lineage and Gbagyi affiliation; commonly abandoned from the mid-twentieth century onward.
- **Body scarification** — Decorative keloid patterning on the torso, arms and abdomen of women, understood in some communities as enhancing beauty and marking passage to adulthood.
- **Bead strings** — Multi-strand neck, wrist and waist beads in glass, stone and seed, worn in graded quantities to signal age grade, wealth and ceremonial role.

## Music & performance

Gbagyi music is built on drums, the single-string bowed fiddle and massed responsorial singing, performed at farming work parties, funerals, chieftaincy installations and harvest celebrations. The *goge* fiddle links Gbagyi practice to a wide Sahelian and Middle Belt fiddling tradition shared with Hausa, Nupe and Zarma musicians, while drum ensembles carry the specifically local repertoire.

- **Drum** (*kundu*) — The principal Gbagyi drum, played in sets of differing pitch to carry interlocking patterns that cue dance movement and, in some contexts, convey formulaic praise phrases.
- **Bowed fiddle** (*goge*) — Single-string gourd-bodied fiddle with a horsehair bow, played by a specialist who both accompanies and sings, commonly with a rattle-player in support; associated with praise, healing and possession contexts across the region.
- **Responsorial song** — Lead-and-chorus singing in which a soloist improvises praise, complaint or narrative over a fixed communal refrain, the dominant vocal form at work parties and funerals.
- **Calabash rattle and gourd percussion** — Netted seed-covered gourds, struck calabashes floating in water and iron scrapers supplying the timeline against which drum patterns are set.
- **Flute and horn** — End-blown reed flutes and animal-horn trumpets used in chieftaincy processions and hunters' celebrations, the horn in particular signalling rank.

## Dance & theatre

Dance among the Gbagyi is communal, occasion-bound and organised by age and gender, with the masked *knunu* tradition as its most theatrical expression. There is no tradition of scripted drama; performance takes the form of masquerade, competitive display dance and the satirical interludes that accompany festival gatherings.

- **Knunu dance** (*knunu*) — Masked or costumed dance performed in concealing regalia of grass, raffia and cloth, appearing at funerals, harvest and initiation occasions and commonly understood as carrying an ancestral or spirit presence rather than representing a human dancer.
- **Harvest circle dance** — Massed dance in concentric rings, women and men in separate arcs, with foot-stamping and hip articulation set against the *kundu* ensemble.
- **Anklet dance** — Women's display dance in which heavy brass anklets are made to sound rhythmically, the ornament functioning simultaneously as instrument and as costume.
- **Hunters' display** — Competitive mimetic dance by hunters' associations re-enacting stalking and the kill, performed with bows, horns and trophy skins.
- **Masquerade satire** — Topical mockery and mimicry delivered from behind the mask, licensing criticism of named individuals and of authority that could not be voiced openly.

## Festivals & rituals

The Gbagyi ritual year follows the agricultural cycle, with the principal public celebrations clustered at harvest and at the installation of titleholders. Life-cycle rites — naming, initiation into adulthood, marriage and above all funerals — are the occasions on which the full material and performance repertoire is deployed. In many communities the older calendrical rites now coexist with, or have been displaced by, Christian and Islamic observance.

- **Harvest celebration** — Post-harvest thanksgiving with drumming, masquerade, wrestling and communal beer, marking the closing of the farming year and the season for marriages.
- **Funeral rites** — The most elaborate Gbagyi ceremony, extending over days for a senior person, with drum ensembles, *knunu* appearances, mass singing and feasting that redistribute a household's stored grain and livestock.
- **Chieftaincy installation** — Investiture of the *Etsu* or local titleholder with regalia, horn calls, processional drumming and the formal presentation of tribute.
- **Earth and ancestral veneration** (*Maigiro*) — Offerings at rock shrines, groves and hearthstones directed to the earth and to lineage ancestors, mediated by lineage heads and by the smith; commonly maintained alongside professed Christianity or Islam.
- **Initiation into adulthood** — Age-grade transition involving seclusion, instruction in obligations, and historically the cutting of identifying facial marks.

## Foodways

Gbagyi cooking rests on the savanna staples of guinea corn, millet, maize, yam and rice, transformed into stiff porridges and fermented gruels and eaten with vegetable soups thickened with seeds and leaves. Locust bean condiment gives the characteristic savoury depth of the Middle Belt kitchen, and pottery is intimately bound to the cuisine: the *randa* holds the water and the beer, and the open-fired cooking pot makes the porridge.

- **Stiff porridge** — Guinea-corn, millet or maize flour stirred into a firm mass and eaten by hand with soup, the everyday centre of the diet.
- **Fermented gruel** — Thin soured grain gruel drunk in the morning and carried to the farm, sweetened or taken with a savoury relish.
- **Locust bean condiment** — Fermented *Parkia* seeds pressed into dark pungent cakes, the principal flavouring of soups and stews.
- **Leaf and seed soups** — Soups thickened with baobab leaf, melon seed or okra, with dried fish, bushmeat or beef where available.
- **Grain beer** — Sorghum or millet beer brewed in large earthen jars for funerals, harvest and work parties, and a marker of the older, non-Islamic ritual economy.

## Oral tradition & literature

Gbagyi verbal art was transmitted orally until the twentieth century, when missionary linguists produced the first orthographies and scriptural translations. The oral corpus comprises migration and origin narratives, praise and lament song, proverb, riddle and animal tale, with the historical experience of raiding and displacement as a recurring theme — the flight to the hills, the loss of farmland, the virtue of endurance under pressure. Proverbs concerning the shoulder-borne load and the patient ox are widely quoted as self-characterisation. Since the late twentieth century a documentary and revivalist literature has grown: Gbagyi cultural associations publish history and language material, radio broadcasting in Gbagyi supports the language, and the dispossession of Gbagyi communities by the building of Abuja has generated an explicitly political written literature of claim and commemoration. Schools of Gbagyi-language publishing remain small, and much of the scholarly record still sits in colonial gazetteers, mission grammars and university theses rather than in print available in the communities themselves.

## Language & religion

Gbagyi and Gbari form a dialect continuum within the Nupoid group of Benue–Congo, tonal languages with noun-class remnants, related to Nupe, Gade and Ebira and quite distinct from the Chadic Hausa that serves as the regional lingua franca. Bilingualism in Hausa is near-universal, and English dominates in education and in the Federal Capital Territory, making intergenerational transmission of Gbagyi an active concern for cultural associations. Religiously the Gbagyi are divided between Christianity — strong in the southern and central areas following early twentieth-century mission work, with denominational concentrations around Minna, Kwali and Bwari — and Islam, longest established where Nupe and Hausa political influence was strongest. Beneath both, the older complex commonly referred to as *Maigiro* persists in attenuated form: veneration of the earth, of granite outcrops and groves, and of lineage ancestors, with the blacksmith and the lineage head as ritual intermediaries. Divination, protective medicine and shrine offerings continue in many communities alongside church and mosque attendance, and funerals in particular remain the site where pre-Abrahamic practice is most visible.
