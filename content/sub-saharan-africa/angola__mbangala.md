## Overview

The Imbangala or Mbangala were groups of warriors and marauders who worked as hired mercenaries in Angola and later founded the Kasanje Kingdom. They appeared in Angola during the early 17th century, although their origins remain debated. The sources describe possible origins in Central Africa, the Lunda Empire, the Bie Plateau, or coastal regions west of the highlands. The Imbangala operated particularly in regions near the Kwanza River, the Kwango River, Baixa de Cassange, and the central highlands. Mbangala is identified as a Bantu language of Angola. Their significance in folk-culture terms lies in their militarised social organisation, initiation rites, armed camps, warfare, oral traditions, and relationships with Portuguese colonial forces.

## Material culture

### Clothing & dress

The sources used describe no everyday or ceremonial clothing system. They state that fighting men wore a collar during training and after initiation until they had killed a man in battle. The sources also state that the Imbangala covered themselves with an ointment called *maji a samba*, which was believed to confer invulnerability when the soldier followed strict *yijila*, or codes. No other garments, head coverings, belts, footwear, or distinctions between men's and women's dress are described.

### Architecture

The Imbangala lived and fought in a *kilombo*, described as an armed camp or village and a temporarily fortified town surrounded by a wooden palisade. Each kilombo had twelve gates corresponding to twelve fighting squadrons.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

 They mention bows, knives, swords, war clubs, and hatchets as weapons, stating that the Imbangala fought with the same weapons as their enemies and that the war club or hatchet was their primary weapon.

### Jewelry & body adornment

 They mention only the training collar worn by fighting men and the ointment called *maji a samba*.

## Music & performance

 The sources do describe public and collective performances connected with warfare and initiation. Children were trained daily in group and individual combat, and ritual human sacrifices were held before large battles and other major enterprises.

## Dance & theatre

 They describe initiation rites, combat training, ritual human sacrifices before large battles, and the use of a reputation for cannibalism and human sacrifice to intimidate attacked populations.

## Festivals & rituals

 They describe several rituals and life-cycle practices. The Imbangala did not permit women to give birth inside the *kilombo*; women left the camp to have their children, and a child was not considered Imbangala until undergoing initiation. Children born inside a kilombo were killed in order to prevent kinship from replacing initiation. Adolescents were captured and forced to serve in the army, while boys had to kill an enemy to be accepted among the men. Those who tried to run away were killed and eaten.

Ritual human sacrifices were held before large battles and other major enterprises. Captured enemies were killed together with cows, goats, and dogs, and the flesh of the victims was eaten together. The *yijila* required infanticide, cannibalism, and an absolute absence of cowardice. In later periods, customs moderated in some areas: cannibalism became restricted to ritual or symbolic occasions, including a ritual known as “eating the old man.”

## Foodways

 They state that the Imbangala’s primary interest as marauders included obtaining large quantities of palm wine. They produced palm wine through a wasteful method of chopping down trees and tapping their fermented contents over a few months.

The sources also describe food practices connected with warfare and ritual. The Imbangala were reputed to eat the flesh of their enemies, and the sources report that killed enemies and their own fighters could be eaten when the latter were judged cowardly. Before large battles and other major enterprises, captured enemies, cows, goats, and dogs were killed and their flesh was eaten together.

## Oral tradition & literature

The origins of the Imbangala remain debated. The sources state that oral traditions of the Lunda Empire led Jan Vansina and David Birmingham to hypothesise that groups of Jaga marauders originated in the Lunda Empire under the leader Kinguri and had fled 1550 and 1612. Another account presents the Imbangala as a local people of southern Angola originating from the Bie Plateau or coastal regions west of the highlands.

The first witness account was published by the English sailor Andrew Battell, who lived among the Imbangala for 16 months around 1600–1601. Their leaders told him that they came from a place called Elembe and had originated from a “page” in its army. Battell’s story was published by Samuel Purchas partially in 1614 and fully in 1625.

## Language & religion

Mbangala is identified as a Bantu language of Angola. They describe ritual beliefs and practices associated with Imbangala warfare and initiation, including the belief that *maji a samba* conferred invulnerability when soldiers followed the *yijila*. These codes required infanticide, cannibalism, and an absolute absence of cowardice. The sources also describe ritual human sacrifice before large battles and other major enterprises.
