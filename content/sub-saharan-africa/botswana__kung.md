## Overview

The ǃKung are one of the San peoples. They live mostly on the western edge of the Kalahari desert, in Ovamboland—northern Namibia and southern Angola—and in Botswana. The names ǃKung and Ju are variant words for “people,” preferred by different ǃKung groups. Estimates of the population vary, but there are probably around 15,000 speakers of the ǃKung dialects. The language belongs to the Kxʼa language family, together with the ǂʼAmkoe language, and makes abundant use of phonemic click consonants. Historically, the ǃKung used hunting and gathering for subsistence; since the 1950s, permanent settlements, cattle ranches, wage-paid agricultural labour, and industrialization have changed their way of life.

## Material culture

### Architecture

The ǃKung traditionally lived a mobile hunter-gatherer life. Current governments of Namibia and Botswana encourage permanent settlements with European style houses. The sources state that changing housing styles and arrangements have made houses less open, reducing outside intervention in domestic conflicts.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

They do mention hunting equipment, including arrows, and state that a successful kill may belong to the person who gave the hunter the arrows, whether that person is a man or a woman.

## Music & performance

The principal performance tradition described in the sources is healing. The ǃKung healing dance, *ǃkia*, begins at sundown and continues through the night. The term can be translated as “trance.” During the dance, healers prepare to enter a trance state and use the healing force called *nǀum*, which is said to heat inside the body, travel up the spine, and reach the brain with therapeutic power. Healers in trance seek out sickness and argue with spirits.

Women have a special medicine called *gwah*. During the Drum Dance, women enter the *ǃkia* state, and the *gwah* travels up the spine and lodges in the neck. To obtain this power, they chop up the root of a short shrub, boil it into a tea, and drink it. The sources state that the power lasts a lifetime, so the tea does not need to be consumed every time.

## Dance & theatre

The sources describe the *ǃkia* healing dance and the Drum Dance as ritual performances connected with trance and curing. Healers dance while preparing to communicate with the spirit world, seek out sickness, and argue with spirits. Women enter the *ǃkia* state during the Drum Dance in connection with *gwah*.

## Festivals & rituals

They do document healing, childbirth, marriage, divorce, and hunting rites.

Healing rites are a primary part of ǃKung culture. Health is understood as equivalent to social harmony, and any ǃKung person can become a healer. Aspirants apprentice themselves to older healers, whose instruction includes entering a trance and rubbing their sweat onto the pupils’ bellies, backs, foreheads, and spines. The sources state that nearly half the men and one-third of the women are acknowledged as having the power to heal.

ǃKung women commonly give birth unassisted, leaving the village camp during labour and delivering the child into a small leaf-lined hole dug into warm sand. The cord is not clamped or cut, and the placenta is placed beside the child as a guardian. Older women later join the mother and child in a ritual welcoming.

Every first marriage is arranged. The marriage-day tradition is a “marriage-by-capture” ceremony in which the bride is forcibly removed from her hut, carried, and laid down in the groom’s hut. After the wedding party, the couple spend the night together, and the next morning the husband’s mother ceremonially rubs them with oil. Half of all first-time marriages end in divorce; after the village has expressed its views, the couple may live separately with their families.

Hunting also has ritual dimensions. A successful hunter returns as if unsuccessful, accepts an older man’s remarks about the failure, and avoids claiming credit. The next day, the group retrieves the animal. Elders distribute the meat, and surplus meat is announced to nearby villages.

## Foodways

The sources describe a subsistence system formerly based on hunting and gathering. Traditional gender roles associated women with collecting plant foods and water and men with hunting, although these roles were not strict and people performed jobs as needed. Women generally prepared food, while men also participated in these activities. They state that women have taken up the preparation of millet, which is more difficult to process than traditional ǃKung foods and requires more time. Children are traditionally nursed for 3–5 years, ending when the mother becomes pregnant with another child. During times of deprivation, infanticide was permitted to preserve resources. The sources also state that the ǃKung do not use contraceptives and generally do not practice abstinence, while experiencing low fertility rates.

Food distribution is strongly associated with social well-being. The person who supplied the arrows may have a claim to the kill, but elders—described as “those of mature years”—allot meat to members of the group. When an animal is too large to move or meat is abundant, nearby villages are invited to collect it.

## Oral tradition & literature

They do record sayings and verbal formulas connected with social life. One saying states: “Healing makes their hearts happy, and a happy heart is one that reflects a sense of community.” In hunting rites, an older man questions a hunter about an apparently unsuccessful hunt, and the hunter is expected to avoid boasting and accept humility.

Kinship terms organize social relations. Conventional kin terms are based on genealogical position, while people sharing the same name are treated as members of the same family and assigned the same kinship term. The sources name this shared-name relationship *ǃkunǃa*. They also describe joking relations, *k”ai*, and avoidance relations, *kwa*. Every member of ǃKung society belongs to one of these categories.

The sources mention the documentary film *A Group of Women*, Marjorie Shostak’s account of Nisa, and later references to ǃKung life in popular culture.

## Language & religion

The ǃKung language, also called Ju, belongs to the Kxʼa language family. Together with the ǂʼAmkoe language, ǃKung forms this family. The language complex is spoken in Namibia, Botswana, and Angola and has many click sounds, tone, and nasalization. The sources distinguish between eleven and fifteen dialects, although the boundaries are unclear. The better-known dialects include Tsumkwe Juǀʼhoan, Ekoka ǃKung, ǃʼOǃKung, and ǂKxʼauǁʼein. Only Juǀʼhoan is written.

The ǃKung recognize a Supreme Being, ǃXu, described as the creator and upholder of life. The Otjimpolo-ǃKung know him as Erob, who “knows everything.” The sources also describe animistic and animatistic beliefs and a belief in the spirits of the dead, *llgauwasi*, who live immortally in the sky and can interact with humans. Shamanism is practiced to communicate with the spirit world and cure “Star Sickness.” A natural healer enters a trance, runs through a fire to chase away bad spirits, and may cure illness by laying hands on the diseased.
