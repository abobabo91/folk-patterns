## Overview

The Obolo, also called the Andoni or Doni, are an Ijaw subgroup of the Niger Delta in southern Nigeria, living across Rivers State and Akwa Ibom State. Their territory runs from Eastern Obolo in Akwa Ibom State westward to the Bight of Bonny in Rivers State — estuarine, creek-cut country whose oil and gas fields supply part of Nigeria's petroleum revenue. The word *Obolo* names the people, their language and their land alike. They speak Obolo, the most divergent member of the Lower Cross subgroup of Cross River, itself a branch of Benue-Congo. In folk-culture terms the Obolo matter for two things the sources document directly: the cast copper-alloy weaponry, bells and figures taken from their shrines by the British in 1904 and now in the British Museum, and a century-long literary movement that carried the language from its first printed almanacs to a complete Bible and a Wikipedia edition.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources used here — the Wikipedia articles on the Obolo people and the Obolo language, and the British Museum catalogue records of the objects shown — describe no Obolo weaving, dyeing or textile tradition, so none is listed. The one textile the records name is not Obolo-made cloth but a wrapping: the ivory side-blown horn from a shrine is bound in **black cotton** at its wider end.

The patterned surfaces the sources do attest are metal rather than cloth, and they are described consistently enough to set out as a repertoire.

**Incised linear and geometric work on cast weapons** — On the copper-alloy spears and the sword, engraved linear and geometric motifs run along the length of the blade or shaft; on the sword the geometry is both incised and hammered.

**White chalky pigment in horizontal bands** — A painted register rather than a cut one. Horizontal bands of a white chalky pigment cross the squared end of one spear, the leaf-shaped head of another, and the blade of the sword. The same banding logic organises the low relief on the cast bell and the leopard-skull sculpture, which makes it the single most consistent decorative habit in this object group.

**Panelled low relief on cast bells** — On the gun-metal bell the low-relief linear and geometric decoration is arranged inside vertical panels and covers the entire outer surface, so the ornament is framed and compartmented rather than continuous.

**Spiral relief on cast sculpture** — The lost-wax leopard-skull sculpture carries low-relief spiral motifs, heavily worn, running vertically along the nasal bone and in horizontal bands across the snout.

**Motif vocabulary.** Incised linear motifs; geometric motifs, incised or hammered; horizontal bands of white chalky pigment; vertical panels enclosing linear and geometric relief; low-relief spirals.

### Clothing & dress

The sources say almost nothing about Obolo dress. The single item of evidence is the cast copper-alloy figure in the British Museum: a male figure seated on a low stool, wearing a triangular hat held on by a cane harness support, a necklace with a long linear pendant, and bracelets at both wrists and both ankles, with a bow in his right hand. What the figure records is therefore a specific costume of rank or ritual office rather than everyday clothing — headgear substantial enough to need a harness, and matched metal ornament at all four limbs. Nothing in the sources distinguishes men's from women's dress, names any garment in Obolo, or describes head coverings, belts or footwear beyond this one hat. One further clothing-adjacent fact appears: John Barbot, visiting Dony Town in 1699, recorded that the king there spoke Portuguese and had accepted Christianity, with priests sent to him from São Tomé and Brazil —.

### Architecture

Two built structures are named, both destroyed: the **House of Skulls** and the **Shrine of Yok-Obolo**, which the British expedition of 1904 demolished. Their form is not described, but the shrine's contents are partly known from the museum records — the ivory horn now in London was part of the contents of a shrine, and came with four human crania joined together by braided vegetable fibre and a fifth separate cranium, which indicates what kind of interior these buildings held.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Metalwork is the one Obolo craft the sources document in any detail, and it is casting rather than smithing. The objects taken in 1904 are cast in copper alloy and in gun-metal, several of them by the **lost-wax** method: a leopard-skull sculpture, a seated male figure, and a clapperless columnar bell with a flat top, a looped strap handle and three lugs for suspension along one side. Weapons were cast in the same alloy — two spears, one with a squared end and one with a leaf-shaped tip, each with a coiled handle, and a sword with a coiled handle and a blade worked with incised and hammered geometry. The coiled handle recurs on all three, which makes it the signature of the group. Alongside these is a **manilla**, the horseshoe-shaped open circle of brass with flattened ends at the opening that served as currency and as a bracelet. The sources record no Obolo pottery or woodwork.

### Jewelry & body adornment

The sources name no Obolo jewellery types, no tattooing, no hairdressing and no body-marking practice. What survives is the evidence carried on the cast figure and in the currency: the seated male figure wears a necklace hung with a long linear pendant and bracelets on both wrists and both ankles, all rendered in the cast metal; and the brass manilla is catalogued as currency and bracelet at once, so the same horseshoe form functioned as money and as something worn.

## Music & performance

Only one Obolo instrument is documented in the sources, and it comes from a ritual rather than a musical context. The **ivory side-blown horn** in the British Museum was part of the contents of a shrine: it is blown from the side, its mouthpiece cut into the inside of the curve about halfway up the length, and its wider end is wrapped in black cotton. It reached the museum together with four human crania braided together with vegetable fibre and one separate cranium, so it belongs to the same assemblage as the House of Skulls and the Yok-Obolo shrine that the 1904 expedition destroyed. The cast **bell** from the same group is likewise catalogued as religious or ritual equipment; it is columnar, clapperless, and fitted with a looped strap handle and three suspension lugs along one side, which means it was struck or hung rather than shaken.

Beyond these two objects the sources record no Obolo song genres, no ensembles, no named performance occasions and no singers. They do document one modern medium for the language: Obolo is used in radio and television programmes and taught at primary and junior secondary level. The hymn and prayer books printed by churches in the area after the first almanacs of the early 1940s are the only song texts the sources mention.

## Festivals & rituals

One annual festival is named. **Ijok-Irin**, a fishing festival, is celebrated every 5 July and is unique to the people of Unyeada Kingdom — one of the Obolo communities whose dialect, Unyeada, is spoken in Andoni Local Government Area of Rivers State. The sources give no other date in the Obolo year and describe no life-cycle rites: nothing on birth, initiation, marriage or funerals.

Religious practice before the colonial period centred on **Yok-Obolo**, whose shrine, together with the House of Skulls, the British destroyed in 1904. Yok-Obolo was feared among neighbouring peoples, who spread word of Obolo ferocity in battle as far as the British. The political consequence of that expedition is documented precisely: having levelled the shrine, the British split Andoni into six parts and attached each to one of the six Native Courts of the Niger Delta, intending to break the Obolo as a political body. Some of the bronze work collected during the expedition is on display in the British Museum, and the objects shown in this atlas are from that group.

Christianity long predates the expedition. When John Barbot visited Dony Town in 1699 he found that the king had accepted Christianity, that priests were sent to him regularly from São Tomé and from Brazil to serve as ministers, and that the king spoke Portuguese.

## Foodways

The only indication of what the food economy rests on is indirect: the annual Ijok-Irin festival at Unyeada is a fishing festival, and Obolo territory runs from Eastern Obolo to the Bight of Bonny.

## Oral tradition & literature

The Obolo have no recorded epic in the sources, but they have an unusually well-documented written tradition for a Niger Delta language, and it began with folktales. ***Ikpa Urọk***, a collection of folktales in the different dialects of Obolo, was published in 1985 — the same year as ***Adasi Ikpa Obolo***, a primer following the approved orthography, and ***Ida Obolo***, the Obolo-language periodical, first issued that year.

The chain runs back further. The first published materials in Obolo were almanacs and calendars printed in the early 1940s by the Andoni Progressive Union, though writing in the language is believed to have begun earlier; hymn and prayer books from local churches followed. Matthew M. Urang wrote the first primer in 1968, the first published non-church book in the language. *Reading and Writing Obolo* appeared in 1978 from the Rivers Readers Project, a Rivers State government initiative producing reading material in local languages so children could become literate in the mother tongue before moving to English; it was revised in 1985 and reissued in 2023.

Later work extends the shelf: the Obolo New Testament in 1991; ***Mbuban Îchaka*** by Isidore Ene-Awaji in 2010, a novel for junior secondary schools and general readership and the first literary work in the language; the complete Bible, finished by the Obolo Language and Bible Translation Project in 2012 and dedicated in 2014, making Obolo the 23rd Nigerian language with a full Bible; the site obololanguage.org in 2016; Obolo Wikipedia on 14 October 2024; and ***Inu Îyiyala Isan̄a***, a translation of Chinua Achebe's *Things Fall Apart*, published in 2025 after years in manuscript.

## Language & religion

Obolo is a Cross River language — the most divergent in the Lower Cross subgroup, a branch of Benue-Congo. It is agglutinative, SVO and tonal, with five tones (low, high, mid, falling, rising), of which only low (`) and falling (ˆ) are written; tone marking is compulsory on the first syllables of verbs and verbal groups, and elsewhere used only to separate minimal pairs. Its six dialect groups, west to east, are **Ataba**, **Unyeada**, **Ngo**, **Okoroete**, **Iko** and **Ibot Obolo**. *Ngo* is the prestige dialect and supplies most of Standard Obolo, though all Standard verbs conjugate on the Ataba pattern. The script is Latin, with an orthography designed with Nickolas Faraclas (1981–83), revised by Uche Aaron in 1991 and published by the NERDC in 2000; the **Obolo Language and Bible Translation Organization** regulates it. Counting shifted from vigesimal to decimal with *Aya Ifuk Obolo* in 1985 (*ge*, *iba*, *ita*, *ini*, *go*; *akọp* ten, *efit* hundred, *obop* thousand). Religion: Christianity accepted by the king of Dony Town before 1699; before the British destroyed its shrine in 1904, *Yok-Obolo*.
