## Overview

The Konso — also called the *Xonsita* — are a Lowland East Cushitic-speaking people of south-western Ethiopia, living south of Lake Chamo in the bend of the Sagan River, with the greatest concentration in the Konso Zone of the South Ethiopia Region. The 2007 Ethiopian national census counted 250,430 Konso, of whom 10,470 (4.18%) were urban dwellers; over 87% lived in what was then the Southern Nations, Nationalities, and People's Region. A few communities reach into northern Kenya. Their neighbours are Omotic-, Sidama- and Oromo-speaking. Konso family traditions, as recorded by Hallpike (1972), describe a composite people drawn from all the surrounding areas. In folk-culture terms they are known for terraced and irrigated hill farming, for large towns each governed by a council of elders, and for the carved memorial figures called *waga*.

## Material culture

### Textile & pattern traditions

The sources document weaving only through the museum collection, which holds both the cloth and the equipment that made it.

**Narrow-strip loom weaving** — The British Museum holds part of a narrow-strip loom with its wooden beater and an attached length of woven cotton cloth, the one direct record here of Konso cloth production. The garments in the same collection are built from the rectangular cotton webs such a loom yields: two rectangles folded and seamed for a pair of shorts, a single folded rectangle with a cut neck opening for a tunic.

**Striped cotton cloth** — The woven pieces are patterned by warp or weft striping rather than by figured ornament. Recorded combinations are black cotton with white, red and yellow stripes; black with white, red and orange stripes and a white cotton cord sewn round the waist and leg hems; and a dark green tunic cloth accented with stripes of light green, yellow and burgundy.

**Fibre string work** — Aprons and chest ornaments are made from worked plant fibre rather than woven cloth: a girl's pubic apron of cotton strings on a waist band, a women's dance apron of fibre string strung with glass beads and hung from a leather belt, and a chestlet of beetle wing cases threaded on string made from *Ensete*.

**Basketry** — A single bag is woven from raffia, grass and bark fibre.

### Clothing & dress

Men's dress in the collection centres on woven cotton shorts, cut from two folded rectangles seamed along the inseam with an extra piece let in as a gusset, and on a folded rectangular tunic with a neck opening cut at the fold. Hide is worked for garments as well: a fringed waist garment is decorated with antelope bone, beads and cowrie shells, and a belt is made from a continuous loop of twisted goat hide carrying two pendants cut from aluminium bullet cases. Women's items include a post-partum waist band of cow hide ornamented with a single cowrie shell, and beaded fibre-string aprons worn in dances. Head coverings are of skin rather than cloth: a boy's cap of goat skin, and a hat of two pieces of soft white sheep leather stitched together. Dance dress is distinguished from daily wear by objects worn on the body — a man's corslet of seeds strung on fibre with a hide waist band, made for dancing, and a chestlet of beetle wing cases worn diagonally across the chest by men when dancing.

### Architecture

The sources describe the shaped landscape rather than the house. Konso agriculture works by irrigation and by terracing mountain slopes, and the people typically live in large towns, each with its own council of elders — a settlement pattern that sets them apart from dispersed homestead farming in the region.

### Ceramics, metalwork & everyday objects

Konso earthenware in the collection is utilitarian: a pot with two ring handles used for boiling water, and a pottery bowl for a smoking water-pipe, still holding traces of tobacco. Wood-carving covers most household equipment — a comb, a coffee cup, a bowl or platter used for eating, a stool whose top is incised with geometric motifs and fitted with a metal disc at the centre of the seat, and a spoon-shaped club ornamented with pokerwork. A gourd bottle carries incised decoration. Ironwork appears as blades and points set into wood: an axe with an iron blade on a curved shaft, a knife with a wooden handle, a spear whose iron spike is bound to the shaft with skin, and a digging stick tipped with iron. Other objects include a hide shield with a wooden strut as a handle, a snuff-box of animal bone with hide ends and thong suspension, and an aluminium strigil bound at intervals with giraffe hair.

### Jewelry & body adornment

Adornment draws on metal, animal materials and imported beads. Aluminium is worked into a woman's armlet of wire and into a mismatched pair of ear-rings finished with giraffe tail hair; giraffe tail hair also forms a necklace tied together in three places. Brass is used for a pair of bracelets worn by a married woman and for a child's anklet of five brass crotals threaded on cotton. One ear-ring is a circular piece of amber with a brass-lined hole and a wire loop. Glass beads are linked into three-string necklets or chestlets on cotton thread, and are set into the fibre aprons and into a rattle of antelope leg bones. A forehead ornament called *hallasha* is cast in metal in the form of a phallus. Ivory is used for a finger ring, recorded as used together with a bride's stick. The ornaments worn in life carried enough social meaning that they were carved onto memorial figures to record the dead person's standing.

## Music & performance

The instruments held for the Konso are wind, struck and rattling. *Pan-pipes* are made from eight stalks of millet bound with vegetable fibre. A flute is carved from wood with four fingering holes, and a whistle is made from a seed pod. A wooden horn or trumpet with a leather suspension loop has a civic rather than musical use: it is sounded for calling assemblies, which fits a society governed town by town through councils of elders. Percussion is worn as much as played — a rattle strung with antelope leg bones decorated with glass beads, and an iron bell with an iron clapper hung from a leather loop. Two ornaments in the collection are explicitly dance equipment: the men's corslet of seeds strung on fibre and the chestlet of beetle wing cases threaded on *Ensete* string and worn diagonally across the chest by men when dancing. Together with the beaded fibre apron used by women in dances, they indicate that Konso performance was carried on the body, with sound produced by the dancer's own movement.

## Dance & theatre

Dance is attested in the sources only through the objects made for it: the man's seed corslet, the beetle-wing-case chestlet worn diagonally across the chest by men when dancing, and the beaded pubic apron of fibre string on a leather belt used by women in dances.

## Festivals & rituals

Konso ritual life in the sources is organised around generation, office and the commemoration of the dead. Konso society retains the *gadaa* generation-grading system of social organisation held in common with Oromo culture, along with comparable high priests and a cult of phallicism — the last of which surfaces directly in the cast metal *hallasha* forehead ornament made in the form of a phallus. Marriage is marked in material terms: brass bracelets are worn by a married woman, an ivory finger ring is used with a bride's stick, and polygyny is an accepted practice. Birth has its own object, a woman's post-partum waist band of cow hide with a single cowrie shell. Death is the best-documented rite. Group members erect carvings called *waga* in memory of a dead man who has killed an enemy or an animal, and the figures are set up in groups representing the man, his wives and his slain adversaries. The Madrid National Museum of Anthropology records that these anthropomorphic posts marked the burials of people of standing — heroes and chiefs — and that only they or their families could meet the cost; the legs are not carved out, so that the post can be driven into the ground. Male figures are shown with sexual organs and head-dresses emphasised in allusion to sexual potency, while the figures of enemies are carved without them. A British Museum *waga* of a female figure carries carved renderings of the personal ornaments worn in life, which record the deceased's status in society; it retains traces of red ochre, as does a second female grave figure. No UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage element in the sources concerns the Konso.

## Foodways

Konso farming is largely agricultural and depends on irrigating and terracing mountain slopes. The staple crops are sorghum and corn; cotton and coffee are grown as cash crops. Cattle, sheep and goats are raised for food and milk. The kitchen objects in the collection give the rest: an earthenware pot with two ring handles used for boiling water, a wooden bowl or platter used for eating, and a wooden coffee cup. Tobacco is consumed in two forms — smoked in a water-pipe whose pottery bowl still held traces of tobacco, and taken as snuff from a bone snuff-box with hide ends. *Ensete* is present as a fibre source for ornament string.

## Oral tradition & literature

The sources report Konso family traditions as a body of historical narrative: they hold that the Konso are a composite people, physically and culturally, whose members originally came from all the surrounding areas. Written Konso is recent. An alphabet was developed by the SNNPR education bureau together with SIL Ethiopia, with financial support from Wycliffe Norway; it is used in a Konso dictionary and has been adopted by the Konso for general use, alongside transcription in the Ethiopic script. The New Testament appeared in Konso in 2002. Scholarly documentation of the language began with Hellenthal's 2004 morphosyntactic study and was extended by Ongaye Oda Orkaydo's 2013 grammar. Kansite Gellebo's 2018 article *Waakka: Contemporary contexts of memorial emblems for Konso heroes* addresses the memorial figures.

## Language & religion

The Konso speak *Afaa Xonso*, a Lowland East Cushitic language of the Cushitic branch of Afro-Asiatic, with about 200,000 native speakers (SIL 2005). It has four dialects — Kholme, Duuro, Fasha and Karatti — shares close lexical similarity with Dirasha, and serves as a trade language beyond Konso territory; Blench (2006) treats the reputed dialects Gato and Turo as separate languages. Unusually for the region, Konso has neither voiced nor ejective consonants but a series of implosive stops, including the very rare uvular implosive /ʛ/, and it distinguishes five short and five long vowels. It is today written in the Ethiopic script. About 60% of the Konso are Christians; others practise a traditional religion centred on the worship of *Waaq*/Wakh, the god of the early faith held to have been followed by Cushitic groups.
